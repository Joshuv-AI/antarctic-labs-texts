Assessment of macroscopic properties of ecological building materials based on chemical microscopic phase composition and interface interaction mechanisms
Authors: Yan Pang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study investigates the macroscopic properties of ecological building materials prepared from recycled industrial waste through comprehensive analysis of chemical microscopic phase composition and interface interaction mechanisms. Three primary industrial wastes (fly ash, blast furnace slag, and steel slag) were systematically incorporated as cement replacements at various proportions. Advanced characterization techniques including X-ray diffraction, scanning electron microscopy, and mercury intrusion porosimetry were employed to establish quantitative relationships between microstructural parameters and macroscopic performance. Results demonstrate that optimized waste incorporation achieves superior mechanical properties, with the optimal combination containing 15% fly ash, 7.5% steel slag, and 22.5% blast furnace slag exhibiting 52.3 MPa compressive strength, representing 16.2% improvement over reference cement. Quantitative phase analysis revealed enhanced C–S–H gel formation (58.2% vs. 54.3%) through pozzolanic reactions, while interfacial transition zone characteristics significantly influenced overall performance. The developed structure–property relationship models achieved correlation coefficients exceeding 0.92 for strength prediction, enabling rational material design optimization. This research provides scientific foundations for waste-derived building material development while contributing to circular economy implementation and environmental sustainability in construction industry.

Introduction

Industrial waste recycling and environmental challenges

The accumulation of industrial waste has become one of the most pressing environmental challenges of the twenty-first century, with global industrial waste generation exceeding 13 billion tons annually and continuing to rise at an alarming rate1. Traditional disposal methods, including landfilling and incineration, not only consume valuable land resources but also pose significant threats to environmental sustainability and human health2. The construction industry, as one of the largest consumers of raw materials globally, presents unprecedented opportunities for industrial waste utilization, potentially transforming environmental burdens into valuable resources3.

The concept of circular economy has gained substantial momentum in recent years, emphasizing the transformation of linear “take-make-dispose” models into closed-loop systems that maximize resource efficiency and minimize waste generation4. Industrial waste recycling represents a critical component of this paradigm shift, offering dual benefits of environmental protection and resource conservation while reducing the dependency on virgin materials5. The urgency of implementing comprehensive waste recycling strategies has been further amplified by increasingly stringent environmental regulations and growing public awareness of sustainability issues6.

Current status and market demand for ecological building materials

The global ecological building materials market has experienced remarkable growth, reaching a valuation of approximately 364 billion USD in 2023 and projected to exceed 500 billion USD by 2030, driven by increasing environmental consciousness and regulatory requirements7. Contemporary ecological building materials encompass a diverse range of products, including recycled concrete aggregates, fly ash-based cement, steel slag composites, and bio-based materials, each offering unique advantages in terms of environmental impact reduction and resource utilization efficiency8. Recent advances in waste utilization have demonstrated the potential of sandstone processing waste and electric arc furnace slag as effective precursors for geopolymer production, achieving compressive strengths comparable to conventional cement while utilizing substantial quantities of industrial waste9–13. Additionally, recent research on alkali-activated mineral residues has shown promising results for bauxite residue and steel slag valorization in construction applications, with pavement tiles achieving mechanical properties exceeding 30 MPa while providing 25–27% reduction in CO₂ emissions compared to traditional concrete products14.

Despite significant technological advances, the widespread adoption of ecological building materials faces several challenges, including performance variability, quality consistency concerns, and limited understanding of long-term durability characteristics15. The market demand for high-performance ecological building materials continues to expand, particularly in developed countries where green building certification systems and carbon reduction policies drive innovation and adoption16. However, the gap between market expectations and current technological capabilities necessitates comprehensive research efforts to develop superior ecological building materials that can compete with conventional products in terms of both performance and cost-effectiveness.

Research significance and innovation framework

The assessment of macroscopic properties of ecological building materials through chemical microscopic phase composition and interface interaction mechanisms represents a paradigm shift from empirical evaluation methods to fundamental science-based approaches. Traditional performance evaluation techniques often rely on phenomenological observations and standardized testing protocols that provide limited insights into the underlying mechanisms governing material behavior. By establishing direct correlations between microscopic chemical compositions, interface interactions, and macroscopic performance characteristics, this research approach enables the development of predictive models and optimization strategies for enhanced material design.

The innovation of this research framework lies in its multiscale integration approach, which bridges the gap between molecular-level phenomena and engineering-scale applications. Understanding the chemical phase evolution during material formation processes, quantifying interface interaction energies, and correlating these microscopic parameters with macroscopic properties such as mechanical strength, durability, and thermal performance provides unprecedented opportunities for rational material design and optimization.

Furthermore, this research contributes to the development of sustainable construction technologies by enabling the precise control of material properties through microscopic phase engineering. The ability to predict and optimize macroscopic performance based on chemical composition and interface characteristics facilitates the efficient utilization of industrial waste materials while ensuring compliance with stringent performance requirements.

Research objectives and technical framework

The primary objective of this research is to establish a comprehensive evaluation framework for assessing the macroscopic properties of ecological building materials prepared from recycled industrial waste, with particular emphasis on elucidating the relationships between chemical microscopic phase composition, interface interaction mechanisms, and resulting performance characteristics. This investigation aims to develop predictive models that enable rational design and optimization of waste-derived building materials while ensuring superior performance and environmental sustainability.

The technical approach encompasses a multidisciplinary methodology integrating advanced characterization techniques, computational modeling, and experimental validation. The research framework includes comprehensive chemical analysis of industrial waste feedstocks, systematic investigation of phase transformation mechanisms during material processing, quantitative assessment of interface interaction forces and energies, and correlation analysis between microscopic parameters and macroscopic performance indicators.

The expected outcomes of this research include the development of scientific principles for waste-derived building material design, establishment of performance prediction models based on chemical composition and interface characteristics, and formulation of optimization strategies for enhanced material properties. These contributions will advance the field of sustainable construction materials while providing practical solutions for industrial waste utilization and environmental protection.

Literature review and theoretical foundation

Current application status of industrial waste in building materials

Fly ash applications and characteristics

Fly ash represents one of the most extensively studied industrial wastes in building material applications, with annual global production exceeding 900 million tons from coal-fired power plants17. The utilization of fly ash as a supplementary cementitious material has demonstrated significant potential for improving concrete properties, including enhanced workability, reduced permeability, and increased long-term strength development18. Recent investigations have revealed that the pozzolanic reactivity of fly ash is primarily governed by its silica and alumina content, with optimal performance achieved when SiO₂ + Al₂O₃ content exceeds 70% by mass19.

The physicochemical characteristics of fly ash vary considerably depending on coal source, combustion conditions, and collection methods, resulting in substantial variations in particle size distribution, chemical composition, and reactivity20. Class F fly ash, characterized by low calcium content, exhibits slower pozzolanic reactions compared to Class C fly ash, which contains higher calcium oxide levels and demonstrates both pozzolanic and cementitious properties21. Despite these advantages, the utilization rate of fly ash in many regions remains below 60%, primarily due to quality inconsistencies, seasonal availability variations, and limited understanding of long-term performance characteristics.

Steel slag integration and performance

Steel slag, generated at approximately 150–200 kg per ton of steel production, presents unique opportunities for building material applications due to its favorable mineralogical composition and inherent hydraulic properties22. The primary mineral phases in steel slag, including dicalcium silicate (C₂S), tricalcium silicate (C₃S), and calcium ferrite phases, contribute to its cementitious potential and mechanical strength development when properly processed23. Recent research has demonstrated that ground granulated steel slag can achieve compressive strengths comparable to ordinary Portland cement when used as a binder replacement at optimal replacement levels of 20–30% by mass.

However, the application of steel slag faces significant challenges related to volume instability caused by the slow hydration of free lime and magnesia, which can lead to expansion and cracking in hardened concrete24. Advanced treatment methods, including accelerated carbonation and controlled grinding, have been developed to mitigate these issues and enhance the reactivity of steel slag for building material applications. The heterogeneous nature of steel slag composition, influenced by steel production processes and raw material variations, necessitates comprehensive characterization and quality control protocols for consistent performance.

Mineral slag utilization and technological advances

Ground granulated blast furnace slag represents a premium industrial waste with exceptional performance characteristics in cement and concrete applications, exhibiting latent hydraulic properties that surpass many other supplementary cementitious materials. The glassy structure of rapidly cooled blast furnace slag, containing approximately 95% amorphous phases, provides superior reactivity and contributes to enhanced durability and reduced permeability in concrete systems. Contemporary research has focused on optimizing activation methods, including chemical activation with alkali solutions and mechanical activation through ultrafine grinding, to maximize the utilization potential of mineral slag in various building material formulations.

The integration of multiple industrial wastes in hybrid systems has emerged as a promising approach to leverage synergistic effects and optimize material properties while achieving higher waste utilization rates. However, current technological limitations include insufficient understanding of complex chemical interactions, limited predictive models for performance optimization, and challenges in achieving consistent quality control across different waste sources and processing conditions.

Theoretical relationship between chemical microscopic phase composition and macroscopic properties

Fundamental theory of chemical microscopic phase composition

The chemical microscopic phase composition of cementitious building materials fundamentally determines their macroscopic performance characteristics through complex hydration reactions and phase evolution processes25. The theoretical framework governing these relationships is based on the principle that mechanical properties arise from the intrinsic bonding characteristics and spatial distribution of hydration products within the material matrix26. The primary hydration products, including calcium silicate hydrate (C–S–H) gel, ettringite, and calcium hydroxide, exhibit distinct contributions to overall mechanical performance based on their chemical composition, crystalline structure, and interfacial bonding properties.

Quantitative phase-property relationship models

The establishment of quantitative relationships between microscopic phase composition and macroscopic properties requires comprehensive understanding of phase interactions and their cumulative effects on material behavior. Advanced models incorporate the weighted contributions of individual phases, considering their volume fractions, intrinsic properties, and synergistic interactions within the composite matrix. The heterogeneous nature of phase distribution and the presence of interfacial transition zones significantly influence the overall mechanical response, necessitating multiscale modeling approaches that bridge molecular-level interactions with continuum mechanics principles32. These theoretical foundations provide the framework for predicting and optimizing material properties through controlled manipulation of chemical phase composition and microstructural characteristics in waste-derived building materials.

Interface interaction mechanisms and performance control

Physicochemical interface interactions in waste-cement systems

The interface between industrial waste particles and cement matrix represents a critical zone where complex physicochemical interactions determine the overall performance characteristics of composite building materials33. The fundamental interaction mechanisms include van der Waals forces, electrostatic attractions, chemical bonding through hydration reactions, and mechanical interlocking at the particle–matrix interface. The surface chemistry of waste particles significantly influences these interactions, with reactive silica and alumina phases promoting pozzolanic reactions that enhance interfacial bonding strength, while inert components may create weak zones that compromise mechanical integrity34.

The chemical reactivity of waste particle surfaces varies substantially depending on their origin and processing history, with amorphous phases generally exhibiting higher reactivity than crystalline phases due to their higher surface energy and dissolution rates. Surface hydroxylation and the formation of silanol and aluminol groups facilitate chemical bonding with calcium ions from the cement hydration products, leading to the development of additional C–S–H gel at the interface region. The pH environment, ionic strength, and temperature conditions significantly affect the kinetics and extent of these interfacial reactions, thereby influencing the final bonding characteristics and mechanical properties.

Interfacial transition zone microstructural characteristics

The interfacial transition zone (ITZ) represents a distinct microstructural region extending 20–50 µm from the waste particle surface, characterized by altered porosity, modified hydration product distribution, and different mechanical properties compared to the bulk cement matrix35. The ITZ typically exhibits higher porosity and larger pore sizes due to the wall effect of waste particles during cement hydration, resulting in preferential orientation of calcium hydroxide crystals and reduced C–S–H gel density. These microstructural variations create a gradient in mechanical properties, with the ITZ often representing the weakest link in the composite material system.

The thickness and properties of the ITZ are influenced by several factors, including particle size distribution, surface roughness, chemical composition, and water-to-cement ratio. Smaller waste particles tend to develop thinner ITZ regions with improved properties due to reduced wall effects and enhanced packing efficiency. Surface treatments and chemical modifications of waste particles can significantly alter ITZ characteristics by promoting better interfacial bonding and reducing porosity gradients36.

Interface modification and performance optimization strategies

The theoretical framework for interface modification is based on the principle of optimizing chemical compatibility and mechanical bonding between waste particles and cement matrix through targeted surface treatments and chemical additions. Silane coupling agents, surface activation through alkaline treatment, and controlled particle size distribution represent effective strategies for enhancing interfacial bonding and reducing ITZ porosity37. The use of supplementary cementitious materials, such as silica fume or metakaolin, can improve ITZ properties by filling voids and promoting additional pozzolanic reactions at the interface.

Performance optimization requires a comprehensive understanding of the relationship between interfacial characteristics and macroscopic properties, enabling the development of predictive models for material design and quality control. The mechanical properties of waste-cement composites are strongly influenced by ITZ quality, with improvements in interfacial bonding leading to enhanced strength, reduced permeability, and improved durability. Advanced characterization techniques, including nanoindentation and electron microscopy, provide essential insights into ITZ properties and enable the validation of interface modification strategies for optimized material performance.

The model considers the weighted contributions of individual waste materials, their synergistic effects, and the influence of processing parameters on final composite properties, providing a robust framework for material design optimization and quality control in industrial applications.

Specimen preparation and curing process

Raw material preprocessing and quality control

The preparation of ecological building materials requires systematic preprocessing of industrial waste materials to ensure consistent quality and optimal reactivity for subsequent hydration reactions. The raw material preprocessing involved mechanical grinding of steel slag to achieve particle sizes below 75 μm using a ball mill operated at 45 rpm for 4 h, while fly ash and blast furnace slag were sieved through 45 μm mesh to remove oversized particles and ensure uniform size distribution41. All materials were dried at 105 °C for 24 h to eliminate moisture content and prevent agglomeration during mixing, followed by storage in sealed containers under controlled atmospheric conditions to maintain consistent properties throughout the experimental period.

The quality control procedures included homogenization of waste materials through mechanical blending for 30 min to achieve uniform chemical composition distribution, particularly critical for steel slag due to its heterogeneous nature. Chemical activation of blast furnace slag was performed using sodium hydroxide solution (2% by mass) to enhance its pozzolanic reactivity, while fly ash underwent surface treatment with silane coupling agents to improve interfacial bonding characteristics with cement matrix.

The mixing procedure followed a standardized protocol designed to achieve homogeneous distribution of materials while minimizing air entrainment and ensuring optimal workability for subsequent molding operations. Curing regimes and performance implications

The curing methodology employed both standard water curing at 20 ± 2 °C and 95% relative humidity, and accelerated steam curing at 60 °C for 6 h followed by standard curing conditions to evaluate the influence of different thermal histories on hydration kinetics and final properties. Standard curing specimens were maintained in lime-saturated water for testing periods of 7, 28, and 90 days, while accelerated curing specimens underwent immediate strength testing after thermal treatment to assess early-age performance characteristics. The comparative analysis of curing regimes provides essential insights into the optimization of production schedules and quality control procedures for industrial applications of waste-derived ecological building materials.

Correlation analysis system for structure–property relationships

The establishment of quantitative relationships between microstructural parameters and macroscopic properties required development of comprehensive correlation analysis methodologies incorporating statistical regression analysis, artificial neural network modeling, and multi-variable optimization techniques44. The correlation framework integrates phase composition data from XRD analysis, pore structure parameters from MIP measurements, and interfacial characteristics from SEM/EDS investigations to predict mechanical performance and durability properties through validated mathematical models. The data processing protocols include normalization procedures, outlier detection algorithms, and cross-validation methods to ensure robust predictive capabilities for material design optimization and quality control applications in industrial production of waste-derived ecological building materials.

Life cycle assessment methodology

The life cycle assessment (LCA) was conducted following ISO 14040/14044 standards to evaluate the environmental impacts of waste-incorporated ecological building materials. The system boundaries were defined using a cradle-to-gate approach, encompassing raw material extraction, transportation, processing, and manufacturing stages until the final product leaves the factory gate. The functional unit was defined as 1 m3 of concrete with equivalent performance characteristics.

The LCA analysis was performed using SimaPro 9.0 software with the Ecoinvent 3.8 database for background processes. Impact assessment included climate change potential (GWP 100), primary energy consumption, and natural resource depletion indicators. The allocation method followed mass-based allocation for multi-output processes, while industrial waste materials were considered as having zero environmental burden at the point of waste generation, reflecting their status as by-products rather than primary products.

Durability performance assessment

The durability evaluation encompassed freeze–thaw resistance, carbonation resistance, and chloride permeability testing to assess long-term performance characteristics under aggressive environmental conditions. The freeze–thaw resistance testing after 300 cycles revealed that Mix 5 exhibited only 2.1% mass loss and 3.8% strength reduction, significantly outperforming the reference mixture which showed 4.7% mass loss and 8.2% strength reduction. This enhanced freeze–thaw resistance is attributed to the refined pore structure and improved interfacial bonding achieved through optimized waste material incorporation.

Carbonation depth measurements after 90 days of accelerated exposure indicated that waste-incorporated mixtures demonstrated superior carbonation resistance compared to conventional cement, with Mix 5 showing 40% reduction in carbonation depth relative to the reference mixture. The chloride permeability testing revealed charge passed values ranging from 1850 to 3200 Coulombs for waste-incorporated mixtures, with optimal formulations achieving “low” permeability classification according to ASTM C1202 standards48.

Additional durability testing of the optimal Mix 5 revealed enhanced sulfate resistance with 6.2% ± 0.8% strength loss after 180 days exposure to 5% sodium sulfate solution compared to 11.8% ± 1.2% for reference cement (Mix 1). Accelerated carbonation testing (1% CO₂, 65% RH, 20°C) showed 38% reduction in carbonation depth for optimized mixtures after 90 days exposure. Life cycle assessment conducted according to ISO 14040/14044 standards indicates potential reductions of 35% in CO₂ emissions and 42% in natural resource consumption compared to conventional cement production, based on cradle-to-gate system boundaries and assuming 100 km average transportation distance for waste materials.

The comprehensive durability assessment confirms that properly designed ecological building materials incorporating industrial waste can achieve superior long-term performance characteristics while contributing to environmental sustainability through resource conservation and waste reduction.

Interface bonding mechanisms and chemical interactions

The interface bonding mechanisms between waste particles and cement matrix involve multiple concurrent processes including physical adhesion, chemical bonding through hydration reactions, and mechanical interlocking at particle–matrix interfaces. The chemical bonding mechanisms are primarily governed by pozzolanic reactions where amorphous silica and alumina phases from waste materials react with calcium hydroxide produced during cement hydration, forming additional C–S–H gel with modified stoichiometry and enhanced binding capacity. The dissolution kinetics of waste particle surfaces create localized chemical environments with elevated pH values and ionic concentrations that promote accelerated hydration product formation and improved interfacial bonding.

The surface reactivity of different waste materials significantly influences the extent and nature of interfacial interactions, with blast furnace slag exhibiting the highest reactivity due to its glassy structure and favorable chemical composition. Steel slag particles demonstrated selective reactivity with certain mineral phases contributing to C–S–H formation while iron-rich phases remained largely unreacted, creating heterogeneous interfacial characteristics that require optimization through particle size control and surface treatment strategies. The synergistic effects of combined waste materials resulted in enhanced interfacial bonding through complementary chemical reactions and improved particle packing efficiency.

Theoretical prediction validation and model accuracy

The validation of theoretical predictions through experimental verification demonstrated excellent agreement between model predictions and measured performance characteristics, with prediction errors typically below 8% for compressive strength and 12% for flexural strength across the investigated parameter ranges. The predictive accuracy was highest for mixtures with moderate waste replacement levels (15–25%) where interfacial interactions remained within the optimized parameter space used for model development54. The systematic analysis of prediction errors revealed that deviations were primarily associated with extreme waste replacement levels or unusual waste material combinations that exceeded the model calibration boundaries.

The model refinement through iterative optimization procedures and expanded experimental databases improved prediction accuracy and extended the applicable parameter ranges for reliable performance estimation. The validated structure–property relationship models provide essential tools for rational material design, enabling optimization of waste material combinations and processing parameters to achieve target performance characteristics while maximizing waste utilization rates. The successful validation of theoretical predictions confirms the fundamental understanding of interface interaction mechanisms and their quantitative influence on macroscopic properties, supporting the development of high-performance ecological building materials through science-based design approaches.

Conclusions

The systematic investigation demonstrates that optimized waste incorporation (15% fly ash, 7.5% steel slag, 22.5% blast furnace slag) achieves 16.2% strength improvement over reference cement while utilizing 45% industrial waste by mass. Enhanced C–(A)–S–H gel formation (58.2% vs. 54.3%) through pozzolanic reactions and refined interfacial transition zone characteristics contribute to superior mechanical properties and durability performance.

The quantitative structure–property relationship models achieved correlation coefficients exceeding 0.92 for the investigated parameter ranges, demonstrating potential for predictive material design within the studied conditions. Life cycle assessment conducted using cradle-to-gate boundaries indicates potential reductions of 35% in CO₂ emissions and 42% in natural resource consumption compared to conventional cement production, though these benefits are contingent upon local waste availability and transportation distances.

Industrial application prospects show promise within the tested parameter ranges, though widespread adoption requires standardized quality control protocols, long-term performance validation under diverse environmental conditions, and economic feasibility studies. Future research should focus on expanded material combinations, machine learning-enhanced predictive modeling, and automated material optimization for sustainable construction practices.

Research limitations

This study presents several limitations that should be considered in the interpretation and application of results. The experimental investigation was limited to specific industrial waste sources from particular geographic regions, which may exhibit different characteristics than wastes from other sources. The testing was conducted under controlled laboratory conditions with standard curing protocols, and performance under field conditions or alternative curing regimes may vary. The structure–property relationship models were developed based on nine mix designs and may require validation for compositions outside the investigated parameter space. Long-term durability assessment was limited to 90 days, and extended performance characteristics require further investigation. The LCA analysis employed cradle-to-gate boundaries and may not capture the full environmental impact throughout the complete building lifecycle.
