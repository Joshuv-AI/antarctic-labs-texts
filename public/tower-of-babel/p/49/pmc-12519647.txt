Comparison of efficacy and safety of different types of electrical stimulation for shoulder subluxation after acute stroke: protocol for a systematic review and network meta-analysis of randomised controlled trials
Authors: Mengfan Kan, Qiang Chen, Yuqin Dan, Linlin Li, Wenkai Li, Linlin Zhang, Yitong Lu, Ziyun Feng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionGlenohumeral subluxation (GHS) is a common rehabilitation challenge in the hemiplegic upper limb following stroke, potentially leading to shoulder pain, secondary brachial plexus injury and various other complications. While electrical stimulation therapies, such as electromyography biofeedback, electroacupuncture and neuromuscular electrical stimulation, have shown promise in managing GHS, some controversy remains. Although clinical trials and meta-analyses have confirmed the efficacy of these therapies, healthcare professionals have yet to reach a consensus on which specific therapy is most effective for reducing shoulder subluxation (SS), alleviating pain and improving quality of life. This study will perform a network meta-analysis to compare the relative efficacy of different electrical stimulation therapies for treating GHS in patients following acute stroke.Methods and analysisWe will systematically search the following databases: PubMed, MEDLINE, Embase, Cochrane Library, Web of Science, Chinese biomedical literature database (SinoMed), Wanfang databases (Wanfang), VIP Journal Integration Platform (VIP) and China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI). Our search will cover the period from the inception of each database until 14 April 2025, and will be restricted to studies published in Chinese or English. The primary outcomes of interest will be the degree of improvement in SS, improvements in quality of life and reductions in pain. We will conduct pairwise meta-analyses using RevMan V.5.3 (The Cochrane Collaboration, Copenhagen, Denmark), and network meta-analyses using ADDIS V.1.16.6 (Drugis, Groningen, The Netherlands) and Stata V.16.0 (StataCorp, College Station, Texas, USA) to compare the relative efficacy of different electrical stimulation therapies. Screening, data extraction, risk of bias assessment and evaluation of the certainty of evidence will all be performed independently by two reviewers to ensure accuracy and reliability. The risk of bias within individual studies will be assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 (ROB 2) tool, and the certainty of evidence will be evaluated using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, and Evaluation (GRADE) and Confidence in Network Meta-Analysis (CINeMA) frameworks to ensure transparency and methodological rigour.Ethics and disseminationEthical approval is not required for this study. The findings will be submitted to a peer-reviewed journal or conference.PROSPERO registration numberCRD42024541228.

Introduction

Stroke ranks among the top three causes of increased mortality worldwide, and the incidence of stroke events in Europe is projected to rise by 34% by 2035.1 2 Glenohumeral subluxation (GHS) stands out as one of the most prevalent secondary limb dysfunctions in stroke patients, with reported incidences ranging from 7% to 81%.3 GHS is influenced by the muscle strength and tone of the affected upper limb, with approximately 73% of cases occurring during the acute stage of stroke.4 Once shoulder subluxation (SS) occurs, the condition of the patient becomes complicated, leading to a series of complications such as shoulder–hand syndrome, post-hemiplegic shoulder pain and secondary brachial plexus nerve injury.57 These complications can significantly impact the recovery of upper limb motor function and proprioception of the patient, ultimately resulting in a decline in activities of daily living (ADL) capacity.8 In addition, long-term rehabilitation treatment will increase patients' psychological pressure, reduce their confidence in recovery and quality of life, and place a heavy economic burden on patients, families and society.9 10 While GHS primarily affects adult stroke patients, its implications for paediatric patients should not be overlooked. Paediatric stroke patients, due to their developing musculoskeletal systems, may experience prolonged recovery periods and increased vulnerability to GHS-related complications, emphasising the need for early intervention strategies.11

GHS remains a challenging problem in rehabilitation clinical work. Currently, the pathogenesis of GHS after stroke remains unclear. Modern medicine believes that it is related to the anatomical structure of the shoulder joint, the strength of shoulder muscles such as the supraspinatus, infraspinatus and deltoid, and the imbalance of muscle tension.1214 Additionally, the locking mechanism of the shoulder joint can be disrupted by the gravitational pull on the affected upper limb, further contributing to the condition.15 16 Furthermore, improper positioning and pulling of the upper limb on the affected side in hemiplegic patients can exacerbate GHS after stroke.1719 Once subluxation occurs, a small percentage of patients may gradually achieve spontaneous reduction during the middle to late stages of spasticity. However, the majority of patients are unable to achieve this on their own. Some patients, due to prolonged subluxation and consequent soft tissue contracture, may ultimately develop permanent disability. Therefore, timely and appropriate treatment of GHS following a stroke is crucial for preventing long-term complications and ensuring better recovery outcomes.

Recent advances in neuroprotective strategies offer promising avenues for improving outcomes in patients with GHS by addressing both neurological and biomechanical aspects of the condition.20

At present, exercise therapy, manual therapy, acupuncture, electrical stimulation therapy, kinesio taping and shoulder protector devices (such as shoulder straps), combined with health education and routine nursing care, are often used in the clinical treatment of GHS following a stroke.1719 Electrostimulation therapy, as a common physical factor therapy, has the advantages of low cost and easy operation and has been widely applied in the treatment of GHS in recent years.2127 Moreover, clinical trials and meta-analyses have confirmed the efficacy of various forms of electrical stimulation therapy, including neuromuscular electrical stimulation (NMES), electromyographic biofeedback, transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation and functional electrical stimulation (FES), in treating GHS in patients with acute stroke.3 28 However, there is no consensus on which intervention strategy has the best effect in reducing the degree of GHS, improving the motor function of upper limbs, alleviating pain and improving the quality of life.

Network meta-analysis (NMA) is a further extension of traditional meta-analysis, which can combine direct and indirect comparison evidence and compare multiple interventions at the same time.29 In addition, NMA can also rank the effectiveness of different interventions to give the optimal treatment plan.30 This study will systematically evaluate the efficacy of electrical stimulation therapy on GHS and compare the relative efficacy and safety of different electrical stimulation therapies in improving GHS in stroke patients through NMA, in order to furnish clinicians with evidence-based guidance for the selection of appropriate treatment protocols.

Methods

Study registration

This protocol adheres to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses protocol (PRISMA-P) guidelines for reporting systematic review protocols.31 This study has been registered on PROSPERO (No. CRD42024541228) on 9 May 2024.

Inclusion criteria of studies

Types of studies

The type of studies included in this review will be randomised controlled trials (RCTs) that are fully reported, with explicit mention of terms such as ‘randomised controlled’ or ‘random’ and specific methods like ‘random number table’. The language of publication will be restricted to Chinese or English.

Types of participants

All participants will meet the following criteria:

Refer to any authoritative or recognised diagnostic criteria for stroke or other similar criteria, clearly confirmed through imaging techniques such as CT or MRI.

Refer to any authoritative or recognised diagnostic criteria for stroke or other similar criteria, clearly confirmed through imaging techniques such as CT or MRI.

X-ray, CT and other imaging examinations were consistent with the diagnostic criteria for SS.

X-ray, CT and other imaging examinations were consistent with the diagnostic criteria for SS.

Participants aged ≥18 years, with stroke onset within 1 month, regardless of gender, nationality, education level or disease severity.

Participants aged ≥18 years, with stroke onset within 1 month, regardless of gender, nationality, education level or disease severity.

Types of interventions

The experimental group will receive electrical stimulation in addition to conventional rehabilitation therapy. Given the results of our previous literature search, the electrical stimulation will include electromyography biofeedback therapy (EMGBF), electroacupuncture stimulation, NMES, FES, transcutaneous electrical stimulation (TENS), low-frequency modulated mid-frequency electricity stimulating, electromyography-triggered neuromuscular stimulation and intermediate frequency pulse electrical stimulation.

Types of control groups

The control group will receive conventional rehabilitation, including any pharmacotherapy or non-pharmaceutical therapy other than electrical stimulation therapy.

Types of outcome measures

Primary outcome

Based on a preliminary search of the relevant literature, the primary outcome measures will include the degree of dislocation of the shoulder joint. The acromiohumeral interval (AHI) is measured by shoulder X-ray to determine the degree of SS.32 A larger AHI indicates a greater degree of SS.

Secondary outcomes

The secondary outcome measures will include:

Upper extremity motor function: Fugl-Meyer Assessment-Upper Extremities (FMA-UE) is used to evaluate the upper limb function of the patients.33 There are 33 items in the scale, and each item is divided into three levels, scoring 0 points (unable to complete), 1 point (partially completed) and 2 points (fully completed), with a total score of 66. The higher the score, the better the upper limb motor function.

Search methods for identification of studies

Electronic searches

The search strategies for all databases will be designed and executed by two researchers (LZ and WL), with consultation from a specialist librarian to ensure comprehensiveness and methodological rigour. We will systematically search PubMed, MEDLINE, Embase, Cochrane Library, Web of Science, Chinese biomedical literature database (SinoMed), Wanfang databases (Wanfang), VIP databases (VIP), and China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI) to collect RCTs on electrical stimulation intervention for GHS following acute stroke. The search will span from the establishment of each database to 14 April 2025. Searches will be conducted using a combination of subject headings and free-text terms, including: ‘Stroke’, ‘Cerebrovascular Accident’, ‘Cerebrovascular Apoplexy’, ‘CVA’, ‘Brain Vascular Accident’, ‘Glenohumeral Subluxation’, ‘GHS’, ‘shoulder subluxation’, ‘SS’, ‘Electric Stimulation’, ‘electromyography biofeedback therapy’, ‘EMGBF’, ‘electroacupuncture stimulation’, ‘neuromuscular electrical stimulation’, ‘NMES’, ‘functional electrical stimulation’, ‘FES’, ‘transcutaneous electrical stimulation’, ‘TENS’, ‘low-frequency modulated mid-frequency electricity stimulating’, ‘intermediate frequency pulse electrical stimulation’, ‘electromyography-triggered neuromuscular stimulation’, ‘randomised controlled trial’ and ‘RCT’. The detailed search strategies for all databases, including PubMed, are provided in the online supplemental material.

Searching other sources

Google Scholar, Medical Matrix and other search engines will be used to search for relevant literature on the Internet, track down references that have been included in the literature, and will contact experts in the field and corresponding authors to obtain useful information not found in the above search.

Assessment for risk of bias in included studies

Two investigators (LZ and WL) will independently conduct methodological quality assessments using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 (RoB 2) tool, in accordance with the Cochrane Handbook V.6.5.35 Risk of bias evaluations will be performed across five core domains and visualised through forest plots generated in RevMan V.5.3. The RoB 2 tool assesses bias across five domains: (1) randomisation process, (2) deviations from intended interventions, (3) missing outcome data, (4) outcome measurement, and (5) selection of reported results. Options for assessing results will include low risk of bias, high risk of bias and unclear risk of bias. In the case of disagreement over the assessment process, a third researcher (YD) will make the final decision.

Grading the quality of evidence

Two researchers (LZ and WL) will independently assess the quality of the evidence using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, and Evaluation (GRADE),36 which considers five downgrading factors (limitations, consistency, directness, imprecision and reporting bias) and three upgrading factors (dose-response relationship, plausible confounding factors and magnitude of effect). The assessment will be conducted in accordance with the latest methodological guidelines. Additionally, we will use the Confidence in Network Meta-Analysis approach to evaluate confidence in the results from the NMA.37 This approach covers six domains: within-study bias, reporting bias, indirectness, imprecision, heterogeneity and incoherence. Each domain will be judged as no concerns, some concerns or major concerns, and judgments across domains will be summarised to obtain four levels of confidence for each relative treatment effect, corresponding to the usual GRADE assessments of very low, low, moderate or high. In the case of disagreement during the assessment process, the two researchers will discuss to reach a consensus. If consensus cannot be reached, a third researcher (YD) will make the final decision.

Statistical analysis

Pairwise meta-analysis

We will conduct pairwise meta-analyses using RevManV.5.3 to compare electrical stimulation therapies for GHS following acute stroke. The primary outcome will be the degree of SS measured by the AHI. Secondary outcomes will include upper extremity motor function (assessed using the Fugl-Meyer Assessment-Upper Extremity (FMA-UE)), pain intensity (evaluated via the VAS, 0–100 mm)), and functional independence (measured by the MBI, range 0–100). Dichotomous outcomes will be analysed using ORs with 95% CIs, while continuous outcomes will be expressed as mean differences (MD) with 95% CIs. Heterogeneity will be assessed through Cochran’s Q test (χ², threshold p>0.10) and quantified via Higgins and Thompson’s I² statistic (low heterogeneity: I² ≤50%; high heterogeneity: I² >50%).38 A fixed-effects model (Mantel-Haenszel method) will be applied for low heterogeneity, whereas a random-effects model (DerSimonian-Laird estimator) will be used for high heterogeneity.39 Sensitivity analyses will compare both models, and prediction intervals will supplement heterogeneity interpretation. For outcomes measured with incompatible scales (eg, VAS vs numerical rating scale), standardisation will be achieved through unit conversion (eg, cm to mm for AHI) or subgroup stratification.

Network meta-analysis

We will use ADDIS V.1.16.6 to conduct NMA of different electrical stimulation interventions. The efficacy and safety of these interventions will be compared and ranked using the surface under the cumulative ranking curve. Within the NMA framework, we will use the Markov Chain Monte Carlo method within a Bayesian framework for computation and statistics. Initially, we will set up four Markov chains for simulation, with a total of 50,000 iterations. The first 20,000 iterations will be used for annealing to eliminate the influence of initial values. When there are closed loops in the evidence network, node-splitting analysis will be used to assess inconsistency. If p>0.05, it indicates that there is no significant inconsistency between direct comparison and indirect comparison, and the consistency model is used for analysis. Otherwise, the inconsistency model is used to analyse. Binary variables will be expressed using OR and 95% credible intervals (CrI), while continuous variables will be represented by MD and 95% CrI. The results of NMA will be presented using forest plots to display the estimates from the Bayesian analysis. For OR values, if the value is >1 for a positive outcome, it indicates that the intervention is superior to the control group. Conversely, for negative outcomes such as adverse events, an OR >1 indicates a higher likelihood of the negative outcome with the intervention. The potential scale reduced factor (PSRF) will also be used to evaluate the convergence degree of the model. A PSRF close to 1 indicates that the model convergence is good and the conclusions obtained are credible. Additionally, we will use Stata V.16.0 to create network plots and funnel plots, and assess publication bias through the funnel plot.

Missing data

When literature data is missing, we will reach out to the corresponding authors via telephone, email or other means to obtain the missing information. Before receiving accurate responses, we will not use any statistical method to analyse the data.

Assessment of reporting bias

We will use a funnel plot to conduct publication bias analysis for pairwise meta-analyses.40 If the scatter plot of the funnel plot is located in the upper-middle portion and the scatter points on the left and right sides are symmetric, it indicates that the study has minimal publication bias. Conversely, if the scatter plot of the funnel plot is located in the lower-middle portion and the scatter points on the left and right sides are asymmetric, it suggests that the study likely suffers from publication bias or substantial publication bias exists. In order to further detect and quantify publication bias, Begg’s test and Egger’s test will be carried out.41 If p>0.05, it indicates that there is no publication bias.

For NMAs we will use the Risk of Bias in NMA tool to systematically assess publication bias risk, evaluating methodological limitations across three critical domains: (1) interventions and network topology, focusing on treatment selection rationale and structural coherence of evidence networks; (2) effect modifiers, examining clinical and methodological heterogeneity; and (3) statistical synthesis, appraising model specification and analytical approaches.

Sensitivity analysis

When I² >50% and p<0.1 indicate high heterogeneity, we will use the leave-one-out method to conduct sensitivity analysis to explore the studies that have a greater impact on the total combined effect size.42 If the total heterogeneity and effect size of the study did not change or changed little after excluding a single study, it indicates that the results of the meta-analysis were stable and reliable. Additionally, we will consider using meta-regression to explore potential sources of heterogeneity if sufficient data are available.

Subgroup analysis

If the total pooled effect size results are stable and the included literatures are sufficient, further subgroup analyses will be performed based on predefined characteristics such as sex, stroke type, stroke stage (by Brunnstrom stage), and follow-up duration to address potential heterogeneity and inconsistencies. If necessary, we will use NMA to explore the sources of heterogeneity, using node-splitting analysis to assess inconsistency within the network of evidence.43
