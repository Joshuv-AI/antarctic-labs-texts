Association of non-alcoholic fatty liver polygenic risk scores with the incident and progressive non-alcoholic fatty liver disease in Chinese population
Authors: Yang Zhou, Di Zhou, Shuohua Chen, Chenlu Yang, Shutong Wu, Xiaomo Wang, Yanqi Lan, Ying Lu, Mengge Zhou, Shouling Wu, Man Li, Li Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) is an escalating health concern, with genetic factors playing a substantial role. This study aimed to establish and validate a polygenic risk score for East Asian populations (PRS-EA).MethodsA meta-analysis of genome-wide association studies on NAFLD in East Asian populations was conducted to develop a PRS-EA model. This model was subsequently validated in the Kailuan cohort by exploring its association with NAFLD development and progression.ResultsA PRS-EA model involving four SNPs (rs2896019, rs3810622, rs58542926, and rs1260326) was constructed. Individuals with PRS-EA scores exceeding 2.33 were associated with a 53% (95% confidence interval, 3%-126%) increased risk of NAFLD development and 50% (95% confidence interval: 3%-120%) of NAFLD progression. A positive joint association was observed between higher PRS and lipid dysfunction in relation to NAFLD occurrence. Hypertension was found to amplify the association between higher PRS and NAFLD progression (hazard ratio: 3.07 vs. 1.22, P for interaction=0.015).ConclusionThis study developed a PRS model specific to East Asian populations, highlighting the potential utility of PRS-EA in assessing and managing NAFLD risk, particularly in individuals with lipid metabolic dysfunction and hypertensionGraphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12263-025-00779-4.

Introduction

Non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) includes a spectrum of liver injuries characterized by excessive hepatic fat accumulation (> 5%) after excluding other causes¹, with a global prevalence of 38%[1]. In China, the prevalence of NAFLD has increased from 25.4% in 2008–2010 to 32.3% in 2015–2018[2]. NAFLD spans from steatosis to more advanced non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH), both of which can progress to liver fibrosis, cirrhosis, and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), and is associated with a higher risk of extrahepatic adverse conditions, such as cardiovascular diseases, certain cancers, and chronic kidney disease, etc [3]. However, not all patients will experience adverse outcomes, highlighting the need to identify patients with a high risk of progression.

Genetic factors are integral to the occurrence and progression of NAFLD [4]. Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have identified several genetic loci associated with NAFLD. Notably, four variants are well-established, each possessing distinct biological functions: patatin-like phospholipase domain-containing 3 (PNPLA3) rs738409, a key regulator of lipid droplet metabolism; transmembrane 6 superfamily member 2 (TM6SF2) rs58542926, which is involved in VLDL secretion; glucokinase regulator (GCKR) rs1260326, modulating glycolysis; and membrane-bound O-acyltransferase domain-containing 7 (MBOAT7) rs641738, which plays a role in lipid remodeling [5–8]. Given these loci affect the disease through diverse mechanisms, relying on a single locus for risk stratification in NAFLD is inadequate [5, 6]. Therefore, the polygenic risk score (PRS) has been developed to systematically evaluate an individual’s genetic predisposition for NAFLD.

Several weighted PRSs were developed from GWAS based on European and American populations, including a score for hepatic fat content (PRS-HFC) and another adjusted for the hydroxysteroid 17-beta dehydrogenase 13 (HSD17B13) rs72613567 (PRS-5) [9, 10]. In these patients, both scores demonstrated significant correlations with NAFLD and were instrumental in indicating the risks of fibrosis and HCC. However, the trans-ethnic applicability of these PRS models is limited because NAFLD is a multifactorial disease influenced by a complex interplay of genetics and environmental exposures that vary across populations.

Genetic and environmental factors may affect the transferability of PRS across different populations [11]. Ethnic stratification is particularly pronounced in NAFLD genetics of NAFLD [12]. For example, the minor allele frequency of PNPLA3 rs738409 is 48% in American [12]11.8% in African [12]; 50% in East Asian populations, and 38% in China [13]. Additionally, China exhibits distinct patterns in NAFLD risk factors, such as economic background, dietary habits, and physical activity levels, compared to the European and American population [2]. This variability highlights the challenge of directly transferring risk models across different populations. Until now, PRS models for NAFLD have been predominantly developed for Western populations, with limited validation in East Asians [14, 15]including China.

Therefore, to enhance the understanding of the prevention and management of NAFLD, we aimed to develop a PRS model for East Asian populations (PRS-EA) by utilizing GWAS data from East Asian populations, and investigated the model’s associations with the occurrence and progression of NAFLD using the Kailuan cohort.

Methods

First, we developed a polygenetic risk score targeting the East Asian population (PRS-EA) based on a comprehensive set of NAFLD susceptibility loci identified in the published GWAS [16–21]. Then, the PRS-EA was evaluated through a nested case-control study and cohort study in the Kailuan cohort, a large-scale, prospective, community-based study established in 2006 in northern China [22].

PRS-EA establishment

PRS-EA was constructed following the polygenic risk score analysis guide [23].

Screening risk loci for NAFLD

Initially, we screened the risk loci identified in published GWAS of NAFLD. Two researchers (in this nested case-control study) independently conducted a systematic literature search, data extraction, and quality assessment. A combination of two subject terms (“Genome-Wide Association Study” and “Non-alcoholic Fatty Liver Disease”) and their synonyms (Supplementary Text 1) were used to search the literature in the PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, and Cochrane databases. Also, “Non-alcoholic Fatty Liver Disease” was used as the search term in the GWAS catalog database [24]. All searches encompassed the database’s inception until February 16, 2025. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) the study population was East Asians (China, Japan, Korea, North Korea, and Mongolia); and (2) the outcome was NAFLD diagnosed by biopsy, clinical diagnostic information from electronic information database record forms, abdominal ultrasound, or nuclear magnetic resonance diagnosis. (3) the language was English. Moreover, we excluded studies that were not original or did not provide sufficient information on the susceptibility locus, including SNP ID, odds ratio (OR), corresponding 95% confidence interval (CI) (or β and standard error), and P value.

Study characteristics, design, population characteristics (sample size, country, ethnicity, age, and sex distribution), locus information (location, corresponding gene, frequency of effect allele, and non-effect allele, beta coefficient or OR, and P value), and locus screening criteria of all the included studies were extracted (Table S1 and S2). Criteria for determining risk loci vary across studies, encompassing loci with P values ≤ 5 × 10−5 or lower.

Quality assessment was conducted using the Newcastle-Ottawa Quality Assessment Scale (Table S3) [25]. Any discrepancies were resolved by consensus after thorough discussion.

Constructing PRS-EA models

A meta-analysis was conducted to obtain the effect of risk loci on NAFLD in the East Asian population. We employed I2 and Q statistics to evaluate the heterogeneity across studies. Given the anticipated heterogeneity among studies, we applied a random-effect model using the DerSimonian and Laird estimator to integrate the effect sizes, complemented by a fixed-effect model for sensitivity analysis. Egger’s regression was employed to examine publication bias for susceptibility loci reported in three or more studies. Then, the SNPs were optimized by selecting those with genome-wide significance (P < 5 × 10−8) and excluding those in linkage disequilibrium (LD, r2 more than 0.8). The LocusZoom plot facilitated this optimization process using the HapMap Phase II JPT + CHB populations (hg19) as the reference panel [26]. Finally, the PRS-EA model was constructed using coefficients from the meta-analysis as weights.

Validation of the PRS-EA

Study design

The PRS-EA was validated by examining its association with the incidence and progression of NAFLD. A nested case-control study within the Kailuan cohort [27]a large-scale, prospective, community-based study initiated in 2006 in northern China, was conducted to explore the association between PRS-EA and NAFLD occurrence. A cohort study was conducted to explore the link between PRS-EA and NAFLD progression in the aforementioned cases of mild NAFLD.

Study population

As previously described [27]the Kailuan cohort (Chinese Clinical Trial Registry number: ChiCTR-TNRC-11001489) consisted of employees and retirees in the Kailuan Group Co., Ltd. who underwent biennial comprehensive health examinations at 11 hospitals of the Kailuan Medical Group, including Linxi hospital, since 2006.

In this nested case-control study, all cases and controls were obtained from the consecutive follow-up population (age ≥ 18 years) of the Linxi Hospital between 2017 and 2018. The following exclusion criteria were applied: (1) absence of ultrasound examination, (2) excessive alcohol consumption (≥ 30 g/day in men or ≥ 20 g/day in women) or missing data on alcohol consumption, (3) positive or missing hepatitis B surface antigen, (4) presence of cirrhosis or a history of malignancy, or (5) absence of blood samples.

The cases in the nested case-control study were selected from the aforementioned consecutive participants who were newly diagnosed with FLD (n = 528), in accordance with the Chinese guidelines for the prevention and treatment of NAFLD [28]. FLD was diagnosed by experienced radiologists using a 3.5-MHz transducer (HD-15, Philips, Bothell, WA, USA) and required the presence of at least two of the following ultrasound phenomena: bright liver, deep attenuation of the ultrasound signal, and vessel blurring. Subsequently, 528 individuals without FLD were selected as the controls. These controls were matched in a 1:1 ratio with cases based on the follow-up period, age (± 5 years), sex, and body mass index (BMI) (± 2 kg/m[2]).

We used a standardized scoring system to assess the degree of steatosis, considering the liver size, margins, echo intensity, and intrahepatic vessel clarity [29]. The scoring criteria were as follows: (a) liver size: normal (0), enlarged (1); (b) liver edge: sharp (0), blunt (1); (c) echogenicity: normal (0), increased (1), gradually decreasing in the far-field (2); and (d) intrahepatic vessels: clear (0) and blurred (1). Hepatic steatosis was categorized as mild, moderate, or severe, corresponding to scores ≤ 2, 3–4, and ≥ 5 points, respectively [29]. Then, we included 426 individuals with mild NAFLD in the aforementioned nested case-control study to examine the correlation between PRS-EA and NAFLD progression. NAFLD progression was defined as the advancement from mild to either moderate or severe steatosis, based on the scoring system described above [21].

Ethical considerations

The study was conducted by the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Ethics Committees of Kailuan General Hospital and the Institute of Basic Medical Sciences, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before participation.

Demographic characteristics and clinical data

Demographic characteristics (age and sex), lifestyle factors (smoking and drinking status), and medical and medication history were assessed using questionnaires. We defined smokers as smoking ≥ 1 cigarette/day for more than 6 months. Daily alcohol consumption was calculated by alcohol consumption (mL)/day × alcohol concentration (%) × 0.8, and then, we defined drinkers as those with average daily alcohol consumption > 0 g ethanol/day. Height, weight, waist, hip circumferences, and blood pressure were measured. Fasting blood glucose (FBG), triglycerides (TG), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-c), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-c), C-reactive protein (CRP), total cholesterol (TC), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), total bilirubin (TBIL), platelet count (PLT), and hepatitis B surface antigen were measured at the central laboratory. Diabetes was diagnosed as fasting glucose ≥ 7.0 mmol/L, current or prior use of hypoglycemic medication, or self-reported diabetes. Hypertension was defined as systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg, diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg, current or prior use of hypertension medication, or self-reported hypertension. Cut-off values for abnormality of TC, TG, and HDL levels were ≥ 3.2 mmol/L, ≥ 1.7 mmol/L, and ≥ 1 mmol/L[29], respectively.

Sample collection, DNA extraction, and genotyping

Whole blood samples were collected from all participants and stored at −80 °C. For this study, samples were thawed and genomic DNA was subsequently extracted using a magnetic bead-based method with the Whole Blood DNA Extraction Kit (Zhongke Bayer Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China).

All cases and controls were genotyped using the Illumina Infinium® Asian Screening Array (ASA) Multiple Disease chip. This whole-genome SNP array contains approximately 740,000 markers and was selected for its excellent coverage in East Asian populations. A quality control protocol was implemented to ensure the integrity and reliability of the data. Samples with a detection rate below 95%, abnormal heterozygosity exceeding six standard deviations, PI-HAT values between samples greater than 0.25, or deviations in principal component analysis (PCA) greater than four standard deviations were excluded from further analysis. The Genipe process utilized SHAPEIT software for haploid phasing to map each sample’s genotype accurately. All SNPs had Information content metric scores ≥ 0.8 and call rate ≥ 0.95.

Statistical analysis

The baseline characteristics of individuals with and without NAFLD were expressed as means (standard deviations) or medians (interquartile ranges) for continuous variables or numbers (%) for categorical variables. The differences were examined using the t-test, the Kruskal-Wallis H, or the chi-square test.

In our nested case-control study, we sought to investigate the association between PRS-EA and NAFLD development by initially exploring the dose-response relationship between PRS-EA and NAFLD. Restricted cubic spline models with five knots placed at the 15th, 30th, 50th, 70th, and 85th percentiles were employed. Participants with a PRS-EA score above 2.23, representing the top 15% of the participants, were categorized as the higher genetic risk group. Then, the following conditional logistic regression models were employed to estimate the effect of higher genetic risk: model 1, adjusted for age and BMI-adjusted; model 2, with additional adjustment for smoking and drinking status; model 3, with additional adjustment for elevated TG, elevated TC, elevated LDL-c, waist-to-hip ratio, hypertension, and diabetes. We also examined the combined effect of metabolic disorders and higher genetic risk on NAFLD risk.

For the analysis of NAFLD progression in the cohort of individuals with mild NAFLD, we generated Kaplan-Meier curves and employed log-rank tests to compare the progression rates between the lower and higher genetic risk groups. The Cox proportional risk regression analyses were used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% CIs. Stratified analyses assessed whether associations differed across subpopulations. Interaction tests were performed to evaluate effect modifications between higher genetic risk and metabolic factors, including TC, TG, HDL-c, LDL-c, WC (waist circumference), hypertension, and BMI. The model was adjusted for the same set of covariates as in Model 3, excluding the stratification variables.

All statistical analyses were performed using R 4.1.3. The meta-analysis for PRS-EA construction was conducted using the meta package. Survival analyses, including Kaplan-Meier curves (survfit), log-rank tests (survdiff), Cox models (coxph), and conditional logistic regression (clogit), were performed with the survival package. The rms package was used for generating restricted cubic splines, while descriptive statistics were handled with base R functions. Statistical significance was set at a two-sided alpha level of 0.05.

After excluding the SNPs (rs738409, rs12483959, rs2143571, rs2281135, rs2073080, rs3761472) which were in LD on chromosome 22 (Fig. S3), we selected the lead SNP rs2896019 along with rs3810622, rs58542926, and rs1260326 to construct PRS-EA as follows: PRS-EA = 0.60 × rs2896019 risk allele number + 0.53 × rs3810622 risk allele number + 0.46 × rs58542926 risk allele number + 0.29 × rs1260326 risk allele number.

Validation of PRS-EA

PRS-EA and NAFLD development

A total of 528 cases and 528 controls were included in a nested case-control study to explore the association between PRS-EA and NAFLD occurrence. As presented in Table 2, the distribution of sex, age, BMI, FBG, CRP levels, diabetes, and hypertension was comparable between the two groups. However, cases exhibited higher levels of waist-to-hip ratio, TC, and TG compared to controls. Although only PNPLA3 rs2896019 was significantly associated with NAFLD occurrence, the NAFLD group demonstrated a higher PRS score than the non-NAFLD group (P = 0.01).

PRS-EA and NAFLD progression

After a median follow-up of 6.12 years, 146 individuals with mild NAFLD advanced to moderate or severe NAFLD. Characteristics of mild NAFLD by genetic risk levels are presented in Table S5. The group with higher genetic risk exhibited a higher incidence density than the lower risk group (76.11 per 1000 person-years vs. 49.83 per 1000 person-years, P = 0.017, Fig. 2). The fully adjusted model revealed an HR of 1.50 (95% CI: 1.03–2.20; Table 3). The effect was more pronounced among individuals with hypertension (P for interaction = 0.015), whereas it was less evident among those with abnormal TC and TC, although these findings did not reach statistical significance, as detailed in Table 3.

Discussion

This study conducted a meta-analysis of GWAS on NAFLD within East Asian populations and developed a risk score model specific to East Asians (PRS-EA). The model was validated in a Northern Chinese cohort, where significantly positive associations were observed between PRS-EA score and the development and progression of NAFLD, independent of metabolic factors. Combined correlations were identified between metabolic dysfunction and higher PRS in the development of NAFLD. Furthermore, it was found that hypertension may modify the association between PRS-EA score and NAFLD progression.

A principal finding of our study is the distinct genetic architecture of the PRS-EA compared to established European-centric models like PRS-HFC and PRS-5[10, 21]. The PRS-HFC model incorporates four SNPs from four genes, PNPLA3 (rs738409), TM6SF2 (rs58542926), GCKR (rs1260326), and MBOAT7 (rs641738); while the PRS-5 model further integrates HSD17B13 (rs72613567). Consistent with these models, our PRS-EA model includes two identical SNPs, TM6SF2 (rs58542926) and GCKR (rs1260326). However, rs641738 in MBOAT7 was excluded due to its lack of significant association with NAFLD in East Asian populations [16–21]. Furthermore, within the PNPLA3 gene region, the lead SNP rs2896019 was selected over the conventionally used rs738409[10, 21] because our meta-analysis indicated that rs2896019 exhibited a stronger association with NAFLD (coefficient: 0.60 vs. 0.45) and greater statistical significance (P: 1.60E-53 vs. 3.29E-33) compared to rs738409 in East Asian populations. Additionally, another SNP of rs3810622 in PNPLA3 was incorporated into the model due to its unique LD characteristics in East Asian populations, as it resides in a different LD block relative to rs2896019. These selections highlight the ethnic specificity of the PRS construction, suggesting that the genetic determinants of NAFLD risk may vary significantly across different populations.

It is crucial to recognize that individual SNPs within the PRS-EA model influence NAFLD risk via distinct biological mechanisms. The PNPLA3 (rs2896019), which is in strong LD with the PNPLA3 I148M variant, primarily impairs TG hydrolysis in hepatic lipid droplets, leading to pronounced hepatic lipid accumulation without significantly altering systemic lipid concentrations [30]. In contrast, the TM6SF2 E167K (rs58542926) variant disrupts low-density lipoprotein cholesterol secretion, promoting lipid retention within hepatocytes while simultaneously reducing circulating lipid levels [31]. The GCKR P446L (rs1260326) variant enhances glucokinase activity, thereby increasing hepatic glucose utilization and potentially promoting lipogenesis [32]. The functional heterogeneity among these genetic variants may thus complicate the joint association between genetic risk and specific metabolic dysfunction. However, the combination of elevated PRS-EA and increased waist circumference, as well as high TG, was associated with a more substantial risk of NAFLD, underscoring the importance of managing TG and central obesity for those with higher PRS-EA.

Several biological mechanisms may explain the significant association between the higher PRS-EA score and the progression of hepatic steatosis in patients with NAFLD. For example, genetic variants in PNPLA3 may further impair hepatic lipid droplet breakdown, resulting in persistent and progressively worsening lipid retention within hepatocytes [30]. Concurrently, variants in TM6SF2 could intensify disease severity by disrupting hepatic lipid export via reduced VLDL-c secretion, leading to sustained intracellular lipid accumulation [31]. Additionally, GCKR variants exacerbate lipid synthesis through enhanced glycolysis and de novo lipogenesis pathways, progressively overwhelming hepatic lipid clearance mechanisms [32]. Collectively, these genetic mechanisms disrupt different aspects of hepatic lipid metabolism, resulting in lipid accumulation that exceeds hepatic clearance capacity and exacerbates existing steatosis, and then contributes to the NAFLD progression.

Hypertension was observed to amplify the effect of genetic risk for NAFLD progression. Literature supports a bidirectional association between hypertension and NAFLD. A meta-analysis by Li et al. (2022), including 11 studies with approximately 70,000 participants, demonstrated a 63% increased risk of NAFLD among individuals with hypertension (pooled HR = 1.63, 95% CI: 1.41–1.88) and a 55% increased risk of hypertension among patients with NAFLD (HR = 1.55, 95% CI: 1.29–1.87) [33]. This reciprocal relationship may be attributed to hypertension-induced insulin resistance, chronic inflammation, and activation of the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system, which exacerbate hepatic lipid accumulation and fibrosis [34]. Conversely, fatty liver disease may trigger systemic metabolic dysregulation and impair cardiovascular function, creating a detrimental feedback loop [34]. This suggests that aggressive blood pressure control in patients with a high PRS-EA may be a crucial strategy to mitigate the risk of NAFLD progression.

In comparison to individuals with hypertension, genetic influences on NAFLD progression appeared more pronounced in individuals with normal lipid profiles (TC and TG), although this interaction did not reach statistical significance. These findings suggest that genetic variants in PNPLA3, TM6SF2, and GCKR may have more direct and discernible effects on hepatic lipid synthesis, transport, and degradation processes in populations without evident metabolic disturbances, thereby further emphasizing their role in liver fat deposition.

This study possesses several strengths. First, the utilization of meta-GWAS methodology to derive coefficient values for the PRS model enhances the robustness of risk estimates by mitigating the influence of biases specific to individual studies. Second, prior research has often been constrained by the use of the International Classification of Diseases (ICD) code, fibrosis score, or fatty liver index as proxies for NAFLD and its various stages [10, 21]. These surrogate markers are limited in accuracy, frequently resulting in over- or under-diagnosis of FLD [35]. In contrast, our study employed ultrasound to define FLD, leading to a more reliable diagnosis.

There are, however, some limitations to this study. Firstly, NAFLD was recently renamed metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) by expert consensus in 2023 [36]. Nevertheless, our study was conceptualized and executed under the NAFLD nomenclature. Given that the vast majority of individuals diagnosed with NAFLD also satisfy the criteria for MASLD [37, 38]the findings retain their relevance under the updated classification. Secondly, the limited availability of GWAS data for the East Asian population necessitated the inclusion of only published significant loci, potentially leading to the exclusion of certain genetic risk signals.,

Thirdly, FLD was diagnosed using ultrasonography in our validation population. NAFLD may be misclassified as non-NAFLD when the liver hepatic fat content is < 20%[39], which may underestimate the association between PRS-EA and NAFLD. Fourthly, we did not conduct stratification analysis according to sex due to the insufficient number of female participants in our study. Hormonal and metabolic differences between sexes may modulate the penetrance of genetic risk variants [40, 41]. For instance, the PNPLA3 I148M variant may exert a more pronounced deleterious effect in women, suggesting that a single, sex-agnostic PRS might exhibit differential accuracy across sexes [40]. Further development of sex-specific PRS is needed to clarify the sex-specific genetic association for NAFLD. Additionally, our study cohort from Northern China may not be fully representative of all of China and East Asians, further underscoring the need for region-specific investigations. Future research should further extend this work to other East Asian populations, increase the sample size, acquire more comprehensive genetic risk data, and employ more accurate diagnostic tools, such as magnetic resonance imaging, to enhance the accuracy and reliability of the study results.

In conclusion, this study developed a PRS model tailored to East Asia populations and validated it within a Northern Chinese cohort. The findings provide important evidence supporting the application of genetic risk scoring in predicting and stratifying the risk of NAFLD, particularly among individuals with lipid metabolic dysfunction and hypertension.
