Anti-plasma cell therapy in patients with monoclonal gammopathy-associated refractory pure red cell aplasia: A report of three cases and literature review
Authors: Li Huang, Meng Li, Ting Niu, Hong Chang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAcquired pure red cell aplasia (aPRCA) is a clinical syndrome that may be secondary to a diverse array of diseases. It is rarely secondary to monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance (MGUS). Currently, there is no consensus on when to initiate anti-plasma cell therapy. This study reports three cases of patients with MGUS-associated aPRCA who responded to anti-plasma cell therapy after failing conventional immunosuppressive treatment. Patient 1 achieved complete remission after treatment with the BD (bortezomib and dexamethasone) and BRD (bortezomib, lenalidomide, and dexamethasone) regimens. She subsequently received lenalidomide as maintenance treatment. She has remained in treatment-free survival (TFS) for 22 months to date. Patient 2 achieved complete remission after treatment with the BRD regimen and was maintained with lenalidomide. She has maintained TFS for 16 months so far. Patient 3 was treated with the BD regimen and became transfusion-independent, but relapsed after treatment discontinuation due to COVID-19 infection.This study suggests that anti-plasma cell therapy may be an effective strategy for aPRCA patients unresponsive to conventional immunosuppressive therapy and exhibiting monoclonal protein (M-protein). Introduction

Acquired pure red cell aplasia (aPRCA) is a syndrome mainly characterized by a decrease in reticulocytes and a significant reduction or absence of erythroid precursor cells in the bone marrow [1]. Secondary aPRCA is often secondary to diseases, poisons, or drugs, and has a complex pathogenesis, largely related to immune mechanisms [1]. Monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance (MGUS) is a common plasma cell disorder, which rarely leads to aPRCA, with only a few reported cases [2]. It is generally believed that patients with MGUS do not require treatment, as treatment may be potentially harmful. To date, there is no consensus on the intervention for patients with MGUS-associated aPRCA. Therefore, more evidence is needed to address this dilemma.

Herein, we report three cases of patients with MGUS-associated aPRCA. Monoclonal plasma cells were identified in all three patients via bone marrow flow cytometry. Imaging studies (X-ray and MRI) revealed no evidence of bone lesions. Due to the absence of bone marrow infiltration on biopsy, immunohistochemical analysis was denied in all three cases on patient preference. Following short-term anti-plasma cell treatment, the hemoglobin levels of the three patients were all improved (Table 1). Among them, two patients achieved complete remission (CR) and have remained off medication, under observation for more than 18 months (Fig. 1). Case presentation

Case 1

A 54-year-old woman had a 5-year history of refractory anaemia. At the onset of the disease, her hemoglobin level was 41 g/L. She had undergone surgery, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy for cervical cancer 10 years prior. Initial treatment with cyclosporine, prednisone, and subsequently sirolimus for 1 year failed to relieve her transfusion dependence. Subsequently, she was treated with the BD regimen (bortezomib and dexamethasone) for three cycles, the frequency of transfusions decreased. The interval between transfusions extended from every 1–2 weeks to approximately once per month, with hemoglobin levels stabilizing around 80 g/L.However, intermittent transfusions were still required, and the patient continued to experience symptoms of anemia such as dizziness and fatigue. Follow-up testing revealed a persistent monoclonal protein (M-protein) level of 4.3 g/L. Due to blood shortages during the COVID-19 pandemic, timely transfusions could not always be ensured when needed. Thereafter, she received the BRD regimen (bortezomib, lenalidomide, and dexamethasone) for 4 cycles. Notably, the monoclonal protein level gradually declined from 18.6 g/L to undetectable, accompanied by recovery in both hemoglobin and reticulocyte levels, ultimately resulting in eliminating the need for blood transfusions. Since then, she has been receiving maintenance therapy with lenalidomide at a daily dose of 10 mg. At present, she has maintained a treatment-free survival (TFS) period of 23 months.

Case 2

A 58-year-old woman presented with fatigue three years ago and was found to have a hemoglobin level of 44 g/L on evaluation. She was diagnosed with MGUS-associated aPRCA, accompanied by large granular lymphocyte leukemia (LGLL). Initial treatment with cyclosporine and sirolimus over a six-month period proved ineffective. However, the hemoglobin levels remained low (61 g/L). The patient continued to exhibit significant symptoms of anemia, including dizziness and fatigue, and remained transfusion-dependent. Bone marrow flow cytometry revealed 0.5% clonal plasma cells. Due to the COVID-19 pandemic in China at the time, which limited the availability of transfusions, treatment with the BRD regimen (bortezomib, lenalidomide, and dexamethasone) was initiated. She received 2 cycles of the BRD regimen. As a result, she achieved transfusion independence, and her hemoglobin level increased to 72 g/L. Thereafter, five cycles of the BRD regimen were administered, leading to a further increase in hemoglobin to 105 g/L, and a negative M-protein test. She was maintained on lenalidomide for only half a year. Subsequently, her hemoglobin returned to normal, and the drug was discontinued for observation. Currently, she has maintained TFS for 16 months.

Case 3

A 69-year-old man was diagnosed with aPRCA 4 years ago. He underwent treatment with cyclosporine for one and a half years and sirolimus for four months, but remained dependent on blood transfusions. Subsequently, an M protein of IgA κ was detected at a level of 6.8%, and then the BD regimen was initiated. After one cycle of treatment, he achieved transfusion independence. After two cycles of treatment, the hemoglobin level increased from 54 g/L to 89 g/L. However, after three cycles of treatment, he discontinued bortezomib due to a COVID-19 infection, which led to disease relapse and a return to transfusion dependence.

Discussion

Secondary aPRCA is a clinical condition characterized by anemia and a marked reduction in the erythroid precursors. Further examinations to clarify the etiological factors are of great significance for the clinical diagnosis and treatment of aPRCA [4]. Currently, cyclosporine A is considered the first-line treatment, with reported efficacy rates ranging from 65–87% [5]. However, immunosuppressive therapy may fail to achieve the desired response in some cases. One potential factor that could contribute to the ineffectiveness of immunosuppressive therapy is the presence of monoclonal protein. The pathogenesis of aPRCA in patients with plasma cell disorders is thought to be that abnormal immunoglobulins produced by myeloma cells inhibit the generation of erythrocyte colonies, resulting in the reduction of colony-forming unit-erythroid (CFU-E) and burst-forming unit-erythroid (BFU-E) [6]. Bortezomib and lenalidomide have been successfully used in cases of multiple myeloma-associated aPRCA, offering new therapeutic options and methodological insights for plasma cell-related PRCA [7]. However, for MGUS, its disease progression tends to be insidious and complicated. Currently, there is no consensus on whether anti-plasma cell therapy should be initiated in MGUS-associated PRCA, mainly due to concerns about adverse drug reactions and excessive immunosuppression. Meanwhile, potential benefits in terms of disease control also exist, and the risk-benefit balance requires further investigation. In a retrospective study of 51 patients with aPRCA, 12 cases (24.0%, 12/51) were found to have M-protein, including 10 with multiple myeloma and 2 with MGUS. In three patients who failed conventional immunosuppressive therapy, anti-plasma cell therapy was initiated, resulting in transfusion independence. One patient received the BD regimen followed by maintenance therapy using lenalidomide; another received the BRD regimen followed by lenalidomide maintenance; and the third was treated with the RD regimen (lenalidomide combined with dexamethasone) followed by lenalidomide maintenance [2]. In another report, two patients with MGUS-associated PRCA achieved rapid remission after receiving 10 to 12 doses of bortezomib and dexamethasone, maintaining treatment-free survival for more than 8 months [8]. In addition, agents such as isatuximab, pomalidomide and daratumumab have been reported in refractory M protein-associated aPRCA [9, 10]. These findings suggest that in selected patients with aPRCA, particularly those with M protein and poor response to immunosuppressive therapy, anti-plasma cell therapy may represent an effective alternative strategy. However, further studies are needed to confirm its efficacy and safety. In our case series, two patients achieved complete remission following seven cycles of anti-plasma cell treatment and lenalidomide maintenance and have remained off treatment without recurrence. The third patient received only three cycles of anti-plasma cell treatment and became transfusions-independent. However, the disease relapsed after discontinuation of bortezomib, despite clearance of M protein. Therefore, in refractory PRCA, the course of anti-plasma cell treatment and the duration of maintenance treatment remain to be considered. These commonalities and differences provide valuable insights into understanding the treatment patterns and prognosis of similar patients.

In summary, our findings suggest that anti-plasma cell therapy may provide clinical benefit in MGUS-associated refractory PRCA to conventional immunosuppression. However, given the limitations of retrospective and small-sample data, treatment decisions should be individualized, and therapy duration requires optimization in future studies.
