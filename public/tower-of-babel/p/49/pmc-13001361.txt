Amitraz poisoning mimicking organophosphate toxicity in a young woman: a clinically diagnosed case report from Western India
Authors: Setu Patel, Bharti Chaudhari, Jay Nagda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAmitraz is a formamidine insecticide and acaricide widely used in veterinary and agricultural settings. Human poisoning with amitraz is uncommon but potentially serious, primarily due to its alpha-2 adrenergic agonist activity, monoamine oxidase inhibition, and prostaglandin synthesis inhibition. It is frequently misdiagnosed as organophosphate poisoning, leading to inappropriate management.Case presentationWe present the case of a 26-year-old female from Himmatnagar, Gujarat, India, who ingested an amitraz formulation (12.5% w/v in xylene) with suicidal intent following a family conflict. The diagnosis was clinically established based on the characteristic toxidrome and identification of the commercial amitraz bottle by relatives. She presented to the emergency room within 30 min of ingestion with progressive drowsiness (Glasgow Coma Scale 11, declining to 6). Her clinical course was marked by central nervous system depression, miosis, transient bradycardia (treated with atropine), mild metabolic acidosis, and transient hyperglycemia (peak random blood glucose 225 mg/dL at 3 h, normalizing spontaneously). No significant respiratory depression or persistent hypotension was observed. She was managed with gastric lavage, activated charcoal, and supportive care in the intensive care unit. The patient regained full consciousness within 8 h and was discharged on the fourth day without sequelae following psychiatric evaluation.ConclusionsAmitraz poisoning, though underrecognized, can present with a clinical picture closely mimicking organophosphate toxicity. This case underscores the importance of early identification of amitraz as the causative agent, distinguishing it from organophosphate poisoning, and initiating prompt supportive management. The prognosis is generally favorable. Emergency physicians, particularly in rural and semi-urban India where veterinary insecticides are readily accessible over the counter, should maintain a high index of suspicion for amitraz toxicity in cases of unexplained CNS depression with miosis and bradycardia.Keywords: Amitraz, Poisoning, Formamidines, Adrenergic alpha-2 Receptor Agonists, Central Nervous System Depressants, Insecticides, Organophosphate Poisoning, Bradycardia, Miosis, India

Introduction

Amitraz (1,5-di-(2,4-dimethylphenyl)-3-methyl-1,3,5-triazapenta-1,4-diene) is a broad-spectrum insecticide and acaricide belonging to the formamidine family, first synthesized in 1969 and commercially available since 1974 under trade names including RIDD® in India [1–3]. Its primary mechanism of toxicity involves stimulation of central and peripheral alpha-2 adrenergic receptors, producing a clinical syndrome closely resembling clonidine overdose, characterized by central nervous system (CNS) depression, bradycardia, hypotension, miosis, and hyperglycemia [1, 4, 5]. Additionally, amitraz inhibits monoamine oxidase activity and suppresses prostaglandin E₂ synthesis [1, 6]. The organic solvent xylene, used as a vehicle in most commercial formulations (12.5–50% amitraz in xylene), contributes additional toxicity [1, 7, 8].

Despite its widespread availability, human amitraz poisoning remains underrecognized [1, 9]. A landmark systematic review by Dhooria and Agarwal (2016) identified only 310 cases across 32 studies, with a case fatality rate of 1.9% [1]. More recently, Jalawadi et al. (2024) documented 76 cases from southern India over a decade, with 97.4% from rural areas [10]. Amitraz is frequently misdiagnosed as organophosphate (OP) poisoning due to overlapping features such as miosis, bradycardia, and CNS depression, leading to inappropriate administration of pralidoxime and atropine infusions [1, 3, 11].

In India, amitraz is classified as a veterinary drug under the purview of the Central Drugs Standard Control Organisation (CDSCO). However, regulatory enforcement is inconsistent, and amitraz-based ectoparasiticides are widely available over-the-counter through veterinary pharmacies, agricultural supply shops, and online marketplaces without mandatory veterinary prescription [10, 12]. This ease of access, coupled with low clinician awareness, contributes to the public health burden of amitraz poisoning, particularly in rural and semi-urban settings. Notably, amitraz is not the only underrecognized pesticide in India; agents such as paraquat also pose significant diagnostic and therapeutic challenges due to limited clinical familiarity [13, 14].

We report a case of intentional amitraz ingestion in a young woman from Gujarat, western India, who presented with the classical amitraz toxidrome and recovered fully with supportive care. This report highlights the clinical features, diagnostic considerations, the controversy surrounding atropine use, and management of this underrecognized poisoning.

Case presentation

A 26-year-old female from Himmatnagar, Gujarat, India, was brought to the emergency department of Fatema Hospital, Himmatnagar, by her relatives approximately 30 min after acute ingestion of an ectoparasitic insecticide. The ingestion was reportedly intentional, precipitated by a family conflict. The relatives brought the bottle of the ingested substance, which was identified as a commercially available amitraz formulation (RIDD®, 12.5% w/v in xylene solvent). Based on the history from relatives and the characteristic clinical toxidrome that subsequently developed, the case was clinically diagnosed as amitraz poisoning. The patient reportedly ingested the formulation directly from the bottle without dilution. The estimated quantity ingested could not be precisely determined. On specific inquiry, the relatives denied administration of any home remedies, emetics, milk, or other substances prior to hospital presentation. The patient was initially conscious at home but became progressively drowsy during transport. There was no history of co-ingestion of any other substance or alcohol.

On arrival in the emergency room, the patient was lethargic but arousable. Her Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score was 11 (E3V3M5). Vital signs were: blood pressure (BP) 110/70 mmHg, pulse rate 88 beats per minute (regular), respiratory rate 16 breaths per minute, and oxygen saturation (SpO₂) 98% on room air. She was afebrile. Cardiovascular and respiratory examinations were unremarkable. Abdominal examination showed a soft, non-tender abdomen. Neurological examination revealed bilaterally constricted pupils (miosis) that were sluggishly reactive to light. Importantly, there were no fasciculations, excessive salivation, lacrimation, or diaphoresis—features that would suggest organophosphate or carbamate poisoning. The patient had no history of vomiting prior to presentation. Initial electrocardiogram (ECG) showed normal sinus rhythm. Random blood glucose (RBG) at presentation was 120 mg/dL. Arterial blood gas analysis at admission revealed a mild metabolic acidosis (pH 7.318, HCO₃⁻ 21 mmol/L, pCO₂ 38 mmHg, pO₂ 104 mmHg).

The patient was immediately shifted to the intensive care unit (ICU). A nasogastric tube was inserted, and gastric lavage was performed using approximately 2 L of normal saline. The aspirated fluid appeared creamy white, consistent with the xylene-based formulation. The gastric aspirate was not sent for toxicological analysis, which is acknowledged as a limitation. Following lavage, activated charcoal (50 g in aqueous suspension) was administered via the nasogastric tube.

Over the ensuing hours, the patient’s level of consciousness progressively deteriorated. The GCS declined to 6 (E1V2M3) at approximately 2–3 h post-admission. However, no clinically significant respiratory depression was observed; the respiratory rate remained above 14 breaths per minute, and SpO₂ stayed above 95% on room air, negating the need for mechanical ventilation. Blood pressure remained hemodynamically stable throughout. A single episode of bradycardia (pulse rate 56/min) was noted at approximately 3 h post-admission, occurring concurrently with maximal CNS depression (GCS 6). Given the symptomatic context (deep CNS depression with bradycardia), 1 mg of intravenous atropine was administered, following which the pulse rate recovered and stabilized at approximately 90 beats per minute. Importantly, the miosis persisted despite atropine administration and resolved gradually only as the overall clinical picture improved, consistent with the alpha-2 adrenergic (rather than cholinergic) mechanism of amitraz-induced miosis.

Gradual improvement in consciousness was noted approximately 8 h after admission. The patient became oriented to time, place, and person, and her GCS improved to 14. As there is no specific antidote for amitraz poisoning, supportive care was continued, including intravenous fluids (normal saline), pantoprazole (40 mg intravenous once daily) for stress ulcer prophylaxis, and ondansetron (4 mg intravenous as needed) for nausea. Continuous monitoring was maintained for hypotension, bradycardia, respiratory depression, and hyperglycemia.

By the following morning (approximately 18 h post-ingestion), her GCS had improved to 15, vital signs were stable, RBG was 112 mg/dL, and she was hemodynamically normal. Pupils had returned to normal size and reactivity. Urine output over 24 h was 1,800 mL (adequate). The nasogastric tube was removed, and the patient was started on a liquid diet approximately 30 h post-ingestion, which was well tolerated. She was transferred to the general ward on Day 2 of hospitalization.

A formal psychiatric evaluation was conducted on Day 3 of hospitalization. The psychiatrist performed a structured risk assessment, which categorized the patient as moderate risk given the impulsive nature of the act (precipitated by acute family conflict) with no prior history of self-harm, no ongoing suicidal ideation at the time of assessment, and adequate social support. No psychotropic medication was initiated. The patient was counseled on coping strategies and stress management, and was referred for outpatient psychiatric follow-up at the nearest Community Health Centre. A safety plan was discussed with the patient and her family prior to discharge. The patient was subsequently discharged on Day 4 in stable condition without any residual neurological deficits.

Discussion

Amitraz poisoning in humans, though historically underrecognized, is emerging as an important cause of insecticide poisoning, particularly in rural India where veterinary products are readily accessible without prescription [1, 3, 10]. The present case illustrates the classical toxidrome of amitraz poisoning—CNS depression, miosis, transient bradycardia, mild metabolic acidosis, and transient hyperglycemia—and demonstrates the favorable prognosis achievable with early recognition and supportive care. This report contributes geographic novelty, being among the first documented cases from western India (Gujarat), and provides detailed temporal clinical documentation including serial blood glucose trends and GCS trajectory, which are often lacking in the published literature.

The toxicity of amitraz in mammals is primarily mediated through three pharmacological mechanisms: (i) alpha-2 adrenergic receptor agonism, (ii) monoamine oxidase inhibition, and (iii) inhibition of prostaglandin E₂ synthesis [1, 4, 6]. The alpha-2 adrenergic agonist activity is the principal driver of clinical manifestations. Stimulation of presynaptic alpha-2 receptors in the CNS reduces sympathetic outflow, resulting in sedation, hypotension, and bradycardia—a mechanism analogous to clonidine toxicity [4, 5]. The hyperglycemia results from alpha-2 receptor–mediated suppression of insulin secretion from pancreatic beta cells [15]. The organic solvent xylene contributes additional toxicity including CNS depression, mucosal irritation, and, rarely, aspiration pneumonitis [7, 8]. Bonsall and Turnbull (1983) noted that xylene may be the more aggressive component, particularly via the oral route [8].

Clinically, amitraz poisoning fits within the “miosis–bradycardia–CNS depression” toxidrome spectrum, which is shared by three broad categories of poisoning: cholinergic (organophosphate/carbamate), opioid, and sedative-hypnotic [1, 11, 16]. Differentiating between these entities requires careful clinical assessment. The hallmark cholinergic features of OP poisoning—fasciculations, excessive salivation, lacrimation, urination, defecation, and diaphoresis (the “SLUDGE” toxidrome)—are characteristically absent in amitraz poisoning [1, 11]. Furthermore, the presence of hyperglycemia and hypothermia, along with the absence of a hypersecretory state, should raise suspicion for amitraz toxicity [3, 11]. The smell of the ingested substance may provide a clue: a solvent or mothball-like odor suggests amitraz (due to xylene), whereas a garlicky odor points toward organophosphates [11]. In the absence of specific toxicological assays such as HPLC for amitraz metabolites (BTS 27271), diagnosis in resource-limited settings relies primarily on clinical recognition of the toxidrome, corroborative history from relatives, and identification of the commercial product [1, 17]. Measurement of serum or erythrocyte cholinesterase levels, which are depressed in OP poisoning but remain normal in amitraz poisoning, provides essential biochemical differentiation and should be performed as a minimum diagnostic workup even in resource-limited settings [1, 3, 11]. In the present case, the absence of cholinergic features, the identification of the amitraz bottle (RIDD®) by relatives, and the characteristic clinical evolution collectively supported the clinical diagnosis.

Our patient demonstrated the two hallmark features of amitraz toxicity—CNS depression and miosis—without major respiratory compromise or persistent hypotension. This relatively milder clinical course, compared to the severe presentation described by Ilyas et al. (2024) where the patient required mechanical ventilation and had a GCS of 3 [18], may reflect differences in the quantity ingested, formulation concentration, and the rapidity of gastric decontamination. Notably, our patient presented within 30 min of ingestion, allowing for early gastric lavage, which may have significantly limited systemic absorption [1, 19].

The use of atropine in amitraz poisoning warrants careful discussion given the pharmacological complexity involved. Atropine is a muscarinic (cholinergic) antagonist, classically indicated for vagally mediated bradycardia and organophosphate poisoning. Amitraz-induced bradycardia, in contrast, is primarily mediated through alpha-2 adrenergic agonism, which reduces central sympathetic outflow [1, 4]. However, alpha-2 adrenergic agonists can also cause bradycardia partly via stimulation of the dorsal motor nucleus of the vagal nerve, providing a vagal component that may be amenable to atropine [20, 21]. Hsu and colleagues demonstrated in animal models that atropine increased heart rate and prevented amitraz-induced bradycardia [21]. Multiple published human case reports have reported apparent efficacy of atropine for amitraz-induced bradycardia [3, 9, 18, 20]. However, the use of atropine remains controversial, as some veterinary toxicology references advise against it [22], and it remains unclear whether the observed improvement represents a true drug effect or the natural self-limiting course of amitraz toxicity.

In our patient, atropine was administered because the bradycardia (HR 56/min) occurred in the context of concurrent deep CNS depression (GCS 6), and the treating physician judged this to represent symptomatic bradycardia warranting intervention. The prompt response (HR recovery to ~ 90/min) suggests a drug-related effect, though spontaneous resolution cannot be excluded. Importantly, the miosis persisted despite atropine, confirming that amitraz-induced miosis is centrally mediated via alpha-2 adrenergic pathways and is not responsive to muscarinic blockade. We emphasize that asymptomatic or hemodynamically stable bradycardia in amitraz poisoning does not warrant atropine therapy; the decision to treat should be individualized based on clinical context, including the degree of hemodynamic compromise and concurrent CNS depression.

There is no specific antidote for amitraz poisoning, and management remains entirely supportive [1, 2, 9]. The alpha-2 adrenergic antagonists yohimbine and atipamezole have shown efficacy in animal models but are not recommended for human use [1, 4]. Gastric decontamination with lavage and activated charcoal is advisable when the patient presents early, though evidence for improved outcomes remains limited [1]. Given the intentional nature of ingestion, psychiatric evaluation was obtained prior to discharge, consistent with WHO recommendations for all cases of deliberate self-poisoning [23].

The present case also highlights the broader issue of underrecognized insecticidal poisonings in India. Beyond amitraz, several other agents with high toxicity but limited clinician familiarity contribute to diagnostic delays and suboptimal management. Paraquat, a bipyridyl herbicide, is a notable example: it is widely available in parts of India, causes irreversible pulmonary fibrosis with high mortality, and is frequently misidentified or inadequately managed due to limited clinical experience [13, 14]. Similarly, agents such as aluminum phosphide and newer-generation pesticides continue to challenge emergency physicians. Enhancing toxicological education and establishing regional poison information centres are critical steps toward improving outcomes for these underrecognized poisonings.

Amitraz poisoning generally carries a favorable prognosis with appropriate supportive care, with a case fatality rate of 1.9% in the systematic review [1]. Recovery typically occurs within 12–48 h [1, 10]. Our patient demonstrated significant improvement within 8 h and full recovery by 18 h, likely attributable to early presentation, prompt gastric decontamination, and moderate poisoning severity.

This report has certain limitations. First, the exact quantity of amitraz ingested could not be determined, precluding dose–response analysis. Second, serum cholinesterase levels were not measured; although the clinical picture and bottle identification made OP poisoning unlikely, cholinesterase measurement would have provided important biochemical confirmation and should be performed as a minimum workup in resource-limited settings. Third, specific toxicological confirmation (e.g., HPLC for amitraz metabolites in urine or blood) was not available at our centre. The gastric aspirate was not referred to a Forensic Science Laboratory (FSL), which could have provided additional confirmation; we recommend that future cases consider FSL referral through medicolegal channels. Fourth, HbA1c was not measured, which would have helped exclude pre-existing diabetes mellitus and strengthened the attribution of hyperglycemia to amitraz toxicity. Fifth, clinical photographs of the pupils were not obtained during the acute phase. Sixth, being a single case report, the findings cannot be generalized. Nevertheless, given the paucity of published literature from western India, this case contributes meaningfully to the clinical understanding of amitraz poisoning.

Conclusions

Amitraz poisoning, though uncommon and underrecognized, carries a generally favorable prognosis with timely supportive care. Clinician awareness remains poor, and the condition is frequently misdiagnosed as organophosphate poisoning due to the overlapping toxidrome of miosis, bradycardia, and CNS depression; the absence of cholinergic features (SLUDGE) and the presence of hyperglycemia should prompt consideration of amitraz toxicity. Atropine may be considered for symptomatic bradycardia, but should not be used routinely for asymptomatic bradycardia, and its effect on amitraz-induced miosis is negligible. The xylene co-solvent in commercial formulations contributes additional CNS depression and mucosal toxicity, and management should encompass xylene ingestion as well. Emergency physicians, particularly in rural and semi-urban India where veterinary insecticides are available without prescription, should maintain a high index of suspicion for this treatable poisoning.
