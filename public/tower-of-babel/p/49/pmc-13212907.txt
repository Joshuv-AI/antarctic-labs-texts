A generic compact model for resistive memories and switches
Authors: Sangwoo Jung, Hyoseob Kim, Jiseon Park, Sungjun Kim, Min-Hwi Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Most previous studies have modeled resistive switching based on physical principles. While physics-driven models provide valuable physical insight, their complexity and device specificity limit scalability and often result in high computational costs in large-scale circuit simulations. To address these limitations, we propose a generic compact modeling methodology in LTspice. This generalized approach constructs compact models for diverse resistive memory technologies using a minimal set of common circuit elements. It integrates nonlinear resistors, capacitors, and switches to reproduce key switching phenomena—including filament formation, polarization-modulated tunneling, and spin-dependent resistance—thereby capturing the essential behaviors of RRAM, TS, FTJ, and MRAM. This generalized framework enables flexible parameter tuning to reflect each device’s distinct switching dynamics. Validation using experimental and reference data confirms that the resulting models accurately reproduce the key switching characteristics across all four device classes. Monte Carlo simulations further demonstrate the framework’s ability to capture variability arising from parameter fluctuations and cycling effects. The proposed framework scales seamlessly from single-device operation to array-level simulation, enabling performance evaluation of SNN- and BNN-based inference systems and supporting accurate estimation of power consumption. Collectively, these results demonstrate the framework’s broad applicability and scalability for large-scale neuromorphic and in-memory computing architectures.

Introduction

The rapid advancement of artificial intelligence and data-driven applications has intensified the demand for scalable and energy-efficient computing architectures. Current transistor-based technologies are facing severe efficiency limits, with the global electricity consumption of information technology projected to reach up to 21% by 20301. To address this impending energy crisis and the scaling limitations, architectures that directly process data within memory—such as in-memory computing (IMC) and neuromorphic systems—are emerging as a promising path to overcome the von Neumann bottleneck2,3.

Emerging memory technologies based on memristive devices are considered key enablers for these paradigms due to their non-volatile storage capability and resistive switching behavior. These properties offer a practical route toward realizing energy-efficient, large-scale computing systems, a potential that is further validated by their growing adoption in industrial embedded applications and cost-competitiveness compared to traditional Flash memories.

Among these technologies, devices such as resistive random-access memory (RRAM), threshold switching (TS) selectors, ferroelectric tunnel junctions (FTJ), and magnetic random-access memory (MRAM) exhibit distinct switching mechanisms suited for non-volatile storage, dynamic selection, and in-memory processing. The unique electrical characteristics and underlying physical mechanisms shown in Fig. 1 have established these devices as promising candidates for diverse next-generation computing platforms. Moreover, their technological readiness is notably advancing, with successful integration into commercial process nodes signaling that they are moving beyond theoretical research into practical implementation4–7.

However, these fundamental operational differences complicate the development of a generalized circuit-level model. Most existing compact models are device-specific or rely heavily on complex physical formulations. This fragmentation increases structural complexity and significantly limits their use in large-scale circuit simulations, thereby hindering reliable exploration of system-level performance and power efficiency8–10.

Recent studies have highlighted these challenges, emphasizing the need for a simplified yet broadly applicable modeling framework. To address these challenges, we propose a generic compact modeling methodology in LTspice. This generalized approach uses a minimal set of circuit components to construct distinct compact models for various resistive memory devices. These models (comprising nonlinear resistors, capacitors, and switches) emulate switching dynamics without detailed physical modeling. This methodology achieves both computational efficiency and broad applicability, accurately reproducing key behaviors such as resistance transitions, nonlinear current–voltage characteristics, and threshold-driven switching dynamics11.

This common framework scales seamlessly from individual device modeling to array-level simulations and supports practical design and analysis at the system level. The methodology was validated comprehensively at both the device and array levels. The resulting models accurately reproduced the key switching characteristics of all four device types against experimental and reference data. Monte Carlo analysis further confirmed the framework’s ability to capture variability arising from fabrication-induced fluctuations and operational noise. Building on this validation, its system-level capability was demonstrated through array-level simulations of SNN- and BNN-based inference systems, enabling accurate evaluation of operational performance and power consumption12.

Methods

A generic compact model for RRAM, TS, FTJ, and MRAM was developed in LTspice using manually constructed circuits composed of nonlinear resistors, capacitors, and switches. Device-level validation was performed by comparing simulation results against DC and transient switching characteristics. Detailed procedures for parameter extraction and model adaptation are provided in Supplementary Note 1. For fabricated RRAM (TiN/Hf/HfO\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_x$$\end{document}/Pt/Ti stack) and TS (W/ZnTe/W) devices, these characteristics were experimentally measured at \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$25\;^{\circ }$$\end{document}C using a Keysight B1500A (HRSMU/WGFMU units). An overview of the manufacturing process and the stack structure of the fabricated memristive devices used for experimental validation is provided in Supplementary Fig. S1. The FTJ and MRAM models were validated against published reference data from Li et al. and Kim et al., respectively. Stochastic behavior was examined via Monte Carlo analysis, using variability parameters extracted from experimental cycle-to-cycle measurements of the fabricated RRAM and TS devices. System-level applicability was verified using array-level LTspice implementations. A co-simulation methodology was employed for SNN inference (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$784 \times 24 \times 10$$\end{document}), where the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$784 \times 24$$\end{document} first layer was simulated in software. Additionally, a BNN convolution system was simulated using an MRAM-based XNOR array to evaluate computational accuracy and power efficiency.

Results and discussion

Device-level simulation

Our device-level analysis covers the four representative memory technologies: RRAM, TS devices, FTJ, and spin-transfer torque MRAM (STT-MRAM). For each technology, a distinct compact model was constructed using the proposed methodology, capturing the core physical switching mechanism with a minimal set of circuit components. A detailed summary of the circuit components and parameters for each device is provided in the Supplementary Table S1.

Resistive random access memory modeling

RRAM exhibits binary switching behavior, alternating between High-Resistance State (HRS) and Low-Resistance State (LRS) under applied voltage bias. This transition originates from the formation and rupture of conductive filaments driven by oxygen ion migration within the HfO\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_x$$\end{document} layer. Under positive bias, oxygen vacancies drift toward the bottom electrode, leading to filament formation and transition to the LRS. In contrast, a negative bias induces localized Joule heating and reoxidation, disrupting the filament and restoring the insulating gap, thereby returning the device to the HRS. Both thermal and field-driven processes govern this bipolar switching mechanism, as shown in Fig. 2a13–15.

Our compact circuit, shown in Fig. 2b, models this filament-based switching mechanism. The circuit consists of a non-linear resistor combined with a series of voltage-controlled switches (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$S_1$$\end{document} to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$S_n$$\end{document}). As the applied voltage exceeds predefined threshold values, these switches are sequentially activated, replicating the progressive growth of the conductive filament. The electrical characteristics of these switches are governed by a voltage-controlled threshold logic defined by two key parameters: the threshold voltage (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$V_t$$\end{document}) and the holding voltage (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$V_h$$\end{document}), as listed in Supplementary Table S1. Specifically, the switch transitions from the High Resistance State (HRS, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_{\text {off}}$$\end{document}) to the Low Resistance State (LRS, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_{\text {on}}$$\end{document}) when the applied voltage exceeds the upper threshold sum (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$V > V_t + V_h$$\end{document}). Conversely, the device reverts to the HRS when the voltage drops below the lower threshold difference (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$V < V_t - V_h$$\end{document}). In this model, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_c$$\end{document} represents the contact resistance at the electrode interface, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_s$$\end{document} accounts for the insulating gap resistance in the HRS, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_f$$\end{document} models the conductive filament in the LRS, while \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C_s$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C_i$$\end{document} describe the gap and cell capacitances, respectively.

While physical models often use internal state variables (e.g., x in Eq. 1) to describe filament evolution, this approach can increase complexity and computational cost in circuit simulators.1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} R(x) = R_{\text {on}} \cdot \exp (-\lambda x) + R_{\text {off}} \cdot \left( 1 - \exp (-\lambda x)\right) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}

To maximize SPICE compatibility and efficiency, we therefore implemented a stateless, voltage-dependent exponential resistance model in LTspice, expressed as2\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} R = R_0 \cdot \exp \left( -\frac{|\text {V(n1)} - \text {V(n2)}| - a}{b}\right) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_0$$\end{document} is a scaling constant, and a and b are fitting parameters calibrated against experimental I–V data. The voltage drop across the non-linear resistor is represented by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$|\text {V}(n1) - \text {V}(n2)|$$\end{document}, where n1 and n2 denote the positive and negative terminals of the resistive element, respectively16–18.

We applied this expression to model the voltage-dependent behavior of both the insulating gap resistance \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_s$$\end{document} and filament resistance \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_f$$\end{document}, reflecting their strong non-linearity under different bias conditions. The exponential form allows for a compact representation of threshold-triggered conduction changes without requiring complex internal state tracking. This strategy achieves both circuit simplicity and an accurate reflection of filamentary switching physics. As shown in Fig. 2c, the simulated I–V characteristics exhibit strong agreement with the measured data obtained from the RRAM device. A deterministic accuracy verification using the best-fitted DC I–V characteristics and a quantitative mean absolute percentage error (MAPE) analysis is provided in Supplementary Fig. S2. The measurement was performed using a double sweep method, where the voltage was swept from \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$-2.5\,\textrm{V}$$\end{document} to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$+2.5\,\textrm{V}$$\end{document} and then back to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$-2.5\,\textrm{V}$$\end{document}—a standard technique for evaluating hysteresis. A compliance current of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$100\,\mu \textrm{A}$$\end{document} was applied, and a single switching cycle was simulated under the same constraint. The simulated curve closely matches the measured data, successfully reproducing the sharp set transition and the relatively fast reset behavior. These results confirm that the proposed circuit model captures the filamentary resistive switching characteristics of RRAM with strong physical consistency19.

To verify the model’s dynamic switching behavior, we performed transient write–read pulse simulations. As shown in Fig. 2d, bipolar voltage pulses (Set and Reset) were applied, followed by small read pulses. The simulated transient current (dashed line) accurately reproduces the abrupt resistance changes and matches the measured read current levels (solid line) after both Set and Reset operations. This demonstrates that the model captures both static and dynamic behaviors of filamentary RRAM under pulse operation. Additional pulse-timing dependent transient switching results across multiple time domains are provided in Supplementary Fig. S320.

We implemented multi-level switching behavior by controlling both the number and threshold voltages of the series-connected voltage-controlled switches (S\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_1$$\end{document} to S\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_8$$\end{document}). As shown in Fig. 2e, each switch activates progressively as the applied voltage exceeds its individual threshold, producing discrete steps in conductance. This mechanism enables a smooth emulation of multiple intermediate resistance states, reflecting partial filament growth or dissolution within the device. During a gradual set voltage sweep from 3.2 to 4.3 V, eight distinct switching events were sequentially triggered, effectively realizing 3-bit resolution in a single cell. The resulting I–V curve exhibits well-defined conductance plateaus, validating that the model captures voltage-dependent modulation and multi-level switching characteristics. The simulation results closely match experimental measurements and confirm the model’s ability to reproduce both gradual and abrupt transitions observed in filamentary RRAM devices21.

Threshold switching device modeling

The switching behavior illustrated in Fig. 3a originates from field-induced reconfiguration of localized defect states within amorphous chalcogenide materials. During electroforming, a strong electric field generates intrinsic defects such as vacancies and interstitials. These defects subsequently migrate and partially align, forming transient conduction pathways within the insulating matrix. When the applied voltage exceeds the threshold voltage \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$V_{\textrm{t}}$$\end{document}, injected carriers gain sufficient energy to trigger impact ionization and defect-assisted tunneling, resulting in an abrupt transition to a low-resistance state. Unlike resistive switching memories governed by stable filament formation, this conductive state is sustained by continuous carrier injection and field-aligned defect clusters. As the voltage decreases below the holding voltage \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$V_{\textrm{h}}$$\end{document}, the electric field can no longer maintain the defect configuration, the accumulated defects relax into disordered states, and the conduction path collapses. Consequently, the device reverts to a high-resistance state. This volatile switching mechanism confines conduction to voltages above the threshold, effectively suppressing leakage current under subthreshold bias23,24.

Ferroelectric tunnel junction modeling

The operation of a Ferroelectric Tunnel Junction (FTJ) relies on ferroelectric polarization modifying the electrostatic potential across its tunneling barrier (Fig. 4a). An applied voltage shifts the conduction band profile, raising or lowering the barrier depending on the polarization direction. During the set operation, the polarization points from the top toward the bottom electrode. This internal electric field lowers the conduction band near the bottom interface and reduces the effective barrier for electrons tunneling from bottom to top. A read voltage of the same polarity therefore increases the tunneling probability and transitions the device into the LRS. Conversely, the reset operation reverses the polarization toward the top electrode, which raises the conduction band and increases both the barrier height and width. This results in a decreased tunneling probability and a transition to the HRS under the same read bias28,29. The interplay between the applied voltage and the polarization-induced internal field governs the readout current by modulating the effective barrier. This dynamic modulation of tunneling probability constitutes the tunneling electroresistance (TER) effect. We validated this mechanism using TCAD simulations with the Centaurus tool, and the results are presented in Supplementary Fig. S630,31.

The compact model for FTJ behavior (Fig. 4b) is based on this principle. It consists of an exponential tunneling resistor (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_{\textrm{FTJ}}$$\end{document})—representing the combined nonlinear tunneling branches (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_{n1}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_{n2}$$\end{document})—and a voltage-dependent ferroelectric capacitance (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C_{\textrm{pol}}$$\end{document}) connected in parallel. The tunneling resistance (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_{\textrm{FTJ}}$$\end{document}) originates from quantum tunneling, and the WKB approximation (Eq. 3) provides the theoretical foundation:3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} T \propto \exp \left( -\frac{2d}{\hbar }\sqrt{2m\phi _{\textrm{eff}}}\right) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}here, m is the electron effective mass and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbar$$\end{document} is the reduced Planck constant. The tunneling current (I) is proportional to this probability and thus follows the same exponential form simplified to:4\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} I \propto \exp \left( -C \sqrt{\phi _{\textrm{eff}}}\right) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where C is a constant incorporating the physical parameters from the previous equation. A crucial aspect of FTJs is that an applied voltage |V| lowers the effective barrier (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\phi _{\textrm{eff}}$$\end{document}), which causes the current I to increase exponentially with voltage. Consequently, the resistance \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R = V/I$$\end{document} decreases exponentially as a function of |V|. This phenomenological, voltage-dependent exponential behavior, though arising from quantum tunneling, is mathematically analogous to the nonlinear conduction modeled in RRAM and TS. We therefore apply the same stateless, voltage-dependent exponential formulation (Eq. 2) to describe this decay for the resistor \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_{\textrm{FTJ}}$$\end{document}. Concurrently, the ferroelectric polarization response is modeled by the voltage-dependent capacitance \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C_{\textrm{pol}}$$\end{document}, which is also fitted using the same phenomenological exponential form. This component models the nonlinear dielectric response (i.e., capacitance increase) as the voltage approaches the coercive region, thereby capturing the hysteretic polarization dynamics. The model’s \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\exp (-|V|)$$\end{document} form, applied to both R and C components, directly reproduces the required behavior, where a and b are fitting parameters that scale and shift the exponential resistance curve to match experimental I–V data. Magnetoresistive random access memory modeling

The magnetic tunnel junction (MTJ) consists of a free layer (FL), a thin insulating tunnel barrier (BL), and a pinned layer (PL). The PL maintains a fixed magnetic orientation, while the FL reverses its magnetization in response to an external magnetic field or a spin-polarized current. The tunneling resistance depends on the relative alignment of the magnetizations of these two layers. In the parallel (P) configuration, minimal electron spin scattering results in the LRS, whereas in the antiparallel (AP) configuration, spin misalignment suppresses tunneling and leads to the HRS. These two stable resistance states form the basis of non-volatile data storage in MRAM. Spin-transfer torque (STT) enables this switching, where a spin-polarized current exerts a torque on the FL to reverse its magnetization, as illustrated in Fig. 5a42,43.

As illustrated in Fig. 5e, the resulting current traces remain clearly separated. This demonstrates the model’s robustness and predictive stability under parameter variations47.

Array-level simulation

The validated device-level models form the basis for subsequent array-level simulations. To demonstrate the system-level applicability of our methodology, we extended the RRAM and MRAM models, as representative examples, into array-level configurations. These array-level simulations enable the evaluation of fundamental computing operations such as Vector-Matrix Multiplication (VMM) and Multiply-Accumulate (MAC), which are key functions in neuromorphic and in-memory computing systems. This approach allows for the estimation of dynamic power consumption under realistic inference conditions, accounting for both device-level switching and interconnect effects. This supports a comprehensive assessment of array performance, including functionality, energy efficiency, and scalability, and demonstrates the practical applicability of our modeling framework.

RRAM array simulation with SNN inference

To perform array-level computing simulations using the proposed generic model, we simulated array operations based on an SNN inference scheme, as illustrated in Fig. 6a48. We employed an off-chip training approach, where the weight map and input encoding were prepared in software and then transferred to the array-level simulation. First, to obtain the weight map, we implemented a \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$784 \times 24 \times 10$$\end{document} artificial neural network (ANN) in PyTorch. The network was trained on the MNIST dataset with a batch size of 32 for 100 epochs. ReLU was used as the activation function and stochastic gradient descent (SGD) was employed as the optimizer. The weights were initialized using He initialization. After training, the weights were quantized to 3-bit precision. The quantized model achieved approximately 95% classification accuracy on the MNIST test set (10,000 images). Next, the trained ANN was converted to an SNN using a rate-coding scheme in software. Pixel intensities were normalized to the range [0, 1] and encoded into spike trains over 25 time steps, where the spike rate was proportional to the normalized intensity. The spikes were applied to the first layer (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$784 \times 24$$\end{document}). The resulting currents were summed through vector–matrix multiplication (VMM) using the trained weights and processed by an integrate-and-fire mechanism. The second layer (24 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document} 10) performed VMM in the same manner and generated output currents. Finally, the extracted weight map was transferred into the array-level simulation as the cell conductance states. The input spike trains were applied to the wordlines to execute VMM, and the output currents were read out from the bitlines and accumulated over the time window. The predicted label was determined by the bitline with the maximum accumulated current.

Subsequently, inference simulations were carried out by applying input spike trains of 1 V amplitude and 4 ms period over 25 time steps to the array. As illustrated in Fig. 6d, an input image of the digit ‘3’ was used, and the output neuron corresponding to the correct label exhibited the highest integrated current, confirming correct classification.

Moreover, classification accuracy was evaluated using 200 MNIST test samples, achieving approximately 95% accuracy (Fig. 6e). Finally, power analysis during inference (Fig. 6f) confirmed that the proposed model quantitatively reproduces realistic array-level behavior, validating its predictive capability for neuromorphic computing simulations. A 2T-2J bitcell configuration was employed to perform hardware-level XNOR operations suitable for binarized inference (Fig. 7b). Each bitcell consists of two MTJs paired differentially, one storing the parallel state (representing a digital 1) and the other the anti-parallel state (representing a digital 0). When differential input signals are applied to the BL and BL_B lines, XNOR logic operations naturally emerge. The resulting currents are then summed through the shared source line (SL) to accumulate the MAC operation across multiple bitcells53.

Conclusion

This work presented a generic compact modeling methodology for RRAM, TS, FTJ, and MRAM. This framework employs a generalized approach, using a minimal set of components (nonlinear resistors, capacitors, and voltage- or current-controlled switches) to construct distinct sub-models. This configuration accurately reproduces device characteristics without requiring device-specific physical equations.

At the device level, the resulting models successfully reflect the intrinsic switching mechanism of each corresponding memory. We demonstrated that the same voltage-dependent resistance formulation (Eq. 2) could phenomenologically capture filament-based switching (RRAM), defect-driven volatility (TS), polarization-modulated tunneling (FTJ), and spin-dependent tunneling (MRAM). All models demonstrated strong consistency with experimental and reference data.

To evaluate scalability, this framework was extended to array-level simulations. The RRAM model was used to implement a multi-level SNN for MNIST inference, and the MRAM model was used to create a 2T-2J differential XNOR array for BNN convolution. Both array simulations demonstrated correct inference behavior and validated the models’ predictive capabilities at the system level.

Nevertheless, the proposed behavioral modeling approach entails inherent limitations compared to physics-based simulations. Specifically, the adoption of a simplified switching mechanism implies that complex transient dynamics—such as the precise pulse-width dependence of switching voltages or the gradual evolution of conductance—may not be fully captured without specific parameter tuning. Furthermore, while the model incorporates the temperature dependence of conduction, the thermal reset mechanism is abstracted as a threshold-triggered event to avoid the complexity of explicitly solving differential heat equations. Relevant temperature-dependent I–V characteristics and compact-model fitting results are provided in Supplementary Fig. S10 and Supplementary Note 2. As a phenomenological model, it necessitates calibration against measured data, and simulations predicting unmeasured physical regimes beyond the characterized range may exhibit increased deviation.

However, these abstractions are intentional and prerequisite for achieving scalability. Unlike conventional physical models, which often involve computationally intensive equations (e.g., dynamic heat distributions), our methodology abstracts these transient physical details to generate compact models suitable for direct integration into circuit-level configurations. This approach enables the efficient simulation of large-scale inference workloads and the accurate estimation of system-level power consumption. Ultimately, this framework effectively bridges the gap between single-device physics and array-level system performance, providing a robust platform for the design and evaluation of heterogeneous memory architectures.
