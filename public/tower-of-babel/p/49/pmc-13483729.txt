Antenatal care utilisation among pregnant adolescents in urban Ghana: a qualitative study of individual, family and health system influences
Authors: Kwabena Opoku-Mensah, Naomi Aggrey Blankson, Eunice Ayimbono Ayimbila, Franklin Glozah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAdolescent pregnancy remains an important public health concern in Ghana and other low- and middle-income settings. Pregnant adolescents often initiate antenatal care later than recommended or attend irregularly, increasing the risk of adverse maternal and newborn outcomes. While determinants of antenatal care use have been widely studied, less is known about how individual, family, and health system factors interact to shape adolescents’ care seeking experiences. This study explored influences on antenatal care utilisation among pregnant adolescents in an urban municipality in southern Ghana.MethodsA qualitative descriptive study was conducted in the La Nkwantanang Madina Municipality in the Greater Accra Region of Ghana. Data were collected through in-depth interviews, focus group discussions, and key informant interviews with pregnant adolescents, caregivers, midwives, and community members. Interviews were conducted in English or Twi, audio recorded, transcribed verbatim, and analysed thematically.ResultsForty-two participants were included: 15 pregnant adolescents, 6 caregivers, 5 midwives, and 16 community members. Three interrelated themes shaped antenatal care utilisation: individual influences, family influences, and health system influences. Delayed pregnancy recognition, stigma, and financial constraints contributed to late initiation of care. Family responses, including support or disapproval, influenced attendance patterns. Health system factors such as provider attitudes, waiting time, privacy concerns, and out of pocket costs also shaped utilisation, although supportive care experiences encouraged continued attendance.ConclusionAntenatal care utilisation among pregnant adolescents is shaped by interacting individual, family, and health system influences. Background

Adolescent pregnancy remains a major public health and development concern globally, particularly in low- and middle-income countries. Recent estimates indicate that approximately 21 million girls aged 15 to 19 years become pregnant each year in low- and middle-income settings, with about 12 million giving birth [1]. Sub-Saharan Africa continues to record some of the highest adolescent fertility rates worldwide, contributing substantially to maternal morbidity, neonatal complications, and preventable mortality. Beyond immediate health risks, adolescent pregnancy is closely linked to school discontinuation, reduced economic opportunities, and intergenerational cycles of poverty [2]. In Ghana, adolescent pregnancy remains a persistent challenge despite ongoing reproductive health interventions. Nationally representative data indicate that approximately 14% of adolescent girls aged 15 to 19 years have begun childbearing, with marked disparities by region, education, and household wealth Ghana Statistical Service [3]. Adolescent childbearing remains higher among girls with lower levels of education and those from poorer households. Despite some progress over time, these patterns highlight persistent inequities in access to reproductive health information and services. Antenatal care is a core component of safe motherhood strategies and plays a critical role in improving maternal and newborn outcomes. The World Health Organization recommends a minimum of eight antenatal contacts during pregnancy to enhance early detection of complications, provide preventive interventions, and promote positive pregnancy experiences [4]. Early initiation and consistent attendance enable timely screening, counseling, nutritional support, and management of pregnancy-related risks. However, evidence across Sub-Saharan Africa shows that adolescent mothers are less likely than older women to initiate antenatal care in the first trimester or complete the recommended number of visits [5, 6], 7, 8]. This pattern places adolescents and their infants at heightened risk of adverse outcomes.

Existing literature demonstrates that antenatal care utilization among adolescents is shaped by a complex interaction of individual, socio cultural, and health system factors. At the individual level, educational attainment, knowledge of antenatal care benefits, perceived susceptibility to complications, and autonomy in decision making influence service uptake [9]. Emotional responses to unintended pregnancy, including fear, shame, and denial, may delay recognition of pregnancy and postponement of care seeking. Adolescents often face limited self-efficacy and restricted control over financial and mobility resources, further constraining access.

Socio cultural contexts also exert strong influence. In many communities, adolescent pregnancy is stigmatized, leading young mothers to conceal their pregnancies or avoid public health facilities to escape ridicule [10]. Parental and partner support has been shown to facilitate early initiation and sustained attendance by providing emotional reassurance, financial assistance, and accompaniment to clinics [8]. Conversely, family disapproval, moral judgment, and partner abandonment can deter adolescents from seeking care. Broader community norms, including religious and cultural expectations regarding sexuality and motherhood, may either reinforce stigma or provide supportive networks [11].

Health system determinants further shape adolescents’ experiences of antenatal care. Studies from Ghana and other African settings report that negative provider attitudes, limited privacy, long waiting times, and perceived discrimination discourage repeat attendance [12–16]. Although several countries have adopted policies promoting free maternal health services, indirect costs such as transportation, laboratory investigations not covered by insurance, and opportunity costs remain significant barriers for adolescents who often lack independent income. The absence of adolescent-friendly services reduces comfort and confidentiality, undermining trust in formal care [11, 17]. Evidence suggests that training providers in respectful and youth responsive care improves satisfaction and utilization [10].

Policy initiatives including free maternal healthcare, community outreach, peer education, and integration of adolescent reproductive health into national strategies have demonstrated positive effects in some contexts [10]. Nonetheless, implementation gaps, resource constraints, and weak accountability mechanisms continue to limit sustained impact. Adolescents remain a particularly vulnerable group within maternal health systems that are often designed around adult women’s needs.

Although quantitative studies in Ghana have identified correlates of antenatal attendance among adolescents, fewer studies have explored how individual, family, and the health system influences intersect to shape lived experiences of care seeking in urban settings. Qualitative inquiry is essential to illuminate the social and relational dynamics underlying statistical patterns. Understanding how adolescents navigate stigma, family expectations, financial limitations, and provider interactions can inform the design of context responsive interventions.

This study therefore examines antenatal care utilization among pregnant adolescents in an urban municipality in Southern Ghana.

Methods

Study design

This study employed a qualitative descriptive design to explore multilevel influences on antenatal care utilization among pregnant adolescents. A qualitative approach was considered appropriate because the study sought to understand lived experiences, perceptions, and social dynamics shaping care seeking behavior. Data were collected through in-depth interviews, focus group discussions, and key informant interviews to allow triangulation of perspectives across adolescents, caregivers, health providers, and community members.

The research team comprised public health researchers with training in qualitative methods and experience in adolescent and maternal health research in Ghana. The lead interviewer had prior experience conducting in-depth interviews and focus group discussions in community and health facility settings. To minimize potential power imbalances, interviews with adolescents were conducted in a private setting within or near the health facility, and efforts were made to establish rapport before initiating formal questioning. The researchers maintained reflexive field notes throughout the data collection and analysis processes to document assumptions, impressions, and evolving interpretations. Regular team discussions were held to reflect on how researchers’ professional backgrounds and prior experiences might influence data interpretation, and to enhance analytic neutrality.

Study setting

The study was conducted in La Nkwantanang Madina Municipality in the Greater Accra Region of Ghana. The municipality is predominantly urban and comprises five sub municipals: Pantang, Danfa, Nkwantanang, Taatanaa, and Social Welfare. The population is largely youthful, and the municipality includes a mix of public and private health facilities, including one hospital, polyclinics, community-based health planning and services zones, and private clinics. Data collection took place at the Madina Polyclinic Kekele and within communities in the Nkwantanang sub municipal area. Madina Polyclinic Kekele is a public health facility serving a densely populated urban population with diverse socioeconomic backgrounds.

Study population and sampling

The study population comprised pregnant adolescents aged 16 to 19 years attending antenatal care services, their caregivers, midwives providing antenatal services, and community members.

Purposive sampling was used to capture diverse perspectives from adolescents, caregivers, midwives and community members with direct experience of adolescent pregnancy and antenatal care utilisation. Pregnant adolescents were identified through antenatal clinic registers and approached with the assistance of midwives. Of the adolescents approached, all agreed to participate. No eligible participants declined participation, and all identified participants were successfully contacted. Caregivers were recruited through adolescents who consented to involve a parent or guardian. Midwives were selected based on their direct involvement in antenatal service provision. Community members were purposively recruited from the study communities based on their residence within the municipality and their familiarity with or exposure to adolescent pregnancy and antenatal care. Participants were selected to reflect a range of community perspectives. Separate focus group discussions were conducted for male and female participants to facilitate open and contextually appropriate discussions. Recruitment continued until thematic saturation was reached, defined as the point at which no new codes or themes emerged from successive interviews. In total, 42 participants were included: 15 pregnant adolescents, 6 caregivers, 5 midwives, and 16 community members who participated in two focus group discussions.

Data were collected using semi structured guides developed by the research team based on the study objectives and relevant literature on adolescent maternal health and antenatal care utilization. Three guides were used:

In-depth interview guide for pregnant adolescents. This guide elicited narratives on pregnancy recognition, timing of antenatal care initiation, knowledge and perceptions of antenatal care, experiences of stigma, financial and logistical challenges, partner involvement, and interactions with health workers.

Key informant interview guide for midwives and focus group discussion guide for community members. The midwife guide captured provider perspectives on adolescents’ antenatal attendance patterns, common barriers, service delivery constraints, and strategies used to support adolescent clients. All guides included open ended questions and probes to support depth, clarification, and follow up. The tools were reviewed among the authors for clarity and cultural appropriateness and were refined before full data collection. The interview and focus group discussion guides are provided as Supplementary File 1.

Data collection procedures

Pregnant adolescents, caregivers, and midwives participated in in-depth or key informant interviews, while community members participated in focus group discussions. In-depth interviews were conducted in either English or Twi depending on participant preference. Each session lasted approximately 45 to 60 min and was audio recorded with participant consent. Field notes were taken immediately after each session to capture contextual observations and non-verbal cues.

Two focus group discussions were conducted, each comprising eight participants. One discussion included male community members and the other included female community members to encourage open expression. Each session lasted approximately 60 to 90 min and was facilitated by a trained research assistant using the discussion guide. The discussions were audio recorded, and data collection continued until thematic saturation was reached.

Data analysis

Audio recordings were transcribed verbatim and translated into English where necessary. Transcripts were cross checked against recordings to ensure accuracy and anonymized prior to analysis.

Thematic analysis, using NVivo 15, was conducted following the six-phase framework described by [18]. This involved familiarization with the data, generation of initial codes through line-by-line coding, grouping of related codes into candidate themes, review and refinement of themes in relation to the full data set, definition and naming of themes, and production of the analytic narrative.

Trustworthiness

Several strategies were employed to enhance rigor [19]. Credibility was strengthened through triangulation of data sources and participant categories. Member checking was informally conducted during interviews by summarizing key points and seeking clarification. An audit trail documenting recruitment, coding decisions, and theme development was maintained to enhance dependability. Thick description of the study setting and participants supports transferability. Reflexive notes were maintained throughout the research process to promote confirmability.

Coding was conducted manually by the lead researcher and independently reviewed by a co-author. Differences in coding interpretation were discussed and resolved through consensus. The final themes reflected patterns consistently observed across participant groups.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Ghana Health Service Ethics Review Committee (GHS ERC/80/02/17). Written informed consent was obtained from adult participants. For participants below 18 years, assent was obtained alongside caregiver consent. Confidentiality was maintained using pseudonyms and removal of identifying information from transcripts. Participants were offered light refreshments in appreciation of their time.

The study is reported in accordance with the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ).

Results

Characteristics of participants

A total of 42 participants took part in the study, comprising 15 pregnant adolescents, 6 caregivers, 5 midwives, and 16 community members who participated in two focus group discussions.

The adolescents were aged between 16 and 19 years. Most had attained junior high school education, with one reporting primary level education. The majority identified as Christian. Twelve adolescents were single, while three reported cohabiting with partners.

Among caregivers, four were female and two were male. The focus group discussions included eight women and eight men aged between 30 and 40 years. Midwives had direct responsibility for providing antenatal care services to adolescents within the study facilities (Table 1).

Most pregnant adolescents were either currently enrolled in school or had recently discontinued their education following pregnancy. Participants’ accounts suggested varying levels of socioeconomic vulnerability, with many adolescents relying on caregivers or partners for financial support, particularly for transportation and health-related expenses. Although detailed socioeconomic data were not systematically collected, narratives indicated that financial dependence and limited access to resources were common across participants. Caregivers were primarily parents or close relatives, including mothers, fathers, and an aunt. Community members were residents of the study area and were recruited to reflect a range of perspectives on adolescent pregnancy and antenatal care.

All adolescent participants were pregnant at the time of the interviews and were attending antenatal care services. The study did not systematically collect detailed quantitative information on gestational age at interview, number of antenatal visits, or continuity of care across facilities. However, participants’ narratives indicated variation in the timing of antenatal care initiation, with several adolescents reporting delayed first visits.

Thematic overview

Analysis of the data generated three overarching themes that shaped antenatal care utilization among pregnant adolescents: individual level influences, family level influences, and health system influences. These themes were interconnected rather than independent. Participants’ decisions to initiate and continue antenatal care were shaped by dynamic interactions between personal experiences, household contexts, and encounters within health facilities. Within each broad category, several sub-themes emerged that either facilitated or hindered timely and consistent attendance (Table 2).

Themes and sub-themes

Individual level influences

Adolescents’ utilization of antenatal care was strongly shaped by personal awareness, emotional responses to pregnancy, perceived stigma, and financial constraints. These individual level influences often determined the timing of antenatal care initiation and consistency of attendance.

Delayed recognition and pregnancy denial

Several adolescents reported that they did not initially recognize early signs of pregnancy. Physical symptoms such as fatigue, breast enlargement, and increased appetite were often misinterpreted as minor illness or routine bodily changes. In some cases, pregnancy was only suspected by parents or peers, leading to delayed confirmation and subsequent postponement of antenatal care.

One adolescent explained:

“I did not even know I was pregnant, it was my mother who saw that I was pregnant and when she saw it the pregnancy was 26 weeks old. During that period when I walk a short distance I feel tired and my breast had become big and I kept eating within short intervals so my mother asked me what was wrong with me and I said I was ok but then she said I was pregnant. I told her she was telling lies so she asked me to buy the pregnancy test and when I did the test at home I saw that I was pregnant.” (Adolescent 3, age 17)

This delayed recognition was not merely informational. It was intertwined with disbelief and denial, particularly among adolescents whose pregnancies were unintended. Emotional resistance to accepting pregnancy often contributed to postponement of antenatal booking.

Fear, shame, and anticipated stigma

Fear of social judgment emerged as a dominant influence on care seeking behaviour. Many adolescents described feelings of embarrassment, particularly those who were still in school. They feared encountering teachers, classmates, or older pregnant women at health facilities. The anticipation of ridicule, rather than direct experience alone, discouraged early attendance.

One adolescent noted:

“When I went the first time, the older women laughed. After that, I waited until I was almost seven months.” (Adolescent 7, age 16)

Another reflected:

“I was afraid that the people in my neighborhood will insult me and say why I have gotten myself pregnant this young.” (Adolescent 11, age 17)

Perceived negative attitudes from health workers further intensified these fears. A participant recounted:

“One nurse said, ‘You’re too small to be a mother.’ I felt like disappearing.” (Adolescent 11, age 17)

Such interactions reinforced adolescents’ vulnerability and, in some cases, led to temporary withdrawal from services. The emotional burden of shame, self-blame, and fear of public humiliation shaped how and when adolescents felt safe to seek care.

However, experiences were not uniformly negative. Some adolescents described supportive encounters that mitigated fear:

“I was scared at first, but the nurse told me every mother feels that way.” (Adolescent 11, age 17)

“Talking with other young mothers helped me not feel alone.” (Adolescent 2, age 16)

These accounts illustrate how emotional responses were fluid and shaped by social interaction. Where reassurance was present, confidence to continue attending antenatal services increased.

Financial constraints and competing priorities

Although maternal healthcare services are officially free under the national health insurance scheme, participants emphasized that indirect costs remained substantial. Transportation expenses, laboratory investigations not covered by insurance, and opportunity costs posed barriers to regular attendance. For adolescents who were financially dependent on parents or partners, these costs created delays in initiating care.

One adolescent explained:

“The car fare is almost the same as buying food; I have to choose.” (Adolescent 14, age 19)

Another described the frustration of wasted resources:

“Sometimes I reach the clinic and they say the midwife has gone. You waste money and time.” (Adolescent 6, age 18)

Midwives also acknowledged this constraint:

“They usually have financial problems because most of them are students and the pregnancy was not planned so they don’t have money to come. They either have to depend on their parents or the person who impregnated them.” (Midwife 2)

For some, financial barriers intersected with emotional uncertainty, resulting in delayed booking:

“The pregnancy was about a month and a half old but because money was a problem we couldn’t start immediately so we waited for some time before starting with the ANC.” (Caregiver 3).

Family level influences

Family dynamics played a decisive role in shaping adolescents’ antenatal care utilization. The family environment functioned both as a source of support and as a barrier, influencing when and whether adolescents-initiated care. Three interrelated subthemes emerged: attempts to terminate pregnancy, parental reactions and decision-making authority, and emotional and financial support.

Attempts to terminate pregnancy and delayed care

A notable factor contributing to delayed antenatal care initiation was initial attempts by families to terminate the pregnancy. Several adolescents reported that before seeking antenatal services, efforts were made to abort the pregnancy. Antenatal care was only pursued when those attempts failed or were abandoned.

One adolescent recounted:

“I reported for ANC when the pregnancy was five months even though I found out I was pregnant in the third month because my mother wanted to abort the baby for me but we couldn’t do it… when we got home I told my mother I will not have the abortion anymore.” (Adolescent 8, age 17)

This account illustrates how antenatal care was not immediately viewed as the first response to pregnancy. Instead, the initial family reaction often focused on concealment or termination. During this period of uncertainty and attempted abortion, adolescents remained outside formal maternal healthcare systems, increasing vulnerability to complications.

Parental reactions and decision-making authority

Family responses to pregnancy disclosure varied considerably. In some households, parents responded with anger, disappointment, or silence, which discouraged adolescents from promptly attending antenatal clinics. Adolescents who perceived disapproval often delayed seeking care out of fear of further reprimand.

However, other adolescents described parental insistence on antenatal attendance as a protective measure:

“My mother told me that if I don’t come for the ANC and it is time for me to deliver the baby, when I go the nurses will insult me so that is why I decided to come.” (Adolescent 12, age 17)

In this instance, attendance was motivated by fear of the health system consequences rather than intrinsic understanding of antenatal benefits. Nonetheless, parental authority influenced behaviour.

Caregivers acknowledged their central role in determining whether adolescents accessed services, particularly where adolescents lacked independent income or mobility:

“When she told me she was pregnant, I was not happy, but I told her we have to go to the clinic so that everything will be checked.” (Caregiver 1)

Decision making power often resided with adult household members, limiting adolescents’ autonomy in initiating care independently. Where caregivers withheld financial support or delayed acceptance of the pregnancy, antenatal booking was postponed.

Emotional and financial support as enablers

Conversely, supportive family environments significantly facilitated antenatal care utilization. Emotional reassurance reduced adolescents’ fear and shame, while financial assistance enabled transportation and payment for laboratory services.

One adolescent explained:

“My mother followed me to the clinic the first time. After that, I felt more confident to go alone.” (Adolescent 4, age 18)

Midwives also observed the influence of family involvement:

“When the mother or the partner supports them, they come regularly. When they are alone, sometimes they stop coming.” (Midwife 4)

Family accompaniment during initial visits appeared to reduce anxiety and normalize care seeking. In some cases, family members actively encouraged consistent attendance to protect both mother and child.

However, the absence of partner involvement emerged as a destabilizing factor. Adolescents whose partners denied responsibility or withdrew support faced greater financial strain and emotional distress, which affected continuity of care.

Family level influences operated as either enabling or constraining forces. Acceptance, emotional reassurance, and financial backing promoted early initiation and sustained attendance, whereas rejection, attempts at pregnancy termination, and delayed financial support contributed to postponed booking and irregular utilization.

Health system influences

Health system related factors shaped adolescents’ experiences of antenatal care through provider interactions, service organization, privacy, waiting times, and indirect costs. Participants described both negative and positive encounters within health facilities.

Provider attitudes and interactions

Adolescents reported varied experiences with midwives and other health staff. Some described encounters characterized by scolding or judgmental remarks.

“One midwife told me, ‘Don’t cry, you made your bed.’ It hurt so much I didn’t return for two months.” (Adolescent 10, age 17)

Another participant shared:

“I heard that the nurses insult young girls who get pregnant, so I was afraid to go.” (Adolescent 6, age 18)

Community members also referred to similar accounts:

“The nurses can say things like you don’t sleep, instead of you sleeping you go and do adult things and don’t learn, so if they say these things to you sometimes you may not feel like going again.” (Community member 3, female FGD)

Midwives acknowledged that adolescents often arrive at the clinic feeling shy or anxious.

“We know they are young and shy. Sometimes they are afraid even before they come inside.” (Midwife 1)

At the same time, several adolescents described supportive interactions.

“The young nurse was friendly. She said, ‘You can still be a good mother.’ That encouraged me.” (Adolescent 13, age 18)

A midwife stated:

“I was taught to give the pregnant adolescents special care because they are young and the pregnancy at that age is stigmatized, so we don’t have to shout at them but treat them nicely.” (Midwife 3)

Participants’ accounts reflected differing experiences across encounters and providers.

Service organization and waiting time

Adolescents described long waiting times at antenatal clinics. Some reported arriving early and remaining at the facility for extended periods before being attended to.

“We come in the morning and sometimes we leave in the afternoon.” (Adolescent 4, age 18)

Others referred to occasions when health staff were unavailable.

“Sometimes I reach the clinic and they say the midwife has gone. You waste money and time.” (Adolescent 6, age 18)

Caregivers mentioned that repeated visits required additional transport expenses and time away from work or household responsibilities.

“If she goes and comes back without being attended to, it means we have to find money again.” (Caregiver 2)

Privacy and confidentiality

Some adolescents expressed concern about being seen by familiar community members at the clinic. They described discomfort when older women or acquaintances were present during visits.

“When I go there and I see people I know, I feel shy.” (Adolescent 1, age 17)

Community members also noted that the antenatal clinic environment is often shared across age groups.

“The young girls sit with older women, so everybody can see them.” (Community member 5, male FGD)

Financial and logistical constraints within the health system

Although antenatal services were described as free under the national health insurance scheme, participants reported that certain laboratory investigations and scans required out of pocket payment.

“Some of the tests are not covered, so they have to pay.” (Midwife 2).

Adolescents described difficulty meeting these additional costs.

“They told me to do a scan, but I had to wait until my mother got money.” (Adolescent 8, 17 years)

Transport to and from the clinic was also mentioned as a recurring expense.

“The car fare is not small, especially if you have to go many times.” (Adolescent 12, age 17)

Accounts from midwives, caregivers, and adolescents highlighted structural and logistical elements within the health system that shaped attendance patterns.

Discussion

This study explored how individual experiences, family dynamics and health system conditions shape antenatal care utilisation among pregnant adolescents in urban Ghana. The findings demonstrate that adolescents’ engagement with antenatal services is shaped by intersecting social, emotional and structural influences rather than isolated individual decisions.

Delayed recognition of pregnancy and emotional responses such as fear, shame, and uncertainty were central to adolescents’ early trajectories of care seeking. Similar patterns have been documented across Sub-Saharan Africa, where unintended pregnancy, stigma and limited reproductive health literacy are associated with delayed antenatal initiation and reduced completion of recommended antenatal contacts [20, 21]. Adolescents frequently lack accurate knowledge of early pregnancy signs and of the preventive role of antenatal care. Evidence from Ghana indicates that limited understanding of antenatal benefits contributes to delayed first trimester attendance among adolescents [22]. Emotional denial and social vulnerability further intersect with informational gaps during early pregnancy recognition, influencing the pace at which formal care is sought.

Stigma emerged as a persistent influence across narratives. Fear of ridicule from peers, community members, and health workers shaped adolescents’ willingness to attend antenatal clinics. Multi country reviews have reported that perceived and enacted stigma surrounding adolescent pregnancy discourages young mothers from engaging consistently with formal health services [8, 10]. Adolescents often navigate conflicting expectations, assuming maternal roles while being judged for early sexual activity. Such tensions contribute to hesitation and irregular attendance. Respectful communication and reassurance from health providers, when present, appeared to reduce anxiety and promote continuity. Evidence increasingly demonstrates that adolescent responsive communication improves maternal healthcare satisfaction and utilization [12, 16].

Financial vulnerability also shaped attendance patterns despite national maternal health financing policies. Although antenatal care is covered under Ghana’s health insurance scheme, indirect costs, transport expenses, and uncovered diagnostic services were reported as barriers. Similar findings across low resource settings show that nominally free maternal services remain financially burdensome due to hidden or ancillary costs [23, 24]. Adolescents, who are typically financially dependent, experience these barriers more acutely than adult women. Examination of affordability must therefore extend beyond policy declarations to consider actual service delivery conditions.

Family contexts functioned as both enabling and constraining environments. Initial attempts to terminate pregnancy prior to seeking formal care reflect broader social and reputational concerns surrounding adolescent pregnancy documented in African settings [25]. Parental authority frequently influenced whether adolescents initiated antenatal services promptly. Studies from Ghana, Nigeria, and Kenya consistently demonstrate that maternal and household support increases the likelihood of early booking among adolescents [9, 26]. Conversely, family rejection or withdrawal of financial support reduces engagement with care.

Partner involvement varied across accounts. Financial and emotional support from partners was associated with more stable attendance patterns, consistent with broader evidence linking male partner engagement to improved maternal health service utilization [27]. In contrast, partner denial or absence compounded adolescents’ financial and emotional vulnerability, intensifying barriers across individual and family domains.

Health system conditions further influenced adolescents’ experiences. Reports of scolding, judgmental remarks, limited privacy, and long waiting times indicate persistent gaps in adolescent-friendly service provision. Research in Ghana and other Sub-Saharan African contexts identifies provider attitudes as a critical determinant of adolescent maternal healthcare engagement [10, 22]. Anticipation of negative treatment may influence delayed initiation. Supportive interactions, reassurance, and confidentiality strengthen trust and continuity. Guidance emphasizes respectful maternity care and adolescent centred service delivery as core components of quality antenatal services, yet implementation appears inconsistent [4, 28].

Operational elements such as scheduling inefficiencies and shared clinic spaces also shaped adolescents’ comfort and attendance. Service organization influences perceptions of safety, dignity, and acceptability within care environments [11, 17, 29, 30]. These structural conditions interact with social and emotional vulnerabilities to influence utilization patterns.

The findings demonstrate that influences on antenatal care utilization do not operate independently. Financial barriers intersect with family dependence; stigma intersects with provider behaviour; and emotional vulnerability intersects with the health system responsiveness. Social ecological perspectives on health behaviour emphasize that individual actions are embedded within relational and structural systems [31]. Adolescents’ care seeking decisions therefore reflect negotiated responses within constrained social contexts rather than isolated individual choices.

Furthermore, the findings highlight the need for adolescent responsive maternal health services in Ghana. Training providers in respectful maternity care, creating private service spaces for adolescents, and strengthening community education on early antenatal care may help reduce delays in antenatal booking. Interventions that involve caregivers and partners may further improve continuity of care.

Limitations of the study

Several limitations warrant consideration. The study was conducted in a single urban municipality in Southern Ghana, which may limit transferability to rural or differently resourced settings. Participant accounts were self-reported and may have been influenced by recall bias or social desirability bias, particularly given the sensitive nature of adolescent pregnancy. Younger adolescents were not represented, and experiences may differ among those at earlier developmental stages. Although multiple participant groups were included, the number of midwives and caregivers was modest. These considerations should be borne in mind when interpreting the findings.

Also, the study sample was drawn from adolescents attending antenatal care services and therefore excludes those who did not access care. This may limit the ability to capture barriers among the most marginalized adolescents who remain outside the formal health system.

Despite these limitations, the study provides detailed qualitative accounts from adolescents, caregivers, community members, and health providers within a defined urban context. The integration of multiple perspectives offers a comprehensive view of how antenatal care utilization unfolds in everyday social and the health system environments.

Conclusion

Antenatal care utilization among pregnant adolescents in this urban municipality in Southern Ghana is shaped by interconnected influences at individual, family, and health system levels. Emotional responses to pregnancy, stigma, financial vulnerability, family decision making dynamics, and service delivery conditions collectively influence timing and continuity of attendance. Strengthening adolescent-responsive maternal health services, reinforcing supportive family engagement, and improving respectful care practices remain important considerations for advancing equitable antenatal care utilization among adolescents.
