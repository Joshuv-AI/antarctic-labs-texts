Development and validation of an explainable machine learning model for predicting multidimensional frailty in hospitalized patients with cirrhosis
Authors: Fang Yang, Chaoqun Li, Wanting Yang, Yumei He, Liping Wu, Kui Jiang, Chao Sun
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe sought to develop and validate a machine learning (ML) model for predicting multidimensional frailty based on clinical and laboratory data. Moreover, an explainable ML model utilizing SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) was constructed. This study enrolled 622 patients hospitalized due to decompensating episodes at a tertiary hospital. The cohort data were randomly divided into training and test sets. External validation was carried out using 131 patients from other tertiary hospitals. The frail phenotype was defined according to a self-reported questionnaire (Frailty Index). The area under the receiver operating characteristics curve was adopted to compare the performance of five ML models. The importance of the features and interpretation of the ML models were determined using the SHAP method. The proportions of cirrhotic patients with nonfrail and frail phenotypes in combined training and test sets were 87.8% and 12.2%, respectively, while they were 88.5% and 11.5% in the external validation dataset. Five ML algorithms were used, and the random forest (RF) model exhibited substantially predictive performance. Regarding the external validation, the RF algorithm outperformed other ML models. Moreover, the SHAP method demonstrated that neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio, age, lymphocyte-to-monocyte ratio, ascites, and albumin served as the most important predictors for frailty. At the patient level, the SHAP force plot and decision plot exhibited a clinically meaningful explanation of the RF algorithm. We constructed an ML model (RF) providing accurate prediction of frail phenotype in decompensated cirrhosis. The explainability and generalizability may foster clinicians to understand contributors to this physiologically vulnerable situation and tailor interventions.Keywords: machine learning, frailty, random forest algorithm, liver cirrhosis, SHapley additive exPlanations

Introduction

Liver cirrhosis accounts for a considerable cause of morbidity and mortality among patients experiencing advanced chronic liver disease [1]. Liver cirrhosis may progress to malignant forms and decompensated stage characterized by variceal hemorrhage, hepatic encephalopathy, and ascites responsible for ~2.4% of global deaths [2]. Nowadays, the majority of therapeutic modalities are concentrated on the prevention and management of common complications outlined above. However, the pathogenesis of liver cirrhosis at distinct stages appears to be complicated and multifaceted, while some important contributory and precipitating factors such as sarcopenia, impaired quality of life, and malnutrition, in addition to frailty, have attracted increasing interest [3]. In this direction, frailty refers to an emerging construct in the realm of hepatology that can be evaluated in physical and/or multidimensional manners.

The selection of suitable measurement of frailty predominantly depends on specific clinical scenarios and targeted populations. In other words, several facets including the patient’s physical conditions, available healthcare resource, and therapeutic triage should be taken into account [4]. The preferable tools for assessing frailty among outpatients are performance-based tests (e.g. liver frailty index and 6-min walking distance) in contrast to self-reported instruments among inpatients [5]. In the circumstance of patients experiencing constant liver diseases deterioration, previous studies have verified the usefulness of activities of daily living, Clinical Frailty Scale, and Frailty Index among critically ill subjects with acute decompensating insults [6–8]. However, the widespread adoption and utilization of aforesaid tools may be hindered in daily clinical practice due to demanding practitioners’ expertise, a time-consuming as well as labor-intensive feature [9]. Therefore, it is imperative to develop simpler modalities identifying patients with frail phenotypes, if possible, on the basis of combined clinical and biochemical parameters that are more commonly performed.

The rapid progression of machine learning (ML) in predicting various clinical outcomes and endpoints has been addressed in the existing literature with favorable performance [10–12]. ML models shed light on handling nonlinear relationships, coping with complicated interactions, and mining subtle correlations within big data, thus providing deep insights into clinical information [13]. These benefits leverage on clarification of potential connections between retrieved clinical characteristics and certain endpoints through collecting massive amounts of data and existing knowledge gaps. Although ML has achieved remarkable and continuous progress in the last decade, one concern is always being raised pertaining to “black boxes” with difficulties to interpret. Of note, many efforts have been made to overcome these barriers by using explainable artificial intelligence, which promotes the understanding of these decision-making and processing steps for clinicians and researchers [14]. Furthermore, this advancement also improves the transparency concerning proposed ML models and, in consequence, facilitates the widespread adoption of artificial intelligence during daily practice. Therefore, we hypothesized that an ML algorithm was capable of identifying patients with cirrhosis experiencing frailty based on a combination of clinical and laboratory data, and applying explainable methods may assist practitioners in interpreting the ML model–dictated prediction for individuals and providing deeper insights into the advent of frail phenotype.

The current study aimed to establish an ML model in a sizable cohort of hospitalized patients with cirrhosis due to decompensating episodes. In addition, external validation was carried out to test the validity of our preliminary findings, and model interpretability was delineated to enable clinicians to make decisions regarding effective interventions.

Materials and methods

Study population

This retrospective study examined several ML models to predict multidimensional frailty in patients with cirrhosis admitted to the Department of Gastroenterology and Hepatology, Tianjin Medical University General Hospital (TJMUGH, a tertiary hospital in North China) and Department of Gastroenterology, The Third People's Hospital of Chengdu (TPHCD, a tertiary hospital in West China). The study protocol was approved by the ethics committee of TJMUGH (IRB2023-YX-013-01) and carried out in alignment with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was provided by all participants. Notably, the dataset derived from TJMUGH was applied for training and testing ML algorithms, while the dataset from TPHCD was applied for external validation.

Diagnosing cirrhosis conformed to medical history, biochemical results, radiological findings, liver stiffness evaluation, and hepatic biopsy if available. We excluded adult patients presenting any of the following characteristics: (i) concomitant acute-on-chronic liver failure; (ii) susceptible/confirmed hepatocellular carcinoma or other malignancies; (iii) incapable of completing the self-administered questionnaire defining frail phenotypes (i.e. requiring more than 120 s to finish a numbers connection test) and (iv) missing clinical information or data. In total, 753 subjects experiencing decompensated cirrhosis on account of acute insults (e.g. esophagogastric variceal hemorrhage, ascites, jaundice, and infection) were included for final analyses from those two institutions. A flow chart concerning the study procedure can be found in Fig. S1. Our datasets were mainly retrieved from two sources: electronic medical record systems and a self-administered questionnaire designated as the Frailty Index. Specially, sorting data comprised general demographics (sex, age), anthropometric estimate (body mass index), disease-specific features (etiologies of cirrhosis: chronic viral/alcohol/autoimmune/cholestasis/cryptogenic; cirrhosis-associated complications: esophagogastric variceal hemorrhage/hepatic encephalopathy/ascites/infection), liver disease–specific scoring systems (Child–Turcotte–Pugh [CTP] score/Model for End-Stage Liver Disease [MELD] score), laboratory data (hemoglobin [Hb]/platelet [PLT]/albumin/total bilirubin [TBIL]/creatinine/sodium/prothrombin time-international normalized ratio [PT-INR]/alanine aminotransferase [ALT]/aspartate aminotransferase [AST]/alkaline phosphatase [ALP]/γ-glutamyl transcriptase [GGT]/lactate dehydrogenase [LDH]) and systemic inflammatory features (lymphocyte-to-monocyte ratio [LMR]/neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio [NLR]/platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio [PLR]).

Outcome variables

The presence of frailty was defined in terms of a variant based on the Carolina Frailty Index (CFI) [15]. Especially, CFI belongs to a self-administered questionnaire of a total of 36 issues covering a range of components including but not limited to the number of comorbidities, frequency of daily medications, instrumental activities of daily living, exhaustion, social activities, depression, physical function, and involutional weight loss in the context of elderly subjects experiencing malignancies. Considering sarcopenia, inflammatory action, and immune dysfunction as commonly pathogenic factors to malignancies and liver cirrhosis in addition to its pathophysiological features, we slightly refined the original scale and established a Frailty Index (Table S1). A qualified questionnaire assessing frailty is supposed to fulfill at least 10 issues. The final score regarding the Frailty Index is calculated by dividing the sum of all points each patient got by the number of completed issues. For instance, a patient obtaining 9 points after completion of all 36 issues has a Frailty Index of 0.25 (9/36). The frail phenotypes can be categorized into two groups: nonfrail (Frailty Index ≤0.38) and frail (>0.38) groups according to a previous report [8].

Machine learning models

The data from TJMUGH were randomly spilt into two sets for training and test at a ratio of 70%:30%. Moreover, the data from TPHCD were used as external validation set. Five supervised ML algorithms were established and tested in the current study: random forest (RF), extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost), artificial neural network (ANN), k-nearest neighbor (KNN), and logistic regression (Logit) [10]. The bootstrap method was carried out with 500 replications to generate the confidence interval pertaining to the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) along with accuracy, specificity, and sensitivity. In this regard, AUC is the most widely representative metric for classification tasks and comparison between distinct models' performance [16]. As for ML algorithms, the Python Scikit-learn library version 1.0.1 (https://github.com/scikit-learn/scikit-learn) was employed. Accordingly, the five ML models were performed in agreement with their hyperparameters set default within the frame of Python Scikit-learn library (Table S2).

On the other hand, the interpretation of the ML models was accomplished in terms of the SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) method. Of note, the SHAP modality has utilities to interpret ML algorithms at the level of the entire cohort as well as individual subject [17]. Each feature-dictated SHAP values for patients in the training and test sets were separately aggregated and averaged with the purpose of elucidating the feature importance for prediction. The global importance regarding each feature was illustrated by the SHAP feature importance plots, that is, a higher mean absolute SHAP value corresponded to higher feature importance pertinent to model prediction. The effect of each feature on the prediction model was illustrated by the SHAP summary plot, where each plot indicated a SHAP value of every patient’s feature in the manner of colors from red to blue corresponding with the high to low values. Furthermore, the impact of certain features on the model prediction was demonstrated by SHAP dependence plots and force plots. All aforesaid SHAP methods were performed in Python by applying SHAP version 0.40.0.

Statistical analysis

As for numeric data, the mean and SD were presented. As for categorical data, the numbers and percentages were presented. Comparisons between groups with and without frailty in relation to clinical and laboratory data were implemented by using the nonparametric Mann–Whitney U test for numeric variables, Pearson χ2 test, or Fisher exact test for categorical variables as appropriate. Moreover, a Spearman correlation was implemented to evaluate collinearity with a threshold >0.8 (no significant collinear variables were found in the present investigation depicted in Fig. S2). The Prism GraphPad version 8.0.1 was adopted for all analyses outlined. A P-value <.05 was set as statistical significance.

Results

Baseline characteristics

In total, 753 patients were enrolled in the current study (Table 1). The presence of frail phenotype was observed in 91 patients, accounting for 12.1% of the entire cohort. The prevalence of frailty was not significantly different between those two institutions (76/622, 12.2% versus 15/131, 11.5%, P = .81). The distribution of major demographics (age and sex), liver disease severity (CTP score and MELD score) and main cirrhosis-associated complications did not significantly differ between the external validation set and the training and test sets. Patients with cirrhosis and advanced age, higher CTP score, lower serum albumin concentration, higher creatinine level, lower ALT level, lower LMR value, higher NLR value, concurrent ascites, and infectious signs were more likely to exhibit multidimensional frailty in the training and test sets.

Model evaluation

As for the primary outcome, that is, the presence of frail phenotype, we constructed five prediction algorithms indicative of RF, XGBoost, ANN, KNN, and Logit. In this direction, the ANN, XGBoost, and RF exhibited relatively higher AUC values (ANN: 0.99, XGBoost: 0.96, RF: 0.91) among the five ML algorithms in the training set (Table 2 and Fig. 1). In the test data set, the RF model achieved the highest AUC value of 0.82 (Table S3). With the external validation data set, the RF model also had the highest AUC and accuracy values at 0.81 and 0.97, respectively (Table 3). As the reference model (i.e. baseline), we have fitted a logistic regression model using significant variables in relation to frailty from the training and test sets as demonstrated in Table 1 (i.e. age, cryptogenic etiology, CTP, albumin, creatinine, sodium, ALT, LMR, NLR, and complications). As depicted in Fig. 1, we can see that the baseline AUC was inferior to those applying ML algorithms, further strengthening the notion that the variables selected for constructing our model were indeed optimal and the RF algorithm could serve as a robust tool in frailty prediction. Taken together, we selected the RF model for all downstream analyses.

Feature importance of the random forest model

As depicted in Fig. 2A, we computed the feature importance of the RF model. The position of each feature on the y-axis unravels its importance. These variables included demographics (age), laboratory data (albumin, creatinine, LDH, ALT, PLT, Hb, AST, GGT, sodium, PT-INR, and TBIL), indicators regarding systemic inflammation (NLR, LMR, and PLR), complications (ascites, esophagogastric variceal hemorrhage, and infection), MELD score, and cryptogenic etiology. The analytes were ordered according to the highest to the lowest mean absolute SHAP value, ranking the impact of selected features on the prediction. The top five risk features comprised NLR, age, LMR, ascites, and albumin.

(A) SHAP feature importance illustrated according to the mean absolute SHAP value of each feature. (B) SHAP summary plot demonstrating the distribution of the SHAP values of each feature. A dot is created for each SHAP value for the model of each patient; therefore, one patient is allocated one dot on the line concerning each feature. Dots are colored in terms of features for the respective patient and accumulate vertically depicting density. The further away a point is from the baseline SHAP value of zero, the stronger it impacts the output.

We sought to understand and achieve an overview of the feature importance through the SHAP method. The global SHAP interpretations are based on calculating the SHAP explanations for all individuals with subsequently averaging them by feature in hopes of getting a cohort view. A larger mean absolute SHAP value pertaining to a feature corresponded to more importance on the model prediction. As depicted in Fig. 2B, a dot is created for each SHAP value for the model of each patient; therefore, one patient is allocated one dot on the line concerning each feature. Dots are colored in terms of features for the respective patient and accumulate vertically depicting density. Red indicates a high feature value (in this case, frailty), whereas blue indicates a low feature value. For instance, patients with a higher NLR value (red plots) were more likely to experience frailty than those with a lower NLR value (blue plots). Similarly, patients with advanced age and concurrent ascites were at greater likelihood of frail phenotype. In Fig. 3, the SHAP dependence plots further clarify the effect of analytes on the RF algorithm prediction. Patients with decreased LMR values and serum albumin concentrations (decrease in the x-axis) were linked to higher SHAP values, suggestive of a higher likelihood of experiencing frailty (increase in the y-axis). Moreover, increased NLR and creatinine levels, advanced age, and the presence of ascites were all associated with frail phenotype.

Machine learning model explanation at the patient level

The SHAP method is advantageous in not only achieving overall explanations at the cohort level but also giving rise to an interpretation for individual prediction at the patient level. In the current study, we applied this method to understand why the RF algorithm drew such conclusions from three patients. Patient 1 (Fig. 4), belonging to the “true negative” group, achieved a low likelihood of frailty (i.e. nonfrailty), and Patient 2, belonging to the “true positive” group, achieved correct prediction of having a high likelihood of frailty (Fig. 5). In contrast, Patient 3 (Fig. S3), close to the “false positive” rather than failed prediction, achieved a high likelihood of frailty.

Patient 1 was a 37-year-old male hospitalized due to variceal hemorrhage on the endoscopy examination. The patient had a MELD score of 13 and was categorized into CTP class B. He presented with an NLR value of 2.75 and an LMR value of 2.49, along with a serum albumin concentration of 34 g/L. Moreover, no ascites was found in this patient. These results implicated that decreased NLR, increased albumin and LMR, absence of ascites, and relatively younger age served as the main contributors to the nonfrail phenotype in the context of cirrhosis.

Patient 2 was a 71-year-old male admitted on account of infection. The patient had an MELD score of 14 and was categorized into CTP class C. He exhibited an NLR value of 21.72 and an LMR value of 0.65 in addition to a serum albumin concentration of 20 g/L. Severe ascites according to ultrasound assessment was observed. Collectively, these findings pinpointed that increased NLR, advanced age, decreased LMR and albumin, and the presence of ascites served as the main contributors to the frail phenotype among patients with decompensated cirrhosis.

Patient 3 was a 64-year-old male admitted due to ascites. He exhibited an NLR value of 19.04 and a serum albumin concentration of 23 g/L. Although several analytes abovementioned impacted the model output, the self-reported questionnaire indicated nonfrailty.

Discussion

To the best of our known, this is the first study applying various ML models to predict the presence of frailty in the context of decompensated cirrhosis. We constructed five ML algorithms for comprehensive prediction, wherein the RF algorithm incorporating certain clinical and laboratory data easily extracted from the electronic medical records exhibited the best performance. Notably, the RF model was further corroborated by using external data from other tertiary hospitals in hopes of assuring the validity and generalizability of these results. The most important features included NLR, age, LMR, ascites, and serum albumin concentrations. Moreover, our study gave rise to clinically meaningful explanations based on samples provided by the SHAP method.

Frailty implicates multidimensional derangements among cirrhotic patients at a decompensated stage, which may be omitted according to traditional scoring systems like CTP or MELD scores. However, the clinical relevance and practical significance concerning the presence of frail phenotype have been closely connected to a spectrum of inferior outcomes, including mortality, prolonged length of hospitalization, high healthcare cost, and disease progression [9]. Considering the information outlined above, it is imperative to predict and identify subjects being prone or already experiencing frailty. Notably, frailty corresponds to a dynamic situation rather than a unidirectional state, indicative of a continuum mode as robust-pre-frail-frail phenotypes [18]. Taken together, we hold the promise that early prevention and timely intervention may, at least to some extent, lead to recovery or reverse frailty with the purpose of achieving better prognostication. Although a myriad of metrics has been implemented to assess frailty in the realm of hepatology, such as the performance-based instrument Liver Frailty Index, one major limitation lies on practical unavailability in patients exhibiting critically ill or severely deteriorated conditions [19]. In this regard, a combination of analytes based on daily retrieved clinical and laboratory data is supposed to shed light on providing more accurate prediction alongside balanced cost-effectiveness.

The ML method represents a powerful and useful computational technique coping with complicated and massive data since it is capable of handling highly heterogeneous datasets and delineating the complex interactions between variables through a flexible and trained approach. Furthermore, it is advantageous in dissecting subtle and nonlinear relationships concerning the data. Combined with easily attainable data from electronic medical records, the development of an ML algorithm–dictated prediction model sounds meaningful [20]. Among five ML algorithms, we found that the RF model exhibited the highest AUC value in the test and external validation cohorts. RF indicates an ensemble classifier incorporating and integrating a decision tree set by majority voting, which has been broadly utilized as a classification model. Accumulating evidence has proved that the RF model exhibits excellent predictive utility in the medical field [21–23]. Correspondingly, the present study employing the RF algorithm to generate a model with more pronounced predictors such as increased NLR, advanced age, decreased LMR, presence of ascites, and lower serum albumin concentrations. All these features are easily retrieved or assessed during daily practice for patients with cirrhosis undergoing acute decompensating insults, making this promising model as an early discriminative modality to determine frail phenotype in critically ill patients upon hospitalization, especially when physical estimations appear to be challenging.

Several factors have consistently been linked to the advent, development, and worsening of frail phenotype in the setting of decompensated cirrhosis. Regarding age, our findings are consistent with information in the field of geriatrics since aging and the progression of cirrhosis may act synergistically aggravating these multimodal health deficits [24]. Furthermore, both in vivo and in vitro studies have clarified that the presence of frailty is closely associated with persistent inflammation characterized by extensive production of pro-inflammatory cytokines [25–27]. Accordingly, both NLR and LMR serve as reliable indicators of dysregulated immune milieus among cirrhosis, mirrored as a culmination of overwhelming inflammatory actions and concurrent immunoparalysis [28–31]. The expression of NLR is associated with predominant circulatory cytokines including IL-6 and IL-8 in patients with cirrhosis undergoing acute deterioration [32]. Additionally, another study unveiled that LMR was the best single analyte to predict bacterial occurrence [33]. Regarding ascites, a previous report on cirrhosis stated that this common complication can lead to declined nutrition intake, decreased physical activity, and extensive energy consumption, raising the likelihood of poor nutritional status and resulting frailty [34]. Regarding albumin, Jha et al. showed that frail subjects experiencing advanced heart failure admitted for heart transplantation were also present with hypoalbuminemia compared with nonfrail counterparts due to hepatic dysfunction [35]. It is highlighted that repeated paracenteses and thoracenteses to manage ascites may result in perpetuated hypoalbuminemia, instigating activation of stress pathways at cellular levels to surpass appendicular muscle synthesis responsible for performance decline [36].

Another concern pertinent to ML models lies in their clinical interpretability since these algorithms are regarded as a “black box” lacking of explanation about how relevant prediction is derived [37, 38]. Therefore, clinicians and researchers may refuse to adopt it since they are hesitant or resistant to make medical decisions/therapeutic triage based on opaque information. Correspondingly, we utilized the SHAP approach to determine the negative or positive effect of certain features on prediction and, in consequence, demonstrated the results for three patients whose frail phenotypes were correctly or “falsely” predicted by inputting selected analytes. The individual explanations were in agreement with the existing literature as abovementioned as well as clinical practice, eventually corroborating the validity and reliability of the RF model. This further supports the notion that the factors identified for establishing our model are indeed optimal and help clinicians comprehensively understand the process of how the developed model makes a particular prediction and increase the opportunity for early interventions (e.g. albumin infusion or infection management).

The limitations warrant clarification. First, the selection of a global estimation concerning multidimensional frailty was dependent on the disease severity of the study populations. There is a lack of extensive validation regarding the proposed Frailty Index for generalizability in other populations. Second, several variables have been neglected in the ML models, such as physical activity, muscle health, and nutritional status, which may impact the algorithm's performance. Notably, lifestyle factors were not included as precipitating factors in the ML algorithms since the assessment of frailty itself has already covered multifactorial aspects like instrumental activities of daily living and physical function. In addition, the presence of specific comorbidities like chronic bronchitis, emphysema, heart disease, and diabetes, as components incorporated in the Frailty Index questionnaire, may represent a closer relationship to lifestyle patterns such as smoking, alcoholism, and sedentary activities. Third, patients experiencing severe hepatic encephalopathy have been excluded from the final analysis, giving rise to potential selection bias. Last, a more heterogeneous population from multiple institutions is warranted to validate the RF model, although our ML model was trained, tested, and externally validated by using data from two high-volume tertiary hospitals with geographical variances in China.

In conclusion, we constructed an ML model based on easily obtained clinical and laboratory data to provide an accurate prediction of frail phenotype in decompensated cirrhosis. The explainability and generalizability may foster clinicians to understand the contributors to this physiologically vulnerable situation and tailor interventions.
