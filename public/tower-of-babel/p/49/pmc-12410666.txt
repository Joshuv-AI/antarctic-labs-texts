Breakthrough SARS-CoV-2 outcomes in immune-disordered people during the Omicron era: a prospective cohort study
Authors: Mackenzie Zendt, Fausto Andres Bustos Carrillo, Viviane Callier, Maureen DeGrange, Anita Ginigeme, Lurline Wu, Bevin Manuelpillai, Ana Ortega-Villa, Emily E Ricotta
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionImmune-deficient/disordered people (IDP) elicit a less robust immune response to COVID-19 vaccination than the general US population. Despite millions of IDP at presumed elevated risk, few population-level studies of IDP have been conducted in the Omicron era to evaluate breakthrough infection-related outcomes.MethodsWe followed a prospective cohort of 219 IDP and 63 healthy volunteers (HV) in the USA from April 2021 (Alpha variant peak) to July 2023 (Omicron XBB variant peak). IDP had a primary or secondary immune disorder. All participants were≥3 years old and received COVID-19 vaccines external to this study. We quantified anti-spike IgG titre levels by ELISA, measured breakthroughs via participant reports and laboratory tests on saliva samples, compared breakthrough incidence among HV and IDP, assessed infection complications (persistent infections, reinfections and post-acute sequelae of COVID-19 (PASC)) and used surveys to capture COVID-19 symptoms and preventive attitudes/behaviours.ResultsAmong IDP, the incidence of initial breakthrough infection was 8.8 (95% CI 4.5 to 62.5) times higher during than before the Omicron era. There were 88 initial breakthrough infections among IDP (incidence rate 23.7/100 person-years) and 28 among HV (27.3/100) throughout the study period. While COVID-19 symptoms were generally mild, five participants, all IDP, were hospitalised. In traditional analyses and an emulated trial, the quantity of anti-spike IgG 1 month after participants’ most recent pre-infection vaccination was not associated with breakthrough. HV and IDP frequently practiced infection-limiting behaviours, but IDP were more likely to continue such behaviours after vaccination. IDP experienced persistent infections, PASC and reinfections more commonly than HV.ConclusionsBreakthrough rates in IDP were largely equivalent to HV. However, IDP experienced a slightly higher frequency of symptoms, hospitalisations, infection persistence, PASC and reinfections than HV. Further study is needed to elucidate the immunological mechanisms that increase the risks of such complications in IDP.

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

Although immunocompromised and immunocompetent participants experienced a largely equivalent incidence rate of initial breakthrough infections (23.7/100 person-years vs 27.3/100 person-years, respectively) from the peak of the Alpha variant in the USA to the peak of the Omicron XBB variant, immunocompromised persons experienced a slightly higher frequency of symptoms, hospitalisations, infection persistence, post-acute sequelae of COVID-19 (PASC, long covid) and reinfections than immunocompetent persons. Individuals with immunocompromising conditions should continue to be vigilant during respiratory virus season, receive updated vaccines when available and, in conjunction with their physicians, be aware of the increased risk they face for infection persistence and long covid, which could require specialised long-term care.

HOW THIS STUDY MIGHT AFFECT RESEARCH, PRACTICE OR POLICY

As COVID-19 variants continue to circulate, it is important for immune-deficient/disordered people (IDP) and other vulnerable populations to know their risk of SARS-CoV-2 infections and resulting complications. In our study, IDP and HV exhibited largely similar breakthrough rates through mid-2023 despite IDP receiving more vaccines and continuing to take preventative steps after vaccination. Nevertheless, IDP appear more prone than immunocompetent individuals to developing post-infection complications like hospitalisations, PASC, persistent infections and reinfections. Consequently, individuals with immunocompromising conditions should continue to be vigilant during respiratory virus season, receive updated vaccines when available and, in conjunction with their physicians, be aware of the increased risk they face of developing potentially longterm outcomes. Similarly, IDP should advocate for official guidance and resources from policymakers that are responsive to their additional risks. Our detailed information on the risk and outcomes of SARS-CoV-2 breakthrough infections will aid individuals with immune disorders and their health practitioners in making informed choices to reduce the likelihood of infection and anticipate possible complications should they occur

Introduction

During the early phase of the COVID-19 pandemic, immune-deficient/disordered people (IDP) experienced extended periods of self-isolation while living in fear of adverse outcomes of SARS-CoV-2 infection, medical rationing, medical ableism and death.13 As the pandemic continued, those fears spread to include post-acute sequelae of COVID-19 (PASC, long covid).4 5 Their concerns were partly realised when evidence emerged that, as a group, IDP experience a significantly less robust immune response to COVID-19 vaccines than the general population.4 6 In the USA, this vulnerable and understudied population accounts for upwards of 15.5 million adults who remain at elevated risk of SARS-CoV-2 infections and associated outcomes. With the epidemiological dynamics shifting from pandemic to endemic transmission of the virus, there remain few studies providing in-depth information on the COVID-19 breakthrough experience in IDP. The existing literature suggests breakthrough infections occur at higher rates in IDP than immunocompetent persons, though available population-level studies in these groups only followed patients through mid-2021, before the emergence of the Omicron variant.7 8

In this large prospective cohort study, we describe the rate and characteristics of SARS-CoV-2 breakthrough infections among IDP; the occurrence of persistent infections, reinfections, PASC; and various attitudes and behavioural strategies that prevent or limit infection. In contrast to most studies of IDP during the COVID-19 pandemic, which focus heavily on immune disorders due to cancers and solid organ transplantations, our study population includes a diverse array of underlying immunological conditions, including inborn errors of immunity. Our results present critical information for both the medical field and COVID-19 patients to understand the ongoing risk and potential outcomes from SARS-CoV-2 infections, especially in the Omicron era.

Methods

Study procedures

From April 2021 through July 2023, we followed 219 IDP and 63 healthy volunteers (HV) throughout the USA in a prospective, longitudinal cohort study designed to assess the immune response to COVID-19 vaccines (NCT04852276).6 9 All participants had to be at least 3 years old and be eligible to receive and scheduled to receive or have already received a COVID-19 vaccine external to this study. IDP had to have a documented primary (inherited genetic condition) or secondary (acquired condition) immune disorder (online supplemental table 1), whereas HV had no known immune disorder and included some unaffected relatives of immune-deficient participants. Potential participants were excluded if they received any non-COVID-19 vaccine within a 14-day period prior or a 28-day period after any COVID-19 vaccine, had any medical condition that contraindicated participation in a research study, or were HIV-positive.

The full methods have been described previously in a study by Zendt et al.6 Briefly, we quantified anti-spike IgG titres 1, 6 and 12 months post-vaccination and collected bi-weekly saliva samples to test for SARS-CoV-2 infection. Anti-spike IgG titres were measured on a µg/mL basis by ELISA.10 Self-reported infections were confirmed via reverse transcription PCR (RT-PCR) when possible (online supplemental figures 1,2). Participants also completed standardised surveys on their medical history, SARS-CoV-2 infections and symptoms, and preventative attitudes and behaviours both pre-vaccination and post-vaccination. Behavioural surveys (online supplemental file 1) were collected from June through August 2022, or on enrolment if after this period.

Statistical analysis

Participants’ person-time at risk of an initial breakthrough infection was calculated from date of completion of their primary vaccination series to the date of first infection, drop out or study cessation (31 July 2023), whichever was earliest. Person-time was calculated from the same start date to 15 December 2021 (selected as the start point of the Omicron era), to calculate incidence in the pre-Omicron era and person-time still at risk after this date was used to calculate incidence in the post-Omicron era. We compared time to infection between HV and IDP (including subgroups of the latter categorised by their underlying immune deficiency) using Cox proportional hazards models, controlling for participant age and titre at the last sample before breakthrough or censoring.

To evaluate the difference in anti-spike IgG titres between those with and without breakthrough infections, we contrasted the median titres at matched points using Wilcoxon rank-sum tests. To enable fair comparisons between IDP and HV on the basis of vaccine doses, participants were classified as completing their primary vaccination series on receipt of two messenger RNA (mRNA)-based vaccines or one receipt of the Ad26.COV.S vaccine (Janssen). Additionally, we conducted a matched target trial emulation11 by treating the most recent 1-month post-vaccination antibody concentration for each participant as the treatment variable, which was separated into none-to-low (<1 µg/mL), medium (≥1 µg/mL to <10 µg/mL) and high (≥10 µg/mL) categories; these levels were selected based on natural breaks in the distribution of post-vaccination anti-spike IgG titres we observed in the data. Three cohort intervals were created based on the major circulating variant at the time of breakthrough (Omicron BA.1, BA.2 and BA.4/5). Follow-up time was calculated from the interval start date until the date of first known breakthrough or censoring at the end of the variant interval, whichever was earlier. Additional covariates in the model included participant age, IDP or HV status, and a time-varying covariate for days from last vaccine dose. Participants who experienced a breakthrough during an interval were dropped from the cohort for subsequent intervals. Participants reporting pre-vaccination (N=10), or pre-Omicron infections (N=18) were excluded from this analysis.

We defined persistent positives as infections with RT-PCR-detectable virus>14 days after an initially positive test, PASC as illness lasting>4 weeks5 and reinfections as separate infections occurring>60 days apart. We compared titres among persistent positives, PASC and reinfections to never-infected participants (matched 2:1 by age, immune subgroup and number of vaccine doses) at the 1-month post-vaccination time point most closely preceding infection. As a sensitivity analysis, we also examined PASC as an illness lasting>3 months. Other comparisons were conducted using χ2 and log-rank tests, as appropriate.

Patient and public involvement

Participants were not involved in the study design, analytical plan or interpretation of the data; however, our work is aligned with the research priorities of IDP, who were excluded from all COVID-19 vaccine trials and who have been an understudied population in relation to COVID-19 outcomes. Plain language summaries, in addition to final manuscripts, of our group’s results have been given to our participants, both for this study and for prior studies.6 The senior author has also participated in outreach activities with patient advocacy groups and the lay media.1214

Results

Participant characteristics

The study began during the peak of Alpha variant transmission in the USA, just as most participants were about to or had recently received their second COVID-19 vaccination. We enrolled 282 total participants, of which 174 were female and 108 were male. The average age across all participants was 44.4 years (IQR: 31–60 years). 10 (3.5%) participants experienced a SARS-CoV-2 infection before their first COVID-19 vaccination (figure 1A). Four infections were caused by pre-Delta variants, and only seven infections (including most pre-vaccination infections) were caused by the Delta variant (table 1). In contrast, Omicron variants caused most breakthrough infections during the study period (figure 1B). Five initial breakthrough infections (all in IDP) occurred before the Omicron era, and 111 initial breakthroughs occurred afterward at a fairly constant rate through the emergence of different Omicron subvariants (figure 1B). These 116 (41.1%) initial breakthrough infections, including 4 that had previously experienced a pre-vaccination infection, comprise the main group of analysis for this study. The full cohort is used for a relatively small number of analyses. Nine participants reported two breakthrough infections during the study period, and one participant reported three; secondary and tertiary breakthrough infections are not considered further due to their low numbers (n=10).

Incidence of initial breakthrough infections

Among the 116 initial breakthrough infections, 88 were among IDP (incidence rate 23.7/100 person-years; 95% CI 19.1 to 29.6) and 28 among HV (27.3/100 person-years; 95% CI 18.1 to 39.5). The resulting incidence rate ratio of 0.9 (95% CI 0.6 to 1.4) indicates that the rate of initial breakthrough among IDP was slightly lower than, though largely equivalent, among HV (p=0.52) (online supplemental table 2). Similarly, during just the Omicron period, incidence was slightly lower among IDP than HV, but not significantly so (rate ratio 0.8; 95% CI 0.6 to 1.4, p=0.39). The time to first breakthrough infection was not significantly different between HV and IDP (figure 2A) or between HV and IDP subgroups (figure 2B).

Examining incidence by time period, we found that the cohort-wide incidence rates were 3.1/100 person-years (95% CI 1.0 to 7.1) before and 35.8/100 person-years (95% CI 29.6 to 43.3) during the Omicron era (online supplemental table 2). The rate ratio of 11.7 (95% CI 6.0 to 85.5) indicates that breakthrough incidence was significantly and overwhelmingly concentrated in the Omicron era (p<0.001). Strictly among IDP, comparing the incidence of initial breakthrough infections during the Omicron era to before the Omicron era resulted in a similar pattern (rate ratio 8.8; 95% CI 4.5 to 62.5; p<0.001)

Breakthrough infection disease experience

Of 116 participants with initial breakthrough infections, 62 (53.4%) completed a survey of COVID-19 signs and symptoms. The most prevalent disease manifestations among IDP were fatigue (93.5%), cough (89.1%), rhinorrhoea (82.6%), fever (69.6%) and pharyngitis (69.6%). In general, COVID-19 symptoms were mild across both IDP and HV, though IDP were significantly more likely to report that they experienced fatigue and nausea/vomiting than HV (figure 1B). Among IDP, the least prevalent COVID-19 manifestations were also the more severe ones: disorientation (8.7%), low blood oxygen (6.5%), kidney injury (4.3%) and blood clots (2.2%). With the exception of kidney injury (6.2%), the other severe manifestations had a prevalence of 0.0% among HV, though differences between IDP and HV across these four conditions were non-significant. Rash, though not a severe condition, was also present in 2.2% of IDP who experienced illness. Five hospitalisations, all among IDP, and zero deaths due to COVID-19 were observed.

Vaccination histories and impact

IDP experiencing breakthroughs received an average of 3.0 monovalent mRNA vaccines prior to their initial infection, compared with 2.7 received by HV (p=0.03). By the time Omicron became the dominant SARS-CoV-2 variant in mid-December 2021, 62.6% (137/219) of IDP and 44.4% (28/63) of HV had received three vaccinations with the first-generation, monovalent mRNA vaccines (p=0.02). Approximately 47% of both IDP and HV received updated bivalent vaccines (targeting the ancestral strain and Omicron BA.4/5), regardless of whether they experienced a breakthrough infection. Among those who ever received a bivalent vaccine, 57% of IDP and 41% of HV did so within 2 months of the updated vaccine becoming widely available (p=0.19). Despite the high prevalence of Omicron BA.4/5 and the emergence of Omicron XBB when the bivalent booster became available (figure 1B), only 12 participants experienced their first infection post-bivalent vaccination (IDP=9, HV=3, p=1.00). The incidence of initial breakthrough infections post-bivalent vaccine (21.5/100 person-years) was lower than the corresponding pre-bivalent incidence (24.9/100 person-years), but it was not significantly different (rate ratio 0.9; 95% CI 0.4 to 1.4; p value=0.63) (online supplemental table 2).

Anti-spike IgG titre and breakthrough risk

We then compared anti-spike IgG titres 1 month after the most recent vaccination in people who went on to experience breakthrough infections and those who did not. In traditional analyses (figure 3A), there was no evidence of an association between 1-month post-vaccination titre levels and the likelihood of breakthrough infection following participants’ second, third and fourth COVID-19 vaccine. Although there were no significant differences between group medians, following doses 3 and 4, there were clearly low, medium and high antibody responders. By treating the resulting groups as hypothetical intervention arms in an emulated trial analysis, we evaluated the probability of breakthrough during each Omicron variant wave (figure 3B). However, even in this more advanced analysis, there was still no evidence of an association between 1-month post-vaccination anti-spike IgG titres and subsequent breakthrough infection.

Behavioural practices and policy preferences

Responses to the behavioural survey (54.3% completion rate) in the full participant cohort showed that both IDP and HV demonstrated a high acceptance of personal protective behaviours (eg, mask wearing, limiting trips outside the home) and particularly high support for policy preferences designed to limit disease transmission (eg, mandatory masking, increased teleworking). Participants reported that these behaviours broadly persisted following vaccination (figure 2C), though IDP were significantly less likely than HV to report that they returned to normal, pre-pandemic life (p=0.01). Post-vaccination, both IDP and HV continued to support public policies limiting the transmission of SARS-CoV-2, but they reported less agreement with continuing personal protective behaviours to limit infections.

Complications of SARS-CoV-2 infections

Despite a relatively similar breakthrough infection rate, IDP in the entire cohort of 282 participants experienced persistent infections (4.6%), PASC (4.6%) and reinfections (4.1%) more commonly than HV (3.2%, 3.2%, 1.6%, respectively; all comparisons non-significant). Of the 10 people experiencing pre-vaccination infection, 4 had breakthroughs after their primary vaccination series; none reported persistent infection or PASC (figure 4A). Among IDP, persistent infections averaged 32 days (SD=22) and PASC symptoms averaged 139 days (SD=165), compared with 22 days (SD=1) and 124 days (SD=80) for HV, respectively. Median pre-breakthrough anti-spike IgG titres among those with reinfections (p=0.02), PASC (p=0.05) and persistent infections (p=0.06) were lower than matched uninfected controls (figure 4B–D). After infection, titres in all three groups increased. More specifically, at the first time point following infection, titres of those with PASC and reinfections increased to comparable amounts, and titres in participants with persistent infections increased to higher levels than those with PASC and reinfections.

PASC, when defined as an illness lasting at least 90 days, occurred in 1.8% (n=4) of IDP and 1.6% (n=1) HV (p=1.00). Discussion

Principal findings

Our study, one of the largest IDP cohorts to focus on non-oncological, non-transplant-related immune disorders, presents a thorough evaluation of breakthrough SARS-CoV-2 infections and outcomes in this population, with a focus on Omicron-era infections. We found that breakthrough rates in IDP were largely similar to HV despite having a less robust immune response to vaccination, more COVID-19 vaccinations and a higher propensity to take steps to limit infections, both overall and during the Omicron era. Despite IDP reporting that they were less likely than HV to return to pre-pandemic life after being vaccinated, IDP experienced a slightly higher frequency of symptoms, hospitalisations, infection persistence, PASC and reinfections than HV. These findings align with hypotheses that suggested Omicron’s impact on IDP would be somewhat mitigated by a broad range of medical countermeasures, multiple vaccinations and immunity from prior infections, but that IDP would continue to be at higher risk for severe infection and PASC.15 As breakthrough incidence was almost 12 times higher during the Omicron era than earlier variants, our results highlight the need to update our understanding of the vaccines’ impact on IDP during this ongoing period of elevated risk.

Strengths and limitations of this study

An important component of the COVID-19 response was the practice of preventative behaviours, which were shown to decrease excess COVID-19 deaths in the USA.16 Personal behaviours aimed at preventing infections have been commonly practiced among people with immune disorders since before the pandemic, as they tend to be infection-conscious more generally due to their increased risk of illness.17 18 However, prior research has established that prolonged practice of personal preventive behaviours—especially at the level undertaken by IDP, who remained exceptionally vigilant for an extensive period of time due to the uncertainty of a newly emerged virus—has a considerable negative impact on well-being.18 19 We included a behavioural survey to understand the behaviours practiced by our cohort as well as to complement and contextualise our seroepidemiological findings. Specifically, we hypothesised that the low level of breakthrough infections in our cohort, especially pre-Omicron, and the largely equivalent rates of breakthrough infection among IDP compared with HV were partly the result of stringent preventative behavioural measures taken by IDP. Indeed, there were only five breakthrough infections in our cohort before the Omicron era, despite the study beginning at the peak of the Alpha variant’s wave. Our results suggest that the preventative behaviours continuously undertaken by our risk-averse, vulnerable participants and their multiple rounds of vaccinations largely protected them from breakthrough infections until the Omicron era. At that point, however, both vaccination and behavioural precautions were insufficient to prevent a barrage of breakthrough Omicron infections, possibly because the variant’s high capacity for immune evasion and high binding affinity for the angiotensin-converting enzyme 2 (ACE2) receptor facilitated a surge of SARS-CoV-2 infections.20 Future population-level research on post-Omicron SARS-CoV-2 variants should include behavioural studies to understand the contribution of personal protective behaviours to the prevention of infection, while keeping in mind the extent to which increased vigilance harms mental well-being.

While we did see significantly more agreement with and practice of preventive behaviours in IDP than HV, even among our HV, these behaviours were high relative to other studies of the general population.2123 This is likely an impact of selection bias, where individuals who were willing to enrol in a vaccine response study and submit many samples over a lengthy follow-up period were more likely also willing to engage in pro-health behaviours. The higher amounts of post-infection complications and lower post-vaccination anti-spike IgG titres6 among IDP than HV suggest that even highly selected, vaccinated and health-conscious IDP have a heightened risk for harmful consequences from SARS-CoV-2 infections. Importantly, only 13 of 63 (21%) HV lived with IDP, suggesting that~80% of our HV were not taking extra precautions because of IDP in their household. Among participants after vaccination, there was near universal agreement that a broad array of public policies should exist to limit SARS-CoV-2 transmission. The lower agreement on a range of preventive personal behaviours suggests our participants had an overall preference for reducing transmission via community-wide rather than personal approaches.

Our study had several limitations. While selection bias likely made our study population more infection-averse than the general US population, it should not have impacted the rate of post-infection outcomes, such as the propensity to develop PASC. Our participants reported being from a mostly non-Hispanic, white background, in part due to lower rates of immune disorders diagnosed in racial/ethnic minorities,24 which may limit the generalisability of our findings. In this study, we were unable to study how anti-spike IgG decay rates impacted breakthrough risk because we were unable to systematically collect samples immediately before infection. Consequently, we decided to compare infection risk at a time when our participants had the highest level of anti-spike IgG—roughly 4 weeks following vaccination—as a way to standardise and thereby control for confounding by IgG decay across participants. Realistically, antibody levels wane at different rates depending on the individual and their immune status. Thus, assuming antibody titres are an adequate correlate of protection against SARS-CoV-2, individuals with more rapid antibody decay will be at risk of breakthrough infection earlier than those whose antibody titres remain elevated longer. Finally, while we actively requested that participants submit samples 1, 6 and 12 months after every vaccination, some samples were missing, limiting analyses on post-vaccination antibody levels. Similarly, and despite frequent reminders by the study team, some participants did not submit routine saliva samples for SARS-CoV-2 monitoring. This may have resulted in an underestimation of infection rates, especially among asymptomatic individuals. Our results generalise to a mostly adult, white population of Americans with immune deficiencies. They may not extend to other populations for which we had few or no participants enrolled in the cohort.

Contextualising our results and future directions

The relationship between anti-spike IgG titres and subsequent breakthrough risk is inconsistent in the literature. While some studies of the general population find that high titres are protective against breakthrough infections, including during the Omicron era, others find just the opposite.2528 In our study, there was no evidence of an association between anti-spike IgG titres and subsequent infection. Quantifying the real-world degree of protection offered by vaccines against breakthrough infection is a difficult analytical challenge complicated by the existence of many SARS-CoV-2 variants, heterogeneous participant histories of prior infection and vaccination and underlying immune status.29 Nevertheless, we did identify an association between pre-infection anti-spike IgG titres and COVID-19 outcomes: individuals with PASC, persistent positives and those with repeat infections had lower IgG responses than matched, uninfected controls before they were infected. While PASC and viral persistence are likely related to immune deficiencies with viral clearance (ie, deficiencies impede the removal of virus and/or viral particles once infected), reinfections are likely due to pro-susceptibility factors that act prior to infection (eg, decreased antibody binding efficiency, minimal T cell clonal expansion). Further investigation is needed to understand these mechanisms, particularly whether reinfections are due to an inability to mount a sufficient immune response or whether the significantly lower pre-infection titres seen in our population experiencing reinfection were an incidental finding.

Recent findings suggest that 6.9% of the entire US population experienced PASC by the end of 2023.30 The findings suggested that those with booster doses had a lower chance of getting PASC. Weighing those probabilities by the percentages of our IDP to receive a booster dose, we would have expected 6.2% of our IDP to experience PASC. Similarly, 6.9% of the 3 million adult participants enrolled in the National COVID Cohort Collaborative had evidence of a reinfection through March 2023.31 At present, we cannot explain why we observed only 4.2% and 4.1% of our IDP cohort experienced PASC and reinfections, respectively. These lower-than-expected findings are especially surprising given that PASC and reinfections likely have a higher prevalence among all IDP in the USA compared with the population averages of 6.9%. It is possible that some of the reduction in risk observed in our IDP and HV is due to their uptake of the bivalent booster being 2.7 times the US national average.32 As the bivalent vaccines increased the immunological diversity against SARS-CoV-2 variants, this broadened response may have prevented Omicron infections from developing into PASC while also preventing subsequent exposures from burgeoning into infections. We were unable to find a population-level estimate of persistent infections in the USA with which to compare our study findings.

Clinical and policy implications

As COVID-19 variants continue to circulate, it is important for IDP and other vulnerable populations to know their risk of SARS-CoV-2 infections and resulting complications. Because most studies of the COVID-19 vaccines in IDP focus on the pre-Omicron period, our results fill a large gap in this literature by providing detailed information on the incidence and outcomes of breakthrough infections during the Omicron era. In our study, the incidence of breakthrough was almost 12 times higher during than before the Omicron era; IDP and HV exhibited largely similar breakthrough rates through mid-2023 despite IDP receiving more vaccines and continuing to take preventative steps after vaccination. Nevertheless, IDP appear more prone than immunocompetent individuals to developing post-infection complications like hospitalisations, PASC, persistent infections and reinfections.

The medical, financial and psychological burden of hospitalisation, PASC and persistent infection can be particularly costly, especially if long-term care and specialty treatment are required. Consequently, individuals with immunocompromising conditions should continue to be vigilant during respiratory virus season, receive updated vaccines when available and, in conjunction with their physicians, be aware of the increased risk they face of developing potentially long-term outcomes. Similarly, IDP should advocate for official guidance and resources from policymakers that are responsive to their additional risks. Our detailed information on the risk and outcomes of SARS-CoV-2 breakthrough infections will aid individuals with immune disorders and their health practitioners in making informed choices to reduce the likelihood of infection and anticipate possible complications should they occur.

Future directions

Ongoing work will characterise the binding and neutralising antibody levels in our IDP from enrolment through the Omicron era. In addition, we are also evaluating the crucial role of T and B cells in the induction of effector and memory responses to SARS-CoV-2 vaccination and infection. This includes studying the SARS-CoV-2 specific T cell receptor repertoire along with T cell receptor beta diversity and beta joining gene usage. Critically, we are also evaluating the B cell repertoire in our cohort, given that heavy and light chain immunoglobulin rearrangements during B cell maturation and post-vaccination somatic hypermutation are responsible for fine-tuning the SARS-CoV-2-specific immune response. Using novel sequencing techniques, we are in the process of characterising immunoglobulin heavy chain gene diversity, which will provide the most comprehensive view of bulk-expressed antibody repertoires to date.
