Identification of novel Gramicidin S analogs from Aneurinibacillus aneurinilyticus isolated from corn steep liquor with potential antifungal activity
Authors: Ksenia Lvova, Manuel Marcos, Benita Pérez-Cid, Xanel Vecino, José Manuel Cruz, Ana Belén Moldes
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn the existing literature, there is ample information available concerning the analogs constituting linear Gramicidin (Gramicidin A). However, the literature lacks information regarding the microbial production of cyclic Gramicidin S (GR-S) and the study of its analogs’ amino acid composition. Thus, in this study, GR-S was produced by Aneurinibacillus aneurinilyticus, isolated from corn steep liquor (CSL) and grown in synthetic or CSL-based medium. The extract was obtained from microbial biomass and underwent analysis with ultra performance liquid chromatography coupled with electrospray ionization tandem mass spectrometry (UPLC-ESI-MS/MS) to ascertain the presence and abundance of GR-S clusters, identifying five GR S analogs. Hence, when using a synthetic medium, the dominant cluster compromised the sequence Val-Orn-Leu-Phe-Pro-Val-Orn-Leu-Phe-Pro. Contrarily, when using CSL-based medium, the main analog was composed of Val-Orn-Leu-Phe-Pro-Val-Lys-Leu-Phe-Pro. In the remaining analogs, one or both Ornithine (Orn) residues were replaced by Lysine (Lys) or diaminobutyric acid (D-Dab). Microscopy images revealed that the use of CSL-based medium resulted in a lower yield of GR-S and a higher accumulation of vacuoles compatible with the presence of intracellular polyhydroxyalkanoates (PHA) when comparing with TSB medium. The antifungal assay revealed a superior conidial inhibition activity of GR-S extract under evaluation against Aspergillus brasiliensis in comparison to the commercial GR-S. Consequently, it can be concluded that this novel microbial GR-S extract exhibits favorable antifungal activity, positioning it as a promising candidate for antimicrobial treatments. However, further research is necessary to address the global challenge of antibiotic-resistant pathogens.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12934-025-02835-5.Keywords: New Gramicidin S, Sporulated Bacillus, Fermentation, Antibiotic, Antifungal

Introduction

Gramicidin S (GR-S) was discovered in 1942 by Gause and Brazhnikova [1] while isolating Bacillus brevis strains from Russian soils for tyrothricin preparation, similar to Dubos’ work. However, they stumbled upon a novel strain of aerobic sporulating bacillus with distinct characteristics from the Gramicidin isolated by Dubos and Hotchkiss [2]. These researchers designated the bacteria producing Gramicidin S as the Gause-Brazhnikova strain while the substance itself was termed Gramicidin S (Soviet Gramicidin).

GR-S is a cationic non-ribosomal cyclic decapeptide composed of ten amino acids: cyclo[Leu-D-Phe-Pro-Val-Orn-Leu-D-Phe-Pro-Val-Orn] (IUPAC condensed description) with a molecular weight of 1141 Da when protonated. Conversely, the linear Gramicidin variant discovered by Dubos comprises 15 amino acids, including L-Gly-L-Ala-D-Leu-L-Ala-D-Val-L-Val-D-Val-L-Trp-D-Leu-D-Leu-L-Trp-D-Leu-L-Tr, along with two variable amino acids. These variable amino acids can be either L-tryptophan, L-phenylalanine, or L-tyrosine, and L-valine or L-isoleucine.

Alenezi et al. [3] discovered that Aneurinibacillus migulanus (A. migulanus) strain Nagano produces two additional GR-S analogs with molecular weights of 1155 and 1169 Da, alongside the recognized GR-S variant weighing 1141 Da. In these analogs, one or two ornithine residues are substituted by lysine in the peptide sequence. Furthermore, the authors investigated a separate strain of A. migulanus, designated as NCTC 7096, which exclusively produces GR-S with a molecular weight of 1141 Da. These authors also noted that A. migulanus NCTC 7096 exhibited lower efficacy against plant pathogens, which could likely be attributed to the composition of GR-S that does not contain Lysine. Other researchers, such as Hori and Kurotsu [4], identified the presence of GR-S analogs in antibiotic extracts. In their study, they worked with Bacillus brevis (B. brevis) Nagano observing that, in addition to the regular GR-S, it produces an analog of the antibiotic where ornithine is substituted by Lysine. Although the linear analogs of Gramicidin are well studied, the cyclic analogs of GR-S remain little explored, especially from alternative fermentation substrates.

On the other hand, despite its efficacy as an antibacterial agent, GR-S encounters limited clinical usage due to its inherent toxicity. Regrettably, GR-S exhibits hemolytic activity not only against bacterial membranes but also against mammalian cells like erythrocytes [5]. Commonly integrated into formulations alongside other antibiotics or antiseptics, GR-S finds utility in various topical applications such as gels, creams, and sprays for treating skin infections, wounds, and burns. Its application also extends to veterinary medicine, especially in combating skin infections in animals [1].

While numerous brands offer commercial linear Gramicidin, the availability of industrially produced GR-S is more limited, often prompting the marketing of modified versions. Described as an antibiotic, it inhibits bacterial growth by binding to the hydroxyl group on the cell wall and forming an intramolecular hydrogen bond with a proton in the molecule. For instance, Biosynth markets GR-S Trifluoroacetate (TFA), a mixture of GR-S and TFA, which is the latter used during downstream processes. (https://www.biosynth.com/p/FG166334/113-73-5free-base-gramicidin-s-tfa-salt). Derivatives of GR-S are typically produced via enzymatic biosynthesis using GR-S synthetases from B. brevis. This process involves fermentation with B. brevis followed by downstream processing to obtain GR-S synthetases and enzymatic synthesis of GR-S in the presence of Valine, Ornithine, Leucine, Phenylalanine, and Proline [6]. Moreover, Boc Science and Abmole (https://www.abmole.com/products/gramicidin-s.html) offer for research purposes high-purity GR-S (> 95%) produced from B. Brevis. However, the product comes at a significant cost. Consequently, there is a clear need for more in-depth research into the microbial production of Gramicidin S, as synthetic methods remain costly and less sustainable. However, attention has recently been drawn to Gramicidin as old drug with new potentials in various diseases, including use as antitumoral and antiviral agents. For instance, Enayathullah et al. [7] have reported new findings about GR-S anti-SARS-CoV-2 activity. Additionally, Sun et al. [8] have indicated that derivatives of GR-S can bind to a protein expressed on tumor cells promoting immune response [8].

Furthermore, Chen et al. [9] have reported that Gramicidin inhibits human gastric cancer cell proliferation. Similarly, another work has shown that Gramicidin inhibits ovarian cancer cells [10].

Current research is focusing on obtaining GR-S with minimal adverse effects, potentially increasing its clinical utility. Researchers have formulated variants of GR-S to attenuate its toxicity and enhance its efficacy [11, 12]. While this approach holds promise, it would be interesting to conduct further investigations to discern any notable differences between GR-S extracts derived from various Bacillus strains regarding fermentation media, or extraction processes. Notably, in such cases, no manipulation of the antibiotic itself would be undertaken, allowing a more comprehensive understanding of its natural variations and properties.

Therefore, this study aims to search deeper into the characterization of a GR-S extract obtained from Aneurinibacillus aneurinilyticus (A. aneurinilyticus) using a sustainable extraction process optimized in a previous work [13]. The extract showed a potential antimicrobial activity against Bacillus licheniformis in comparison with commercial GR-S containing TFA, and it is necessary to analyze in more depth the composition of these GR-S extracts for further studies that promote the sustainable industrial production of antibiotics. Hence, for comparative purposes, GR-S was produced by fermentation using A. aneurinilyticus cultivated on different carbon sources (a synthetic medium, or corns steep liquor combined with glucose or starch). A rapid and selective analytical method based on ultra performance liquid chromatography-electrospray ionization tandem mass spectrometry (UPLC-ESI-MS/MS) will be used. A comprehensive characterization of the obtained extracts regarding amino acid sequence will be performed using a mMass software. To explain possible differences in GR-S production and extraction yields, images of A. aneurinilyticus biomass will be obtained by the Field Emission Scanning Electron Microscope (FESEM) and High-resolution Transmission Electron Microscopy (HRTEM) should be included in the study. Moreover, a new antimicrobial test utilizing Aspergillus brasiliensis will be developed to evaluate the potential antifungal activity of the novel GR-S extract under evaluation in comparison to commercially available GR-S containing TFA salt.

Materials and methods

Microbial production of Gramicidin (GR-S) using A. aneurinilyticus isolated from corn step liquor

GR-S producing strain of A. aneurinilyticus (CECT 9939) isolated from corn steep liquor was used for the antibiotic production [13]. To prepare inoculum, the strain was inoculated into 100 mL of the synthetic medium, Tryptic Soy Broth (TSB), which was previously sterilized in autoclave (P Selecta PRESOCLAVE II, Spain) at 121 °C during 15 min, and it was grown for 48 h at 37 °C on a rotary shaker at 150 rpm. After preculture, the microbial inoculant (1%) was used for cultivation performed in an Erlenmeyer flask in triplicate with 100 mL of fresh sterilized TSB during three and seven days under the same conditions described for inoculum preparation.

Moreover, for GR-S production, additional media (100 mL) based on CSL were prepared in triplicate using commercial CSL provided by FeedStimulants (Zoetermeer, The Netherlands). Fermented media based on CSL were prepared with 400 g/L of CSL supplemented with soluble starch (Carlo Erba, Italy) or D(+)glucose anhydrous (Scharlau, Spain) at 20 g/L. Fermentation was established following the same protocol described previously when TSB was used as nutritional medium.

Extraction of Gramicidin S (GR-S) from microbial biomass

GR-S was extracted from microbial biomass with the aid of acidic ethanol as an extractant under optimal conditions using the protocol established in the previous work [13]. For that purpose, A. aneurinilyticus cells from fermentation flasks were separated from fermentation medium by centrifugation at 5000 rpm during 20 min at 4 °C followed by two washing steps with Milli-Q water. Subsequently, biomass pellets derived from 300 mL of fermentation media underwent treatment with a solution comprising ethanol and 0.2 N HCl (9:1 v/v). The biomass used in each extraction batch corresponds with the ratio of fermented medium to solvent 3:1 (v/v). Gramicidin extraction was performed at room temperature during two hours of agitation at 300 rpm. Following the extraction process, the microbial biomass was centrifuged at 5,000 rpm at 4 °C, and the supernatant containing GR-S was collected for evaluation of the GR-S extract by sextuplicate using UPLC-ESI-MS/MS. The precipitate containing biomass was stored at 4 °C for evaluation of cell morphology.

To evaluate the antifungal capacity of ethanolic GR-S extract, the ethanol was concentrated by evaporating it at room temperature to achieve concentrations of 6 mg/mL of GR-S. Subsequently, GR-S extract was neutralized at pH 7 using 1 M NaOH and diluted with ethanol at concentrations ranging from 2 to 6 mg/mL of GR-S. As a control, commercial GR-S at 2 mg/mL diluted in ethanol was used.

Analysis of Gramicidin S (GR-S) by ultra performance liquid chromatography-electrospray ionization tandem mass spectrometry (UPLC-ESI-MS/MS)

In the first instance, GR-S in the samples was detected by mass spectrophotometry operated in positive electrospray ionization (ESI) mode in a mass range between 100 and 2,000 Da using as ion source a voltage of 4500 V with an end voltage between 300 and 500 V with two different devices: Solaris XR FT-ICR MS, Bruker Daltonics and tims-TOF mass Bruker Daltonics, Bremen, Germany. Data acquisition was performed using the FtmsControl software version 2.3.0 or timsControl software version 5.1, respectively and data processing was performed using the DataAnalysis software, version 5.2 from Bruker Daltonics.

The quantification of GR-S in the samples was then conducted using Ultra Performance Liquid Chromatography (UPLC) coupled with Electrospray Ionization- tandem Mass Spectrometry (ESI-MS/MS). Prior to UPLC analysis, the samples underwent filtration through a polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) polymer with a pore size of 0.45 μm. The UPLC analysis utilized a Zorbax Eclipse Plus Phenyl-Hexyl column (dimensions: 2.1 × 100 mm; pore size: 1.8 μm). The method was performed at 30 °C with a flow rate of 500 µL/min and an injection volume of 5 µL. The mobile phase consisted of two solvents: solvent A (Milli-Q water with 0.1% formic acid) and solvent B (acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid). The gradient program for solvent B was defined as follows: 44% from 0 to 1 min, 98% from 6.1 to 8 min, and 44% from 8.1 to 10 min. GR-S in the sample extracts was quantified by comparison with commercial GR-S TFA salt (Byosynth, Slovakia) dissolved in ethanol. During the ESI-MS/MS analysis, nitrogen gas was used as the nebulizing agent at a pressure of 8.0 bar and as the drying gas with a flow rate of 8.0 L/min at 220 °C. Quantification was carried out using TASQ 2021b software from Bruker, while sequencing was conducted with the mMass 5.5.0 program.

Microscopy analysis of A. aneurinilyticus biomass

Field Emission Scanning Electron Microscope (FESEM) and High-resolution Transmission Electron Microscopy (HRTEM) were employed to obtain the images of the biomass after gramicidin extraction from both TSB and CSL based media.

Examination under Field Emission S Microscope (FESEM)

Prior to microscopy examination, the biomass underwent two washes in distilled water. Subsequently, a pellet was obtained using a centrifuge (Micro 185, Hettich, Germany) at 14,000 rpm for five minutes. Following the washing step, the biomass was immersed and left at 4 °C for three hours in a fixative solution composed of glutaraldehyde 2.5% and cacodylate buffer 0.1 M with pH 7.2. After fixation, the samples were centrifuged (14,000 rpm, 5 min), washed three times during 10 min in a fresh solution of cacodylate buffer 0.1 M, and preserved in the same buffer at 4 °C until microscopy analysis.

The stored samples intended for FESEM observation were incubated in cacodylate buffer 0.1 M containing osmium tetroxide 2% at 4 °C for one hour and washed with fresh cacodylate buffer twice. The biomass was dehydrated by replacing water with ethanol and dried in a critical point dryer (Baltec CPD030). Subsequently, the biomass was coated with thin gold films in a sputter coater (Emitech K550X) and the images were generated by employing a Field Emission Scanning Electron Microscope (JEOLJSM6700F) equipped with a lower detector (LEI).

Examination under High-resolution Transmission Electron Microscopy (HRTEM)

For HRTEM imaging, the pre-treatment with the same fixative solution employed for FESEM visualization was followed by washing in cacodylate buffer. Subsequently, the biomass was immersed in a solution composed of cacodylate buffer (0.1 M, pH 7.2) and osmium tetroxide (2%) for one hour at 4 °C. After washing in the same buffer for one hour, the dried samples were embedded in Epon prior to cutting semi-thick sections (0.70 micron–70 nm) using an ultramicrotome (Leica Reichert Ultracuts). The ultra-fine sections were prepared and selected for viewing after uranyl and lead staining in the High-resolution Transmission Electron Microscopy (JEOLJEM1010, 80Kv) equipped with a digital camera (CCD Orius Digital Montage Plug-in camera, Gatan Inc., Gatan, CA, USA) and software (Gatan Digital Micrograph, Gatan Inc.).

Antifungal activity

The antifungal activity of GR-S extract produced by A. aneurinilyticus was established against Aspergillus brasiliensis (CECT 2574) using a spot-on-lawn method. To prepare the test plates, 500 µL of the fungal spore suspensions were inoculated into 25 mL of Sabouraud Dextrose Agar (SDA) medium after it was cooled to 45°. The inoculated medium was then poured into sterile Petri dishes and allowed to solidify.

Afterward, the SDA plates inoculated with the fungus at 37 °C were subjected to antifungal tests using 10 µL of different ethanolic formulations. These contained either the commercial GR-S at 2 mg/mL or the microbial GR-S extract, which has been concentrated up to 6 mg/mL (by evaporating ethanol at room temperature) or diluted with ethanol up to 1 mg/mL, pH in formulations was adjusted at 7 with NaOH (1 M). In the study, ethanol was also included as a negative control, representing the solvent without the antibiotic.

The inhibition area was measured with an image processing program ImageJ (version is Java 1.8.0_345), quantifying the area of clear zones [14].

Statistical treatment of data

The GR-S extracts obtained after three and seven days of fermentation were subjected to t-test, p = 0.05 for pairwise comparison.

Statistical analysis of antimicrobial data was carried out by means of the SPSS 24.0 Statistics software (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA). One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) using the Tukey-b post-hoc test (p < 0.05) was employed to establish significant differences among the experimental data compared.

Results and discussion

Identification of Gramicidin S (GR-S) analogs produced by A. aneurinilyticus using different fermentation media

In this study, TSB or CSL based media supplemented with glucose or starch were used as nutrient sources to produce GR-S by A. aneurinilyticus isolated from corn steep liquor. After the first screening, it was observed that extracts contained multiple GR-S analog clusters (see Table S1 in supporting material). The masses of different clusters of GR-S analogs were detected and corroborated by ESI-MS/MS and quantified by UPLC ESI-MS/MS. The average neutral mass of these analogs varied between 1113.3959 and 1169.5024 Da, observing 5 GR-S analogs with a similar molecular weight but with variations in their RT that ranged between 1.6 and 3.9 min. Hence, Table S1 lists molecular formula, average neutral mass, monoisotopic mass, retention time (RT), isolated ion, mandatory confirmation ions and sequence of the different analogs found in GR-S extracts. Also, Table S1 includes the sequence of amino acids provided by the sequencing with mMass 5.5.0 software. The standard molecule of GR-S is characterized by an average neutral mass of 1141.4491 Da, a monoisotopic mass of 1140.7059 Da, with a RT of 2.3 min. The analogs with neutral masses of 1113.3959 Da and 1127.4225 Da with RT of 1.7 min and 1.6-2.0 min correspond to standard GR-S molecule with the loss of 2 CH₂ and 1 CH₂ groups respectively. In contrast, the analogs with neutral masses of 1155.4758 Da and 1169.5024 Da represent GR-S standard molecule with the addition of one CH₂ and two CH₂ groups respectively. Regarding the sequence of amino acids, the standard GR-S is composed of Val-Orn-Leu-Phe-Pro-Val-Orn-Leu-Phe-Pro [5, 15].

However, different GR-S analogs with the same average neutral mass of 1141.4491 Da were also detected where Orn was replaced by D-diaminobutyric acid (D-Dab) and Lysine (Lys) respectively. These analogs were detected from the same isolated mass (571.3602 m/z) possessing the same mandatory confirmation ions but detected at different RT. In the analogs with average neutral mass of 1127.4225 Da and 1113.3959 Da, it can be deduced that Orn was substituted by one or two molecules of D-Dab, respectively. In contrast, in analogs with average neutral mass higher than 1141 Da, Orn was substituted by one or two Lys residues, corresponding to the 1155 Da and 1169 Da analogs, respectively. These amino acid substitutions in the standard GR-S molecule were also reported in GR-S analogs produced by A. migulanus [3]. D-Dab (C₄H₁₀N₂O₂) has a molecular weight of 118.07 Da, Lys (C₆H₁₄N₂O₂) weighs 149.19 Da, and Orn (C₅H₁₂N₂O₂) weighs 132.16 Da. The molecular weight differences between D-Dab, Lys, and Orn align with the loss or addition of CH₂ groups mentioned earlier. D-Dab, a type of diamino acid like Lys and Orn, is not found in proteins but it is commonly observed in peptide antibiotics produced through fermentation processes [16].

The extracts were obtained after centrifugation of biomass and extraction with ethanol following the procedure established by Lvova et al. [13] in a previous work. The results show that a higher concentration of GR-S was produced in the synthetic media compared to CSL-based media. Figure S1 (Supplementary material) displays the MS and MS/MS spectra of commercial GR-S along with one of its analogs with an average neutral mass of 1141.4491 Da and 1155.4758 Da. In contrast, Figure S2 (Supplementary material) presents the spectra for analogs with average neutral mass of 1169.5024, 1127.4225, and 1113.3959 Da. In all MS spectra, the isolated ion mass and mandatory confirmation ions are observed, included in Table S1 (included in Supporting material).

In the GR-S extract obtained from biomass grown in TSB, the predominant analog had an average molecular weight of 1141 Da, followed by analogs with average weights 1155, 1127, 1169, and 1113 Da. However, when CSL was used as a fermentation medium, A. aneurinilyticus produced extracts where the primary analog with average weight of 1155 Da with the following by average 1141, 1169, 1127, and 1113 Da of analogs. Minimal differences in the quantity or type of GR-S were observed when glucose was replaced by starch in the CSL-based media. Similarly, Alenezi et al. [3] identified GR-S-like molecules with molecular weights of 1155 and 1169 Da in A. migulanus extracts, which exhibited surfactant activity. These molecules were also detected in commercial GR-S by LC-MS by the same authors. The surfactant activity of the cell-bound extract produced by A. aneurinilyticus was previously demonstrated in earlier studies [17, 18]. Moreover, Bouchard et al. [19] reported that Gramicidin A, obtained from Fluka Chemika-Biochemika-Analytica (Buchs, CH), exhibited various analogs with molecular weights ranging from 1812 to 1843 Da. These variations were also attributed to the variation of aminoacidic chain regarding the presence of valine or isoleucine, as well as changes in molecular weight caused by the binding of Na+.

Additionally, Fig. 1 illustrates the percentage distribution of GR-S analogs in the extract across different media, showing a consistent presence of GR-S-like analogs among the various fermentation media. In TSB medium, A. aneurinilyticus produced 32.8% GR-S (1141 Da), 28.5% GR-S (1155 Da), 21.1% GR-S (1127 Da), 11.5% GR-S (1169 Da), and 6.8% GR-S (1113 Da). In contrast, the CSL-based media yielded 39.7–40.1% GR-S (1155 Da), 26.8–28.2% GR-S (1141 Da), 14.7–15.8% GR-S (1169 Da), 13.3–13.4% GR-S (1127 Da), and 4.0-4.1% GR-S (1113 Da) observing a negligible effect on the qualitative distribution of GR-S analogs regarding TSB-based media.

Some researchers have reported that extracts containing Lys exhibit increased antibiotic activity. For example, Tamaki et al. [20] discovered that a cyclopeptide composed of 12 amino acids, cycle(-Val-Orn-Leu-D-Phe-Pro-D-Lys-)2, which is similar to GR-S but includes an additional Lys residue, demonstrated double the antibiotic activity of GR-S. Furthermore, Alenezi et al. [3] found that GR-S extracts containing Lys also exhibited enhanced activity. Based on this information, it can be speculated that GR-S extracts produced during intermediate fermentation time in TSB, as well as those from CSL-based media, may possess higher antimicrobial activity than extracts obtained from biomass grown in TSB after longer fermentation time, as the latter contain a lower percentage of GR-S analogs with Lys (1155 Da and 1169 Da), as can be observed in Fig. 1.

Bouchard et al. [19] have also observed that Gramicidin A presented different analogs with the molecular weights between 1843 and 1812 Da depending on the presence of Valine and/or Isoleucine, where variations in molecular weight were due to the bind of Na +.

Microscopy analysis of A. aneurinilyticus biomass

Cells of A. aneurinilyticus cultured in TSB or CSL-based media and subjected to Gramicidin extraction with ethanol were examined via electron microscopy to investigate morphological changes influenced by the nature of the fermentation media. Figure 2 presents FESEM images of cells from both TSB and CSL-based media post-extraction highlighting a higher cell density in TSB compared to CSL. The CSL medium appears to contain solid particles that obscure the cells, offering greater protection and limiting the accessibility of the extractant. Additionally, the cell membranes in TSB-grown bacilli exhibit a rougher texture than those grown in CSL, suggesting increased metabolic activity associated with GR-S production. These membrane alterations in cells grown in TSB may facilitate the permeation and release of GR-S into the extracellular environment during ethanol extraction. This observation aligns with Berditsch et al. [21], who reported enhanced GR-S production in A. migulanus cells displaying a rough membrane phenotype.

FESEM photomicrographs of A. aneurinilyticus cells growth in TSB (A) or CSL-based media (B) at 37 °C after seven days of fermentation

In TSB-grown cells, both longitudinal and cross-sectional views (Fig. 2) revealed nanostructures consistent with GR-S clusters similar to those reported byBerditsch et al. [21] for A. migulanus. The authors observed that during the initial stages of Gramicidin biosynthesis, electron-dense structures are primarily located near the plasma membrane within the periplasmic space with Gramicidin nano-globules later expanding laterally and apically.

In this work, the TSB-grown cells (Fig. 3) exhibited thinner and less defined membranes compared to the CSL-grown cells, which likely promotes the permeation of GR-S and accounts for the higher extraction yield in ethanol from TSB-grown cells. After extraction, the TSB-grown cells showed external membrane damage, releasing plasmatic material (Fig. 3D-F), indicating they are more susceptible to physical changes provoked by acidic ethanol extraction of GR-S than those grown in CSL media. However, cells exhibit remarkable resilience in acidic ethanol, where they retain practically all their structural integrity. However, it is noteworthy that cells undergoing growth on TSB exhibit more pronounced alterations. Moreover, observing the FESEM images in Fig. 2, it is evident that Bacillus growth on CSL exhibits a more homogeneous size of approximately 2 μm. Conversely, when it is grown in TSB, the size can be significantly higher and heterogeneous, reaching up to 8 –10 μm in some cells. Furthermore, from the HRTEM images in Fig. 3, it can be observed that only approximately 8.6% of cut cells growth in TSB (Fig. 3A) present well defined structures matching with presence of vacuoles, whereas approximately 36.0% of cut cells growth in CSL (Fig. 3G) present a clear presence of vacuoles compatible with PHA storage.

HRTEM photomicrographs of: A. aneurinilyticus cells grown in TSB, after seven days of fermentation at 37 °C, before (A-C) and after extraction of GR-S (D-E); and grown in CSL before (G-I) and after extraction of GR-S (J-L)

Nampuak and Tongkhao [22], in their observations, utilized SEM and TEM to examine Bacillus cereus cells under various conditions. They noticed the formation of holes on the cell surface after treatment with okra mucilage [22]. In reference to A. aneurinilyticus cells cultivated in CSL-based media, a significantly higher number of vacuoles was observed, which is likely associated with the storage of polyhydroxyalkanoate (PHA) (Fig. 3G-I). The PHA production in A. migulanus cells in nutrient-restrictive conditions along with fewer nanogranules of GR-S was found by Berditsch et al. [21]. Other studies have also reported that PHA-producing bacteria grow in nutrient-deficient environments and tend to accumulate these polymers in poor growth conditions [23, 24]. This phenomenon can occur in CSL-based media, encouraging the production of this polymer while suppressing GR-Synthesis. It can explain the lower concentration of GR-S produced by A. aneurinilyticus in CSL-based media compared to TSB. Additionally, the spores were detected in both TSB and CSL media, though they were more clearly distinguishable in the latter. In CSL-grown cells, after the antibiotic extraction by ethanol, structures can be observed resembling endospores and exospores, respectively. These sporulated structures are characterized by a thin cortex, an interspace zone, and an abundant core region containing nucleoid material, forming the central zone. Sporulation in Bacillus involves asymmetric cell division, leading to the formation of two distinct cell types: the endospore and the mother cell, which aligns with the images in Figs. 3J-L. The endospore coat, a multilayered shell that protects the bacterial genome during stressful conditions, consists of numerous proteins [25].

Antifungal activity of Gramicidin S (GR-S) analogs produced by A. aneurinilyticus

GR-S is known for its efficacy against bacteria, although its antifungal activity is less well established with scarce publications. Hence in this work, tests were made of the antifungal activity of GR-S extracts produced by A. aneurinilyticus against Aspergillus brasiliensis in comparison with commercial GR-S. Since the antibiotic has lower efficacy against fungi than against bacteria, any enhancement in its antifungal activity would be more evident. Figures 4 includes the areas of clear zones observed for Aspergillus brasiliensis cultivated on SDA after 48–65 h at different concentrations of GR-S extracts produced by A. aneurinilyticus in comparison with commercial GR-S at 2 mg/mL.

Antifungal test of GR-S extract (GR-Extract) produced by A. aneurinilyticus, after 7 days fermentation at 37 °C, compared with commercial GR-S against Aspergillus brasiliensis grown on SDA during 48 h (A) or 24 and 65 h (C) together with their plates showing inhibition zones at 48 h (B) and 65 h (D). Different Latin letters within the same graphic indicate significant differences among the results according to the Tukey-b test (p < 0.05) for one-way ANOVA analysis

The first assay involved performing an antimicrobial assay over a 48-hour incubation period (Fig. 4A, B). This assay employed two concentrations of microbial derived GR-S extracts from A. aneurnlyticus, at 2 mg/mL and 6 mg/mL, in conjunction with the commercial GR-S at 2 mg/mL. The results of Fig. 4 (A and C), related to antifungal test of GR-S extract compared with commercial GR-S, were statistically compared by means of the one-way ANOVA analysis (Tukey-b post hoc test; p < 0.05) and significant differences were found (p values always close to 0.000) between the clear zone (cm2) obtained with the commercial GR-S and GR-S extract, the last case obtaining higher inhibition zones for all incubation times tested (24, 48 and 65 h). This fact demonstrates that the GR-S extract obtained in this work has a superior antifungal activity regarding the commercial GR-S. In addition, GR-S extracts at 1 and 2 mg/L provided similar inhibition results (clear zone in cm2) at both 24 and 56 h of incubation time, indicating that GR-S extract concentrations higher than 1 mg/mL do not significantly improve the inhibition results of the microorganism in the range studied.

Figure 4 shows a clear decrease in conidia formation in presence of GR-S, which is indicative of inhibited fungal growth and reproduction. The concentration of GR-S employed in the present study falls within the range utilized by Kurata and coworkers [26]. These researchers employed formulas containing 5 mg/mL of GR-S extract in methanol, although with a higher volume of antibiotic (50 µL) compared to 10 µL used in the present study.

Recently, some authors [27] have claimed the value of GR-S analogs as a resilient scaffold for the design of next generation antimicrobial peptides, which have improved therapeutic indices and potency against resistant pathogens. This aligns with the results obtained in the current study, but obtaining the GR-S analogs through fermentation rather than chemical synthesis.

Conclusions

Ultra performance liquid chromatography coupled with electrospray ionization tandem mass spectrometry (UPLC-ESI-MS/MS) analysis using mMass software was successfully employed to investigate the conformational structure of GR-S analogs produced by A. aneurinilyticus grown in TSB or CSL and extracted with ethanol. Five GR-S clusters were identified, characterized by molecular weights of 1169, 1155, 1141, 1127, and 1113 Da, each consisting of ten amino acids: Val, Leu, Phe, and Pro as fixed components, with Orn being variable and potentially replaced by Lys or Dab, providing twelve different molecular structures, associated to five GR-S analogs.

The presence of these clusters was independent of the nature of fermented medium used (TSB or CSL), although a reduced production rate was noted in CSL-based media. A distinct phenotypic variation was observed in A. aneurinilyticus grown in TSB versus CSL media; TSB cultures displayed rougher cells, while CSL cultures had softer cells and showed increased production of PHA, which inhibited GR-S metabolism. Despite this, the qualitative production of Gramicidin analogs remained relatively stable across both phenotypes, while the primary difference was the lower yield of GR-S in cells grown in CSL-based media. In reference to the antifungal activity, the microbial produced GR-S extract, derived from A. aneurinilyticus, isolated from CSL, exhibited a larger inhibition conidia area against A. brasiliensis compared to the commercial GR-S containing TFA at the same antibiotic concentration. Therefore, this study not only enhances our understanding of GR-S biosynthesis, providing insights into its molecular structures and the impact of different media on its production yield, but also demonstrates a superior antifungal activity of GR-S analogs produced from A. aneurinilyticus compared to commercial GR-S.

In conclusion, microbial GR-S produced by A. aneurinilyticus, isolated from CSL, and its analogs demonstrate promising antimicrobial activity, likely influenced by the presence of structurally related GR-S analogs that may act synergistically. Although the precise contribution of individual analogs or specific amino acid substitutions to the overall antimicrobial effect remains unclear, the complexity of separating and evaluating each GR-S analog poses significant technical and economic challenges, particularly for industrial scale production. Hence, future studies would be necessary to elucidate the relationship between structure and antimicrobial and cytotoxicity activity of the new GR-S analogs discovered in this work, possibly involving synthetic analog generation or advanced separation process, that could offer deeper insight for the development of more effective antibiotics.
