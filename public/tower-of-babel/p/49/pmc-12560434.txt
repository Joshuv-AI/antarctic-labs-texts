Investigation of the effects of different rapid palatal expansion methods on the nasal passage and pharyngeal region
Authors: Gizem Y. Özen, İsmail Ceylan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aimed to analyze the effects of tooth, tooth-bone, and bone-supported RME (rapid maxillary expansion) appliances on the nasomaxillary complex and nasopharyngeal area among individuals with maxillary transverse stenosis using CBCT (cone-beam computed tomography) and rhinomanometry. It also comparatively assessed the effects of these expansion methods.MethodsThe study used the CBCT images and rhinomanometry records of 45 patients with maxillary transversal stenosis, aged between 10 and 17 years, who underwent RME with Hyrax, hybrid, and mini-screw appliances. It also collected records from these patients at three stages: at the beginning of treatment, after active expansion, and after a 3-month retention. The study used CBCT images for dentoalveolar, skeletal, nasal, and pharyngeal measurements and evaluated rhinomanometry records for nasal airway resistance.ResultsCBCT recordings showed no significant within-group differences in measures of changes in pharyngeal and nasal volumes, whereas there were significant increases for these measures between recording periods in all RME groups. Furthermore, these increases remained mostly stable at the end of the retention period. Assessments of rhinomanometry recordings showed no significant within-group differences, whereas nasal resistance decreased during the inspiratory and expiratory phases in all RME groups, with some significant differences between periods. However, after retention, nasal resistance generally tended to return to baseline values.ConclusionsThe study revealed that the tooth, tooth-bone, and bone-supported RME appliances significantly increased the nasopharyngeal volume, and decreased nasal resistance.Trial registrationThe compliance of the study with the ethical rules was unanimously approved by the Atatürk University Faculty of Medicine Clinical Research Ethics Committee, with the decision dated September 30, 2021, and numbered B.30.2.ATA.0.01.00/378.

Background

Malocclusions can manifest in any of the three spatial planes of the dentofacial complex and are influenced by a combination of congenital, hereditary, and environmental factors. As an integral component of this system, the maxilla is susceptible to both structural and positional alterations resulting from these influences. Maxillary transverse deficiency is among the most common skeletal discrepancies involving the maxilla and is frequently associated with a narrow palate accompanied by anterior and/or posterior crossbites [1]. In the etiology of maxillary transverse deficiency, factors such as mouth breathing, atypical swallowing, postural retroposition, atypical sucking habits, low tongue posture, tongue thrust, and airway obstructions or narrowings caused by conditions like allergic rhinitis and nasal polyps play a role [2–4].

Rapid maxillary expansion (RME) is the most widely adopted treatment modality for correcting maxillary transverse deficiency [5]. Angell introduced the rapid maxillary expansion treatment in 1860 [6]. However, the modern RME treatment began with the appliance invented by Haas and named after him [5]. Subsequently, Biederman introduced the Hyrax appliance, a more hygienic alternative to the Haas expander, which gained popularity among clinicians [7]. Ludwing et al. proposed the use of a hybrid Hyrax appliance in the mixed dentition stage, which is applied with support from the permanent first molar teeth in the posterior region and two mini-screws inserted on the palate in the anterior region [8]. Mommaerts introduced the rapid maxillary expansion procedure using a bone-supported transpalatal distractor, and the orthodontic practice began to utilize these appliances more frequently [9]. The bone-anchored maxillary expander (BAME) has recently emerged as one of the most commonly used devices in this category [10], has recently become the most widely used appliance. Several studies in the literature have analyzed the effects of RME on the nasopharyngeal complex and nasal resistance [11–14]. As a result, they reported an increase in nasal [11, 15–17] and pharyngeal volume [15–18] and a decrease in nasal resistance [11, 12, 14, 19, 20]. The downward movement of the palate and nasal base due to rapid maxillary expansion increases the nasal cavity [5, 21–24]. This volumetric gain has been shown to enhance nasal breathing capacity and airflow, with several studies reporting sustained benefits post-retention [21, 23, 25]. Hershey et al. [26] stated that RME increased nasal capacity and decreased nasal airway resistance (45–53%); additionally, such a decrease remained stable at the end of the retention period. They also reported an improvement in the nasal septum [26]. Ribeiro et al. identified a volumetric increase in the nasal cavity and nasopharynx after an RME appliance [17]. However, Hartgerink et al. found an RME decrease in nasal airway resistance, though individual variances made it impossible to anticipate the effects [27]. Additionally, RME has been associated with improved blood oxygenation and favorable effects on nocturnal enuresis, potentially due to its impact on nasal volume and airway resistance [28–30].

Although several studies have investigated the effects of rapid maxillary expansion (RME) on the nasomaxillary complex, most have focused on a single appliance type and either volumetric or functional outcomes alone.

There is a lack of studies that simultaneously assess both airway volume and nasal resistance across different RME modalities using standardized methods.

To address this gap, the present study compares the effects of three expansion types—tooth-borne (Hyrax), tooth–bone–borne (hybrid), and bone-borne—on upper airway volumes (nasopharyngeal, retropalatal, retroglossal, nasal) via CBCT and nasal resistance via rhinomanometry.

These appliances were selected due to their distinct anchorage mechanics, which may lead to differing skeletal and airway responses.

Methods

This study was funded by the Atatürk University Scientific Research Coordination Center with the Scientific Research Project code TDK-2021–10015. The compliance of the study with the ethical rules was unanimously approved by the Atatürk University Faculty of Medicine Clinical Research Ethics Committee, with the decision dated September 30, 2021, and numbered B.30.2.ATA.0.01.00/378. Clinical trial number: not applicable.

The study used CBCT images and rhinomanometry records of 45 patients who applied to the Atatürk University Faculty of Dentistry, Department of Orthodontics, with a request for treatment and met the criteria defined below. These patients were divided into three groups to carry out the research, and each group consisted of 15 patients, regardless of gender. The study implemented the Hyrax expansion appliance (Fig. 1) in the first group, the hybrid expansion appliance (Fig. 2) in the second group, and the mini-screw supported expansion appliance (Fig. 3) in the third group. Of these patients, a total of three CBCT and rhinomanometry records were taken in the following order: before treatment (T0), after active expansion (T1), and after the 3-month retention period (T2). The study group consisted of patients who met the following criteria: they had maxillary transverse stenosis, were between the ages of 10 and 17, had never had orthodontic treatment, did not experience any craniofacial anomaly of congenital and/or genetic origin, did not have a neuromuscular disease, did not complain about pain.Fig. 1Hyrax Expansion ApplianceFig. 2Hybrid Expansion Appliance and Screw positions of Hybrid Expansion Appliance

Researchers informed the patients not to rotate the appliance on the very implementation day and trained the patient’s parents on tightening the expansion screw after the implementation process. Accordingly, in the first five days, the screw was asked to be expanded a 2-quarter turn, and at the end of the 5th day (10-quarter turn), an occlusal film was taken to check whether there was any gap in the suture palatine media. Five days later, patients who had a gap in the palatine media suture were called for control. They were instructed to expand the screw a 2-quarter turn a day until it reached the 20-quarter turn, and they were called for recontrol. Patients were also instructed to expand the screw 1-quarter turn per day from the 20-quarter to the 40-quarter turn, and they were called for control every 10-quarter turn. After the expansion, they fixed the RME screw and followed the 3-month retention period.

Researchers had the tomography records using the “NewTom VGİ-EVO 3D CBCT” device (NewTom FP, Quantitative Radiology, Milan, Italy) at Atatürk University, Faculty of Dentistry, Department of Oral and Maxillofacial Radiology. Data were obtained in all patients using the standard cone-beam technique at 110 kVp (kilovolt peak) and 3.00 mA (milliampere). The exposure duration was 1.8 seconds, and the focal spot was 0.3 mm (milimeter). Researchers acquired the images from all patients in a 19 × 24 FOV (field of view) area by rotating the device in a 360-degree circle around the patient’s head and neck region. The ‘Dolphin Imaging Software’ application, which is accessible at the Department of Orthodontics, Atatürk University Faculty of Dentistry, was utilized to assess the images. The “Homoth Rhino 4000” device (Homoth Medizinelektronik GmbH and Co. KG - Leibnizstraße 13–15, 24568 Kaltenkirchen, Germany) positioned in the Ear, Nose, and Throat Diseases outpatient clinic of Atatürk University, Faculty of Medicine, was used to obtain rhinometry recordings.

Based on the reference ‘Long-term effects of mini-screw–assisted rapid palatal expansion on airway,’ it has been calculated using the G*Power program that to achieve statistical significance for the difference in nasopharyngeal volume (mm³) between T1 (3412.89 ± 1425.84 mm³) and T3 (4771.17 ± 1135.28 mm³) with 80% power and 95% confidence level, each group should include 10 patients. Considering a 20% dropout rate, it was decided to include 12 patients in each group.

Twenty-one randomly selected patients (seven from each group) had their tomographic record measurements (T0/T1/T2) and rhinomanometry records and measurements (T0/T1/T2) repeated four weeks and two weeks later, respectively. Intra-observer agreement (reliability) was analyzed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). The study considered the following correlation levels as excellent (>0.90), good (0.90 − 0.75), moderate (0.75 − 0.50), and poor (< 0.50) correlation in the analysis [31]. As a result, the intra-observer agreement test (ICC) used in the study revealed that the results were excellent (>0.90). The Houston analysis [32] also concluded that the measurements were repeatable without any significant error. Houston analysis, defined by Houston and colleagues, evaluates the statistical difference between data obtained at two different time points while taking measurement error into account.

Results

Discussion

This study found no significant intergroup difference based on RME considering the measurements (nasal, nasopharyngeal, retropalatal, retroglossal, and total respiratory tract volumes) to evaluate the changes in nasal and pharyngeal respiratory tract volume. All three RME groups had significant increases throughout the active expansion period, according to an analysis of the data for intra-group changes on the measurements in question. Despite some partial reversals over the retention period, these increases have remained mainly stable. This indicates that the volumetric gains are evident in the short term, but longer follow-up periods are needed to assess long-term stability. These short-term changes may be influenced by tissue remodeling or transient soft tissue adaptation, both of which require longer observation to determine permanence.

The literature review provably revealed that RME dramatically increases nasal and pharyngeal airway volume and dimensions, and thus, patients might breathe nasally instead of orally [21, 33] Kim et al. also reported that RME with mini-screw support significantly increased nasal, nasopharyngeal, and total pharyngeal volume at the end of the retention period [15].

Smith et al. assessed the changes in nasal and nasopharyngeal volume on CBCT recordings after maxillary expansion using the Hyrax expander. Accordingly, they observed a significant increase in nasal and nasopharyngeal volume, an insignificant decrease in oropharyngeal volume, and a negligible increase in hypopharyngeal volume [34].

Evaluating the changes in nasal and pharyngeal volume on CBCT recordings after mini-screw-assisted RME, Li et al. identified substantial increases in nasal and nasopharyngeal volumes, slight increases in retropalatal and retroglossal volumes, and an insignificant decrease in hypopharyngeal volume [16].

Ribeiro et al. studied changes in nasal dimensions and naso/oropharyngeal volume after bonded RME appliances in the mixed dentition period. Accordingly, they found that while there were insignificant changes in other parts of the nasal cavity and the nasopharyngeal volume, there were significant increases in the lower one-third of the nasal cavity and oropharyngeal volumes [17].

DiCosimo et al. used CBCT recordings to evaluate the changes in total nasal volume and naso/oropharyngeal volume in 28 patients who underwent RME with a hyrax-type device and compared the outcomes with a control group. As a result, all measurements displayed a substantial increase in the study group, and for both total nasal volume and nasopharyngeal volume, these increases were significantly higher than in the control group [18].

Erdur et al. used CBCT recordings to study the alterations in pharyngeal volume among patients who underwent symmetric and asymmetric expansion with the bonded RME appliances. Accordingly, they observed significant increases in upper and total pharyngeal volume but insignificant increases in lower pharyngeal volume in both expansion types [13].

Iwasaki et al. reported significant increases in total pharyngeal and retropalatal volume and insignificant increases in oropharyngeal volume after RME appliances. Based on a comparison, they concluded that the increase in total and retropalatal volumes in the RME group was significantly higher than those of the control group [35].

Niu et al. analyzed the effects of RME on nasal and pharyngeal volume in comparison with a control group and reported that all measurements (nasal, total pharyngeal, naso/velo/oropharyngeal volume) increased significantly in the RME group; however, there was no significant difference, except for nasal volume, when compared with the control group [36].

Smith et al. reported insignificant decreases in oropharyngeal and hypopharyngeal volume, and aside from this finding, the results of the current study are essentially in parallel with the literature on nasal and pharyngeal volume. An overall assessment of the current study findings observably revealed that inspiration and expiration resistances decreased. These findings indicate that all three RME techniques considerably lessen respiratory resistance and substantially relieve breathing. However, it is unclear whether this reduction in resistance is due to permanent structural changes or temporary tissue adaptation.

The findings of the present study align closely with previous literature in demonstrating increases in both nasal and pharyngeal airway volumes. However, a notable limitation in the existing body of research is that most studies have assessed only one or, at best, two types of expansion appliances. Furthermore, investigations that concurrently evaluate both nasal and pharyngeal airway regions remain scarce; these anatomical domains are typically examined in isolation. For example, many studies have focused exclusively on either tooth-borne or bone-borne expanders, thereby limiting the scope for comprehensive comparative analysis. In contrast, the present study uniquely investigates three distinct RME modalities—tooth-borne, tooth–bone–borne (hybrid), and bone-borne—within the same patient cohort. By employing CBCT imaging to assess both nasal and pharyngeal airway volumes simultaneously, this study provides a more integrated perspective and contributes original and valuable insight to the orthodontic literature.

DiVece et al. rhinomanometrically evaluated the changes resulting from RME (Hyrax) in 30 patients aged between 7 and 11 and determined a significant decrease in total nasal inspiratory and expiratory resistance and nasopharyngeal obstruction. They also emphasized that RME critically impacts nasal resistance; hence, patients with mild to moderate respiratory problems could benefit from this technique by improving their upper airway patency [20]. Hershey et al. reported that RME reduced nasal resistance and that this decrease remained stable during the 3-month retention period [26]. As a result, the current study findings are fully consistent with the significant reduction achieved in respiratory resistance during the inspiration and expiration phases. Matsumoto et al. reported that RME decreased nasal resistance in the short term (3 months), whereas this resistance increased again in the long term (30 months). They also remarked that RME has unstable healing effects on nasal resistance; hence, it is not the only treatment option for patients with respiratory issues and that multidisciplinary treatment approaches would be more beneficial for these patients [30]. In nasal resistance, the results of the present study are compatible with Matsumoto et al. for the three-month retention phase but are incompatible with the long-term (2.5-year) findings. This data indicates that short-term results in nasal resistance may be misleading, requiring long-term analyses to make more accurate and precise conclusions. Ottaviano et al. studied anterior rhinomanometry recordings in patients with mixed dentition who underwent RME and found that this procedure potentially improved nasal respiratory capacity; however, nasal resistance did not significantly improve at the end of the retention period (6 months) [14]. After analyzing two distinct patient groups who underwent RME/SARME (SARME: surgically assisted rapid maxillary expansion) with an acrylic-bonded appliance using acoustic rhinometry, Babacan et al. reported that both expansion methods substantially increased nasal volume and decreased nasal resistance [11]. In 46 individuals aged between 12 and 14, Gökçe et al. used acoustic rhinometry to assess the changes in nasal volume and minimum cross-section area using tooth-supported (Hyrax), dental tissue-supported (traditionally bonded), and bone-supported (mini screw) RME. Accordingly, they reported that all three RME approaches significantly increased nasal volume and minimum cross-section area [37].

Sökücü et al. analyzed the effects of RME and Fan-type RME on nasal volume and minimum cross-sectional area (MCA) using acoustic rhinometry and reported that both methods substantially increased the considered parameters; however, the effects of RME remained more stable [38].

Bazargani et al. found that the bone-supported expansion appliance yielded more nasal airflow and had lower nasal resistance values than the tooth-supported appliance when comparing the effects of the two appliances using acoustic rhinometry [39]. The current study’s findings on increased nasal airflow and decreased nasal resistance are consistent with those reported by Bazargani et al. [39].

Baratieri et al. systematically reviewed the studies, focusing on RME-based analysis of the nasal structure, nasal airflow, and nasal resistance. As a result, using rhinomanometry analysis, they generally found a decrease in nasal respiratory tract resistance and an increase in total nasal airflow. They also reported an increase in nasal cavity volume and minimal cross-sectional area based on acoustic rhinometry measurements [12]. Considering the parameters on this topic, the current study findings align with the existing literature.

Most studies in the literature have been limited to evaluating only one or, at most, two types of expansion appliances, and nasal resistance assessments have typically relied on indirect or subjective methods. As a result, the number of studies utilizing rhinomanometry remains remarkably low. However, rhinomanometry is a non-invasive, rapid, and dynamic diagnostic tool that allows for objective measurement of nasal airflow resistance in real time. The originality of the present study lies not only in its inclusion of three distinct RME modalities—tooth-borne, tooth–bone–borne (hybrid), and bone-borne—but also in its use of rhinomanometry, a technique rarely employed in previous research. By integrating this underutilized diagnostic method, our study provides a more comprehensive comparison of the short-term effects of different expansion protocols on nasal resistance, offering clinically relevant insights that enhance the current orthodontic literature.

The follow-up period in this study was limited to only 3 months; therefore, the long-term stability of the observed changes in nasal and pharyngeal airway volumes could not be evaluated. Additionally, the study relied solely on CBCT and anterior rhinomanometry for assessment, excluding other functional diagnostic tools such as acoustic rhinometry or nasal spirometry. This may have limited the comprehensiveness of the physiological analysis of the upper airway. Furthermore, the evaluations were conducted exclusively on individuals aged 10 to 17 years, which restricts the generalizability of the findings to adult populations.

Conclusion

This study comparatively evaluated the effects of three different rapid maxillary expansion (RME) methods on nasal and pharyngeal airway volumes and nasal resistance. The findings revealed significant volumetric increases and resistance reductions across all groups. However, these changes were more pronounced and stable in the mini-screw-supported (bone-borne) expander group, suggesting that mini-screws may offer a more effective skeletal response. The study contributes original value to the literature by incorporating both objective measurement tools (CBCT and rhinomanometry) and multiple expander types. Nevertheless, future studies with longer follow-up periods are needed to assess the long-term outcomes.
