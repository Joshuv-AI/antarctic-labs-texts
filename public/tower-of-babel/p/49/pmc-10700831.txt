Internal mammary artery injury complicating a Surfacer inside-out central venous catheterization
Authors: Kihoon Bohle, Bright Benfor, Simon J Montelongo, Dylan E Brooks, Eric Peden
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe report the case of a near fatal arterial injury in a patient undergoing an inside-out catheter placement through an occluded central venous system using the Surfacer device (Bluegrass Vascular). The right internal mammary artery was inadvertently lacerated during the procedure, leading to cardiovascular collapse. The patient was rescued by transfusion, placement of a chest tube, and coil embolization of the right internal mammary artery. Postprocedure analysis of intraoperative cone beam computed tomography revealed that this injury was predictable on imaging, underscoring the need for advanced imaging guidance to enhance the safety of this procedure.Keywords: Inside-out, Surfacer, ESRD, Central venous catheter, TDC, CVC complications, Hemothorax, RIMA, Dialysis access, Hemodialysis

Thoracic central venous obstruction (TCVO) is common in patients with end-stage renal disease related to use of central venous catheters.1, 2, 3 First-line treatment usually involves balloon angioplasty with or without stenting.4 However, in some cases, attempts at guidewire recanalization can fail. The Surfacer inside-out technique (SIO) has been designed to achieve right-sided tunneled dialysis catheter (TDC) placement through occluded veins.5 The reported technical success ranges from 90% to 97%,5,6 and device-related complications have not yet been reported. Although not part of the instructions for use for the Surfacer device (Bluegrass Vascular), our center routinely uses intraoperative cone beam angiography to enhance the safety of the procedure.7 The present case report illustrates a rare, but potentially fatal, complication of the Surfacer procedure. The patient provided written informed consent for the report of her case details and imaging studies.

Case report

A 71-year-old woman with end-stage renal disease presented to our institution for access creation. She had prior thrombosed bilateral arm accesses and a history of adhesions after laparotomy, reducing her candidacy for peritoneal dialysis. Her only functional access remained a right femoral TDC.

Preoperative venography showed a type 3 TCVO extending from the confluence of the brachiocephalic veins to the subclavian veins bilaterally, with an occluded left brachiocephalic vein stent. Previous attempts at both antegrade and retrograde conventional guidewire recanalization failed. Thus, the patient was recommended for a Surfacer procedure to be followed later by hemodialysis reliable outflow graft placement.

The procedure was performed under general anesthesia in a hybrid suite. A left femoral arterial line was placed for monitoring, and a left femoral venous sheath was placed for injection during planned on-table cone beam computed tomography (CBCT). The TDC in the right femoral vein was used to achieve access into the superior vena cava (SVC) and was replaced with a 12F reinforced sheath. After performing a cavogram, the workstation sheath of the Surfacer system was inserted to engage the SVC stump, and a venogram was obtained to delineate the anatomy of the occlusion (Fig 1, A). Next, the device was inserted (Fig 1, B) and advanced to the level of the occlusion (Fig 1, C). CBCT was then performed to map out the trajectory. This showed the device to be aiming for the subclavian artery (Fig 2, A-C). It was, therefore, repositioned by withdrawing the Surfacer device and reengaging the workstation sheath in a different portion of the SVC stump. The Surfacer device was passed to the tip of the workstation sheath, and a second CBCT scan showed a more anterior projection that avoided the subclavian artery (Fig 2, D-F). A radiopaque target was then placed on the skin at the supraclavicular region, and the device was advanced in a straight line under three-dimensional and two-dimensional fusion guidance (ie, CBCT overlayed on live fluoroscopy) up to the level of the clavicle. Next, the needle wire guide was set to the appropriate angle and the needle wire was projected toward the target until it pierced through the skin to achieve through and through access. A 5F sheath was then placed over the externalized wire, and a pull-back venogram showed contrast entering the pleural space but no obvious arterial enhancement. Because of the pleural space entry, we decided to use a lower profile catheter, which was placed over the externalized wire with the use of an 8F peel-away sheath.

After removal of the peel-away sheath, more bleeding than usual was encountered at the wound, and the patient became hypotensive at 37/29 mm Hg, with fluoroscopy showing a large right hemothorax (Fig 3, A). At this point, wire access had been given up; thus, we passed a wire through the port catheter and planned readvancement of a larger sheath but inadvertently lost wire access. Using our sheath from the femoral access, an occlusion balloon was placed in the SVC for hemostasis (Fig 3, A), thinking that perhaps this was bleeding from the SVC into the pleural space. However, the balloon had no effect on the hypotension. Fluids and vasopressors were concomitantly started, 900 mL of packed red blood cells was transfused, and a chest tube was urgently placed, evacuating a large quantity of blood.

Fluoroscopy and angiography showing arterial injury. A, Hemothorax of right lung with placement of chest tube and balloon. B, Right internal mammary artery extravasation (black arrow). C,D, Successful coil embolization (black arrow).

Next, the left femoral arterial line was replaced with a sheath, and an aortogram was performed, revealing extravasation from the right internal mammary artery (RIMA; Fig 3, B), which was then catheterized and embolized via a hydrogel coil (Fig 3, C and D). After stabilization, the SVC balloon was removed, chest cavity irrigation was performed via thoracoscopy, and a TDC was placed in the right common femoral vein. A review of the intraoperative CBCT scan revealed that the RIMA was in the projected device path (Fig 4). The patient was managed in the intensive care unit postoperatively and underwent thoracoscopic washout 2 days later. The postoperative course was prolonged, with respiratory complications requiring mechanical ventilation and debilitation requiring physical therapy. The patient was discharged to inpatient rehabilitation at 20 days and was subsequently discharged home on day 27. At her 30-day follow-up after discharge, the patient was doing well but still recovering strength.

Discussion

This case illustrates an arterial injury complicating an SIO procedure that led to a hemothorax with hemodynamic instability. Cases of major intrathoracic hemorrhage have been reported during other sharp recanalization techniques.8, 9, 10 However, to the best of our knowledge, no such report has been attributed to the Surfacer procedure. The hemothorax management outlined in the present report mirrors that of established protocols for arterial complications of central venous catheter placement involving hemodynamic stability with pressors and fluids, chest tube placement, and potential arterial embolization.11 Several learning points can be drawn from this case. First, although rarely reported, we believe arterial injury should be suspected first in the event of hemodynamic instability, and an angiogram should be performed for timely diagnosis and management. Second, this complication underscores the need for intraoperative CBCT to enhance the safety of this procedure.7 The safety of this technique is based on the anatomic assumption that the central veins are more anterior to arterial structures in the supraclavicular space.12 However, our previous study demonstrated that this anatomic configuration is not predictable and is affected by vessel tortuosity, positional maneuvers of the arm, and the presence of a rigid device in the venous system.7 This can result in injury to an arterial structure lying in the path of the device, which was the case for the RIMA in this report. In our previous study, we aborted 3 of 17 cases due to projected injury, including the innominate and subclavian arteries.7 In the present report, the anatomic course of the RIMA was clearly visible on intraoperative imaging. However, our primary focus was on safeguarding the innominate artery and the SCA. Regrettably, in the process, we inadvertently overlooked the RIMA. It was only in hindsight that we realized its presence within the path of the device on the CBCT scan. This experience underscores the importance of meticulous attention, not only to the larger arteries, but also to the smaller branches when scrutinizing intraoperative imaging. Such diligence is essential to avoid catastrophic complications, as illustrated in the present report. More careful imaging review could have resulted in further device adjustments to avoid injury or procedure abortion. Finally, it is imperative to consider the potential need for advanced support and resuscitative intervention when planning this procedure. Performing SIO procedures in settings with ready availability of the necessary equipment and expertise is essential for successful timely rescue of patients in the case of complications. Without excellent anesthesia personnel and access to blood products, we believe this patient would not have survived.

Conclusions

The use of the SIO system remains a viable technique for achieving central venous catheterization in patients with TCVO but clearly has some potential hazards. Although rare, this case demonstrates that the risk of arterial injury is not negligible and could be mitigated by performing this procedure at institutions with access to both advanced intraoperative imaging to avoid injury to major anatomic structures and resources for advanced support and resuscitation.
