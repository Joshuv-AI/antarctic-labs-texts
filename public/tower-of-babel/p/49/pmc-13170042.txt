Distinct clinical profiles and outcomes in young versus older adults with acute coronary syndrome: a comparative cohort study
Authors: Hengtong Wang, Yawei Xiang, Limin Chen, Yanshuo Ni
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAcute coronary syndrome (ACS) in young adults (≤ 40 years) exhibits distinct characteristics compared with older populations, yet detailed comparative data in Chinese cohorts remain limited.MethodsThis comparative retrospective cohort study analyzed 112 consecutive ACS patients aged ≤ 40 years (Young Group) and 112 matched non-young ACS patients (age > 40 years, Non-Young Group) undergoing coronary angiography at a tertiary center between September 2018 and November 2024. We evaluated clinical profiles, angiographic features, revascularization strategies, and 12-month major adverse cardiac events (MACE).ResultsCompared with the Non-Young Group, young patients exhibited a higher male predominance (p < 0.001) and a lifestyle related risk profile characterized by significantly higher rates of current smoking (p < 0.001) and obesity (p < 0.001). Young patients more frequently presented with STEMI (p = 0.009) and single vessel disease (p = 0.011), with thrombotic lesions being the dominant etiology (p = 0.005). Regarding treatment, the Young Group had a significantly higher utilization of drug coated balloons (p < 0.001) and lower stent implantation rates (p < 0.001). At 12 months, the MACE rate was significantly lower in the Young Group (p = 0.038). Within the young cohort, multivessel disease (aHR 2.89), LVEF < 50% (aHR 3.25), and stent non-implantation (aHR 2.30) were independent MACE predictors. Clustering of ≥ 2 key modifiable risk factors (dyslipidemia, obesity, smoking) conferred a 3 to 5 fold higher MACE risk in young adults (p < 0.001).ConclusionsYoung ACS patients possess a distinct clinical and angiographic profile characterized by a higher thrombotic burden and lifestyle related risk factors compared with older patients. While they exhibit more favorable short term survival and respond well to stent-less revascularization strategies, their long term prognosis is heavily dependent on the aggressive modification of synergistic modifiable risk factors.

Introduction

Acute coronary syndrome (ACS), characterized by atherosclerotic plaque rupture or erosion with superimposed thrombosis, represents a critical manifestation of coronary artery disease (CAD) [1]. Traditionally considered a disease of older adults, ACS incidence among young adults (≤ 40 years) has risen alarmingly in recent decades, paralleling global increases in obesity, dyslipidemia, and smoking [2, 3]. Epidemiological studies indicate that young patients now constitute 5–15% of all ACS cases [4], with distinct demographic, pathophysiological, and prognostic features compared to older cohorts [5].

Young ACS patients exhibit unique risk profiles, with smoking, dyslipidemia, and obesity predominating over traditional factors like diabetes and hypertension [3, 6]. Notably, non-atherosclerotic mechanisms—including spontaneous coronary artery dissection (SCAD), hypercoagulable states, and autoimmune disorders—contribute disproportionately in this population, especially among women [5, 7]. Despite generally having less extensive coronary artery disease, young ACS patients frequently present with ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) and high thrombotic burden [8].

Current evidence gaps persist regarding optimal management strategies for young ACS patients. While guideline-directed medical therapy forms the cornerstone, revascularization approaches (particularly stent implantation rates) vary considerably in real-world practice due to concerns about long-term device complications and alternative pathologies [9]. Furthermore, long-term outcomes remain understudied, with conflicting data on whether favorable short-term prognosis translates to sustained benefit without aggressive risk factor modification [4, 8].

This comparative retrospective cohort study aimed to delineate the distinct clinical profiles, angiographic features, treatment patterns, and 12-month outcomes of young ACS patients (≤ 40 years) by comparing them with a matched cohort of non-young patients in a large Chinese tertiary center. By identifying predominant risk factors, sex-specific comorbidities, and independent predictors of major adverse cardiac events (MACE), we seek to inform tailored prevention and management strategies for this increasingly prevalent and vulnerable population.

Materials and methods

Study population and design

This comparative retrospective cohort study included 112 consecutive patients aged ≤ 40 years (Young Group) diagnosed with ACS who were admitted and underwent coronary angiography (CAG) and subsequent treatment at the North District of the First Affiliated Hospital of Anhui Medical University between September 2018 and November 2024. To characterize the distinct clinical features of the young cohort, a control group of 112 non-young ACS patients (age > 40 years) was enrolled using a 1:1 consecutive matching strategy. Specifically, for each enrolled young patient, the subsequent consecutive non-young ACS patient admitted to the same center was selected to minimize temporal bias regarding treatment standards. All patients received index hospitalization treatment during this period according to contemporary clinical guidelines.

ACS was defined and classified into STEMI, non-ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI), or unstable angina (UA) according to the 2019 Chinese Guidelines for Emergency Rapid Diagnosis and Treatment of Acute Coronary Syndrome [10]. Patients were excluded from both groups if they had: (1) a history of prior coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG); (2) ACS precipitated by severe non-cardiac conditions (e.g., trauma, severe anemia); or (3) incomplete clinical or angiographic data. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Anhui Public Health Clinical Center (Approval number: PJ-YX2025-004).

Data collection

Clinical data were systematically extracted from electronic medical records, encompassing: (1) demographics (age, sex), admission date, presenting symptoms, and comorbidities (autoimmune diseases, chronic kidney disease [CKD], hematologic disorders); (2) cardiovascular risk factors: hypertension (defined by the 2024 ESC Guidelines for the management of elevated blood pressure and hypertension [11]), diabetes mellitus (diagnosed per the 2024 ADA Standards of Medical Care in Diabetes [12]), current smoking status, obesity (body mass index [BMI] ≥ 28 kg/m²), dyslipidemia (defined by the 2023 Chinese Guidelines for Lipid Management [13]), and premature family history of coronary artery disease (CAD) (first-degree relative diagnosed with CAD before age 55 years for males or 65 years for females); (3) initial cardiac troponin (cTn) levels and 12-lead electrocardiogram (ECG) findings; (4) transthoracic echocardiography parameters, specifically left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) measured during the index hospitalization, with severe left ventricular systolic dysfunction defined as LVEF ≤ 40% according to the 2024 Chinese Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Heart Failure [14].

Coronary angiography and intravascular treatment

All patients underwent diagnostic CAG via radial or femoral access using the Judkins technique, performed by two experienced interventional cardiologists. Angiographic analysis assessed: (1) the culprit vessel(s) (left anterior descending [LAD], left circumflex [LCX], right coronary artery [RCA]; major branches such as diagonal or marginal branches were counted as separate vessels); (2) stenosis severity: 100% (total occlusion), 75–99% (severe stenosis), 50–74% (moderate stenosis), < 50% (mild stenosis); (3) presence of thrombus, ulceration, or spontaneous coronary artery dissection (SCAD); (4) coronary slow flow. Although optical coherence tomography (OCT) was not routinely performed to definitively differentiate plaque rupture from erosion, the underlying etiology was inferred from angiographic morphology. Lesions were classified as “thrombotic/complex” (suggestive of plaque rupture or erosion) based on the presence of visible thrombus, ulceration, or haziness. “Calcified nodules” were screened based on the presence of severe fluoroscopic calcification at the culprit site. Post-procedural coronary blood flow was quantitatively assessed using the Thrombolysis in Myocardial Infarction (TIMI) flow grade classification [1], where TIMI grade 3 indicates normal flow and complete perfusion. For the purpose of this study, clinically significant obstructive coronary artery disease was defined as ≥ 50% diameter stenosis in any major epicardial coronary artery (≥ 2.0 mm diameter). Consequently, multivessel disease was classified based on the number of vessels meeting this ≥ 50% threshold: single-vessel disease (SVD), double-vessel disease (DVD) (including left main disease), or triple-vessel disease (TVD). Treatment modalities recorded included: diagnostic CAG only, intracoronary thrombolysis, thrombus aspiration, percutaneous transluminal coronary angioplasty (PTCA) (including drug-coated balloon [DCB] angioplasty), coronary stent implantation (including bioresorbable scaffolds), and referral for coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG).

Pharmacotherapy management

Peri-procedural and discharge medications were documented, encompassing the use of antiplatelet agents (specifically identifying single vs. dual antiplatelet therapy [DAPT] regimens), statins, β-blockers, angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEIs) or angiotensin II receptor blockers (ARBs), calcium channel blockers (CCBs), coronary vasodilators (e.g., nitrates), and anticoagulants.

Follow-up and outcomes

All patients were followed for a minimum of 12 months post-discharge. Data were obtained through a comprehensive review of the hospital’s electronic medical record system, covering both inpatient re-admissions and outpatient clinic visits. To minimize loss to follow-up and capture events occurring outside our institution, status and adverse events were further verified via telephone interviews with patients or their immediate family members. The primary outcome was the occurrence of major adverse cardiac events (MACE), defined as a composite of cardiac death, recurrent myocardial infarction (MI), hospitalization for heart failure, or urgent revascularization. All-cause mortality and cardiac-related rehospitalizations were also recorded.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 24.0, IBM Corp). Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median (interquartile range) depending on normality, and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages (%). Comparisons between the Young and Non-young groups were performed using the Student’s t-test or Mann-Whitney U test for continuous variables, and the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables, as appropriate.

For the prognostic analysis within the young cohort, univariate Cox proportional hazards regression identified potential predictors of 12-month MACE. Given the limited number of outcome events (n = 24), to prevent model overfitting, a rigorous variable selection strategy was employed. Only variables demonstrating strong statistical significance (p < 0.05) in the univariate analysis were entered into the multivariate Cox regression model. Furthermore, due to the narrow age range (19–40 years) and the overwhelming male predominance (92.9%) in this specific cohort, age and sex were excluded from the multivariate adjustment to preserve statistical power (events per variable). Hazard ratios (HRs) and adjusted hazard ratios (aHRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported. Kaplan-Meier survival curves stratified by key risk factor burden (dyslipidemia, obesity, smoking) were generated, and differences assessed using the log-rank test. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

The study population comprised 224 patients, divided into the Young Group (≤40 years, n = 112) and the matched Non-Young Group (>40 years, n = 112). The mean age was 35.6 ± 4.1 years in the Young Group compared to 64.8 ± 9.2 years in the Non-Young Group (p < 0.001).

Significant differences in demographic and clinical profiles were observed (Table 1). The Young Group exhibited a striking male predominance (92.9% vs. 67.9%, p < 0.001). regarding cardiovascular risk factors, young patients were characterized by a "lifestyle-related" risk profile, with significantly higher prevalence of current smoking (51.8% vs. 24.1%, p < 0.001) and obesity (53.6% vs. 31.3%, p<0.001). Conversely, the Non-Young Group had a higher burden of metabolic and chronic comorbidities, including hypertension (65.2% vs. 42.9%, p=0.001) and diabetes mellitus (41.1% vs. 26.8%, p = 0.024).

Admission ACS subtypes differed significantly between groups. Young patients more frequently presented with STEMI (43.8% vs. 26.8%, p = 0.009), whereas unstable angina was more common in the Non-Young Group. Consequently, positive initial cardiac troponin levels were observed in 78 young patients (69.6%), aligning with the diagnosis of myocardial infarction. Comorbidities such as autoimmune diseases were exclusive to the Young Group (n = 2), while chronic kidney disease (CKD) was more prevalent in the Non-Young Group (8.9% vs. 3.6%). Echocardiography revealed that while preserved LVEF (≥ 50%) was common in both groups, young patients had a slightly higher prevalence of preserved systolic function (89.3% vs. 80.4%, p = 0.082).

Coronary angiographic characteristics

Angiographic analysis revealed distinct patterns of coronary involvement (Table 2). Young patients were significantly more likely to present with single-vessel disease (48.2% vs. 31.3%, p = 0.011), most frequently involving the LAD. In contrast, the Non-Young Group exhibited a higher burden of complex multivessel disease, with triple-vessel disease present in 41.1% of patients compared to only 21.4% in the Young Group (p = 0.002).

Regarding lesion morphology and etiology, young patients demonstrated a predominance of thrombotic/complex lesions (67.9% vs. 49.1%, p = 0.005), suggestive of plaque rupture or erosion on a less calcified background. Conversely, severe coronary calcification (suggestive of calcified nodules or advanced atherosclerosis) was virtually absent in the Young Group (0.9%) but was a common feature in the Non-Young Group (33.9%, p < 0.001). Angiographically normal coronaries with slow flow were more frequently observed in young patients (8.0% vs. 1.8%, p = 0.030), pointing towards alternative mechanisms such as vasospasm or microvascular dysfunction in this population.

Another young patient with unstable angina was found to have spontaneous coronary artery dissection (Fig. 1C), highlighting the heterogeneity of etiologies in this population.

Among patients requiring intervention, the Young Group demonstrated a clear preference for “stent-less” strategies; DCB angioplasty was utilized in 34.8% of young patients compared to only 10.7% of non-young patients (p < 0.001). Conversely, stent implantation was the dominant strategy in the Non-Young Group (74.1% vs. 29.5%, p < 0.001), consistent with the higher prevalence of calcified and complex lesions. Intracoronary thrombus clearance (thrombolysis/aspiration) was more frequently required in the Young Group (22.3% vs. 11.6%, p = 0.032). Referral for CABG was rare in young patients (0.9%) but significantly more common in the Non-Young Group (7.1%, p = 0.018) due to extensive triple-vessel disease.

Angiographic success (TIMI grade 3 flow) was high in both groups, though slightly superior in young patients (97.6% vs. 94.7%, p = 0.325), reflecting their lower incidence of microvascular obstruction despite high thrombus burden.

Pharmacotherapy management

Pharmacological therapy adhered to contemporary guidelines in both cohorts (Table 4). All patients in both groups (100%) received antiplatelet therapy, with dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT) being the standard of care. Statin utilization was similarly high (96.4% vs. 94.6%, p = 0.528).

However, significant differences were noted in the use of secondary prevention medications. The Non-Young Group received significantly more ACEIs/ARBs (62.5% vs. 42.9%, p = 0.003) and beta-blockers (83.9% vs. 70.5%, p = 0.018), likely secondary to their higher prevalence of hypertension and multivessel disease. In contrast, coronary vasodilators (primarily nitrates and calcium channel blockers) were more frequently prescribed in the Young Group (71.4% vs. 53.6%, p = 0.006), consistent with the higher incidence of vasospasm and slow-flow phenomena in younger ACS patients.

Clinical outcomes and adverse events

Clinical outcomes were compared between the two groups during the index hospitalization and the 12-month follow-up period (Table 5). During the index hospitalization, the clinical course was generally stable in both groups, with no in-hospital deaths. However, the Non-Young Group experienced a higher rate of acute heart failure (8.9% vs. 2.7%, p = 0.046).

At 12 months, the Young Group demonstrated a significantly lower MACE rate compared to the Non-Young Group (21.4% vs. 33.9%, p = 0.038). The drivers of MACE differed notably: in the Young Group, events were primarily urgent revascularizations (13.4%) and heart failure hospitalizations (4.5%), with zero cardiac deaths. In contrast, the Non-Young Group had a higher incidence of cardiac death (5.4% vs. 0.0%, p = 0.013) and recurrent myocardial infarction (8.9% vs. 3.6%, p = 0.098). One non-cardiac death occurred in the Young Group due to massive gastrointestinal hemorrhage.

Univariate and multivariate analyses

To identify specific predictors of poor prognosis within the vulnerable young population, Cox regression analysis was performed for the 24 MACE events in the Young Group (Table 6). Univariate analysis identified four variables strongly associated with MACE (p < 0.05): multivessel disease, LVEF < 50%, stent non-implantation, and CKD.

Impact of key risk factor clustering on clinical outcomes

The synergistic effect of modifiable risk factors (dyslipidemia, obesity, and smoking) was analyzed within the Young Group. Patients were stratified by the number of these factors present (Table 7). We observed a clear dose-response relationship: the 12-month MACE rate rose from 6.3% in patients with zero risk factors to 37.9% in those with all three (Log-rank p < 0.001, Fig. 2). After adjusting for baseline LVEF, the presence of ≥ 2 risk factors conferred a 3- to 5-fold increase in MACE risk (aHR 3.07 for 2 factors; aHR 4.89 for 3 factors, both p < 0.001). Notably, 75.0% of all MACE events in the young cohort occurred in patients with at least two of these key modifiable factors.

Discussion

ACS predominantly affects older adults; however, rising living standards and the adoption of adverse lifestyle or dietary habits have contributed to its increasing incidence among younger populations in recent years [15, 16]. While large scale registry studies such as the report by Zanchin et al. [17] have characterized the clinical phenotypes and outcomes of young ACS patients undergoing percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) in Western populations, our study delineates these features within a contemporary Chinese cohort. By utilizing a matched non-young control group, we identified that young adults possess a unique lifestyle related risk profile and a distinct pathophysiological substrate. Our study adds to the previous information by specifically quantifying the synergistic impact of risk factor clustering and evaluating the outcomes of a stent-less revascularization strategy in this population.

Our cohort demonstrated a striking male predominance, consistent with established literature indicating estrogen’s cardioprotective role in premenopausal women through lipid modulation, improved glucose tolerance, and blood pressure regulation [18]. The attenuation of this protective effect post-menopause corresponds with elevated ACS risk in women. Dyslipidemia, obesity, smoking, hypertension, diabetes, and premature family history of coronary artery disease constitute established risk factors for early-onset ACS [19, 20]. Our comparative analysis reinforces this paradigm, revealing that young patients have significantly higher rates of current smoking and obesity than their older counterparts. Smoking potentiates ACS risk through endothelial injury from nicotine or carbon monoxide inducing coronary spasm and thrombosis and by dysregulating lipid metabolism [21]. Obesity further exacerbates risk via its association with hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia. Although hypertension and diabetes were significantly more prevalent in the non-young group, they remain critical contributors in the young. Notably, only 13.4% of our young cohort lacked identifiable risk factors, underscoring lifestyle modification as a critical preventive strategy.

Among female patients, 2/8 had concomitant autoimmune disorders aligning with reports implicating autoimmune diseases, fibromuscular dysplasia, and premature menopause as ACS risk factors in young women [22]. While oral contraceptive use has been associated with premature ACS [23], no enrolled females reported long term usage. Among males, 4 patients had advanced chronic kidney disease (CKD Stage 3–5), with observed hypercoagulability and hypoalbuminemia likely contributing to heightened thrombotic burden during ACS events, a phenomenon widely reported [24, 25].

Peerwani et al. [26] identified STEMI as the most common ACS subtype in young adults, a pattern corroborated by our data where STEMI was significantly more frequent in the young than in the non-young group. Young patients typically exhibit less complex coronary pathology characterized by single vessel disease, complete occlusions, and thrombus rich lesions with minimal calcification [27]. This contrasts sharply with the extensive triple vessel disease and severe calcification observed in our non-young control group. Transient platelet aggregation and thrombus dissolution may explain the lower grade stenoses observed in some young cases. Our cohort included 25 patients requiring intracoronary thrombolysis or thrombectomy, supporting this model of high thrombotic burden. One illustrative case involved recurrent thrombotic STEMI in a male patient with aneurysmal dilation at the right circumflex ostium, suggesting congenital coronary anomalies or prothrombotic states. Less common etiologies, such as SCAD, coronary embolism, and cocaine abuse [28], were also observed, including one unstable angina case with SCAD treated with bioresorbable stent implantation.

Revascularization approaches revealed a distinct preference for stent-less strategies in the young cohort. Drug coated balloon (DCB) based strategies predominated, while stent implantation rates were significantly lower than in the non-young group. This reflects both clinical reluctance to use permanent implants in young patients and the suitability of their lesions for less invasive management. This aligns with domestic studies reporting lower stent utilization in young versus elderly ACS patients [29]. Pharmacotherapy adhered to guideline recommendations as all patients received dual antiplatelet therapy, 96.4% received statins, and beta blockers were widely prescribed for ventricular remodeling. Coronary vasodilators were frequently used in the young, consistent with the higher prevalence of vasospasm and slow flow phenomena. These practices reflect contemporary ACS management principles outlined in the 2023 ESC Guidelines [30].

Comparative analyses confirm a superior 12-month prognosis in young versus non-young ACS patients, characterized by lower mortality and fewer heart failure hospitalizations [31, 32]. Our results showed a 21.4% MACE rate in the young compared to 33.9% in the older group, with cardiac death being exclusive to the non-young cohort. However, long-term outcomes in the young depend critically on lifestyle modification and comorbidity control. Despite favorable short term survival, young ACS patients remain at high risk for recurrent ischemic events if modifiable factors are not addressed.

Our multivariate analysis identified multivessel disease, LVEF < 50%, and stent non-implantation as independent predictors of 12-month MACE in young ACS patients, reinforcing the prognostic impact of anatomical complexity and ventricular dysfunction. These findings align with Gulati et al. (2023), who similarly demonstrated that incomplete revascularization and reduced LVEF independently increased MACE risk in young STEMI cohorts [33]. Notably, CKD showed marginal significance, suggesting its risk may be mediated through comorbidities like accelerated atherosclerosis or microvascular disease [34]. Critically, risk factor clustering revealed a dose dependent effect where young patients with ≥ 2 key risk factors (dyslipidemia, obesity, smoking) exhibited a 3 to 5 fold higher MACE risk compared to those with no risk factors. This synergism mirrors Virani et al. (2024), where young ACS patients with modifiable risk factor burden had higher long term mortality [35]. Our results emphasize that while short term outcomes are generally favorable, long term prognosis in young ACS is heavily contingent on aggressive risk factor modification, particularly targeting the triad of dyslipidemia, obesity, and smoking to mitigate the cumulative atherosclerotic burden.

Several limitations of this study warrant consideration. First, as a retrospective, single center analysis, there is an inherent risk of selection bias, and the findings may reflect the specific practice patterns of a tertiary center in China. Second, although we expanded the study to include a matched non-young control group, the sample size for specific etiologies like SCAD remains relatively small. Third, the underlying plaque morphology was inferred from angiographic features rather than confirmed by intravascular imaging such as OCT or IVUS. Finally, while a 12-month follow-up provides insights into outcomes, longer term longitudinal data are required to fully evaluate the lifetime cardiovascular risk in these young adults.

Conclusion

This study delineates the distinct clinical and pathophysiological profile of young adults with acute coronary syndrome through a rigorous comparison with a matched older cohort. Young patients are characterized by a pronounced male predominance, a high prevalence of smoking and obesity, and a frequent presentation as ST elevation myocardial infarction involving single vessel thrombotic disease. In contrast to the calcified and multivessel involvement typical of older patients, the young cohort demonstrates a favorable response to conservative and stent-less revascularization approaches, particularly drug coated balloon angioplasty. While the 12 month prognosis in young adults is superior to that of older patients with zero cardiac mortality observed, their risk of recurrent events is significantly amplified by the clustering of modifiable risk factors. Multivessel involvement and ventricular dysfunction remain critical independent predictors of adverse outcomes in this population. These findings underscore the necessity of moving beyond traditional age based management to implement aggressive, tailored lifestyle interventions and optimized medical therapy to mitigate the cumulative atherosclerotic burden in young ACS survivors.
