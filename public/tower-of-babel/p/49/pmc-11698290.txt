Piperacillin-tazobactam and nephrotoxicity: Myth busted?
Authors: Muhammad Momin Khan, Kundan Kumar Maheshwari, Maisam Ali Rajput, Farina Fatima Siddiqui
Published: 2024

Dear Madam,

Due to its broad-spectrum bactericidal coverage, piperacillin-tazobactam (PT) is widely prescribed as a common antibiotic within the intensive care unit. It combines a beta-lactam antibiotic and a beta-lactamase inhibitor, effective against a wide range of community-acquired and nosocomial bacteria. However, it is not completely free of shortcomings; it is known to commonly cause gastrointestinal adverse drug reactions such as diarrhea and abdominal cramps, skin reactions, electrolyte imbalances, and in rare cases, life-threatening anaphylactic reactions.[1] In the proximal renal tubule cells, PT appears to utilize organic ion transporters called, “(OAT)-1 and OAT-3” for excretion.[2]

Previously, numerous retrospective studies, including systematic reviews, and meta-analyses have described the concomitant administration of PT and Vancomycin to increase the risk of drug-induced acute kidney injury (AKI).[3] Several mechanisms have been hypothesized including injury to proximal renal tubule cells and interstitial nephritis. Nevertheless, the suggested link between this combination and AKI has attracted controversy for several reasons. For instance, the connection is not universally established and various studies have reported conflicting results. Second, previous studies have utilized serum creatinine as a surrogate indicator of kidney injury. However, it is neither a specific nor a sensitive marker and may be falsely elevated in the study participants. Finally, published studies have largely adopted an observational study design limited by unmeasured confounding factors that may explain the hypothesized association. Conversely, in a one-of-a-kind animal model study, PT exhibited nephrotoxicity due to oxidative stress and mitochondrial damage despite the population of experimental animals being considered very small.[4] Nonetheless, the US Food and Drug Administration maintains that the combined use of these drugs raises the risk of AKI.

However, in October 2023, the Antibiotic Choice on Renal Outcomes randomized clinical trial was published in JAMA.[5] The ACORN trial gained significant attraction in the medical community for several reasons. It was the first prospective randomized study to have evaluated the comparative safety of PT and cefepime in patients with suspected infection within 12 h of presentation. A total of 2511 patients were enrolled in the trial, including those with preexisting AKI at baseline. A clinical decision support tool was utilized to randomize the eligible participants at the prescriber level. The revolutionary study reported no clinically or statistically significant difference, in the incidence of AKI in both study arms (odds ratio, 0.95; 95% confidence interval, 0.80–1.13).[5] Considering that up to three-fourths of the patients in each study group were co-administered Vancomycin at enrollment, it can be hypothesized that the findings may apply to the combination of the drugs rather than PT itself. Hence, is PT safe from the kidney injury perspective?

PT competes with other organic substances such as creatinine for secretion into the proximal renal tubules. Consequently, increased serum creatinine levels may not accurately indicate true kidney injury. In light of this, the use of PT may be deemed appropriate, particularly considering the potential issues associated with alternative Gram-negative coverage drugs such as Cefepime. However, physicians must exercise caution when prescribing this medication until the warning is lifted.
