Impact of an algorithm-based postoperative pain management in patients undergoing abdominal surgery—A prospective interventional study
Authors: R. Priyanka, Lakshmi Ramamoorthy, Lenin Babu Elakkumanan, Biju Pottakkat, H. T. Lalthanthuami, Unnikrishnan Puliakkuth
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:The fear of pain is ranked next to the fear of death. Globally, 50% to 75% of postoperative patients experience severe postoperative pain. Pain resource nurses function as role models in pain assessment and management. This study aimed to assess the adequacy of pain management and the effect of algorithm-based postoperative pain management following abdominal surgeries.MATERIALS AND METHODS:A two-phase study was conducted over 6 months in the Surgical Gastroenterology department of a tertiary care center, involving 70 postoperative patients divided equally between the phases using consecutive sampling. The second phase, the primary focus, employed a one-group, post-test-only design, measuring breakthrough pain with the Numerical Rating Scale. Pain management during this phase was guided by a structured, algorithm-based protocol along with standard care. In contrast, the first phase used a quantitative, cross-sectional observational design with patients receiving routine care. Pain severity, quality indicators, and patient satisfaction were assessed using the ASSIST–Patient Satisfaction Survey at multiple points in both phases. This design enabled a systematic comparison between traditional practices and the targeted intervention implemented in Phase II.RESULTS:The study results showed that more patients in group 1 experienced severe pain at specific times after surgery compared to group 2 (24 hours postsurgery: 52.8 ± 30.2 vs. 27.4 ± 13.7, P = 0.000; 48 hours postsurgery: 34.2 ± 21.5 vs. 29.1 ± 15.6, P = 0.257; 72 hours postsurgery). The patients’ satisfaction scores increased after 48 hours postsurgery in group 2 (P < 0.05).CONCLUSION:The current study demonstrated that algorithmic pain management can improve the quality indicators of pain management, pain relief, and patient satisfaction scores among postoperative patients.

Introduction

Postoperative pain is one of the most common and expected outcomes of surgery.[12] Although it is predictable and usually temporary, improper management of postoperative pain remains a widespread global problem.[3] Effective pain control is widely recognized as a crucial part of quality healthcare and a human right, reflecting both ethical duties and clinical skills. Despite this, many patients still experience moderate to severe pain following surgery, which can cause physical and mental health problems. These include increased cardiovascular stress, decreased wound healing, longer hospital stays, and lower patient satisfaction.[45]

Recently, considerable progress has been made in understanding pain mechanisms and developing active management strategies. The introduction of multimodal analgesia, evidence-based pain management guidelines, and multidisciplinary acute pain services has improved patient outcomes in many healthcare facilities.[6] However, implementing these strategies remains variable, especially in resource-limited areas like India. Challenges such as the absence of effective pain management protocols, inadequate staff training, and the increased workload of anesthesiologists have created substantial gaps between recommended, evidence-based best practices and their actual application.[78] Therefore, many patients still experience poorly managed postoperative pain. Nurses, who are on the front lines of care, have an exceptional opportunity to help close this gap. They devote the most time to patients during recovery, frequently monitoring their condition, checking pain levels, and responding quickly to discomfort.[4910] This close interaction makes nurses essential for effective pain management. Though current nursing practices often depend on personal experience or personal judgment rather than standardized, evidence-based guidelines. This results in differences in pain assessment and treatment, which can compromise the quality and consistency of care.

To address these issues, a structured, nurse-led approach based on an algorithm has been implemented as an operative strategy for improving postoperative pain management. This strategy provides nurses with a clear, stepwise framework to assess, intervene, and regulate pain management as per patients’ pain scores and clinical responses. By standardizing procedures, algorithm-based models reduce subjective differences, increase nurses’ decision-making process, and enable timely communication with anesthesiologists and surgeons.[1112] In abdominal surgeries, where inadequate pain control can affect early ambulation and respiratory weaning, these structured interventions are specifically valuable for helping faster postoperative recovery and higher patient satisfaction. This study aims to evaluate the effectiveness of an algorithm-based, nurse-led framework for the adequacy of pain control among patients undergoing abdominal surgery. By integrating evidence-based algorithms into nursing practice, this study hopes to strengthen the role of nurses in postoperative care and contribute to safer, more effective, and equitable pain management.

Materials and Methods

Study design

This study employed a quasiexperimental, nonblinded, two-phase design to evaluate postoperative pain management. Phase I utilized a cross-sectional observational design, enrolling consecutive postoperative patients who received standard institutional pain management to establish baseline data on pain outcomes. Phase II employed a one-group post-test-only design to evaluate the effect of an algorithm-based pain management strategy added to standard care. The use of asynchronous phases without randomization was chosen for logical reasons to facilitate unit-wide implementation and to avoid contamination between groups. However, we acknowledge that this design introduces a risk of temporal and selection bias. To mitigate this, the study was conducted exclusively in the Surgical Gastroenterology Ward with the same surgical team performing similar elective abdominal surgeries in both phases, thereby standardizing the surgical trauma and patient population. In both phases, postoperative pain outcomes—including severity, duration of severe pain, pain interference, and patient satisfaction—were measured at 24 hours, 48 hours, 72 hours, day 5, and day 7 postsurgery. Phase II aimed to determine the additional benefits of the algorithm compared to the established baseline from Phase I.

Settings and participants

This study was conducted in the intensive care units and the Surgical Ward of the Gastroenterology discipline of a tertiary care center in South India. To avoid unintentional differences in surgical types during the two phases of the study, it was conducted exclusively in the Surgical Gastroenterology Ward, where the same surgical team performed elective abdominal surgeries. These surgeries were included over one year, from 2021 to 2022. Patients who had received any prior surgeries, had a history of Severe comorbidities (>Grade-IV ASA physical status), were hemodynamically unstable, had a previous history of narcotics abuse, or had chronic pain on analgesics and with known psychiatric or antiepileptic medications were excluded from the study. A total of 86 patients were assessed for eligibility. After exclusions, 70 patients were enrolled, with 35 participants consecutively recruited into each phase. Sampling and sample size

Every consecutive patient who met the eligibility criteria during the study period was enrolled in both phases of the study. The sample size was determined using Open Epi Version 3 for comparing two means. The calculation was based on a large effect size (d = 0.86), derived from the mean difference in patient satisfaction scores for pain treatment between the control group and the intervention group (Mean difference = 0.3, standard deviation = 0.35). With a desired power of 90% and a 5% significance level, the required sample size was calculated to be 32 in each group. Accounting for a 10% dropout rate, the total sample size was rounded up to 70, with 35 participants in each group.[13] It is recognized that the use of a large effect size is optimistic, and the true power of the study to detect smaller, yet clinically meaningful, differences may be lower; this is noted as a limitation in the discussion.

Variables

Independent variables included patients’ age, sex, diagnosis, type of surgery, type of incision, duration of surgery, anesthesia type, intraoperative complications, and postoperative hemodynamic status. The outcome variable of this study included pain scores (worst pain severity, time spent in severe pain, pain interference, and patient satisfaction).

ASSIST Patient Satisfaction Survey: Revised American Pain Society Patient Outcome Questionnaire (APS-POQ-R) was used to assess the level of pain, in which pain is rated between 0 and 10. The numerical pain scale ranged from no (0), mild (1-3), moderate (4-6), and severe (7-10) pain. This questionnaire includes the subscales of quality indicators, pain during patient activities (including at rest, while repositioning, sitting, sleeping, and walking), pain relief, side effects of drugs, and the patient’s satisfaction score. The Cronbach alpha scores for the ASSIST Pain questionnaire are as follows: the total 23-item scale has a Cronbach alpha of 0.85, indicating good overall reliability. The individual subscales demonstrate strong internal consistency with the Affective subscale at 0.82, Interference with Activity at 0.82, and Perceptions of Pain Care at 0.70. The Adverse Effects subscale has a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.25, which is low; however, this subscale was retained for consistency with the validated APS-POQ-R instrument and to provide descriptive data on side effects, but its results were interpreted with caution and were not used for primary inferences. The Revised American Pain Society Patient Outcome Questionnaire (APS-POQ-R) was identified as a valid measure for assessing the severity of pain and satisfaction with postoperative pain management.[13]

Ethical consideration

This study was approved by the Research Monitoring Committee and the Institute Ethics Committee for Nursing courses (Reg. No.: JIP/IEC/2020/MSN/05) to protect human subjects from risk. This study was not registered in a clinical trials registry. The confidentiality of the data, the right to withdraw from the study, and the anonymity of the participants were clearly explained before data collection. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before enrolment, and the Helsinki guidelines were followed. Patient data were stored confidentially.

Statistical and data analysis

All baseline and outcome variables were analyzed using IBM SPSS 25.0 for Windows on an intention-to-treat basis. The distribution of categorical variables, such as gender, education, occupation, marital status, presurgical diagnosis, ASA physical status classification system, type of surgery, surgical site, mode of administration, and use of patient controlled analgesia, was expressed as frequencies and percentages. Univariate associations of baseline characteristics between the groups were examined using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for categorical variables and the Student t-test or Mann-Whitney U test for continuous variables. Given the nonrandomized, two-phase design, one-way ANOVA and other group comparison tests were employed to evaluate differences in outcomes between the Phase I (baseline) and Phase II (intervention) as measured by pain scores, patient satisfaction scores, and duration of hospital stay. This analytical approach is considered appropriate for comparing two independent groups in this quasiexperimental design. For primary and secondary endpoints, a P value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

There were a total of 35 participants in both phases of the study. The mean age of the participants in both groups was comparable at 42.8 ± 14 and 45 ± 13.8. Similarly, the mean BMI was 21.1 and 22.8. Male preponderance (54.3%, 74.3%) was noted in both groups. The groups were homogeneous in terms of diagnosis, presurgical diagnosis, ASA grading, and type of surgical incision, which is being presented as a supplementary table. The majority of participants in both groups belonged to Chronic Calcific Pancreatitis (22.9%, 31.4%). In presurgical diagnosis, most of the participants were categorized under pancreas (28.6% and 25.7%). In ASA grading, 62.9% of participants in Group 1 and 71.4% in Group 2 belonged to Grade 1. The majority of participants in Group 1 (71.4%) and Group 2 (88.6%) underwent laparotomy surgery. Most participants had no comorbidities (62.9%, 74.3%), and a large proportion had diabetes as a comorbidity (69.2%, 100%). The groups were comparable in terms of smoking and alcohol intake, as P < 0.29.

The mode of analgesia in both groups on the first postoperative day was only Intravenous (65.71%, 65.71%), followed by combined modes, such as Intravenous and epidural (34.28%, 31.42%) [Table 1].

Discussion

This quasiexperimental study evaluated a structured, algorithm-based approach to managing postoperative pain in patients undergoing abdominal surgeries at a tertiary care center in South India. The primary value of the algorithm lies in its ability to standardize nursing assessments and prompt timely interventions, thereby addressing a critical gap in postoperative recovery. The study observed that implementing an algorithm-based postoperative pain management strategy improved pain control and increased patient satisfaction. Therefore, it can be considered an effective intervention for pain management.

The age distribution in the current study shows that most participants were between 40 and 45 years old, with a higher number of males. This finding is consistent with a study conducted by Subramanian et al.,[13] in which the mean age of participants was 55.6 ± 13.9 years, and the female-to-male ratio was 40:60.[14] Regarding diagnosis, most participants in Group 1 underwent Frey’s procedure (22.9%), while most in Group 2 underwent gastrectomy (25.7%). All participants received general anesthesia, and the majority of them in both groups underwent laparotomy incision, which aligns with a similar study reporting that 75.3% of participants underwent general anesthesia.[1516] While these demographic comparisons establish a basic similarity in study populations, cultural attitudes toward pain, and variations in nurse training protocols, which were not explored in the cited literature.

The main issue with postoperative pain management is underestimating its severity. Proper pain assessment not only helps determine the intensity and type of pain but also supports the development of effective strategies for managing acute pain.[13] The algorithmic approach implemented in this study directly targeted this issue by empowering nurses within a structured framework. Involving nurses in Acute Pain Services and training them in postoperative pain management will help fill this gap. The role of qualified nurses in managing postsurgical pain has become increasingly important.[17] Since nurses are familiar with surgical procedures and the intraoperative status of patients, they can play a key role in controlling postoperative pain in collaboration with surgeons and anesthesiologists.[181920] The results of this intervention effect can be explained as standardized decision-making by training of nurses with clear protocols for rescue analgesia, which reduces the variability in care. Second, it promotes a faster response to breakthrough pain, as nurses are accountable to act without waiting for physician orders. In this study, group 1 was surveyed for the quality indicators of pain before implementing an algorithmic approach to pain management. The results revealed that, during the first 48 hours after surgery, participants’ pain scores were persistently high, highlighting the need for implementing an algorithmic approach to pain management. Similar results are evident in current literature.[1620]

The algorithmic approach significantly reduced patients’ perception of postoperative pain. More patients experienced severe pain at the indicated time points after surgery in group I compared to the Intervention group. Consistent with these findings, a study by Fang et al.,[1] who also adopted a similar methodology, enrolled the patients at two different phases in two different groups, including those who were hospitalized before and after nurse-led pain relief model implementation. Results showed that more patients reported lower pain in the group that received nurse-led, algorithm-based postoperative pain management.

The current study identified a significant reduction in postoperative pain during rest and activities, such as repositioning, walking, sitting, falling asleep, and staying asleep. Studies reveal that pain scores are higher during activities compared to rest, which restricts their postoperative movement and ambulation, emphasizing the need for adequate postoperative pain management.[2122] The study also explored side effects of rescue analgesics, i.e., Inj Tramadol, which did not increase the complications of nausea, vomiting, and respiratory suppression in the intervention group. The patient’s satisfaction with their pain management was higher in the intervention group from day 2 onwards till discharge, which was significant at P < 0.05. These results were in harmony with evidence that supports accurate pain assessment at frequent intervals, allowing nurses to take initiative and act immediately.[232425] Using an algorithmic approach for postoperative pain relief would potentially improve other clinical benefits for the patients, including early ambulation, reduced complications, and shorter hospital stay, though these aspects were not studied in detail in this study.

Although this study explored the possibility of implementing algorithm-based pain management in a quasiexperimental approach, it also had several limitations, as it was conducted across two different time frames. There is a possibility of sample selection bias and time-related confounders, including staff experience, that may have also influenced the study results. Additional limitations include its single-center design, the lack of clinical trial registration, and the nonrandomized allocation of participants, which heightens the risk of temporal biases, including staff skill mix over time. Similarly, the lack of randomization of participants, a single-center study, restricted to patients who underwent abdominal surgeries, small sample size all these factors may affect the generalizability of the study findings. Therefore, while promising, these findings should not be overgeneralized to propose immediate implementation in all centers. Instead, they strongly indicate the need for a prospective, multicenter randomized controlled trial with a larger sample size. Such a study should be rigorously designed to confirm efficacy, evaluate a broader range of clinical outcomes.
