Prognostic Value of Next‐Generation Flow Cytometry Versus Dual‐Tracer PET/CT for Minimal Residual Disease Assessment in Multiple Myeloma
Authors: Runfeng Zhang, Wenjia Zhu, Lili Cheng, Jianhua Du, Fujing Zhang, Qiao Yang, Huijuan Wang, Yun Leng, Miao Chen, Li Huo, Junling Zhuang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTTo evaluate the comparative prognostic utility of next‐generation flow cytometry (NGF) and dual‐tracer PET/CT in measuring minimal residual disease (MRD), this prospective real‐world study enrolled 118 newly diagnosed multiple myeloma (NDMM) patients. Patients underwent post‐treatment assessment using NGF (sensitivity 10−5) and dual‐tracer PET/CT using 11C‐acetate (AC‐PET) and 18F‐FDG. While AC‐PET showed a numerically higher detection rate compared to FDG‐PET in paired analyses (n = 45), a significant discordance was observed between imaging and immunophenotypic assessment. NGF detected MRD in 38.2% of paired cases compared to 11.8% by AC‐PET; however, AC‐PET uniquely identified metabolic activity in extramedullary sites for two NGF‐negative patients, highlighting the value of functional imaging in resolving spatial heterogeneity. In the landmark survival analysis (n = 104), NGF positivity was identified as a robust independent predictor for inferior progression‐free survival (HR 2.21, 95% CI 1.01–4.81; p = 0.046) and overall survival (HR 4.97, 95% CI 1.23–20.01; p = 0.026), transcending baseline risk stratifications. Furthermore, longitudinal monitoring revealed that patients achieving sustained MRD negativity experienced significantly superior outcomes compared to those with transient negativity or persistent disease. These findings establish NGF as the standard prognostic biomarker for MM, while AC‐PET serves as a critical complementary tool for detecting extramedullary escape, supporting a risk‐adapted MRD strategy that integrates molecular depth with metabolic imaging.Keywords: minimal residual disease, multiple myeloma, next‐generation flow cytometry, positron emission computed tomography, prognosis

Introduction

The importance of monitoring minimal residual disease (MRD) in bone marrow for multiple myeloma (MM) has been highly addressed recently. Different techniques, such as next generation flow cytometry (NGF) and next generation sequencing (NGS), are currently being used to assess MRD [1, 2], which have been included in the International Myeloma Working Group (IMWG) criteria for defining a negative MRD state [3]. MRD monitoring has been established as an independent prognostic factor with an impact on survival and as a potentially relevant endpoint in clinical trials [4, 5].

Myeloma cells could distribute heterogeneously in bone marrow, and thus sampling from a single location may not provide an accurate assessment of disease status, and routine biopsy of multiple skeletal sites is impractical [6]. Additionally, the possibility of extramedullary disease (EMD) should be considered, as it is associated with a poor prognosis [7]. To address this limitation, imaging techniques have been integrated into MRD assessment. Therefore, imaging techniques such as positron emission tomography‐computed tomography (PET‐CT) are required to overcome the questions above. PET/CT has shown prognostic value for progression‐free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS) in MM [8, 9, 10], and has also been included as a new category in the IMWG consensus response criteria [3].

Currently, numerous pieces of evidence have proved the role of 18F‐fluorodeoxyglucose PET/CT (18F‐FDG PET/CT) in MM. Extensive studies have been conducted on the role of 18F‐FDG PET/CT in the diagnostic phase, prognostic evaluation, and response to treatment, with highly significant findings [11, 12]. While 18F‐FDG provides valuable prognostic information, its sensitivity is limited by the variable glucose metabolism of myeloma cells and physiological background uptake. Consequently, tracers targeting alternative metabolic pathways, such as lipid synthesis and membrane turnover, have emerged. Studies utilizing 11C‐choline and 11C‐acetate have demonstrated superior sensitivity over 18F‐FDG in detecting intramedullary myeloma and differentiating diffuse infiltration. Our previous study showed that AC‐PET outperformed FDG‐PET in tumor burden evaluation and disease progression prediction in NDMM [13]. Recently, limited case series have suggested the potential of AC‐PET in the MRD setting [14]. However, these studies were largely exploratory or restricted to small sample sizes. A prospective comparison of the prognostic potency between next‐generation flow cytometry (NGF) and dual‐tracer PET/CT—specifically utilizing the more sensitive 11C‐acetate—in a real‐world cohort remains lacking.

This prospective, real‐world observational study evaluates MM patients to address three gaps: comparative sensitivity of AC‐PET versus FDG‐PET in detecting focal/diffuse lesions, prognostic concordance between NGF and PET/CT, and clinical significance of longitudinal MRD status.

Methods

Patients

This prospective real‐world observational study included 118 newly diagnosed multiple myeloma (NDMM) patients diagnosed at Peking Union Medical College Hospitals between September 2015 and January 2025. The patients' disease response status was assessed using NGF, as well as 11C‐acetate and 18F‐FDG PET‐CT. Baseline characteristics of the patients were collected before their first treatment. NGF and both PET‐CT scans were performed after achieving a partial response (PR) to analyze the MRD status in the bone marrow and the metabolic response in EMD or focal lesions. During follow‐up, the response was assessed according to the International Myeloma Working Group (IMWG) consensus criteria. Cytogenetic risk was classified based on established criteria. MRD continued to be assessed every year during maintenance. This study was approved by the institutional review board of Peking Union Medical College Hospital (0711).

Clinical Characteristics

The hemoglobin level, serum lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), M protein quantification by serum protein electrophoresis (SPE), and the 24‐h urine light chain were determined. International staging system (ISS) and D‐S staging were recorded according to the IMWG criteria. Chromosome and genetic aberrations of CD138‐sorted bone marrow plasma cells were examined by fluorescence in situ hybridization. High‐risk disease was defined as any of the following abnormalities: 1q21 amplification, 17p deletion, and t (4; 14) or t (14; 16) translocations.

Disease Assessment by NGF

Bone marrow samples were collected after induction and consolidation therapy when the clinical response reached PR for NGF immunophenotypic studies. Experienced clinicians evaluated MRD using the EuroFlow criteria in Beijing Chao‐Yang Hospital. The first aliquot of fresh bone marrow aspirate was subjected to NGF MRD detection. Following the EuroFlow standard operating procedures, we employed two 8‐color antibody panels (CD138/CD27/CD38/CD56/CD45/CD19/CD117/CD81 and CD138/CD27/CD38/CD56/CD45/CD19/cκ/cλ) to acquire 1 × 107bone marrow nucleated cells. The assay sensitivity was 3 × 10−6. Samples demonstrating absence of monoclonal plasma cells at the 1 × 10−5 level were considered NGF‐MRD negative, consistent with current IMWG recommendations.

Disease Assessment by PET‐CT

NGF and PET/CT assessments were performed within a median interval of 14 days. All patients fasted for at least 6 h, and their blood glucose concentration was measured before the injection of PET radiopharmaceuticals (all had glucose levels of < 8 mmol/L). 11C‐acetate was administered intravenously, followed by a non‐contrast limited whole‐body CT scan and PET data acquisition. Approximately 15 min after the completion of 11C‐acetate imaging, 18F‐FDG was injected intravenously. Another non‐contrast limited whole‐body CT scan and PET data acquisition were performed 60 min after 18F‐FDG administration. This constituted a dual‐tracer PET/CT scan. The CT protocol included specific parameters. The results of both PET‐CT scans were evaluated by nuclear medicine clinicians. PET positivity was defined based on IMWG and IMPeTUs criteria. For 11C‐acetate PET, a positive result was defined as: (1) Focal uptake: distinct areas of increased tracer accumulation exceeding the surrounding background; or (2) Diffuse uptake: generalized bone marrow uptake intensity exceeding the liver (Deauville score > 4). In cases of discrepancy between tracers, the 11C‐acetate result was prioritized for statistical analysis due to its demonstrated higher sensitivity in this cohort.

Statistical Analysis

Data pre‐processing involved checking for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test and visual inspection of Q‐Q plots. Outliers were evaluated and no significant data transformation was required. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± SD or median (range) depending on their distribution. Categorical variables were compared using Fisher's exact test or Chi‐square test as appropriate. McNemar's test was used to compare the sensitivity of paired PET/CT tracers (11C‐acetate vs. 18F‐FDG). Survival curves (PFS and OS) were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared via the two‐sided Log‐rank test. The proportional hazards assumption for the Cox regression models was verified using Schoenfeld residuals. To minimize guarantee‐time bias, a landmark analysis was performed measuring PFS from the time of MRD assessment. Cox proportional hazards models were used for uni‐ and multivariable analyses to determine hazard ratios (HR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI). All statistical tests were two‐sided, and a p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY) and GraphPad Prism version 9.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA).

Results

Cohort Characteristics

Among the cohort, patients were stratified by MRD assessment modality: 58 underwent NGF alone, 14 received FDG‐PET, and 34 underwent combined NGF and Dual‐Tracer PET/CT evaluation (Figure 1). Baseline characteristics of the 118 NDMM patients enrolled in this study are summarized in Table 1. The median follow‐up time for the entire enrolled cohort (n = 118) was 44.1 months (range, 10.7–184.3 months) from the time of diagnosis. The cohort had a median age of 57.8 years (range: 13–76) with a male predominance (64/118, 54.2%). Cytogenetic profiling revealed a high prevalence of adverse abnormalities, with 1q21 amplification in 43.1% (47/109) and del(17p) in 14.7% (16/109). Most patients (56/118) were classified as ISS Stage III. Treatment approaches incorporated autologous stem cell transplantation (ASCT) in 55.1% (65/118) and predominantly featured VRD (45/118, 38.1%) and BCD (38/118, 32.2%) regimens. Depth of response assessment showed that 62.7% (74/118) achieved stringent complete response (sCR), correlating with NGF‐defined MRD negativity in 61.9% (73/118) post‐induction. Elevated lactate dehydrogenase (LDH > 250 U/L), indicative of tumor metabolic burden, was observed in 19.1% (21/110) of evaluable patients.

Comparative Sensitivity of PET‐CT Assessment

Forty‐five patients underwent paired AC‐PET and FDG‐PET imaging. In the paired cohort of 45 patients who underwent both dual‐tracer PET/CT scans, 11C‐acetate PET identified positive lesions in 6 patients (13.3%), whereas 18F‐FDG PET identified positive lesions in 3 patients (6.7%). McNemar's test yielded a p value of 0.375, indicating no statistically significant difference in overall positivity rates, likely due to the limited sample size. However, discordant analysis revealed five discordant cases; four were AC‐positive/FDG‐negative, and one was AC‐negative/FDG‐positive. The agreement between the two tracers was fair (Cohen's Kappa = 0.39, Table 2). To visually illustrate the superior sensitivity of 11C‐acetate in detecting residual disease, a representative case is shown in Figure 2. In this patient, 18F‐FDG PET/CT showed restricted metabolic activity (Figure 2B), whereas 11C‐acetate PET/CT revealed significantly more extensive disease involvement (Figure 2A). This discordance suggested that AC‐PET provides complementary metabolic information, particularly for detecting lesions with low glucose avidity.

In the paired cohort of 34 patients who underwent both assessments, significant discordance was observed (Table 3). NGF detected MRD positivity in 13 patients (38.2%), whereas 11C‐acetate PET was positive in only 4 (11.8%), a statistically significant difference (p = 0.022, McNemar's test). Despite the lower overall sensitivity of imaging, the low concordance (Kappa = 0.07) underscores the complementary value of the two modalities. While NGF was superior for detecting low‐level intramedullary disease (11 patients were NGF+/PET–), 11C‐acetate PET uniquely identified residual metabolic activity in 2 patients who were NGF‐negative. These specific cases highlight the potential of functional imaging to detect focal lesions or extramedullary disease that may be missed by random bone marrow sampling. Prognostic Impact of NGF‐Defined MRD Status

Comparative baseline characteristics of 104 newly diagnosed multiple myeloma (NDMM) patients stratified by NGF‐defined MRD status are detailed in Table 4. The cohort demonstrated significant disparities in critical prognostic parameters between MRD‐negative (n = 66) and MRD‐positive (n = 38) groups. Most notably, patients with persistent MRD positivity were significantly older (60.2 ± 7.4 years vs. 56.3 ± 9.9 years; p = 0.024), indicating age as a biological determinant of treatment resistance. Furthermore, depth of response profoundly influenced MRD status: achievement of sCR/CR was substantially higher in the MRD‐negative cohort (90.9% vs. 73.7%; p = 0.025), underscoring the interdependence of conventional response criteria and molecular remission.

In the landmark analysis of 104 evaluable patients, measuring survival from the time of MRD assessment, NGF positivity was significantly associated with inferior outcomes. The median follow‐up time from the landmark time point (MRD assessment) was 20.1 months (range, 0.7–73.4 months). Among 104 NDMM patients with NGF‐based MRD assessment, MRD negativity (n = 66, 63.5%) was associated with significantly different PFS compared to MRD positive patients (Median survival 62.6 vs. 27.5 months, Log‐rank p = 0.0321, Figure 3A). The 2‐year PFS rate was 75.8% in the NGF‐negative group compared to 50.0% in the NGF‐positive group. NGF positivity was associated with a trend towards inferior overall survival (Log‐rank p = 0.0374, Figure 3B). In univariable Cox regression analysis, NGF positivity was identified as a significant risk factor for both PFS (HR 2.25, 95% CI 1.08–4.70; p = 0.031) and OS (HR 3.41, 95% CI 1.00–11.66; p = 0.045), as detailed in Table 5. Patients who were NGF‐positive had a significantly higher risk of progression or death compared to NGF‐negative patients (adjusted HR 2.21, 95% CI 1.01–4.81; p = 0.046), independent of age, ISS stage, high‐risk cytogenetics, and ASCT status. Similarly, NGF positivity was a strong independent predictor of overall survival (adjusted HR 4.97, 95% CI 1.23–20.01; p = 0.026). Notably, in the multivariable OS model, ISS stage III (HR 4.81, 95% CI 1.12–20.73; p = 0.033) and ASCT (HR 8.63, 95% CI 1.73–42.92; p = 0.008) were also significant predictors (Table 6).

To investigate whether baseline characteristics could predict post‐induction MRD status, we performed logistic regression analysis to identify potential baseline predictors for persistent NGF‐MRD positivity (Table 7). In univariable analysis, older age was the only factor significantly associated with a higher likelihood of NGF positivity (OR 1.06 per year, 95% CI 1.00–1.11; p = 0.040). Other baseline characteristics, including high‐risk cytogenetics, ISS stage, and receipt of ASCT or daratumumab‐based therapy, were not predictive of MRD status. In the multivariable model, age maintained a borderline association (adjusted OR 1.06, 95% CI 1.00–1.12; p = 0.056), while no other factors reached statistical significance. This lack of strong correlation between baseline risk features and post‐treatment MRD status reinforces the concept that MRD is an independent biomarker of treatment response that cannot be reliably surrogated by baseline stratification.

Longitudinal MRD Status

Serial MRD assessments in 33 patients identified four distinct trajectories: sustained MRD negativity (≥ 2 consecutive MRD negative: n = 13), MRD conversion from MRD negative to MRD positive (n = 7; the shortest interval was 7 months), MRD clearance (MRD positive to MRD negative: n = 6), and persistent MRD positive (n = 7). Sustained negativity and MRD clearance correlated with superior PFS than any other groups (Figure 3C,D). Multivariable Cox regression demonstrated an 85.7% reduction in progression risk (HR = 0.143, 95% CI 0.017–1.194, p = 0.073) for sustained MRD undetectable patients.

Discussion

The negativity of MRD can predict outcomes of patients and can be included as an endpoint and for response adapted therapy [5, 15]. The optimal application of MRD assessment in real‐world clinical practice remains a subject of intense debate, primarily due to the heterogeneity of detection methods and unresolved questions about prognostic superiority. Currently, BM next generation multiparametric flow cytometry (NGF) and next generation sequencing (NGS) are being used to assess MRD and have been included in the International Myeloma Working Group (IMWG) criteria for defining MRD negative state [3]. However, a single bone marrow sample may not be sufficient to assess certain presentations of the disease, such as focal involvement or EMD, which can lead to false negative results [7, 16]. Therefore, functional imaging techniques like MRI and PET/CT are used as complementary methods for MRD evaluation.

Current guidelines appropriately restrict NGF‐driven MRD assessments to patients achieving complete response (CR), given the limited utility in partial responders. While MRD positivity persists in 20%–40% of CR patients, implementing NGF at the very good partial response (VGPR) stage risks false reassurance due to incomplete clonal clearance, arguing against premature adoption in suboptimal responders [5, 17]. Instead, emphasis should focus on serial NGF evaluations post‐CR to guide maintenance strategies, as sustained MRD negativity—typically defined by consecutive assessments at 6‐ to 12‐month intervals—has been shown to mitigate high‐risk genetic profiles, effectively homogenizing outcomes across R‐ISS stages. Paiva et al. [18] showed that MRD negativity homogenizes outcomes across R‐ISS stages, suggesting deep remission can counteract genetic risk.

FDG PET/CT is a valuable tool for evaluating disease burden in MM patients [8, 19], yet with high specificity rather than sensitivity. In our previous study, a more sensitive and specific tracer, 11C‐acetate labeled PET has been proved to detect more lesions than classical 18F‐FDG PET/CT in newly diagnosed MM patients. Here, we demonstrated again that 11C‐acetate PET/CT showed a numerically higher detection rate of MRD than FDG PET/CT, including both diffused and focal lesions, although the difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.375) in this paired cohort. The results were consistent with other studies. AC‐PET has also shown superiority in tumor burden and prognosis evaluation for other cancers [20, 21, 22] and newly diagnosed MM patients [13, 23, 24]. Consistent with prior case series by Ngai et al. [14], our findings suggest that AC‐PET may offer superior sensitivity for intramedullary disease compared to FDG‐PET. However, the broad application of 11C‐acetate is logistically limited by the need for an on‐site cyclotron due to the short half‐life of Carbon‐11. In this context, 18F‐labeled lipid tracers, such as 18F‐fluorocholine, offer a more scalable alternative. A recent study demonstrated the feasibility of 18F‐fluorocholine PET/CT for MRD assessment in multiple myeloma, showing promising lesion detection capabilities comparable to lipid‐pathway interrogation [17].

Functional imaging retains a niche role in addressing specific clinical dilemmas. For patients with baseline extramedullary disease (EMD) or discordant MRD status (e.g., NGF‐negative but metabolically active lesions), AC‐PET may provide critical complementary insights due to its enhanced avidity for extramedullary clones compared to FDG‐PET, albeit with lower sensitivity than NGF [19, 24]. Current evidence supports integrating AC‐PET into MRD workflows for EMD‐predominant cases or when clinical suspicion of progression contradicts NGF findings [8, 18]. These methodological trade‐offs underscore the importance of risk adapted MRD strategies, where molecular techniques define therapeutic endpoints while advanced imaging resolves spatial heterogeneity. MRD negativity by NGF correlated with markedly prolonged PFS and OS consolidating its role as a pivotal prognostic biomarker. Our landmark analysis, which strictly rigorously eliminated guarantee‐time bias, confirmed that NGF positivity is a robust independent predictor of inferior PFS and OS. This supports the IMWG emphasis on flow cytometry for MRD‐driven risk stratification, though PET/CT retains value in detecting EMD, as seen in three PET‐positive/NGF‐negative cases. The results were consistent with previous studies showing that the two methods complement each other in lesion detection [8, 10, 16, 25]. The prognostic value of PET/CT had also been discussed in several independent studies and meta‐analysis [4, 8, 19], and thus was acknowledged as a MRD evaluation tool [5].

Finally, the timing of MRD assessments warrants refinement. Although mid‐treatment evaluations (median 6 months post‐induction) may stratify early responders, long‐term outcomes depend on sustained negativity over ≥ 12 months [5, 15]. Harmonizing assessment intervals with treatment phases—aligning bone marrow and imaging evaluations at key decision points—could optimize prognostic resolution while minimizing noncontributory testing.

The study also had several limitations. Firstly, the sample size was relatively small and not all patients finished the dual tracer examination and NGF evaluation, which may cause selection bias. Secondly, although we intended to control the evaluation to be conducted simultaneously, some patients were unable to finish all the evaluations at once. Also, the time between the beginning of treatment and the response assessment by PET‐CT and NGF could vary, and this may interfere with survival results, causing guarantee‐time bias. Another limitation is that baseline PET/CT scans were only performed in around two‐thirds of patients (30/46). In the absence of a baseline reference, post‐treatment “negativity” in patients with potentially non‐avid disease at diagnosis might be misinterpreted as a metabolic response. Although NGF positivity was a strong predictor for OS, it should be noted that the hazard ratio in the multivariable model had a relatively wide confidence interval (95% CI 1.23–20.01), likely reflecting the limited number of death events in our cohort.

This study solidifies NGF as the standard for MRD assessment. AC‐PET plays a role in detecting extramedullary escape as a complementary method. Sustained MRD negativity emerges as a critical determinant of long‐term outcomes, transcending conventional risk stratification. Future protocols must integrate longitudinal NGF monitoring with advanced imaging to address spatial heterogeneity and guide response‐adapted therapy.
