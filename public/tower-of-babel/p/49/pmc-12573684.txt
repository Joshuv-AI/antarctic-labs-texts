ABSTRACT
PURPOSEAmong childhood cancer survivors, we evaluated not previously explored relationships between colorectal subsequent malignant neoplasm (SMN) incidence and colorectum-specific radiation dose metrics currently used in radiation therapy (RT) planning and expanded upon previously reported chemotherapy associations.METHODSThe Childhood Cancer Survivor Study (CCSS) includes 5-year survivors of childhood cancer diagnosed between 1970 and 1999. RT was assessed as mean colorectal dose (MCD) and the percent volume (VX Gy) receiving ≥5, 10, 20, 30, and 40 Gy. Chemotherapy was assessed as cumulative doses for procarbazine and platinum agents, cyclophosphamide-equivalent doses for alkylating agents, and doxorubicin-equivalent doses for anthracyclines. Piecewise-exponential models and excess rate ratio (ERR) models evaluated dose-response relationships for the incidence of colorectal SMNs. Reference groups were those not receiving the assessed treatment(s).RESULTSAmong 25,723 survivors (median follow-up = 28.5 years; range = 5.0-48.9), 104 colorectal SMNs were identified. A dose-response relationship was observed between MCD and colorectal SMN rates; incidence rate ratios (IRRs) for 10 to <20 Gy and ≥20 Gy were 3.6 (95% CI, 1.9 to 6.9) and 8.3 (95% CI, 3.9 to 17.8), respectively. When ≥20% of the colorectum volume was irradiated, IRRs increased with increasing volume. The V20 Gy IRRs were 3.8 (95% CI, 1.9 to 7.6), 4.9 (95% CI, 2.0 to 12.0), and 8.7 (95% CI, 3.5 to 21.6) for irradiated volumes of 20% to <40%, 40% to <80%, and ≥80%, respectively. The IRR was 1.8 (95% CI, 1.0 to 3.0) for doxorubicin-equivalent dose ≥250 mg/m2, 3.7 (95% CI, 2.2 to 6.4) for cyclophosphamide-equivalent dose ≥6,000 mg/m2, and 4.5 (95% CI, 2.0 to 10.1) for platinum dose ≥450 mg/m2. For procarbazine dose, the IRR was 6.3 (95% CI, 3.0 to 13.2) for 4,200 to <7,036 mg/m2 and 9.0 (95% CI, 4.3 to 18.9) for ≥7,036 mg/m2. In the absence of RT, colorectal SMN rates increased with exposure to any platinum-based agent (IRR, 3.8 [95% CI, 1.1 to 12.7]), alkylator (IRR, 4.8 [95% CI, 1.6 to 14.4]), or procarbazine (IRR, 16.9 [95% CI, 5.9 to 48.8]). Colorectal SMN rates increased linearly with procarbazine dose (ERR per 1,000 mg/m2 = 73.0 [95% CI, 26.4% to 119.6%]) and MCD (ERR per 1 Gy = 20.8 [95% CI, 9.0% to 32.5%]). Quadratic ERR models did not improve data fit compared with linear ERR models.CONCLUSIONThese RT and chemotherapy dose-response relationships can better inform contemporary RT planning for pediatric patients and surveillance guidelines for high-risk survivors.

INTRODUCTION

Subsequent malignant neoplasms (SMNs) are the second leading cause of death in childhood cancer survivors.1-4 The risk for a subsequent colorectal cancer is three- to four-fold higher in childhood cancer survivors compared with the general population.5,6 Both radiation therapy (RT) and chemotherapy are established risk factors for colorectal SMNs in childhood cancer survivors.6-15 However, previous studies evaluating chemotherapy and colorectal SMN risk in childhood cancer survivors were limited by small sample size, and RT studies have not considered RT doses specifically to the colorectum. For previously explored RT variables, categorical classification9-12 (RT as yes/no), SMN proximity categories,6,9 RT delivered to the abdomen and/or pelvis,11,13 and dose to the SMN site7,8,14 have been reported as risk factors for colorectal SMNs (or SMN studies that include colorectal cases) in childhood cancer survivors. Although these less specific RT metrics are valid descriptors, they do not take patient-specific RT field blocking into account and thus do not fully capture dose distributions across the colorectum or to specific colorectal segments.16 A recent case-control study17 among survivors of primarily adult-onset Hodgkin lymphoma reported a linear dose-response between colorectal-specific mean RT dose and colorectal SMNs, which was stronger with increasing cumulative procarbazine dose. Yet, there remains a paucity of data regarding RT metrics commonly used by the radiation oncology community that are applicable to patients with childhood cancer and survivors. Specifically, RT mean dose and dose-volume to the colorectum and its segments have not been investigated.

Colorectal cancer screening has reduced mortality from colorectal cancer in the general population.18-22 Thus, it is critical to identify treatment-related risk factors associated with colorectal SMNs to inform screening strategies in high-risk survivors. Additionally, for newly diagnosed children requiring abdomen and/or pelvic RT, establishing risk associated with dose-volume metrics could inform treatment plan optimization and/or selection of alternate treatment modalities (eg, proton v photon) to reduce colorectum dose. Therefore, the objectives of this study were to evaluate the associations between colorectal SMN incidence and colorectal-specific RT with dose-volume data and chemotherapeutic doses in a large childhood cancer survivor cohort.

METHODS

Population

The Childhood Cancer Survivor Study (CCSS) is a retrospective cohort with longitudinal follow-up of 25,723 five-year survivors of childhood cancer diagnosed between 1970 and 1999 with a variety of malignancies before age 21 years at one of 31 North American institutions. Cohort details were previously published.23-25

SMN Ascertainment

Within the CCSS cohort, 104 colorectal SMNs were identified from self-report questionnaires or the National Death Index and confirmed by pathology report, medical record, or death certificate review. The anatomic site and pathologic subtype of colorectal malignancies were based on the International Classification of Diseases for Oncology, Third Edition (ICD-O-3), with a fifth digit behavior code of 3.26 For tumor location, ICD-O-3 site codes were categorized as colon-only or rectum-only and by colorectal subsites for analyses (Data Supplement, Table S1, online only).

Treatment Exposures

Demographic and primary cancer-related characteristics were collected from questionnaire responses.25 Patient records were reviewed for cancer treatment exposures.25 Treatment exposures included as yes/no qualitative variables were chemotherapy, chemotherapeutic group types, procarbazine, nonprocarbazine alkylating agents,15 stem-cell transplant, RT, RT to specific body regions, and total-body irradiation. Chemotherapy cumulative doses included doxorubicin-equivalent dose27 for anthracyclines, cyclophosphamide-equivalent dose28 for alkylating agents, and platinum cumulative dose29,30 for platinum agents. We included procarbazine because previous studies reported increased colorectal SMN risk with this specific alkylating agent.6,17,31 For nonprocarbazine alkylating agents, procarbazine exposure/dose was removed from the cumulative alkylating agent dose (Data Supplement, Table S2, contains a list of specific agents and their frequency of use). RT dose variables included the maximum target RT dose to the abdomen or pelvis, colorectal maximum RT dose, mean colorectal RT dose (mean colorectal dose [MCD]), and mean RT dose to each colorectal segment. Dose-volume data consisted of the percent of colorectal volume receiving ≥5(V5 Gy), 10(V10 Gy), 20(V20 Gy), 30(V30 Gy), and 40(V40 Gy) Gy. We included V5 Gy and V10 Gy with maximum dose <20 Gy (V20 Gy = 0%) to evaluate the effect of low doses to large volumes. Our validated colorectum RT dosimetry methods were previously published16,32-34 and are briefly summarized in the Data Supplement. Dose-volume metrics are visually illustrated in the Data Supplement (Fig S1).

Statistical Analysis

We calculated standardized incidence ratios (SIRs) and absolute excess risks (AERs) for colorectal SMN incidence for all CCSS participants compared with expected number in the general population from SEER.35 Demographics, health behaviors, and treatment exposures were considered. Cumulative incidences of colorectal SMN overall and by subgroup were estimated using years since primary childhood cancer diagnosis accounting for death as the competing risk; incidences were also calculated using attained age.36 Incidence rates by age at colorectal cancer diagnosis were calculated for the overall CCSS cohort and by high-risk groups and were compared with general population rates from SEER.37

Multivariable piecewise-exponential models assessed the adjusted associations of the treatment-related risk factors and multiplicative interactions between RT and chemotherapy with colorectal SMN incidence. Adjusted IRRs were calculated from piecewise-exponential models adjusted for several factors on the basis of a priori hypotheses and for significant (P < .05) univariate treatment-related factors (univariate screening details and results are provided in the Data Supplement, Table S3). We assessed collinearity between MCD with each dose-volume metric using correlation coefficients (results and discussion provided in the Data Supplement).

Subanalyses restricted the analysis cohort as follows: for chemotherapy variables, we reported multivariable models restricted to survivors not treated with RT to evaluate chemotherapy effects while fully excluding the potential residual confounding effect of RT. For nonprocarbazine alkylating agents, we excluded survivors treated with procarbazine. Similarly, for RT variables, we excluded survivors treated with procarbazine.

For MCD and procarbazine dose, we reported adjusted excess rate ratio (ERR) models to quantify the excess risk of colorectal SMN associated with each unit increase of dose (ie, gray for RT dose and mg/m2 for procarbazine doses). ERR models were adjusted for the same variables used in the adjusted piecewise-exponential models. We compared ERR models with the linear RT form and quadratic RT form using a likelihood ratio test.38 We also examined if there is an additive interaction between MCD and procarbazine dose.

Interactions between MCD and procarbazine were tested in the piecewise-exponential model and the ERR model using the likelihood ratio test. P values for interaction were adjusted for multiple testing of seven interactions by Bonferroni correction. All reported P values were two-sided and considered statistically significant at the .05 level. All statistical analyses were performed using SAS (version 9.4; SAS Institute, Cary, NC).

RESULTS

Table 1 provides demographic and treatment characteristics of the survivors (N = 25,723) as well as clinical and pathologic characteristics of the colorectal SMN cases (n = 104). The median time from primary cancer diagnosis to colorectal SMN diagnosis was 29.9 years (IQR, 24.0-35.4). The median age at colorectal SMN diagnosis was 39.7 years (IQR, 31.8-45.2). Nineteen (18%) cases occurred in individuals younger than 30 years. Thirty-three patients with a colorectal SMN were deceased, of whom 20 (60.6%) died of colorectal SMN.

SIRs and AERs

The SIR for colorectal SMN was 2.6-fold higher in childhood cancer survivors than in the general population with 9.4 additional colorectal SMN cases per 100,000 person-years (Table 2). SIRs were elevated for survivors who received anthracyclines (SIR, 3.3 [95% CI, 2.4 to 4.4]), alkylators (SIR, 3.8 [95% CI, 3.0 to 4.8]), abdominal and/or pelvic RT (SIR, 5.3 [95% CI, 4.0 to 6.9]), platinum-based agents (SIR, 5.9 [95% CI, 3.0 to 10.6]), and procarbazine (SIR, 7.9 [95% CI, 5.5 to 11.0]). Among treatment-related variables, the AER was highest (61.6 per 100,000 person-years) for those exposed to procarbazine.

Chemotherapy Effects: Piecewise-Exponential Modeling

In multivariable models for chemotherapy associations (Fig 2; Data Supplement, Table S6), rates increased for survivors who received any chemotherapy (IRR, 2.2 [95% CI, 1.1 to 4.6]), anthracycline (IRR, 1.7 [95% CI, 1.1 to 2.6]), alkylating agent (IRR, 2.7 [95% CI, 1.6 to 4.4]), platinum agent (IRR, 3.2 [95% CI, 1.6 to 6.1]), or procarbazine (IRR, 5.5 [95% CI, 3.1 to 9.9]) compared with those not exposed. For dose-response analysis, increased rates were observed for doxorubicin-equivalent dose ≥250 mg/m2 (IRR, 1.8 [95% CI, 1.0 to 3.0]), cyclophosphamide-equivalent dose ≥6,000 mg/m2 (IRR, 3.7 [95% CI, 2.2 to 6.4]), and platinum dose ≥450 mg/m2 (IRR, 4.5 [95% CI, 2.0 to 10.1]). For procarbazine, increased rates were observed for cumulative dose ≥4,200 mg/m2 with IRRs of 6.3 (95% CI, 3.0 to 13.2) and 9.0 (95% CI, 4.3 to 18.9) for doses of 4,200 to <7,036 and ≥7,036 mg/m2, respectively. When restricting chemotherapy multivariable models to survivors without RT, increased IRRs were observed for those exposed to any alkylating agent (IRR, 4.8 [95% CI, 1.6 to 14.4]), procarbazine (IRR, 16.9 [95% CI, 5.9 to 48.8]), or platinum-based agent (IRR, 3.8 [95% CI, 1.1 to 12.7]; Fig 2B; Data Supplement, Table S7). For nonprocarbazine alkylating agents, when restricting the analysis to survivors without procarbazine (Fig 2C), the high-dose category of ≥6,000 mg/m2 was significant (IRR, 2.6 [95% CI, 1.3 to 4.9]).

Radiation Effects: Piecewise-Exponential Modeling

In multivariable models, survivors who received maximum colorectal RT doses 10 to <20 Gy and ≥20 Gy had IRRs of 2.6 (95% CI, 1.1 to 6.0) and 3.0 (95% CI, 1.7 to 5.5), respectively (Fig 3A). Colorectal SMN rates increased with increasing mean RT doses ≥10 Gy to the colorectum or individual colorectal segments (all P < .01, except sigmoid; Data Supplement, Table S6). Multivariable RT models were also provided for MCD in 5-Gy bins (Data Supplement, Table S8) and restricted to survivors without procarbazine (Fig 3B). For each dose-volume metric (Fig 3C), rates increased with increasing irradiated volume above 20%. For example, for V20 Gy, the IRR was 3.8 (95% CI, 1.9 to 7.6), 4.9 (95% CI, 2.0 to 12.0), and 8.7 (95% CI, 3.5 to 21.6) for irradiated colorectal volumes of 20% to <40%, 40% to <80%, and ≥80%, respectively (Data Supplement, Fig S4A). Likewise, IRRs increased as the dose to a given volume of colorectum increased. For example, when 20%-40% of the colorectum was irradiated, the IRR was 2.8 (95% CI, 1.3 to 5.9), 3.8 (95% CI, 1.9 to 7.6), 4.5 (95% CI, 2.1 to 9.7), and 5.4 (95% CI, 2.0 to 14.3) when doses were ≥5, 20, 30, and 40 Gy, respectively (Data Supplement, Fig S4B). We also assessed the effect of low dose to large volumes by considering V5 Gy and V10 Gy with maximum dose <20 Gy (V20 Gy = 0%) and did not observe a clear trend of colorectal SMN risk, which may be due to limited sample size (Data Supplement, Table S6). To our knowledge, our study is the first to assess colorectal SMN risk for specific colorectal segments (ascending, transverse, descending, and sigmoid colon and rectum) using segment-specific mean RT dose; similar rates were observed across the different segments (results in the Data Supplement, Table S6, and discussion in the Data Supplement). No multiplicative interaction was found between RT and chemotherapy for colorectal SMN incidence after Bonferroni correction (Data Supplement, Table S9).

ERR Modeling

With linear ERR modeling, the colorectal SMN rate increased by 73.0% (95% CI, 26.4% to 119.6%) per 1,000 mg/m2 increase in procarbazine dose and by 20.8% (95% CI, 9.0 to 32.5) per 1 Gy increase in MCD (Fig 4). Quadratic ERR models did not improve the fit compared with the corresponding linear models, suggesting no significant departure from linearity for procarbazine (P = .18) or for MCD (P = .58). In the linear ERR RT dose model, there was no statistically significant additive interaction between MCD and procarbazine when considered as either yes/no (P = .26) or categorical dose (P = .72) variables.

DISCUSSION

To our knowledge, this is the most extensive study of colorectal SMN risk among survivors of childhood cancer, comprehensively reporting risk associated with cumulative doses of chemotherapeutic agents and the distribution of RT dose across the colorectum. Consistent with our findings, previous studies of childhood cancer survivors have shown associations between gastrointestinal or colorectal SMN risk and alkylating agents,8,15 platinum drugs,6 and anthracyclines,7 but few studies have assessed dose-dependent relationships with anthracyclines,7 procarbazine,6,17 and platinum agents.40 Results of previous studies were not statistically significant7,40 or were not focused specifically on colorectal SMNs6 in pediatric populations.17,40 For dose-response relationships by chemotherapeutic groups, we observed that colorectal SMN rates increased with increasing cumulative doses of anthracyclines, alkylating agents, or platinum-based agents. For procarbazine, rates also increased with increasing cumulative dose in survivors with or without RT. Two previous studies reported a dose response with procarbazine. One study of colorectal SMN risk in survivors of primarily adult Hodgkin lymphoma17 reported a rate ratio of 2.9 for procarbazine doses >8,400 mg/m2. Another study of GI6 SMN risk in childhood cancer survivors reported a hazard ratio of 3.15 for procarbazine doses ≥7,036 mg/m2. Our study, focused only on colorectal SMN in childhood cancer survivors, also reported a dose response with procarbazine, but reported a higher risk (IRR, 9.0; 95% CI, 4.3 to 18.9 for ≥7,036 mg/m2) than either of those studies at the same/similar dose level. Of note, procarbazine dose ≥7,036 mg/m2 had a rate of similar magnitude to a MCD ≥20 Gy (IRR, 8.3 [95% CI, 3.9 to 17.8]). We also reported that colorectal SMN rates increased linearly with procarbazine dose (ERR of 73.0% per 1,000 mg/m2). In unrestricted and restricted analyses, for nonprocarbazine alkylating agents, the yes/no effect was marginally significant, while the high-dose category (≥6,000 mg/m2) was significant. Of these agents, the most frequently used was cyclophosphamide followed by nitrogen mustard (mechlorethamine), which could be the focus of future studies (Data Supplement, Table S2).

Childhood cancer survivors who received ≥20 Gy MCD had >12-fold higher risk (SIR, 12.6 [95% CI, 6.7 to 21.5]) than the age-sex–matched general population and their AER was >1 additional case per 1,000 survivors annually. This group's absolute risk is approximately equal to the rate of the US population at age 60s for whom screening is already recommended. We observed increased rates for MCD 10 to <20 Gy (IRR, 3.6 [95% CI, 1.9 to 6.9]) and ≥20 Gy (IRR, 8.3 [95% CI, 3.9 to 17.8]), but not for 0.1 to <10 Gy (IRR, 1.1 [95% CI, 0.6 to 2.0]). Furthermore, we observed a dose-response relationship even in the absence of procarbazine (Fig 3B). While a recent case-control study,17 among survivors of primarily adult-onset Hodgkin lymphoma, reported a linear dose-response between MCD and colorectal SMNs, to our knowledge, our study is the first to report an association in a pediatric cohort. Previous studies assessing the RT dose-response effect on colorectal SMNs in childhood cancer survivors were limited to prescription/delivered dose or local RT dose at the SMN site.7,14 Here, we observed greater stratification of IRRs when assessed by MCD as opposed to abdomen and/or pelvic body region maximum target RT dose, that is, local prescription/delivered dose (Fig 3). For abdomen and/or pelvic body region RT doses, rates were elevated increased for doses of 10 to <20 Gy (IRR, 3.3 [95% CI, 1.5 to 7.2]) and ≥20 Gy (IRR, 3.3 [95% CI, 2.0 to 5.4]), but the difference between these dose groups was not statistically significant. By contrast, when IRRs were evaluated by MCD, the IRR for 10 to <20 Gy (IRR, 3.6 [95% CI, 1.9 to 6.9]) was 2.3-fold lower than for the dose group ≥20 Gy (IRR, 8.3 [95% CI, 3.9 to 17.8]). This observation highlights that colorectum-specific dosimetry is more individualized, accounting for the shape of the colorectum and regions of the colorectum shielded by patient-specific treatment field blocks.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to quantify the relationship between colorectal SMN risk and RT dose-volume metrics applicable in contemporary pediatric radiation oncology. Specifically, as the percent volume receiving ≥5, 20, 30, and 40 Gy increased, rates increased when the irradiated colorectal volume exceeded 20%. Rates markedly increased when large volumes of the colorectum received high doses, with IRRs as high as 11.8 for V40 Gy ≥ 40% and 21.3 for V30 Gy ≥ 80%. We also observed increased rates for high doses to small volumes (IRR, 5.3 for V40 Gy of 0.1% to <20%). Two previous studies8,13 assessed volume effects on colorectal carcinoma8 or adenoma13 risk using a surrogate exposure measure: the number of irradiated colorectum segments8 or an assigned value of percent colorectum irradiated on the basis of RT field type.13 Although these studies assessed volume effects, dose-volume metrics, which are commonly used in radiation oncology, were not explored.

We did not observe a multiplicative interaction between RT and chemotherapy on colorectal SMN rates, which was also not seen in previous studies of survivors of primarily adult-onset survivors of Hodgkin lymphoma17,31 and adult survivors of testicular cancer.40 However, it is possible that our study, with 104 cases, was underpowered to detect such an interaction. Although an interaction was not observed, we observed that exposure to any alkylator, procarbazine, or platinum-based agent was associated with increased colorectal SMN rates in the absence of exposure to any RT (Fig 2B).

We observed that excess colorectal SMN rate increased linearly with MCD (ERR per 1 Gy 20.8%; 95% CI, 9.0% to 32.5%). Although our ERR estimate is higher than the 9.2% overall ERR per Gy (95% CI, 2.5% to 23.0%) reported for survivors of primarily adult-onset Hodgkin lymphoma,17 the overlapping confidence intervals indicate that true risk may be similar. Solid cancer studies in other populations, for example, therapeutic radiation exposure41 and at atomic bomb (neutron) exposure,42,43 also indicate higher values for children exposed to ionizing radiation at younger versus older ages. For MCD, the quadratic ERR model did not improve the data fit compared with linear ERR models. This finding was also observed in an atomic bomb study.43 A linear model suggests that there may be no threshold dose below which there is no added colorectal cancer risk from RT. Although the V5 Gy piecewise-exponential model shows no risk below 20% volume irradiated (IRR, 1.0 for V5 Gy of 0.1% to <20%), risk is elevated when moderate volumes 20%-40% (IRR, 2.8 [95% CI, 1.3 to 5.9]) and large volumes 40% to <80% (IRR, 3.9 [95% CI, 1.9 to 8.0]) and ≥80% (IRR, 5.2 [95% CI, 2.7 to 10.2]) receive ≥5 Gy. Taken together, these findings highlight the importance of reducing MCD and V5 Gy in contemporary RT treatment planning as much as possible without compromising dose coverage of the tumor volume. Moreover, since contemporary RT techniques, such as intensity-modulated RT, increase low-dose radiation to surrounding normal tissue,44 understanding colorectal SMN risk in low-dose regions (as quantified by the V5 Gy model) may become more relevant for the growing population of survivors treated with contemporary RT techniques for whom empiric evidence on SMN risk is virtually lacking to date.45-48

As clinicians evaluate colorectal SMN risk in long-term survivors of pediatric cancer, the dose-response relationships by chemotherapeutic groups reported here are valuable new knowledge. However, colorectal-specific RT doses are not yet relevant in survivorship care because this level of dosimetry was not reported for survivors treated in earlier eras of RT. As such, colorectal SMN risk could be assessed on the basis of the most detailed RT data available in patients' medical records, which may include any RT, abdomen and/or pelvic RT, or total prescribed dose to the abdomen and/or pelvis. To meet that continued need, this study also reports IRRs on the basis of those metrics. Currently, on the basis of recommendations from the US Children's Oncology Group (COG), the only treatment exposures that result in early colorectal cancer screening are any exposure to abdominal, pelvic, spinal (lumbar, sacral, or whole), or total-body RT.49 On the basis of our findings, it may also be important for exposure to any alkylating agent (IRR, 2.7 [95% CI, 1.6 to 4.4]) or any platinum-based agent (IRR, 3.2 [95% CI, 1.6 to 6.1]) to be considered in guidelines as we observed similar rates in multivariable analysis as was observed with any exposure to any abdominal and/or pelvic RT (IRR, 3.3 [95% CI, 2.1 to 5.3]). Additionally, our findings suggest that detailed dose-response data should also be considered to start early screening, such as high RT dose to large colorectal volumes (V30 Gy > 80%), MCDs ≥10 Gy, alkylating dose ≥6,000 mg/m2, and/or platinum-based dose ≥450 mg/m2.

As for the age to initiate screening, the same rate (30.5 per 100,000) in which colorectal cancer screening is recommended for the general population at age 45 years39 was observed at younger ages for the overall CCSS cohort (37.9 years), and for survivors who received any chemotherapy (37.6 years), alkylating agent (33.1 years), abdominal/pelvic RT (29.4 years), or procarbazine (25.6 years). Our findings for the RT risk group are in agreement with the current US COG guidelines,49 which recommends screening 5 years after RT or at age 30 years (whichever occurs last). For exposure to procarbazine, our results indicate that earlier screening (age 25.6 years) could be considered for survivors who received any procarbazine; however, studies with larger sample size are needed to further confirm the initiation of earlier screening on the basis of procarbazine exposure.

In contemporary radiation oncology, multiple different treatment modalities (eg, proton and photon) as well as image guidance and even motion management strategies are available and can be selected on a case-by-case basis to optimize RT with the aim of delivering the full prescription dose to the target volume while minimizing dose to organs at risk. Key to optimization is the definition of organ-at-risk dose constraints to minimize specific acute and late effects. In alignment with this common practice, associations between colorectal SMN risk and colorectal-specific RT dose and dose-volume metrics can be immediately translated into pediatric RT treatment planning. Beyond the keeping of colorectal RT doses as low as possible, constraints could be set to spare colorectum dose, for example, mean dose <10 Gy, V20 Gy <20%, and maximum dose <40 Gy. When achieved, colorectal SMN risk may be reduced. When not achieved, these dose-volume metrics can be reported in the medical record and allow for future risk assessment. Moreover, a 2024 report from the Pediatric Normal Tissue Effects in the Clinic called for the need for dose-volume–based risk assessment, but also cited a lack of epidemiologic studies that included risk models on the basis of detailed organ-specific dosimetry.46,50 This work is a first step toward filling that gap in knowledge.

In summary, this study provides a comprehensive report of colorectal SMN risk in childhood cancer survivors associated with cumulative doses of chemotherapeutic agents and the distribution of RT dose across the colorectum, that is, dose-volume metrics. Our findings are directly applicable to contemporary pediatric oncology practice and survivorship care, and of potential relevance for consideration in colorectal SMN surveillance guidelines as detailed RT dose-volume metrics become more available in the medical records of future survivors of childhood cancer.
