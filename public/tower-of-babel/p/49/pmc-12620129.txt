Testing Sonication and Different Transport Media to Enhance Microbial Detection in Hard‐to‐Heal Venous Leg Ulcers
Authors: Adéla Holubová, Niels A. J. Cremers, Martin Krause, Lada Cetlová, Jana Matějková, Andrea Pokorná
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHard‐to‐heal wounds are frequently associated with underlying conditions such as diabetes, vascular disease, and biofilm‐related infections. Accurate identification of microbial origin is essential, but is often hindered by biofilms. This study evaluated whether sonication of wound dressings, combined with different sample transport methods, improves bacterial detection in venous leg ulcers. In a prospective observational case–control study, six patients with hard‐to‐heal venous leg ulcers received treatments with medical‐grade honey (MGH) (n = 1), ceramic dressings – “Cerdak” (n = 2), or hydroactive dressings (n = 3). Three microbiological samples were collected per patient: (1) conventional wound swab (Levin's technique) in Amies medium; (2) sonicate fluid from used dressings transported in sterile tubes without medium; and (3) sonicate fluid in haemoculture tubes (BACT/ALERT). Total pathogen count and diversity were compared across sampling methods. Patient quality of life (QoL) was assessed using the Wound‐QoL instrument. Sonication revealed additional pathogens not detected by conventional swabs. The highest number of pathogens was found in sonicate fluid transported in haemoculture tubes (n = 43), followed by swabs (n = 39) and sterile tube transport (n = 30). Adequate treatment significantly improved patients' QoL during the healing process. Dressing sonication, especially with haemoculture tube transport, enhances microbial identification and may improve diagnosis and management of hard‐to‐heal wounds.

Summary This study evaluated whether sonication of wound dressings, combined with different transport media, improves bacterial detection compared with conventional swabs in hard‐to‐heal venous leg ulcers.In a prospective case series (n = 6), sonication fluid transported in haemoculture tubes (BACT/ALERT) revealed the highest number of pathogens (n = 43), followed by swabs (n = 39) and sterile‐tube sonicates (n = 30).Findings suggest that sonication with haemoculture tube transport may enhance microbial detection and could serve as a useful complementary diagnostic approach in the management of hard‐to‐heal wounds, warranting further investigation in larger cohorts.

Introduction

The number of hard‐to‐heal wounds continues to increase [1]. Based on the 2019 data, chronic nonhealing wounds impact 10.5 million (up 2.3 million from the 2014 update) of U.S. Medicare beneficiaries [2]. The increase in the prevalence of chronic wounds is associated with an increase in non‐infectious diseases with significant morbidity or disabling potential, as well as life‐threatening complications. The most common wounds are driven by metabolic disruptions (e.g., diabetes), vascular deficits (e.g., venous or arterial insufficiency), or mechanical impacts (e.g., persistent/localised pressure). The term “chronic wound” has traditionally been used to describe wounds that fail to progress through the normal phases of healing within an expected timeframe. However, this terminology is increasingly regarded as outdated, as the word “chronic” is commonly associated with non‐curable or lifelong conditions, which can inadvertently suggest that these wounds are not expected to heal. To reflect a more accurate and optimistic understanding of the healing potential, the term “hard‐to‐heal wound” is now preferred. This term acknowledges that with appropriate assessment, interventions, and resources, these wounds have the potential to progress toward healing. Hard‐to‐heal wounds are a global problem, costing millions of dollars a year in developed countries, and are characterised by microbial complications, including local or systemic infection, presence of biofilms, delayed healing, and the proliferation of multidrug‐resistant bacterial strains [3]. A hard‐to‐heal wound, also named a difficult‐to‐heal wound, is a skin lesion that heals unusually slowly. A hard‐to‐heal wound is usually present for at least 4 weeks, depending on the definition [4]. Hard‐to‐heal wounds have a major impact on the quality of life (QoL) of the patient and their caregivers and a negative financial impact on the healthcare economy [5, 6]. Therefore, the goal of treating hard‐to‐heal wounds is not only to heal them but also to relieve unpleasant wound symptoms, such as pain and odour [7]. An objective assessment of the general condition of the patient, the QoL of the patient with a hard‐to‐heal wound, the wound base and its surroundings, and the microorganism at the base of the wound, is very important to develop a qualitative treatment plan that will lead to a healing process and thus reduce the cost of treatment [8, 9, 10, 11].

An individualised approach that can consider the unique requirements and preferences of the patient is essential to achieve an effective healing process. Hard‐to‐heal wounds are often colonised, which leads to dysregulated hyperinflammation, sometimes referred to as “granulitis” by various types of microorganisms. Redness, swelling, increase in local temperature, tissue damage, and pain are local signs of wound inflammation. Prolonged healing, presence of abnormal granulation tissue (hypergranulation, fiery granulation tissue), increase in wound size, and increased amount of exudate are other signs of inflammation in hard‐to‐heal wounds [12].

The patient's skin is populated with bacteria, fungi, viruses, and mites that maintain the balance. Disruption of this system leads to skin infection. Invasive strains of bacteria can result in a delayed healing process, inflammation of the wound and surrounding area, or persistent inflammation [13]. Hard‐to‐heal wounds are often colonised by biofilm. This is a community of microorganisms that consists of diverse colonies enclosed in a matrix that protects them. Biofilms play a key role in the persistence of infection and antimicrobial resistance. Biofilm is responsible for prolonging wound healing time and the development of inflammation. Studies report a prevalence of biofilm in chronic wounds ranging from 20% to 100%, making it a topic of considerable attention in wound healing. The polymicrobial nature of biofilms in chronic wounds creates a synergistic effect that leads to non‐virulent bacterial species becoming virulent and causing host damage [14]. Biofilms represent a reservoir of potentially infectious microorganisms that are resistant to antimicrobial agents [13].

Many different antimicrobial agents exist, such as antibiotics, silver, honey, iodine, hypochlorous acid, polyhexanide/betaine complex, and gentian violet/methylene blue. Antibiotic therapy based solely on the presence of bacteria without incorporating local signs of inflammation is not an appropriate approach and is potentially dangerous regarding the emergence of polyresistant microbial strains, ultimately leading to increased costs of therapy and waste of resources [15, 16]. However, given the increasing rate of antibiotic resistance, the use of antibiotic treatment should be refined. In the fight against the spread of resistance, the correct use of antibiotics is a priority [3, 17]. Alternative antimicrobial agents, such as wound dressings that contain medical‐grade honey (MGH) or silver particles, are clinically proven to be effective and should be considered. Quality care should limit the spread of antibiotic resistance [1]. Microorganisms at the wound bed can be detected by swabs or sonic fluid analysis. The sensitivity and specificity of pathogen detection and their amounts in the swab and the sonication fluid obtained from the used wound dressings are the focus of this study. In our study, we compared the detection of microorganisms from a wound swab (transport in a sterile tube with Amies transport medium), sonication fluid from wound dressing material (transport in a sterile tube without transport medium), and sonication fluid collection with sample transport in a haemoculture tube (BACT/ALERT Culture Media Bottles). Sonication is a laboratory method that can be used to release planktonic forms of bacteria from the surface of objects without damaging them. Sonication is a laboratory method that can be used to break down biofilms and recover planktonic forms of microorganisms without damaging their cells [18]. The main objective of the study was to determine which laboratory method would allow for more accurate detection of pathogens at the base of the wound. Another aim was to assess whether bacterial load (quantity of pathogens) would be detected in the samples collected and to test whether the sonication method would allow more accurate detection of bacterial load than the wound base swab. Also, the influence of the transportation medium on the number of detected pathogens was investigated.

Materials and Methods

Patient Selection

In a prospective observational study—case–control series, we plan to monitor and treat patients with leg ulcers of venous aetiology. The selected patients were treated with MGH‐based wound care dressings (L‐Mesitran range), hydroactive, and ceramic dressings (“Cerdak”). At periodic intervals (14 to 30 days), samples were taken from the wound bed and the dressing materials. Wound swabs were taken using the Levine technique upon the first presentation in our department to determine bacterial colonisation [19]. Standard laboratory protocols and molecular testing using matrix‐assisted laser desorption/ionisation time of flight mass spectrometry (MALDI‐ToF MS) were followed to identify the microorganisms and to determine their susceptibility and resistance profiles [20]. The dressing material was removed from the wound under sterile conditions and placed in a sterile container containing sterile physiological solution. The trapped bacteria can then be released using ultrasonic energy to form a sonicate that can be analysed bacteriologically. The wound bed swab was sent in a sterile tube with Amies transport medium, the first sonicate fluid was transported in a sterile tube without transport medium, and the second sonicate fluid was transported in a haemoculture tube (BACT/ALERT Culture Media Bottles) (Figure 1).

The method of sampling (schematic description).

The number of pathogens captured in all three samples was monitored. Sonication is a laboratory method by which biofilms can be disintegrated to obtain planktonic forms of microorganisms without damaging their cells [18]. It is thus possible to capture bacterial strains that cannot always be detected with standard swabs. Sonication is an alternative method that can be used for ‘extended’ bacterial diagnostics and the capture of strains that are also involved in biofilm formation. In clinical practice, we use a BSTSONIC sonicator with a power of 480 W and a frequency of 40 kHz, with a timer from 1 min, cleaning medium temperature 23°C. This temperature and sonication time were used to create the sonication fluid. These values should ensure that the pathogens present in the sonicated matrix do not die. The sonication was performed to augment bacterial diagnostics, for the detection of bacterial strains that cannot be detected with a standard wound swab, for the detection of biofilm‐forming bacteria, and for more detailed documentation of the bacterial colonisation of the wound.

A total of six patients were included in our prospective case–control study (Table 1). The average age was 62.8 years (min. 40 and max. 73, with a median of 67.5 years). All patients had been diagnosed with leg ulcers with the presence of hard‐to‐heal wounds with treatment lasting more than 6 weeks. Previous therapy consisted of iodinated povidone solution, ointment, polyhexanide antiseptic solution, wound gel, greasy tulle and chloramphenicol. All patients showed local signs of wound infection, and signs of systemic infection were absent. The average assessment of pain intensity during the day reached 5.5 points, procedural pain averaged 7.8 points according to VAS (0 to 10 points scale). Still, one patient had analgesic therapy. Therefore, the pain value was reduced.

Wound Care and Assessment Details

The basic treatment process was the same in all patients. Mechanical debridement of the wound bed (Debrisoft and UCS Debridement), dressing with antiseptic solution for 15 min. (NAWAlution), application of protective spray (Cutimed PROTECT Spray) on the periwound area were performed. The protective surrounding spray was used both to protect the wound edges and area from exudate, but also to prevent contamination of the dressings by pathogens from the skin. Patients performed mechanical debridement themselves at home, which helped with wound hygiene and the healing process. Showering of the wound and its area, as well as wound hygiene (according to Defying hard‐to‐heal wounds with an early antibiofilm intervention strategy: wound hygiene) [23], were performed at every dressing. Five patients performed the dressing themselves, and one patient was cared for by home care nurses. The first follow‐up examination was performed 14 to 30 days after the local treatment was instituted. Wounds were assessed according to the “Wound Assessment Triangle” designed for comprehensive wound assessment [24]. Pain was assessed using the visual analogue scale (VAS; 0–10 points), for both daily and procedural pain (during the procedure—dressing changes). Body mass index and detection of lower limb ischemic disease (performed using the toe brachial index method) were assessed at the initial examination. The patient's subjective feelings about QoL with hard‐to‐heal wounds were assessed at every examination using the Questionnaire on QoL with chronic wounds [25].

Patients were informed about the study, and all gave written consent for their photographs and data to be used for publication, provided their anonymity and privacy were guaranteed. The principles of the World Medical Association's Declaration of Helsinki were followed.

Results

Case 1

A 62‐year‐old female patient presented with a venous leg ulcer at right lower leg (Figure 2a). Comorbidities included diabetes mellitus (DM) (oral antidiabetic drugs, insulin therapy, initial glycemia 9.1 mmol/L, glycated haemoglobin 81 mmol/mol), hypertension (HT), chronic venous insufficiency (CVI), obesity (BMI 53.9), sleep apnoea, erysipelas on the right leg, and cataract (awaiting cataract surgery). The toe brachial index (TBI) was 1.46 on the right toe and 0.84 on the left toe. Upon presentation, the wound was present for more than 4 months and previous treatment with povidone‐iodine solution and ointment, and polyhexanide antiseptic solution were ineffective.

Local treatment therapy L‐Mesitran Ointment (MGH), L‐Mesitran Tulle (MGH), Zetuvit Plus Silicone border (Absorbent dressing) (b) Follow‐up examination on day 15. (c) Follow‐up examination on day 33. Wound dimensions were 2 cm in length, 5.5 cm in width, and 1.5 cm in depth. The wound consisted of roughly 85% granulation tissue and 15% slough. Medium levels of exudate (thin/watery) were produced. Peri wound skin was without pathological signs. Local signs of infection included local warmth, exudate, delayed healing, and malodour. At the initial time point, the daytime pain level was 4 and during treatment 6 (VAS 0 to 10). Wound bed swabs showed positive colonisation with one microorganism— Serratia marcescens . MGH was indicated for the local treatment, L‐Mesitran Ointment was applied inside the lesion and covered by L‐Mesitran Tulle to ensure constant contact of MGH with the wound bed. Zetuvit Plus Silicone border was applied as a secondary dressing. The patient performed wound dressings at 48 h intervals at home. In addition, the application of compression bandages was recommended. At the first follow‐up examination, the wound improved, and its area reduced (Figure 2b). The wound bed consisted of 90% granulation tissue and 10% slough. Local warmth, exudate, and malodour resolved and disappeared after 14 days of treatment. In the swab, two bacteria were indicated—newly Staphylococcus aureus —the assumption is that there was contamination of the wound bed during dressings in the home environment or it could be also influenced by the release of pathogens from the biofilm. There were three pathogens in the sonicate in the haemoculture— S. marcescens, S. aureus and Granulicatella adiacens (Table 2). Hygiene of the wound and surrounding area was performed by the patient before the arrival of the home care nurses. Nurses performed the dressing and application of compression bandages. However, despite the education on hygiene and regular follow‐up, the wound gradually became contaminated with other pathogens until local inflammation developed. At the follow‐up examination on day 91 (Figure 2f), pathogens identified at the wound bed were— S. aureus (heavy growth, +++), Streptococcus agalactiae (heavy growth, +++), Klebsiella oxytoca (heavy growth, +++), S. marcescens. Due to the signs of local inflammation and overall symptoms, the patient was indicated to antibiotic therapy (Amoxicillin + clavulanate 1 tablet every 8 h) and the topical treatment materials were changed. Gentamicin gel (manufactured in the pharmacy) and Aquacel Ag + Extra were used for the wound bed, and RespoSorb Super was chosen as a secondary dressing due to the large wound secretion. After the application of antibiotic treatment (topical and systemic) and change of topical treatment, the local findings around the wound and wound bed improved (results no longer part of this study).

Case 2

A 56‐year‐old female patient presented with a venous leg ulcer at left lower leg (Figure 3a). Comorbidities included CVI, condition after thrombosis of the left lower leg, condition after varices surgery on the left lower leg, Leiden mutation, repeated venous lower ulcers. Initial glycemia 6.2 mmol/L, glycated haemoglobin 33 mmol/mol. BMI was 28.2 (overweight). The TBI was 1.33 on the right toe and 1.23 on the left toe. Initial Wound‐QoL was 27 points. Upon presentation, the wound was present for more than 2 months and previous treatment with povidone‐iodine solution and ointment. Signs of local infection were exudate, delayed healing, malodour. The dressings were provided by the patient alone in the home environment.

The wound dimensions were 4 cm in length, 2 cm in width and depth 1 cm. The wound consisted mainly of roughly 5% granulation tissue and 95% slough. Medium levels of exudate (thin/watery) were produced. Peri wound skin was without pathological signs. Local signs of infection included exudate, delayed healing, and malodour. At the initial time point, the daytime pain level was 5 and during treatment 8. Wound bed swabs showed positive colonisation with one microorganism—Staphylococcus coagulase‐negative and Yeast (sporadic). Chosen local treatment was HydroClean (Hydroactive dessing). RespoSorb Super was applied as a secondary dressing. The patient performed wound dressings at 48 h intervals at home. In addition, the application of compression bandages was recommended. At the first follow‐up examination, the wound was better. The wound has reduced its area. Hyperhydration of the edges due to the hydroactive dressing occurred. There is an 60% incidence of slough and 40% incidence of granulation tissue at the wound bed (Figure 3b). At the follow‐up examination on day 76, a microbial infection was found in the area of the wound and antimycotic ointments were applied (Figure 3e). The Effect of Test Method on the Total Number of Pathogens and Patients QoL

We have presented six cases with venous leg ulcers in which the number of pathogens is analyzed using different techniques. In five of the six cases presented, microbial contamination was present in the wound bed. Figure 8 shows an overview of the number of pathogens obtained using the different sampling methods per case. In case 3, microbial contamination was not detected by any of the examination methods. Therefore, we believe that the samples in this case were collected correctly, but pathogens were not present at the wound bed. In 4 of the 5 cases, the highest number of pathogens detected was demonstrated from sonication fluid (total n = 43) sent in haemoculturing tubes (BACT/ALERT Culture Media Bottles) to the laboratory. Thirty‐nine pathogens (n = 39) were detected using conventional wound swabbing (Levin's technique). The lowest number of pathogens was detected in sonication fluid (n = 30) sent in sterile tubes. Detected pathogens included viridoid streptococci and a spectrum of opportunistic ( E. faecalis, E. coli, S. coagulasenegative, S. agalactiae ) and invasive bacteria ( S. aureus, S. marcescens, M. morganii, S. agalactiae (ß‐hem. streptococcus sc. B), S. agalactiae (ß‐hem. streptococcus sc.), S. epidermidis, S. mitis, Corynebacterium sp., C. indologenes, K. oxytoca, Acinetobacter sp.) Antibiotic treatment was indicated in only one uncompliant patient (case 1, detailed presentation is part of discussion) despite the demonstrated microbial load in the wound. In three of the six cases (cases 3, 5, and 6) complete wound healing occurred; in two cases (cases 2 and 4) the wounds were almost healed at the end of the study. None of the cases showed allergic reactions or other adverse effects to the topical material used. In five of the six cases, patients and their families were able to carry out wound dressings at home by themselves or with the help of family caregivers after proper education, and all found it easy. In only one case (case 1) home care nurses visited the patient. All dressings were carried out at 48‐h intervals to prevent biofilm development at the wound bed.

In five of the six cases presented, the healing process was positively influenced, the wound bed was cleaned, pain was eliminated, odour and exudation were reduced. In five of the six cases there was an improved QoL with a hard‐to‐heal wound.

Wound progression, pathogen loading, and the healing process are interrelated factors that affect the patient's QoL. In a hard‐to‐heal wound, there is often a significant microbial load that slows or blocks healing. Pathogenic microorganisms can colonise the wound, leading to inflammation and thus increasing pain, odour and exudation, which negatively affects both the physical and psychological state of the patient.

Conversely, if the pathogen load is effectively controlled—for example, through antibacterial therapy, optimized local care, and patient cooperation—the healing process can be accelerated. Cleaning the wound bed, eliminating dead tissue from the wound bed, and reducing inflammation contribute to improved patient comfort. Relief of pain and other bothersome symptoms leads to an improved mental state, greater mobility and an overall better perception of QoL.

Discussion

The main objective of the presented study was to find out which sampling and test method would allow the detection of the greatest number of pathogens at the wound bed. The aim was to assess whether the bacterial load (number of pathogens) would be detected in the sampled material and to test whether the sonication method would allow the detection of a greater bacterial load (number of pathogens) than the swab. The next aim was to determine if pathogens attached to the local materials that were applied to the wound for 48 h, which were subsequently released into the sonication fluid and detected using a laboratory method. The method chosen was a prospective observational study—a case–control series (n = 6). Six patients with lower leg ulcers of venous aetiology were included in the study. The selected patients were applied different materials for moisture wound healing: MGH dressing, hydroactive dressing or ceramic dressing (“Cerdak”). The wound bed swab was sent in a sterile tube with Amies transport medium, the first sonicate was transported in a sterile tube (sonication fluid) and the second sonicate was transported in a haemoculture tube (BACT/ALERT Culture Media Bottles). The bacterial load of pathogens collected in all three samples was examined and monitored (the number of pathogens). There was no possibility to evaluate the bacterial loads in terms of virulence and infectiousness of pathogens as it always depends on the patients' immune system status.

Authors Al‐Gharibi et al. reported that wound healing is considered cost‐effective only if it is cost‐effective in terms of time and money [26]. These ideas are supported by authors Lindholm and Searle, who reported that healing time, frequency of dressing changes, and complications are three important factors affecting cost [27]. Olsson et al. state that hard‐to‐heal wounds are a health problem that has devastating effects on patients and contributes to high costs to healthcare systems and society [28]. Sun et al. states that bacterial infection is a complication of wounds that can significantly delay healing [29]. The financial cost of wound care in developed countries is around 1%–4% of total healthcare expenditure [28, 30]. Martinengo et al. describe that chronic wounds are a major public health challenge [31]. It is expected that the number of patients with hard‐to‐heal wounds will continue to grow due to increasing age and increasing comorbidities such as obesity, diabetes mellitus and cardiovascular disease, thereby increasing their economic impact [4, 32, 33, 34]. Bates agrees with previous authors and states that wounds, especially hard‐to‐heal wounds, are a major problem for the system [35].

Hard‐to‐heal wounds often relate to healthcare‐associated infection (HAI). Authors Al‐Tawfiq and Tambyah state that HAI is one of the major complications of modern medical treatment [36]. The World Health Organization (WHO) reports that out of every 100 patients in acute care hospitals, 7 patients in high‐income countries and 15 patients in low‐ and middle‐income countries will acquire at least HAI during their hospital stay [37]. The OECD (Organisation for Economic Co‐operation and Development) also states that HAIs poses a significant threat to the population across all countries worldwide (including G7 countries) [38]. It notes that the microorganisms causing these infections are very often resistant to available treatments [37, 38]. It has been found that up to 68% of the total microbial resistance burden is mainly caused by four resistant microorganisms, namely E. coli , third‐generation cephalosporin‐resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae , methicillin‐resistant S. aureus (MRSA) and carbapenem‐resistant Pseudomonas aeruginosa [38]. Author Andersen describes that using the right antibiotics is crucial to avoid resistance [39]. A bacterium is sensitive to antibiotics if the drug inactivates or kills the bacterium, if it does not, it is resistant. Bacteria may also form biofilms, and antibiotic‐resistant strains are often more adherent to the epithelium compared to sensitive bacteria [39]. Treatment of wound infection caused by multidrug‐resistant (MDR) bacteria is challenging because conventional medicines are unable to cure such infections [40]. Andersen reports that as early as 1945, Alexander Fleming warned about the emergence of antibiotic resistance [39]. It has been noted that the most important modifiable risk factor for antimicrobial resistance includes inappropriate treatment or prescription of antibiotics. It is reported that about one‐half of antibiotic prescriptions in ambulatory care is inappropriate, including the actual choice of antibiotics, its dosage, duration of use, etc. [41]. The authors Hurlow and Bowler reported that in the treatment of hard‐to‐heal wounds, systemic antibiotic therapy is often appropriate and essential for acute wound infections, but is often used inappropriately, excessively, and unsuccessfully for chronic wound infections [42]. Also, Sagi and Patzakis stated that excessive use of antibiotics contributes to the development of antibiotic resistance [42, 43].

HAIs are not limited to hospitals but can be present in all healthcare settings, including outpatient care providers and home care agencies. This may also have been the reason why, in the first case, the patient was seen by other healthcare providers who scanned dressings and assessed the wound bed where home care nurses attended to the patient.

We believe that in case 1, the necessity of antibiotic treatment was caused by multiple reasons, including compensated (HbA1C—81 mmol/mol) diabetes mellitus, morbid obesity (BMI 53.9), cataract, and malhygiene of the patient. Visits to other healthcare facilities to remove dressing material and visits by home care agency nurses may also have contributed to the increasing number of pathogens in the wound. The wound and its peri‐wound area showed signs of acute local infection. Pathogens found S. aureus (sensitivity: Amoxicillin clavulanate, gentamicin), S. agalactiae (ß‐hem. streptococcus sc. B) (sensitivity: penicillin), S. marcescens (ampicillin and amoxicillin clavulanate resistant, gentamicin sensitive), E. faecalis (sensitive: ampicillin). The patient was using MGH in her local treatment. Authors Mandal et Mandal present that the therapeutic value of honey has been documented in the oldest medical literature, and it has been known since antiquity that honey has antimicrobial effects and heals wounds [44]. Mayer et al. stated that various cultures have used honey as a traditional medicine for centuries to treat different disorders [45]. Studies by many authors show that medicinal honey has antimicrobial effects [15, 16, 46, 47, 48].

Due to its antimicrobial mechanisms, including acidic pH, osmotic activity, and slow release of hydrogen peroxide, MGH is effective against a wide range of pathogens, including multi‐resistant bacteria, fungi, and viruses [15, 49, 50]. Moreover, the use of MGH in wound management can reduce the use of antibiotics and topical antiseptics [51]. Repeated use of MGH materials is without risk of developing resistance [15, 46]. In addition, the antimicrobial activity of honey will reduce the number of bacteria in the wound, while the carbohydrate in honey offers an alternative odourless nutrient source for the smelling tissue proteins, thereby reducing malodor [52, 53]. This ability is most evident within 24 h of applying honey to the wound [54].

Another measure in case 1 was the re‐education of the patient and home care nurses on the observance of personal hygiene and hygiene of the wound and its surroundings, the use of protective items during dressing changes, and the focus on hand hygiene. In the interventions proposed, we agreed with the author Andersen 2019, who recommended the principles of hygiene and infection control [39]. He further states that effective hygiene reduces the incidence of infections and prevents the unnecessary use of antibiotics with subsequent possible development of resistance. He also describes that an important principle is the adequate selection of antibiotics. This is primarily a matter of choosing an antibiotic to which the bacterium is sensitive. However, antibiotics should be used minimally [39]. ACSQHC (Australian Commission on Safety and Quality in Health Care), 2023 states that Hand Hygiene Promotion is recommended as part of the multi‐modal strategy [55]. The next step in the local treatment was to change the local material with MGH to an antiseptic material with silver. Negut et al., report that the treatment of skin wounds is a key area of research due to the important function of this tissue [56]. When the skin is damaged, bacteria can soon penetrate the subcutaneous tissues, which can lead to life‐threatening infections. As a result, topical materials with antimicrobial agents can be used to enhance the healing process. Tamilarasi et al. add that antimicrobial wound dressings hold great promise for reducing the risk of infection and improving the healing rate of hard‐to‐heal wounds [40].

Studies researching the sonication fluid produced from used local wound healing material were not found in professional journals. Professional literature from the field of orthopaedics was found where the sonication method has been used to demonstrate fracture‐related infection or Periprosthetic Joint Infection (PJI). This meta‐analysis compared the diagnostic accuracy of dithiothreitol (DTT) and sonication. The pooled sensitivity and specificity showed no statistically significant differences between DTT and sonication [57]. Another study in the field of orthotics involved sampling bacteria from the biofilm adhered to the surface of the prosthesis. The reported sensitivity for the diagnosis of PJI for periprosthetic tissue cultures and sonicated fluids is 54% and 75% each, respectively. The specificity is 98% and 87%. The sonication technique has been shown to be a reliable test for the diagnosis of PJI after TKA with higher sensitivity and specificity than conventional periprosthetic tissue culture [58]. Another study on PJI is the study by Ji et al. [59]. Of the 64 patients enrolled, 36 had PJI and 28 had aseptic failure. Direct sonication fluid and conventional synovial fluid had sensitivities of 91.7% and 55.6% (p < 0.001) and specificities of 82.1% and 92.9%. Fourteen cases of PJI were detected by culture of direct sonication fluid but not by culture of synovial fluid. Higher sensitivity was achieved by direct sonication of tissue only than by direct sonication of the implant only (88.9% vs. 75.0%) [59].

Further research on sonication would be advisable. Comparative studies between sonication and other diagnostic methods should be performed, as there might be other confounding factors that we are not able to assess now in such a cohort, and yet we consider our work an important milestone and possibly a motivation for further research. There is a need to make an in‐depth comparison between the effectiveness of sonication and traditional methods of diagnosing wound bed pathogens. Identify whether there are specific situations or wound types where sonication provides better results in terms of sensitivity. Investigate the long‐term effects and safety of using sonication on wounds and surrounding tissues. Evaluate whether there are any side effects or complications associated with repeated use of this method. To formulate precise procedures and protocols for the possibility of greater reliability of sonication usage and sharing of knowledge between different research groups. Study the various parameters of sonication (e.g., frequency, duration) and their effect on outcomes. Study the possibility of standardising protocols for different types of wounds and the extent of infectious symptoms (both local and systemic). Analyse the cost‐effectiveness of sonication. Perform a cost–benefit analysis of sonication compared to conventional diagnostic methods. To determine whether the use of sonication is economically viable. Examine the effectiveness of sonication on different types of wounds (acute vs. hard‐to‐heal wounds, superficial vs. deep, with or without local signs of inflammation). Identify the specific microbial spectrum that is best diagnosed by sonication.

Strengths and Limitations of Our Study

To the best of our knowledge and information, this is the first study to use sonication to analyse the bacterial load in multiple patients with hard‐to‐heal wounds of the same aetiology seen by a single care provider (one healthcare facility). Possible weaknesses of the study conducted that need to be taken into account are a limited sample of patients, which limits the possibility of generalising the results. The missing comparative data. Deficiency of studies investigating the use of sonication as a microbiological diagnostic tool versus conventional wound bed swabs. Variability in the methodology performed in published studies. Differences in the performance of sonication between studies may affect the consistency and reproducibility of results, as mentioned also in the further research suggestions.

Future Research Directions

From our study, it is clear that using the correct transport medium can affect the number of detected pathogens. To further optimise the best diagnostic method, comparisons between swab method (conventional Levin technique wound bed swab or swab taken from dressing material), sonication fluid investigation (both from wound bed and wound dressing), and the effect of transport medium (sterile tube with Amies transport medium, or haemoculture tubes both from swab and sonication fluid taken from wound bed and from dressing materials) should be included in one experiment to allow better comparison between treatment and methods. Another important area to explore is the cost‐effectiveness of using new methods to identify bacterial loads in the wound. Our study has laid the first foundations for the potential use of sonication in a group of patients with the same wound type, enabling the identification of requirements for a more precise design of follow‐up studies.

Conclusions

In our prospective observational case series, we demonstrated in a group of 4 of 6 patients that the transport of sonicated fluid in haemoculture tubes (BACT/ALERT Culture Media Bottles) objectified the highest number of pathogens of all the investigative methods performed in this study. In one patient, no microbial load was found at the wound bed in any of the tested samples. It has been shown that using the results of sonication fluid from haemoculture tubes, invasive strains of pathogens can be better targeted to indicate the need for antibiotic treatment and thus reduce antibiotic resistance. It was confirmed that despite objective local signs of inflammation, antibiotic treatment was used in only one patient (uncompliant patient). It was demonstrated that good hygiene of the wound bed and periwound area, early intervention against biofilm growth, and antimicrobial local dressing materials were sufficient to eliminate the microbial load at the wound bed. It was shown that quality education of the patient and his family about carrying out dressings in the home environment led to a more effective healing process. It was also shown that local treatment of hard‐to‐heal wounds using dressing materials for the phased wound healing led to the effective healing process and reduced the financial cost of care, as 5 out of 6 patients performed the dressing changes themselves after careful education in a manner consistent with the requirements for appropriate antiseptic approaches. We showed that the healing process significantly improved the patients' QoL. This work is a pilot study, and a larger cohort is planned to confirm these findings, as a larger sample size will be needed in future studies.
