Microbiological analysis of tunneled hemodialysis catheters isolated from patients receiving hemodialysis in Saskatchewan
Authors: Murugesan Sivaranjani, Haley Sanderson, Chinenye R Nnajide, Anna Martens-Koop, Joseph M Blondeau, Rodrick Stryker, Aaron P White
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAim: To compare the microbial communities inside hemodialysis catheters from symptomatic and asymptomatic patients to determine their differences.Materials & methods: Catheters (n = 41) were removed from patients in the Saskatchewan Health Authority over an 18-month period. The catheter section inside the body was flushed and the contents were evaluated using culture-dependent and culture-independent analysis.Results: All catheters were colonized by bacteria, with considerable overlap between groups based on microbial communities and the individual species detected. More Gram-negative species were detected by sequencing, whereas predominantly Gram-positive strains were cultured. Antibiotic resistance and biofilm formation was widespread and not correlated with either catheter group.Conclusion: Common pathogens were detected in each set of catheters, therefore predicting infections based on the microbiology is difficult.Keywords: : Antibiotic resistance, biofilm, bloodstream infection, catheter, hemodialysis, microbial community, nanopore

Background

Tunneled hemodialysis catheters (THCs) placed in a central vein are frequently used for temporary vascular access in renal dialysis patients until a polytetrafluoroethylene graft or an arteriovenous fistula is available to use [1]. If all options for the placement of a graft or fistula have been exhausted, THCs are used for permanent vascular access [2]. However, THCs are not ideal for long-term hemodialysis due to the high rate of thrombosis and infections, which results in significant morbidity and mortality [3]. These infections also typically lead to inflammation, higher risks of cardiovascular events and central venous stenosis, in addition to sepsis and embolic complications. Recently, it has been estimated that central line-associated bloodstream infections (CLABSI) occur in hemodialysis catheters at a rate of 5.4–6.5 per 1000 catheter days [4].

Invasion of the bloodstream by microbes and the subsequent CLABSI can occur by extraluminal or intraluminal routes. In the extraluminal route, microorganisms migrate from the skin of the catheter insertion site to the catheter tip and then spread to the blood stream [5]. The intraluminal infection route involves microbial colonization of the catheter lumen and is thought to be due to improper aseptic procedure during catheter manipulation by healthcare personnel [6]. Other mechanisms include translocation of pathogens from the mouth or gut into the bloodstream, where they can then seed the catheter. Once microorganisms are inside the catheter lumen or in the bloodstream, they may adhere directly to the catheter surface or become embedded within a fibrin layer attached to the catheter. The typical microbial colonizers tend to exist in biofilms, a mode of growth where bacterial cells aggregate and become embedded in a self-produced extracellular matrix, in contact with a physical surface. Biofilms are known to form naturally on the inside of catheters [7] and are often comprised of several different species [8]. CLABSIs are often caused by Gram-positive bacteria, predominantly coagulase-negative staphylococci (CoNS) and Staphylococcus aureus [9], although gram-negative bacteria can also be the causative agents [10].

If patients acquire bloodstream infections, it is assumed that the infectious agent originated from an “infected” hemodialysis catheter [3], which is subsequently removed and replaced with a new line. Hemodialysis catheters can also be used for long periods of time without complications and are considered sterile when removed as part of standard patient care. These two scenarios fit with the dogma that “infected” catheters are colonized by microorganisms whereas “sterile” catheters are not. However, several recent studies focusing on microscopic examination of catheter lumens have shown that nearly 100% of catheters are colonized [5,11,12]. Part of the problem contributing to the dogma of “infected” versus “sterile” catheters is the inability to culture bacteria, even though prokaryotic cells appear to be present when catheters are visualized under the microscope [13]. In this study, we performed culture-dependent analysis with rich, nonselective media to try to grow bacteria, based on the concept demonstrated by Lau et al. [14], and used DNA sequence-based methods to compare the microbial communities inside hemodialysis catheters from asymptomatic and symptomatic patients.

Materials & methods

Sample collection, culturing of catheters & patient clinical reports

THCs were collected from renal dialysis patients at St. Paul’s Hospital and Royal University Hospital, Saskatoon, Saskatchewan, Canada between March 2021 and November 2022. Catheters were not collected specifically for this study, but rather were removed as part of the best practice and standard of care. Consent was obtained for the medical procedure to remove the catheter. The catheters were classified as “symptomatic” and “asymptomatic”. Symptomatic catheters were removed urgently due to existence of a non resolving infection in patients, including exit-site infections, tunnel infections or bacteremia. Asymptomatic catheters were removed from patients as no longer needed due to recovered kidney function, the establishment of other dialysis access (i.e., arteriovenous fistula, peritoneal dialysis catheter), or “trading” the catheter for another THC in same or opposite jugular vein; these patients had no signs of infection related to catheter use. The majority of catheters were removed from consecutive adult patients (>17 years age); some catheters were accidentally discarded, and catheters that were removed in evening or weekend periods were not included.

Removal of THCs was performed under aseptic conditions by nephrologists from a hospital based in-center hemodialysis unit. After removal, catheters were placed in sterile specimen bags, stored at 4°C and transferred to VIDO within six hours. Catheters were removed from the specimen bags in a biological safety cabinet in the White laboratory and the outside of the catheters were wiped clean and disinfected by treatment with 70% ethanol. Flame-sterilized cutting tools were used to excise an approximately 10 cm long catheter segment that was internal from the cuff and the distal tip (Figure 1). To remove catheter contents and any microbes attached to the inside of each tunneled line, a P1000 pipette (#17014382, Mettler-Toledo) with sterile tip was used to form a seal and each side of the tunneled catheter was flushed repeatedly (approximately 20 times) with 1 ml of sterile PBS. The collected cell contents were diluted to a final volume of 4 ml. One hundred microliter aliquots of the catheter flush contents were spread onto 12 different solid media (Table 1) and incubated at 37°C for 3 days. For the first 20 catheters analyzed, we inoculated a replicate set of agar plates and grew them under anaerobic conditions in sealed jars containing an Oxoid AnaeroGen 2.5 l Sachet (#OXAN0025A, ThermoFisher Scientific). However, we discontinued this growth step because we could not control for the presence/absence of oxygen after catheter removal or during catheter handling in our laboratory.

Growth of microorganisms, bacterial species identification & antimicrobial susceptibility testing

Morphologically distinct colony types from each agar plate were streak-purified onto Columbia blood agar, before cultivating in Mueller–Hinton II (MH II) broth (Becton, Dickinson) and storing as frozen stocks in 20% glycerol at -80°C. Organism identification was performed at Prairie Diagnostic Services (www.pdsinc.ca) by matrix-assisted laser desorption ionization-time of flight mass spectrometry (MALDI–TOF). Antimicrobial susceptibility testing was performed with a Vitek II instrument using the GPS 67 card for Gram-positive bacteria and the N391 card for Gram-negative bacteria. For MRSA, organisms screening as resistant to oxacillin and cefoxitin were further tested for altered penicillin-binding protein production using a latex agglutination assay. There were four strains that we could not determine the resistance patterns for: Paenibacillus spp. (HC-01), Bacillus simplex (HC-06), Brevibacterium spp. (HC-09)and Micrococcus luteus (HC-50).

DNA extraction & 16S rRNA gene sequencing on the MinION™ platform

After removing aliquots for bacterial culture analysis, cell contents from the remaining catheter flush samples were sedimented by centrifugation (10,000 × g, 15 min), and stored at -20°C. Cell pellets were thawed, and total genomic DNA was extracted using the GenElute Bacterial Genomic DNA Extraction Kit (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), following the Gram-positive bacterial preparation outlined in the user guide. Purity of the DNA was assessed and quantified using a DeNovix spectrophotometer/fluorometer (#DS-11 FX; FroggaBio Inc.).

The 16S rRNA gene sequence libraries were prepared using the 16S Barcoding Kit 1–24 (#SQK-RAB204; Oxford Nanopore Technologies (ONT), Oxford, UK), according to the manufacturer’s protocol. For each catheter sample, 16S rRNA genes were polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplified from 10 ng input DNA using 1 μl (10 μM) of the barcoded primer set 27F (5′-AGAGTTTGATCMTGGCTCAG-3′) and 1492R (5′-CGGTTACCTTGTTACGACTT-3′) and 25 μl LongAmp™ Taq 2× Master Mix (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA) in a total reaction volume of 50 μl. PCR amplification was performed at 95°C for 1 min, 25 cycles of 95°C for 20 s, 55°C for 30 s, 65°C for 2 min and a final extension step at 65°C for 5 min. The PCR reactions were transferred to Eppendorf DNA LoBind microcentrifuge tubes and products were purified by adding 30 μl magnetic beads (NucleoMag NGS beads, Macherey-Nagel, Allentown, PA, USA) to each tube, and eluting bound DNA in 10 μl of 10 mM Tris-HCl pH 8 + 50 mM NaCl. The amount and purity of DNA was quantified using a Qubit Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Ottawa, ON, Canada). DNA from 12 samples (n = 12 barcodes) was pooled to reach a total DNA amount of 100 ng; the final library was prepared as per the manufacturer’s instruction (ONT, Oxford, UK) and loaded to the MinION flow cell (v R.9.4.1; ONT, Oxford, UK) via the SpotON sample port and sequenced for 24–48 h. Base calling of the Nanopore sequence reads was performed using MinKNOW [15] (v2.0) and Guppy [16] (v2.1.1).

Bioinformatic workflow & statistical analysis

The quality and quantity of the raw Nanopore sequence reads was determined using NanoStat [17] (v1.5.0). The reads were trimmed using Porechop [18] (v0.2.4) and the bacterial composition of the samples was determined by using the “abundance” feature of Emu [19] (v1.0.1) on the University of Saskatchewan advanced high performance computing cluster large memory nodes using a maximum of 800 GB of RAM. The heatmap and stacked barplot showing the compositions of the samples were generated using JupyterLab [20] (v1.0.0) and the python packages: Pandas [21] (v2.0.0), NumPy [22] (v1.24.2), Seaborn [23] (v0.11.2) and Matplotlib [24] (v3.5.3).

We performed one “reagent” negative control that we subjected to 16S sequencing, to identify any potential contaminants. This led to the detection of Salmonella enterica, which we had anticipated since this is a major focus of our lab's research. This known pathogen might have been introduced during the DNA extraction or PCR amplification steps from the laboratory environment. We reasoned that if Salmonella was truly present inside catheters, we expect that the patients would have symptoms of an infection. S. enterica is rarely, if ever, detected in association with catheters in our region (Dr. R. Stryker, personal communication), and in the case of the 66 catheters collected in this study, S. enterica was not detected at the catheter site or any other body sites while the catheters were in situ (Table 2). Therefore, when S. enterica was identified by 16S sequencing in any catheter samples, it was deemed to be a contaminant and removed from analysis.

To generate the final stacked barplot Figure, total abundance data were curated by removing bacterial species that were represented by <1% of total 16S reads and the resulting data was imported into GraphPad Prism v. 10. The bacterial species above this 1% threshold were organized by order (i.e., Bacillales, Burkholderiales, Enterobacterales and Pseudomonadales); there were three remaining species that were placed into an Extra Gammaproteobacteria group. Coloring of each group of species was organized according to the Microshades scheme [25].

To evaluate alpha diversity, sample microbiota was estimated using Shannon index and Choa1 index. The Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used to compare significant differences in the alpha diversity indices between symptomatic and asymptomatic groups (p < 0.05). The similarity of the microbial community structure among all samples was evaluated using principal coordinates analysis at the operational taxonomic unit level based on Bray–Curtis dissimilarity. This work was done using the Vegan [26] (v2.6-4) and Microbiome [27] (v1.22.0) packages in R.

Biofilm growth & analysis

Staphylococcus spp. strains were grown in BHI broth and all non-Staphylococcus strains were grown in Lysogeny broth (LB). For each strain, 5 ml of broth was inoculated from frozen stocks and cultures were incubated at 37°C for 18 h. To determine the conversion factor for cell number as a function of optical density for each strain, stock cultures (1 ml) were prepared from overnight cultures to an optical density at 600 nm (OD600) of 1.0, and the cell number was determined by serial dilution and plating.

Bacterial cultures were diluted to deliver 107 cells in 15 μl into wells of non-tissue-culture-treated, polystyrene 96-well plates (Falcon no. 351172) containing 135 μl of 12 different media: tryptic soy broth (TSB), ½ TSB, TSB + 0.2% glucose, TSB + 1.5% glucose, TSB + 1% glucose + 4% NaCl, TSB + 2% sucrose, LB, LB + 2% glucose, brain heart infusion (BHI), BHI + 1% glucose + 4% NaCl, Mueller–Hinton + 2% NaCl and M63 minimal media + 0.3% glucose + 0.5% casamino acids. For each strain, duplicate 96-well plates were inoculated, lids were put on top and sealed with parafilm, and the cultures were incubated on a tilting platform shaker at 37°C for 24 or 48 h. Each bacterial strain tested was grown with at least six technical replicates and three biological replicates.

Cell biomass was quantified by crystal violet (CV) staining [28]. The 96-well plates were washed twice by being submerged into a water tray, followed by shaking into a waste tray to remove non attached cells. After air drying for 10 min, 125 ml of CV solution (0.2% wt/vol in 80% methanol) was added to each well, and the plates were incubated for 15 min at room temperature. After staining, plates were washed twice with water and vigorously tapped on paper towels to remove any excess liquid, followed by air drying for 30 min. One hundred and eighty microliters of 33% glacial acetic acid (v/v) was added to each well to dissolve the CV attached to the biofilms, and the plates were covered and incubated for 15 min at room temperature. One hundred twenty-five microliters of solution from each well was transferred into a new clear flat-bottom 96-well plate (Greiner Bio One, no. 655101), and the absorbance at 570 nm was measured.

The CV absorbance values for each bacterial strain were scored according to an established biofilm classification system [29]. Biofilm formation was characterized as strong (mean OD >4 × ODcontrol); moderate (4 × ODcontrol > mean OD >2 × ODcontrol); weak (2 × ODcontrol > mean OD > ODcontrol); or none (ODcontrol > mean OD). The ODcontrol was the CV value corresponding to the uninoculated media control wells.

Synergistic biofilm growth assay

Twelve Staphylococcus strains and P. aeruginosa strain HC9-2 were tested for biofilm growth together. Overnight cultures of each strain were normalized to an OD600 of 1.0, 1 ml of cells were sedimented by centrifugation (10,000 × g, 2 min) and resuspended in 500 μl of the appropriate growth media. To reach a total volume of 150 μl in each well, 7.5 μl of P. aeruginosa and 7.5 μl of the Staphylococcus strain were inoculated into wells of 96-well plates containing 135 μl of six different media: TSB, ½ TSB, TSB + 1.5% glucose, TSB + 2% sucrose, BHI and BHI + 1% glucose + 4% NaCl. Strains were also grown individually by inoculating 15 μl of the 1.0 OD stock cultures into the wells; media only control wells remained uninoculated. Individual strains were tested with three technical replicates and strain combinations were tested with six technical replicates. For each Gram-positive strain tested, three plates were inoculated, and cultures were grown at 37°C with slight rocking on a tilting platform shaker for 24, 48 or 72 h. After incubation, the plates were processed and the attached biomass in each well was quantified by CV staining following the procedure outlined above. To make comparisons between groups easier, the CV values from Pseudomonas-only wells, Staphylococcus-only wells and test wells containing two strains were normalized by dividing by the CV values from the uninoculated media control wells.

Results

The catheter related bacterial communities are similar from symptomatic & asymptomatic patients

THCs suspected of being contaminated by pathogenic bacteria (symptomatic THCs) and THCs removed due to recovery of kidney function or the patient no longer needing hemodialysis vascular access (asymptomatic THCs) were collected from renal dialysis patients in the Saskatchewan Healthy Authority. A total of 41 THCs were included in this study (symptomatic, n = 21; asymptomatic, n = 20). Overall, 35 THCs (85%) were positive for bacterial presence by 16S amplification and Nanopore sequencing (Table 2); a total of 6,414,530 high-quality sequence reads were generated with an average of 183,272 reads per sample (Supplementary Table S1). We detected 41 different bacterial species with more than 1% abundance from the 35 THCs (Figure 2A). Almost all the 16S sequence reads (93.5%) corresponded to bacterial species that were detected in both asymptomatic THCs and symptomatic THCs, whereas 3.4% of the 16S reads corresponded to species that were only detected in asymptomatic THCs and 3.1% for species only detected in symptomatic THCs (Figure 2A). Dominant species detected in both THC groups were Achromobacter spp., Enterobacter spp., E. coli, Pseudomonas spp., Serratia spp. S. epidermidis and Stenotrophomonas maltophilia (Figure 2B). Reads corresponding to S. aureus were detected in eight symptomatic catheters and one asymptomatic catheter and Haemophilus influenzae was detected only in two symptomatic catheters.

Bacterial strains were isolated from both symptomatic & asymptomatic THCs

Bacteria were isolated from THCs using 12 standard microbiologic media (Table 1) and the species of the morphologically different isolates were identified using MALDI–TOF. In total, we recovered 32 strains from 12 different bacterial species, 18 strains from symptomatic THCs and 14 strains from asymptomatic THCs (Table 2). In many cases, the same bacterial species had been detected in the clinic at other body sites while the catheter was in situ. Forty-eight percent of the symptomatic THCs (10 of 21) and 45% of asymptomatic THCs (9 of 20) were culture-positive. Among the 32 strains, 30 were Gram-positive and only two were Gram-Negative bacterial isolates. Most of these strains were common CLABSI-causing pathogens, such as S. aureus, coagulase-negative Staphylococcus species (CoNS), E. coli and P. aeruginosa. Overall, S. epidermidis, other coagulase negative staphylococci and S. aureus were most abundant. P. aeruginosa and E. coli were each detected from one catheter each (Table 2). S. aureus strains (n = 8) were only grown from symptomatic THCs.

Diversity of bacterial communities identified in asymptomatic & symptomatic THCs

We generated community profiles for individual catheters based on the proportions of 16S reads. Bacteria from the Order Enterobacterales (Figure 3A, green shades) were predominant in both asymptomatic and symptomatic catheters, whereas bacteria from Order bacillales were more predominant in the symptomatic catheters (Figure 3A, red shades). S. aureus was detected as the dominant species in four symptomatic catheters. To compare the microbial diversity of the different THC groups, two alpha diversity metrics were calculated: Chao-1 and Shannon diversity. Chao-1 is a marker of species richness and is based upon the number of rare classes of operational taxonomic units [30]. The calculated Chao-1 values were higher in asymptomatic THCs, but the average was not statistically different from symptomatic catheters (Figure 3B). Shannon diversity combines abundance as well as the number of species in each group to provide an estimate of bacterial community evenness [30]. In this case, symptomatic THCs had significantly (p = 0.03) higher species evenness when compared with asymptomatic THCs (Figure 3B). To further evaluate the community variability, we calculated beta-diversity in the symptomatic and asymptomatic THC groups by using a non-phylogeny-based distance metric (i.e., Bray–Curtis dissimilarity). Principal coordinate analysis showed that the asymptomatic and symptomatic THCs had similar bacterial communities, since the two groups of catheters did not form distinct clusters (Figure 3C).

Culture-dependent & -independent analysis of asymptomatic & symptomatic THCs

When the results from conventional culturing and 16S sequencing were combined, bacteria were detected in 100% of THCs. Thirty two percent of THCs (13 of 41) tested positive for bacteria by both techniques, 54% (22 of 41) were culture-negative but yielded 16S bacterial sequence reads, and bacterial strains were cultured from 15% of THCs (6 of 41) that were sequence-negative (Table 2). Of the 13 THCs that were positive by both culture and 16S sequencing, there was a considerable overlap in terms of species detected (Supplementary Figure S1). In general, most of the cultured species/strains were also detected by 16S sequencing, indicating that they represented live bacteria. Most strains cultured belonged to Gram-positive bacterial species (Table 2), while the majority of 16S sequence reads corresponded to Gram-Negative bacterial species (Figure 3A). Many species within the polymicrobial community were most frequently identified by 16S sequencing rather than culturing.

Antibiotic resistance was detected in bacterial strains from asymptomatic & symptomatic THCs

We performed antimicrobial susceptibility testing on all strains isolated from THCs, against 31 different antimicrobials. All strains showed resistance to one or more antibiotics except P. aeruginosa HC9-2 and Staphylococcus lugdunensis HC66-1 (Figure 4). Nine S. epidermidis strains were identified as methicillin-resistant (MRSE) and five strains were identified as methicillin-sensitive (MSSE). MRSE strain HC3-2, isolated from an asymptomatic catheter, was also resistant to vancomycin and linezolid and would be classified as multidrug resistant. For S. aureus, four of eight strains were classified as methicillin-resistant (MRSA), and the four methicillin-sensitive strains were resistant to one or more antibiotics (Figure 4). Staphylococcus capitis, Staphylococcus hominis and S. warneri strains were mostly susceptible with resistance to only one or two antibiotics. E. coli strain HC1-1 showed resistance to ampicillin, tetracycline and trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole. The resistance of a particular strain did not appear to be influenced by whether the catheter originated from a symptomatic or asymptomatic patient.

Biofilm formation by catheter related microorganisms

Biofilm formation is thought to be the main strategy for microorganisms to colonize the inside of catheters [7]. It is difficult to mirror what the actual “in vivo” environment is, therefore, it is common practice to test multiple growth media to trigger the bacterial strains to form biofilms, so that we can study them [31]. We screened 26 strains for their ability to form biofilms in 12 different growth media. Strains formed strong biofilms in an average of approximately five different growth media, ranging from the strongest biofilm formers – S. epidermidis strains HC57-1 (12 of 12 media), and HC8-1 (11 of 12 media), to the weakest biofilm formers – S. aureus strain HC10-1 and E. coli strain HC1-1 (0 of 12 media) (Figure 5). P. aeruginosa strain HC9-2 formed strong biofilms in eight of 12 selected media. We were intrigued by S. epidermidis and P. aeruginosa because these two species were detected together in four THCs: asymptomatic catheters HC16 and HC18, and symptomatic catheters HC9 and HC11. In each case, the species were present in an approximate 3:1 S. epidermidis to P. aeruginosa ratio (Figure 3A). We wanted to investigate joint biofilm formation by strains from these two species, as well as between P. aeruginosa and other Gram-positive bacterial strains. The 12 best biofilm formers: S. epidermidis HC8-2, HC8-3, HC36-1, HC57-1 and HC65-1; S. aureus HC22-1, HC32-1, HC56-1 and HC63-1; S. capitis HC45-1; S. hominis HC50-1; and S. lugdunensis HC66-1 (Figure 5) were grown together with P. aeruginosa strain HC9-2 in six different growth media. Three S. epidermidis strains and one S. aureus strain produced stronger biofilms when grown together with P. aeruginosa as compared with each strain grown alone (Table 3). For each combination of strains, we identified at least two different growth conditions as the starting point to investigate synergistic biofilm formation between Gram-positive and -negative organisms in the future.

Discussion

This study was initiated with the belief that a better understanding of the microbial communities associated with catheters is essential for the prevention and treatment of catheter related infections. We analyzed the bacterial communities of 20 asymptomatic and 21 symptomatic THCs by using conventional culturing as well as 16S rRNA sequencing to identify species. As revealed primarily by 16S sequencing, there was considerable overlap in the bacterial diversity and community structure between the symptomatic and asymptomatic catheter groups. There were some differences in the distribution of species, but we found that 94% of sequence reads were from bacterial species that overlapped between asymptomatic THCs and symptomatic THCs. Our results in Canada are similar to a recent study from France, where researchers found significant overlap in the microbial species present in symptomatic and asymptomatic catheters [11]. Based on these data, we would assume that the potential to cause catheter related infections is not radically different between asymptomatic and symptomatic THCs. However, there were still some differences because S. aureus was exclusively cultured from symptomatic THCs in this study. Other bacteria isolated by culturing were common catheter pathogens, including coagulase-negative Staphylococci, with S. epidermidis the most abundant, E. coli, Enterobacter, Morganella, Stenotrophomonas and Pseudomonas species [32,33]. The presence of these potential pathogens in catheters from both symptomatic and asymptomatic THCs prevented us from drawing generalized conclusions regarding their association in causing infections. The detection and abundance of S. aureus turned out to be the most predictive factor for infection status.

The connection between culture-dependent and culture-independent analysis was variable for THCs in this study. For the 13 THCs that were positive for both types of analyses, there was generally good agreement in that the species that were cultured were also detected by 16S sequencing, similar to analysis of the human microbiome by Lau et al. [14]. Gram-positive organisms (94%) were the predominant microbes isolated from the 19 THCs that were culture-positive, with only a small proportion of Gram-negative organisms (6%). Staphylococcus was the most frequent causative agent of catheter related bloodstream infections in hemodialysis patients in our cohort, consistent with recent studies [34]. At the species level, S. aureus was more abundant in the symptomatic group. Similarly, Mohamed et al. [35] reported that 84% of bacterial species identified from blood cultures were Gram-positive and <5% of Gram-negative bacterial species were identified as an infectious agent attributed to bloodstream infection in hemodialysis patients in Ireland. In our study, we do not know the reason for detecting more Gram-negative bacteria (88%) than Gram-positive bacteria (22%) by 16S sequencing. It could be that the bacterial species detected by sequencing represent noise and are below the culturing threshold that we have achieved in our methodology. However, it should be noted that many of the organisms detected by sequencing and culturing after catheter removal, were also identified in the clinical reports for these patients while the catheter was still in use. There is also the possibility that gut translocation can lead to transient bacteremias that do not lead to systemic infections. 16S sequencing also cannot differentiate between live and dead bacteria, and it is possible that empirical antibiotic treatment of the patients could influence the ratios detected. There were six THCs in which no bacteria were detected by 16S sequencing, which most likely reflects a problem during DNA purification and PCR amplification, since we cultured E. coli, S. epidermidis, S. lugdunensis and B. simplex from these THCs. Importantly, none of the following species were detected: Campylobacter, Shigella, Listeria, Vibrio and Yersinia as well as C. difficile, Enterohemorrhagic E. coli and Enteropathogenic E. coli, which were all pathogens that were excluded from laboratory confirmed bloodstream infections [36]. Otherwise, many of the organisms detected are common colonizing/contaminant organisms [36]. We also analyzed previously >150 isolates from other hospitals [31] and found very similar strains as our current study. Our results confirm that the simple presence of bacterial species known to cause catheter related infections does not necessarily predict infection. We hypothesize that the combination of bacterial species and their virulence factors and/or capacity to form biofilms might be more important than microbial diversity in the development of catheter related infections among hemodialysis patients.

Conventional culturing is still considered the gold standard for bacterial identification [37], in part because the antibiotic resistance patterns can be determined for any isolates. In our study, resistance did not appear to be correlated with infection status of the THC. For example, the E. coli strain from an asymptomatic catheter (HC01) was resistant to ampicillin, tetracycline and trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole, whereas P. aeruginosa from a symptomatic catheter (HC09) was susceptible to all tested antibiotics. In 16 of 21 symptomatic THCs, from patients that had non resolving infections at the time of catheter removal, we detected either S. aureus or CoNS or both. S. aureus strains were only cultured from symptomatic THCs and half of the isolates were MRSA and others were resistant to one or more antibiotics and could be characterized as multi-drug resistant. In contrast, methicillin resistant S. epidermidis were isolated from both asymptomatic and symptomatic THCs. Isolate HC3-2, in particular, would be considered clinically significant because it was also resistant to vancomycin and linezolid. Unfortunately, we did not have access to all the patient history, so we do not know if antibiotic treatments had been given previously. The patient with this catheter had no microbiological testing performed while the catheter was in use, and no signs of infection. Other CoNS, such as S. capitis, S. hominis and S. lugdunensis were exclusively isolated from asymptomatic THCs and were generally antibiotic susceptible. Staphylococcus warneri was isolated from a symptomatic catheter (HC25) but S. aureus and S. epidermidis strains were also cultured, perhaps representing a polymicrobial infection. We hypothesize that the antibiotic resistance profiles of colonizing strains may be independent of the patient treatment history.

Biofilms are thought to be involved in many human infections, and it is assumed that most species/strains that colonize catheters do so in the form of biofilms [38]. Biofilms are defined as sessile communities of microbial cells that are irreversibly attached either to abiotic surfaces or to each other, and that are embedded in a matrix of self-secreted extracellular polymeric substances [39]. In the case of catheters, biofilm formation is complicated by the presence of human red blood cells and clots, or the fibrin sheath that sometimes forms around the end of the catheter [37,40], in addition to the presence of multiple microbial species [41]. In general, most of the Gram-positive strains that we isolated were classified as good biofilm formers, with strong biofilm formation in an average of five media types, and some strains being positive in all 12 media tested. Only one E. coli strain and one S. aureus strain were completely negative for biofilm formation, but it is possible that more comprehensive screening would identify additional media or growth conditions where all strains would form biofilms. It is also hard to reproduce the flow conditions that would naturally occur inside a catheter [42]. We were intrigued by potential biofilm formation of S. epidermidis and P. aeruginosa, which were detected together in 10% of the catheters analyzed. Previous studies have shown that S. epidermidis can be found together with P. aeruginosa in chronic wound infections as well as on indwelling catheters [43,44]. In contrast, other researchers have shown that P. aeruginosa can disrupt S. epidermidis biofilms, despite missing key virulence determinants (i.e., alkaline protease, elastase and pyocyanin) but also that some S. epidermidis strains are more resistant [45,46]. We identified three strains of S. epidermidis and one strain of S. aureus, from a total of twelve Staphylococcus strains, that formed strong biofilms together with P. aeruginosa in at least two different growth media. These growth conditions could represent the starting point for future studies, especially since the strains we tested all originated from catheters. Some polymicrobial biofilm models have been developed [47] but more research is needed to understand the basic principles and strain characteristics necessary for colonization.

Conclusion

Bacteria were detected in 100% of THCs when combining culture-dependent and culture-independent analysis. Based on 16S sequencing, and the observations of others [41], it is likely that most THCs in patients are colonized with multiple microbial species. One of the caveats of our study is that 16S sequencing cannot detect fungal species (i.e., Candida albicans, C. glabrata or C. auris), which are common causes of bloodstream infections in hospitalized patients [48] and had been detected in clinical swabs from some of the patients in our study. Knowing the influence of fungal colonization would add another layer of information. Due to the exploratory nature of this study, the data were more qualitative than quantitative and we focused on the overlap in species detected between the two groups of catheters. We do not know if there is a colonization threshold that needs to be reached for infections to occur, or whether colonization with a single species, such as S. aureus in our study is more the norm. Nevertheless, it is difficult to understand what a predictor of infection could be.

After completing this study, we are in a better position to design a systematic analysis of catheters and ensure that the overall number of sequence reads is more consistent between catheters, which will allow for accurate statistical comparisons. Further investigations on multiple nephrology units, combining culture techniques, quantitative sequencing analyses and microscopic techniques would provide insight into the colonization dynamics of THCs. Ultimately, this can lead to identifying microbial signatures that are more predictive of catheter related infections and determining the difference between bacterial noise and colonization that can turn into an infection.
