Surviving against the odds: a qualitative exploration of the resilience of adults experiencing homelessness in Accra, Ghana
Authors: Benedict Osei Asibey, Brahmaputra Marjadi, Elizabeth Conroy
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMost research on the homeless population has focused on the negative attributes and adversities they face. While these are important issues for research, focusing on them alone through a deficit lens may further advance the belief that such people have little or no capacities and skills for survival. This study seeks to address this gap by exploring the resilience of people experiencing homelessness in Accra, Ghana.MethodsQualitative data were collected via in-depth interviews with 27 purposively sampled homeless adults aged 18 + years. Interview data were analysed using interpretative phenomenological analysis.ResultsThree main themes were identified from the data: internal/individual resilience including their personal strengths; survival through resourcefulness including diversified income sources; and aspects of the participants’ social and physical ecology (facilitative environment) that protect against homelessness-related adversities.ConclusionsThis study provides some valuable insights into sources of resiliency for people experiencing homelessness in Ghana. The study’s findings highlight the need for government to provide adequate housing, resources, and targeted policies and interventions for the homeless population in Accra and other similar settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24669-x.

Background

Homelessness is a social and public health problem that results in extreme inequalities [1]. Experiencing homelessness is associated with engagement in health risk behaviours such as substance use [2] and violence and abuse [3]. In addition to high illness burden and mortalities [4], people who experience homelessness may have limited knowledge and information about needed health services, or services may not meet their needs [5], resulting in poor access and underutilisation of primary and preventive services [6, 7] and increased use of emergency and in-patient services [8]. Homelessness can also be both the cause and result of financial strain and family relational dysfunction [9]. People who experience homelessness are exposed to poor sleeping environments [10], stigmatisation and social exclusion [11], and commonly have disrupted education and employment [12].

Despite the vulnerabilities and inequalities experienced by people who are homeless, there is increasing acknowledgement of their resilience in surviving homelessness, with factors like sense of identity and self-worth, personal value systems, informal social networks, spirituality, and creativity identified as important to their survival [13, 14].

Resilience has been typically defined in reference to an adverse experience or a particular population and is understood to mean people’s ability to overcome and recover from hardship [12, 15]. Studies of resilience have suggested that people draw strength from a range of personal attributes, such as direct actions, social support, optimism, positive thinking, patience and endurance, prayer and spirituality, and focusing on children or the future [12, 16]. Ungar argues further that people’s resilience is a result of a combination of personal and environmental factors [17]. When facing a challenge, an individual’s resilience depends more on the capacity of the physical and social ecology to potentiate positive development under stress, than the capacity of individuals to exercise personal agency [17, 18]. Within the social ecologies of resilience framework, emphasis is given to community [family relations, friends] social structures, services (e.g. welfare, health and education) and socio-cultural factors as the central and most important resilience resources [17–20]. Ungar also identifies resilience as a universal concept that is understood similarly by different cultures although the specific processes of resilience are meaning-based and therefore context dependent [17]. Previous research on resiliency has mostly utilised homeless populations from Western countries. Just as the structural determinants and context of homelessness can differ across Western and non-Western countries, so too the experience of resiliency may differ given differences in cultural beliefs and value systems. This paper explores resilience among homeless people in Ghana, a country with homelessness estimate of 100,000 to 120,000 [21], including over 50,000 people sleeping rough in the Accra (the capital city) where the study was conducted. Despite the high prevalence of homelessness in Ghana, research on resiliency in the homeless adult population is scant. Two relevant studies in Ghana have focused on children living on the street aged up to 16 years [22, 23] – similar research among adults has not been conducted. The scarcity of research on the resiliency of the homeless population limits our understanding of how best to support this population to exit homelessness and improve their wellbeing.

Given the limited understanding of resiliency for people who experience homelessness in Ghana, this study draws on qualitative interview data to explore processes of resilience for this population in Accra. Guided by the Ungar’s resiliency framework, the study aims to inform policies and practice interventions to improve wellbeing and bolster pathways out of homelessness, improve social services, and promote urban development in Ghana and other sub-Saharan African countries through a strengths-based approach [24–26]. The Ungar’s resiliency framework is particularly relevant to the Ghanaian context given it accounts for individual, community and cultural variability in resilience and differential impacts [17], A strengths-based approach has a focus on resilience, rather than the more often used deficit lens that focuses on adversities alone such as substance use [2], poor health [27], and experience of social stigma [28]. Although the negative attributes of the homeless population are important issues for research to consider, focusing on them alone may further advance the belief that they have little or no capacities and skills to survive and improve themselves. A strength-based approach is important for informing tailored policies and interventions to support the homeless population survive and ultimately exit homelessness.

Methods

This qualitative study was part of a larger community-based mixed-methods research project to explore the intersecting issues of urban/socioeconomic migration, homelessness, health, and health care utilisation in the Ghanaian context. Approved by Western Sydney University Human Research Ethics Committee [H13120] and authorised by the Ghanaian Department of Social Development in Accra, this research project employed a survey of 305 homeless adults followed by in-depth interviews with a sub-set of 27 participants. The present report utilised the qualitative data only.

Study population, recruitment, and consent

The study population were adults aged 18 years and above with at least six months homelessness experience in Accra. Described in our previous paper [29], the research adopted the inclusive European Typology of Homelessness and Housing Exclusion (ETHOS) definition of homelessness [30] to categorise people as experiencing either sheltered or unsheltered homelessness. Facilitated by local social workers, trained research assistants fluent in the local Twi language recruited study participants from streets, slums, emergency shelters, and residential alcohol and other drugs (AOD) rehabilitation facilities in Accra.

Interview participants were purposively sampled from the survey participants based on (1) residential stability and duration of homelessness; (2) health characteristics (burden of illnesses and self-rated health status); (3) social stigma score; (4) rate of health care utilisation (low/high); (5) substance use; and (6) experience of violence. The selection process involved two steps. First, research assistants (guided by the pre-determined criteria) noted specific survey participants as potentially eligible based on their survey responses. At the end of the survey, these individuals were re-informed of a possible interview, and if they consented, their contact details were taken. Second, at the end of each day the first author (BOA) reviewed the survey responses of potential interview participants to confirm their eligibility for the interview. Individuals confirmed as potential participants were contacted to discuss the day, time, and location for the interview. Most of the participants agreed to do the interview a day or two after the survey.

On the interview day, BOA provided and explained the Participant Information Sheet and obtained written informed consent in the Twi or English language. Participants could withdraw their participation at any time. Thirty-eight participants were invited for interview; five declined on the interview day for personal reasons. From the 33 people who agreed to participate, interviewing was ceased after 27 participants due to data saturation i.e., new interviews only repeated ideas and concepts expressed in earlier interviews [31].

Data collection

In-depth interviews were chosen for its flexibility and creation of a relaxed atmosphere for interaction [32], to avoid discomfort due to sensitive and confidential nature of some of the topics discussed, and its suitability for exploring people’s thoughts, perceptions, and feelings about a particular phenomenon [33]. Questions to elicit participants’ strengths and resilience included:

What difficulties do you face as someone who is experiencing homelessness?How do you handle such difficulties?What has helped you to survive the adversities associated with being in a homeless situation?What are your hopes for the future and your plans to improve your current situation?

Based on participants’ answers, several probing questions were asked to obtain more information.

The interview guide was translated to Twi language by a translator from the Department of Linguistics at the University of Ghana, Accra and back-translated by a translator from the Department of Modern Languages at the Kwame Nkrumah University of Science and Technology, Kumasi. The authors compared the back-translation with the original English-language guide. Discrepancies were resolved in an online meeting (via zoom) facilitated by the first author BOA who can speak, read, and write the Twi language.

Minor modifications were made after the back-translation but did not change the actual meaning of the questions. Terms such as ‘homeless’, junky, HIV and AIDS, and suicide were removed due to potential stigmatisation and offensive nature and replaced with more culturally accepted terms such as ‘street life’ for rooflessness, ‘ghetto life’ or ‘ghetto living’ for those in shacks and slums, and ‘addiction’ for ‘junky’.

The interview guide was also pilot tested to examine its suitability. The pilot testing was conducted with ten adults who were experiencing homelessness conveniently selected based on recommendation from the social workers. Individuals who participated in the pilot testing went through same recruitment, inclusion, informed consent, and participation procedures as other respondents. The interview guide was able to capture all relevant data, and the participants in the pilot testing found the interview guide suitable.

All but two of the interviews were conducted by BOA; interviews with two Muslim women were conducted by a Muslim female research assistant. Interviews were conducted at community centres and playing grounds for the people sleeping rough and those in slums, as well as common rooms of emergency shelters and AOD rehabilitation centres for participants residing in those facilities. There was a pre-interview discussion to ease stress and build rapport between the interviewers and the participants.

The 30 to 60 min interviews, conducted in the local Twi language (n = 24) and English (n = 3) were audio recorded with participants’ permission, and transcribed by BOA. Participants were reimbursed with mosquito repellent (valued at AU$5) and were able to check-in to any of the two partner AOD rehabilitation centres free of charge for mental health support in case of psychological harm and distress.

Data analysis

Interviews recorded in English were transcribed using online transcription tool and proofread by the authors. Interview recordings in Twi were translated into English during transcription conducted independently by the first author BOA and one translator from the Department of Modern Languages at Kwame Nkrumah University of Science and Technology, Kumasi. The two transcripts were compared to assess agreement. Interview transcripts were imported into the NVIVO software version 20 for analysis. Interpretative Phenomenological Analysis (IPA) [34] was conducted to develop in-depth and detailed descriptions of the participants’ personal lived experiences. In applying the principles and guidelines for the IPA, the lead researcher read the transcripts to immerse with the data, identified interesting issues, and transformed initial notes into short phrases to obtain the essence of what was identified in the transcripts. Core themes were identified together with their sub-themes and relationships for clustering according to conceptual similarities, and various clusters were given descriptive labels. The authors met regularly to discuss and refine codes and themes after double checking. The lead researcher wrote the interpretative phenomenological analysis of the identified themes. Each theme was illustrated with quotations from transcripts, followed by analytical comments. All names of people mentioned in transcripts have been replaced with pseudonyms.

Researcher positionality and reflexivity

To increase the robustness and ensure credibility of the research process, the authors addressed their potential influence on the collection, analysis, and interpretation of data through their personal values, experiences, perceptions, attitudes, and relationships with the study population.

The lead author BOA is a tertiary educated Ghanaian, who grew up in an extended family of mixed low and high-educated individuals in Ghana. As a rural-urban migrant who has never experienced homelessness or housing instability, BOA does not consider himself to possess a first-hand understanding of the complexities of experiencing homelessness in a Ghanaian city. Although an outsider to homelessness experience, BOA has lived, studied, and worked in city neighbourhoods with high number of people who experience homelessness for 16 years, and participated in research and recreational activities involving people who experience homelessness. This long relationship with people who experience homelessness has made BOA empathetic to their conditions including health, lack of support and services, experience of violence, and stigmatisation. This could potentially affect the interpretation of the research results (particularly using IPA).

Acknowledging the potential influence of BOA’s position and experiences on the research, he kept a reflective research diary to document all self-evaluations and critiques throughout the study. All sentiments and the possible effects on the research process, results, and interpretations were recorded in the reflective diary at the end of each day of data collection and analysis. This diary captured additional notes about interactions with participants or the data, including thoughts and feelings during the day. During analysis and interpretation, BOA conducted regular checks to ensure that narratives and interpretations were data-driven. BOA also used ‘bracketing’ of his experience with the homeless population – which is a method to mitigate the influence of preconceptions that may taint a qualitative research process.

More than two-thirds had resided in at least two communities in the past six months. The participants were mostly men, aged 20 to 57 years, with low education levels and mostly unemployed migrants from outside the Greater-Accra region (Table 1).

Surviving homelessness

The study participants discussed their experiences of homelessness and associated experiences of profound adversities. They were afflicted by diseases and experienced poor health and commonly referred to the economic hardships, violence, stigmatisation and discrimination which led to further isolation from mainstream society and more critically, underutilisation of health care. However, all participants described their efforts and ways to manage these adversities.

Three major themes were identified that reflected Ungar’s conceptualisation of resilience as an interaction between personal strength or internalised resilience and a potentiating environment. Theme one was participants’ internalised resilience, including their personal strengths that were integral to their survival. Within this theme, three sources of personal strength were identified as sub-themes: determination/motivation to survive based on others’ successful exit from homelessness; courage to succeed for children or other family members; and religious/spiritual beliefs. Theme two was participants’ resourcefulness by engaging in diverse income-generating activities. Theme three was the protective aspects of the participants’ social and physical ecology (facilitative environment) against homelessness-related adversities, including the availability of peer relationships and charitable acts, and the participants’ ability to leverage support from them. The main themes and their sub-themes are presented below with representative quotes.

Theme one: internal resilience and personal strength to survive homelessness

The participants frequently described themselves as “survivors” who relied on internalised resilience to manage and overcome the multiple daily adversities experienced while homeless. Internal resilience included personal strength and attitudes such as confidence, hope and optimism, and physical strength. Osofo, a self-described ‘hardcore’ person, explained that surviving the violence, stigmatisation, and discrimination required physical strength:

Look, nobody will fight for you if you don’t show strength. If they [other street dwellers] see that you are weak you will never be happy. People will bully you for all your personal stuff. When you go outside this area, the police will harass you, people will insult you and wrongly accuse you of stealing. I have lived through all these for such a long time because I have developed the strength to survive.

Look, nobody will fight for you if you don’t show strength. If they [other street dwellers] see that you are weak you will never be happy. People will bully you for all your personal stuff. When you go outside this area, the police will harass you, people will insult you and wrongly accuse you of stealing. I have lived through all these for such a long time because I have developed the strength to survive.

Participants’ internal resilience was driven by factors such as others’ successful exit from homelessness, courage to succeed for children or other family members, and religious/spiritual beliefs, presented below as subthemes.

Drawing strength from other people’s success stories

Participants described drawing their strength from the successes of people who have exited homelessness. Such strength was reflected in John’s story about rough sleeping, how he was motivated to check into a residential AOD rehabilitation facility upon seeing that another “street boy” had successfully rehabilitated and working with an NGO as a driver:

Anthony was my guy on the streets… We used to blaze [smoke cannabis] together. Once he left this place after fighting over gambling money… I saw him three years later and he was driving an NGO car, wearing their T-shirt, and campaigning against drugs use. He is like a poster boy, some kind of a success story, and leading the team to reach out to the ghettos… I told him I want to be like him, and they brought me here for rehabilitation.

Once he left this place after fighting over gambling money… I saw him three years later and he was driving an NGO car, wearing their T-shirt, and campaigning against drugs use. He is like a poster boy, some kind of a success story, and leading the team to reach out to the ghettos… I told him I want to be like him, and they brought me here for rehabilitation.

Some participants expressed belief in themselves as changed persons due to the strength and motivation derived from others’ success stories. Asamoah, a resident at AOD rehabilitation facility talked about his transformation in life: “I encouraged myself with the successes of the people I met here. Now, I have the strength to survive” Although no longer the rich banker and business owner that he once was, Asamoah had accepted his new life: “I don’t have the money and luxury anymore, but I am at peace with myself, and I want to work with the NGO after a successful rehabilitation.” These stories reveal that, the people who experience homelessness draw hope and motivation for better outcomes from others’ success stories.

Courage to succeed for family and children

In facing adversities, participants commonly mentioned the importance of family particularly children as a source of their internalised resilience. To them, taking care of their family and children provided them a sense of joy, accomplishment, and motivation to work hard for a future out of homelessness. For Adoley, being a responsible mother encouraged her to overcome the everyday violence and stigma to provide for her children:

I can’t turn my back on them or give them up for adoption. They are my children, they follow me everywhere, and I am always thinking about them. It worries me when my child is crying because she is hungry. So, I have vowed never to let that happen ….

I can’t turn my back on them or give them up for adoption. They are my children, they follow me everywhere, and I am always thinking about them. It worries me when my child is crying because she is hungry. So, I have vowed never to let that happen ….

Naa, Prince, and Osofo described how the thoughts about their children saved them from dying by suicide. Prince, who lived at a marketplace, described having several suicidal thoughts after losing his hand in a car accident: “If not for my three girls, I may have committed suicide by this time…They are the only reason why I’m still surviving in this life.” Being able to financially support his three children, Prince now feels a sense of pride and accomplishment, with the motivation to live longer, work hard, and support them. Naa who was experiencing chronic illness described having considered suicide but stopped after thinking about her grandchild’s future: “I couldn’t do it [suicide] because I was feeling that who is going to take care of my granddaughter for me. This small girl is all the hope that I have left. The only time I smile is when playing with her”.

A few participants described their intimate partners as their main source of hope and motivation, including as the reason for their decision to stop risky behaviours. Lily described her husband as the main reason for quitting street gang life and resettling in a shack in a different community: “He pursued me to leave the streets and come to his place… It was difficult and emotional leaving the people I had known for years, but I had to look at the future. These accounts suggest the importance family and children as a source of personal strength for people who experience homelessness to survive adversities.

Religious faith and spirituality as sources of strength

Although their religious faith varied, having faith and engaging in prayers were dominant in participants’ stories and experiences as source of resilience to manage and overcome adversities. Some participants thanked the supernatural power for providing for them in difficult times. Lily described her transitioning from two years in the streets into a kiosk in Accra’s Central Business District as “an unexpected miracle from God”:

Once there is God, I know I have hope… Getting this place [kiosk] when I had no hope is even a big miracle. I slept in the street for two years but through God, I met a man who offered me job when I was not even expecting it… I know God will take me out of here too.

Once there is God, I know I have hope… Getting this place [kiosk] when I had no hope is even a big miracle. I slept in the street for two years but through God, I met a man who offered me job when I was not even expecting it… I know God will take me out of here too.

For some, religious beliefs and/or spirituality were their only sources of strength when they had no trust and support from family, friends, and the government. Sarah commented: “My family and friends have failed me when I most needed their support. The government has failed me… All I have now is my faith in God. I look up to him, I pray and hope things will work.” Asamoah explained how the spirituality element was an important factor in the success of his alcohol rehabilitation: “At [treatment facility] here we dwell in the word of God and our procedures are based on the Bible. In addition to the rehabilitation process, we also pray to God for strength… For me, the God factor is huge in my recovery process.” The participants’ accounts reveal the importance of faith and spiritual beliefs in driving the resilience and personal strength of people experiencing homelessness to survive adversities.

Theme two: demonstrating resourcefulness through diverse income generating activities

For many participants, the best homelessness survival strategy has always been self-reliance, and engaging in diverse income-generating activities was important to survive. Income-generating activities mostly involved what participants described as informal, less desirable, insecure, and unsustainable activities. For the men, such activities included loading and pushing trucks, loading buses, construction labour, and street hawking: “We hustle for money to survive… I load the truck and push it from the station to the marketplaces where the cars can’t go. [Osofo] Jarrah was learning a trade as an auto-mechanic apprentice and at the same time had a job as a bus conductor.

The women mostly combined street hawking with other activities or sold a variety of items, as described by Rita:

I sell many things in the street… In the morning, I sell bread, meat pie, and hot beverage because people need breakfast. In the afternoon, I sell water and soft drinks up to about 8 o’clock… when it rains, I sell shower cap and umbrella… During the dry season I sell body cream and lip shine.

I sell many things in the street… In the morning, I sell bread, meat pie, and hot beverage because people need breakfast. In the afternoon, I sell water and soft drinks up to about 8 o’clock… when it rains, I sell shower cap and umbrella… During the dry season I sell body cream and lip shine.

Similarly, Gifty cleaned a restaurant in the mornings and engaged in street hawking in the afternoon: “I sell the drinks only in the afternoon because people buy more when the weather is hot… Early in the morning I quickly rush to clean the chop bar before customers start coming”.

Engagement in multiple income-generating activities demonstrates the participants’ resourcefulness to survive the economic challenges of being homeless but also their reliance on poorly paid employment. Although their jobs were not the ideal employment that they hoped for, most participants agreed that such low-income informal activities fitted their circumstances of being in a homeless situation, and for many, having no professional skill. The little incomes were paid immediately and gave them some control over their lives. without depending on community organisations.

Theme three: Social and physical ecology of homelessness- taking advantage of peer support and charitable acts

The participants’ accounts revealed some aspects of their social and physical ecology that enabled their resilience to survive homelessness. This included forming peer support networks to counter social exclusion, the nature of which differed based on housing situations. The street and slum dwellers’ groups were mostly based on gang, ethnicity, or religious affiliation, and appeared to have a strong sense of community by offering support, protection, and shared resources, as Kwabena described:When you look at us from the outside you will think we don’t get along. We live individually but we have a community here… Me, personally I have decided to protect the young ones and make sure that nobody from outside beat them… The area boys also contribute and pay hospital bills when one is sick.

When you look at us from the outside you will think we don’t get along. We live individually but we have a community here… Me, personally I have decided to protect the young ones and make sure that nobody from outside beat them… The area boys also contribute and pay hospital bills when one is sick.

Supportive relationships also included helping others find a job or sharing a job with peers, as described by Osofo: “If I don’t have money, my friend can come and tell me that he has a job so I should help him do it and then he will pay me. When I also get a job, I will call someone to help me and pay him.”

So, most of them can’t make it… Without our leadership and support many of them jump the fence back to the streets… So, we have decided to make this place like a community, and we provide all the peer support that people need. For the women in emergency shelters, important peer support included encouraging and advising others to overcome situations like domestic violence and problematic substance use that led to homelessness. Sarah described the importance of such peer support in overcoming her alcohol addiction:It was not easy when I first came here but some of the women here helped me. We shared our stories, they comforted me and made me realise the sense of belonging. Now, I don’t even think about the problems that I went through for over four years that made me become addicted to alcohol.

In all, while the experience of homelessness was often described as full of unsafe, violent, poor, and unhealthy conditions, the participants’ accounts demonstrate a strong bond and sense of community. Such camaraderie and supportive relationships were important as they provided both practical and emotional support.

In addition to relying on supportive peer relationships, the participants acknowledged the importance of charitable community organisations to survive homelessness, including providing food, clothing, toiletries, and bedsheets. Charity organisations also provided health screening and disease treatment for health problems that might otherwise have been missed due to high service cost. For Asamoah, a health screening revealed the seriousness of his eye problem that he had not paid attention to:I had a problem reading the newspaper, but I didn’t take it seriously… After that [screening] they told me I need to be serious…even the glasses that I am wearing is from them.

I had a problem reading the newspaper, but I didn’t take it seriously… After that [screening] they told me I need to be serious…even the glasses that I am wearing is from them.

The women acknowledged the importance of breast cancer screening and maternal health education provided by community organisations: “When I was pregnant, there was one social worker from [charity organisation] who educated me all the time, and she made sure that I never miss even one antenatal.” [Sarah].

Community organisations helped some participants enrol in AOD rehabilitation programmes and provided skills and vocational training to improve their lives. For example, Lily learned baking through a training programme by a community organisation:

They were doing it for the girls in the street and slums… they taught us how to do things like making pastries, soap, yoghurt… Many of the girls didn’t finish but I was determined to do it and now that is my work.

They were doing it for the girls in the street and slums… they taught us how to do things like making pastries, soap, yoghurt… Many of the girls didn’t finish but I was determined to do it and now that is my work.

The men who benefited from such training programmes in craftsmanship (Sark), carpentry (Amankwah), and auto-mechanics (Jarrah) expressed their confidence in applying their skills and improving their lives. Jarrah described some others who benefited from the training programme and have successfully exited homelessness:I came to meet two guys here who were beneficiaries of the same training programme. Now one is working for a fitting shop in Accra here, and the other person has gone back to his hometown to work there. I know I will also succeed.

I came to meet two guys here who were beneficiaries of the same training programme. Now one is working for a fitting shop in Accra here, and the other person has gone back to his hometown to work there. I know I will also succeed.

The narratives above demonstrate the importance of community-based care and support to the survival of people experiencing homelessness. The participants relied on both their personal coping strategies and resources as well community or societal level resources to meet the challenges associated with being homeless.

Discussion

This study opens the discourse about the need for strength-based policies and strategies to support people experiencing homelessness through an exploration of their resilience. In line with Ungar’s assertion that people’s resilience is shaped by their environment and cultures [18], this study revealed some unique and culturally meaningful ways resilience is shaped in Ghana’s urban homeless population. The study participants described the resources they used for problem solving through internal resilience.

While talking about constraints of homelessness, participants described their reliance on supportive peer networks and the charitable acts from individuals and community organisations. Peer relationship and social networks have been identified as important coping resources among street youth, particularly newcomers [35] and among college students experiencing homelessness [36]. Supportive peer relationships within the homeless sub-culture serve as a source of motivation and guidance for individuals as they navigate challenges while homeless and strive to sustain stable accommodation [36]. This study’s participants sought support from their peers, as well as charitable community organisations, to address their material needs, social connectedness and protection.

This study supports the contention that a resilient attitude of people experiencing homelessness can be influenced by the success of other people with similar experiences, which has been identified in previously studies [37, 38]. Despite experiencing several challenges, some people can exit homelessness and succeed in business activities, skill and job acquisition, and participate in advocacy and community work. =. Previous studies have also reported the importance of children and family and spirituality among homeless youth and adults in Africa and Western countries. A study of people experiencing homelessness in the United States reported that having children and grandchildren was the main reason for not dying by suicide [16], associating family and children with joy and motivation. Similarly, having a sense of spirituality emerged as an important contributor to resilience as it helped people experiencing homelessness feel a sense of connection and a source of motivation to meeting goals. This study also supports the contention that spirituality and belief in higher power influences the resilient attitude of people experiencing homelessness. Previous studies have linked spirituality to overall well-being in both homeless youth and adult populations. In Ghana, Oppong Asante [22] found that beliefs in an eternal being and religiosity promote hope and resilience among street adolescents. Studies on homelessness in North America [39, 40] also reported spirituality and religious faith as instrumental in overcoming difficulties.

The participants’ resourcefulness in finding diverse income sources suggests their hardworking attitude, desire to be independent, and personal agency, as noted by a Brazilian study [41]. Provision of skills and vocational training, and development of policies and interventions to facilitate obtaining employment without discrimination, would help achieve the desired economic independence.

Implications for policy and practice

This study has implications for improving the wellbeing of people who experience homelessness, strengthening the capacity of social workers who work with the vulnerable and disadvantaged population, and addressing the urban homelessness problem in Ghana. The findings suggest the need for a resilience-focused strength-based approach from social welfare and community organisations, including the use of peers in these services. By recognising and focusing on their resilience, services delivered by social welfare and community organisations may better resonate with the homeless population and empower them to take charge of their own lives rather than depending on charity from individuals and organisations [42]. Strengthening resilience may be achieved through strategies to promote peer relationships, such as sports, entertainment, and arts, as suggested in other studies [22, 43]. Engaging in social activities, if supported through social welfare and community organisations, could help people experiencing homelessness establish or strengthen social connections, keep healthy, and stay away from violence.

Role modelling from people who have successfully exited homelessness as a source of internal resilience implies the need for social welfare and community organisations to use peers in their outreach activities and service delivery to enhance the dissemination of important information, including strategies to manage stress and trauma, minimise harm from problematic substance use, and access support services. Also vital is the recognition of the importance of charitable acts in surviving homelessness by the participants, which suggests the need for the government through the Department of Social Development and NGO sector to tailor their outreach and other programmes towards enhancing and using their skills to their own benefit. This aim could be achieved if the social welfare and community organisations recognise the resilience and survival skills of people experiencing homelessness.

This study also suggests the need to strengthen the capacity of social workers who work with the vulnerable and disadvantaged population to recognise and address challenges through health, community, and social work education. Social workers need to acknowledge that just like the general population, people who experience homelessness also possess strength and resilience to cope and overcome adversities. Acknowledging such strength and resilience is an important step towards designing and implementing effective interventions. The capacity of social workers can be achieved through the integration of different strength-based intervention strategies into training curricula and social work education in Ghana. Like the RESHAPE (reducing Stigma among healthcare providers to improve mental health services) intervention in Nepal [44], a connect-based intervention which promotes contacts between trainees and the homeless population (via placements) could increase self-awareness, reduce stigmatising attitudes, and recognise strength and resilience. In addition, social and community workers training could incorporate strength-based modules, dialogues, and short training sessions. To effectively implement strategies to strengthen social workers capacity to recognise and explore peoples’ strength, universities and other training institutions need to build strong partnerships with the agencies and organisations that work with the marginalised and vulnerable population in Ghana, such as mental health services, drug and alcohol services, and community and religious organisations.

Drawing on Ungar’s framework’s assertion that resilience is shaped by people’s environment, this study has implications for a broader government policy and support to address the urban homelessness problem and to create a potentiating environment for people to resolve their homelessness. Confirming the well-established role of housing as a social determinant of health and wellbeing [45] and given the affordable housing crises and high prevalence homelessness in Ghanaian cities, the government of Ghana can support the NGO sector already providing short-term hostels and emergency shelters for people who sleep rough in Accra, particularly women with children and people with disabilities. Government can also collaborate with private housing developers to provide long-term safe and affordable shelters for the homeless population, while working with NGOs and social welfare to provide wrap-around case management support to help people maintain their tenancies. This may include assisting them to negotiate with landlords and helping them manage other problems such as mental health problems, problematic substance use, and violence which may affect their ability to sustain a tenancy.

This study also demonstrates the need for a national strategy to address the excessive rural-urban and inter-regional migrations. The government can implement policies and interventions aimed at creating potentiating environment in rural areas and less developed regions to prevent internal migration, which is a strong driver of homelessness in Accra. Along with addressing the push factors of migration like poverty and unemployment, limited educational and social opportunities, and lack of basic infrastructure, strategies to strengthen the retain factors in the rural areas and less developed regions will be important in addressing the migration problem. The socio-economic development of rural regions through policies that improve education, health care, and social services should be strengthened. Also, policies and strategies to provide easy accessibility to socio-economic infrastructure and services will help reduce internal migration and urban homelessness, given internal migrations are also driven by the people’s desire to fulfil the dream of city life with easy accessibility to socio-economic infrastructure and services. Also, strategies for equitable provision of water, electricity, roads, and entertainment will help reduce the migration intentions of residents in rural areas and less developed regions.

Limitations

This study’s results should be considered in the context of some limitations. The qualitative nature of the study with a purposively drawn sample means that the findings cannot be generalised. It is not clear how this study’s sample compares with those in other cities in the country with respect to their composition, demographic, migration, housing characteristics, and exposure to different vulnerabilities. This is particularly the case because Accra attracts more internal and international migrants than other cities in Ghana, and the few emergency shelters and drug and alcohol rehabilitation centres are mostly located there compared to other cities. However, the amount/depth/richness of qualitative data, and a review of studies in other countries, indicate several similarities. Therefore, it is reasonable to believe that at least some elements of resilience among homeless people are transferable to other settings. Also, this paper was entirely based on self-reported data from interviews with people who experience homelessness, some of whom may have provided responses that reflect their social desirability rather than their actual situation. However, participants’ anonymity and confidentiality were ensured to foster honesty and trust, and to encourage them to be more truthful and forthcoming. The interviewers were also trained and encouraged to identify signs of social desirability bias in participants’ answers, including vague responses and address these with gently probing questions to clarify and ensure accuracy. Also, BOA’s positionality may have influenced data collection or analysis. This was addressed by keeping a reflective research diary to document all self-evaluations and critiques throughout the study. The authors also ensured narratives and interpretations during fata analysis were data-driven through regular checks.

Conclusions

This study has demonstrated the many resources and sources of resilience among adults experiencing homelessness for surviving adversities in Accra, Ghana. The study highlights the need for government to place greater attention to policies and strategies that focus on the strength and resilience of the homeless population. Collaboration with people with lived experience of homelessness to utilise strength-based approaches, provision of skills and vocational training to achieve economic independence and providing safe and stable housing should be leveraged in improving and supporting this resilience. This study also highlights.

how resilience can be fostered among people who experience homelessness in similar cities in sub-Sahara African countries with similar socio-demographic and economic characteristics as Ghana.
