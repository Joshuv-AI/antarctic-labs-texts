Beyond genotype: clustering analysis highlights clinical heterogeneity in paediatric familial Mediterranean fever
Authors: Isild Mahé, Claire Billard, Federica Anselmi, Linda Rossi-Semerano, Caroline Galeotti, Isabelle Kone-Paut, Perrine Dusser
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFamilial Mediterranean fever (FMF) is an autoinflammatory disease caused by mutations in the MEFV gene. Despite therapeutic advances with colchicine and interleukin-1 (IL-1) inhibitors, FMF shows marked interindividual variability, including among symptomatic heterozygotes, challenging genotype-phenotype correlations.ObjectivesTo identify clinically meaningful subgroups of paediatric patients with FMF using a clustering approach and to characterise their clinical features and disease trajectories.MethodsWe conducted a monocentric retrospective study including 185 paediatric patients diagnosed with FMF between 2004 and 2025 according to Eurofever criteria. Patients were stratified as genetically confirmed FMF (homozygous, compound heterozygous) or symptomatic heterozygotes. Demographic, clinical, laboratory and treatment data were collected longitudinally. Clustering analysis was used to identify clinically meaningful subgroups.ResultsThe median age at onset was 2.5 years and the median diagnostic delay was 24 months. Ninety-three (50%) patients were symptomatic heterozygotes. Compared with genetically confirmed FMF, heterozygotes had milder disease, lower colchicine doses and less frequent IL-1 inhibitor use. Cluster analysis identified five distinct subgroups, ranging from asymptomatic or mild heterozygotes without treatment to early-onset homozygous patients with severe disease requiring higher-dose colchicine and IL-1 inhibitors. Not all heterozygous patients had a mild disease course. Sex, age at onset, genotype, attack frequency and inflammatory markers contributed to cluster differentiation. Severe phenotypes were enriched in female patients with early-onset M694V homozygosity.ConclusionCluster analysis identified heterogeneous patterns of disease expression, suggesting that the genotype alone does not fully explain clinical variability in FMF. These findings highlight the value of multidimensional approaches to better characterise disease heterogeneity.Keywords: Familial Mediterranean Fever, Child, Risk Factors, Rare Diseases

Introduction

Familial Mediterranean fever (FMF) is the historical prototype of autoinflammatory diseases. The discovery of the MEFV gene in 1997 marked a turning point in our understanding of innate immune dysregulation.1,3 FMF is also the most prevalent monogenic autoinflammatory disease, particularly among Mediterranean populations, with an estimated prevalence of 1/1000 in Turkey and up to 1/500 in Armenia, compared with 1/48 000 in non-endemic regions such as Slovakia.4 5 FMF typically presents with recurrent episodes of fever and polyserositis lasting 48–72 hours,6 7 often triggered by physical or emotional stress.8 Although the usual age at onset is around 4 years, 10% of patients experience their first symptoms during infancy.9 Chronic inflammation may lead to long-term complications, among which amyloid A (AA) amyloidosis remains the most emblematic.10

Daily colchicine therapy has dramatically improved the prognosis of FMF, reducing the frequency and severity of attacks,11 12 preventing amyloidosis13,15 and improving quality of life. Advances in genetic testing have facilitated early diagnosis and timely colchicine initiation, while interleukin-1 (IL-1) inhibitors have proven to be effective and safe in patients with colchicine resistance or intolerance.16,19 Despite these advances, FMF management remains challenging. Historically, disease severity has been associated with biallelic pathogenic variants in exon 10 of MEFV, particularly M694V, M684I, M680I and V726A. Homozygosity for M694V is linked to earlier onset, more severe disease and higher risk of AA amyloidosis.20,24 These patients often require higher colchicine doses, increasing the risk of intolerance or resistance.25

However, FMF is far from being a uniform disease. The recognition that heterozygous carriers may also develop symptomatic forms has challenged the classical Mendelian paradigm.26 In clinical practice, patients with a single MEFV mutation display a broad phenotypic spectrum, ranging from mild or intermittent inflammation27 to classical FMF features,28 sometimes overlapping with other inflammatory conditions such as spondylarthritis or Crohn’s disease.29,32 This variability complicates risk stratification and therapeutic decision-making.33 Although several studies have explored genotype-phenotype correlations, none have attempted to identify clinically homogeneous subgroups based on multidimensional clinical characteristics.

Beyond the genetic distinction between patients with homozygous FMF (FMF-HOM), compound heterozygous FMF (FMF-CH) and symptomatic heterozygous FMF (FMF-SH),34 little is known about the diversity of clinical trajectories within FMF. However, genotype-phenotype correlations alone do not fully explain the marked variability observed in clinical practice. Patients sharing the same genotype may experience very different disease severity, treatment requirements and long-term outcomes.35,37 This is particularly evident among FMF-SH carriers, whose clinical course ranges from mild intermittent symptoms to severe inflammatory disease requiring biologic therapy. Most previous studies have analysed FMF populations using predefined genetic or clinical categories. However, such approaches may fail to capture the complex interactions between genetic, demographic, clinical and biological factors that shape disease expression. Data-driven methods such as clustering analysis may help identify clinically meaningful subgroups beyond traditional genotype-based classifications. A better characterisation of this clinical intra-FMF heterogeneity is essential to identify prognostic subgroups, anticipate therapeutic needs and guide individualised care. Accordingly, the objective of this study was to identify clinically meaningful subgroups of paediatric patients with FMF using an unsupervised clustering approach, and to subsequently characterise their clinical, biological and therapeutic features, including patterns of disease trajectories.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

We conducted a monocentric, retrospective cohort study including all patients diagnosed with FMF between January 2004 and March 2025 at the Paediatric Rheumatology Department of Bicêtre University Hospital, a national reference centre for autoinflammatory diseases (CEREMAIA). Diagnosis was established according to the Eurofever/Paediatric Rheumatology InterNational Trials Organisation (PRINTO) criteria for FMF.38

Study population

Patients were eligible if they were diagnosed before 18 years of age, had at least two follow-up visits and available MEFV genotyping results. Exclusion criteria were incomplete clinical data, uncertain diagnosis, first visit after age 18 years or only a single available visit. Only patients fulfilling the Eurofever/PRINTO classification criteria for FMF were included in the cohort. As a result, patients fulfilling diagnostic criteria for periodic fever, aphthous stomatitis, pharyngitis and cervical adenitis (PFAPA) were not eligible for inclusion. Particular attention was paid to the duration of febrile episodes and associated symptoms, as FMF-related pharyngitis was defined by short-lasting episodes (≤48 hours), which are incompatible with PFAPA diagnostic criteria. Ear-nose-throat (ENT) manifestations recorded in this study mainly corresponded to such short-lasting pharyngitis episodes occurring during inflammatory attacks.

Genetic classification

Patients were stratified into two groups:

Genetically confirmed FMF (cFMF): patients carrying biallelic pathogenic MEFV variants, either FMF-HOM or FMF-CH.

Genetically confirmed FMF (cFMF): patients carrying biallelic pathogenic MEFV variants, either FMF-HOM or FMF-CH.

FMF-SH: patients carrying a single pathogenic MEFV variant who fulfilled the Eurofever/PRINTO classification criteria for FMF, typically presenting with recurrent, self-limited episodes of fever and serositis lasting 24–72 hours and showing a clinical response to colchicine.

FMF-SH: patients carrying a single pathogenic MEFV variant who fulfilled the Eurofever/PRINTO classification criteria for FMF, typically presenting with recurrent, self-limited episodes of fever and serositis lasting 24–72 hours and showing a clinical response to colchicine.

Pathogenic variants considered for inclusion were M694V, M694I, V726A, M680I and R761H. Variant classification was based on the Infevers and ClinVar databases. The E148Q variant was not included in the analysis because its pathogenic role remains debated and it is often considered a low-penetrance variant. To avoid potential misclassification, only variants consistently considered pathogenic were retained for clustering analyses.

Data collection

Demographic, clinical, laboratory and therapeutic data were retrospectively extracted from electronic health records (the ORBIS electronic health record system, Assistance Publique-Hôpitaux de Paris, France) and the national ‘Base Maladie Rare’ (BAMARA) registry.39 Clinical variables included age at onset, age at diagnosis, diagnostic delay, family history, attack features (fever, serositis, arthritis, myalgia, erysipelas-like erythema) and presence of persistent inflammation. Laboratory variables included peak C reactive protein (CRP) and serum amyloid A (SAA) levels during and between attacks. Therapeutic data included colchicine dose, treatment response, use of IL-1 inhibitors and treatment duration.

Follow-up data were collected longitudinally until the last available visit or transition to adult care. Colchicine doses were expressed in mg/day and categorised according to age-specific paediatric dosing based on European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology (EULAR) recommendations.40

Variables

Prespecified variables were selected based on their clinical relevance to FMF phenotype and management.

Key demographic, genetic and clinical variables included sex (male/female); age at onset (<1 year, 1 to <4 years or ≥4 years); genotype (FMF-HOM, FMF-CH, FMF-SH); attack frequency before treatment (<1, 1–2 or >2 attacks per month) and CRP level during attacks (<30 or ≥30 mg/L), using a prespecified exploratory threshold.

Treatment-related variables included background therapy (none, colchicine alone or colchicine plus IL-1 inhibitor). In this cohort, IL-1 inhibitors were used exclusively as add-on therapy to colchicine. Colchicine doses were recorded in mg/day. For analyses, they were classified as normal (within recommended range) or high (higher than recommended).

Definitions

A higher than recommended colchicine dose was defined as >0.5 mg/day for children <5 years, >0.75 mg/day for those <10 years or >1 mg/day at any age according to current age-based paediatric recommendations for the use of colchicine.41 This categorisation of colchicine doses reflects clinical practice and does not imply a statistical adjustment.

Inflammation at the last visit was defined as CRP ≥5 mg/L or SAA ≥9 mg/L. Treatment response was defined as the absence of inflammation and satisfactory clinical control, including an Autoinflammatory Disease Activity Index <9 and a symptom-free interval between visits.42

Colchicine resistance was defined as ≥1 attack per month or persistent subclinical inflammation despite the maximum tolerated dose. Remission was defined as at least six consecutive months without clinical or biological activity.

Clustering analysis

Variable selection was prespecified on clinical grounds, based on prior literature, expert consensus and relevance to disease phenotype and management in FMF, rather than being data-driven. Only clinically relevant categorical variables with <20% missing data were retained to ensure model stability. Patients with missing values on these key variables (n=36) were excluded. Multiple correspondence analysis (MCA) was first applied to summarise associations between categorical variables, and Ward’s hierarchical clustering was then performed on the first three factorial axes, selected based on inertia gain and interpretability. The optimal number of clusters (k=5) was determined by examining the dendrogram and the relative loss of inertia together with clinical interpretability of the resulting groups. No formal internal validation or resampling-based stability analysis was performed, given the exploratory nature of the study. The clustering results should therefore be considered exploratory even though the identified clusters were supported by clinical coherence across subgroups.

Cluster membership was subsequently used as a grouping variable for descriptive and comparative analyses of clinical, biological and therapeutic characteristics. Longitudinal disease trajectories were visualised using swimmer plots, capturing key milestones such as age at onset, initiation and escalation of colchicine, introduction of IL-1 inhibitors and last follow-up status. Swimmer plots provide a descriptive visual representation of individual trajectories without formal time-to-event modelling.

This unsupervised, data-driven approach aimed to capture the multidimensional heterogeneity of FMF and to identify clinically meaningful subgroups beyond conventional genotype-based classification.

Statistical analysis

Analyses were performed using SAS V.9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, North Carolina, USA). Categorical variables were expressed as numbers (percentages) and compared using the χ² or Fisher’s exact test, while continuous variables were expressed as median (IQR) and compared using the Wilcoxon or Kruskal-Wallis test, as appropriate. Given the skewed distributions and small sizes of some subgroups, only non-parametric tests were used. A two-sided p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. The study was not designed with an a priori sample-size calculation and all comparative analyses should be regarded as descriptive and hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory.

Due to the limited sample size of the cohort, multivariable models that would simultaneously adjust for genotype, age at onset, follow-up duration and calendar or treatment period were not performed as this would have required to further fragment the sample size, reducing thus the interpretability of results.

Results

Study population

Between 2004 and 2025, a total of 210 patients were diagnosed with FMF. Twenty-five of them were excluded because of missing key variables or insufficient follow-up, leaving 185 patients for the final analysis (figure 1). Of the 185 included patients, 98 (53%) were males (table 1). The median age at symptom onset was 2.5 years (IQR 1.2–4.5), with a median diagnostic delay of 24 months (IQR 8.9–58.2). Ethnicity data were available for 175 patients. Most patients were of Mediterranean origin (131, 74.9%), followed by Asian (16, 9.1%) and other Caucasian (16, 9.1%); mixed (10, 5.7%), sub-Saharan African (1, 0.6%) and unknown (1, 0.6%) origins were less frequent.

The median follow-up duration was 5.5 years (IQR 3–9). Triggering factors were identified in 73 of 162 patients (45%). Most commonly reported triggers were stress or strong emotions (47, 29%), physical exertion (23, 14%), travel (14, 9%) and sleep deprivation (14, 9%). Fever during attacks lasted a median of 2 days (IQR 2–3), and prodromes were reported in 68 of 139 patients (49%). The most frequent manifestations were gastrointestinal (157, 85%), musculoskeletal (139, 75%), pulmonary (89, 48%), cutaneous (74, 40%) and ENT involvement (47, 26%). No amyloidosis was observed during the follow-up period.

Colchicine treatment was started in 169 patients (91%) at a median age of 5.9 years (IQR 3.6–9.1), while 20 (10%) patients required IL-1 inhibitors, initiated at a median age of 7.7 years. The distribution by genotypes was as follows: 64 (35%) FMF-HOM, 28 (15%) FMF-CH and 93 (50%) FMF-SH. The most frequent mutations were M694V (142, 77%) and M694I (27, 15%).

Clinical differences according to genotype

We compared 93 FMF-SH with 92 genetically patients with cFMF (table 2, figure 2, online supplemental table 1). Overall, patients with FMF-SH had milder disease expression. They more frequently reported aphthous ulcers between attacks (18/93, 19% FMF-SH vs 6/64, 9.4% FMF-HOM vs 0/28, 0% FMF-CH, p=0.012) and less often identified triggering factors compared with patients with FMF-HOM (30/81, 37% FMF-SH vs 34/56, 61% FMF-HOM vs 9/25, 36% FMF-CH). Clinical manifestations during attacks such as abdominal pain (70/93, 75% FMF-SH vs 58/64, 91% FMF-HOM vs 24/28, 86% FMF-CH, p=0.044), chest pain (31/93, 33% FMF-SH vs 40/64, 62% FMF-HOM vs 15/28, 54% FMF-CH, p=0.001), myalgia compared with FMF-HOM (34/93, 37% FMF-SH vs 42/64, 66% FMF-HOM vs 10/28, 36% FMF-CH, p<0.001) and erysipelas-like erythema (8/93, 9% FMF-SH vs 17/64, 27% FMF-HOM vs 6/28, 21% FMF-CH, p=0.007) were less common than in cFMF. Colchicine treatment duration was shorter (median in months, 48 FMF-SH vs 96 FMF-HOM vs 108 FMF-CH, p<0.001), higher-dose regimens were less frequent (55% FMF-SH vs 89% FMF-HOM vs 75% FMF-CH, p<0.001) and physician-guided colchicine discontinuation during follow-up was more common (10% FMF-SH vs 2% FMF-HOM vs 0% FMF-CH %).

Clinical heterogeneity across the cohort

Beyond genotype-based differences, substantial heterogeneity was observed across the cohort (figure 3).

Female patients more frequently reported chest pain during attacks (61% vs 34%, p≤0.001) and musculoskeletal pain between attacks (31% vs 17%, p=0.03), whereas males more often presented with fever (98% vs 91%, p=0.048) (online supplemental table 2).

Early-onset patients (<1 year) required higher colchicine doses (p<0.001) and had longer delays before treatment initiation (median 43 vs 24 vs 12 months, p=0.001), while late-onset patients (≥4 years) more often experienced attacks without fever (p<0.001) (online supplemental table 3).

Patients with higher CRP levels during attacks more frequently reported triggering factors (53% vs 23%, p=0.010), whereas those with lower CRP values had more aphthosis (33% vs 10%, p=0.005) (online supplemental table 4). Patients with frequent attacks (>2/month) had earlier onset (p<0.001) and more often required IL-1 inhibitors (p=0.006) (online supplemental table 5).

Patients requiring both colchicine and IL-1 inhibitors had earlier onset and more severe clinical manifestations (online supplemental table 6). Similarly, those receiving higher colchicine doses had an earlier disease onset (2.2 vs 3.2 years, p=0.021), more severe symptoms and were more frequently FMF-HOM (47% vs 14%, p<0.001) (online supplemental table 7).

Finally, persistent inflammation and treatment response at last visit were associated with genotype and treatment exposure, with patients with FMF-HOM more frequently exhibiting persistent inflammation (60% vs 30%, p=0.005) (online supplemental tables 8,9).

Although known genotype-phenotype associations were reflected in the clustering structure, genotype alone did not fully determine cluster membership. In particular, symptomatic FMF-SH were distributed across several clusters with markedly different disease severity and treatment requirements. In addition, early age at onset and sex emerged as important contributors to disease evolution.

Discussion

In this monocentric paediatric cohort, we characterised the clinical heterogeneity of FMF using unsupervised clustering combined with longitudinal trajectory visualisation. To our knowledge, this is the first study to apply such a methodology to paediatric FMF.

As expected, clustering independently reproduced well-established genotype-phenotype associations, particularly the severity associated with M694V homozygosity and the generally milder disease course observed at the population level among FMF-SH. Importantly, the fact that these known associations emerged from an unsupervised analysis supports the robustness and clinical relevance of the identified clusters.

In addition, our study provides new insights into FMF heterogeneity. Importantly, our findings confirm that genotype alone is insufficient to explain disease heterogeneity, as substantial variability was observed within each genetic subgroup. In particular, patients with FMF-SH did not form a single uniformly mild group, but were distributed across several clusters with distinct disease severity, inflammatory burden and treatment requirements. In addition, early age at onset and sex emerged as important contributors to disease evolution, refining risk stratification beyond genotype alone.

Previous studies have demonstrated the impact of the M694V variant, especially in FMF-HOM, who typically exhibit earlier onset, higher inflammatory burden and greater colchicine requirements, with some progressing to biologic therapy or amyloidosis.23 Our results align with these findings: patients with FMF-HOM carrying M694V were over-represented in the most severe clusters, characterised by early onset, frequent attacks, higher colchicine doses and frequent use of IL-1 inhibitors. These findings confirm the central role of this genotype in determining disease severity, while also illustrating that disease trajectories remain heterogeneous even within this group.

Conversely, patients with FMF-SH showed, at the cohort level, a generally milder disease course, with fewer attacks, lower inflammatory markers and lower colchicine requirements. However, clustering revealed substantial heterogeneity within this group, ranging from very mild or minimally symptomatic phenotypes requiring no long-term treatment to more severe disease courses requiring prolonged colchicine exposure or escalation to IL-1 inhibition. These findings challenge the traditional binary view of FMF severity based solely on genetic status and support a continuum model of disease expression. Historically, FMF-SH were considered to display only mild or incomplete forms of FMF, with limited inflammatory burden.40,42 More recent data, however, suggest that some FMF-SH may present phenotypes indistinguishable from those of biallelic patients, including early onset, frequent attacks and risk of AA amyloidosis.43 44 Our results further support this evolving view and indicate that FMF-SH should not be considered a benign carrier state, but rather part of a broad FMF spectrum. 45 46 47

The identified clusters illustrate a continuum of disease expression, ranging from very mild phenotypes to severe early-onset disease requiring intensive therapy. The mildest cluster comprised exclusively patients with FMF-SH with few clinical manifestations and no treatment requirement. Of importance while some presented minor symptoms such as lymphadenopathy, none of them fulfilled the PFAPA criteria. At the opposite end of the spectrum, the most severe cluster consisted predominantly of female patients with FMF-HOM with very early onset, higher colchicine requirements and frequent use of IL-1 inhibitors. Intermediate clusters included patients with moderate phenotypes, some well controlled on colchicine alone and others requiring biologic therapy at a later stage. This gradation of disease expression highlights the importance of multidimensional approaches that integrate genotype, age at onset, sex, inflammatory profile and treatment response, rather than relying solely on static genetic categorisation.

An exploratory observation was the predominance of female patients in the most severe clusters. This observation should, however, be interpreted cautiously, as the present clustering analysis was not designed to formally assess independent predictors of disease severity and no multivariable adjustment was performed. Although this study was not designed to investigate sex-related mechanisms, this finding is consistent with reports of sex differences in autoinflammatory diseases in adults.48 Several hypotheses have been proposed, including hormonal modulation of inflammasome activity, sex-related differences in immune responses or a higher prevalence of iron deficiency among female patients with FMF.49 50 However, these mechanisms remain speculative, particularly in paediatric populations. Notably, some female patients in our cohort presented severe disease requiring therapeutic escalation before puberty, suggesting that factors beyond pubertal hormonal influences may contribute to this association. This observation warrants further investigation and may support closer monitoring of girls with early-onset FMF.

These findings have several clinical implications. Early identification of patients at risk of severe disease could help optimise follow-up strategies and therapeutic decision-making. In particular, female patients with early-onset homozygous M694V mutations may benefit from closer monitoring, rapid colchicine dose escalation and earlier consideration of IL-1 inhibition. Conversely, recognising mild phenotypes, especially among patients with FMF-SH, may help avoid overtreatment and unnecessary long-term drug exposure. Integrating cluster-derived profiles into clinical evaluation could therefore support a more personalised approach to FMF management.

From a methodological perspective, this study highlights the value of combining unsupervised clustering with longitudinal trajectory visualisation to capture disease dynamics. Given the exploratory nature of the study, no formal validation strategy (eg, external validation or stability analysis) was performed. The clinical coherence of the identified clusters and their consistency with known genotype-phenotype associations provide supportive, but not definitive, evidence for the relevance of the clustering structure. The clusters identified in this study should therefore be interpreted as exploratory, hypothesis-generating patterns of disease expression rather than definitive or generalisable disease subtypes. In addition, treatment intensity partly reflects clinical management decisions and evolving therapeutic strategies and may therefore capture aspects of disease severity as well as physician-driven treatment patterns. Conventional genotype-based analyses may overlook subgroups of patients who share similar clinical courses despite different genetic backgrounds. By integrating MCA with trajectory-based visualisation, we identified distinct disease patterns that might remain obscured using conventional approaches. Such strategies may be applicable to other autoinflammatory diseases characterised by incomplete genotype-phenotype correlations.

Despite these important findings, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the retrospective single-centre design may have introduced selection bias and incomplete data. Second, as a national reference centre for autoinflammatory diseases, our cohort may include a higher proportion of severe or complex cases, which may limit generalisability. Third, although this cohort is among the largest paediatric FMF series with detailed follow-up, the sample size remains modest for complex multivariate clustering which entails several statistical limitations: (i) no a priori power calculation was performed, and the study was not designed for confirmatory hypothesis testing. The clustering and subgroup comparisons should therefore be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating; (ii) no formal internal validation or stability analysis of the clustering was performed, and these exploratory clusters therefore require external validation in larger multicentre cohorts before being considered robust; (iii) visualisations of trajectories through swimmer plots are descriptive and do not replace formal longitudinal or time-to-event analyses, which would require larger, prospectively followed cohorts with standardised visit schedules. Overall, these methodological constraints are inherent to exploratory, data-driven approaches and reinforce that the present findings should be considered hypothesis-generating; (iv) observed patterns resulting from these analyses should be interpreted cautiously as they were not powered for multivariable analyses. As an example, in this cohort, severe phenotypes were more frequently observed among female patients with early-onset M694V homozygosity. However, this association may be confounded by genotype distribution and other factors. Confirmation in larger, prospectively analysed cohorts will be needed to determine whether a sex-related effect on disease expression can be reliably established. Fourth, no cases of amyloidosis were observed during follow-up, precluding analysis of long-term complications across clusters. Fifth, the study period (2004–2025) spans >20 years, during which diagnostic approaches, treatment thresholds and dosing practices as well as access of IL-1 inhibitors have evolved. As analyses were not formally stratified or adjusted for calendar period, era-related changes in management are a potential source of confounding influencing both cluster composition and observed outcomes and may limit the generalisability of the clusters.

Finally, the interpretation of FMF-SH remains challenging. Although strict inclusion criteria were applied, including the use of Eurofever/PRINTO classification criteria and careful exclusion of alternative diagnoses such as PFAPA based on clinical features (notably short-lasting febrile episodes), genetic testing was limited to routine sequencing of MEFV coding regions and did not systematically assess intronic or structural variants. Therefore, the possibility of undetected biallelic variants or alternative inflammatory diagnoses cannot be completely excluded.

Despite these limitations, our study has several strengths, including a well-characterised paediatric cohort with prolonged follow-up, systematic clinical documentation and the use of established unsupervised statistical methods. The identification of clinically coherent clusters supports the internal validity of our findings and provides a framework for future studies exploring disease stratification in FMF.

Several questions regarding long-term outcomes remain open. The transition from paediatric to adult care represents a critical period, and it remains unclear whether patients with severe paediatric trajectories will maintain high disease activity, develop additional autoinflammatory or autoimmune conditions or achieve sustained remission after puberty. Similarly, the long-term impact of persistent low-grade inflammation, particularly on fertility, metabolic health and quality of life in female patients, deserves further investigation. Prospective multicentre cohorts following paediatric patients with FMF into adulthood will be essential to address these questions and to determine whether early cluster assignment predicts adult disease trajectories.

In summary, our findings highlight the substantial clinical heterogeneity of paediatric FMF beyond genotype alone and support the use of multidimensional approaches to better characterise disease trajectories and guide personalised management.

Conclusion

In this monocentric paediatric cohort, we show that FMF presents substantial clinical heterogeneity beyond the classical genotype-phenotype paradigm. Using an exploratory clustering approach, we identified five descriptive patient subgroups associated with different patterns of disease severity, treatment requirements and inflammatory profiles over time.

Patients with homozygous M694V FMF, particularly females with early-onset disease, appeared more frequently in the most severe clusters and required higher colchicine doses and more frequent use of IL-1 inhibitors. Importantly, symptomatic heterozygous patients did not constitute a single, uniformly mild group, but displayed variable disease expression.

These findings highlight the clinical heterogeneity observed among patients fulfilling FMF classification criteria beyond genotype alone. Early identification of high-risk profiles may enable closer monitoring and timely therapeutic escalation, whereas recognition of mild phenotypes could help avoid overtreatment and unnecessary drug exposure.

These findings should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating, and require confirmation in larger prospective multicentre studies before any clinical implementation.
