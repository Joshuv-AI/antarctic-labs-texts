Screening Tool Improves Recognition of Movement Disorders by Internists and Paediatricians in Patients With Inherited Metabolic Diseases

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMovement disorders are common in inherited metabolic diseases (IMDs) and significantly impact quality of life. Unfortunately, they are often underrecognised by metabolic physicians. This study investigated whether a new screening tool improves recognition of movement disorders in IMD patients by non‐neurologists. Internists and paediatricians specialized in IMDs assessed videos of 44 IMD patients who perform four specific neurological tests, to evaluate the presence and phenotype of a movement disorder. Initially, baseline assessments were performed without the tool. Eight months later, the same videos were assessed using an instructional video demonstrating the four neurological tests, and a screening tool including these tests with 14 yes/no questions regarding observed abnormalities. One hundred and forty‐nine assessments were conducted by 15 metabolic internists and paediatricians. At baseline, 70% (105/149) of assessments correctly determined the presence of a movement disorder. Sensitivity of the tool was 68%. In the second measurement, when the outcome of the tool was considered decisive (with at least one positive answer indicating a movement disorder), 79% (117/149) of assessments were correct. Sensitivity of the tool increased to 87% (Chi‐square = 15.75, p < 0.001). All severe and moderately severe movement disorders were recognized in the second measurement. The tool and instructional video did not enhance recognition of specific movement disorder phenotypes. However, the tool did improve the accuracy of treatment indications (chi‐square = 4.000, p < 0.05). The correct use of the screening tool significantly increased the sensitivity and thereby the recognition of movement disorders in IMD patients, though referral to a neurologist remains necessary for phenotype identification.

Background

Inherited metabolic diseases (IMDs) frequently affect the central nervous system (CNS), resulting in neurological symptoms [1, 2, 3]. Depending on the specific metabolic defect, various parts of the CNS may be affected [2, 4, 5]. IMDs can disrupt the physiology and connectivity of brain regions involved in movement, of which neurons in the basal ganglia are particularly vulnerable [2, 6, 7, 8]. The basal ganglia are involved in the execution and control of movement, and damage to these structures is associated with a variety of movement disorders [9].

Movement disorders, characterised by involuntary or disturbed movements, can be classified into hypokinetic and hyperkinetic movement disorders, and ataxia. Parkinsonism is a hypokinetic movement disorder and is characterised by a reduction and slowing of movements [10, 11]. Hyperkinetic disorders manifest as an excess of involuntary movements and can be subdivided into dystonia, myoclonus, chorea, tremor, and tics [1, 10]. In ataxia, there are no involuntary movements at rest, but the coordination of movements is disturbed. Dystonia and ataxia are the most common movement disorders in patients with an IMD [1, 6]. The recognition and accurate classification of movement disorders in patients with IMDs are crucial, as such disorders significantly impact quality of life, and some can be treated symptomatically [1, 12, 13]. However, recognizing movement disorders in IMD patients can be challenging, due to the combination of more than one movement disorder and other neurological symptoms, resulting in a complex clinical picture [2, 8, 13]. Moreover, patients with an IMD are often treated by multiple specialists, especially internists and paediatricians, whom are not trained to recognise movement disorders, contributing to the underdiagnosis of these disorders [8]. This highlights the need for improved recognition and appropriate management of movement disorders in patients with an IMD, potentially enhancing their quality of life.

Koens et al. [8] developed a screening tool to assist non‐neurologists in recognizing and assessing movement disorders in IMD patients aged 4 years and older (Figure 1). First, demographic and clinical information from the included IMD patients were collected. Then, the patients were assessed by three international movement disorder experts. A tool was designed based on the parts of the neurological examination that most contributed to the decision on whether there was a movement disorder. The tool was designed to be short, easy to use, and sensitive to all types of movement disorders. It includes four neurological tests (arm posture, Archimedes spiral, top‐nose test and walking), and 14 yes/no questions. A positive answer (yes) indicates an abnormal outcome in the neurological examination. In Koens et al. [8], it was suggested that the presence of a movement disorder is indicated when at least one positive answer (yes) is given on the tool. However, the tool has not yet been evaluated for use by non‐neurologists.

Therefore, this study aims to investigate whether the use of this screening tool, accompanied by an instructional video which demonstrates the clinical manifestations of the various movement disorders, can improve the recognition of movement disorders by internists and paediatricians who are specialized in IMDs.

Patients and Methods

Patients

IMD patients (from the prior study [8]), with or without movement disorders, for whom all aspects of the screening tool were recorded on video, were included. Patients younger than 4 years were excluded, as neurological evaluation differs in this age group. Evaluations by the movement disorder experts regarding the presence, type, severity of movement disorders, and treatment indications from the prior study, will serve as the gold standard in this study, based on a majority rule (2/3 or 3/3 agreement). The reliability of the experts' ratings was assessed using the Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC), with an ICC1k value of 0.86 (95% CI: [0.76, 0.92]), indicating high agreement and reliability.

The included patients were grouped based on the dominant phenotype of their movement disorder. The patients and/or caregivers provided consent for the use of their videos in this study. This study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the University Medical Centre Groningen (METc 2017/574).

Participants

Participants included metabolic internists and paediatricians from various academic centres in the Netherlands, selected based on their expertise in IMDs. Each participant was assigned nine or ten patients through stratified randomisation, ensuring a variety of movement disorder subtypes for assessment. No additional information about the patients' medical history was provided. Demographic and clinical experience data of the participants were collected through a questionnaire.

Development of the Instructional Video

An instructional video was developed for this study, demonstrating the four neurological tests from the screening tool, through computer animation. For tics, tremor, myoclonus, chorea, dystonia, parkinsonism and ataxia the neurological tests are illustrated, highlighting specific aspects to observe. The video was developed by Creabiz Studio (Supporting Information S1).

Video Assessments

The study was divided into two phases. In the baseline measurement, participants individually assessed videos of nine or ten patients, evaluating the presence and severity of movement disorders, as well as identifying specific movement disorder phenotypes. Additionally, participants were asked to determine whether they would initiate treatment for the movement disorder or refer the patient to a neurologist.

Eight months later, in the second measurement, participants were asked to reassess the same patients by using the screening tool and the instructional video. The components of the screening tool were incorporated into the assessment form in this second phase (Supporting Information S2). Participants were not informed about the cutoff value of the screening tool, as this threshold had not yet been evaluated. They completed the tool by answering its questions and subsequently evaluated the presence of a movement disorder (final assessment). Participants were free to interpret the tool's results and the final assessment was made independently of the tool's outcome.

After completing both measurements, internists and paediatricians filled out a feedback questionnaire to evaluate the screening tool (Supporting Information S3).

Statistical Analysis

To determine the accuracy of the correct identification of a movement disorder (final assessment) from the measurements, the final assessments were compared with the gold standard. Additionally, in the second measurement, the final assessments were compared with the answers on the screening tool (yes/no). Discrepant assessments were identified: (1) the final assessment indicates no movement disorders, but there was at least one positive answer (yes) on the screening tool or (2) the final assessment indicates a movement disorder, but all answers on the screening tool were negative (no). Chi‐square test for unpaired, binomial data was used to analyse the difference in accuracy between the discrepant and non‐discrepant assessments. Next, a correction was made by adjusting the discrepant assessments to match the tool's outcome. If the tool has one or more positive (yes) answers, the final assessment will indicate a movement disorder. If all answers are negative (no), the final assessment will indicate no movement disorder.

McNemar's test for paired binomial data was used to analyse the difference in accuracy of the final assessments between the baseline and second measurement, as well as the difference in sensitivity and specificity. Supporting Information S4 illustrates the steps of the methods used to analyse the difference in accuracy. In addition, positive and negative predictive values (PPV and NPV) were calculated to evaluate the diagnostic accuracy of the tool.

A mixed logistic regression model was used to analyse the influence of movement disorder severity, as well as the specialization and years of work experience of internists and paediatricians, on the accuracy of the final assessments at baseline and at the second measurement. Movement disorder severity was determined in the prior study [8], categorising groups as mild, moderate, and severe. For work experience, participants were divided into two groups: those with less than 5 years of work experience and those with more than 5 years of work experience.

The accuracy of the internists' and paediatricians' diagnoses of the type of movement disorder was compared with the gold standard, focusing on true positives, false negatives, and false positives. McNemar's test was used to analyse the differences in these outcomes between the baseline and second measurement. In addition, McNemar's test was used to analyse the difference in correctly determining the treatment indication between the measurements.

Descriptive statistics were applied to assess the referral decisions, most informative component of the tool, additional information needed for assessments, and the outcomes of the feedback questionnaire.

R (version 4.4.1) was used for all analyses. A p value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Presence of a Movement Disorder

Fifteen IMD specialists evaluated nine or ten patients each per measurement, resulting in a total of 149 assessments per measurement.

At baseline, the average correctly assessed patients for the presence of a movement disorder per IMD specialist was 7.1 (±1.2). Overall, there were 105 (70%) correct final assessments regarding the presence or absence of a movement disorder.

During the second measurement, raters answered at least one question positive (yes) on the screening tool but denied the presence of a movement disorder in the final question in 26 assessments. In two assessments, it was the other way around, with exclusively negative (no) answers at the tool, yet a movement disorder was diagnosed in the final question. Of these 28 discrepant assessments, only 32% (9/28) of the final assessments were correct. However, when we only look at the cases where the raters followed the outcome of the screening tool (n = 121), 81% of the assessments were correct. The Chi‐square test showed a significant difference between the discrepant and non‐discrepant assessments (Chi‐square = 24.445, p < 0.001).

Therefore, the 28 discrepant assessments were corrected based on the tool's outcome, with a least one positive answer (yes) indicating a movement disorder. After this correction, the average correctly assessed patients for the presence of a movement disorder per IMD specialist was 7.9 (±1.4). Overall, there were 117 (79%) correct final assessments, showing a trend towards significance compared to the baseline (Chi‐square = 3.184, p = 0.074) (Table 4).

The Influence of Severity, Specialization, and Years of Work Experience

A significant influence of severity on the accuracy of the final assessments was found in both measurements. For both the baseline and second measurement, a statistically significant linear relationship was observed (baseline: z = 3.302, p < 0.001; second: z = 2.787, p = 0.005), indicating that as severity of the movement disorder increases, the likelihood of a correct assessment increases significantly. Additionally, a statistically significant quadratic relationship was observed for the baseline measurement (baseline: z = 2.853, p = 0.004), indicating that the accuracy of the final assessment not only increases with higher severity, but also in the absence of a movement disorder. However, the quadratic relationship for the second measurement is not significant (second z = 1.539, p = 0.124) (Supporting Information S5).

A significant difference was also found between the measurements, with moderate and mild movement disorders being recognized more often during the second measurement (Chi‐square = 18.831, p < 0.001) (Table 4).

The two work experience groups, consisting of participants with less than 5 years and those with more than 5 years, were compared. No significant difference was found in the likelihood of a correct assessment between these groups in either measurement (baseline: z = −1.571, p = 0.116; second: z = −1.183, p = 0.237) (Supporting Information S5).

No significant difference was found in the likelihood of a correct assessment between internists and paediatricians in both measurements (baseline: z = −0.835, p = 0.405; second: z = −0.883, p = 0.377) (Supporting Information S5).

No significant differences were found in the influence of specialization or years of work experience on the likelihood of correct assessments between the two measurements, indicating no significant differences in the influence of these variables with or without the use of the screening tool (specialization: z = 0.088, p = 0.930; work experience: z = 0.174, p = 0.862) (Supporting Information S5).

Treatment Indication and Referral

Of the 44 assessed patients for whom experts in the prior study [8] established a treatment indication for the movement disorder, 25 (57%) were also given a treatment indication by the internists and paediatricians at baseline, and 32 (73%) in the second measurement. This improvement in providing a treatment indication is significant (Chi‐square = 4.000, p < 0.05). A treatment indication was incorrectly given 28 times in the baseline measurement, compared to 33 times in the second measurement.

Of the patients diagnosed with a movement disorder by internists and paediatricians, they referred 93% (87/94) to a neurologist. There are 6 (14%) assessed patients, for whom experts indicated a treatment indication, who were not referred by the IMD specialists, both at baseline and in the second measurement.

Most Informative Component of the Tool

The ‘top‐nose test’ was most frequently (48%) mentioned by the IMD specialists as most informative for assessing the presence of a movement disorder, followed by the ‘walking’ component (32%), then ‘drawing a spiral’ (12%), and finally ‘arm posture’ (8%). In two assessments, the question about the most informative component was not answered.

Additional Information Needed for Assessments

In the baseline measurement 52 responses were given in which the internists and paediatricians indicated additional information they considered necessary during the assessment. These responses were grouped with the following outcomes: additional neurological examination (15), severity of symptoms (11), medical history (10), specific part of the examination (6), underlying condition (6), general physical examination (2), dominant hand (1), and current treatment (1).

In the second measurement 31 responses were collected and grouped similarly, with the following outcomes: medical history (10), severity of symptoms (5), current treatment (3), extended observation (3), underlying condition (3), specific part of the examination (3), example examiner (2), dominant hand (1), and general physical examination (1).

Feedback Questionnaire

Nine responses were received for the feedback questionnaire. All internists and paediatricians (100%) found the screening tool useful for assessing patients. All (100%) felt the instructional video improved their ability to recognise different movement disorder phenotypes. They all (100%) indicated they would use the screening tool for future neurological examinations of IMD patients. Additionally, all IMD specialists (100%) felt more confident in their ability to recognise movement disorders in the future. Discussion

This is the first study aimed at improving the recognition of movement disorders in IMD patients by IMD specialists who are non‐neurologists. We showed that correct use of the screening tool, with at least one positive answer (yes) indicating a movement disorder, significantly improved the recognition of movement disorders by internists and paediatricians with a sensitivity of 87%. In addition, the use of the tool led to an improvement in providing treatment indications. Our findings are important because movement disorders are common in IMD patients but are often overlooked, despite their significant impact on quality of life [1, 13, 14, 15]. Since movement disorders may be treatable, adequate recognition is important [12, 14]. Unfortunately, neurologists specialized in IMDs are not very prevalent, and therefore this screening tool was developed to support internists and paediatricians specialized in IMDs by the recognition and classification of movement disorders, and thereby to identify patients who should be referred to a neurologist.

As mentioned above, the sensitivity of the screening tool in detecting the presence of a movement disorder was 87%, which is a clear improvement compared to the baseline measurement without the use of the tool (sensitivity of 68%). On the other hand, specificity significantly decreased from 79% to 48%. This indicates that while the tool leads to better recognition of patients with a movement disorder, it also results in more false positives, so incorrectly identifying patients as having a movement disorder. Ideally, the tool would have improved both sensitivity and specificity. Nevertheless, we consider it more important to identify patients with a movement disorder, as missing a treatable movement disorder can significantly impact quality of life [1, 13, 14, 15]. Therefore, we accept the lower specificity, acknowledging that in practice, this may lead to more unnecessary referrals to a neurologist.

Interestingly, the recognition of a movement disorder was already relatively high during the baseline measurement in which internists and paediatricians recognized 68% of movement disorders without the use of the screening tool. This is higher than expected based on our own practice and the literature [8, 13, 14, 15]. One possible explanation is that the participating internists and paediatricians are experts in IMDs, which increases their exposure to neurological disorders. In addition, they were consciously looking for movement disorders during this study, leading to increased awareness. Furthermore, the screening tool was indirectly used during the baseline measurement by only showing fragments of the four neurological examination aspects included in the tool. The high percentage at baseline is a positive outcome but also raises the question of whether the baseline scores realistically reflect clinical practice, where internists and paediatricians often have limited time and may not directly see an indication to perform a neurological examination. Ideally, patients should have been physically examined in the consultation room without further instructions, which may have resulted in different scores from the current baseline measurement. However, this approach is challenging to implement, as it is difficult to ask doctors to score movement disorders without informing them of this task.

Another disadvantage of assessing videos instead of patients in the consultation room is that assessors depend on the available footage and the camera's perspective. Seeing the patient in the consultation room could lead to a different assessment. Moreover, the IMD specialists did not receive additional patient information during this study. Knowledge about the patient's complaints and the IMD present, can of course further facilitate the recognition of a movement disorder. This information will also provide insight into current treatments and the severity of the symptoms, allowing them to better assess whether starting treatment or referral is necessary. The need for this information is also evident in the responses from the IMD specialists to the question about additional information needed for assessments.

Among patients for whom internists and paediatricians exclusively provide negative (no) responses on the tool, the NPV was 52%. This indicates that 52% of these patients truly did not have a movement disorder. However, this also implies that 48% were incorrectly classified as negative, despite having a movement disorder, albeit one of mild severity. This indicates that despite the use of the screening tool, some mild movement disorders are missed. One possible reason is that the neurological examination in the tool is not comprehensive enough, making the movement disorder unrecognizable based on the available videos. During the screening tool's development, the experts had access to more video fragments when determining the movement disorder. However, a short, easy‐to‐use instrument was chosen, which did not include tapping and eye movements, although experts considered these useful for the assessments.

Another explanation might be that mild movement disorders are more easily overlooked by the untrained eye. This was also found in analysing the influence of severity of the movement disorder on the accuracy of the final assessments, with mild movement disorders being the least often recognized by the IMD specialists. This finding is consistent with previous research, where the least agreement between movement disorder experts was found for mild movement disorders [8]. In contrast, all severe and moderately severe movement disorders were recognized by the IMD specialists in the second measurement. This is important as the more severe the movement disorder, the bigger the impact on quality of life. Treatment in these cases is therefore more often needed than in mild movement disorders.

This study found that correct treatment indications were given significantly more frequent in the second measurement, compared to the baseline measurement. This outcome could partly be explained by the increase in the recognition of patients with a movement disorder, or simply more awareness of treatment options because of the participation in the study. However, 14% (6/44) of the assessed patients with a treatment indication were not referred, and as a result, they would not be seen by a neurologist and would not receive the appropriate treatment.

When looking at the movement disorder phenotype, the screening tool with instructional video did not lead to an improvement in diagnoses. One possible explanation is that the expressions of the phenotypes in the patients were more subtle than those shown in the instructional video, or that the video was not informative enough for the IMD specialists. Moreover, 41% of the patients in this study have more than one movement disorder, which can lead to a more challenging clinical picture to assess [2, 8, 16]. In addition, patients with IMDs may show other neurological symptoms (e.g., weakness due to myopathy), further complicating the recognition of the specific type of movement disorder [2, 13, 16]. Therefore, referral to a neurologist remains necessary.

When examining the responses where the internists and paediatricians indicated additional information needed during the assessment, one outcome is particularly noteworthy. In the baseline measurement, IMD specialists requested additional neurological examinations 15 times, compared to 0 times in the second measurement. This indicates that when IMD specialists know how to score the neurological examinations, they are less likely to ask for additional tests. The conclusion that can be drawn is that structured tests with a scoring system reduces the need for additional tests to form a conclusion, thereby potentially saving time in assessing patients.

Finally, based on the feedback questionnaire all internists and paediatricians thought the tool is useful for assessing patients and would use (part of) the screening tool in the future. They also believe that the tool enhances their ability to recognise movement disorders and different phenotypes. This positive reception suggests that the screening tool can be effectively implemented in the consultation room, supporting non‐neurologists in assessing IMD patients. A recommended adjustment, as mentioned in the feedback questionnaire, is to make the screening tool digitally fillable. In addition, a brief instruction should be included on how to use the tool and interpret its results, stating that one or more positive answers (yes) suggest the presence of a movement disorder, and that in these cases, referral to a neurologist is recommended.

The screening tool, and particularly the instructional video, is also more broadly applicable for teaching about movement disorders. In regions where access to movement disorder specialists is limited, it could support clinical learning and assist in early diagnostic decision‐making, although in‐person training remains recommended whenever possible.

A limitation of this study is the small number of participants. Only fifteen internists and paediatricians completed both measurements. However, each participant assessed nine or ten patients, resulting in a total of 149 assessments. Therefore, despite the small group of participants, the number of assessments is sufficient to draw meaningful conclusions.

Another limitation is the lack of a diagnostic gold standard for many movement disorders. Therefore, the diagnosis of movement disorders and phenotypes in this study was established based on a consensus of at least two of the three international movement disorder experts. For myoclonus and tremor, electrophysiological recording can provide a solution, but this is more challenging to assess in combined phenotypes and not always easily available for internists and pediatricans [17]. Although in our expertise centre research is being done on the use of computer‐assisted classification tools, these are not yet available in clinical practice [18].

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that the use of the screening tool significantly improves the recognition of movement disorders in IMD patients by internists and paediatricians specialized in IMDs. Sensitivity increases from 68% to 87% when the outcome of the screening tool is decisive, with at least one positive answer indicating a movement disorder. In a feedback questionnaire all participants thought the tool is useful for assessing patients and they would use (part of) the screening tool in the future. Although the tool improves the recognition of movement disorders, its use did not contribute to the ability to distinguish different phenotypes. This emphasizes the importance of referral to a neurologist for further phenotyping and to start appropriate treatment that could improve the quality of life for these patients. Our screening tool could be a good guide to identify patients who should be referred.
