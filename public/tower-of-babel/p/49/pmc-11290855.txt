Verification of the usefulness of shape acquisition using plastic casts during fabrication of a metal strut ankle-foot orthosis
Authors: Shinnosuke Sato, Koutaro Ishikawa, Kousuke Yajima, Haruka Nagumo, Minoru Murayama
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Abstract [Purpose] When fabricating metal strut ankle-foot orthoses, previous studies have reported that shape acquisition is more efficient with a plastic cast than with a plaster of Paris bandage; however, this has not been quantitatively evaluated. This study aimed to clarify the usefulness of plastic casts by quantitatively evaluating the time spent and body shape accuracy among different methods of shape acquisition. [Participants and Methods] We compared the time spent and acquired shapes among the plaster of Paris bandage, plastic cast, and trace methods in 30 limbs of 15 healthy participants. Three-dimensional scans of the lower legs of participants were used as references to compare the three methods. [Results] The plastic cast method required less time than the plaster of Paris bandage method and provided a more accurate shape than the trace method did. However, the plastic cast method resulted in an increased overall width and circumferential diameter owing to the thickness of the stockinette and plastic cast. [Conclusion] The plastic cast method may be useful in reducing the time and burden of fabricating metal strut ankle-foot orthoses. The use of slightly narrow dimensions during the metal strut bending process can account for the increased width and diameter.Keywords: Metal strut ankle-foot orthosis, Plastic cast, Shape acquisition

INTRODUCTION

There are two methods of orthosis fabrication. One is to use a positive model obtained with a plaster of Paris bandage, and the other is based on tracing and measurements1). Metal strut ankle-foot orthoses (AFOs) can be fabricated by taking measurements and tracing. However, Takashima recommend the use of a positive model obtained with a plaster of Paris bandage in patients with significant ankle deformities, such as varus/valgus deformity, requiring three-dimensional support and correction2).

The plaster of Paris bandage has the advantages of being very well fitted to the shape of the patient’s body, highly moldable, and inexpensive, but it has also the disadvantages of soiling the patient’s and fabricator’s body/clothes and the floor during casting, and imposing a significant cleanup burden after the casting process3). These drawbacks led us to focus on a plastic cast as an alternative to the plaster of Paris bandage.

Plastic casts are mainly used for immobilization of fractures, protection of wounds, and correction. They have the advantages of being strong, tear-resistant even when wet, and lightweight, while having the disadvantages of being difficult to fit into shape and having sharp edges unlike the plaster of Paris bandage4). Watanabe noted that plastic casts can be used as improvised orthoses and can produce the desired effect in a short period of time during the patient’s training in the hospital setting5). A study by Mikami et al. reported a shape acquisition method using a plastic cast, which compensated for the aforementioned disadvantages of the plaster of Paris bandage and improved the work efficiency3). A study by Kuramoto and Hukube comparing the use of the plaster of Paris bandage and a plastic cast in casting for trunk orthoses showed that, consistent with the report by Mikami et al., the plastic cast compensated for the disadvantages of the plaster of Paris bandage and reduced the burden on the patient and the fabricator, and they showed its features including being safer during incision and lightweight, but more expensive than casting with the plaster of Paris bandage6). However, neither of these two studies included quantitative evaluations, such as comparison of the actual work time and reproducibility of the acquired model with respect to the shape of the patient’s body3, 6).

The objective of this study was to clarify the usefulness of plastic casts by quantitatively evaluating the work time and reproducibility of the shape of a patient’s body with different methods of shape acquisition.

PARTICIPANTS AND METHODS

The participants were 15 undergraduate students (10 males and 5 females, age 21 ± 0.3 years, height 166 ± 8.9 cm, weight 61.2 ± 13.7 kg) at Niigata University of Health and Welfare, and the shapes of both legs from the knee down were acquired from a total of 30 limbs of these participants.

The method of obtaining the shape using the plaster bandage was the same procedure2) as that for obtaining the shape in the production of the shoe horn brace (Fig. 1, right). The plaster of Paris bandage used was Plasrun Gyps (Alcare Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan; 2 strips, 2 rolls per limb). Three strips of Plasrun Gyps were applied to the incision as a splint. The positive model was molded using Grade A calcined gypsum (Yoshino Gypsum Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan).

Condition just before the metal strut bending process.

The method of obtaining the shape by tracing followed the usual procedure2) (Fig. 1, left).

The method of shape acquisition using a plastic cast was as follows. A 10-cm-wide stockinette was applied to the participant’s lower limb, and the stockinette was covered by plastic wrap to avoid wounding of the skin with plaster shears and sticking of the plastic cast (Fig. 2a). Then plastic casting was applied so that the entire area was covered with one or two layers of the plastic cast, with a manipulation was applied to reproduce the shape of the bony prominence (Fig. 2b). The ankle alignment was set, and after complete curing of the cast, a mark was placed on the lower end of the fibular head. The plastic cast was incised together with the wrap while pulling the stockinette from the upper edge slightly lateral to the anterior aspect of the lower leg. After the incision reached the dorsal curve of the ankle, another incision was made gradually from the tip of the toe and was connected to the incision made in the first half of the procedure (Fig. 2c). During the incision, the handle of the plaster shears was held down near the screw to prevent skin tension and pressure due to contact with the tip and handle of the shears at the ankle (Fig. 2c, right). The incised part of the model removed from the lower limd was then sutured with a tacker, and the model was used to perform the measurements described in the next section (Fig. 1, center). The plastic cast used was OrthoCast (OSA G.K., Tokushima, Japan; 4 inches in width, one roll per limb).

The method of shape acquisition using a plastic cast. a. Preparation for shape acquisition by plastic casting, b. Shape acquisition by plastic casting, c. Plastic cast incision, d. Plastic cast models.

Three undergraduate students (Junior in college. Each of the three students practiced shape acquisition taking on both legs of 10) who had practiced shape acquisition with the three methods, namely, the plaster of Paris bandage, plastic cast, and trace methods, acquired the shapes of the participants’ limbs using these methods, and the work time for each method was measured. Then, the students fabricated and marked the positive model by the plaster of Paris bandage method, marked the model with the plastic cast method, and drew the model by the trace method, and the work time until just before the metal strut bending process was measured for each method (Fig. 1). The data of the participant’s lower leg were obtained by 3D scanning as reference for comparison with the measurements of the models fabricated by the plaster of Paris bandage method and plastic cast method and the measurements by the trace method. A 3D scanner (Structure sensor; Occipital, Inc., Boulder, CO, USA) was used to obtain 3D data of the participants’ lower legs. Then, 3D-CAD software (Orten Shape; Proteor, Dijon, France) was used for measurement, smoothing, and other modifications. The following five measurements were taken for comparison: height to the lower end of the fibular head, circumferential diameter and width at 50 mm distal to the lower end of the fibular head (center position of the lower leg cuff), and height and width of the center of the lateral malleolus. Measurements were taken using a caliper gauge for width and a tape measure for circumferential diameter. The tape measure for measuring circumferential diameter and the caliper gauge for measuring width were placed perpendicular to the long axis of the lower leg.

For the comparison of the shape acquisition time and the work time until just before the metal strut bending process, as well as the comparison of the shape, we visually examined the distribution of the measurements in a frequency table, and found that many of the measurements were not normally distributed, leading to the decision to use nonparametric tests for these comparisons7). For the comparison of the work time required for shape acquisition and the work time until just before the metal strut bending process, the Friedman test was performed on the measurement time for 30 limbs for each shape acquisition method with the unit of measurement time being standardized to seconds, followed by a multiple comparison test using Bonferroni’s method to compare the three groups. Similarly, for the comparison of shapes, the Friedman test was performed on the measurements obtained by each shape acquisition method minus the measurements obtained by 3D scanning, followed by a multiple comparison test using Bonferroni’s method to compare the 3 groups. All comparisons were performed with a significance level of 1% using the statistical software JSTAT (Ver. 22.1 J).

This study was conducted after obtaining approval from the ethics committee at Niigata University of Health and Welfare (approval number: 19244-240308) and obtaining written informed consent from the participants after explaining the study objectives, methods, and personal information protection in writing and verbally.

DISCUSSION

The overall work time with the plastic cast method was approximately 90 min shorter than that with the plaster of Paris bandage method. We attribute this to the curing time of the plastic cast being shorter than that of the plaster of Paris bandage and there being no need to fabricate a positive model, thus eliminating much of the work before the metal strut bending process. Although the work time with the plastic cast method was about 3 min longer than that with the trace method, the overall work time could be considered to be about the same.

The circumferential diameter at 50 mm distal to the lower end of the fibular head was 7.5 mm larger with the plastic cast method compared with the reference dimensions obtained by 3D scanning. This is likely because the circumferential diameter increased due to the thickness of the stockinette and plastic cast used for shape acquisition. With the trace method, the circumferential diameter was 15 mm smaller than the reference dimension. A likely explanation for this is that 3D scanning to obtain the reference dimension and shape acquisition with the plaster of Paris bandage and plastic cast methods were performed in the sitting position with the long axis of the lower leg at 90 degrees to the floor, while shape acquisition with the trace method was performed in the supine position with the knee extended, causing the gastrocnemius muscle to be stretched, resulting in the smaller circumferential diameter. Similarly, the width at 50 mm distal to the lower end of the fibular head was 7.5 mm narrower with the trace method. The width of the center of the lateral malleolus was 5 to 6.5 mm narrower with the trace method than with the plaster of Paris bandage and plastic cast methods. We attribute this to the sole of the foot not being grounded during measurement with the trace method, which causes the ankle to supinate to a greater extent than when the sole of the foot is grounded. This is accompanied by movement of the subtalar joint and causes the external rotation of the ankle axis, which might have prevented the caliper gauge from being applied in the proper direction for measurement. In addition, several participants complained of pain during incision of the plastic cast due to pressure on the bony prominence of the dorsal foot caused by the tip of the plaster shears. A solution to this problem may require reconsideration of the incision method.

The present study has demonstrated that shape acquisition by the plastic cast method not only compensates for the disadvantages of the plaster of Paris bandage, such as soiling of the surrounding area and the burden of cleaning, as previously reported by Mikami et al. and Kuramoto and Hukube3, 6), but also can reduce the participant’s restraint time and the interval from shape acquisition to the metal strut bending process compared with the plaster of Paris bandage method. However, compared with the other two methods, the plastic cast method increased the overall width and circumferential diameter due to the thickness of the stockinette and plastic cast; thus, it may be necessary to make the dimensions a little narrower during the metal strut bending process. In addition to this, the fact that the plastic cast method is more expensive than the plaster of Paris bandage method suggests the need to select a shape acquisition method that is appropriate for the case and circumstance. The plaster of Paris bandage method was associated with smaller variations in circumferential diameter and width than the other two methods, making it suitable for cases requiring higher reproducibility of the shape of the patient’s body, although it is considered less efficient in terms of work time and workload. The method based on tracing and measurements can be performed in a shorter time than the other two methods, but produced smaller dimensions. Hanaoka and Ishida suggest that the quality of shape acquisition by the trace method depends on the skill of the person performing the work8). Considering these factors, the trace method may not be suitable for the fabrication of metal strut AFOs, except for patients with minimal foot deformity or those who are unable to maintain a sitting position.

Limitations of the present study include the fact that the study only included healthy participants of a certain age group, implying that shape acquisition with the plastic cast method in this study may not be generalizable to patients with significant ankle deformity. Further studies are thus needed to verify the effectiveness of the method in patients with ankle deformity.

In this study, we were able to clarify the usefulness of plastic casts by quantitatively evaluating the work time and reproducibility of the shape of a patient’s body with different methods of shape acquisition. The results of the present study are expected to help in the selection of a shape acquisition method that is appropriate for each patient and the circumstances in clinical practice.
