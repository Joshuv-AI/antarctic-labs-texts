Letter to the Editor: Financial Incentives for Obstetric Care in Underserved Areas: Stabilization, Substitution, or Insufficiency?
Authors: Ho Kyung Sung, Hye Sook Min
Published: 2025

To the Editor:

We read with interest the article by Ko et al.,1 which evaluated the impact of financial support for obstetrically underserved areas (OUAs) in Korea using a nationwide Difference-in-Differences design (DiD). Their analysis provides a crucial insight: the policy did not achieve a net increase in the physician supply but instead triggered a substitution effect, replacing a large number of part-time specialists with a smaller number of full-time ones. Based on this outcome, the authors concluded that the financial incentives were insufficient. While this finding is an important contribution, we offer several considerations to strengthen the causal claim and propose an alternative interpretation of the policy’s effect.

First, the validity of the DiD design, implemented via a two-way fixed effects model, hinges on the parallel trends assumption.2 The article does not present diagnostic evidence, such as an event-study plot, to substantiate the assumption that the trend in obstetrics/gynecology (OB/GYN) availability in OUA regions would have been parallel to that in non-OUA regions in the policy’s absence. This omission is critical, as pre-existing divergent trends could confound the estimated policy effects. Furthermore, the broad definition of the control group (defined as “all other rural districts”) may not be sufficiently comparable. Employing a matching strategy, such as propensity score matching, to construct a more homogenous control group could have strengthened the causal claim by making the parallel trends assumption more plausible.

Second, while the model includes city-fixed effects, it may not adequately address time-varying confounders that are also affected by past policy exposure, a critical issue in longitudinal observational research.3 For instance, regional birth rates or the number of women of reproductive age could influence both a region’s future OUA status and its physician supply, while also being influenced by the pre-existing healthcare environment shaped by the policy itself. Standard fixed-effects models can produce biased estimates in the presence of such feedback loops. Therefore, employing advanced causal inference methods, such as G-methods,4 would be necessary to more robustly estimate the policy’s true causal effect.

Third, the distinction between full-time and part-time specialists adds valuable granularity, but the outcome measures remain limited to physician headcounts. These metrics do not fully capture the policy's operational impact on service delivery. A more comprehensive assessment could be achieved by incorporating functional outcomes, such as the actual volume of deliveries performed, the availability of 24-hour emergency obstetric care, and changes in patient travel times for prenatal and delivery services. Such data would clarify whether the observed shift to full-time staffing translated into a tangible improvement in access to and quality of care. Moreover, this shift itself could be a demographic artifact, potentially resulting from changes in preferences and the outflow of younger, part-time physicians rather than a policy-induced conversion, further illustrating the limitations of headcount-based metrics. Crucially, the two primary policy instruments—grants and reimbursement add-ons—operate via different mechanisms. The delivery reimbursement add-on is closely tied to service volume.5 In areas where service volume is declining due to factors such as a shrinking reproductive-age population, this policy’s effects may be muted. Therefore, applying a single model for both grant-based support (which incentivizes hiring) and reimbursement-based support (which incentivizes volume) may have its limitations.

Finally, we propose an alternative interpretation of the study’s results. Given the systemic pressures facing rural healthcare, it is conceivable that OUA regions were on a path toward an accelerated collapse of obstetric services. In this context, the observed outcomes—a stable number of full-time specialists amidst a decline in total specialists—might not represent policy insufficiency but rather a partial success in mitigating what could have been a more catastrophic deterioration. The financial support may have functioned as a crucial backstop, enhancing employment stability and thereby enabling the conversion of precarious part-time roles to more secure full-time positions. This interpretation, while more optimistic, is highly policy relevant as it reframes the program’s value from “restoration” to “stabilization.”

In conclusion, the work by Ko et al.1 is an insightful evaluation of a key public health program. We suggest that future research building on this foundation could deepen our understanding by testing the parallel trends assumption and incorporating functional outcome measures. This would yield more actionable insights for policymakers working to ensure equitable access to obstetric care.
