Development and testing of a scale for nurses’ knowledge, attitudes and practices regarding insulin injection-induced lipohypertrophy: A Delphi research and cross-sectional survey
Authors: Qin Zhang, Xinyu Li, Min Chen, Ping Jia, Yan Zhu, Li Wang, Li Xiao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aimed to develop a scale for nurses’ knowledge, attitudes and practices regarding the prevention and treatment of subcutaneous lipohypertrophy associated with insulin injections and evaluate its reliability and validity.DesignA Delphi research and cross-sectional survey.SettingMedical, education and nursing experts from seven different regions conducted Delphi consultation by E-mail or Wechat, whereas clinical nurses from primary, secondary and tertiary general hospitals in different regions of Sichuan Province were conveniently selected for the questionnaire survey.ParticipantsA total of 16 female experts aged 35–58 years participated in the Delphi expert consultation process; 812 clinical nurses participated in the questionnaire survey.Outcome measureContent validity, internal consistency reliability and construct validity of the scale.ResultsThe scale comprises 3 dimensions and 36 items, with a content validity index of 0.99, a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.93, a split-half reliability of 0.87 and a test–retest reliability of 0.93. Exploratory factor analysis revealed a cumulative variance contribution rate of 53.38%, whereas confirmatory factor analysis indicated a χ2 degree of freedom 2 ratios of 2.65, a goodness-of-fit index of 0.86, a root mean square error of approximation of 0.06, a root mean square error of 0.04 and a comparative fit index of 0.90.ConclusionThe developed scale demonstrated strong reliability and validity, enabling the assessment of nurses’ knowledge, attitudes and practices regarding the prevention and treatment of lipohypertrophy associated with insulin injections.

Introduction

Diabetes is a prevalent chronic condition globally, and insulin is a vital component of its treatment.1 In China, approximately 92% of individuals with diabetes use insulin pens for self-administered injections,2 often multiple times per day. Prolonged use of insulin injections can result in skin complications at the injection site, such as lipohypertrophy (LH). LH is a common complication of insulin therapy,3 with incidence rates ranging from 37.0% to 87.5%.4 5 However, patients with diabetes and LH face challenges in terms of managing their blood sugar levels, increasing the risk of acute and chronic complications6 and leading to insulin waste, thereby increasing the economic burden on families and society.7 8 Factors contributing to LH include improper rotation of injection sites and needle reuse.7 9 Existing research indicates that structured health education can rectify improper injection practices, thus preventing LH development.10 Nurses are not only the main personnel providing injection technology education11 but also the executors, guides and supervisors of insulin injection, and their knowledge, attitudes and practices regarding the prevention and treatment of LH significantly affect patients’ injection practices. Thus, nurses play a key role in the field. However, studies on LH have primarily focused on its prevalence and risk factors, and specific assessment tools for evaluating nurses’ knowledge, attitudes and practices regarding the prevention and treatment of LH are lacking.12 Therefore, based on the Knowledge, Attitudes and Practices theory, our research team developed a scale to evaluate nurses’ knowledge, attitudes and practices concerning the prevention and treatment of LH. The aim was to provide a scientific instrument for researchers and managers to conduct comprehensive assessments and offer novel insights for effectively addressing LH. Methods

Compiling the first draft of the scale for nurse’s knowledge, attitudes and practices regarding lipohypertrophy

A research team consisting of six members—two deputy chief nurses, three head nurses and one nurse—was formed. Three individuals had master’s degrees, one was an undergraduate student and two are current master’s students. The primary tasks assigned to the team included the following: conducting a literature review, literature management and analysis, database development, survey administration, organisation of expert feedback and questionnaire refinement, execution of questionnaire surveys and data compilation and analysis.

The framework and item pool of this scale were based on the Knowledge, Attitudes and Practices theory. In this study, ‘knowledge’ referred to nurses’ theoretical understanding of LH related to insulin injections; ‘attitudes’ referred to nurses’ professional attitudes, prevention and treatment beliefs, self-demands and willingness to learn about the prevention and treatment of LH; and ‘practices’ referred to the behaviours and practices taken by nurses to assess the risk of LH in patients, prevent and manage LH and provide targeted health education to patients. The Chinese search terms included ‘insulin/insulin injection/subcutaneous insulin injection’ and ‘fat hyperplasia/subcutaneous induration/subcutaneous fat hyperplasia/fat hypertrophy’. The Chinese databases included the China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), Wanfang, China Biomedical Literature Service System and Chinese Medical Journal Full Text. Using the CNKI database search as an example, the following formula was used: (Topic=subcutaneous fat hyperplasia+fat hyperplasia+subcutaneous induration+fat hyperplasia) AND (Topic=insulin+insulin injection+subcutaneous insulin injection). The English databases included the PubMed, Cochrane Library, Ovid EMBASE and Web of Science. The English database search used a combination of MeSH subject terms and free words for systematic retrieval. For the PubMed search, the following formula was used: (MeSH=“Insulin”) OR (Title/Abstract=“Insulin, Regular/Regular Insulin/Double Insulin/Insulin, Soul/Novolin”) AND (Title/Abstract=“Subcutaneous hyperplasia/Subcutaneous induration/Lipohypertrophy/subcutaneous lipohypertrophy”). The types of literature include guidelines, consensuses, systematic evaluations and randomized controlled trials. Guidelines or expert consensus were employed as the main reference materials, supplemented by the conclusions of other literary types. The search period ranged from database establishment to March 2023. The collected research information included the concept, clinical features or manifestations, risk factors or related factors, hazards or adverse effects, evaluation diagnostic methods, prevention and treatment measures and other LH-related aspects.

Professionals involved in the survey had to possess a bachelor’s degree or higher, hold intermediate or advanced professional technical certifications, have a minimum of 10 years of experience in endocrinology-related management, education, teaching or clinical practice, willingly participate in the inquiry process and commit to completing the study in its entirety. Inquiry surveys were disseminated through email, WeChat or face-to-face interactions to solicit expert opinions on the significance of the items and gather relevant suggestions for modifications. The expert inquiry outcomes were utilised to compute the expert authority coefficient (Cr), coordination coefficient (calculated by SPSS), item importance score (very important=4 points, important=3 points, not important=2 points, very unimportant=1 point) and coefficient of variation (SD/mean of item importance scores). Cr = [expert’s familiarity with LH-related knowledge +judgement basis for item importance score]/2. Among them, very familiar=1 point, relatively familiar=0.8 points, generally familiar=0.5 points, unfamiliar=0.2 points, very unfamiliar=0 points; when the judgement basis was derived from theoretical analysis, large, medium and small values were 0.3, 0.2 and 0.1 respectively. When the judgement basis was derived from practical experience, large, medium and small values wer indicated by 0.5, 0.4 and 0.3 points, respectively, and when the judgement basis was derived from reference materials and intuitive feeling, the average value was 0.1 points. Items with an importance score ≥4.0 or a coefficient of variation <25% were retained,13 whereas the items and content of the scale were adjusted based on the specific modification recommendations provided by the experts, in conjunction with group deliberations and literature evaluations.

Fifteen nurses who met specific criteria were purposefully selected for a preliminary survey. Informed consent was obtained before the questionnaires were distributed to each nurse. The scales aimed to assess nurses’ comprehension of the items, clarity of the language used, identification of any ambiguities and suggestions for potential modifications. The inclusion criteria for participation were as follows: holding a valid nursing licence, having provided nursing care to patients receiving insulin injections within the past year and voluntarily agreeing to participate in the study. The exclusion criteria were continuing education, internship nurses and nurses who had been absent from work for at least 6 months because of sick leave, maternity leave or studying abroad.

Patient and public involvement statement

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, or conduct, or reporting, or dissemination plans of our research.

Reliability and validity testing of the knowledge, attitudes and practices scale

Survey targets

A purposive sampling technique in conjunction with quota sampling was employed to select nurses who met specific inclusion and exclusion criteria for two cross-sectional surveys. The initial survey was used for project analysis and exploratory factor analysis, whereas the subsequent survey was used for confirmatory factor analysis. The inclusion and exclusion criteria were the same as those for the small-scale preliminary survey. To ensure the reliability of the factor analysis outcomes, the sample size needed to be 5–10 times the number of items,14 considering a 20% invalid questionnaire rate.

Survey tools

The research team reviewed the literature on relevant topics and designed a general information questionnaire according to the research objectives. Data on sex, educational background, professional title, working experience, hospital level, department name, whether the participants were specialist nurses for diabetes and whether the participants had studied or worked in endocrinology (≥ 1 month) were collected.

The knowledge dimension included the options ‘Yes’, ‘No’ and ‘Unclear’, with a score of 1 assigned for a ‘Yes” response and no points allocated to the other options. The attitude dimension included the options ‘Completely agree’, ‘Somewhat agree’, ‘Uncertain’, ‘Somewhat disagree’ and ‘Completely disagree’. The practice dimension included the options ‘Always’, ‘Often’, ‘Sometimes’, ‘Occasionally’ and ‘Never’. For the above two dimensions, the corresponding scores for the response options were 5, 4, 3, 2 and 1 point, respectively. The standardised score was computed by dividing the actual score for each dimension by the maximum theoretical score for that dimension and then multiplying by 100%. A higher standardised score indicated a greater overall level of knowledge, attitudes and practices regarding LH.

Data collection and quality control methods

A cross-sectional survey was carried out utilising Questionnaire Star QR codes for the distribution and collection of surveys. The first survey was conducted from 28 June 2023 to 6 July 2023, and the second survey was conducted from 18 September 2023 to 27 September 2023. The assistance of the heads of Endocrinology, Internal Medicine, Geriatric Medicine and other departments treating patients receiving insulin injections from hospitals in different regions of Sichuan Province was required to distribute the questionnaire. They were briefed on the survey’s objectives, questionnaire content, inclusion and exclusion criteria for survey participants, and targeted distribution through the WeChat group for diabetes specialist nurse training. Measures were implemented to ensure that each IP address could only complete the questionnaire once, and submission was only possible after all questions were answered. Throughout the data collection process, careful attention was given to the fundamental structure of the data. In cases where a particular group was overrepresented, adjustments were made to the questionnaire distribution strategy. Each questionnaire was meticulously reviewed to eliminate any invalid submissions, such as those with identical responses for all options, completion times <2 min, regular answers or confusing logic.

Item analysis methods

This study used three distinct approaches for conducting item analysis: (1) the correlation coefficient approach: items within the knowledge dimension were scored as either 1 or 0, whereas items in the attitude and practice dimensions were scored on a scale ranging from 1 to 5. This discrepancy in scoring necessitated the calculation of correlation coefficients between individual items within their respective dimensions and the total score. Items with a correlation coefficient <0.4 were considered non-compliant.15 (2) T-tests: independent sample t-tests were used to compare the high-scoring group (the top 27%) and the low-scoring group (the bottom 27%) regarding the total score. Items with no significant differences did not meet the requirement.15 (3) Cronbach’s α coefficients: if removing an item increased the Cronbach’s α coefficient of the questionnaire, then the item did not meet the requirement.16 If an item met both of the above conditions, it was removed.15

Reliability testing methods

This research used Cronbach’s α coefficients and a split-half reliability test to evaluate the internal consistency reliability of the scale, which generally required Cronbach’s α coefficients value >0.70 and the split-half reliability value >0.80.15 A subset of 20 survey participants was retested after a 2-week interval to evaluate test–retest reliability, which generally required a value >0.70.15

Validity testing methods

Six experts (they were from the expert database that participated in the Delphi consultation in the early stage) were invited to assess the relevance of each scale item to its respective dimension concept. Ratings of highly relevant, moderately relevant, weakly relevant and irrelevant were assigned 4, 3, 2 and 1 point, respectively. The content validity index (I-CVI) for each item and the overall content validity index of the scale (S-CVI) were calculated using experts’ ratings. The S-CVI was determined by summing the I-CVIs for all items and dividing by the total number of items.

Construct validity was evaluated through exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses. The sampling adequacy was determined by the Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) test and Bartlett’s test of sphericity. A KMO value >0.8 and a Bartlett’s test P value<0.05 indicated suitability for factor analysis.15 Principal component analysis and maximum variance orthogonal rotation were used in exploratory factor analysis. The criteria for assessment were as follows13: ① the common factors aligned with the scale’s conceptual framework, with a cumulative variance contribution >40%, ② each item had a factor loading >0.40 on its respective common factor, and there was no shared loading. (Each item had a factor loading >0.40 on only one common factor). Confirmatory factor analysis was conducted to evaluate the model’s fit to the observed data via maximum likelihood estimation. The fitting indicators were as follows: ① χ2 to df ratio (χ²/df, should be <3), ② goodness-of-fit index (should be ≥0.85), ③ root mean square error of approximation (should be <0.08), ④ root mean square residual (should be <0.05), ⑤ comparative fit index (should be >0.90), ⑥ parsimony-adjusted normed fit index (should be >0.50) and ⑦ parsimonious comparative fit index (should be >0.50)

Statistical methods

SPSS 25.0 was used for tasks such as descriptive analysis, item analysis and exploratory factor analysis, and AMOS 21.0 was used for model validation. Metric data that adhered to a normal distribution or closely approximated a normal distribution are described as the mean ± SD; otherwise, they are described as the median (IQR). Count data are presented as frequencies and percentages. The established significance level for hypothesis testing was α<0.05.

Results

Literature review

A total of 2019 articles were retrieved (including 170 articles from CNKI, 104 from the Wanfang Database, 155 from the China Biomedical Literature Service System, 57 from the China Medical Journal Full Text Database, 134 from PubMed, 65 from the Cochrane Library, 845 from Ovid-EMBASE, 480 from the Web of Science, 0 from UpToDate and 9 from the International Guidelines Collaboration Network). After removing duplicate articles, the title was read first, before the abstract, to remove obviously unrelated articles, leaving 173 articles. After further reading of the full texts, 15 articles were included: two guidelines, one expert consensus, two systematic reviews, six randomised controlled studies, one case–control study, one quasi-experimental study and two cross-sectional studies. They are shown in online supplemental file 2. Key information was extracted from these studies based on the research purpose, and the extracted content was classified and organised according to the operational definitions of knowledge, attitudes and practices. The preliminary proposed item pool included a total of 62 items. After two group discussions, a preliminary scale for evaluating nurses’ knowledge, attitudes and practices regarding LH was developed, comprising 3 dimensions and 54 items. To enhance the reliability of survey outcomes and simplify the identification of invalid responses, a reverse item was included in each dimension.

Expert inquiry and preliminary survey

A total of 16 female experts from seven distinct regions were selected to participate in the survey. Their ages varied from 35 to 58 years, with an average age of 45.38±7.86 years. Among the experts, five held intermediate professional titles, six held associate senior professional titles, and five held senior professional titles. Regarding their educational background, four held bachelor’s degrees, 10 held master’s degrees, and two held doctoral degrees; five experts had 10–15 years of experience in diabetes-related fields, four had 16–20 years of experience and seven had >20 years of experience. The response rates for the initial and subsequent rounds of inquiries were 80% and 100%, the authority coefficients of the experts were 0.89 and 0.90 and the Kendall harmony coefficients were 0.19 and 0.21, respectively (all p<0.001). In the first round, the average importance score for items ranged from 3.69 to 5.00, with the coefficient of variation ranging from 0% to 31%. In the second round, one item had an average importance score of 3.94, whereas the remaining items were rated at ≥4.0, with a maximum coefficient of variation of 20%.

Following the first round of expert inquiry, eight items were removed, two items were included and 16 items were adjusted according to the average importance rating, coefficient of variation and participant expertise. After the second round of expert inquiry, one item was removed, and five items were modified. The test scale contained a total of 47 items, including 23 items in the knowledge dimension, 7 in the attitude dimension and 17 in the practice dimension, and one reverse item was set for each dimension.

Fifteen questionnaires were disseminated for a small-scale preliminary survey, and all were returned within a timeframe of 5–10 min. Subsequently, the research team synthesised feedback obtained from the presurvey, incorporating opinions and suggestions to modify six textual elements.

General information of the respondents

In the first survey, a total of 331 questionnaires were distributed, for which 297 valid responses were obtained, yielding a response rate of 89.73%, meeting the minimum sample size requirement (47×5÷0.8=294). These collected questionnaires were used for project analysis and exploratory factor analysis. In the second survey, a total of 555 questionnaires were distributed, for which 515 valid responses were collected, resulting in a recovery rate of 92.79%, meeting the minimum sample size requirement (36×5÷0.8=225). These responses were used for confirmatory factor analysis. The detailed information of the survey respondents is shown in table 1.

Item analysis

Nine items were excluded based on the principle of eliminating entries. However, following deliberation within the research team and considering the participants’ expertise and experience, eight items were deleted. Consequently, the scale comprised 39 items distributed across three dimensions, all of which were positive expressions; 17 items were related to knowledge, 6 pertained to attitudes and 16 were associated with practices.

Validity test

The initial exploratory factor analysis revealed that the KMO value was 0.88, and Bartlett’s sphericity test result for χ2 was 7968.54 (p<0.001). Three items were identified for removal from the scale due to having double loads in the knowledge and practice dimensions, with factor loadings>0.4. Notably, the items ‘Insulin is injected into the area of lipohypertrophy, and the pain in this area is less severe than that in normal tissue’ and ‘Subcutaneous lipohypertrophy associated with insulin injections generally does not require treatment’ exhibited factor loading values of 0.39 and 0.38, respectively. Despite falling slightly below the standard (the loading value of the item in the corresponding common factor should be >0.40), after discussion among all members of the research group, it was agreed that they should be retained. Because the former related to the clinical characteristics of LH and the latter stated that the content of the item was the treatment of LH, both of which had important measurement value for understanding the knowledge mastery of the survey subjects, and nurses, as professionals, should also understand this part of the knowledge. Subsequently, the second exploratory factor analysis was performed on the remaining items, yielding a KMO of 0.88 and a Bartlett’s sphericity test result for χ2 of 7095.00 (p<0.001). Following the theoretical framework encompassing three dimensions (knowledge, attitudes and practices), three common factors with eigenvalues>1 were identified, collectively explaining 53.38% of the variance. The factor loading values for each item across the respective factors ranged from 0.38 to 0.95 (The results of the exploratory factor analysis are shown in online supplemental file 1, and the measurement meaning of item is shown in online supplemental file 3). The final scale comprised 36 items distributed among three dimensions: 16 items in the knowledge dimension, 6 in the attitudes dimension and 14 in the practices dimension. The knowledge dimension measured the knowledge level of nurses about the concept of LH (K1), clinical features (K2 and K3), diagnostic tools (K4 and K5), clinical hazards (K6, K7 and K8), risk factors (K9–K12), and prevention and treatment measures (K13–K16). The attitudes dimension measured nurses’ professional cognition of LH (A17 and A18), prevention and treatment beliefs (A19 and A20), self-demand (A21) and willingness to learn (A22). The practices dimension measured the behaviour status of nurses’ assessment of LH (P23–P25), intervention (P26–P31) and health education (P32–P36).

The confirmatory factor analysis revealed that the initial model did not demonstrate an optimal fit. Subsequently, the model was revised according to the size of the modification index (MI) value in the output result of the Amos software, resulting in fit indices of the revised model falling within acceptable parameters (table 2 and figure 2).

Reliability test

The overall Cronbach’s α coefficient of the scale was 0.93, with Cronbach’s α coefficients for each dimension ranging from 0.79 to 0.96. The overall split-half reliability was 0.87, with split-half reliabilities for each dimension ranging from 0.87 to 0.94. The overall test–retest reliability was 0.93, with test–retest reliabilities for each dimension ranging from 0.82 to 0.95.

Discussion

The design of the scale is systematic and scientific

This study was based on a systematic review of relevant literature on the topic using literature analysis and group meetings to incorporate items regarding knowledge, attitudes and practices related to the prevention and treatment of LH as much as possible. The knowledge dimension involves the concepts of LH, clinical features, diagnostic tools, clinical hazards, risk factors, prevention, treatment measures, etc. The attitudes dimension involves items such as nurses’ professional cognition, prevention and treatment beliefs, willingness to learn and self-demand regarding LH. The practices dimension involves nurses’ assessment behaviours regarding LH, assessments of the risk of LH in patients, assessments of their knowledge of insulin injections, evaluations of patients’ injection techniques, implementation of intervention measures, such as learning guidelines for insulin injection, provision of education about insulin injections to patients, improvement of patients’ injection techniques, guidance of patients to regularly check the skin at the injection site, teaching of examination methods, including informing patients of the correct methods for handling LH, and performance of follow-up; the items regarding these topics made the measurement content of the scale more comprehensive.

Reliable measurement results are inseparable from scientific measurement tools. In scale development, to ensure the scientificity of the tool, the research team strictly followed the scale development procedure. On the basis of a systematic literature search, a pool of entries as established as comprehensively as possible according to the research purpose. In the inquiry stage, strictly including domain experts who met the inquiry standards. The scale’s content and structure were adjusted based on experts’ opinions and item importance ratings. Through a small range of pre-tests, the language expression, internal logic and comprehension difficulty of the items were modified to further improve the quality of the scale. Then, through purposive and quota sampling, the respondents were selected according to the inclusion and exclusion criteria, the items of the scale were further adjusted by item analysis and exploratory factor analysis, and finally the ability of the scale to fit the actual data was tested by confirmatory factor analysis to ensure the scientificity of the scale.

The scale can effectively measure the level of knowledge, attitudes and practices of nurses regarding the prevention and treatment of LH

Reliability and validity are indicators of whether a survey tool can stably, truthfully and accurately measure things being measured.14 The Cronbach’s α coefficients for the overall and various dimensions of the scale were all >0.7, and the split-half reliability values for the overall and various dimensions were all >0.80, indicating a high level of internal consistency of the scale.15 Test–retest reliability can reflect the stability of a survey tool.14 The test–retest reliability of this scale after 2 weeks, both overall and for each dimension, met the requirement, indicating the high stability of the scale.

For the validity evaluation of the scale, both content validity and construct validity analyses were adopted. The overall content validity of the scale was >0.90, the content validity of each dimension was >0.78, indicating good content validity of the scale, and the measurement content was suitable for the measurement topic.17 The research team constructed the scale framework based on the Knowledge, Attitudes and Practices theory. Therefore, the preliminary concept involved three scale dimensions. During the exploratory factor analysis, three common factors with eigenvalues>1 were extracted, contributing to a cumulative variance of 53.38%. The scale structure was consistent with the preliminary concept. However, during the confirmatory factor analysis, the fit of the initial model was not ideal, with a χ2 df ratio >3.0. Model modifications were made based on the MI, and two pairs of measurement error terms were released. The χ2 df ratio improved from 3.07 to 2.65, and other fit indices improved compared with those before parameter release. The modified model fit indices were within the reference range.18 Therefore, the model has a strong ability to fit actual data, and the construct validity of the scale is good.

The scale provides a practical evaluation tool for nurses, managers or researchers in the field.

The population of patients with diabetes is large, and the incidence rate of diabetes is increasing annually.19 As an important treatment for diabetes, insulin is becoming more widely used due to the increasing number of patients. As a common complication of insulin injections, LH not only directly or indirectly damages the health of patients with diabetes but also increases the economic burden on families and society. Nurses, as the main implementers of patient education and instructors of insulin injection techniques, can significantly influence the injection practices of patients or caregivers through their knowledge, attitudes and practices regarding LH. Therefore, nurses may play an important role in the prevention and treatment of LH. The scale consists of 36 items with concise language, takes approximately 5–10 min to complete, is easy to implement and has strong operability. Nursing staff can evaluate their knowledge level and practice gap in preventing LH according to the content of the scale to improve the knowledge structure, standardise the injection behaviour, improve the risk awareness of LH prevention and implement health education in a targeted manner. At the same time, this scale measures the overall cognition, prevention and treatment attitude and behaviour of clinical nurses on LH, which is helpful for managers to understand the knowledge mastery, prevention and treatment attitude and practical behaviour of nurses in different regions or hospitals, and compare the specific differences of nurses in different regions or hospitals to provide reference for formulating training strategies, improving the training system and implementing targeted education and training. In addition, if a region intends to carry out diabetes specialist nurse training, in the course design stage, this scale can be used to investigate the trainees, especially focusing on nurses’ understanding of the hazards, risk factors, prevention and treatment measures, visual and palpation examination methods and health education content of LH to improve the course content and highlight the focus of the course, and it can be investigated again after the training to evaluate the training effect. At the same time, it provides a survey tool for researchers in related fields to broaden research ideas and improve the prevention and treatment of LH.

Conclusion

The scale for evaluating nurses’ knowledge, attitudes and practices regarding LH developed in this study is systematic, scientific and useful and has good reliability and validity. It can be used by nurses to assess their knowledge and practice gaps in LH prevention and provide a survey tool for managers or researchers to understand or compare the current status of nurses’ knowledge, attitudes and practices of LH in different regions or hospitals. However, in the reliability and validity tests of this study, the samples were only from Sichuan Province, China, and the sample size from first-level hospitals was small, which may affect the results. Further efforts are needed to increase the sample size and selection range for further validation of the scale.
