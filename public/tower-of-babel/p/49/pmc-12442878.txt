Selective ion transport of nonlinear resistive switching by hierarchical nanometer-to-angstrom channels for nanofluidic transistors
Authors: Xiaoyi Hu, Hengyu Xu, Jun Lu, Fenglu Cui, Heng-An Wu, Fengchao Wang, Lei Jiang, Kostya S. Novoselov, Huanting Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Nanoconfined selective ion transport shows promise for achieving biomimetic ion separation and iontronics information transmission. However, exploration of tunable nonlinearity of ion transport is formidable due to the challenge in fabrication of nanochannel devices of exquisite nanoconfined architectures. Here, we report a hierarchical metal-organic framework (MOF)–based nanofluidic device of multiscale heterogeneous channel junctions to achieve unprecedented triode-like nonlinear proton transport, in contrast with diode-like rectifying transport for metal ions. Through experiments and theoretical simulations, we unveil the underlying mechanism for this unique nonlinear proton transport property, i.e., the gating effect from the built-in electric potential across the MOF phase junctions enabled by voltage bias above a threshold. As a proof-of-concept application demonstration, the nanofluidic device exhibits an ionic memory property as a nanofluidic memristor. This finding of proton-specific nonlinear resistive switching and memristive phenomenon can inspire future studies into nanofluidic iontronics and mass transport by rational design of coupled nanometric and angstrom-sized confinement.

INTRODUCTION

Exploration of nanoconfined selective ion transport is surging for potential breakthroughs in both fundamental science and practical engineering applications in bionic device fabrication, precise mass transport and reaction, and other energy-water nexuses (1–4). Diverse biological systems serve as the most delicate prototypes at multiple dimensions that have inspired artificial analogs via nanofluidics that focus on electrokinetic ion transport under nanoconfinement (5, 6). For example, biological ion channels inspire researchers to develop artificial nanofluidic membranes to achieve biomimetic ultrafast selective and rectifying ion conduction (7–9). Beyond the mass transport, nanofluidics can use dissociated ions as charge carriers in aqueous media, resembling the working context of biological memristors (e.g., the ligand-gated biological ion channel), and has spawned fluidic iontronics with nanofluidic devices as desirable nanofluidic transistors to amplify or switch electrical signals and power as electronic counterparts (10, 11), which is a promising alternative to achieve neuromorphic-like computing by bridging gaps between electronic information transmission and biological neural networks (12, 13). Following the emergence of electronic neuromorphic devices, nanofluidic memristors (i.e., two-terminal nanofluidic devices that exhibit a history-dependent nonlinear flow of charged ions in electrolyte solutions) have been reported to preliminarily mimic the core synaptic functionality of neuromorphic computing in brain neural networks by asymmetric nanofluidic devices (14–16) featuring the rectifying ion transport property (i.e., nanofluidic diode) enabled by polarity switching of drain-to-source reservoir voltage bias (VDS). In this regard, resistive switching materials and devices of biomimetic tuning principles are indispensable for the construction of novel iontronic transistors (17–21). However, progress in nonlinear ion transport dynamics has stagnated since the reported nanofluidic diode (22, 23) and activated or saturated nonlinear conduction for metal ions (24–27), due to the difficulty in the implementation of effective ionic heterojunctions with a precise ion conduction tuning manner. So far, it is formidable to develop new nanofluidic transistors of an ion-specific resistive switching model for biomimetic aqueous iontronics.

Nanofluidic devices with exquisite architectures for delicate confinement are the prerequisite to realize these intriguing tunable ion transport properties (28–30). The most recent nanofluidic studies have focused on improving the ion–channel wall interactions to modulate ion transport rates by controlling the aperture size to be angstrom scaled and as even as possible (31–34). Crystalline porous materials, such as metal-organic frameworks (MOFs), have the advantages of well-defined channel architectures and diverse channel chemistries (35, 36). They have emerged as a superior multidimensional platform for the regulation of molecular and ion transport with atomic-scale precision (37–39). However, the study of MOF-based nanofluidic transistors is still in its infancy, and their potential for iontronic applications remains to be explored. Therefore, we proposed to construct multidimensional ionic heterojunctions in one device by a hierarchical MOF to explore selective ion transport and understand the underlying mechanisms.

Here, we report unprecedented nonlinear proton transport with multistage resistance switching by a hierarchical MOF–based nanofluidic transistor device (h-MOFNT), contrasting with diode-like metal ion transport achieved. This nanofluidic device was prepared by the assembly of hierarchical MOF crystals in a polymer single nanochannel (NC), processing multidimensional channel heterojunctions. Through experiments and simulations, we unveiled the underlying mechanisms behind this nonlinear proton transport in h-MOFNT, demonstrating the self-gating effect from the built-in electric potential across the channel heterojunctions within the MOF phase induced by transphase proton conduction that can only be activated by the applied voltage VDS above a threshold, a unique mechanism for nonlinear ion transport. As a proof-of-concept application, h-MOFNT exhibits triode-like charge conduction as an innovative nanofluidic memristor of short-term memory property. These ion-responsive self-gating resistance switches achieved without a physical gating electrode are expected to inspire future research on advanced fluidic iontronics through rational design of multidimensional channel heterojunctions.

RESULTS

Preparation of hierarchical MOF–based nanofluidic device

We prepared a hierarchical MOF–based nanofluidic device with multiple heterojunctions by assembling hierarchical Zr-MOF-SO3H crystals in the small side (i.e., tip side) zone of a bullet-shaped amino-functionalized polyethylene terephthalate NC (PET NC), leaving the local zone adjacent to the large side (i.e., base side) uncovered by the MOF phase (Fig. 1, A and B). Briefly, the amino-modified PET NC film with a single bullet-shaped nanochannel was clamped between two cells: the cell facing the tip side was filled with ligand aqueous solution, while another cell was placed with metal precursor aqueous solution (fig. S1, A to D). The metal precursor and ligand molecules can meet within the PET NC and then form nucleation that further grows into a highly intergrown MOF polycrystalline phase on the tip side of the substrate (fig. S1E), as confirmed by the scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images and powder x-ray diffraction (PXRD) patterns (figs. S2 and S3). We chose the PET NC as the substrate for constructing the MOF-based nanofluidic device because the free benzoic acid groups dangling from the PET channel walls are expected to act as ligand units to coordinate with the secondary building units (i.e., Zr─O clusters) for facilitated MOF heterogeneous nucleation and growth. As a result, the degree of intergrowth between the MOF polycrystalline phase and the PET channel should be sufficient to minimize the impact of possible gaps between the MOF phase and the PET channel wall. Preparing dense MOF polycrystalline membranes with intrinsic free acidic groups has been highly challenging, and our method is effective for small-scale synthesis of the MOF polycrystalline phase.

The asymmetrical h-MOFNT contains two types of heterogeneous channel junctions with contrasting scale and junction direction. First, the void one-dimensional (1D) PET NC of a hundred nanometers in diameter links the dense MOF phase to form a heterojunction interface in the radial direction of the h-MOFNT [Fig. 1B (i)], while on the tip side of the h-MOFNT, the MOF polycrystalline phase consists of NC-AC junctions due to two distinct lattice phases interconnected with each other [Fig. 1B (ii) and fig. S4]. One phase is the perfect crystalline phase constructed by 12-connected Zr─O clusters and BDC-SO3H units, which has angstrom-sized channels of ~6 Å aperture size (denoted as MOF-AC phase). Another phase is the missing-cluster defect phase constructed by both 9-connected and 12-connected Zr─O clusters linked by H2BDC-SO3H units (monosodium 2-sulfoterephtahlic acid), which has mesoporous channels of ~2 nm (denoted as MOF-NC phase) (40, 41). These hierarchical channels were observed from the N2 isotherms of the MOF particles synthesized by the hydrothermal method (fig. S5). These two lattice phases are orderly distributed throughout the MOF in both the radial and axial directions of the h-MOFNT, which results in the second heterojunctions within the MOF phase, i.e., MOF-NC and MOF-AC junctions in the h-MOFNT [Fig. 1B (ii)].

Nanofluidic selective nonlinear ion transport

We investigated current-voltage (I-V) characteristics of diverse electrolyte solutions in h-MOFNT with two electrode terminals (Fig. 1A). h-MOFNT shows an “S”-shaped I-V curve for 0.1 M HCl (Fig. 1C). In contrast, diode-like I-V curves for 0.1 M chloride metal ion solutions (KCl, NaCl, LiCl, CaCl2, and CeCl3) were obtained with a higher ion rectification ratio than HCl (Fig. 1D and fig. S6). The magnified I-V curves of HCl and KCl at the positive VDS (0 to 2 V) are shown in Fig. 1E for clear comparison. For HCl, there was a fast current increase under a low voltage bias range (0 to 0.2 V), followed by a modest increase at an intermediate transition range (0.3 to 0.8 V), and it lastly reached a saturation current level at a higher voltage bias (0.9 to 2 V) of an extremely slow current increase with the voltage bias, while K+ ion followed Ohm’s law in the whole positive voltage bias range approximately as shown by the linear I-V curve for KCl. This nonlinear I-V property for HCl solution in the h-MOFNT is analogous to the output current features of electronic field-effect transistors (FETs) but without using any physical gating electrode as in previous electronic or nanofluidic FETs (42, 43). To distinguish it from diode-like nonlinear KCl transport, we noted this unprecedented nonlinear proton conduction phenomenon as the “triode-like” characteristic, while the polymer substrates do not have this function (fig. S7). We conducted drift-diffusion experiments of the h-MOFNT to determine cation transference numbers of 0.86 and 0.81 for HCl and KCl, respectively (fig. S8), indicating the predominant contribution of protons and K+ to the observed electrolyte-responsive nonlinear ion conduction property. We further calculated the resistance by linear fitting of the I-V curves by stages (Fig. 1F). Proton nonlinearity conduction can be divided into a three-stage resistance switching in two individual voltage polarity ranges, achieving a resistance ratio of up to ~220 of high voltage against low voltage bias (i.e., 1.3 × 105 megohms versus 6.1 × 102 megohms) at the positive voltage range. As a comparison, diode-like K+ conduction only has a two-stage resistance variation with a resistance ratio of ~50, enabled by voltage polarity changing: lower resistance in the tip-to-base side direction and much higher resistance in the opposite direction, corresponding to the rectification ratio (fig. S6B).

We further investigated the concentration effect on nonlinear proton nanofluidic transport in h-MOFNT by the I-V and conductance-voltage (G-V) characteristics. As shown in Fig. 2A and fig. S9, robust triode-like nonlinear conduction was achieved in HCl solutions with a concentration ranging from 1 mM to 1.0 M, due to high stability of MOF crystallinity in the electrolyte solutions studied as shown by the stable PXRD patterns of MOF in aqueous solution (fig. S10). Considering the nonlinear I-V characteristic, we used the instantaneous Ohm’s law to calculate the conductance, i.e., G(t) = I(t)/VDS(t), as shown in Fig. 2B. In general, proton conductance was reduced with the increasing voltage bias for all concentrations investigated. The HCl conductance demonstrated a linear dependence on electrolyte concentration in log-log coordinates (Fig. 2C). Over the concentration range studied, the conductance of HCl with VDS below 1.0 V was much higher than the bulk values and decreased to be lower than the bulk values with VDS above 1.0 V, indicating the switching resistance for proton transport with the voltage bias. In addition, triode-like I-V curves for HNO3 solutions were also observed (Fig. 2D), demonstrating the universal nonlinear transport property for protons by the h-MOFNT.

Understanding of the ion-specific nonlinear mass transport mechanisms

These distinct nonlinear ion conduction performances for proton over metal ions indicate that the h-MOFNT has an electrolyte-responsive property from the nanoconfined interactions of cations with the hierarchical channels of the MOF phase and the substrate polymer channel wall. In HCl solutions, the protonation of both μ-OH groups from the Zr─O cluster of the MOF phase and free –NH2 groups in the uncovered PET NC wall made the whole channel of the h-MOFNT positively charged, whereas the h-MOFNT was negatively charged in KCl solution (pH = 5.8), due to deprotonation of free –SO3H groups in the MOF ligand units and the residual –COOH groups in the uncovered PET NC (fig. S11). These contrasting unipolar charge structures can be verified by the zeta potentials of MOF particles dispersed in the corresponding solutions (table S1). For KCl solution, the negatively charged MOF channels can favor K+ conduction while restraining anion transport by electrostatic interaction, which leads to the high cation transference number of h-MOFNT. Asymmetries in both aperture channel size and negative charge density are present between the tip and base side of h-MOFNT for KCl solution (table S2) due to the primary PET NC-MOF heterojunction [Fig. 1B (i)], which can induce metal ion enrichment or depletion in h-MOFNTs with negative or positive voltage bias applied, qualitatively evidenced by finite element method simulations (fig. S12), the same as in the literature (7).

Regardless of voltage bias polarity, triode-like proton conduction is dependent on voltage bias amplitude, for which we propose that the secondary NC-AC heterojunctions of the MOF phase [Fig. 1B (ii)] play an essential role for this VDS-activated resistive switching. In general, the MOF NCs serve as the dominant pathways for proton conduction through the MOF polycrystalline phase, while the MOF ACs of higher steric effect are short circuited (Fig. 3A). When the VDS was below the threshold potential (Vth) of 0.2 to 0.3 V, protons can transport ultrafast through MOF NCs via Grotthuss hopping facilitated by sulfonate groups along the wall of the MOF NCs [Fig. 3A (i)]. With VDS increasing above Vth, partial protons can enter MOF ACs from upstream MOF NCs, i.e., achieving transphase proton transport [Fig. 3A (ii)], and then become trapped there close to the entry pore mouth because of a higher energy barrier for proton conduction in individual MOF ACs. With further increase of VDS, the MOF ACs can gradually enrich protons to obtain a higher concentration than that in the adjacent upstream MOF NCs. As a result, the local built-in electric potential (ΔE) is established across the MOF-NC and MOF-AC junction, highlighted by the orange zone in Fig. 3A (ii), which functions as a virtual internal gating electrode to modulate the proton conduction in two aspects. First, the established ΔE will counteract the proton drift driven by the VDS applied. Second, the local ΔE zone could perturb the otherwise smooth route for proton Grotthuss hopping along the MOF-NC wall and switch to transport mainly in the center of the MOF-NCs with decreased facilitated effect from the –SO3H groups, which results in gradually saturated proton conduction. This gating effect of the built-in electric potential across the NC-AC junction can elucidate the resistance switch from the low resistance state (0 to 0.2 V) to the high resistance state (0.9 to 2 V) triggered by the increasing positive voltage bias as shown in Fig. 1 (E and F). In contrast, there is no threshold voltage effect for K+ (Fig. 3B).

The proposed mechanism can also explain the switching back to the low resistance state at low voltage bias from the initial high resistance state at high voltage bias, e.g., the scanning VDS from −2 to 0 V. First, with the high voltage bias of −2 V initially applied, the proton can immediately exhibit transphase conduction to accumulate in ACs so that the local internal potential (ΔE) in the individual NC-AC junction can be instantaneously built, which, in return, counteracts the proton drift in MOF NCs driven by the voltage bias applied across the h-MOFNT. With the voltage bias reducing but still at a high level (e.g., above −1.8 V), the built-in ΔE can increase gradually and then can reach a steady status with the voltage bias above a value, such as above −0.9 V. During this above voltage bias range (−2 to −0.9 V), the reversal ΔE persistently maintains its offset effect for the proton transport throughout the h-MOFNT, keeping this high resistance state III at the negative voltage bias in Fig. 1F. Afterward, when the voltage bias further reduces, the ΔE begins to diminish because these accumulated protons in MOF AC can diffuse back to the upstream MOF NC. These accumulated protons can scarcely pass throughout the angstrom-sized AC to transit from one NC to another NC because the tandem NCs with an aperture size of ~2 nm are expected to be the continuous highway in MOF phase for fast proton transport with much less resistance, which will be discussed in later simulations. In other words, the MOF-AC phase can be regarded as an isolated trap region for proton enrichment and temporary storage, while the MOF-NC phase serves as the dominant pathway available for proton conduction in the overall MOF polycrystalline phase. Therefore, an interim stage with moderate resistance arises, as shown by the state II at the negative voltage bias in Fig. 1F. Last, with the voltage bias reduced to below −0.3 V, the residual ΔE becomes too small to exhibit an obvious influence on the proton drift. In this low voltage bias range (−0.2 to 0 V), the proton transport through the NCs becomes much smooth, so it lastly switches back to the low resistance model, as shown by the state I in Fig. 1F. The qualitative correlation of the reducing voltage bias from −2 to 0 V for HCl solutions with the three states of resistance switching is illustrated in fig. S13.

Overall, the diagram of the nanofluidic ionic circuit by a single h-MOFNT can be depicted in Fig. 3C. In general, the dense MOF polycrystalline phase and the void PET NC zone constitute resistors in series for electrokinetic cation conduction through the h-MOFNT. This voltage-activated proton enrichment in the MOF-AC phase induces a built-in internal electric potential E ( E∝n·ΔE ) in the overall MOF polycrystalline phase, which effectively regulates proton transport through the h-MOFNT. This nanofluidic device achieves self-gating charge conduction as the electronic FETs but avoids the use of a physical gate electrode. In addition, the working principle for the nanofluidic proton triode in our h-MOFNT is different from that of previous nanofluidic bipolar transistors with a sandwich-like charged structure (i.e., positive-negative-positive junctions), which reported similar nonlinear characteristics for metal ions under circumscribed concentrations and pH ranges (24, 25).

To support our hypothesis, we investigated the conduction of protons compared to K+ in the MOF hierarchical channels by comprehensive theoretical simulations (Fig. 4) based on Zr-MOF-SO3H models from the literature (fig. S14) (41). The molecular dynamics (MD) simulations revealed that hydrated K+ ions and protons in the MOF-NC phase remained in the almost fully hydrated states because their hydrated diameters (6.6 and 5.6 Å) are smaller than ~2 nm–sized MOF NCs, and partial dehydration occurred for ions in MOF ACs (fig. S15), due to the strong size-exclusion effect of the 6 Å–sized triangle window aperture of MOF ACs, indicating a low possibility for transphase conduction for K+ ions. The calculated proton and K+ mobility values in the MOF NCs were three orders of magnitude higher than those in MOF ACs, i.e., ~10−7 m2/V·s versus 10−10 m2/V·s (fig. S16), which supports the above assumption that the MOF NCs serve as the preferential pathway for both cation transports. As further illustrated in Fig. 4A and fig. S17, MOF-NC and MOF-AC phases are 3D interconnected, and the NC-NC connection in the MOF phase is in a straight manner, compared to the AC-AC connection with high channel tortuosity, excluding the possibility of AC-AC transport for ions. We calculated the energy barrier for ion transport in the sole MOF-NC phase and the possible transphase conduction from the MOF-NC to the MOF-AC phase through density functional theory (DFT) calculations (fig. S18A). The contrasting energy barriers for K+, i.e., 0.22 eV for transport in MOF NCs versus 0.47 eV for transphase conduction, indicated the low possibility of transphase transport for metal ions, thus excluding the threshold voltage effect. For protons, transphase conduction was more likely to occur because of the relatively smaller energy barrier difference (i.e., 0.13 eV versus 0.28 eV). Besides, the low-energy barrier for proton transport induced by the Grotthuss hopping not only leads to ultrafast proton transport in MOF NCs at low voltage bias but also prompts protons to overcome the energy barrier of transphase conduction to enter the MOF ACs (Fig. 4A) when the external electric field exceeds the threshold and then to accumulate there due to the deep potential well (fig. S18B). As a result, the MOF-AC phase can accumulate protons, causing a much higher proton concentration than that in the MOF-NC phase, evidenced by the markedly increasing proton concentration in the MOF-AC phase with applied voltage bias by MD simulations (Fig. 4B). In contrast, the K+ concentration in MOF-ACs only slightly increased and remained well below the bulk solution level. We also calculated the energy barrier for the proton transport from one AC to another AC of up to 0.75 eV, which supports the hypothesis that the accumulated proton transport throughout the MOF-AC phase is impossible.

We further investigated the local built-in electric potential induced by these accumulated protons in the MOF-AC phase (Fig. 4C and fig. S19). Compared to K+ without cation enrichment, the local potential strength and vibrations across the MOF-AC and MOF-NC phase heterojunctions markedly increased for HCl. Moreover, a higher external electric field enabled more protons to enter and accumulate in the MOF-AC phase, resulting in a much higher internal electric potential. Consequently, proton mobility markedly decreased with increasing electric field compared to the fluctuating K+ mobility around a certain value (Fig. 4D). Conversely, the Cl− mobility in MOF slightly varied with voltage applied (fig. S20), supporting the minimal contribution of anions to the triode-like resistance switching characteristic of HCl. We also investigated the variation of the hydrogen network profile in the MOF-NC phase (fig. S21), showing the diminishing of local hydrogen networks adjacent to the MOF-NC wall, which vividly illustrates the disturbance effect of the built-in internal electric field on proton hopping by constricting the accessible highway. Last, the simulated ionic current can be calculated through the following equation: I=μcqEFS , where c, q, F, and S are the ion concentration (fig. S22), ion valence, Faraday constant, and pore area of the MOF, respectively. As shown in Fig. 4E, the simulated ionic currents with the voltage bias are qualitatively consistent with the observed triode-like saturation proton and linear K+ conduction in the single polarity voltage range. These results illustrate the validity of the proposed mechanism for this unprecedented triode-like proton nonlinear conduction by the h-MOFNT.

Electronic-like and biomimetic iontronic applications

To demonstrate the promising application of h-MOFNT in iontronics, we fabricated five h-MOFNT samples to design a fluidic circuit by programming them in parallel, as shown in Fig. 5A. With increasing numbers of parallel h-MOFNTs from single to quintuple, the fluidic circuit with the set 0.1 M HCl produced a series of nonlinear I-V curves (Fig. 5, B and C), mimicking the output current characteristics of electronic FETs enabled by increasing the gating voltage. Furthermore, the h-MOFNT demonstrated an obvious hysteretic loop effect by scanning loop voltage and exhibited pinched hysteresis loops with the scan rate reducing from 500 to 100 mV/s (Fig. 5D and fig. S23), indicating the dependence of the nonlinear proton transport on voltage scanning frequency. These two features along with the intrinsic triode-like I-V signify a fresh nanofluidic ionic memristor of the h-MOFNT, in contrast with no hysteresis effect by the substrate amino-PET NC (fig. S24). Notably, prescanning at negative voltage polarity (e.g., −2 to 0 V) can greatly enhance the postscanning current amplitude at the opposite voltage polarity (e.g., 0 to +2 V) and vice versa (Fig. 5D), which is more obvious at a higher voltage scanning frequency. Taking currents at ±1 V as an example, the absolute I+1V at state II and I–1V at state IV are 46.3 and 20.5% higher than the corresponding current at former state I and III, respectively (fig. S25). We further tested two other scanning voltage scopes with the reversed scanning order (Fig. 5E), i.e., starting from 3 to −3 V and then scanning back to 3 V or starting from 1 to −1 V and scanning back to 1 V, and the h-MOFNT exhibited the same fluidic memristive and learning property.

This memristive and learning property of the h-MOFNT can be explained by switching gating effect of internal electric potential in the MOF hierarchical phase on subsequent reversal transport of the proton under applied voltage, as shown in Fig. 5F. In state I (−2 V → 0 V), the local electric potential ΔE is rapidly generated because of the proton transphase conduction, and ΔE maintains at a high level for a short time duration even with the switching of voltage polarity although then decaying gradually. In state II (0 V → +2 V), the residual ΔE can favor proton transport driven by the applied positive voltage because they are in the same direction. Meanwhile, the reverse local potential can be generated gradually (noted as ΔE′), as the voltage bias increases above 0.3 V because of the accumulation of protons in fresh MOF ACs. Meanwhile, the accumulated protons in MOF ACs in state I can drift back to the MOF NCs facilitated by the applied voltage, making ΔE vanish in the end of state II. In state III (+2 V → 0 V), without the favorable superposition effect from ΔE, proton conduction driven by the applied voltage can be effectively compromised by ΔE′, resulting in the relatively lower current than that in state II. In state IV (0 V → –2 V), the residual ΔE′ can promote proton conduction due to the superposition effect of ΔE′ and the applied negative voltage, and ΔE is again established, which is analogous to that in state II. The evolution of the forward ΔE and reverse ΔE′ with the voltage bias scanning history (take a scanning rate of 200 mV/s as an example) is quantitatively illustrated in Fig. 5G. Overall, the h-MOFNT can memorize the in situ established ΔE and ΔE′ for about 10 s, and this fluidic ionic memory can be enhanced by a high-voltage scanning frequency, demonstrating the short-term memory property and a biomimetic plasticity learning manner of our nanofluidic transistor.

DISCUSSION

We demonstrated that a MOF-based nanofluidic device with multidimensional heterojunctions can achieve unprecedented nonlinear selective ion transport, i.e., nonlinear triode-like proton conduction versus diode-like metal ion conduction. Notably, the nonlinear proton transport with saturation phenomenon stems from the gating effect from the built-in electric potential across the MOF intrinsic NC-AC junctions enabled by transphase conduction specific for protons. The common rectifying metal ion conduction results from the MOF-polymer channel heterojunction. Although h-MOFNT exhibits promising iontronic properties as a nanofluidic memristor, with stunning biomimetic learning capacity, how to integrate this phenomenon into aqueous electrolyte-based iontronics for practical applications needs to be further investigated. Because the memristive nonlinear ion transport property comes from MOF intrinsic heterojunction structures, it is promising to design the advanced fluidic chip based on programming MOF of single or several unit cell thickness, which can provide an alternative method to address the downscale challenge of electronic chips. This work will possibly evoke future studies on tunable charged mass transport and nanofluidic iontronics by rational design of heterogeneous confinement systems for promising liquid-based information storage and even brain-like computing.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Preparation of MOF-based nanofluidic device

The single-channel amino-modified PET NC was used as the substrate to prepare h-MOFNT. The detailed preparation procedures for the substrate can be seen in the Supplementary Materials. A total of 0.402 g of H2BDC-SO3Na was dissolved in 5 ml of Milli-Q H2O (solution #1), and 0.347 g of ZrO(NO3)2·xH2O was dissolved in 5 ml of Milli-Q water (solution #2), which were both preheated to 90°C. The substrate film was clamped within a homemade interfacial synthesis device with two separate cells. The cell facing the tip side of the amino-modified PET NC was filled with 4.5 ml of solution #1, and the other cell facing the base side was filled with 4.5 ml of solution #2. Afterward, the device was sealed in a polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE)–lined autoclave, transferred into an oven preheated to 100°C, and then maintained for 48 hours. After the oven cooled to room temperature, the MOF-modified substrate film (i.e., h-MOFNT) was taken out, washed thoroughly with deionized water, and lastly dried at room temperature for the following I-V curve measurements. A multiple amino-PET NC film with a channel density of 108/cm2 was used to prepare multiple-channel h-MOFNTs for SEM and PXRD characterization.

Electrochemical ion transport measurements

The substrate polymer NCs or the h-MOFNT was placed into a nanofluidic measurement cell consisting of two separated PTFE chambers. The two reservoirs were filled with electrolyte solutions (HCl, HNO3, KCl, NaCl, LiCl, CaCl2, or CeCl3). We used Ag/AgCl electrodes in each chamber to apply voltage (VDS) across the substrate polymer NCs or h-MOFNT. The electrodes were connected to Keithley picoammeters (6517B/2636) with the anode placed in the cell facing the base side of the substrate or the h-MOFNT to obtain I-V curves. The voltage scanning started from −2 V, decreased to 0 V, and then increased to 2 V with the voltage polarity changing from negative to positive. Therefore, positive voltage values mean the external electric field applied to the base side of the membranes, while negative values correspond to the electric potential being applied to the tip side. Each test was performed at least three times to obtain the average current values at different voltages.

MD simulations

The unit cell of the MOF (Zr-MOF-SO3H) (41) was used in this work, as shown in fig. S14A. The unit cell is duplicated in the x direction to build a 2 × 1 × 1 supercell with a size of 83.07 Å by 41.535 Å by 41.63 Å (fig. S14B). Then, two water reservoirs of the same size as the supercell are created and placed on both sides of the supercell along the x direction (fig. S14, C and D). This “water reservoir–MOF–water reservoir” model is then relaxed in the NVT ensemble, where moles (N), volume (V), and temperature (T) are kept constant, for 1 ns, where the water molecules spontaneously enter the MOF phase. When stability is reached, the density of the water reservoirs is 0.97 g/cm3, which is consistent with that of the bulk density. Zr-MOF-SO3H and water molecules inside the MOF are extracted from the water reservoirs for the following simulations.

To reveal the ion distribution in MOF, two kinds of solution reservoirs (HCl and KCl) with a density of 1 g/cm3 and a concentration of 1 M were constructed. The reservoirs are the same size as the supercell of the MOF. After that, the solution reservoirs (HCl or KCl) are placed on both sides of the water-sucked Zr-MOF-SO3H along the x direction to create a “solution–water-sucked MOF–solution” model, as shown in fig. S14E. The simulation models are relaxed in the NVT ensemble under a series of electric fields ranging from 0 to 1 V/Å along the x direction for 1 ns. Owing to the ion concentration difference and electric field, the ions migrate into Zr-MOF-SO3H. In this regard, the distributions of H+ and K+ in the MOF and their concentrations in the MOF-NC and MOF-AC phases were investigated.

Ion mobility in the MOF phase

The Zr-MOF-SO3H and electrolyte solutions (HCl or KCl) are extracted from the final configurations of the distribution simulations. Moreover, to reduce the computational consumption of the mobility simulation, the right unit cell of this Zr-MOF-SO3H configuration is selected to construct a 41.535 Å–by–41.535 Å–by–41.63 Å simulation model with periodic boundaries, as shown in fig. S14C. Then, electric fields ranging from 0 to 1 V/Å are applied to the simulation model along the x direction under the NVT ensemble. Notably, the transport of H+ obeys the Grotthuss mechanism, so the velocity of H+ was calculated following the literature (44).

Local internal electric field in MOF with accumulated H+ in the MOF-AC phase

The Zr-MOF-SO3H wetted with electrolyte solutions extracted from the final configuration of the ion distribution simulation under 1 V/Å is used to calculate the local electric field in the MOF with accumulated H+ in the MOF-AC phase. The ions and water in the MOF-AC phase and the water in the MOF-NC phase are removed before the calculation to ensure that the electric fields are excited by Zr-MOF-SO3H and H+ in the MOF-NC phase. After removal, H+ is recognized as the probe to scan the simulation box with a sampling interval of 0.5 Å. During each scan, the Coulomb interaction of the probe is calculated and recorded to evaluate the strength of the built-in electric field. For comparison, the local electric field of the pure MOF phase was also calculated by the same method. The results of the internal electric fields in MOF with and without H+ in the angstrom pores in the MOF-AC phase are shown in Fig. 4C and fig. S19.

DFT calculations

The DFT calculations were performed by the Quantum ESPRESSO package with the projector augmented wave method (45). The Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof functional of the generalized gradient approximation was used to describe the exchange-correlation interaction (46). The Monkhorst-Pack grid of k-points used in the calculations was 1 × 1 × 1. Kinetic energy cutoffs of 60 and 480 rydberg (Ry) were chosen for the wave functions and the charge density, respectively. The DFT-D3 scheme was adopted to include the correction of van der Waals interactions (47). All structures were fully optimized until all atomic forces decreased to less than 1.5 × 10−3 Ry. The convergence criterion was set to 7.3 × 10−9 Ry for the total energy.

The correlated energy barriers of H+ and K+ transport in Zr-MOF-SO3H were determined by finding the minimum energy path (MEP) from one lattice site to an adjacent site using the climbing image nudged elastic band (CI-NEB) method as implemented in Quantum ESPRESSO (48, 49). The two end point (initial and final) configurations were obtained from MD simulations and fully relaxed afterward. Because two kinds of pores (micropores and mesopores) exist in Zr-MOF-SO3H, two corresponding combinations of end point configurations were selected for both H+ and K+, specifically, from micropores to micropores and from mesopores to mesopores. After obtaining the MEP between the initial and final states, a series of intermediate atomic configurations, known as images, is generated with a constant spacing between the two end points. We adopted 10 images for the calculations, and these images formed an “elastic band.” During the CI-NEB calculation, the forces acting on each image are determined by calculating the energy and gradients at each image position. These forces drive the images to move along the band, and the positions of the images were adjusted iteratively until the energy along the band was minimized and the transition state was found.
