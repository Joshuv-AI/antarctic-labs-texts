Ni-NiO Heterojunction: A Binder-Free Catalyst for Enhanced Oxygen Evolution Reaction
Authors: Nastaran Farahbakhsh, Majid Shahsanaei, Patrick Hartwich, Shiva Mohajernia, Manuela S. Killian, Sina Hejazi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
In this study, we applied a two-step electrochemical anodization process to produce highly porous nanostructured nickel suboxides. We then annealed these materials in different environments: air, Ar, and Ar/H2. Annealing in a reductive environment (Ar/H2) resulted in a Ni-NiO heterojunction with a high defect density, as confirmed by the Mott–Schottky analysis. Our results demonstrate that these defects and active sites significantly enhance the electrocatalytic activity for the oxygen evolution reaction (OER). Utilizing X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), field emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM), high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HR-TEM), and electrochemical analysis, we demonstrate that the heterojunction system containing Ni-NiO, formed through annealing in an Ar/H2 atmosphere, acts as a highly efficient electrocatalyst for the OER. This catalyst achieves an impressively low overpotential of 293 mV at 10 mA cm–2, a Tafel slope of 74 mV dec–1, and exhibits outstanding stability, maintaining performance over 1000 cycles. Notably, our most optimized NiO x electrode outperforms the conventional reference RuO2 electrode by a factor of 1.72. Our findings demonstrate the potential of binder-free Ni-NiO heterojunctions in developing high-performance electrocatalysts for alkaline electrolysis.

Introduction

Electrochemical water splitting offers a sustainable pathway to hydrogen, an essential fuel and chemical feedstock for overcoming global energy and environmental challenges. , The discovery of efficient electrocatalysts for water splitting, including the hydrogen evolution reaction (HER) and oxygen evolution reaction (OER), , is vital for developing clean and sustainable energy alternatives. Mainly, OER electrocatalysts present more complexities, as generating a single oxygen molecule requires transferring four electrons and removing four protons from water. − Although noble metal oxides like IrO2 and RuO2 demonstrate high activity for OER in acidic environments, their extensive application is restricted due to their high cost and scarcity. , Consequently, there has been a notable shift in focus toward exploring OER in alkaline environments, where electrocatalysts based on nonprecious transition metals like Ni, Co, and Fe have demonstrated both high activity and stability. ,

Transition metal oxides and hydroxides, such as NiOOH and CoOOH, are highly effective for the OER in alkaline media due to their ability to form in situ oxyhydroxide intermediates. , According to the Sabatier principle, the catalytic performance of these materials is closely linked to the metal–oxygen (M–O) bond strength, with optimal M–O bonds enhancing OER efficiency. , Various fabrication methods for NiO have been explored, including atomic layer deposition, chemical vapor deposition, electrochemical deposition, and hydrothermal reactions. , Despite their high surface area, traditional NiO nanoparticles are hindered by issues like agglomeration and the necessity for binders, which compromise their overall efficiency and conductivity. Anodic growth techniques offer a promising alternative by enabling the direct formation of highly ordered NiO nanostructures on metallic substrates, eliminating binder issues, enhancing electron transfer, and significantly improving electrocatalytic performance in alkaline electrolyzers. ,,

Beyond synthesis methods, optimizing defects and interfaces is essential for further enhancing the catalytic performance of transition metal oxides. , Ni active centers and point defects improve electronic and interfacial properties, boost conductivity, and enhance charge transfer, which is crucial for electrocatalytic efficiency. , Ni­(OH)2, particularly in the form of NiOOH, is noted for its superior electrocatalytic behavior in the OER, with NiOOH identified as the main active component in alkaline conditions. , Some studies show that the concentration of Ni3+ in Ni x Co3–x O4 improves OER performance by shifting the valence band and facilitating OH* intermediate adsorption. , Similarly, constructing heterojunctions can optimize the catalytic performance. For instance, developing a NiO/RuO2 heterojunction on nickel foam enhances activity and stability for water splitting. Another study engineered a Ni/NiO hierarchical heterojunction, improving reaction kinetics and conductivity. These studies demonstrate that interface engineering effectively modulates local electronic structures, leading to new catalytic sites and improved conductivity and catalytic activity. Mirshokraee et al. reported that Ar/H2 treatment and pyrolysis significantly influence the evolution of active sites in electrocatalysts derived from Ni-phthalocyanine, leading to nanoparticle formation at higher temperatures. Analysis using the X-ray absorption near edge structure (XANES) and extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) confirmed the absence of oxygen interaction. Furthermore, electrochemical results indicated enhanced HER activity due to the formation of nickel nanoparticles. Similarly, Wang et al. employed electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) to investigate defect structures in Ni-NiO catalysts reduced at different temperatures, revealing that the Ni-NiO/450 sample possesses a significantly higher concentration of defects, including oxygen vacancies and Ni2+ deficiencies. These defects, induced by partial reduction, are closely associated with forming Ni-NiO heterojunctions and are crucial for enhancing catalytic performance through improved metal-oxide interactions.

In this study, we synthesized nanostructured NiO using a simple anodization technique. We then annealed the samples in various environments to systematically enhance the density of the OER active sites, fine-tune the binding energies to balance intermediate adsorption and desorption, and form Ni-NiO heterojunctions under reductive conditions. We assessed the electrocatalytic performance of the NiO x electrodes by analyzing their initial chemical composition, including higher oxidation states of nickel, active nickel sites, point defects, and microstructural characteristics. We evaluated the catalytic performance using linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) and determined the chemical state and composition through X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurements. Additionally, we examined the morphology of the nanostructures by using field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM) and high-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM).

Experimental Section

Synthesis of NiO Nanostructures

Ni foil (99.9% pure) with a 0.125 mm thickness was sectioned into 1.5 × 1.5 cm pieces. The samples were first sonicated in ethanol, then in deionized water for 5 min, and finally dried under N2 flow. The anodic structure was produced via a two-step anodization process using a two-electrode setup, where the Ni foil acted as the anode and Pt served as the cathode. The Ni foil was pressed against the O-ring aperture of the electrochemical cell to establish contact with the electrolyte.

To begin, the samples were electrochemically polished through anodization in a solution made of 0.5 M HCl and 2 vol % deionized water in ethylene glycol. This 15-min procedure at 6 V and 20 °C effectively removed the residual oxide layer from the metal foil, ensuring the homogeneous growth of NiO during subsequent anodic oxidation. After being electropolished, the Ni foil was rinsed with deionized water and isopropanol to eliminate any remaining electrolytes.

In the subsequent step, nanosponge NiO layers were developed using an electrolyte containing 0.5 M ammonium fluoride (NH4F) in 85% phosphoric acid (H3PO4), at a voltage of 6 V and a temperature of 20 °C for 3 h using a power supply (Keithley 2000). Immediately after the anodization process, the electrodes were thoroughly rinsed with ethanol, immersed in ethanol for 10 min, and subsequently dried under a nitrogen gas flow. ,

Thermal Annealing of Electrodes in Different Environments

To investigate how the annealing atmosphere influences the crystallinity of anodic structures, samples were annealed in air, Ar, and Ar/H2 (95/5 vol %) at 450 °C using a heating rate of 5 °C/min for 12 h and labeled as NiO Air, NiO Ar, and NiO Ar/H2.

Preparation of References: RuO2/Ni Foil and NiO Powder

For comparative analysis, the benchmark electrode utilizing nickel foil was produced by coating it with RuO2 at a density of 10 mg/cm2 for the OER. A total of 10 mg of RuO2 catalyst was combined with 500 μL of ethanol and 20 μL of Nafion, then ultrasonicated for 30 min to create a uniform ink solution. Subsequently, 130 μL of this ink solution was drop-cast onto nickel foil and dried in a vacuum oven at 60 °C. This procedure was repeated in a fourth step. The same process was performed for preparing the NiO powder reference electrode using 10 mg/cm2 NiO powder.

Characterizations

X-ray diffraction (XRD) was performed using a Panalytical X’Pert Pro diffractometer with a Cu Kα source to investigate the phase structure of the NiO samples after various annealing treatments. The electrode structures were examined using a Zeiss 55 field emission scanning electron microscope (FE-SEM), and elemental composition analysis was carried out with an energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) detector for the as-anodized Ni, NiO Air, NiO Ar, and NiO Ar/H2 electrodes. A scanning transmission electron microscope (S/TEM, FEI Talos F200X) was utilized for high-resolution structural analysis.

The chemical composition of nanostructured NiO and surface constituents was analyzed through X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) using a Surface Science Instruments SSX-100 S-probe photoelectron/ESCA spectrometer. Monochromatized Al Kα radiation was employed for the XPS measurements, with core-level binding energies calibrated to the C 1s reference peak set at 284.8 eV.

Electrochemical Measurements

Electrochemical tests were conducted using a BioLogic SP-200 electrochemical device in a standard three-electrode system. The experiments were performed in a 1 M KOH electrolyte solution with platinum foil as the counter electrode and a Hg/HgO electrode (in 1 M KOH) as the reference electrode (its potential was routinely verified and adjusted as necessary). Potentials measured for this reference electrode were recalibrated to the reversible hydrogen electrode (RHE) scale using the eq: 1 ERHE=EHg/HgO+0.059×pH+EHg/HgO0

The potential of the reference electrode relative to the working electrode is denoted as EHg/HgO , while EHg/HgO0 represents the standard half-cell reduction potential of the Hg/HgO electrode, which is 0.0983 V vs the standard hydrogen electrode (SHE) at 25 °C. Additionally, the inherent resistance of the electrolyte (R s), determined from the electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS), was accounted for by applying iR compensation to adjust the potentials accordingly. The associated overpotential, η, was derived using the eq: 2 η=ERHE−iRs−1.23V where i is the current.

The catalysts were evaluated based on a specified testing protocol in 1 M KOH. This protocol encompassed the measurements of the open-circuit potential (OCP) for 15 min, electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) across a frequency range of 100 kHz to 100 mHz with a signal modulation amplitude approximating 10 mV around the OCP, cyclic voltammograms (CV) at a scan rate of 10 mV s–1, and linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) at a consistent scan rate of 1 mV s–1 with 85% iR correction. Mott–Schottky impedance evaluations were performed utilizing an AC amplitude of 5 mV RMS spanning a frequency range from 200 kHz to 1 Hz. The applied potential ranged from −0.5 to 0.6 V vs Hg/HgO in a 1 M KOH solution at 25 °C. Mott–Schottky plots were then generated from the data at a frequency of 15 kHz. The electrochemical capacitance of the electrodes was evaluated within a potential range of 0.05 to 0.25 V vs Hg/HgO, using scan rates varying from 10 to 100 mV s–1. Stability measurements were conducted using cyclic voltammetry (CV) between 0.35 and 0.75 V vs Hg/HgO at a scan rate of 100 mV s–1 for 1000 cycles, and LSV was performed before and after CV cycling at a scan rate of 10 mV s–1. In addition, chronopotentiometry (CP) was carried out at a current density of 10 mA cm–2 for 24 h to assess the OER stability.

Results and Discussion

Electrochemical anodization is a straightforward technique for creating self-organized nanosponge oxide structures on various metal substrates such as Ni, Ti, and others. Sultan et al. demonstrated an approach to obtain anodic NiO nanosponge with a tunable thickness of ∼3 μm, which resulted in optimized charge-transfer characteristics. In their study, they annealed the anodized nanosponge in air. To engineer active sites in the porous oxide layer and examine their effects on the electrode’s catalytic performance, we controlled the annealing environment in this study while keeping the anodization conditions consistent. The as-anodized Ni was annealed in oxidative (air), inert (Ar), and reductive (Ar/H2) environments and is subsequently referred to as NiO Air, NiO Ar, and NiO Ar/H2.

Precise gravimetric measurements were conducted using an analytical balance (±0.01 mg) to evaluate the active material loading, as summarized in Table S1. The Ni foil with an exposed area of 1 cm2 weighed 89.51 ± 0.01 mg before anodization and 87.80 ± 0.01 mg after the formation of the NiO nanosponge layer, indicating a net mass loss of 1.71 ± 0.02 mg cm–2. This reduction is attributed to Ni dissolution during both the electropolishing and anodization steps, where oxygen incorporation does not fully compensate for the metal loss, as both dissolution and oxidation processes occur simultaneously. Following postannealing under different atmospheres, the recorded weights showed minimal variation (≤±0.1 mg), indicating negligible mass change during thermal treatment.

The morphology of the nanosponge NiO films, synthesized via the two-step anodization of Ni foils and subjected to annealing treatments to enhance their electrocatalytic properties and crystallinity in three different environments, is shown in Figure a–f. Due to nickel’s high reactivity during anodization, the Ni foil tends to heat up, leading to excessive etching, which can hinder oxide formation. To address this, the anodization process was conducted at a controlled temperature, with the Ni substrate cooled throughout. This technique ensured consistent pore distribution (Figure S1a).

The composition of the nanostructured NiO was assessed through XRD, EDS, and XPS, with variations depending on the annealing environment. Figure a presents the XRD patterns of as-anodized Ni, NiO Air, NiO Ar, and NiO Ar/H2. All electrodes exhibit broad characteristic diffraction peaks, ascribed to the nanostructured nature of the oxide layer. Moreover, a pronounced peak associated with metallic Ni (JCPDS no. 04-0850) is evident in all samples. Three prominent peaks at 44.5°, 51.8°, and 76.4° correspond to the (111), (200), and (220) crystalline planes of Ni, respectively. The XRD pattern of the as-anodized Ni sample reveals the formation of partially crystalline NiF2 (JCPDS 74-2140). Four distinct diffraction peaks observed at 27.1°, 34.8°, 40.1°, and 55.8° correspond to the (110), (101), (111), and (220) crystalline planes of NiF2, respectively. However, the predominant feature in the nonannealed sample is the broad peak shape, suggesting the presence of amorphous NiO and signifying the low crystallinity of the as-prepared material. Signals characteristic of NiO emerge postthermal treatment at 450 °C in air, Ar, and Ar/H2 environments. This indicates the conversion of amorphous Ni x F y O z /NiF2 to partially crystalline nickel oxide. Research has demonstrated that small NiO crystallites initially develop within an amorphous NiO matrix upon annealing of the anodic nanosponge. , After thermal annealing in the air or an inert environment, diffraction peaks corresponding to NiO (JCPDS 47-1049) become evident. The peaks observed at 37.2°, 43.3°, 62.8°, and 75.4° are associated with the (111), (200), (220), and (311) crystalline planes of cubic NiO, respectively. Nevertheless, the absence of crystalline NiO is noted after annealing in the reductive Ar/H2 environment.

The EDS of these samples (cf. Figure b) shows that the main constituents are Ni, O, and F, accompanied by a small amount of P and C. The as-anodized Ni sample shows Ni, O, traces of C and P, and large amounts of F. The atomic percentages of Ni, O, and F were 22%, 19%, and 44%, respectively (see Table S2). The large amount of fluoride in as-anodized Ni is related to the anodization electrolyte, which contained NH4F. In the literature, a conversion to NiO x upon annealing in air was described in detail. Interestingly, annealing in inert or reductive environments decreases the amount of F substantially; the atomic proportion of F after thermal treatment in air, Ar, and Ar/H2 decreased to 3, 5, and 3%. The results demonstrate that following annealing in both air and Ar environments, the atomic percentage of O increased to 47% for the NiO Air sample and 51% for the NiO Ar sample. The lower amount of oxygen (24 wt %) in the NiO Ar/H2 sample was attributed to the reducing environment.

The XPS survey spectra (Figure S3a) indicate the presence of Ni 2p, O 1s, and C 1s signals on the surface of the catalysts. Small signals attributed to the presence of F and P are also detected, originating from the anodization of the electrolyte containing NH4F and H3PO4. The chemical states of Ni 2p, O 1s, and F 1s are examined in detail through high-resolution XPS spectra. Ni 2p3/2) and 879–880 eV (Sat. Ni 2p1/2) each. , In the NiO Ar/H2 sample, the observed broadening of the Ni 2p peak provides evidence of metallic Ni.

Table S3 presents the atomic percent distribution for all annealing environments, while detailed spectra of the Ni 2p region are visualized in Figure c–e. The deconvolution of XPS peaks reveals the formation of Ni–O and Ni–OH in the Ni 2p spectra for NiO Air, NiO Ar, and NiO Ar/H2 (Figure c–e). The deconvoluted signals of the Ni 2p region indicate the presence of multiple chemical states, specifically Ni2+ and Ni3+, associated with Ni–O (at 854–855 eV) and Ni–OH (at 856–857 eV), respectively. ,− Additionally, a signal attributed to metallic Ni (Ni0) at a binding energy of 853 eV is exclusively observed for NiO Ar/H2 (Figure e). The presence of metallic species is consistent with annealing in a reducing environment, as reported in the literature. The percentage of Ni as Ni0 2p3/2 in the NiO Ar/H2 sample is 10%, indicating that some Ni nanoparticles are converted into their metallic form during annealing in the reducing environment.

The high-resolution spectrum of the O 1s region for each sample and the overlay of the O 1s spectra for all samples (Figure S3c) reveal three oxygen contributions: Ni–O, Ni–OH, and ads H2O, as shown in Figure f–h. The deconvoluted O (Ni–O) peak at 529–530 eV corresponds to the characteristic metal–oxygen bond. The O (Ni–OH) peak, observed at 531–532 eV, is typically linked to defect sites characterized by reduced oxygen coordination and the presence of oxygen species adsorbed on the surface, such as hydroxides. Furthermore, the O (ads H2O) peak at 532–533 eV is typically attributed to physically or chemically adsorbed water on or within the interface of the material. The atomic percent distribution determined by XPS and the chemical composition of oxygen-related compounds in NiO samples are shown in Table S3.

The NiO Air and NiO Ar samples show residual fluoride, specifically 1.9 and 2.2 at.% (Table S3), respectively, indicating possible contamination by fluoride. However, previous studies have shown that this effect is limited to the surface, and air annealing effectively removes fluoride from the entire oxide structure. The F 1s region (Figure S3d) shows a significant reduction in fluoride content, down to 0.5 at.% after annealing in Ar/H2 environments.

The analysis using XPS, EDS, FE-SEM, STEM, and HR-TEM indicates that the annealing process causes structural and compositional changes, resulting in the formation of a Ni-NiO heterojunction in a reducing environment.

Electrochemical Catalytic Activity for OER

Electrocatalytic characterization was performed to evaluate the activity of the nanostructured NiO for the OER in 1 M KOH electrolyte with a three-electrode system: Nanostructured NiO, Hg/HgO electrode, and Pt foil were employed as the working, reference, and counter electrodes, respectively. The electrocatalytic reactions depend on both the carrier concentration (defects) and the charge transfer capacity of the electrocatalyst sample. Mott–Schottky plots were employed to investigate changes in the density of carrier concentration (ND) during the changing annealing environment (Figure a). The NiO Air sample shows a typical ND value of 1.715 × 1024 cm–3, which increases to 7.386 × 1024 cm–3 for NiO Ar. After annealing in Ar/H2, the ND for NiO Ar/H2 increases approximately 38.2-fold to 0.282 × 1027 cm–3, consistent with the effects of the reducing environment on defect density. Electrochemical analysis successfully confirms that annealing in a reductive environment, particularly in the NiO Ar/H2 sample, enhances the concentration of active sites involved in the oxygen evolution process.

(a) Mott–Schottky plots at f = 15 kHz for NiO Air, NiO Ar, and NiO Ar/H2; (b,c) LSV polarization curves and Tafel slopes of NiO Air, NiO Ar, and NiO Ar/H2, RuO2/Ni foil, NiO powder, and blank Ni foil in 1 M KOH at a scan rate of 1 mV s–1; (d) Nyquist plots of NiO Air, NiO Ar, and NiO Ar/H2 (with the equivalent circuit model shown in the inset), and the surface area is consistent across all samples; (e,f) electrochemical stability tests of the NiO Air, NiO Ar, and NiO Ar/H2 samples: (e) 1000 cycles of cyclic voltammetry curves for each sample, (f) iR-corrected polarization curves of each sample at a scan rate of 10 mV s–1, both initially and after 1000 cycles; inset of (f): chronopotentiometry curve recorded at a current density of 10 mA cm–2 for 24 h for NiO Air and NiO Ar/H2.

To assess the electrocatalytic performance of the OER for nanostructured NiO electrodes, LSV polarization was conducted. For comparison, the electrocatalytic activities of blank Ni foil, NiO powder, and a benchmark catalyst, RuO2 on Ni foil (termed as RuO2/Ni foil), were also evaluated. Figure b and Table S4 present the iR-corrected LSV polarization curves recorded in 1 M KOH at a scan rate of 1 mV s–1. The anodically grown NiO Air electrode showed a significant improvement in electrocatalytic activity compared with the Ni foil substrate and the NiO powder electrode. The NiO Air nanosponge achieved an overpotential of 422 mV at a current density of 10 mA cm–2, whereas the Ni foil substrate and NiO powder electrode required 576 mV and 536 mV, respectively, to reach the same current density (η10 = 576 and 536 mV). Notably, by changing the annealing environment to a reductive atmosphere, the NiO Ar/H2 sample demonstrates outstanding catalytic efficacy for the OER. It achieves a remarkably low overpotential of 293 mV at a current density of 10 mA cm–2, comparable to the 263 mV achieved by RuO2/Ni foil.

The NiO Ar sample required an overpotential of 405 mV to reach a current density of 10 mA cm–2, notably higher than that of the NiO Ar/H2 sample. This difference emphasizes the advantages of a reductive atmosphere, which substantially increases the number of active sites and surface defects on the nanostructured electrode.

The LSV polarization curves display an oxidation peak at approximately 1.41 V vs RHE preceding the sharp rise in current density due to the water oxidation reaction during oxygen evolution (inset in Figure b). This oxidation peak arises from the formation of the active site NiOOH, which results from the oxidation of Ni2+ to Ni3+. NiOOH is recognized as the active site that catalyzes the OER. As a result, the intensity of this distinctive peak, specifically the area enclosed by the peak, indicates the quantity of NiOOH generated in the system. This demonstrates a positive correlation with the efficiency of the electrocatalyst. However, for the NiO Ar/H2 sample, small redox peaks are evident, suggesting the lowest overpotentials. These are associated with a notable shift of the oxidation peak toward a less positive region. Furthermore, the oxidation peak of the redox reaction for this sample is shifted to a lower potential (compared to the NiO Air and NiO Ar samples), reaching approximately 1.39 V vs RHE. This shift indicates improved electrical conductivity after annealing in the Ar/H2 environment. According to the literature, , the count of electrochemically available Ni atoms can be determined by the charge associated with the Ni2+/Ni3+ redox reaction, as represented by the eq: 3 Ni(OH)2+OH−↔NiOOH+H2O+e−

As shown in Figure b, much smaller redox peaks can be found on the NiO Ar at higher potential, indicating that the electrochemically accessible Ni atoms in the samples with annealing in the Ar environment are negligible.

The kinetic properties of electrodes are commonly assessed and quantified by using Tafel slopes. The Tafel slope provides insights into the electrocatalyst’s ability to enhance the current density with the rise in overpotential, indicating the necessary potential increase to amplify the current density 10-fold. A lower Tafel slope is preferable, as it allows for higher current densities at reduced overpotentials, resulting in more efficient electricity usage. The results are illustrated in Figure c and concisely summarized in Table S4 for easy comparison. The sample NiO Ar/H2 outperforms all other electrodes, boasting a Tafel slope of 74 mV dec–1. The Tafel slopes for the NiO Air, NiO Ar, NiO powder, and Ni foil electrodes are 192, 144, 278, and 245 mV dec–1, respectively. The reduced Tafel slope of the NiO Ar/H2 sample contributes to its low overpotentials, even at high current densities. The Tafel slope value for NiO Ar/H2, notably better than the reference electrode RuO2/Ni foil (which has a Tafel slope of 118 mV dec–1), highlights the efficient kinetics for the OER activity.

Mott–Schottky analysis was conducted to determine the defect density of the NiO Air, NiO Ar, and NiO Ar/H2 electrodes and examine their correlation with key catalytic performance metrics, including overpotential and Tafel slope, as shown in Figure S4. The results indicate a significant correlation between defect density and catalytic performance, as demonstrated by a linear fit (R 2 = 0.97) between defect density and overpotential. This suggests that the overpotential decreases as the defect density increases for the NiO Ar/H2 electrode. Moreover, a polynomial relationship between the defect density and Tafel slope was observed, with a higher defect density leading to a lower Tafel slope.

The enhanced catalytic activity observed in the Ni-NiO heterojunction is attributed to the synergistic effects of interface engineering. Previous studies show that adding Ni induces electronic interactions that enhance catalytic activity, as supported by density functional theory. This approach proves effective for developing high-performance electrocatalysts for OER and water splitting. Additionally, studies of NiO x films exposed to oxygen and water vapor at high temperatures reveal that oxygen vacancies form and are filled with OH during heating. Repeated thermal cycles lead to stable NiOOH formation and surface restructuring. DFT calculations further suggest that oxygen vacancies and Ni0 create a state within the NiO band gap, influencing the Fermi edge. These findings are consistent with experimental observations and reinforce the proposed catalytic enhancement mechanisms.

To conduct further assessment and comparative analysis of the electrocatalytic kinetics of the annealed NiO samples, measurements of EIS were performed (Figure d). The results obtained from the EIS spectra offer insights into the charge transfer resistances at the electrode–electrolyte interface, a critical parameter for evaluating the OER performance of the electrode. The EIS measurements were carried out at an overpotential of 300 mV (iR-corrected) over a frequency range of 10 kHz to 0.1 Hz in 1 M KOH.

The Nyquist plots for the NiO Air, NiO Ar, and NiO Ar/H2 electrodes, along with the corresponding equivalent circuit model shown in the inset, are displayed in Figure d. The Nyquist plot features a semicircle in the high-frequency range, indicating R ct and illustrating the resistance associated with charge transfer at the electrode–electrolyte interface. In contrast, the low-frequency region shows a near-vertical line, suggesting a diffusion-limited process. Notably, among the tested electrodes, the NiO Ar/H2 electrode exhibited the lowest R ct value at 28.4 Ω, compared to 53.2 Ω for NiO Air and 389 Ω for NiO Ar, demonstrating higher charge-transfer kinetics. Additionally, the nearly vertical alignment of the NiO Ar/H2 Nyquist plot along the imaginary axis indicates minimal diffusion resistance and a highly capacitive nature. The reduced resistance of this electrode, in comparison to those of the others, implies improved electron mobility and enhanced charge transport efficiency throughout the material. Its defect structure primarily influences the diverse electrical properties of nanostructured NiO. Additional details, including the EIS parameters derived from the equivalent circuit models, are provided in Table S5.

The electronic properties of the NiO samples, which are critical for their OER performance, exhibit variations influenced by defect and interface engineering. The parameters extracted from EIS data (Text S1 and Table S5) enable the estimation of dielectric properties. These properties suggest changes in the oxide layer’s electronic structure, with the NiO Ar/H2 sample indicating enhanced electronic conductivity. In contrast to the semiconductive characteristics of NiO Air and NiO Ar, which can limit electron transport and catalytic activity, NiO Ar/H2, featuring a Ni-NiO heterojunction, displays semimetallic behavior. The semimetallic nature of NiO Ar/H2 enhances electron mobility, facilitating faster charge transfer and improving the adsorption and transformation of the OER intermediates, particularly during the rate-determining O–O bond formation step. Mott–Schottky analysis indicates that NiO Ar/H2 has a higher donor density, increasing the charge carrier availability. This correlates with decreased charge transfer resistance (R ct), an improved Tafel slope, and overall better OER activity.

Cyclic voltammograms (CVs) at different scan rates were recorded in the nonfaradaic region for NiO Air, NiO Ar, and NiO Ar/H2 to estimate the double-layer capacitance (C dl), as shown in (Figure S5a–c). Capacitive currents at 1 V vs RHE were plotted against scan rates, and linear fitting was applied to determine C dl (Figure S5d). The C dl values for NiO Air, NiO Ar, and NiO Ar/H2 were approximately 5.4 mF cm–2, 0.9 mF cm–2, and 0.2 mF cm–2, respectively. These correspond to electrochemically active surface areas (ECSA) of ∼135 cm2, 22.5 cm2, and 5 cm2, respectively, assuming a specific capacitance (C s) of 40 μF cm–2. The higher C dl of the NiO Air electrode is attributed to its nanosponge and mesoporous structure, which provides a larger electrochemical surface area, facilitating more significant ion adsorption and a higher double-layer capacitance. In contrast, the NiO Ar/H2 electrode exhibits a lower C dl due to surface sintering, which reduces the effective surface area. However, despite its lower C dl, the NiO Ar/H2 electrode demonstrates enhanced OER activity, likely due to its defect-rich surface and formation of a Ni-NiO heterojunction. This heterojunction, along with the nanoparticles created on the surface during annealing in a reductive environment, introduces additional active sites, which facilitate better oxygen evolution kinetics.

Performance stability is crucial when evaluating the suitability of the OER electrodes in alkaline electrolytes. Initially, the NiO Ar/H2 electrodes, along with the NiO Air and NiO Ar samples, were subjected to 1000 CV scans (see Figure e). Subsequently, the polarization curves were measured before and after the procedure, as illustrated in Figure f. In general, the NiO Ar/H2 electrode shows a commendable performance during the test. Notably, the most active NiO sample also demonstrates the most stable performance. The long-term stability of the electrodes was also assessed by monitoring the potential over time at a constant current density of 10 mA cm–2. As depicted in the inset of Figure f, both NiO Air and NiO Ar/H2 maintain their activity with minimal degradation after 24 h.

Conclusions

In this study, enhanced electrocatalytic activity in the oxygen evolution reaction using binder-free Ni-NiO heterojunction is demonstrated. The nanostructured NiO was successfully synthesized on a Ni foil substrate through a straightforward two-step anodic growth technique, coupled with defect engineering via heat treatment in various environments (air and Ar), with a particular emphasis on Ar/H2 reductive atmospheres. The nanostructured NiO Ar/H2 displays superior efficiency for oxygen evolution, characterized by a minimal overpotential and noteworthy electrochemical durability. Overall, the most optimized NiO x electrode demonstrated a Tafel slope of 74 mV dec–1 in 1.0 M KOH, which is significantly lower than that of the conventional reference RuO2 electrode (with a slope of 118 mV dec–1). The carrier concentration density in NiO Ar/H2 improved by a factor of 38.2 compared to the other electrodes, illustrating the impact of the reducing environment on the defect density. Drawing from the insights in this paper, it is evident that the combined influence of (i) surface modification, i.e., introducing optimum defect density and active centers, and (ii) the advantageous geometrical attributes of the binder-free Ni-NiO heterojunction, are pivotal in boosting charge collection efficiency. This synergy offers excellent potential for developing advanced electrodes optimized for the oxygen evolution reaction.
