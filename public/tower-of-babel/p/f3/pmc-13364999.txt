Initiation of Collapse in Osteonecrosis of the Femoral Head: A Patient‐Specific Finite Element Simulation Study
Authors: Kosei Sakamoto, Goro Motomura, Ryosuke Yamaguchi, Takeshi Utsunomiya, Yusuke Ayabe, Yasuharu Nakashima
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCollapse in osteonecrosis of the femoral head (ONFH) is thought to result from stress concentration at the sclerotic boundary zone, but its precise initiation site has not been identified. We aimed to identify the initiation of collapse using patient‐specific finite element models (FEMs) that incorporate the sclerotic boundary zone. Ten hips from 10 patients with type C ONFH who had both pre‐collapse (stage 2) and post‐collapse (stage 3) computed tomography (CT) images were analyzed. Actual collapse sites were identified on post‐collapse CT images or, when inconclusive, determined using micro‐CT and subsequent histopathological examination of resected specimens. Patient‐specific FEMs were constructed from pre‐collapse CT scans with incorporation of the sclerotic boundary zone. For comparison, FEMs were also generated for four femoral heads without sclerotic changes on the articular surface. Equivalent stress on the articular surface and simulated initiation sites of collapse were evaluated and compared with actual collapse sites. In all 10 femoral heads, actual collapse occurred at the interface between the lateral sclerotic boundary and the adjacent necrotic lesion. FEMs demonstrated stress concentration at the sclerotic boundary, and simulated initiation sites corresponded to actual collapse sites. In contrast, femoral heads without sclerotic changes showed no stress concentration at the articular surface, and no simulated collapse was observed on the femoral head surface. In conclusion, collapse in ONFH initiates at the interface between the lateral sclerotic boundary and the adjacent necrotic lesion, highlighting the critical biomechanical role of the sclerotic boundary zone.

Introduction

Osteonecrosis of the femoral head (ONFH) often leads to collapse of the femoral head, resulting in hip joint deformity and functional disability [1, 2, 3]. Therefore, preventing collapse is important for joint preservation [4]. Currently, treatments such as core decompression and cell therapy aim to prevent collapse by promoting regeneration of necrotic bone [5, 6]. However, the underlying mechanism of collapse remains unclear, and a better understanding of this mechanism is essential for developing effective preventive strategies.

Regarding the mechanisms of collapse, Bullough and DiCarlo [1] hypothesized that collapse is initiated by stress concentration at the junction between the thickened sclerotic trabeculae of the reparative zone and the necrotic trabeculae. Subsequently, several studies using finite element models (FEMs) have demonstrated that sclerotic changes induce stress concentrations at this boundary [7, 8]. Histological analyses have also shown that, from the very early to the advanced post‐collapse stage, collapse consistently occurs adjacent to the lateral sclerotic boundary of the necrotic lesion [9, 10]. These findings suggest that the sclerotic boundary of the necrotic lesion may play an important role in the initiation of collapse. However, no study has directly identified the initiation site of collapse in pre‐collapse femoral heads, and therefore the mechanism of collapse has not yet been fully elucidated.

Finite element analysis (FEA) is well suited for investigating mechanical failure mechanisms because it enables patient‐specific biomechanical simulations based on computed tomography (CT) data [11, 12, 13]. In fracture analysis, FEMs are typically constructed from pre‐fracture bone and used to simulate failure progression through nonlinear analyses [14, 15, 16, 17, 18]. Importantly, the validity of such models has been demonstrated in studies showing strong agreement between FEM‐predicted fracture loads or patterns and results from mechanical testing of femoral and radial bone [16, 17]. However, despite this established validity of FEM in failure analysis, collapse simulation in ONFH has not yet been performed.

The aim of this study was to identify the initiation of collapse in ONFH using patient‐specific FEMs that incorporate the sclerotic boundary zone.

Methods

This study was a retrospective comparative study (Level of Evidence: III) and was approved by the institutional review board of our hospital (approval date: 19 August 2022; approval no.: 22462). The study protocol adhered to the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki (2024). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Patients

Ten hips from 10 patients who visited our institution between January 2016 and December 2023 and were diagnosed with pre‐collapse ONFH (case nos. 1–10) were enrolled in this study. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) meeting the Japanese Investigation Committee of Health and Welfare (JIC) diagnostic criteria for ONFH based on plain radiography and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) findings [19]; (2) availability of both asymptomatic pre‐collapse (stage 2) and post‐collapse (stage 3) CT images; and (3) necrotic lesions classified as type C using anteroposterior radiography or MRI. All asymptomatic pre‐collapse (stage 2) CT images were retrospectively identified from routine preoperative CT examinations performed when the contralateral hip had progressed to collapse and surgical treatment was being considered. At our institution, preoperative CT imaging for ONFH routinely includes the entire pelvis to enable comprehensive anatomical evaluation and surgical planning. As a result, the asymptomatic hip without collapse was included within the field of view. The present study retrospectively utilized these clinically acquired images; no additional CT scans were obtained for research purposes. The interval between the asymptomatic pre‐collapse (stage 2) CT and the post‐collapse (stage 3) CT corresponded to the clinically observed progression to collapse, with a mean interval of 19 ± 22 months (range, 4–79 months). The stage and type classification of ONFH were based on a system developed by the JIC [19, 20]. In stage 1, no specific findings of osteonecrosis were observed on radiography. However, specific findings were observed using MRI, bone scintigraphy, or histology. In stage 2, demarcating sclerosis was observed on radiography without femoral head collapse. In the present study, progression to stage 3 was determined when either a collapsed contour of the femoral head was identified on radiography or CT, or when MRI revealed bone marrow edema distal to the band pattern, based on previous reports indicating that such MRI findings are strongly associated with subchondral fracture and symptomatic progression [10, 21, 22]. The type classification was based on T1‐weighted MRI images or anteroposterior radiographs [19, 20]. To comprehensively evaluate the necrotic area, anteroposterior radiographs were used for staging in stage 2 and beyond, while the mid‐coronal slice of MRI was used for assessment in stage 1 patients. Type C lesions occupy more than the medial two‐thirds of the weight‐bearing portion. Type B lesions exist between one‐third and two‐thirds of the weight‐bearing portion, and type A lesions occupy the medial one‐third or less of the weight‐bearing portion [20].

For biomechanical comparison, we additionally analyzed four patients without radiographic sclerotic changes at the articular surface in the FEA. These cases were included to illustrate stress distribution patterns in lesions lacking a sclerotic boundary. Two normal contralateral femoral heads were obtained from patients with unilateral ONFH (case nos. I and II), and one stage 2 type A femoral head with ONFH (case no. III), in which sclerotic changes were present at the necrotic boundary but did not extend to the articular weight‐bearing surface, was also included. These three femoral heads were followed clinically and radiographically for a minimum of 10 years (range, 10–17 years), during which no collapse was observed. One stage 1 type C femoral head with ONFH (case no. IV) was included to represent a lesion without radiographic sclerosis and subsequently progressed to collapse within 8 months. The demographic data for both the stage 2 type C group and the group without sclerotic changes on the articular surface are summarized in Supplementary Table S1.

Computed Tomography and Micro‐Computed Tomography

At our institution, patients with hip disease undergo helical CT using the same method, with a 512 × 512 matrix, a field of view of 320–400 mm for axial images, and a slice thickness of 1–2 mm (Aquilion ONE; Canon Medical Systems Corporation, Tochigi, Japan). During the scan, the patients were placed in the supine position with the hip and knee joints fully extended, and the lower legs were secured to the table using a below‐knee splint to maintain rotational position.

After surgery, the entire resected femoral head was analyzed using micro‐computed tomography (micro‐CT) (R_mCT T1; Rigaku, Tokyo, Japan) prior to pathological sectioning. Scanning parameters were as follows: tube voltage, 60 kV; tube current, 60 μA; spatial resolution, 50 μm per pixel; and slice thickness, 0.4 mm.

Evaluation of Actual Collapse Sites in Post‐Collapse Images

Actual collapse sites were evaluated using post‐collapse CT images to determine whether they were located at the interface between the lateral sclerotic boundary and the adjacent necrotic lesion based on previous reports [9, 10]. Post‐collapse in vivo CT scans obtained during the early phase after symptom onset were primarily used to identify the actual collapse site, because these scans were considered to best reflect the initial morphology of collapse with minimal temporal delay from symptom onset. When the collapse sites were inconclusive on in vivo CT images, micro‐CT images and histopathological examinations of the surgically resected femoral head after collapse were used for assessment. Micro‐CT data were available for five of the 10 stage 2 type C femoral heads. In the five cases in which micro‐CT data were not available, detailed evaluation using micro‐CT was difficult because substantial collapse progression had already occurred between the time of the in vivo CT scans and surgical resection of the femoral heads, or the datasets were affected by technical issues related to the micro‐CT equipment. Given the potential limitations of micro‐CT in visualizing subtle structural discontinuities of the subchondral bone, histopathological assessment was additionally performed as a complementary approach to improve diagnostic accuracy.

Patient‐Specific Three‐Dimensional Finite Element Models

We used Mechanical Finder version 12 (Research Center of Computational Mechanics, Tokyo, Japan) to construct patient‐specific FEMs that included geometries specific to each patient's CT scans, and then performed the analyses [8, 23]. Surface models (hemipelvis, femur, necrotic lesion, and sclerotic boundary zone) were constructed using CT (Figure 1a). In accordance with previous studies [24, 25, 26], the articular cartilages of the pelvis and femur were each assumed to have an average thickness of 2 mm and to be completely bonded, and based on this assumption, the femoral head model was expanded by 4 mm to construct the cartilage model [8, 23].

Construction of patient‐specific finite element models (FEMs) and loading conditions. (a) Pre‐collapse computed tomography (CT) images were used to trace the region of interest and create three‐dimensional (3D) surface models of the hemipelvis, femur, necrotic lesion, and sclerotic boundary zone. (b) The inner tetrahedral mesh and outer shell of the femur and pelvis were generated to reproduce cortical and trabecular bone structures. (c) Applied boundary and loading conditions of the model.

Using findings from previous studies [8, 23, 27], we set the inner tetrahedral mesh size of the FEMs to 2–4 mm. To observe the extent of the collapse in detail, the mesh sizes of the necrotic lesion and sclerotic boundary zone were reduced to 0.2–1 mm. Thin shell elements of the bone (hemipelvis, femur, necrotic lesion, and sclerotic boundary zone) were created on the outer surface with a thickness of 0.4 mm to enhance the strength, similar to that of cortical bone [16] (Figure 1b). These shell elements enable the observation of failure on the external surface of the bone [15, 16, 17, 18]. To investigate model convergence, five models with different mesh sizes were created: (i) 1–3, (ii) 2–4, (iii) 3–5, (iv) 4–6, and (v) 5–7 mm (Supplementary Table S2). We were unable to use the model with a mesh size of 1–3 mm owing to the limited capacity of our computer. Therefore, we selected a mesh size of 2–4 mm because the percentage change between (i) and (ii) was only 0.7% of the total strain energy and 2.6% of the ratio of failed shell elements to total elements.

The elastic modulus of each bone (hemipelvis, femur, necrotic lesion, and sclerotic boundary zone) was set according to Hounsfield unit values on CT images, as previously reported [16, 26], using the equations proposed by Keyak et al. [11]. The Poisson's ratio of the bone was set to 0.3 [23, 28]. The elastic modulus and Poisson's ratio of the articular cartilage were 15 MPa and 0.45, respectively [27], while for the shell elements, both were adjusted to match those of adjacent tetrahedral elements.

Boundary and Loading Conditions

The pelvis, articular cartilage, femur, necrotic lesion, and sclerotic boundary zone were fixed in contact [29]. The cartilage mesh placed between the femur and pelvis shared nodes with both structures, with the interface set for complete fixation. Additionally, the necrotic lesion, sclerotic boundary zone, femur, and articular cartilage were set to share nodes. The pelvis was fully restrained at the sacroiliac joint and pubic symphysis [30] (Figure 1c); however, the other components of the FEMs were not restricted, allowing small deformations to occur. The femur was modeled in a 25° flexed position [31], and a load was applied to the distal femur in the direction of the functional axis of the femur (Figure 1c) [32].

Evaluation of Equivalent Stress on the Articular Surface

Following the methodology described in a previous study [8], the Drucker–Prager equivalent stress at the lateral sclerotic boundary on the mid‐coronal slice was measured under a uniform load of 2000 N. The measurement method followed that described in the previous study [8]; corresponding coronal slices from MRI, CT, and FEM were compared to identify the center of the lateral sclerotic boundary. For the evaluation of the normal femoral head, the center point was placed at the center of the outer superior quarter arc.

Nonlinear FEM Analysis

In the nonlinear analysis, shell element failure was regarded as collapse [15, 16, 17, 18]. The criterion for the shell element failure was defined as the point at which the Drucker–Prager equivalent stress equaled the element yield stress, according to the equations proposed by Keyak et al. [13]. The load applied to the distal femur was gradually increased from 0 N at a rate of 100 N/s. Initiation sites of collapse were evaluated. After the collapse was initiated, the load was further increased to evaluate the spread of collapse.

Validation of the Models

The validity of the FEMs was evaluated by confirming that the simulated collapse sites corresponded to the actual collapse sites on post‐collapse CT images. When the collapse sites were inconclusive on in vivo CT images after collapse, micro‐CT images and histopathological examination of the surgically resected femoral head after collapse were used for comparison.

Histopathological Examination of Femoral Heads

In accordance with previous reports [8, 10, 33], the surgically resected femoral heads were sectioned coronally at 5‐mm intervals and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde. The sections were decalcified using KCX for 7 days, embedded in paraffin, and cut coronally into 4‐μm‐thick slices. Continuous coronal slices were stained with hematoxylin and eosin and examined under a microscope to evaluate the presence of subchondral fracture. Subchondral fracture was histopathologically defined as a discontinuity of the subchondral bone plate, with or without a trabecular fracture line [10].

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using the JMP software (version 17.0; SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA). Equivalent stress values at the lateral sclerotic boundary were compared between the stage 2 type C group (n = 10) and the group without sclerotic changes on the articular surface (n = 4). Because of the small sample size and the non‐normal distribution of the data, the Mann–Whitney U test was used to assess differences between the two groups. Differences were considered statistically significant when the p‐value was < 0.05.

Results

Actual Collapse Sites in Post‐Collapse Images

In all 10 femoral heads in the stage 2 type C group, actual collapse occurred at the interface between the lateral sclerotic boundary and the adjacent necrotic lesion. In two femoral heads in which the collapse sites were inconclusive on in vivo CT images, micro‐CT images and histological examination of the surgically resected femoral heads confirmed subchondral collapse at the same interface. Equivalent Stress on the Articular Surface

All 10 femoral heads in the stage 2 type C group demonstrated marked stress concentration at the lateral sclerotic boundary on the articular surface (case nos. 1–10, Figure 3a), with a mean equivalent stress of 2.5 ± 0.3 MPa. In contrast, the group without sclerotic changes on the articular surface showed no stress concentration at the corresponding region (case nos. I–IV, Figure 3b–d), and their equivalent stress values were significantly lower than those in the stage 2 type C group (0.6 ± 0.2 MPa, p = 0.006). Simulated Initiation Sites of Collapse and Spread of Collapse

In all 10 FEMs of femoral heads in the stage 2 type C group (case nos. 1–10), simulated collapse was consistently initiated at the interface between the lateral sclerotic boundary and the adjacent necrotic lesion on the articular surface (Figure 4a). The collapse initiation sites corresponded to the stress concentration sites in each model (Figure 4b). After collapse initiation, collapse spread along the interface (Figure 5). In contrast, for the group without sclerotic changes on the articular surface (case nos. I–IV), no collapse was observed on the femoral head surface. For example, as shown in Figure 4c, the FEM analysis identified a simulated femoral neck failure pattern in a normal femoral head, rather than collapse at the articular surface. Discussion

This study is the first to demonstrate that collapse in ONFH is initiated precisely at the interface between the lateral sclerotic boundary and the adjacent necrotic lesion, as revealed by patient‐specific pre‐collapse FEMs. This initiation site was consistently observed across all stage 2 type C cases, supporting the concept that the sclerotic boundary plays a central biomechanical role in triggering collapse.

The present results clearly demonstrated this consistent initiation pattern, with collapse beginning at the interface between the lateral sclerotic boundary and the adjacent necrotic lesion. This finding aligns with our previous histological and FEM‐based studies [7, 8, 9, 10], which demonstrated that sclerotic changes generate stress concentration at the sclerotic boundary of the necrotic lesion and that early collapse occurs adjacent to this region. Conversely, stage 1 ONFH without sclerotic change showed neither stress concentration nor collapse in FEM analyses, indicating that sclerotic change in the pre‐collapse phase of ONFH is a prerequisite for failure initiation [1, 7, 8, 9, 10]. The 10 patients included in this study were selected because their stage 2 type C lesions share a characteristic size and spatial pattern of necrosis and sclerotic boundary, allowing broader applicability of the findings. Understanding the biomechanical implications of these sclerotic changes provides valuable insights into ONFH progression.

While sclerosis develops as a reparative response attempting to reinforce the necrotic lesion [1, 9], its heterogeneous stiffness may paradoxically create localized stress concentrations that initiate structural failure [34]. These findings suggest that the degree and spatial pattern of sclerotic change may determine whether the reparative process remains biomechanically protective or becomes maladaptive. Future studies quantifying the extent and distribution of sclerotic changes may help stratify collapse risk and identify critical thresholds for structural failure.

The patient‐specific FEMs developed in this study provide a novel framework for investigating collapse initiation in ONFH. Unlike conventional radiography or CT, which delineate morphological changes but cannot determine where structural failure will begin, FEM analysis enables visualization of stress distribution and identification of a mechanically vulnerable interface during the pre‐collapse stage. Recognition of this spatially consistent high‐risk region improves understanding of collapse progression and may assist clinicians in focusing radiographic surveillance on stage 2 type C lesions. Although the present study does not establish predictive capability, it refines the structural understanding of collapse initiation and provides a biomechanical basis for future investigations aimed at individualized risk assessment. Further validation incorporating both collapse and non‐collapse cases will be necessary to enhance the translational applicability of this approach.

It should be recognized that subchondral fractures may also occur in ONFH cases without a well‐demarcated lateral sclerotic band on conventional imaging, including cases in which sclerotic changes are only sparsely distributed around the necrotic boundary rather than forming a continuous sclerotic band. Such cases may demonstrate a more indolent clinical course. The present study specifically focused on stage 2 type C lesions with a well‐developed lateral sclerotic boundary, as our primary objective was to identify the initiation site of collapse within this clearly defined pathological context. Therefore, the findings of this study should be interpreted as being primarily applicable to ONFH cases with evident sclerotic boundary formation. Cases in which sclerotic changes are only sparsely distributed around the necrotic boundary may follow a different clinical or biomechanical course. Whether these presentations represent a distinct biomechanical pathway or a variation along a spectrum of the same collapse mechanism remains unclear and warrants further investigation.

This study has several limitations. First, it was retrospective with a small sample size, and all 10 patients in the collapse group were male. Although no evidence suggests sex‐specific differences in collapse initiation, larger and more balanced cohorts are needed to validate these findings. A prospective multicenter study is currently underway. Second, our FEMs reproduced a hip flexion angle of 25°, corresponding to the heel strike phase at the beginning of the stance phase of the gait cycle. This loading configuration was selected because prior biomechanical studies have demonstrated that hip joint stress may peak at heel strike [31]. However, dynamic muscle forces and time‐dependent changes in joint loading were not incorporated, which may influence stress distribution. While many previous FEM studies have assumed standing weight‐bearing conditions [8, 26, 35, 36, 37, 38], the present static analysis at 25° of hip flexion represents a reasonable approach to approximating peak loading during gait. Future models incorporating dynamic muscle forces and time‐dependent joint loading across different phases of gait may provide further insight into the mechanisms of collapse. Third, phantom‐based calibration was not performed during CT acquisition, which may affect the absolute values of the assigned elastic modulus. However, because identical CT scanner settings were used for all participants and the relative stress distribution within each model was preserved, the influence of this limitation is likely small. Fourth, all stage 2 type C lesions in the present cohort eventually progressed to collapse, and therefore no direct comparison with non‐collapse stage 2 type C cases was possible. Although type C lesions are known to have a high collapse rate, comparative analysis between collapse and stable lesions will be essential to determine whether patient‐specific FEM analysis can serve as a predictive tool. Future prospective studies incorporating both outcomes are warranted.

In conclusion, this simulation study demonstrates that collapse in ONFH is initiated at the interface between the lateral sclerotic boundary and the adjacent necrotic lesion, highlighting the critical biomechanical role of the sclerotic boundary zone. These findings not only clarify the site at which structural failure begins in ONFH but also provide a biomechanical rationale for future risk assessment and treatment strategies aimed at preventing collapse.
