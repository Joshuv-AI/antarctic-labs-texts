SLC7A11-specific CAR-T cell therapy potently targets colorectal and pancreatic cancer
Authors: Xiaoxue Pan, Jichang Li, Jing Zhu, Xin Wang, Yucun Liu, Xiaodong Tian, Yinmo Yang, Pengyuan Wang, Shanwen Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummarySLC7A11 is highly expressed in various solid tumors, including colorectal cancer and pancreatic cancer. The aim of the study was to report the therapeutic potential of SLC7A11 CAR-T therapy. The SLC7A11-specific antibodies were generated by hybridoma and humanization technologies. The cytotoxicity was validated in vitro through co-culture assays of effector cells with cancer cell lines. The anti-tumor studies in vivo were evaluated by cell line-derived xenograft (CDX) and patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models. We first confirmed the tumor specificity of SLC7A11 and then successfully developed SLC7A11-specific CAR-T cells. CCK-8 and LDH cytotoxicity assays demonstrated that cancer cells co-cultured with CAR-T cells exhibited higher mortality rates. Animal experiments showed that SLC7A11 CAR-T treatment suppressed the tumor growth without causing significant abnormalities in blood biochemical parameters. In conclusion, SLC7A11 CAR-T cell therapy showed remarkable anti-tumor capabilities and safety in colorectal and pancreatic cancer.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is a leading cause of cancer mortality worldwide, and the conventional treatment for CRC mainly relies on surgery and chemotherapy. Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) is a highly lethal tumor with limited treatment options and poor patient survival.1 Cell therapy for solid tumors constitutes a therapeutic approach that leverages immune cells to selectively identify and target cancer cells. Among these, chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-T cell therapy has demonstrated significant efficacy primarily in hematologic malignancies as well as in solid tumors.2 SLC7A11 (solute carrier family 7 member 11) is a member of the SLC family 7, which is primarily involved in the transport of cystine and glutamate on the cell membrane, cancer cells mainly rely on SLC7A11 to import extracellular cystine.3 The SLC7A11-GSH has been validated as a master regulator of intracellular redox balance.4 Research from recent years has shown that SLC7A11 is highly expressed in a variety of solid malignant tumors, such as CRC, breast cancer, pancreatic cancer, and ovarian cancer.5 SLC7A11 overexpression promotes tumor growth through suppressing ferroptosis, which presents potential vulnerabilities for therapeutic targeting in cancers with high expression of SLC7A11.6

CARs are typically generated by introducing a single-chain variable fragment (scFv) against a tumor-associated antigen as the extracellular domain, which is linked to the signaling domain encoding the T cell receptor signaling module.7 Co-stimulatory domains are usually molecules derived from the CD28 and CD137.8 In previous studies, small-molecule inhibitors targeting SLC7A11, such as Erastin and IKE, have demonstrated significant inhibitory effects on various tumor cell lines.9,10 We developed SLC7A11-specific antibodies by hybridoma and humanization technologies, concurrently, surface plasmon resonance (SPR) experiments were employed to confirm the interaction between the antibody and SLC7A11. Our research primarily demonstrated the anti-tumor effects of SLC7A11 CAR-T in CRC and pancreatic cancer.

Results

Antibody design and affinity evaluation

By applying a cell-based in vivo immunoassay and standard hybridoma technology, we generated a human SLC7A11-specific murine monoclonal antibody, designated 1A4 (Figure 1A). Upon verifying the successful knockdown of SLC7A11 via lentiviral transduction (Figure 1B), we utilized flow cytometry to measure the mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) in shSLC7A11 DLD1 and shNC DLD1 cells. This analysis provided indirect evidence that our antibody specifically targets SLC7A11 (Figure 1C). SLC7A11 expression level was then evaluated among the 15 kinds of human normal tissue samples (Figure 2B). Then, we used QuPath software to calculate the IHC score of HColA180Su20, and the samples from the CRC tissues and normal tissues were divided into four groups by quartiles (Figure 2C). We used a pie chart to display the distribution of expression levels between the two groups: in the tumor group, 72% samples were highly expressed (++ and +++), and 46% samples exhibited extremely high expression (++ and +++); meanwhile 25% samples were highly expressed in the normal group, and only 1% sample exhibited extremely high expression of SLC7A11 (Figure 2D). We subsequently detected the expression level of SLC7A11 in freshly collected CRC samples with flow cytometry, and the MFI indicated that the expression of SLC7A11 on cancer cells was higher than that in normal cells (Figures 2E and 2F). The anti-SLCA11 scFv was linked to the intracellular signaling domain from human 4-1BB and CD3ζ chains via the human CD8α hinge and the CD8α transmembrane regions (Figure 3D), and the construction of CAR vector map was illustrated in Figure 3E. After the sorting and activation of the T cells, they were transduced by lentivirus, and the transfection efficiency into primary human T was detected 96 h later. The E:T ratio was 1:1 when culturing with DLD1 cells and 2:1 when culturing with HT29, HCT116, MIA PaCa-2, and PANC-1 cells. The CCK-8 assay was also used to detect the survival rate of target cells after 24 h of co-culture, as shown in Figure 4A. In vivo anti-tumor studies on preclinical mouse models

For the cell line-based CRC xenograft models, the mice were divided into 3 groups (PBS, UTD, and CART) with the average tumor volume of around 100 mm3 and received 200 μL PBS or 6×106 effector cells in 200 μL of PBS via the tail vein. On the 5th and 30th day post tail-vein injection, peripheral blood samples were collected for biochemical analysis to assess potential liver and kidney injuries that may have occurred during the treatment. The results were shown in Figure 6A, and the blood biochemistry results of the CART group mice were all within the normal range. Tumor volume and body weight changes in mice were monitored every two days. The tumor volumes in the CART group of mice were significantly decreased compared with those in both the PBS-injected control (CON) group and the UTD group on day 30, and there were no significant differences in mouse body weight among the three groups (Figures 6B and 6D). On the 30th day, the mean tumor volume in the UTD group was 370.77 mm3; in the CART group, it was 131.47 mm3; and in the PBS group, it was 461.54 mm3. After euthanizing the mice, the tumors were excised and photographed (Figure 6C). The infiltration of human T cells, as detected by immunostaining, revealed that human T cells exhibited enhanced infiltration into residual tumors following CAR-T cell infusion in comparison with tumors treated with UTD-T cells (Figure 6E). Hematoxylin and eosin staining was employed to examine the pathological morphology of vital organs, revealing no significant abnormalities (Figure 6F). Discussion

Cancer and cardiovascular disease are the leading causes of death globally.11 Both CRC and PDAC are prevalent malignant tumors of the digestive tract, typically associated with poor outcomes when diagnosed at advanced stages.12,13 More than 2 million individuals are diagnosed with CRC each year worldwide.14 The treatments for CRC are multifaceted, including surgery, chemotherapy, radiation therapy, targeted therapy, and immunotherapy. Some achievements have been made with targeted therapies, such as the synergistic effect of protein kinase B-Raf inhibitors, epithelial growth factor receptor (EGFR) inhibitors, and monoclonal antibodies targeting the vascular endothelial growth factor receptor in CRC.14 With the introduction of surgical techniques such as laparoscopic technology and neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy, the treatment methods for pancreatic cancer are continuously evolving.15 However, these advancements have not resulted in a significant improvement in therapeutic outcomes.

T cells engineered to express CARs with tumor specificity have shown remarkable success in treating patients with hematologic malignancies, characterized by the clinical application of CD19, CD20, and BCMA CAR-T therapies.16,17,18 Conversely, the research and application of CAR-T therapy for solid tumors encounter obvious challenges, including a suppressive tumor microenvironment, antigen heterogeneity, and T cell exhaustion. However, the emergence of therapeutic targets and advancements in combination therapies have facilitated the entry of several CAR-T therapies targeting solid tumors into clinical trials, particularly those directed against HER2, Claudin 18.2, CEA, and EGFR.19

SLC7A11 is overexpressed in various cancers and regulates tumor development, proliferation, metastasis, and treatment resistance, positioning it as a promising therapeutic target for a plethora of malignancies, including CRC.20 Parts of anti-cancer effects have been observed by blocking the SLC7A11-GSH axis in some tumors, such as the ferroptosis induced in PDAC through cysteine depletion.21,22 However, due to its unique structure with 12 transmembrane domains and the brevity of its antigenic determinants, previous targeting strategies have primarily focused on small-molecule inhibitors rather than monoclonal antibodies. Upregulation of SLC7A11 in cancer is necessary to adapt to the high oxidative stress in microenvironment and maintain cellular redox homeostasis.20 Indeed, small-molecule inhibitors of SLC7A11, such as IKE and Erastin, can induce apoptosis and ferroptosis, thereby effectively exerting cytotoxic effects on tumor cells. In our previous work, we successfully developed a monoclonal antibody targeting SLC7A11 through live-cell immunization and multiple rounds of screening, followed by successful humanization. This advancement enabled us to create a CAR-T therapy specifically targeting SLC7A11. To our knowledge, this study represents the first cellular therapy targeting a member of the SLC family. Considering the diverse roles of various family members of SLC in common malignancies, such as CRC, lung cancer, pancreatic cancer, and breast cancer, our work pioneers a direction in the exploration of cellular therapy targets.

Tumor-specific antigens are a class of antigens that are highly expressed on cancer cells or the surface of cancer cells. Therapies that use tumor-specific antigens as targets, such as CAR-T cell therapy, generally have good safety because they tend to attack only cancer cells and not affect normal cells. On-target off-tumor is still a possible side effect of CAR-T therapy in humans.23 Therefore, an appropriate CAR-T target needs to possess tumor specificity. The results from the tissue microarray and western blot indicated that SLC7A11 was highly expressed in the majority of CRC and PDAC tissues. Additionally, it had a low expression level in the vast majority of normal tissues. We retrieved the SLC7A11 protein sequences of human and mouse from the UniProt database and compared the sequences using Clustal Omega. We found the differences to be minimal; therefore, we considered that the humanized antibody possesses a similar safety profile to the murine antibody (Figure S4A).

SLC7A11 CAR-T cell therapy had shown excellent anti-tumor effects in vitro. In vivo experiments targeting SLC7A11 CAR-T therapy demonstrated that it could restrict the further growth of tumor. Particularly, half of the mice showed complete tumor regression after the tail vein injection of SLC7A11 CAR-T cells based on the humanized SLC7A11 monoclonal antibodies in PDX models. In addition, the mice injected with CAR-T cells did not exhibit abnormal weight loss or mortality. The animal experiments had preliminarily confirmed the safety of this cell therapy.

Nevertheless, owing to the unique and complex tumor immune microenvironments, the advancement of CAR-T therapy in solid tumors remains highly challenging. The presence of dense fibrotic matrix in solid tumors reduced CAR’s ability to migrate and invade tumor cells.24 The combination therapy of CAR-T cells is an emerging treatment strategy that may improve the therapeutic effect. Immune checkpoint inhibitors, such as anti-PD-1/PD-L1 antibodies, can enhance the activity of CAR-T cells by blocking the immune evasion mechanisms of tumor cells.25 Both molecularly targeted drugs and CAR-T cell therapy are specific biological treatment modalities. Their combined use can modulate the immune response and improve the tumor microenvironment.26 Also, the development of bispecific CAR is a promising way to enhance the tumor cell recognition specificity, limiting attack to the normal cells.24 The reduction of T cell exhaustion is also a potentially effective approach to enhance the anti-tumor effect of CAR-T therapy.

CAR-T therapy’s effectiveness in treating hematological tumors has been widely recognized, but further exploration is needed for its application in solid tumor treatments. Based on the anti-SLC7A11 antibody we developed, we constructed CAR-T cells and discovered their therapeutic potential in CRC and pancreatic cancer. We have validated the therapeutic efficacy using various animal models and optimized the scFv derived from humanized antibody sequences.

In conclusion, SLC7A11 CAR-T therapy for colorectal and pancreatic cancer is a potentially effective and safe therapeutic approach. To our knowledge, this research represents a pioneering demonstration of the efficacy and preliminary safety of targeting SLC family proteins in CAR-T therapy. It establishes a research trajectory for CAR-T therapies aimed at solid tumors, specifically for colorectal and pancreatic cancer.

Limitations of the study

In this study, we utilized male mice exclusively, which limits our ability to assess potential sex differences in the observed effects. Future studies should consider incorporating both male and female subjects to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the phenomena under investigation. The NCG mouse models used lacked functional immune cells, which limited the precision of the response to acute inflammation. To more accurately assess the potential risk of acute inflammatory responses particularly cytokine release syndrome induced by CAR-T cells, it is necessary to conduct safety studies in humanized mouse models.

Experimental model and study participant details

Animal

Animal experiments were performed in the Laboratory Animal Center of Peking University First Hospital. Healthy male NCG (NOD/ShiLtJGpt-Prkdcem26Cd52Il2rgem26Cd22/Gpt, Strain NO. T001475) mice aged 6-8 weeks were used as experimental mice in vivo antitumor studies that purchased from GemPharmatech. The ethic of experimental animals was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Peking University First Hospital (J2023087).

Human tissue samples

The colorectal cancer tissues and paired normal tissues for the in vitro experiment were derived from three Chinese colorectal cancer patients (two males aged 62 and 66 years respectively, and one female aged 60 years) of East Asian origin. The colorectal cancer tissue for the PDX model was obtained from a 66-year-old East Asian Chinese colorectal cancer male patient. The pancreatic cancer tissues and paired normal tissues were derived from three Chinese pancreatic cancer patients (one male aged 61 years, two females aged 61 and 65 years respectively) of East Asian origin. The samples were randomly selected from patients who underwent surgical treatment at Peking University First Hospital between 2023 and 2025. All patients were informed and provided written informed consent prior to the surgery. The acquisition of human samples was approved by the Ethics Committee of Peking University First Hospital (2023-599).

Cell lines

The human colorectal cancer and pancreatic cell lines DLD1 (RRID: CVCL_0248), HT29 (RRID: CVCL_0320), HCT116 (RRID: CVCL_0291), PANC-1 (RRID: CVCL_0480) and MIA PaCa-2 (RRID: CVCL_0428) were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection. Human embryonic kidney 293T (RRID: CVCL_0063) cell lines were obtained from the National Collection of Authenticated Cell Cultures of China. Human embryonic kidney 293F (RRID: CVCL_6642) cell lines were obtained from National Biomedical Laboratory Cell Resource Bank. Cells were authenticated by STR profiling and tested routinely before use to avoid mycoplasma contamination. 293T and 293F cells were cultured in DMEM with 10% FBS and 1% Penicillin-Streptomycin. DLD1 cells were cultured in RMPI-1640 with 10% FBS and 1% Penicillin-Streptomycin. HT29, HCT116 cells were cultured in McCoy’s 5A with 10% FBS and 1% Penicillin-Streptomycin. PANC-1 and MIA PaCa-2 cells were cultured in DMEM with 10% FBS and 1% Penicillin-Streptomycin. The culture conditions were maintained at 37°C in a 5% CO2 incubator.

Primary cell cultures

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated from the peripheral blood of six healthy male and two healthy female Chinese donors aged 20 to 60 years of East Asian origin. All donors gave written informed consent for the use of their PBMCs for research purposes. The acquisition was approved by the Ethics Committee of Peking University First Hospital (2023-599). T cells were isolated from PBMCs and subsequently cultured in ImmunoCult™-XF T Cell Expansion Medium. The culture conditions were maintained at 37°C in a 5% CO2 incubator.

Method details

Development and characterization of anti-SLC7A11 antibodies

To prepare murine monoclonal antibodies, the SLC7A11 protein overexpression in 293F cells was used as an immunogen, and immunized BALB/c mice through subcutaneous multi-point injection, with an immunization dose of 1x106 cells per mouse. After multiple boosters, we collected mice serum for flow cytometry titer testing to ensure that the immune response had reached a sufficient level. Subsequently, we collected splenocytes from the immunized mice and fused them with myeloma cells to form hybridoma cells. We screened successfully fused hybridoma cells by using selective culture medium, followed by limiting dilution to obtain monoclonal hybridoma cells. Each cloned cell was independently cultured, and the antibodies in the culture fluid were collected for flow cytometry titer testing to select the monoclonal cells with the highest titer. After selecting the monoclonal cells with the highest titer, we carried out amplification and antibody preparation. The obtained monoclonal antibodies were used for subsequent experiments after purification and characterization, designated 1A4. The 1A4 antibody was conjugated to phycoerythrin (PE) fluorophore using established antibody–fluorophore coupling chemistry. Following the suppression of SLC7A11 expression via a lentiviral-mediated knockdown in DLD1 cells, we utilized the PE-labeled 1A4 antibody to assess the mean fluorescence intensity. The CDR grafting technique, in conjunction with computer-aided structural modeling, was utilized to humanize murine monoclonal antibodies, achieving a humanization level exceeding 90%. The resulting humanized antibody was designated as humanized-1A4.

Surface plasmon resonance

Antibody affinities were quantified by SPR. SLC7A11 was immobilized on an NTA sensor chip, and serial dilutions of the murine and humanized antibodies were flowed across the surface to determine their binding affinity toward SLC7A11. Kinetic analysis was performed using a 1:1 binding model to obtain the association (ka) and dissociation (kd) rate constants, from which the equilibrium dissociation constant (KD) was calculated.27

Immunohistochemical (IHC) scoring

The HOrgN120PT02 microarray containing human normal tissue samples and HColA180Su20 containing primary colorectal cancer tissues and adjacent normal tissue were immunostained with the 1A4 antibody to determine the expression of SLC7A11. The QuPath software was used for processing results of the tissue microarrays and the immunohistochemistry score reflected the level of SLC7A11 expression. The microarray derived from colorectal cancer patients were divided into four groups according to the scores by quartiles, with “-“, “+”, “++”, and “+++” representing the different expression levels respectively.

Assessment of tumor-associated antigen

The Tumor Dissociation Kit was utilized for the dissociation of both tumor and normal tissues from colorectal cancer patients. After the tissues were digested into single-cell suspensions, flow cytometry was employed to detect the expression level of SLC7A11 by 1A4 antibody.

The expression levels of SLC7A11 in pancreatic cancer tissues and adjacent normal tissues were detected by the Western Blot (WB). Tissues were lysed by RIPA buffer supplemented with phosphatase and protease inhibitors to obtain the total protein solution, which was then centrifuged to collect the supernatant. Protein concentration was determined using the BCA assay. Protein samples were subjected to SDS-PAGE gel electrophoresis for separation, followed by transfer of the proteins onto the PVDF membrane. Nonspecific binding sites were blocked with 5% BSA, and the membrane was then incubated with primary antibodies against SLC7A11 overnight at 4°C. On the following day, the membrane was incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies. Finally, images were captured using a chemiluminescent imaging system under the action of ECL.

The culture of primary T cells

T cells were negatively selected from PBMCs using human T Cell isolation kit and activated by 10 ng/ml rhIL-2 and CD3/CD28 in the human T cell expansion medium. The culture conditions were maintained at 37°C in a 5% CO2 incubator. We used flow cytometry to detect the efficiency of separation. After T cell sorting, the distribution of CD4 and CD8 cells in peripheral blood were immediately detected by flow cytometry. CD25 and CD69 were employed to assess T-cell activation at baseline and 48 hours post-stimulation.28,29 The expression of TIM3 and LAG3 was used as an indirect indicator of successful T cell activation.30

SLC7A11-CAR construction

The anti-SLC7A11-specific scFv was derived from a mouse anti-SLC7A11 antibody 1A4. The sequence of scFv had the structure of VH-(G4S)3-VL. The CAR contained the CD8α signal peptide, followed by scFv linked in-frame to the hinge domain of the CD8α molecule, the transmembrane region of the CD8α molecule, and the intracellular signaling domains of the CD137 and CD3ζ molecules. The upgraded anti-SLC7A11-specific scFv was derived from the humanized antibody.

Lentivirus production

The coding sequence of the representative CAR was inserted into the lentiviral vector GV401 and named pLV-CAR (SLC7A11). pHelper 1.0 vector and pHelper 2.0 vector were used to package pLV-CAR (SLC7A11) as a virus in human embryonic kidney 293T cells.

Generation of CAR-T cells

Approximately 48 hours after the completion of T cell sorting and activation, the T cells were transduced with lentivirus at a multiplicity of infection of 10, 20, 50 in 24-well plates with the presence of 5 μg /ml polybrene. Flow cytometry was employed to assess transfection efficiency at 96 hours post-transfection. At the same time, qPCR was employed to assess alterations in CAR expression in T cells following lentiviral transduction, relative to UTD-T cells. The heavy and light chain sequences used to generate the scFv sequences for the construction of the CAR in CAR-T cells have been deposited in Mendeley Data.

Cytotoxicity assays in vitro

The human colorectal cancer cells DLD1, H29, HCT116 and the human pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma cells MIA PaCa-2 and PANC-1 were cocultured with the CAR-T cells at an effector to target (E:T) ratio of 1:2, 1:1 and 2:1 in RMPI 1640. Following 24 hours of coculture, used PBS to wash 3 times to wash off the CAR-T cells. CCK8 tests were used to detect the cell viability to ensure the optimal effector to target ratio. According the results, DLD1 was co-cultured with UTD-T cells, MOCK-T cells and CAR-T cells at an E:T ratio of 1:1. HT29, HCT116, MIA PaCa-2 and PANC-1 were cocultured with UTD-T cells, MOCK-T cells and CAR-T cells at an E:T ratio of 2:1. Set up the control group with no treatment. After 24 hours, CCK8 tests were used to detect the cell viability. LDH assay were used to reflect the tumor lysis rate, DLD1 was cocultured with UTD-T cells, MOCK-T cells and CAR-T cells at an E:T ratio of 1:1. HT29, HCT116, MIA PaCa-2 and PANC-1 were cocultured with UTD-T cells, MOCK-T cells and CAR-T cells at an E:T ratio of 2:1. The readings from the Microplate reader was used to calculate the lysis rate of tumor cells.

DLD1-RFP cells were plated in a 96-well plate. CART, UTD and MOCK T cells were added at an E:T ratio of 1:1. Following 24 hours of coculture, we observed the survival of tumor cells using a fluorescence microscope, and measured the cytotoxicity among the groups by the MFI randomly selected three fields of view from each well.

Annexin-V and PI apoptosis assay kit was used to validate the cytotoxicity of CAR-T cells against colorectal cancer and pancreatic cancer cell lines. Following 20 hours of coculture, apoptosis rate was detected by flow cytometry.

After 24 hours of co-culture, the supernatant was collected by centrifuge. For the measurement of the release level of cytokines, IL-6, TNF-α, IFN-γ and IL-2 were detected by ELISA.

In vivo anti-tumor studies

For the colorectal cancer cell line-based xenograft models, 3×106 DLD1 cells in 200 μl PBS were injected subcutaneously into the right flank of NCG mice. When the tumor volume grows to approximately 100 mm3, the mice were divided into three groups and respectively received 200 μl PBS, 6×106 UTD-T cells and the 6×106 CAR-T cells in 200 μl of PBS via the tail vein. On day 5 and day 30, peripheral blood was taken from the mice, and the biochemical indicators were measured for safety assessment. Tumor volume was measured with a caliper and calculated by the following equation: Volume=Length×width2/2

The peritoneal implantation metastasis model was used to evaluate the efficacy of CAR-T therapy and its inhibitory effect on tumor metastasis.31,32 2×106 DLD1-Luc cells were intraperitoneally injected to NCG mice on day 0, bioluminescence flux imaging of mice was detected by In Vivo Imaging Systems on day 7. Based on the imaging results from day 7, twelve mice were divided into three groups and were given PBS, 8×106 UTD-T cells, or CAR-T cells via tail vein injection. Bioluminescence was detected every 7 days, with the observation terminating on day 28.

For the pancreatic cancer cell line-based xenograft models, 2×106 MIA PaCa-2 cells in 200 μl PBS were injected subcutaneously into the right flank of NCG mice. When the tumor volume grows to approximately 130 mm3, the mice were divided into three groups and respectively received 200 μl PBS, 8×106 MOCK-T cells and the 8×106 CAR-T cells in 200 μl of PBS via the tail vein.

For the colorectal cancer PDX models, NCG mice were implanted PDX tissue fragments (approximately 10 mm3) subcutaneously on the right flank. When the tumor volume grows to approximately 150 mm3, the mice were divided into three groups (PBS, MOCK and CART) and respectively received 200 μl PBS, 8×106 MOCK-T cells and the 8×106 derived from humanized sequences CAR-T cells in 200 μl of PBS via the tail vein.

Analysis of CAR-T cells persistence and infiltration

Whole blood obtained at day 14 by retro-orbital bleeding in pancreatic cancer CDX mice were analyzed for the presence of CAR-T cells as follows: 50 μL of blood was incubated with the antibody human CD4 and CD8. After the incubation, the cells were then submitted to flow cytometry analysis. Absolute counting beads were used to count the absolute number of cells. To assess the infiltration of human T cells into tumors, formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded human tumor tissues were immunostained using an anti-CD3 antibody. Different levels of CD3 expression were evaluated by the IHC plugin of Image J.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Details of each experiment can be found in the figure legends. For comparisons between two groups, if the data from both groups followed a normal distribution and had equal variances, the T-test was used for statistical analysis; otherwise, the non-parametric rank sum test was employed. For multiple group comparisons, if the data met the criteria of normal distribution and homogeneity of variance, the ANOVA was used for statistical analysis; otherwise, the non-parametric test, such as the Kruskal-Wallis H test was applied. Data were presented as mean, mean ± SD or mean ± SEM. In the in vitro experiments, the value of n represented the number of independent experimental replicates. In the in vivo experiments, the value of n corresponded to the number of animals used. p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism v9.4.1 (Significance levels: ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p <0.01, ∗∗∗p <0.001, ∗∗∗∗p <0.0001).
