Bilateral repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation modulates the hemispheric imbalance in major depressive disorder
Authors: Yaqun Chen, Li Wan, Shuqi He, Qinghui Zhang, Tao Yang, Shuang Zheng, Wen Xie, Xinyang Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundMajor depressive disorder (MDD) is characterised by interhemispheric functional imbalance, yet the neuromodulatory mechanisms underlying its correction remain poorly understood. Repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) has shown promise in restoring hemispheric balance, but evidence regarding its effects on alpha‐band functional connectivity (FC) is limited.AimsThis randomised, two‐arm controlled trial aims to elucidate the neural mechanisms by which bilateral rTMS ameliorates interhemispheric functional imbalance in patients with MDD by reducing alpha‐band functional hyperconnectivity in the left hemisphere.MethodsFifty‐two patients with MDD were randomised to active (n = 31) or sham (n = 21) bilateral rTMS targeting the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (dlPFC). Active stimulation involved 20 Hz over the left dlPFC and 1 Hz over the right dlPFC. Participants underwent emotional attention network task testing with simultaneous electroencephalography recording before and after 10 sessions of rTMS. Phase slope index (PSI) was used to quantify directional FC in the alpha band (8–13 Hz). Clinical symptoms and behavioural performance were also assessed.ResultsActive rTMS significantly improved sleep quality (PSQI, F = 5.52, p = 0.023) and reduced reaction times under both congruent and incongruent emotional conditions (−in−: t = −2.23, p = 0.031, +in−: t = −2.01, p = 0.05). Alpha‐band FC analysis revealed decreased left hemispheric hyperconnectivity in the active group, particularly in the occipital, left temporoparietal and left frontocentral regions (t ranging from 2.42 to 3.65, all p = 0.038). The sham group showed placebo‐driven improvements in subjective mood but no significant FC changes.ConclusionsBilateral rTMS modulates interhemispheric imbalance in patients with MDD by reducing pathological alpha‐band FC in left hemispheric regions, which is correlated with improved emotional processing and attentional performance. These findings support the use of alpha‐band FC as a biomarker for rTMS efficacy and highlight the potential of network‐targeted stimulation protocols for the individualised treatment of MDD.

INTRODUCTION

Major depressive disorder (MDD), a common psychiatric condition, involves functional abnormalities across multiple brain networks. Recent studies have revealed significant interhemispheric functional imbalances in patients with MDD. This imbalance is reflected not only in brain structure and neural activity but also in functional connectivity (FC) patterns between the hemispheres. Neuroimaging evidence suggests that alterations in white matter tracts within the right temporal and frontal lobes may be associated with impairments in executive function and emotional regulation. 1 Neuroelectrophysiological investigations further indicate increased alpha‐band oscillatory power over the left prefrontal cortex, suggesting reduced cortical activation in this region—in contrast with overactivation in the right prefrontal cortex, which results in an asymmetric ‘left‐low, right‐high’ pattern. 2 Moreover, emotional dysregulation is closely linked to aberrant connectivity in regions such as the left ventrolateral prefrontal cortex. 3 Although these findings preliminarily outline the landscape of interhemispheric imbalance in MDD, the underlying structure‒function causal relationships and regulatory mechanisms remain to be fully elucidated.

Notably, although core brain regions such as the left prefrontal cortex in patients with MDD exhibit reduced neural activity, multiple neuroimaging and electroencephalographic studies suggest that this region and its associated networks may show enhanced FC, particularly within the alpha band during resting‐state or emotional tasks. 4 , 5 This may reflect compensatory hyperconnectivity within neural networks aimed at maintaining basic functions. However, in MDD, such abnormal connectivity is inefficient and rigid, impairing the efficiency and flexibility of neural information processing and compromising emotional and cognitive functioning. 6 Therefore, the aim of this study, employing bilateral repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS), is not to further suppress left‐hemispheric activity but rather to modulate this pathological FC, thereby restoring network efficiency and interhemispheric balance.

rTMS, a noninvasive neuromodulation technique, is a novel approach to ameliorating interhemispheric imbalance in patients with MDD. Compared with pharmacotherapy and unilateral rTMS, bilateral rTMS has demonstrated superior clinical benefits. 7 Research indicates that bilateral stimulation can synchronously modulate the bilateral dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (dlPFC), resulting not only in good tolerability but also in significant alleviation of depressive symptoms. 8 The mechanism may involve dynamic regulation of aberrant neural circuits, such as reducing FC between the left dlPFC and bilateral anterior insula 9 or, modulating sensorimotor network connectivity to improve cognitive and emotional functioning. 10 Furthermore, multiple studies have suggested that baseline FC can predict treatment response to rTMS, suggesting that rTMS exerts therapeutic effects by remodelling brain network function. 11 , 12 Thus, bilateral rTMS shows unique potential to rebalance interhemispheric activity.

In selecting the stimulation protocol, this study adopted bilateral rTMS over theta‐burst stimulation (TBS) or other deep stimulation techniques, primarily because of its advantages in clinical applicability and parameter optimisation. Although deep stimulation techniques can target broader brain regions, their high cost limits widespread clinical adoption. 7 In contrast, the efficacy of the bilateral rTMS protocol—particularly the combined mode of high‐frequency (20 Hz) stimulation over the left hemisphere and low‐frequency (1 Hz) stimulation over the right—has been validated in multiple studies. This approach enhances left hemispheric excitability while suppressing right hemispheric overactivity, thereby promoting a more comprehensive restoration of interhemispheric balance. 13 , 14 Building on evidence that high‐frequency rTMS (> 5 Hz) alleviates depression by modulating cortical excitability and plasticity, 15 this study applied 20 Hz (rather than 10 Hz) to the left dlPFC. This was to explore its potentially stronger effect on depression‐relevant network connectivity within safe parameters.

At the level of neural oscillations, activity in the alpha band (8–13 Hz) is considered closely related to the therapeutic effects of rTMS. Patients with MDD often exhibit abnormally increased alpha power, which may reflect impaired cortical–subcortical circuit integration. 16 Studies have shown that rTMS can ameliorate symptoms by modulating alpha oscillations—for example, reducing left prefrontal alpha power, 17 suppressing abnormal alpha connectivity 18 and correlating with treatment response. 19 Additionally, alpha subbands may serve as specific biomarkers for MDD. 20 Therefore, using alpha‐band activity as a research metric not only helps elucidate the mechanisms of rTMS but also holds promise as an objective neurophysiological indicator for treatment evaluation.

Based on this background, the present study aims to investigate the mechanism by which bilateral rTMS modulates interhemispheric imbalance in MDD, with a specific focus on changes in alpha‐band FC. We hypothesise that bilateral rTMS can ameliorate interhemispheric imbalance by reducing excessive alpha‐band FC in the left prefrontal, temporoparietal and occipital regions. This study will test this hypothesis at both the behavioural and neural network levels, providing a theoretical basis and practical guidance for individualised rTMS treatment targeting brain network dynamics.

METHODS

Participants

Sample size estimation was conducted via G*Power with an effect size set at d = 0.80, a statistical power of 0.8, and a significance level of α = 0.05. The analysis indicated that each group required 26 participants. This study employed a randomised, double‐blind, sham‐controlled design. The randomisation sequence was generated by a researcher not involved in the trial execution using SPSS 27.0, and group allocation was secured within an electronic system. For participant blinding, the sham stimulation group received stimulation with the coil placed with the back against the scalp (delivering no effective magnetic field), ensuring that both groups underwent identical procedures and remained unable to distinguish their group assignment. Experimenter blinding was achieved by having an independent third party responsible for randomisation and parameter settings; the experimenters administering the stimulation and those conducting the electroencephalography (EEG) recordings were unaware of the group allocation. For data analysis blinding, all data were de‐identified, and analysts worked exclusively with coded files to ensure objectivity of the outcome measurements.

After screening and randomisation, 56 participants were enrolled in total. This included 34 participants in the active group, of whom three withdrew due to relocation or work commitments (dropout rate: 8.82%), and 22 participants in the sham group, of whom one withdrew for work‐related reasons (dropout rate: 4.55%). Although the final sample size of the sham group was slightly lower than the minimum required by the a priori power calculation due to initial under‐enrolment and one dropout for work‐related reasons, the between‐group differences in the primary outcome measures showed large effect sizes, and the dropout rate was low, suggesting limited impact on the overall conclusions. Future studies with larger sample sizes are warranted to validate the robustness of these findings. Ultimately, 52 participants were included in the analysis, comprising 31 in the active group and 21 in the sham group (figure 1). Among the participants, 15 were diagnosed with first‐episode MDD and 37 with recurrent MDD.

Two psychiatrists performed independent clinical interviews to diagnose MDD on the basis of the Chinese version of the 11th Revision of the International Classification of Diseases (ICD‐11). The groups were matched for sex, age and education level. The inclusion criteria were as follows: age between 18 and 60 years; normal hearing and normal or corrected vision; right‐handedness; stable medication dosage for over one month; and a confirmed MDD diagnosis according to the Chinese version of ICD‐11 criteria. The exclusion criteria included any history of neuromodulatory treatment (such as electroconvulsive therapy, rTMS, transcranial direct current stimulation or transcranial alternating current stimulation) within the past 6 months; cardiovascular or neurological disorders; head trauma with loss of consciousness; physical abnormalities; epilepsy; pregnancy or breastfeeding; history of syncope; concurrent participation in other research studies; or inability to complete experimental tasks.

All participants provided written informed consent after receiving a detailed explanation of the experimental procedures and precautions in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Paradigm and procedure

The emotional attention network task (ANT) presented stimuli randomly via E‐Prime (Psychology Software Tools). The experiment had four stimulus picture types (a central target picture and four flankers), including those reflecting the same emotion type (congruent trials): +in+ and −in− and those reflecting the opposite emotion type (incongruent trials): −in+ and +in−. Each trial started with one of the three types of cues (central, spatial or no cue). Figure 2 illustrates the flow of the experiment. The pictures used in the experiment were selected from the Geneva Affective Picture Database (GAPED).

Cue‐type was used to evaluate alerting effects (RTno cue‐RTcue) and orienting effects (RTineffective cue‐RTeffective cue). The flanker type was used to evaluate conflict effects (RTincongruity‐RTcongruity). Conflict effects were further categorised into conflict positive effects and conflict negative effects on the basis of the affective type of the target picture.

EEG recording and preprocessing

The participants sat alone in a magnetically shielded room to complete the ANT and collect EEG data. EEGs were acquired via a 64‐lead EEG amplifier (BioSemi). Sixty‐four Ag/AgCl scalp electrodes were fixed according to the international standard 10–20 system. The sampling rate was 1024 Hz and the impedance of each electrode was kept below 5 kΩ.

EEG data were analysed via CURRY 9 (Compumedics Neuroscan). The steps of EEG data preprocessing were as follows: (1) removing useless channels and interpolating channels with poor data quality; (2) re‐referencing to a whole‐head averaged reference; (3) filtering the EEG signal via a bandpass filter (0.5–70 Hz); (4) employing independent component analysis (ICA) to identify and remove components associated with ocular and muscular activities, thereby correcting for physiological artefacts; (5) subsequently, globally applying an amplitude threshold to the ICA‐corrected data to reject artefacts: time segments where the absolute voltage value exceeded ± 100 μV in any channel were marked and removed. This threshold, based on the re‐referenced data, aimed to eliminate residual extreme noise; and (6) selecting a time window from 200 ms before stimulus onset to 800 ms after stimulus onset as the event time window and performing baseline correction. Finally, the conditions were segmented according to different stimulus types. This preprocessing procedure adhered to the component‐based EEG processing standards. 21

Functional connectivity

Using the Hermes toolbox (Version 2020‐04‐26) within MATLAB (v. R2018b; MathWorks), we computed the phase slope index (PSI) 22 , 23 in the alpha (8–13 Hz) frequency band from the preprocessed, epoched data. This yielded 64 × 64‐channel FC matrices for the time window spanning 200 ms prestimulus to 800 ms post‐stimulus. The 64 channels were grouped into 7 predefined regions of interest (ROIs), namely, the left frontocentral (LFC), right frontocentral (RFC), mid frontocentral (MFC), left temporoparietal (LTP), right temporoparietal (RTP), centroparietal (CP) and occipital (OCC), on the basis of standard anatomical mappings (figure 3A). This parcellation was based strictly on the standard electrode positions of the International 10–20 system and their corresponding macroanatomical locations projected onto the MNI152 standard brain space. 24 , 25 This approach groups anatomically adjacent and functionally related electrodes, which is a widely adopted practice for defining scalp‐level ROIs in group EEG studies when individual structural MRI is not available for coregistration. The specific electrode composition of each ROI is shown in figure 3A. PSI values were then averaged across all channel pairs within and between these ROIs for statistical analysis.

The formula for calculating PSI is: PSI=ψxy=ψ˜xystd(ψ˜xy), in which ψ˜xy=ℑ∑f∈FKxy∗(f)Kxy(f+δf), K xy (f) is the complex coherency between signals X and Y, δf is the frequency resolution, ℑ(·) denotes the imaginary part and F is the set of frequencies over which the slope is summed. Usually, this formula is normalised by using an estimate of its standard deviation. std(ψ˜xy)=kσ is assessed by dividing the whole data set into k epochs, calculating k values of ψ˜xy, ψ∼kxy, from data with the kth epoch removed and then taking σ as the standard deviation of the distribution of ψ∼kxy.

PSI is a directional connectivity metric derived from complex‐valued coherence that quantifies the consistency of phase differences with respect to changes in frequency. The underlying principle is that if neural waves propagate at similar velocities across frequencies, the phase difference between two signals increases with frequency, yielding a positive phase spectrum slope. The PSI thus estimates the strength and direction of information flow between brain regions: a negative value (PSI < 0) indicates information flow from channel A to B, whereas a positive value (PSI > 0) reflects flow from B to A (Supporting Information S1).

rTMS intervention

This study utilised a TMS device (Youde YD‐MT500, Netherlands) equipped with a navigation system (Nena V3.4, Netherlands). The neuronavigation system was equipped with four sensors: Sensor 1 was designated for the pointer, Sensor 2 was connected to the TMS device and Sensors 3 and 4 were used for head tracking, enabling the precise positioning of stimulation targets. The specific procedures are as follows: First, the resting motor threshold (RMT), defined as the minimum stimulation intensity over the left motor cortex required to elicit electromyographic responses in the right abductor pollicis brevis in at least 5 out of 10 consecutive trials, was determined for each participant. Second, the preconstructed MRI‐based head model of each participant was imported into the Neural Navigator system (Nena, Version 3.4.1). The participant was then positioned supine and fitted with Sensors 3 and 4, which were attached to a headband. Sensor 1 was subsequently used to locate the nasion, tip of the nose, left ear and right ear of the participant sequentially. This procedure was performed to achieve precise registration for identifying the individual’s dlPFC. The stimulation target in this study was the bilateral dlPFC, anatomically defined as Brodmann areas 9 and 46. To achieve precise and reproducible individualised targeting, we employed standard coordinates within the MNI152 standard space: (−38, 44, 26) for the left dlPFC and (38, 44, 26) for the right dlPFC. Positional calibration was subsequently conducted via Sensors 1 and 2, and the target stimulation site was selected on the computer. Finally, the stimulation coil was positioned close to the participant’s head. By observing the distance displayed on the computer, the targeted dlPFC was accurately located, with the distance maintained within a range of less than 30 mm. Once the optimal position was identified, the stimulation coil was secured via a mechanical arm, and stimulation therapy was initiated.

The active rTMS parameters were set as follows: right dlPFC stimulation at 1 Hz, 100% RMT intensity, with a 2‐second interpulse interval, totalling 800 pulses over 14 min; left dlPFC stimulation at 20 Hz, also at 100% RMT, delivered in trains of 10 pulses with 2‐second intertrain intervals, totalling 1500 pulses over 6 min. For sham rTMS, the same stimulation protocol and parameters were used, but without actual magnetic field delivery. Instead, electrodes applied to the left or right forehead delivered weak currents to simulate the electric field sensation of real stimulation. The electrical stimulation parameters were set as follows: monophasic square wave, pulse width 100 μs and current intensity 4 mA. All participants wore earplugs to reduce acoustic noise during the procedure. 14 The selected 20 Hz stimulation frequency falls within the parameter range specified by internationally recognised safety guidelines. 26 No serious adverse events related to this stimulation frequency have been observed in previous studies. Consequently, the parameter regimen employed in this study demonstrates favourable safety and feasibility.

Study design

Patients with MDD were screened for eligibility according to the inclusion and exclusion criteria and assigned to either the active group or the sham group. Before the treatment, all the participants completed an emotional ANT, and EEG signals were recorded. All the participants subsequently underwent 10 rTMS sessions over a 2‐week period. Following the treatment, the emotional ANT was readministered, and EEG data were collected within 1–5 days to minimise potential interference from rTMS on EEG signals.

The participants were required to complete the following scales both before and after rTMS treatment: the Symptom Checklist‐90 revised (SCL‐90); 27 the Self‐Rating Depression Scale (SDS); 28 the Self‐Rating Anxiety Scale (SAS); 29 the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI); 30 and the Positive and Negative Affect Scale (PANAS). 31 Objective tests were performed before and after treatment for the final 52 patients with MDD by assessors who were not aware of the grouping, which was based on the nine‐item depression assessment, combined with daily case records from two clinicians to assess MDD. Discontinuation and the use of antidepressants during the study were recorded.

Safety and adverse events

Safety was assessed during treatment by recording patient self‐reported adverse events and by actively asking about prespecified symptoms. The most commonly reported adverse reactions included seizures, headache and dizziness. Other adverse reactions included scalp pain, nausea, chest tightness, anxiety, eyelid twitching, tinnitus, gastrointestinal symptoms, irritability and general malaise.

Statistical analysis

SPSS v.27.0 software was used to analyse the data. Chi‐square analysis and independent samples t tests were used to test for differences in demographic information and dosage between the two groups. A 2 × 2 ANOVA was used to assess the interaction and main effects between the active and sham groups across pretest and posttest measurements on various scales. A 2 × 4 ANOVA was performed to assess the interactions and main effects between the two groups (active and sham groups) and the four experimental groups (+in+, −in−, +in− and −in+). A 2 × 4 ANOVA was subsequently conducted to assess the interaction and main effects between the groups (active and sham) and the subcomponents of the attentional network (alerting effect, orienting effect, conflict positive effect and conflict negative effect). If interaction effects were present, further simple effects analyses were conducted. Finally, Pearson correlation analyses were conducted between the changes in PSI (pretest minus posttest) for both hemispheres and the corresponding changes in clinical symptoms and behavioural performance in the two groups, to further validate the relationship between rTMS‐induced neuromodulation and clinical efficacy.

Network‐based statistics (NBS) methods were used to compare FC differences between the active and sham groups before and after rTMS treatment. Each connection was first statistically analysed between groups or conditions. Next, the connection significance level edge p = 0.01 was determined to screen out FCs that had reached the above significance level. The size of each connectivity component was calculated via the extent approach. The significance of the subnetwork was assessed using 5000 group‐label random permutations, controlling for multiple‐comparison error (familywise error rate, FWER). Finally, subnetworks that pass the significance test (p < 0.05) were retained, and their nodes, edges and statistics were recorded.

RESULTS

Clinical outcomes

The overall sample exhibited moderate to severe depression at baseline, as indicated by a mean depression score and SDS. With regard to pharmacotherapy, the majority of participants remained on a stable regimen of antidepressants for at least 1 month prior to and throughout the study period. The antidepressants used by patients included selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors, serotonin and norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors, norepinephrine and dopamine reuptake inhibitors and tricyclic antidepressants. Each type of medication was taken at a regular dose. There were 38 single‐type users, 10 multiple‐type users and four others with no record. There was no significant difference in the duration, dosage or type of medication used between the two groups (table 1).

At baseline, there were no significant differences between the active and sham groups. After rTMS treatment, both groups exhibited significant reductions in negative mood (active group: F = 12.93, p < 0.001, partial η 2 = 0.22; sham group: F = 7.87, p = 0.007, partial η 2 = 0.15), SDS (active group: F = 8.64, p = 0.005, partial η 2 = 0.16; sham group: F = 8.77, p = 0.005, partial η 2 = 0.16) and nine‐item depression assessment scores (active group: F = 103.34, p < 0.001, partial η 2 = 0.68; sham group: F = 52.28, p < 0.001, partial η 2 = 0.52). The active group demonstrated significant improvement in SCL‐90 total scores (F = 8.16, p = 0.006, partial η 2 = 0.15), whereas the sham group showed only marginally significant improvement. However, no significant intergroup differences were observed. Compared with pretreatment values, the active group presented significantly lower PSQI scores (F = 5.52, p = 0.023, partial η 2 = 0.11), whereas the sham group showed significant improvement in positive mood (F = 5.52, p = 0.023, partial η 2 = 0.11), with a marginally significant improvement in SAS scores (F = 3.84, p = 0.056, partial η 2 = 0.08). Furthermore, active rTMS maintained stable therapeutic effects 2 weeks after treatment completion (mean [SD] = 5.35 (5.11), F = 168.59, p < 0.001, partial η 2 = 0.78; Supporting Information S1: table S1).

According to reports of adverse effects, only one participant reported mild dizziness and scalp tingling during treatment, and another reported slight nausea. No seizures occurred throughout this study. All observed adverse effects were transient and typically resolved spontaneously within 30 min to 2 h after stimulation. No serious adverse events occurred, and no long‐term risks associated with rTMS treatment were identified.

Behavioural results

On the emotional ANT, there was no difference in reaction time between the two groups at baseline. After rTMS treatment, the reaction time of the active group was significantly lower than that of the sham group in both the congruity (−in−: t = −2.23, p = 0.031, Cohen’s d = 0.66) and incongruity (+in−: t = −2.01, p = 0.05, Cohen’s d = 0.59) conditions. Furthermore, the alerting effect of the active group was significantly lower than that of the sham group (t = −2.03, p = 0.048, Cohen’s d = 0.60), whereas there were no significant differences in the orienting effect, conflict positive effect or conflict negative effect between the groups.

A comparison of the pre‐ and post‐treatment reaction times revealed a significant decrease in reaction time for both the congruity condition (+in+: t = 2.21, p = 0.044, Cohen’s d = 0.71; −in−: t = 2.63, p = 0.019, Cohen’s d = 0.84) and the incongruity condition in the active group (+in−: t = 3.11, p = 0.007, Cohen’s d = 0.88; −in+: t = 2.36, p = 0.032, Cohen’s d = 0.79). In addition, the alerting effect showed a marginally significant decrease for the active group (t = 2.01, p = 0.054, Cohen’s d = 0.71), whereas no significant differences were noted in the orienting, conflict positive or conflict negative effects (figure 4 and table 2).

Additionally, we analysed the accuracy data from the emotional ANT. Across all experimental conditions, there were no significant differences in accuracy between the active and sham groups either before or after treatment (online Supporting Information S1: table S2, F ranged from 0.01 to 2.65, all p > 0.05, partial η 2 ranged from < 0.01 to 0.07). Functional connectivity

At baseline, in the alpha band, there was no significant difference between the active group and the sham group in any of the four conditions. After the treatment, in the alpha band, the active group had significantly smaller PSI values than the sham group in the congruity (−in−) condition (online Supporting Information S1: table S3, t ranging from 2.42 to 3.65, all p = 0.038). The observed decrease in PSI values indicates a significant weakening in the strength of information flow from both the LTP and LFC to the OCC. Similarly, the information flow from the OCC to the LTP and LFC also decreased. Furthermore, there were no significant differences in PSI values before and after treatment in the sham group.

Direction of neural projections

Compared with the sham group, the active group presented significantly fewer projections from the LTP, LFC, MFC and RTP to the OCC, as well as markedly fewer efferent signals from the OCC to the LFC and LTP under the congruity condition. Additionally, the active group presented weaker projections from the LFC to the LTP and OCC, as well as reduced reception of signals from the RFC and OCC, than the sham group. Projections from the RFC to the LFC and MFC were also significantly decreased in the active group. In contrast, the sham group showed no significant change in neural projections within the left hemisphere, whereas the active group demonstrated a reduction in hyperconnectivity. These results suggest that rTMS may specifically modulate hyperactivity in the left hemisphere, providing preliminary support for the hypothesis that rTMS corrects interhemispheric imbalance (figure 3B and 3C).

Collectively, the observed widespread reduction in efferent projections from left hemispheric regions (LFC, LTP) and towards the OCC in the active group signifies that bilateral rTMS effectively attenuated the pathologically enhanced directional information flow within the left hemisphere.

Exploratory analysis of other frequency bands

Based on a priori hypothesis, the primary analyses focused on alpha‐band activity. Exploratory analyses were also conducted in the delta (1–4 Hz), theta (4–8 Hz), beta (13–30 Hz) and gamma (30–80 Hz) bands to provide a more comprehensive picture. We found that the PSI values in the delta band were generally close to zero, indicating no detectable effective directional information flow. In the gamma band, none of the comparisons reached statistical significance. In the theta and beta bands, although sporadic, condition‐specific differences were observed (detailed results are listed in online Supporting Information S1: tables S4–S6, t ranging from 2.42 to 4.44, p ranging from 0.001 to 0.026). These findings did not form a consistent pattern across different experimental conditions and are not directly related to the core a priori hypothesis concerning the alpha band and interhemispheric balance.

Correlation analysis between PSI and behavioural and clinical symptoms

The results revealed that, in the active group, reductions in PSI values in both left (r = 0.45, p = 0.012) and right (r = 0.50, p = 0.004) hemispheres were positively correlated with improvements in sleep quality (PSQI). In contrast, in the sham group, a reduction in right‐hemisphere PSI values was significantly negatively correlated with a decrease in negative mood (r = −0.49, p = 0.025). Regarding behavioural measures, in the active group, decreased left‐hemisphere PSI values were significantly negatively correlated with improved reaction times in both alerting effect (r = −0.58, p = 0.018) and orienting effect (r = −0.69, p = 0.003). No significant correlations were observed in the sham group. Effect sizes for Pearson’s r were interpreted as small (r = 0.10), medium (r = 0.30) and large (r = 0.50) according to Cohen’s guidelines. 32 The effect sizes ranged from medium to large (Supporting Information S1: table S7).

DISCUSSION

Main findings

This study compared the therapeutic outcomes between the active and sham rTMS groups. Following treatment, the active group showed significant improvement in PSQI scores, suggesting a specific modulation of sleep physiology. Recent studies have shown that rTMS may modulate rapid eye movement (REM) sleep and alleviate depressive symptoms by regulating key limbic circuits involved in sleep regulation and emotional processing, such as the amygdala–hippocampal circuit. 33 Further studies have confirmed that rTMS ameliorates depressive symptoms by improving sleep quality. 34

In contrast, the sham group exhibited marked improvements only in positive mood and anxiety. This pattern aligns with the well‐documented placebo effect inherent in controlled rTMS trials, which exerts a significant influence on subjective mood ratings. A recent empirical study confirmed that positive pretreatment expectations serve as an important predictor of clinical improvement following rTMS treatment, directly underscoring the role of expectation‐driven mechanisms. 35 This observation is further corroborated by a meta‐analysis quantifying the placebo response in rTMS trials for depression. 36 This suggests that the improvement in the sham group may mainly originate from psychological expectations and nonspecific intervention effects.

Critically, this nonspecific intervention effect did not extend to objective behavioural performance, physiological functions or core neurophysiological measures. In marked contrast, the active group demonstrated significant improvements in both sleep quality and reaction time during the emotional conflict task, and gains were absent in the sham group. More importantly, only active rTMS significantly reduced PSI values in the alpha band and attenuated neural projections between left hemispheric regions in MDD, supporting our primary hypothesis that rTMS modulates interhemispheric imbalance. Compared with sham rTMS, active rTMS led to a significant reduction in directional neural projections within and between the LFC, LTP and OCC. This specific, quantifiable modulation of brain networks was completely absent in the sham group. We interpret this connectivity reduction not as a functional deficit but as normalisation of the inefficient, compensatory hyperconnectivity characteristic of MDD‐related interhemispheric imbalance.

The observed alpha‐band ‘hyperconnectivity’ in the left hemisphere in patients with MDD may reflect a compensatory yet globally inefficient neural state. 6 When key regions such as the prefrontal cortex exhibit impaired regulatory capacity for emotion and cognition due to disease, other associated regions (e.g., the temporoparietal and occipital cortices) may increase FC in an attempt to maintain fundamental brain operations, resulting in a pattern of overcompensation. However, this hyperconnectivity is inherently energetically costly and rigid, compromising the efficiency of neural information processing and manifesting as core MDD symptoms, including cognitive rigidity, excessive self‐focus and heightened negative affect. Thus, this increase in connectivity represents a maladaptive pathological manifestation rather than an indicator of healthy neural function.

Our findings indicate that rTMS may promote more balanced and efficient interhemispheric functional specialisation by suppressing this inefficient compensatory connectivity—particularly the information flow from the LTP and the LFC to the OCC—thereby facilitating the improvements in emotional and attentional processing observed in our behavioural results.

These findings are consistent with existing literature: multiple studies have reported abnormally increased alpha‐band FC in the left frontal, temporal and parieto‐occipital regions in MDD, 4 with connectivity strength positively correlated with depression severity, 5 suggesting maladaptive neural compensation. Studies applying intermittent theta‐burst stimulation to the dorsomedial prefrontal cortex (dmPFC) also reported reduced connectivity between the left dlPFC and the posterior parietal region. 37 Other rTMS studies similarly reported decreased FC in the dlPFC‐striatal pathway. 38 The bilateral stimulation protocol used in this study—high‐frequency (20 Hz) excitation over the left hemisphere and low‐frequency (1 Hz) inhibition over the right—precisely targets abnormal compensatory connectivity in the left hemisphere, thereby facilitating the restoration of interhemispheric balance.

The LFC, LTP and OCC constitute a key network involved in emotion regulation, attentional control and visual information processing. Specifically, the LFC is responsible for top–down emotional modulation, LTP is associated with attentional allocation and self‐referential processing and the OCC is involved in the preliminary processing of emotional visual cues. Excessive connectivity among these regions may lead to heightened focus on negative emotional stimuli and reduced efficiency in attentional control. The reduction in FC following rTMS intervention suggests normalisation of information transmission within this network, thereby improving emotional processing capacity and cognitive efficiency. This neural mechanism is further supported by behavioural evidence: the active group presented significantly shorter reaction times under both congruent and incongruent emotional valence conditions, indicating enhanced attentional allocation and conflict resolution abilities.

Moreover, the significantly greater reduction in left hemispheric projections than in right projections further indicates that rTMS specifically modulates left hemispheric imbalance. This result is consistent with numerous previous studies demonstrating that rTMS can regulate FC within frontal‐striatal, 38 , 39 frontal‐occipital 40 and temporoparietal networks, 41 , 42 thereby promoting normalisation of whole‐brain network dynamics and ultimately leading to improvements in clinical symptoms.

The therapeutic efficacy of rTMS appears to be closely linked to its ability to modulate alpha‐band oscillations. Elevated alpha power and connectivity in patients with MDD are thought to reflect cortical–subcortical disinhibition and inefficient communication. 16 Active rTMS significantly suppressed exaggerated alpha‐phase coupling in the LFC, LTP and OCC, regions previously associated with emotional dysregulation and attentional deficits. 40 , 41 This suppression may enhance neural efficiency by desynchronising overly coherent alpha rhythms, thereby facilitating more adaptive processing of emotional and cognitive stimuli. 18 , 43 Furthermore, the greater reduction in left hemispheric versus right hemispheric connectivity underscores the role of bilateral rTMS in rebalancing hemispheric asymmetry—enhancing excitability in the left dlPFC while inhibiting right‐sided overactivity. 13 , 14

To further validate the relationship between rTMS neuromodulation and its clinical efficacy, we conducted correlation analyses between changes in PSI and improvements in behaviour and symptoms. The results showed that, in the active group, reduced left‐hemisphere PSI was significantly negatively correlated with enhanced efficiency of attentional networks (alerting and orienting effects), suggesting that decreased FC in the left hemisphere directly contributed to improved cognitive performance. Furthermore, reductions in PSI in both hemispheres were positively correlated with improved sleep quality, indicating that sleep improvement may not be a unilateral effect but rather a result of global network optimisation. This underscores the broad clinical significance of rTMS‐mediated rebalancing of interhemispheric function. In contrast, the sham group did not exhibit consistent neuro‐behavioural correlations, further supporting the fact that its improvements were primarily driven by nonspecific factors.

Our study sample comprised patients at different stages of MDD, including both first‐episode and recurrent presentations. We acknowledge that neuropathophysiological substrates, patterns of brain network compensation and potential responsiveness to neuromodulation may differ across these disease stages. 44 , 45 Nonetheless, our randomised group allocation ensured a balanced distribution of first‐episode and recurrent patients between the active and sham stimulation groups. This design feature protects the internal validity of our between‐group comparisons (i.e., the assessment of the specific rTMS effect) against confounding by disease stage. Consequently, the primary effect we observed, namely the modulation of left hemispheric alpha‐band functional hyperconnectivity by active rTMS, likely reflects a common, intervenable neural network pathology across disease stages rather than a stage‐specific artefact.

However, we did not perform a prespecified subgroup analysis based on disease stage. Therefore, we cannot precisely quantify or differentiate potential effect modifications of rTMS between first‐episode and recurrent patients. Future studies with larger samples are warranted to conduct prospective, stage‐stratified subgroup analyses. Such work is crucial to determine whether the therapeutic mechanisms of rTMS evolve with illness chronicity and to inform the development of more individualised, stage‐specific treatment strategies.

Limitations

However, this study has several limitations. First, the sample size was limited, and the exclusion of left‐handed participants led to the results being limited in generalisability for left‐handed patients with MDD. Second, patients with MDD continued to use medication during the treatment period, which may have affected the accuracy of the research results. Third, there is a lack of long‐term follow‐up data, making it impossible to evaluate the durability of the rTMS effects. Finally, this study focused on the alpha band; future research should extend to other frequency bands, such as the theta and gamma bands, to provide a more comprehensive understanding of rTMS‐induced modulation of brain networks.

Future research should include large‐scale, multicentre clinical trials involving diverse patient populations to validate the generalisability of the findings. Systematic long‐term follow‐up is necessary to assess the durability of therapeutic effects and the need for maintenance treatment. Additionally, comprehensive analysis of rTMS‐induced oscillations in the theta, beta and gamma bands should be performed via multiband EEG. Furthermore, stimulation parameters should be optimised, and multimodal neuroimaging (such as EEG‐fMRI) combined with causal modelling should be employed to clarify the mechanistic pathways linking rTMS‐induced network changes to symptom improvement, thereby advancing truly personalised treatment.

Implications

This study demonstrated that bilateral rTMS effectively modulates interhemispheric imbalance in the alpha band in patients with MDD. Behaviourally, it resulted in faster reaction times during emotional tasks; neurophysiologically, it normalised left hemispheric hyperconnectivity, particularly within the OCC‐LTP‐LFC network. These findings consistently support the view that rTMS attenuates inefficient compensatory connectivity in the left hemisphere, re‐establishes interhemispheric balance and thereby improves emotional and cognitive functioning.

Clinically, this study provides evidence supporting rTMS as a nonpharmacological alternative for treating patients with MDD, particularly those who are intolerant to or have contraindications for medication. The favourable tolerability of the treatment supports its clinical feasibility and acceptability.
