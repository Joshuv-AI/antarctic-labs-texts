Whole genome sequencing analyses of Chinese Glaesserella parasuis isolates reveal insights into their genomic characterization, virulence genes and antibiotic resistance genes
Authors: Qi Sun, Bo Hong, Chengjun Zhang, Dongxian He, Xuexiang Yu, Xugang Ku, Wei Zeng, Tuo Xu, Zhixian Yu, Shuo Zhang, Ping Bai, Wentao Li, Qigai He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGlaesserella parasuis is a pathogen that frequently targets the upper respiratory tract of pigs, and it poses a considerable threat to the pig industry. The aim of this study is to enrich the genetic database of G. parasuis, as well as increase the understanding of the population structure, genetic evolutionary characteristics, potential virulence factors and antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs) associated with this pathogen. This study utilized whole genome sequencing (WGS) to conduct genomic analyses on 123 G. parasuis isolates that were isolated from diseased pigs between 2017 and April 2022.ResultsThe findings of this study indicated that there was variation in both genome size (2.12 Mb ~ 2.54 Mb) and G + C content (39.50% ~ 39.99%). Multilocus sequence typing (MLST) analysis revealed 99 STs out of the 123 G. parasuis isolates, with 83 STs (83.84%) being new discoveries. Phylogenetic analysis revealed two major lineages distinguished by serovars and STs. Potential virulence genes (tufA, lonA, and apa1) in G. parasuis were first reported in this study. Multiple antibiotic resistance genes (floR, tet(B), tetR, aph(3’)-Ia, strA, strB, sul2 and blaRob−1) were widely present in G. parasuis. Additionally, both integrative and conjugative elements (ICEs) as well as integron mobile elements (IMEs) were shown to be significantly present in G. parasuis.ConclusionsBy analyzing genomic data from Chinese pig farms, we have gained valuable insights into the genotypes and antibiotic resistance of G. parasuis in swine populations, this knowledge will enhance our ability to prevent and control this disease effectively. The research findings indicate the prevalence of potential virulence factors, ARGs, ICEs and IMEs in G. parasuis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-026-12636-8.Keywords: Glaesserella parasuis, Whole genome sequencing, Multilocus sequence typing, Phylogeny, Virulence genes, Antimicrobial resistance genes, Integrative and conjugative element

Introduction

Glaesserella parasuis (formerly Haemophilus parasuis) is a Gram-negative bacterium of the Pasteurellaceae family, which is the causative agent of Glässer’s disease [1, 2]. G. parasuis causes pneumonia and pleurisy, polyserositis, meningitis and septicaemia [1, 3]. G. parasuis is also a major factor contributing to the porcine respiratory diseases complex (PRDC) [4], which is widely prevalent in several countries around the world and has caused incalculable economic losses to the pig industry [5, 6]. China is one of the largest pork producers in the world and has been heavily affected by G. parasuis, which is one of the most prevalent pathogens in both healthy and diseased pigs, which has a wide range of serotypes [7, 8]. However, multilocus sequence typing (MLST) is a genotyping method based on sequence data from 7 housekeeping genes that can define the population structure and genetic diversity of G. parasuis more accurately than serotyping [9–11].

The use of antibiotics plays a key role in the control of diseases caused by bacteria. A variety of transferable antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs) have been identified in the G. parasuis, including β-lactam resistance genes (blaROB−1 and blaTEM), aminoglycoside resistance genes (aac (6')-Ib-cr), tetracycline resistance genes (tetB, tetH and tet(O)), sulfonamide resistance genes (sul1 and sul2), and chloramphenicol resistance genes (flor) [12–18]. In the other hand, virulence genes have been identified to play a key role in the pathogenicity of G. parasuis, such as hemolysin operon (hhdBA), iron acquisition genes (tbpA/B and fhuA), neuraminidase (nanH), sialyltransferase genes (lsgB) and cytolethal distending toxins (CDTs) [19–22]. To date, however, there are still many ARGs and virulence genes of G. parasuis that have not been identified.

Integrative and conjugative elements (ICEs) are significant components of the bacterial mobile genetic elements (MGEs) that can carry virulence factors and antibiotic resistances, providing an important driving force for bacterial adaptation and evolution [23]. In addition, integrative and mobilizable elements (IMEs) are important carriers of bacterial virulence factors and antibiotic resistances, which cannot complete self-transferability but can be mobilized by ICEs or other splicing elements [24]. Furthermore, novel ICEs have been identified in G. parasuis, demonstrating their important implications for the spread of antimicrobial resistance [25, 26].

Recently, whole genome sequencing (WGS) has emerged as an efficient method for studying the genome sequences of bacterial pathogens, facilitating the identification of genomic variations [27] and the prediction of antibiotic resistance [28]. Using the WGS method, numerous studies have reported that the genomic diversity of G. parasuis is high, which is reflected in population structure, extensive genomic variation, pathogenicity and antibiotic resistance [29–32]. Recently, several studies have provided important information on the genetic characteristics, virulence factors and ARGs of G. parasuis. However, the distribution of virulence factors and antibiotic resistance genes and the genetic evolutionary relationships between G. parasuis are still unclear.

In this study, we conducted sequencing on 123 isolates of G. parasuis, which were isolated from Chinese pig farms between January 2017 and April 2022 and compared and analyzed their genomic characteristics, population structure, antibiotic resistance genes and potential virulence factors. These discoveries will not only enhance the genetic database of G. parasuis, improving our understanding of its population structure, genetic evolutionary characteristics, the distribution of virulence gene resistance gene, but will also provide insights into strategies for treating and preventing G. parasuis infections in China.

Results

General characterization, pan-core genome and ortho average nucleotide identity analysis

On average, the genome size obtained from each individual strain was 2.24 Mb (with a range of 2.12 Mb to 2.54 Mb), and the G + C content ranged from 39.50% to 39.99%, with a stable average of 39.84% (Table S2). The findings of the coding gene predictions showed that out of the 123 sequenced G. parasuis, the number of predicted coding genes varied from 2193 to 2547 for each individual strain. Additionally, the expected length of coding sequences (CDS) ranged from 793.11 to 900.68 bp on average and the data generated in this study have been deposited in the GenBank database (Table S3).

Heatmap was used to visualize the Ortho ANI values between the two isolates (Fig. 1). The analysis demonstrated that those sequenced isolates belong to the same species, with an Ortho ANI value ranging from 96.28% to 99.99%. According to the Ortho ANI clustering analysis, those G. parasuis isolates can be categorized into two primary groups. The reference strain SC1401 was categorized into a primary group with 69 other isolates. Another primary group was formed, consisting of 58 isolates, including reference strains SH0165, ZJ0906, SH0104, and HPS-1.

Heat map of Ortho ANI between each two isolates of G. parasuis. From blue to yellow to red, the similarity gradually increases

Based on the reference strain SH0165, we conducted a pan-genomic analysis on 123 sequenced isolates (Table S4). The analysis revealed that all isolates shared a total of 1452 genes, which were designated the core-genome, and constituted 57.01% to 66.21% of the total genome. Statistics relating to the dispensable genome revealed that the number of accessory genes ranging from 502 to 833, which accounted for between 22.51% and 32.71% of the total genome. In addition, the number of unique genes encountered a smaller variation, with values ranging from 0 to 31, which represented between 0.00% and 1.24% of the total genome. This result suggested that there was significant heterogeneity and diversity within the G. parasuis genome.

Multilocus sequence typing of G. parasuis

The numbers of alleles for seven housekeeping genes and the sequence types (STs) of 123 G. parasuis were determined and utilized in this study. The research findings indicated that within all seven housekeeping genes, infB displayed the highest number of alleles with a count of 30, followed by frdB (27), 6pgd (25), and mdh (24). In contrast, g3pd exhibited the lowest number of alleles, with only eight detected in this study. Furthermore, the seven housekeeping genes exhibited a total of 28 new alleles. Among these, atpD had the highest frequency of occurrence at 31.82% (7/22), followed by frdB (7/27, 25.93%), mdh (5/24, 20.83%), and infB (5/30, 16.67%). On the other hand, rpoB had the lowest frequency (1/13, 7.69%) (Table 1). The new alleles for each housekeeping gene were: atpD (55–60, 62), frdB (106–109, 111–113), mdh (73–75, 77, 78), infB (82–84, 86, 87), g3pd (28) and rpoB (35). We discovered a unique variation of the atpD gene (atpD62) that has a single base deletion at position 303 in its sequence. Typically, the length of the atpD gene is 519 bp, while the atpD62 is slightly shorter at 518 bp.

The 123 G. parasuis sequenced in this study were assigned to 99 STs, and 83 STs (83/99, 83.84%) were new. New STs were obtained: ST634-ST662, ST664-ST677, ST679-ST685, ST686-ST697, ST699-ST715, and ST717-ST721. Out of a total of 99 STs, ST387 was the most common with 7 isolates, followed by ST649 with 4 isolates, ST670 and ST680 with 3 isolates, and 10 other STs with only 2 isolates detected. This study analyzed a total of 123 isolates, which were classified into 11 different serovars, as well as nontypeable isolates (NT). Some isolates share the same serovar and sequence type (ST), such as ST649 (serovar 4) and ST361 (serovar 13). However, we also noted that within the seven isolates of G. parasuis belonging to ST387, there were four distinct serovars present. Furthermore, it has been noted that isolates from the same farm may share the same ST despite having different serovars. This suggested that there is no significant correlation between serovars and STs. Details of alleles, STs and serovars are listed in Table S5.

The goeBURST algorithm was used to cluster the STs of 123 G. parasuis isolates into 11 clonal complexes (CCs) and 63 singletons (Fig. 2). The largest CC was composed of ST179, ST468, ST636, ST688, ST699, and ST713, and the predicted founder is ST179. We also analyzed all the STs of G. parasuis (n = 911) in the PubMLST database (Fig. 3). The results showed that 723 STs (ST1-ST723) were clustered into 75 CCs and 350 singletons. The largest CC comprised 34 STs; within this CC, ST314 was identified as the group founder and six subgroup founders were also found, and this particular CC314 encompasses our earlier prediction of ST179 and ST219 (both are subgroup founder), which aligns with our previous projection regarding 123 isolates. CC670 was composed of ST670, ST634, ST648, ST684 and ST702, and the ST670 strain predicted to be the group founder. The ST669 strain uploaded in this study was predicted to be the group founder in a CC669 with 11 STs, but ST669 strain was predicted to be singleton in the prediction of 123 isolates. There seemed to be no significant correlation between CC and the time and location from which the isolates were isolated.

Phylogenetic analysis of G. parasuis isolates based on SNPs

A phylogenetic tree based on SNPs of our 123 G. parasuis and five reference isolates resolved two well-supported lineages (Fig. 4). Lineage Ⅰ (lineage in blue) contained a total of 58 isolates, including SH0165, SH0104, ZJ0906 and HPS-1; and lineage Ⅱ (lineage in green) contained 70 isolates, including SC1401. Serovars 5 and 14 were observed only in lineage Ⅰ (blue), while serovars 8, 9, and 15 were only present in the lineage Ⅱ (green); and serovars 1, 4, 7, 12, 13 and NT isolates were distributed in both lineages. The study found that isolates with the same ST were clustered in the same lineage, for instance, all seven ST387 isolates were identified in lineage Ⅱ (green), whereas the four ST649 isolates were present in lineage Ⅰ (blue). However, there was no observed correlation between phylogeny and the aspects of isolation such as region, year, site, or disease.

Potential virulence genes of G. parasuis isolates

Using the VFDB and PHI databases, we identified a total of 38 potential virulence genes across 123 G. parasuis isolates. Each of these isolates displayed a significant number of potential virulence genes, with a range between 25 and 33 genes per isolate (Fig. 5). The following 25 potential virulence genes were present in all 123 G. parasuis isolates, of which those annotated by the VFDB included: transferrin-binding protein A (tbpA) associated with iron intake; hemeB and hemeE associated with heme biosynthesis; kdsA, gmhA/lpcA, rfaD, rfaE, galU, galE, lpxC and lgtF associated with LOS; mrsA/glmM, yhxB/manB and pgi associated with exopolysaccharide; and tufA associated with attachment. Those annotated by the PHI database include: vacJ, htrA, iscU, lonA, metJ, hfq, dksA, leuC, smpB and guaB; with vacJ (outer membrane lipoprotein) and htrA (heat stress-requiring protein A) marked as genes for G. parasuis virulence. In addition to the 25 potential virulence genes mentioned above, we also identified various other potential virulence genes with differing distributions across 123 G. parasuis isolates. The highest number of detections was for ompP2 (118/123, 95.93%), followed by hsdR (65/123, 52.85%), oadB (54/123, 43.90%), gpT (41/123, 33.33%), wecB (37/123, 30.08%), ABZJ_RS06285 (37/123, 30.08%) and tbpB (22/123, 17.89%); and ompP2, hsdR and gpT were marked as genes for G. parasuis virulence. In addition, we predicted some potential virulence genes that were only distributed in a few isolates, such as: neuA (5.69%), apa1 (4.07%), A1S_0052 (2.44%), A1S_0053 (2.44%), rmlB (0.81%) and HI_RS04620 (0.81%). Moreover, we identified three new potential virulence genes (tufA, lonA, and apa1) that were previously not reported in G. parasuis.

The detection rates of potential virulence genes in 123 G. parasuis isolates

The distribution of the genes wecB and ABZJ_RS06285 was found to be identical among the 123 isolates. A1S_0052 and A1S_0053 were detected only in three G. parasuis isolates, all belonging to serovar 14. These three isolates were located on the same lineage but were distantly related. In addition, we found that the detection rates of ompP2 (54/54, 100%), oadB (51/54, 94.44%), hsdR (51/54, 94.44%), gpT (39/54, 72.22%) and tbpB (19/54, 35.19%) were higher in lineage Ⅰ (blue) of the phylogenetic tree than in lineage Ⅱ (green) (92.75%, 4.34%, 20.29%, 2.90% and 4.34%, respectively); but the detection rates of two genes (wecB and ABZJ_RS06285) (3/54, 5.56%) were lower in lineage Ⅰ (blue) of the phylogenetic tree than in lineage Ⅱ (green) (49.28%) (Fig. 7).

Antibiotic resistance genes of G. parasuis isolates

The CARD database was utilized to identify antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs) and discovered a total of 20 ARGs in 123 G. parasuis isolates. It was detected that several isolates would possess multiple ARGs, with some isolates of G. parasuis containing as many as nine different ARGs. A total of four β-lactamases were detected in this study, with the highest detection rate being blaRob−1 (20/123,12.26%), followed by blaRob−10 (4/123, 3.25%) and blaRob−3 (2/123, 1.63%), and only one strain carrying blaRob−2 (0.81%). A total of six aminoglycoside ARGs, aph(3’)-Ia, strA (aph(3’’)-Ib), strB (aph(6)-Id), aadA1 (ant(3’’)-Ia), aac(6’)-aph(2’’) and AAC(3)-IId, were detected in this study, with detection rates of 26.83% (33/ 123), 25.20% (31/123), 25.20% (31/123), 3.25% (4/123), 1.63% (2/123) and 0.81% (1/123), respectively; and the distribution of strA and strB among the 123 isolates were identical. Three tetracycline ARGs were detected, with the highest detection rate of 31.70% (39/123) for both tet(B) and tetR, and the distribution of isolates for these two ARGs was found to be the same. Additionally, three isolates of G. parasuis were found to carry tet(H). This study identified four phenicol ARGs, among which floR had the highest detection rate (32.52%, 40/123). The remaining three genes, catA8, catA3, and cmlA9, were detected at lower detection rates of 1.63%, 0.81%, and 0.81%, respectively. The three remaining ARGs were sulfonamide sul2 (31/123, 25.20%), lincosamide lnu(C) (5/123, 4.07%) and macrolide ErmT (3/123, 2.44%). In total, 20 ARGs were examined in this study, and the top seven were floR, tet(B), tetR, strA, strB, sul2 and blaRob−1, while the rest had a detection rate of less than 5% (Figs. 6 and 7).

ICE and IME of G. parasuis isolates

Upon scanning for integrative and conjugative element (ICE) and integrative mobilizable element (IME), a total of 44 ICEs were detected in 39 G. parasuis isolates (39/123, 31.73%). Remarkably, three of those isolates were found to contain two ICEs concurrently. Moreover, a total of 34 ICEs were detected in 33 G. parasuis isolates (33/123, 27.64%). There was also one strain that contained two IMEs at the same time. Furthermore, we uncovered 10 G. parasuis isolates that were found to carry both ICE and IME at the same time (10/123, 8.13%) (Fig. 7).

The statistics on antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs) distributed among ICEs and IMEs showed that five ICEs carried ARGs, with the largest number of ARGs carried by ICEs being five. The most commonly detected ARG was tet(B), which was found in four out of the five ICEs. In addition, six IMEs were found to carry ARGs, with the largest number of ARGs being four, and three different aminoglycoside ARGs were predicted to be present in the IMEs (Table 2).

Discussion

G. parasuis is a significant pathogen and one of the primary secondary agents of porcine respiratory disease complex (PRDC), exhibiting a high prevalence rate in China [4, 50, 51]. In this study, we conducted whole-genome sequencing on 123 G. parasuis isolates obtained from diseased pigs in China between 2017 and April 2022. Our analysis focused on comparing and analyzing their genomic characteristics, population structure, antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs), and potential virulence genes. The findings of this study offer valuable insights into the understanding of the genome of G. parasuis in China.

In this study, we sequenced 123 G. parasuis and determined that their average genome size was 2.24 Mb and the average G. parasuis genome G + C content was 39.84%. These results were consistent with G. parasuis genomic characteristics that have been previously described, indicating stability within a certain range of genome size and G + C content [36, 52, 53]. In our study, we found that the number of genes predicted in G. parasuis ranged from 2193 to 2547, which is a wider range than what was previously reported in the NCBI database and other studies [31, 32, 54]. The core genomes of 123 isolates in this study ranged from 57.01% to 66.21% (1452 genes), and the pan-genome of G. parasuis was an open pan-genome, which is consistent with previous studies [52]. In terms of the total genome, accessory genes comprised between 22.51% and 32.71%, while unique genes accounted for 0.00% ~ 1.24%. These results suggested that Chinese isolates exhibit significant diversity, and these genetic differences were critical for understanding the phylogeny, pathogenicity and environmental adaptability of G. parasuis [30, 32, 52, 53].

This study classified all G. parasuis isolates used in the MLST analysis. A total of 99 STs were identified in 123 G. parasuis isolates, of which 83 were new STs, and this finding significantly enhances the existing database. Furthermore, we have discovered a unique allele of the atpD gene (atpD62), which has a base deletion occurring at position 303 of its sequence, and this variant is the only known atpD with a base deletion allele that has been identified thus far (confirmed by using Sanger sequencing). This study also revealed that the serovars of identical ST isolates may differ, for example, among seven ST387 isolates, four different serovars were identified. This supported the finding that there was no discernible relationship between serovars and STs from the majority of earlier investigations [55, 56]. And the results of goeBURST analysis showed that there were a few CCs and a large number of singletons, which illustrated the lack of a stable population structure and the high level of heterogeneity within G. parasuis [56–58].

A maximum likelihood tree was constructed based on SNPs, which could divide our 123 G. parasuis isolates and five reference strains into two well-supported lineages, and two sub-lineages. However, we were unable to identify a definitive correlation between the population structure and the background of isolates (year, region, site, and disease). There was only a correlation between MLST and population structure, as isolates with the same ST were found on the same lineages. This also reflected the relevance of phylogenetic analysis based on SNPs and MLST [59]. The results revealed by the phylogeny are almost the same as those of previous studies, indicating that the transmission of G. parasuis may be intricate [30, 31].

In this study, we observed that each of the sequenced isolates contained a substantial number of potential virulence genes, ranging from 25 to 33. Additionally, we found that among the 123 G. parasuis isolates, 25 potential virulence genes were shared. These genes tbpA, rfaD, rfaE, galU, galE, vacJ and htrA were present in all 123 G. parasuis isolates and have been consistently linked with the pathogenicity of G. parasuis across numerous studies [21, 60–62]. Those three genes (ompP2, hsdR and gpT) were widespread in G. parasuis and had also been demonstrated to be strongly associated with the virulence of G. parasuis [63]. The research revealed that the five genes (ompP2, oadB, hsdR, gpT and tbpB) were more frequently present in lineage Ⅰ (blue). However, lineage Ⅱ (green) also had a notable occurrence of highly virulent serovars (specifically, serovars 1 and 13). As a result, it may not be totally accurate to determine the strain’s pathogenicity purely based on serovars [31, 64].

In agreement with previous studies, we also predicted a number of potential virulence genes that have been shown to be associated with virulence in other bacteria [30]. For instance, there are several genes linked to LOS, such as kdsA, gmhA/lpcA, lpxC, and lgtF, and it has been demonstrated that kdsA is closely associated with the virulence of Brevundimonas spp., similar to gmhA/lpcA and Haemophilus influenzae [65, 66]. Recently, a number of potential virulence genes (iscU, metJ, hfq, leuC and dksA) have been tagged in strain YHP1818 (GenBank: NZ_CP071487). The iscU gene has been found to impact the bacterial Fe-S cluster sensor. In Salmonella enterica, the absence of this gene can affect the efficiency with which the bacterium is able to invade epithelial cells [67]. Hfq genes can affect mRNA stability and translation, and several studies have shown that they are associated with virulence in a variety of bacteria, including A. pleuropneumoniae and Haemophilus ducreyi [68, 69]. The dksA gene has the ability to influence RNA polymerase-binding transcription factors, in H. ducreyi, the deletion of dksA can result in the dysregulation of virulence determinants [70]. In Shigella flexneri, dksA can regulate the expression of the hfq gene and thus affect the virulence of this bacterium [71]. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report describing the presence of the tufA, lonA, and apa1 genes in G. parasuis. For example, the tufA gene plays a key role in attachment by expressing elongation factor Tu (EF-Tu), which interacts with cell nucleolin, and has been shown to be associated with virulence in Brevundimonas spp [66]. According to studies, both the lonA and apa1 genes are potential virulence genes A. pleuropneumoniae that are linked to biofilm formation, colonization, and pathogenicity of the strain [72, 73].

In this study, a total of 20 ARGs were identified in 123 G. parasuis isolates. Numerous isolates carrying multiple ARGs, indicating that Chinese G. parasuis isolates have a wide range of ARGs. The highest detection rate of all ARGs was for floR, followed by two tetracycline ARGs (tet(B) and tetR), three aminoglycoside ARGs (aph(3’)-Ia, strA and strB) and the sulfonamide sul2 and β-lactamases blaRob−1. These ARGs have been reported in G. parasuis and are widely distributed, and the variance in detection rates could be attributed to antibiotic usage disparities in pig farms [16, 30]. Our study revealed that strA and strB were present in the same isolates as tet(B) and tetR, respectively. The co-occurrence of these similar ARGs may aggravate bacterial resistance against this class of drugs. BlaRob−10 is a recently discovered class A beta-lactamase mediating cephalosporin and penam resistance, which was annotated in CARD found in G. parasuis [48]. In our study, all isolates carrying blaRob−10 were isolated after 2021. However, it is worth noting that strain H160 (GenBank: WIGU01000059.1), which was found in the NCBI database to carry this same antimicrobial resistance gene, was actually isolated from China as early as in 2013.

Integrated conjugation elements (ICEs) and integrated mobile elements (IMEs) are essential components of the bacterial mobile genetic element (MGE); they all play vital roles in transmitting bacterial resistance [23]. In this research, we observed a detection rate of ICE at 31.71%, notably, three isolates were found to harbor two ICEs concurrently. Furthermore, the detection rate of IME was recorded at 27.64%, with ten G. parasuis isolates carrying both ICEs and IMEs, indicating a significant level of genetic plasticity. In this study, it was predicted that both ICE and IME carry several ARGs; and these components were found to be prevalent in various other bacteria as well, like S. suis, P. multocida. and A. pleuropneumoniae [25, 74, 75]. Those results suggested that ICEs and IMEs could potentially have a significant impact on gene (especially antibiotic resistance genes) transfer between various isolates [25].

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study has contributed to the G. parasuis genome database and identified numerous new STs, revealing greater diversity and heterogeneity in the genome. This study also identified potential virulence genes (tufA, lonA, and apa1) within G. parasuis, which were firstly reported. This study found a significant presence of ARGs (floR, tet(B), tetR, aph(3’)-Ia, strA, strB, sul2 and blaRob−1) in Chinese isolates. Additionally, the study indicated a substantial presence of both ICEs and IMEs. These findings suggest the need for further research on emerging antibiotic resistances and potential virulence factors.

Materials and methods

Isolate collection

The G. parasuis isolates involved in this study were identified and serotyped in the prior study [8] using relevant PCR assays [33, 34]. More information on the isolates can be found in Table S1.

Genome sequencing, assembly, and bioinformatics analysis

G. parasuis was cultured using tryptic soy broth (TSB) or tryptic soy agar (TSA) (Difco Laboratories, Detroit, USA) with 10 µg/mL nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD) (Biosharp Life Science, Anhui, China) and 10% (v/v) inactivated cattle serum (Henan Jushi Biotechnology Co., Zhengzhou, China). Genomic DNA from isolates was extracted using a TIANamp Bacteria DNA Kit (TIANGEN, Beijing, China), and the concentration and purity of the bacterial genomic DNA were monitored on 1% agarose gels and analyzed on a NanoDrop2000 system (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Pair-end sequencing was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq PE150, with a read length of 150 bp at each end (Tsingke Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China). Those data of WGS obtained in this study were assembled using SPAdes (v3.15.4) [35].

We selected SH0165 (CP001321) [36] as the reference strain, and the pan-genome of G. parasuis was analyzed using BPGA (v1.3.0) [37]. Ortho average nucleotide identity (Ortho ANI) was calculated by using OAT (v1.3.0) [38] with default parameters to elucidate the interspecific relationship of these isolates. A heatmap was generated, and correlation analysis was performed by hierarchical clustering using TBtools (v 1.098774) [39]. Sequence types (STs) were identified with the MLST services in the Center for Genomic Epidemiology (https://cge.food.dtu.dk/services/MLST/) [11] and the G. parasuis PubMLST database (https://pubmlst.org/organisms/glaesserella-parasuis) [40]. Global optimal eBURST (goeBURST v1.2.1) [41] was used to specifically examine the relationships within the clonal complexes (CCs) of STs of the 123 isolates investigated and all the STs at the G. parasuis PubMLST database, with visualization of the results using PHYLOViZ 2.0 [42]. Single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) were called, filtered and site validated by using CSI Phylogeny 1.4 (https://cge.food.dtu.dk/services/CSIPhylogeny/) (SH0165 [GenBank: CP001321] as reference; Select minimum distance between SNPs (prune): 10 bp; Select min. relative depth at SNP positions: 10%; Select min. Z-score: 1.96) [43]. Phylogeny was inferred based on the concatenated alignment of the high-quality SNPs of the 123 G. parasuis and 5 previously published genome sequences (SH0165 as reference, ZJ0906 [GenBank: CP005384], SC1401 [GenBank: NZ_CP015099], SH0104 [GenBank: NZ_CP024412] and HPS-1 [GenBank: CP040243]) [43]. Phylogenetic inference was conducted using a maximum-likelihood optimality criterion as implemented in W-IQ-TRE (https://www.hiv.lanl.gov/content/sequence/IQTREE/iqtree.html)(Substitution model: GTR; Bootstrap brach support: UltraFast, # of replicates = 1000; Single branch test: SH-aLRT branch test, # of replicates = 1000, Approximate Bayes test; Root tree: Simple midpoint) [44]and iTOLv6 (https://itol.embl.de/) [45]. Comparison of whole gene sequences with the Virulence Factors of Pathogenic Bacteria (VFDB) [46], Pathogen Host Interactions Database (PHI) [47] and Comprehensive Antibiotic Research Database (CARD) [48] using BLAST [49] (E-value≤1e− 5, minimal alignment length percentage ≥ 80%) for pathogenicity and drug resistance analysis. ICEs and IMEs were identified using ICEfinder (https://bioinfo-mml.sjtu.edu.cn/ICEfinder/ICEfinder.html ) [23].
