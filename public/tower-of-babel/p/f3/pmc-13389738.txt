The impact of pre-employment education on self-efficacy in newly graduated nurses: evaluation of a collaborative workshop between academia and clinical practice
Authors: Kohei Sekiguchi, Hirohito Nanbu, Masae Takamura, Koichi Yoshimura, Ai Ooyama, Yukari Katayama, Yasuyo Kusumoto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objective: This study aimed to evaluate the impact of pre-employment education on self-efficacy among newly graduated nurses. The intervention consisted of a workshop collaboratively developed by academic and clinical institutions.Materials and Methods: A single-group pretest–posttest design was employed. A one-day workshop was conducted in February 2025 for 24 newly graduated nurses scheduled for employment at a regional hospital. The workshop addressed proactive engagement, communication skills, stress management, and interaction with second-year nurses serving as role models. All 24 participants completed both pre- and post-workshop assessments. Self-efficacy was measured using the General Self-Efficacy Scale, and predictive factors were examined using validated instruments.Results: Self-efficacy scores significantly increased from 7.9 ± 3.1 to 9.0 ± 3.3 after the workshop (P=0.040). Significant improvements were also observed in confidence in nursing skills (P=0.001), social skills (P=0.015), confidence in proactive behavior (P=0.012), and clarity of career image (P=0.001). Multiple regression analysis indicated that changes in career image (β=−0.479, P=0.011) and social skills (β=0.359, P=0.050) were significant predictors of self-efficacy improvement (adjusted R2=0.314).Conclusion: The collaborative pre-employment workshop effectively enhanced self-efficacy in newly graduated nurses. Interventions that promote role modeling and interpersonal skill development before employment may represent a valuable approach to supporting professional readiness and retention.

Introduction

In recent years, the landscape of healthcare, medicine, and welfare in Japan has undergone rapid transformation. With increasing specialization and technological advancement, the roles expected of nursing professionals have become highly diverse, necessitating the development of nurses capable of effectively responding to societal needs.

In this context, the Wakayama Nursing Practice Research Center was established in 2021 through collaboration between a nursing university (hereafter referred to as University A) and a regional hospital (Hospital B). The center aims to support nurses in clinical practice, education, and research at both institutional and regional levels, thereby contributing to the enhancement of nursing quality and the healthcare system as a whole1).

As part of its activities, the center began hosting workshops in 2022 to address the reduction in clinical training opportunities for nursing students caused by the COVID-19 pandemic. These workshops were designed to help soon-to-be-employed nurses acquire essential nursing skills. Although infection prevention measures have since enabled students to resume some clinical practicums, the need for ongoing professional development remains. From 2024 onward, the workshop content was expanded beyond technical skill acquisition to include broader support strategies that foster the growth of newly graduated nurses.

Self-efficacy, as defined by Bandura, refers to an individual’s belief in their capability to perform the behaviors required to achieve a specific outcome2, 3). Individuals with higher self-efficacy are more likely to engage in challenging tasks and persist until completion despite difficulties2, 3). Longitudinal studies have demonstrated that self-efficacy at the beginning of employment influences new nurses’ motivation to remain in their positions up to six months later; higher self-efficacy is associated with a reduced intention to leave4).

Furthermore, several factors have been identified as being associated with self-efficacy among newly graduated nurses, including nursing skills5), social skills6), problem-solving orientation7), and continuous learning8). After entering the workforce, new nurses are immediately placed in specialized clinical environments and are expected to confront various challenges as members of the healthcare team. In such contexts, the capacity to view these challenges as opportunities for growth—and to persist despite difficulties—makes self-efficacy a crucial attribute.

We propose that providing pre-employment education focused on developing nursing skills, social skills, problem-solving orientation, and continuous learning can foster self-efficacy in newly graduated nurses. Moreover, previous studies have emphasized the importance of role models, indicating that new nurses often regard second-year nurses as professional benchmarks and strive to narrow the gap between themselves and these near-peer models9). Opportunities for dialogue with such role models before employment may enable nursing graduates to visualize their future professional identities more clearly and strengthen their confidence in their own abilities—making role-model interaction a potential predictor of self-efficacy.

Based on these considerations, we hypothesized that providing pre-employment education encompassing nursing skills, social skills, problem-solving orientation, continuous learning, and career image would enhance self-efficacy among newly graduated nurses. Although pre-employment training programs for new graduate nurses have been reported, few studies have specifically examined the relationship between such programs and self-efficacy. Furthermore, to the best of our knowledge, no previous studies have explored approaches aimed at fostering self-efficacy in newly graduated nurses through collaboration between educational institutions and clinical settings.

We therefore propose that educational collaboration between academic and clinical institutions can offer valuable support to strengthen new nurses’ ability to approach and overcome professional challenges with self-efficacy.

The purpose of this study was to clarify the impact of pre-employment education on the self-efficacy of newly graduated nurses.

Materials and Methods

1. Study design

A single-group pretest–posttest design was employed. A pre-employment workshop was implemented for newly graduated nurses, and surveys were administered before and after the intervention. Furthermore, assuming a medium effect size (d=0.5), a significance level of 0.05, and a statistical power of 0.8, the required sample size was calculated to be 34 participants.

2. Workshop overview and content

The objective of the workshop was to support newly graduated nurses in smoothly transitioning into clinical practice, enhancing their self-efficacy, engaging confidently in their duties, and adapting effectively to their work environment. Overview and specific methods of the pre-employment training workshop

Date conducted: February 2025 (1 session).

(1) Overview of the workshop

The lecture component covered two topics: Proactive Engagement in Clinical Practice and Building Interpersonal Relationships and Effective Stress Management. These lectures were delivered by university faculty members (approximately 12 years of teaching experience) and facility managers (approximately 15 years of clinical experience and 5 years of teaching experience). The practice and dialogue components included nursing-skill training and interaction with second-year nurses. Second-year nurses provided hands-on technical guidance and shared their professional experiences. University faculty members (approximately 12 years of teaching experience; five individuals) and facility managers (approximately 15 years of clinical experience and 5 years of teaching experience; three individuals) supervised workshop operation and time management.

To examine the validity of the workshop, we hypothesized that stimulating predictors of self-efficacy in pre-employment novice nurses—such as nursing skills, social skills, problem-solving orientation, lifelong learning, and career image—would enhance their overall self-efficacy. Therefore, the workshop’s validity was evaluated by assessing changes in self-efficacy and its associated predictors before and after participation.

(2) Implementation of the Workshop

Lectures were delivered in a classroom setting incorporating elements of active learning among participants. The workshop also included five activity booths—four dedicated to skill practice and one to dialogue. Each booth accommodated 6–9 participants, who were divided into 2–3 subgroups of three participants each. Participants rotated sequentially through the booths.

3. Data collection

(1) Study period

February 2025

(2) Participants

The workshop was conducted at the Wakayama Nursing Practice Research Center, which is jointly operated by University A and Hospital B. The participants comprised 65 nursing graduates scheduled to begin employment at Hospital B.

(3) Recruitment and consent procedures

(i) Approval was obtained from the heads of the participants’ nursing schools and their future employing facilities. The research objectives and procedures were explained both verbally and in writing.

(ii) The workshop was promoted to nursing graduates during scheduled gatherings at Hospital B using posters. Interested participants accessed a registration form through a QR code linked to Microsoft Forms.

(iii) At the workshop venue, the study was explained to participants both orally and in writing. Those who provided informed consent completed an online questionnaire via Microsoft Forms and an anonymous self-administered paper questionnaire. Each participant created a unique password (six or more alphanumeric characters) prior to the workshop to allow their pre- and post-intervention responses to be linked anonymously. The paper questionnaires were submitted in a sealed collection box.

4. Survey items

Pre- and post-workshop surveys were administered using both online and paper-based questionnaires. The following items were assessed:

(1) Pre-workshop survey

(i) Demographic information: age, gender, and nursing education institution.

(ii) General self-efficacy:

The General Self-Efficacy Scale10) was used. This validated 16-item instrument consists of three subscales: Initiative in Action, Anxiety about Failure, and Social Positioning of Ability. Items are scored dichotomously (Yes=1, No=0), with some items reverse-coded. The total score ranges from 0 to 16, with higher scores indicating greater self-efficacy.

(iii) Predictive factors related to self-efficacy:

1. Confidence in nursing skills

Assessed using a 10-cm Visual Analog Scale (VAS), ranging from 0 (no confidence) to 100 (very confident).

2. Social skills

Measured using Kikuchi’s Social Skill Scale-18 (KiSS-18)11). This validated instrument includes 18 items across six subscales: basic skills, advanced skills, emotional processing, non-aggressive conflict resolution, stress management, and planning skills. Items are scored on a five-point scale (1–5), with higher total scores (range: 18–90) indicating stronger social skills.

3. Confidence in proactive behavior (reflecting problem-solving orientation and continuous learning)

Assessed using a 10-cm VAS ranging from 0 (no confidence) to 100 (very confident).

4. Career image

Defined as the envisioned self as a nurse one year after employment. Measured using a 10-cm VAS ranging from 0 (no image) to 100 (clear image).

5. Trait anxiety

Trait anxiety was assessed using the Japanese version of the State-Trait Anxiety Inventory Form JYZ revised by Hidano et al12). This validated 20-item instrument contains both positively and negatively worded items, scored on a four-point scale (1–4). Total scores range from 20 to 80, with higher scores indicating greater anxiety. Trait anxiety reflects relatively stable personality characteristics and is considered an important predictor of self-efficacy13). Although a further revised version of this instrument was published in 2021, the 2017 edition obtained before the start of the study was used. Because the 2021 revision was a refinement of the existing scale, it was judged unlikely to substantially affect the present study’s objective of measuring trait anxiety.

(2) Post-workshop survey

The same self-efficacy and predictive-factor items were reassessed after the workshop.

5. Data analysis

The normality of the variable distributions (self-efficacy, confidence in nursing skills, social skills, confidence in proactive behavior, career image, and trait anxiety) was examined using the Shapiro–Wilk test.

Paired t-tests were performed to compare pre- and post-workshop scores for each variable.

Multiple-regression analysis (using the forced-entry method) was conducted to identify predictors of change in self-efficacy. The dependent variable was the change in self-efficacy score (post − pre), and the independent variables were the changes in factors that showed significant differences in Step 2.

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 30.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). A two-tailed P-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

(1) Self-efficacy

Self-efficacy scores significantly increased from 7.9 ± 3.1 before the workshop to 9.0 ± 3.3 after the workshop, with a mean difference of 1.1 ± 2.5 (P=0.040, Cohen’s d=0.36).

(2) Predictive factors influencing self-efficacy

(i) Confidence in nursing skills:

Increased from 34.9 ± 16.4 to 57.2 ± 23.7, with a mean difference of 22.3 ± 19.0 (P=0.001, d=1.36).

(ii) Social skills:

Increased from 66.1 ± 9.1 to 68.0 ± 9.3, with a mean difference of 1.9 ± 3.5 (P=0.015, d=0.21).

(iii) Confidence in proactive behavior:

Increased from 55.9 ± 22.8 to 64.8 ± 22.8, with a mean difference of 8.9 ± 16.0 (P=0.012, d=0.39).

(iv) Career image:

Increased from 53.0 ± 27.6 to 68.0 ± 18.6, with a mean difference of 15.0 ± 19.0 (P=0.001, d=0.54).

(v) Trait anxiety:

Slightly decreased from 40.9 ± 8.4 to 39.9 ± 9.1, with a mean change of −1.0 ± 4.6; however, this difference was not statistically significant (P=0.245, d=0.12).

Discussion

The results of this study demonstrated that the educational workshop developed through collaboration between academic and clinical institutions contributed to an increase in self-efficacy among newly graduated nurses prior to employment. Furthermore, confidence in nursing skills, social skills, confidence in proactive behavior, and career image—all identified as predictive factors influencing self-efficacy—were improved through participation in the workshop.

In addition, multiple-regression analysis revealed that changes in career image and social skills were the most influential predictors of increased self-efficacy. Previous studies of newly graduated nurses have reported that multiple-regression models predicting self-efficacy accounted for approximately 17.8–31.0% of the variance6, 8).

In the present study, career image and social skills—identified as predictors influencing self-efficacy in pre-employment newly graduated nurses—explained 31.4% of the variance. This relatively high explanatory power provides statistically persuasive support for the study hypothesis.

1. Self-efficacy

In recent years, the roles expected of nursing professionals have become increasingly diverse because of advances and specialization in healthcare. Newly graduated nurses are expected to assume various responsibilities as full members of clinical organizations from the beginning of their employment. In this context, self-efficacy—defined as the belief in one’s capability to perform the actions necessary to achieve specific outcomes2)—serves as a crucial psychological resource. Individuals with higher self-efficacy are more likely to engage in challenging tasks, persevere, and complete them despite difficulties13).

Participants in this study exhibited a pre-workshop mean self-efficacy score of 7.9, which was higher than the average score of 6.0 reported for newly employed nurses4). Their average social skill score was 66.1, exceeding the mean of 58.3 reported among female university students11), and their trait anxiety score was 40.9, lower than the average of 47.712). These results suggest that participants had moderately high self-efficacy, average interpersonal skills, and typical anxiety traits, implying that they were capable of coping with challenges, building relationships, and maintaining emotional stability.

The workshop was designed to stimulate these factors by integrating evidence from previous studies. Confidence in nursing skills5), social skills6), problem-solving orientation7), and lifelong learning8) have all been identified as contributors to self-efficacy. In addition, prior research has shown that second-year nurses frequently serve as role models for newly graduated nurses and influence their career-image formation9). Accordingly, the workshop incorporated opportunities for interaction with second-year nurses to foster the development of a realistic career image.

The strength of this study lies in its implementation of an academic–clinical partnership. Theoretical lectures were complemented by practice-based learning facilitated by second-year nurses. This early exposure enabled participants to enhance their self-efficacy even before entering clinical practice, as reflected by the significant increase in mean scores from 7.9 to 9.0.

Self-efficacy typically peaks at the time of employment and declines within the first six months, likely due to the unfamiliarity of the work environment and initial difficulties in achieving success4). Therefore, pre-employment intervention may serve as a buffer against this decline. The present findings support this hypothesis, suggesting that early reinforcement of self-efficacy promotes persistence and resilience during the transition to clinical roles.

2. Predictive factors and support for enhancing self-efficacy

Significant improvements were observed in confidence in nursing skills, social skills, confidence in proactive behavior, and career image. Multiple-regression analysis indicated that changes in career image (β=−0.479) and social skills (β=0.359) were the most influential predictors of self-efficacy enhancement.

Aligning expectations with actual nursing practice can help prevent transition shock. Discrepancies between expectations and reality have been shown to reduce newly graduated nurses’ self-efficacy14). In this study, dialogue with second-year nurses likely assisted participants in visualizing their future roles and establishing attainable goals. Limited clinical exposure before employment may also undermine confidence and preparedness15), highlighting the importance of providing realistic pre-employment experiences.

Interpersonal factors—such as social skills, peer support, and team relationships—play a central role in help-seeking behavior6). Workshop components addressing communication and stress management likely enhanced perceived competence in this area. While not CBT-based, the structured format and peer interaction may have had similar benefits, as CBT has been shown to improve self-efficacy and resilience16). The involvement of second-year nurses provided mentorship that supported both emotional well-being and career identity development17). Although trait anxiety did not show a statistically significant change in this study, it is considered a stable personality characteristic12, 13). Because participants already exhibited average levels of anxiety and the workshop was conducted one month before employment, job-entry-related anxiety may not yet have been fully activated at the time of data collection.

3. Relevance to international research

Internationally, core competencies and self-efficacy have been shown to be negatively correlated with transition shock18). Likewise, pre-graduation characteristics and workplace environments have been identified as influential factors affecting the transition experiences of newly graduated nurses19). These findings indicate that strengthening self-efficacy before employment is essential for facilitating a smoother transition into professional practice.

Moreover, mentoring programs have demonstrated benefits such as reduced anxiety and enhanced satisfaction among nursing students; improvements in self-efficacy have also been suggested20), supporting the design of our workshop that included guidance from second-year nurses.

Limitations

This study has several limitations.

First, the sample size was relatively small, which may have reduced the statistical power to detect significant differences. Therefore, effect sizes were reported alongside P-values to complement the interpretation and strengthen the scientific validity of the findings.

Second, the participants in this study exhibited slightly higher baseline levels of self-efficacy. Consequently, the outcomes may differ when applied to individuals with average or lower levels of self-efficacy.

Third, although self-efficacy significantly improved following the workshop, previous research has indicated that self-efficacy tends to decline within the first six months after employment. Hence, longitudinal studies are warranted to examine the sustainability of this effect.

Finally, because this study employed a single-group pretest–posttest design, future research should adopt a comparative design incorporating both control and intervention groups to enable more rigorous evaluation of the workshop’s content and effectiveness.

Conclusions

The collaborative workshop between academic and clinical institutions significantly enhanced the self-efficacy of newly graduated nurses. Improvements in career image and social skills—key predictors of self-efficacy—were particularly important. Providing opportunities for newly graduated nurses to align their career image with realistic role models and to strengthen interpersonal communication skills represents essential educational support for promoting self-efficacy prior to employment.

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee for Human Subjects of Tokyo Healthcare University (Approval No.: T24-06B). The purpose, significance, and procedures of the research were explained to participants both verbally and in writing. Participation was voluntary, and students were informed that they could refuse participation without any disadvantage. Anonymity was maintained; therefore, withdrawal after submission was not possible. Consent was obtained by checking the consent box on Microsoft Forms or by submitting an anonymous paper-based questionnaire. Submission of either form was regarded as consent to participate in the study. No personal identifiers were collected, and pre- and post-workshop responses were linked using self-set passwords. Participants were informed that the collected data would be used solely for academic publication. Additionally, the self-efficacy and social skill scales were used under free-use terms for academic and non-profit purposes, while the trait anxiety scale was purchased from the publisher and used under license.
