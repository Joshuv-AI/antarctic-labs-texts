Methotrexate-induced myelopathy: a great imitator of relapsed malignant lymphoma
Authors: Miwako Ishikawa, Hikaru Watanabe, Hirotomo Nakashima, Tadashi Ozawa, Yuhei Anan, Reiji Koide, Ryota Tanaka, Shigeru Fujimoto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMethotrexate-induced myelopathy is a rare adverse effect of intrathecal methotrexate, characterized by acute or subacute myelopathy–like neurological manifestations and magnetic resonance imaging findings resembling subacute combined degeneration. Because neurological symptoms often precede radiological abnormalities, accurate diagnosis can be challenging, particularly in patients with hematological malignancies, in whom central nervous system relapse must also be considered.Case presentationA 61-year-old man with extranodal natural killer/T-cell lymphoma, nasal type, developed progressive lower limb weakness and sensory disturbance four days after his 11th course of intrathecal methotrexate for central nervous system relapse. Initial spinal magnetic resonance imaging showed no abnormalities; however, subsequent imaging revealed longitudinally extensive, ascending T2-hyperintense lesions in the posterior and lateral columns of the thoracic spinal cord without gadolinium enhancement. Methotrexate-induced myelopathy was diagnosed based on clinical and radiological findings. Despite intravenous administration of vitamin B12 and folate, as well as oral supplementation with S-adenosylmethionine, spinal lesions extended rostrally without further neurological deterioration. One month later, the patient developed progressive unilateral axonal neuropathy of the left lower limb. Subsequent skin biopsy confirmed relapse of extranodal natural killer/T-cell lymphoma.ConclusionsThis case illustrates that methotrexate-induced myelopathy may present with delayed radiological abnormalities and can closely mimic relapse of the underlying malignancy. Furthermore, the development of peripheral axonal neuropathy during the clinical course of methotrexate-induced myelopathy should prompt careful evaluation for disease recurrence in patients with hematological malignancies.Keywords: Methotrexate-induced myelopathy, Aggressive lymphoma, Subacute combined degeneration, Autologous hematopoietic stem cell transplantation

Introduction

Methotrexate (MTX) is a key agent in the treatment of malignant lymphoma with central nervous system (CNS) involvement [1]. However, MTX also carries a risk of CNS toxicity, including myelopathy as a rare outcome [2]. This condition is characterized by neurological findings suggestive of acute transverse myelitis and imaging findings resembling subacute combined degeneration [3–5]. Due to its rarity and the fact that neurological symptoms frequently precede imaging abnormalities, diagnosis can be challenging [6]. Differentiation from CNS relapse of the underlying hematological malignancy is essential.

We report a case of MTX-induced myelopathy that developed four days after completing the 11th course of intrathecal MTX for CNS relapse of extranodal NK/T-cell lymphoma, nasal type (ENKTL). Following diagnosis, autologous hematopoietic stem cell transplantation was performed, but the patient exhibited progression of axonal neuropathy in the left lower extremity one month later. A subsequent skin biopsy for newly appearing erythema confirmed relapse of ENKTL.

Case presentation

A 61-year-old man undergoing treatment for malignant lymphoma was admitted to our hospital. Seven years earlier, he had been diagnosed with stage I extranodal NK/T-cell lymphoma, nasal type (ENKTL), by nasal biopsy (soluble interleukin-2 receptor 1180 U/mL, Epstein-Barr virus-DNA 1.3 × 102 copies). He achieved remission following three courses of dexamethasone, etoposide, ifosfamide and carboplatin (DeVIC) chemotherapy and 50 Gy of radiotherapy. One year before this presentation, erythematous skin lesions developed, and a skin biopsy confirmed relapse of NK/T-cell lymphoma. Five months prior to presentation, gastrointestinal bleeding occurred as an adverse event on day 11 of dexamethasone, methotrexate, ifosfamide, L-asparaginase, and etoposide (SMILE) chemotherapy, leading to discontinuation of chemotherapy. Intrathecal MTX-containing injections were continued for leptomeningeal involvement. Four months prior to presentation, the MTX dose had been reduced to 1 g/m2, and a second course of SMILE therapy had been administered. Two months prior to presentation, Positron Emission Tomography with Computed Tomography (PET-CT) revealed faint uptake in the left tibia. Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis showed 15 cells/μL with cytology class V, consistent with CNS involvement. Because cerebrospinal fluid cytology confirmed central nervous system involvement, spinal MRI was not performed at that time. Dexamethasone, high-dose cytarabine, and cisplatin (DHAP) therapy was therefore initiated. During treatment, CSF cytology remained class I.

Four days before the onset of neurological symptoms, the patient received his 11th cycle of intrathecal MTX (15 mg), cytarabine (40 mg), and prednisolone (10 mg). At that time, CSF examination revealed 1 cell/μL and cytology class I. Four days later, he was referred to the neurology department for abnormal sensations in bilateral lower limbs. Examination revealed proximal-dominant lower limb weakness with a positive Babinski sign on the right. Lumbar spine MRI showed no marked abnormalities. On day 5, bladder and rectal disturbance appeared. MRI of the thoracic spine performed on day 7 showed no abnormalities. By day 9, muscle strength had declined to Medical Research Council (MRC) grade 1 in the iliopsoas, quadriceps, and hamstrings. CSF cell count and cytology remained normal. Considering the possibility of MTX-induced myelopathy, supplementation with vitamin B12 and folate was initiated. On day 10, MRI of the spine demonstrated longitudinally extensive hyperintense lesions in the posterior columns of Th9–Th12 on the T2-weighted image without gadolinium enhancement (Fig. 1).

Differential diagnoses included CNS relapse, varicella-zoster virus (VZV) myelitis [7], and immune-mediated disorders such as multiple sclerosis and neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder [8]. However, CSF cell counts were not elevated, and both multiplex PCR and VZV-DNA PCR yielded negative results. CSF cytology, oligoclonal bands, and immunoglobulin G index were negative, while CSF levels of IL-6 and myelin basic protein (MBP) were elevated. Blood tests were negative for antinuclear antibody, anti-SS-A, and anti-SS-B antibodies; serum vitamin B12 was within normal limits, with mild folate deficiency. Other potential diagnoses were excluded, and subsequent spinal MRI revealed T2-hyperintense lesions in both posterior and lateral columns, suggestive of nutritional or metabolic disorders (e.g., vitamin B12 deficiency, copper deficiency, nitrous oxide toxicity) [9].

Paraneoplastic neurological syndromes were considered less likely, as no relevant onconeural antibodies were identified except Tr (paraneoplastic cerebellar degeneration) and mGluR5 (limbic encephalitis), and the clinical course was inconsistent [10].These findings are characteristic of MTX-induced myelopathy, leading to a definitive diagnosis [9].

MTX-induced myelopathy is a therapy-related spinal cord disorder associated with intrathecal chemotherapy [2]. The pathophysiology remains unclear, and definitive treatment has yet to be established. Previous reports have suggested potential benefits from multiple folate-related metabolites [2]. In this case, vitamin B12 and folate supplementation were initiated from day 10. On day 24, the MRI lesions became more conspicuous (Fig. 2). Despite extension of the spinal lesion up to Th5 on day 29, neurological deficits did not worsen, and mild recovery of bilateral lower limb strength (from MRC grade 0 to 2) was observed after one month (Fig. 3). However, bladder and rectal dysfunction persisted with poor clinical outcome. On day 44, spinal MRI revealed further extension to Th2 without neurological deterioration (Fig. 4). Based on previous reports describing recovery with S-adenosylmethionine (SAMe) supplementation [11, 12], therapy with SAMe (600 mg/day in three divided doses) was initiated on day 50 with approval from the institutional ethics committee and the consent of the patient. As a conditioning regimen prior to transplantation, high-dose chemotherapy with TBC (thiotepa, busulfan, and cyclophosphamide) was administered, followed by autologous hematopoietic stem cell transplantation on day 52. Follow-up thoracic MRI showed no further progression of spinal lesions (Fig. 5). However, on day 69, weakness of the left tibialis anterior worsened (MRC grade 2 → 1), with nerve conduction studies revealing delayed latency and reduced amplitude in the left peroneal nerve. On day 74, weakness of the left gastrocnemius (MRC grade 4 → 1) was observed, while the right leg remained unchanged. Serial nerve conduction studies showed progressive amplitude reduction predominantly in the left leg, raising suspicion of axonal degeneration. MRI of the lower extremities revealed atrophy and Short TI Inversion Recovery (STIR) hyperintensity predominantly in the fascia of the left flexor digitorum longus, peroneus, and gastrocnemius, suggesting ongoing denervation. Although further investigations were considered to clarify the cause of the axonal polyneuropathy and muscle atrophy, they were not performed due to the patient's refusal of invasive procedures. Four months after onset, cutaneous relapse of NK/T-cell lymphoma was confirmed by skin biopsy, and additional systemic treatment was withheld. The progressive unilateral axonal neuropathy, muscle atrophy on MRI, and clinical history of relapsed lymphoma raised suspicion for neurolymphomatosis.

Although contrast-enhanced MRI and (18)F-fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) -PET/CT were not performed, neurolymphomatosis due to peripheral nerve infiltration of lymphoma was considered in the differential diagnosis.

No neurological recovery was observed following SAMe supplementation, which was discontinued due to neutropenia. Neutrophil counts recovered promptly after discontinuation.

Discussion

This case provides several important clinical insights into the diagnosis and management of MTX-induced myelopathy.

First, MTX-induced myelopathy is characterized by neurological findings suggestive of transverse myelitis and MRI features resembling subacute combined degeneration [4, 5]. Notably, radiological abnormalities may appear after the onset of neurological symptoms. In patients with malignant lymphoma presenting with paraplegia, intramedullary spinal cord lymphoma should be considered in the differential diagnosis. Most cases represent secondary involvement associated with leptomeningeal dissemination rather than isolated lesions. Intramedullary spinal cord lymphoma typically presents as poorly circumscribed T1-isointense and T2-hyperintense lesions with contrast enhancement and variable spinal cord enlargement, reflecting its infiltrative growth pattern. Diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) may also aid in differentiation, as lymphoma often demonstrates restricted diffusion due to high cellularity [13].

In the present case, however, spinal MRI showed longitudinally extensive T2-hyperintense lesions without gadolinium enhancement or significant spinal cord enlargement. In addition, radiological abnormalities appeared after the onset of neurological symptoms, and the clinical course was not consistent with the rapid and infiltrative progression typically observed in spinal cord lymphoma. These findings argue against intramedullary lymphoma.

Second, if neurological deterioration occurs during the remission phase of MTX-induced myelopathy, central nervous system relapse of the underlying malignancy should be considered [14, 15]. In this context, paraneoplastic neurological syndromes may also be included in the differential diagnosis. Paraneoplastic neurological syndromes associated with malignant lymphoma have been reported, particularly in relation to anti-mGluR5 antibodies [16]. However, these antibodies are most commonly associated with Hodgkin lymphoma, whereas the present case involved a non-Hodgkin lymphoma, making such an association less likely. Anti-Tr/DNER antibodies have also been reported in association with lymphoma and are typically associated with subacute progressive cerebellar ataxia [17]. This clinical phenotype differed from that observed in the present case, suggesting a low likelihood of a causal relationship. Therefore, the detected paraneoplastic antibodies were considered unlikely to account for the neurological manifestations in this patient.

MTX-induced myelopathy is a treatment-related complication associated with intrathecal chemotherapy [2]. The exact pathophysiology remains unclear. MTX inhibits dihydrofolate reductase in the methionine cycle, reducing levels of tetrahydrofolate, a critical methyl donor. This leads to impaired methylation of DNA, RNA, amino acids, proteins, and phospholipids, as well as reduced polyamine synthesis. In addition, homocysteine and adenosine accumulate. These biochemical changes are hypothesized to contribute to neurotoxicity through impaired methylation and the accumulation of toxic metabolites [2].

A comparable pathophysiological mechanism is seen in subacute combined degeneration due to vitamin B12 deficiency, as vitamin B12 acts as a cofactor in the methionine cycle [18]. Both MTX-induced myelopathy and B12-deficiency-related subacute combined degeneration share MRI findings of T2 hyperintensities in the posterior and lateral columns of the spinal cord [9], suggesting mechanistic similarities.

While B12 deficiency is treatable with vitamin B12 supplementation [7, 10], no established therapy exists for MTX-induced myelopathy. Although several reports have suggested benefit from supplementation with various folate metabolites [13, 14], clinical responses appear inconsistent. Two case reports have described neurological improvement with supplementation of SAMe, a downstream metabolite in the methionine cycle [11, 12].

MTX-induced myelopathy has also been associated with genetic polymorphisms in the methylenetetrahydrofolate reductase (MTHFR) gene. MTHFR polymorphisms reduce the methylation capacity of tetrahydrofolate, impairing downstream metabolic cycles [19–21]. In the present case, genetic testing for MTHFR polymorphisms was not performed due to lack of patient consent. However, supplementation with SAMe (600 mg, three times daily) was initiated on day 50 after symptom onset. Despite this, the patient developed progressive axonal neuropathy in the left lower extremity. Muscle atrophy and STIR hyperintensities in the left flexor digitorum longus, fibularis, and gastrocnemius muscles were observed on plain MRI, suggesting progression of denervation [14, 15, 22, 23].

Although gadolinium-enhanced MRI and FDG-PET/CT were not performed, the clinical course and imaging findings may be consistent with neurolymphomatosis due to CNS relapse of NK/T-cell lymphoma [14, 15, 22, 23]. One report has described the diagnostic challenge of distinguishing MTX-associated peripheral neuropathy from neurolymphomatosis [24]. However, because reports of MTX-associated peripheral neuropathy are rare, clinicians should primarily suspect disease recurrence when axonal neuropathy develops during the course of MTX-induced myelopathy.

Conclusion

MTX can induce therapy-resistant myelopathy. Neurological features often mimic transverse myelitis, and spinal MRI frequently demonstrates findings resembling subacute combined degeneration. The MRI findings in the present case were distinct from those typically observed in intramedullary spinal cord infiltration by malignant lymphoma.

The radiological lesions can ascend during the clinical course. When peripheral neuropathy progresses despite stabilization of MTX-induced myelopathy, further evaluation for relapse of the underlying malignancy is essential.
