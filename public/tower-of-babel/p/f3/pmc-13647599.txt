Modified apexification with MTA and non-vital bleaching as a comprehensive case management of a necrotic immature tooth: A case report
Authors: Udit Shankar, Navneet Kaur, Anjali Tuteja, Harpreet Kaur Gill
Published: 2026

Background

“Apexification is defined as a method of treatment for immature permanent teeth in which root growth and development ceased due to pulp necrosis. It aims to induce root-end closure without promoting canal wall thickening or continuous root lengthening. Apexification can be achieved in two ways: (1) as a long-term procedure using calcium hydroxide (CH) dressing to allow the formation of a biologic hard tissue barrier, or (2) as a short-term procedure, creating an artificial apical plug of Mineral Trioxide Aggregate (MTA) or other bioceramic material” [1].

CH apexification typically takes many months for the formation of a calcified tissue barrier at the apex, and this barrier has a limited ability to seal and sets poorly. In contrast, MTA offers superior sealing properties and can serve as an immediate apical barrier. Consequently, in recent years, MTA and Biodentine have largely replaced CH in apexification procedures [2]. Regenerative endodontics represents an innovative and rapidly advancing area in treating immature teeth with necrotic pulpal tissue. This approach has been characterized as a “paradigm shift” offering potential for continued root development and root end closure [3].

The glossary of endodontic terms defines “regenerative endodontics as biologically-based procedures designed to physiologically replace damaged tooth structures, including dentin and root structures, as well as cells of the pulp-dentin complex” [4]. These focus on healing apical lesions, relieving symptoms, and supporting continued root development. They also help strengthen dentin to reduce the risk of root fractures while promoting the regeneration of the pulp-dentin complex, restoring its normal function, immune response, and sensory perception [5–7]. Despite their effectiveness in healing apical lesions, the success rate of pulp regeneration through REPs remains uncertain. Further long-term clinical studies and histological evaluations are needed to establish their reliability and practical use in everyday dental treatments [8].

In scenarios involving significant coronal structure loss due to trauma or decay in immature young permanent teeth with necrotic pulpal tissue, where an intraradicular post/core is essential, apexification is deemed a more suitable treatment approach than regenerative endodontic. The American Association of Endodontists (AAE) also supported this finding [7, 9]. To enhance patient compliance and reduce treatment time, single-visit MTA apexification is preferred to CH apexification. However, to address the absence of continued apical root development following traditional apexification, the modified apexification technique [10] seems to be the preferred treatment choice for this case.

Case presentation

A male patient, aged 11 years presented with the chief complaint of pain in the upper front tooth region persisting for the past three months. He reported a history of trauma that occurred 4–5 years ago, during which the upper front tooth was fractured after hitting it against a water tap. However, the broken fragment could not be located. Following the incident, the child’s parent consulted a general physician, who provided symptomatic treatment. The patient also reported a history of bleeding from the tooth at the time of trauma but denied any history of nausea, vomiting, or unconsciousness.

The tooth exhibited a complicated crown fracture with an oblique fracture line extending from the mesial cervical third to the incisal edge, with no clinical or radiographic signs of luxation injury (Fig. 1a). Mild discoloration and tenderness to percussion without mobility were evident. The radiographic assessment indicated that the tooth had a nearly completed root length development with immature wide-open apex (Cvek Stage 4) [11] with a periapical radiolucency (Fig. 2b). For endodontic evaluation, the cold test was done for tooth #11, which yielded a negative response, resulting in a definitive diagnosis of pulp necrosis with symptomatic apical periodontitis.

Intraoperative records (a) Rubber dam isolation (b) Working length determination (c) Resorbable collagen placed 1–2 mm short of apex followed by MTA (4 mm) (d) Immediate Post Operative Radiograph After Obturation with thermoplasticised gutta-percha 2 mm short of CEJ

The patient was presented with two treatment options: apexification and regenerative endodontic procedure (REP). However, given the uncertain success rate (60–100%) [12] due to the unpredictable outcome of the REP, the extended treatment time, and the possible requirement of a post/core in the future, a more definitive treatment approach was selected. This involved a single-step modified apexification procedure using MTA, followed by nonvital bleaching for tooth #11. This technique seeks to promote continued apical root development and enhance the strength of root structure, offering a viable alternative to the conventional apexification procedure [10]. The treatment plan was thoroughly explained to the patient, and written consent was acquired.

Local anesthesia was delivered using 2% lidocaine combined with 1:100,000 epinephrine. A rubber dam was secured to maintain effective isolation (Fig. 2a). A round bur (No. 8) was used to create the access opening and later perfected using an Endo-Z bur (DENSTPLY Maillefer, United States). The appropriate working length was measured and established with #60 K-file in conjunction with an apex locator (CanalPro™ Apex Locator; COLTENE, Switzerland) and later verified by an intra-oral periapical radiograph (IOPA) (Fig. 2b). The root canal was chemomechanically debrided using minimal instrumentation with an #80 K-file to disrupt the biofilm without aggressively removing dentin. After instrumentation, irrigation was done with 3% sodium hypochlorite (Hyposol; Prevest DenPro, India), followed by saline, and thereafter 17% EDTA (NeoEDTA; Orikam, India) was used as a final irrigant for smear layer removal. The canal was then dried using sterile paper points (Coltene, Switzerland). A non-setting CH dressing (RCCal; Prime Dental, India) was used as an intracanal medicament for 14 days. A sterile cotton pellet followed by a temporary restorative material was used to seal off the access cavity.

At the next scheduled appointment, the patient exhibited no symptoms. After the rubber dam application, CH intracanal dressing was removed with thorough irrigation using saline and 3% NaOCl irrigation. The canal was then dried thoroughly using sterile paper points. At the same visit, the modified apexification was carried out on tooth #11. A resorbable collagen of 3 mm (Colo Plug; Cologenesis, India) was placed 1–2 mm short of the apex and carefully compacted using a suitable hand plugger to avoid MTA extrusion into the periapical area. According to the manufacturer’s guidelines, MTA (Angelus; USA) was mixed and then delivered into the canal using a straight MTA carrier. Hand and finger pluggers (Mani Inc.; Japan) were utilized to compact to it. A 4 mm MTA apical plug was established and confirmed through radiographic examination (Fig. 2c).

After 15 min, the remaining canal was filled using the thermoplasticised gutta percha (Denjoy obturation system, Denjoy, China), along with zinc oxide eugenol root canal sealer (Tubili-Seal, Kerr, California, United States) positioned 1–2 mm below the cementoenamel junction in preparation for non-vital bleaching. A layer of Glass Ionomer Cement (GIC) (GC Fuji IX™ GP EXTRA Packable Posterior, United States) was then applied over the gutta-percha. (Fig. 2d) The patient was recalled one week later for non-vital bleaching. A 35% hydrogen peroxide gel (Florence, Prevest DenPro, India) was applied inside the pulp chamber and compressed using a damp cotton pellet before sealing the access cavity with a temporary restorative material (Neotemp; Orikam, India). Due to limited color enhancement observed following the first application, a second bleaching session was repeated after a one-week interval. The subsequent application resulted in a satisfactory improvement in tooth color. To finalize the treatment, the tooth was restored with a composite resin and bonding system (Fig. 3). Follow-up visits were scheduled at 3, 6, and 12 months post-treatment.

Immediate post operative view after bleaching and composite build up wrt #11

The tooth remained asymptomatic throughout all follow-up visits, with no clinical or radiographic signs of failure. At the 3-month evaluation, there was no radiographic evidence of root development; however, progressive root end closure was observed at 6 and 12 months (Fig. 4a&b). By the 12-month follow-up, diffuse intracanal mineralization was clearly visible in the newly formed apical portion of the root canal, suggestive of ongoing root maturation or the formation of a calcific barrier (Fig. 4c ). Clinical evaluation at the 12-month follow-up demonstrated satisfactory clinical and functional outcomes (Fig. 5).

Discussion

Depending on the case, some immature young permanent teeth with necrotic pulps may be ideally suitable for REP, while others can be treated using REP or apexification. “Based on Cvek’s classification of root development, it is recommended that immature permanent teeth with necrotic pulp at stage 1 (less than 1/2 of root formation with open apex), stage 2 (1/2 root formation with open apex) and stage 3 (2/3 of root development with open apex) are suitable for REP” [7]. The rationale for this recommendation lies in the characteristics of thin canal walls, a wide open apex, and short roots, since apexification does not facilitate root maturation, such as thickening of canal walls or further root end development. On the other hand, immature permanent teeth at stage 4, which is defined by an almost fully developed root with an open apex, can be treated using either REP or Apexification, as their canal walls offer sufficient thickness and strength [8]. However, when a post is necessary for coronal restoration, REP is not a viable option, and treatment with an apical MTA plug and root canal filling is more appropriate [7, 9].

Several materials, including CH, Biodentine, and MTA, are effective choices for apexification procedures [13]. However, CH presents several limitations, including, the necessity for multiple visits, unpredictable timeframe for an apical barrier formation (5 to 20 months), greater potential for reinfection, and associated increased risk of root fracture [14]. According to a study by Shin et al. 2011, a notable reduction in the fracture strength of immature permanent teeth treated with CH was seen after just one year of treatment [15]. This increased fragility is particularly concerning in young patients whose teeth are still in the developmental stage. Evidence from systematic reviews has consistently shown that CH is surpassed by MTA in apexification procedures, demonstrating higher success rates and better radiographic outcomes outcomes [16]. Another critical limitation of CH is its tendency for resorption and weak adhesion to dentin, which can lead to complications such as reinfection of the root canal [17]. This issue is particularly problematic in cases of necrotic pulp, where maintaining a well-sealed environment is essential for successful treatment. Furthermore, prolonged usage of CH as a root-canal medicament has been found to alter the pH of dentin, potentially affecting its acidic organic components [18]. These changes can weaken dentin structure, thereby making the tooth more fragile and prone to fractures.

Given these drawbacks, calcium silicate-based apexification has gained widespread acceptance, leading to favorable healing results [19, 20]. As a bioactive material, it facilitates apex closure by stimulating the development of new mineralized tissues [21] while also promoting reparative dentin formation and cementogenesis in addition to preserving pulp integrity. It is biocompatible with periapical and periradicular tissues, further enhancing its therapeutic benefits [22]. Additionally, it expands during the setting process, especially in moist environments, ensuring a tight and durable seal [23]. Due to its exceptional sealing properties as well as tissue compatibility, MTA has gained preference as the material of choice in apexification procedures. The results of this case demonstrated signs of root elongation, corroborating MTA’s proven role as a bioactive agent in apexification procedures.

For root maturation, preservation of Hertwig’s epithelial root sheath (HERS) cells is paramount, since they have a key function in root development [24]. They stimulate the differentiation of apical papilla stem cells into odontoblasts, facilitating root dentin formation. Through epithelial-mesenchymal interaction, dental follicle stem cells are signaled to become cementoblasts leading to cementum production [25]. Therefore, HERS cells contribute to the root elongation, root canal wall thickening, and root end closure. However, damage to HERS cells caused by chronic apical periodontitis or any physical trauma during procedures like induction of periapical bleeding for revascularization can disrupt and halt further root development [10]. This further justifies the preference for apexification procedures over revascularization. The conventional apexification using MTA, for open apex management, often tends to hinder the ongoing root development. This may be due to the elevated pH level (12.5) of the material, which has the potential to adversely affect HERS and the cells within the apical papilla. This hypothesis warrants additional investigation, as in-vitro analyses have indicated that CH (pH:12.5) supports the viability and proliferation of apical papilla stem cells across various concentrations in culture environments and human dentin discs [26, 27]. Conversely, in vivo histological examinations have demonstrated that vital pulp tissues can become necrotic when directly exposed to CH, likely due to the material’s high pH, as observed in applications such as pulp capping [28].

The present case was managed using a modified apexification technique in which MTA was strategically positioned 4–5 mm coronally from the root apex, with a resorbable collagen matrix serving as a barrier to separate the material from the periapical tissues. This innovative approach appears to facilitate the deposition of hard tissue along the apical root canal walls, suggesting that the HERS cells and potentially viable apical papilla cells are surviving and functioning effectively [10]. At 12 months follow up, radiographic evidence of root end development from baseline further supported the hypothesis that these cells remained active in regenerative processes despite the elevated pH typical of MTA. This outcome may be attributed to the collagen matrix placed as a barrier, which prevented the direct contact of MTA with the periapical cells, thereby preserving their viability. This aligns with studies that emphasize the importance of maintaining a conducive environment for cellular activity during apexification procedure [29]. The technique offers a promising alternative for managing teeth with open apices, promoting continued development of the root structure and improved treatment outcomes for apexification.

In a case report by Songtrakul et al. (2019), 10 cases of immature permanent teeth with necrotic pulp, treated with modified apexification demonstrated favorable outcomes, with a follow-up of 2–4 years. 8 out of 10 cases showed root development, increased apical wall thickness, and apical closure. Two cases lacked continued root development, likely due to HERS cell damage [10]. A similar technique of modified apexification employing Biodentine demonstrated continued root development after 18 and 30 months of follow-up as reported by Thakur (2024) [30].

In the present case series, the diffuse mineralization in the apical canal space suggests that mineralized tissue-forming cells have indeed migrated and contributed to hard tissue formation. Studies have shown that the quality of the hard tissue barrier can significantly influence the long-term success of the treatment, with well-formed barriers providing better resistance to reinfection and structural integrity [31]. The current case report indicates that the modified apexification technique may facilitate the formation of a hard tissue barrier, but further histological analysis is necessary to elucidate the specific characteristics of the tissue formed to confirm the presence and quality of mineralized tissue in the apical canal space, as well as to understand the underlying biological mechanisms that contribute to successful apexification outcomes.

Pulp necrosis often causes tooth discolouration due to intra-pulpal haemorrhage and protein degradation. Moreover, haemoglobin breakdown of the pulp remnants by hydrogen sulfide-producing bacteria can form black ferric sulfide, further darkening the tooth [32]. Following apexification, non-vital intracoronal bleaching was employed to address the patient’s concern for tooth discoloration from necrotic pulp tissue. Prioritizing noninvasive techniques like non-vital bleaching minimizes the risk of additional trauma, making it an optimal approach for addressing esthetic concerns in developing dentition.

Conclusion

The successful management of the present case using the modified apexification technique for promoting root end development and apical closure while also addressing aesthetic concerns of the patient via non-vital bleaching offers a viable option for treating discoloration in immature permanent necrotic teeth. Further long-term studies could expand upon the long-term effectiveness, clinical predictability, and patient satisfaction of the modified apexification technique as a reliable treatment for necrotic young permanent teeth.
