Cultural understandings of inclusion in Southeast Asia
Authors: Deborah Rhodes, Ernest Antoine, Adi Abidin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis conceptual position article explores cultural values’ influence on disability inclusion in Southeast Asia. This is relevant to progress on the United Nations Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities and Sustainable Development Goal 10 on reduced inequalities and SDG 17 on partnerships. Progress and pathways towards increased disability inclusion and ‘leaving no-one behind’ reflect factors related to attitudes, accessibility, participation, and service delivery, as well as power, politics, and economics, all of which are influenced by cultural values. Values influence how disability and inclusion are understood in society, in policies and services. Values affect motivation and ‘how change happens’ in different contexts. This article applies a framework for understanding cultural value differences to disability and inclusion, and prospects for changes in policy in Southeast Asia. A case study about Indonesia illustrates the application of the framework on understanding of disability, inclusion and change in one country. The article encourages more nuanced, strengths-based, and culturally informed approaches, to enable change-makers to navigate complexities associated with achieving disability inclusive societies.Keywords: Disability inclusion, cultural values, Southeast Asia, Indonesia, disability rights, SDG 10: Reduced inequalities, SDG 17: Partnerships for the goals

Introduction

To achieve disability inclusive societies in Southeast Asia, changes are required in national and local policies, institutions, attitudes, approaches, practices, buildings and public spaces, and other aspects of life and society. All these elements are influenced by deeply held cultural and religious values, alongside political and economic factors. This conceptual position article argues that understanding about how cultural values manifest across three elements—disability, inclusion, and how change happens—will support people and organizations involved in achieving disability inclusion in Southeast Asia.

A strengths-based approach to supporting change towards inclusion is useful (Winterford, Rhodes, and Dureau 2023), consistent with a theme in the disability movement ‘focus on abilities, not disabilities,’ (Handicap International 2018), and reflecting lessons on ‘how change happens’ (Green 2016). In this case, identifying strengths found in Southeast Asian cultures and organizations, is a useful basis for respectfully working towards disability inclusive societies. This contrasts with a problem-based approach which would analyse gaps or problems for which solutions or ‘fixes’ are offered. A human rights-based model of disability policy and a strengths-based approach are linked (Winterford, Rhodes, and Dureau 2023: 92). Just as a focus on a person’s abilities contributes to a sense of feeling valued, included and holders of potential, a focus on strengths in cultures, organizations of persons with disabilities (OPDs), and government agencies, can contribute towards positive change (compared with a focus on deficits). One disability sector leader noted: ‘A strengths-based approach provides a positive approach to getting into the right frame of mind to be able to make progress on any issue. A strengths-based approach is critical for advocating the change, for inclusion and for equality in contexts where the playing field is not level: …that means in every context. A strengths-based approach …. reminds us to focus on our competence and contribution as part of the overall effort to bring about change (Setareki Macanawai quoted in Winterford, Rhodes, and Dureau 2023: 188).

The Southeast Asian region comprises 11 countries: Indonesia, Thailand, Myanmar, Philippines, Singapore, Cambodia, Laos, Vietnam, Malaysia, Timor-Leste, and Brunei. Governments of all these countries have signed the Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities (CRPD). This article recognizes differences between cultures, and the influences of these differences on the nature of disability inclusion in each place. While global and regional agendas express shared principles and agendas, variations in implementation between countries are inevitable because of these differences.

Global agendas on disability inclusion comprise: UN 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development, which includes Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) and the CRPD. In Southeast Asia, agendas are defined in: The Incheon Strategy for Persons with Disabilities in Asia and the Pacific, 2013-2022; and the Association of Southeast Asian Nations (ASEAN) Enabling Masterplan 2025: Mainstreaming the Rights of Persons with Disabilities.

Pathways to change in disability inclusion to date and potential scope for change are influenced by cultural values which shape understanding about disability, inclusion and how change happens in organizations and societies. Bringing about more inclusive societies involves engaging with and potentially changing deeply held existing values. The nature and extent of change in each place depends on the starting point, levels of demand for change, motivation and energy for change and leadership, among other factors. Understanding about cultural values and how they vary in different contexts, matters.

This article first identifies links between cultural values and disability then summarizes selected elements of a framework for understanding cultural value differences (Hofstede 2011). This framework is then applied to disability and inclusion concepts and experiences in Southeast Asia. Themes were identified by applying each of the value dimensions to the authors’ understanding and experience of disability rights in the region. One author has lived experience in Indonesia, one has expertise in cultural values in Southeast Asia and the third has supported disability rights in the Asia Pacific region as a consultant and NGO founder. The Indonesia case study, selected because of one author’s experience, illustrates nuances associated with application of a cultural lens. A brief discussion about cultural values in global and regional documents and progress towards implementation follows. Finally, brief suggestions are offered to support disability policy change.

Culture and disability

Developmental disability (including intellectual, sensory-related, communication and language and physical disabilities) is integral to the human experience. WHO and World Bank note that everyone is likely to experience disability, either permanently or temporarily, at some point in their life (WHO and The World Bank 2011, 3): inevitably this includes people from all cultures. People may not be defined by their disability (Kathy 2015, 14): other aspects of identity, including cultural identity, may be more important. Defining disability and inclusion is complex and in part, this reflects different cultural perspectives.

Theoretical connections between culture and disability have been considered in United States and Europe (Ingstad and Whyte 1995; Groce 2005; Bickenbach 2009; Waldschmidt 2018). Bickenbach (2009) recognized cultural aspects of CRPD noting ‘realisation of human rights will always be a matter of political negotiation, and that a social commitment to equality demands that we ensure that only transparent, fully informed and fully participatory procedures, respectful of difference are employed’ (2009: 1111). Waldschmidt (2017) noted ‘disability is …. a difference constructed in and through society and culture’ (2017, 69). The social model of disability (Shakespeare and Watson 2001) considers disability stems from communities, services and spaces that are not accessible or inclusive, so that society places limits on a person, not their disability. Understanding social values, which underpin social-level responses and barriers, and how they can change, are important Waldschmidt (2018) for informing a rights-based approach to disability policy in each country.

Studies linking culture and disability in Asia are emerging. Chang (2024) found ‘disability in East Asia is a complex phenomenon shaped by colonial historical legacies, socio-economic transformations, state interventions, familial welfare systems, civil society engagements, cultural perceptions, and contested human rights discourse’ (2024: 8). Chou et al. (2024) researched physical, attitudinal, and cultural barriers in four Asian cities, finding people with disabilities confirmed disability is constructed by cultural and social norms. They noted services are ‘often motivated not by the pursuit of social justice and respect for all citizens but by pity and the quest for merit, mediated by the judgment that the disabled have brought their misfortune upon themselves’ (2024: 8) and called for ‘cultural and geopolitical sensitivity in the implementation of the CRPD in Asian contexts’ (2024: 10).

Power and leaders bring about changes in disability inclusion policies. Cultural values influence people’s and countries’ understanding about power, why and how it is shared, and who should be included and valued within societies (Leftwich 2009, Green 2016). Policy developers and disability leaders who navigate between global agreements and national or local realities, and partners working cross-culturally, will benefit from understanding connections between power and leadership, cultural values, and inclusion.

A rights-based approach to disability policy focuses on rights of individuals as rights holders. CRPD and SDGs require leaders, government officials, communities, and family members (duty bearers) to change attitudes, behaviour, policies, approaches, and practices. Rights holders and duty bearers are citizens in countries with widely varied cultures: achieving disability inclusion therefore requires engagement with context-specific realities.

Cultural and religious values run deep in Southeast Asia, affecting all aspects of life. When seeking any change, values cannot be ignored, circumvented or over-ruled, despite shared aspirations or best intentions. Dismissing culture as a ‘blockage’ or only as a source of negative effects, risks irrelevance, unsustainability, and backlash. Values can be both the source of conflict and discrimination, and foundations for unity and supportive, inclusive communities. Similarly, religions include both elements of inclusion or charity, and exclusion and separation. Leaders are largely responsible for determining positive or negative manifestations of values, and for achieving change.

Understanding political and leadership factors in each context is necessary for the selection of strategies to achieve disability inclusion. Support for OPDs (UN PRPD 2024) and disability movement leadership are well-recognised as crucial. If leaders generate sufficient demand for change, and have authority and ability to make change happen in their context, then change is possible. Partnerships with external organisations which are culturally informed are more likely to make successful contributions.

Cultural value differences

Cultural values are beliefs and ideas that shape lives and institutions in societies differently across the world. In addition to influencing food, dress, arts, ceremonies, and behaviour (objective aspects of culture), they shape how people see their roles and others’ roles in society, how decisions are made, how power and resources are distributed, the ‘rules of engagement’ and degrees to which change is accepted, among other things (subjective aspects). Distinctions between cultural values and cultural practices are important, as they are often conflated. This article focuses on cultural values, not objective aspects of culture, recognising they are connected. Values are neither good/bad nor right/wrong, but ‘different.’ Individuals may or may not be aware of them, but values influence perceptions, feelings, and behaviours, including in government and institutions. They influence the nature of policies and prospects for change. Changing behaviour when not aligned with values, is possible, but generally not sustainable.

Historically, cultural values develop to serve a purpose, such as keeping societies together or protecting groups from external threats. However, values result in exclusion or reduced opportunities for some people, so seeking greater inclusion is the equivalent of bringing about change for those not served by prevailing values. Choices about whether values are sources of negative consequences or benefits are often made by leaders and dominant institutions. Strengthening and expanding positive aspects of values can help negate and crowd-out harmful or exclusionary aspects.

Much has been written about culture in different disciplines (e.g. Trompenaars and Hampden-Turner 1997; House et al. 2004; Lewis 2005). A well-known categorization of value differences by Hofstede (1980, 2011) and Hofstede and Hofstede (2005) forms a theoretical framework for understanding differences between cultures. Hofstede defines culture as ‘the collective programming of the mind that distinguishes the members of one group or category of people from others’ (2005: 4) and argues that cultural values influence the behaviour of those within different groups. Variations in behaviour between groups reflect variations in the values that underpin each culture (Hofstede and Hofstede 2005). Cultural values reinforce members’ sense of identity, inclusion, and purpose, and group members generally reward behaviour which is consistent with those values and reject those that are not. Cross-cultural collaboration, especially on change agendas, requires understanding of values that underpin behaviour (Rhodes 2022).

Hofstede initially identified four distinct cultural value dimensions to compare groups. Subsequent research has increased the number of dimensions and added to the data set, confirming the framework’s validity (Culture Factor 2025). A brief description of selected values dimensions is provided below (Hofstede 2011).

Power distance

Power distance refers to the extent to which less powerful members of society expect and accept that power is distributed unevenly. Members of a community that is high on this index (more hierarchical cultures) will generally accept authority without much question. In cultures low on the index (more egalitarian cultures), people prefer greater equality of power distribution and are more critical of authority. Southeast Asian countries share values that are relatively high on power distance.

Individualism and collectivism

This dimension describes the degree of interdependence of members in a community. Members of a more individualist culture emphasise individual achievements, priorities, rights (including immediate family), and decision-making. In these cultures, individuals’ self-image reflects their own achievements and life events. Collectivist cultures prioritise a group’s shared goals and interpersonal relationships, however defined (e.g. extended family, clan, village, language/religious group, or ethnic group). Members take care of each other in exchange for loyalty, and systems exist for group decision-making. Individual identity is primarily drawn from group membership. All Southeast Asian cultures are more collectivist than individualist, with some variations.

Motivation towards achievement and success

This dimension refers to relative emphasis between ‘getting things done’ or maintaining harmonious relationships. In task-oriented cultures, people are driven by competition and attainment. In contrast, cultures which prioritise relationships place more effort on caring for others and quality of life, and primary motivation for effort is seeking group consensus before acting. Southeast Asian cultures score moderately on this dimension, suggesting a mix of task and relationship-oriented values prevail (Hofstede 2011).

Uncertainty avoidance

This dimension refers to ways communities deal with uncertainties: some are more comfortable with and embrace uncertainty, risks and opportunities, than others. Communities with low scores tend to value experimentation, adaptation, problem-solving and risk-taking, i.e. they are comfortable with change and not knowing the future. Communities with high scores prioritise stability and certainty, refraining from taking risks associated with unpredictability. Typically, they apply strict beliefs and have strong institutions to maintain status quo. Most Southeast Asian countries are moderate to high on this dimension: Singapore scores low (Hofstede 2011).

As with all frameworks, limitations apply. A cultural lens is just one lens for understanding, alongside other disciplinary lenses. Using these dimensions is not intended to ‘box’ a particular culture or person, but to stimulate discussions about comparative values and consequences. Categories do not imply everyone in a culture shares similar values: there is much diversity. Categorisations are also dynamic and flexible over time: people’s values may shift during their life and communities may shift values following disasters or other events. Cultural values can and do change, though generally not fast, nor in predictable or linear ways: leadership is often key. Citing this framework is intended to confirm that values exist and they matter when countries and people are involved in change.

Implications of cultural values for disability inclusion in Southeast Asia

Cultural values and disability

Cultural values influence understanding of disability in many ways. Applying a cultural values lens to disability in Southeast Asia reveals five inter-related topics: language; disclosure; status and sense of belonging; expected behaviour and independence; and face-saving.

Language about disability is critically important globally for people with disabilities (Rahman 2019), and cultural values have a strong influence on language. Language about disability commonly reflects how society attaches labels to define people with disabilities. Language can both exclude and stigmatize people who are seen as different, and include and support people within groups. Takamine (2004) noted the significance of stigmatising language as part of the suite of conditions creating barriers for people with disabilities in Asia. In collectivist cultures, disability is often portrayed as ‘deviance’ from what is understood as the community ‘norm.’ When conformity to a group’s norms is prioritised, ‘othering’ language and attitudes emerge. Efforts have been made to encourage language changes since the first Asian and Pacific Decade of Persons with Disabilities, and continue in the current third decade (UN ESCAP 2023).

Quantifying the prevalence of disability is a complex and contested process (WHO and The World Bank 2011) involving many cultural issues, including perspectives of disability, and authorities’ use of data. Disclosure by people with disabilities and their families in surveys can be influenced by perspectives about who asks questions and for what purpose, and consequences of answering one way or another. Shame, fear, risks of losing control over one’s life or family members, can influence the extent to which people reveal or hide disabilities, alongside different understandings about what constitutes disability.

People with disabilities in Southeast Asia are perceived generally to have relatively low status vis-à-vis people without disabilities: a cause of disadvantage and result of hierarchical values. Where there is competition for limited access and services, consequences are more significant than in higher-resource contexts. Some religions portray a person’s disability as the result of a previous generation’s sin, illustrating how a person with disability has little power over their own life. Stigma and discriminatory attitudes are experienced because of the way values are manifested and applied. In high power distance contexts, leaders with disabilities can hold power, though exclusion and inaccessibility reduce access to positions of authority.

Collectivism in Southeast Asian countries influences how people with disabilities are expected to behave, and how people with disabilities see themselves, including expectations of independence. Even among educated and urban communities, collectivism maintains mutual obligations between group members. Being able to depend on group members is a positive aspect of collectivist cultures in low-resource settings. Ideas about individual independence may clash with collectivist expectations that families must provide care and support to members with disabilities. All religions in Southeast Asia—for example Buddhism, Islam, Christianity, and Hinduism—include processes for ‘taking care of’ people deemed less advantaged, particularly through poverty. This often manifests in showing pity for others, perpetuating discriminatory attitudes and language towards people with disabilities. However, where there is motivation to provide care for others, there are opportunities for discussion and shifting perspectives towards rights-based thinking.

A strength of collectivist values in Southeast Asia is that a sense of belonging contributes to positive mental health outcomes. When positive, collectivist values can contribute to emotional security and strong group identity. When people are excluded from groups, consequences are particularly harmful: community expectations and governments assume people will be supported by families, so alternatives are commonly not available.

Perceptions about roles of OPDs are influenced by cultural values. The multiplicity of OPDs in Southeast Asia reflects cultural norms about how groups and organizations operate. In a collectivist culture, one’s primary identity is commonly life-long and related to one’s extended family, ethnicity, or clan, so assumptions that collectivist behaviour applies to groups comprising people with disabilities may need to be tested. Shared experience of disability may not replace other forms of identity.

‘Saving face’ (not ‘losing face’) is a manifestation of collectivist values in Southeast Asia relevant to disability inclusion. It is defined as avoidance of direct communications which may cause perceived offence or reduce another’s reputation or honour. It influences self-perceptions and others’ perceptions of people with disability, plus interactions within groups, and even national self-image. In practice, it can result in signs of difference from the wider group being hidden to avoid shame or minimise ill-treatment, meaning families may keep members with disabilities hidden, reducing access to information, education, and employment. To maintain essential family support, people with disabilities may find it difficult to challenge this experience. The phenomenon may also contribute to OPDs minimising critique of leaders and to Asian Governments changing laws to comply with CRPD (to ‘save face’ globally).

In summary, while cultures vary across Southeast Asia and are changing, millions of people hold mindsets about the importance of including and caring for people with disabilities and/or believe people with disabilities need to be segregated, pitied, or kept hidden. Cultural values directly affect prospects for inclusive societies. Leaders and policy-makers, including people with disabilities, are raised within cultural contexts and while myriad opportunities exist to engage in change processes, expectations that societies can be transformed using a rights-based approach need to be realistic.

Cultural values and inclusion

‘Inclusion’ focuses on societies’ approaches to valuing their members equally, regardless of individual characteristics. Dominant values in Southeast Asia generally contradict the idea of equal inclusion of citizens. In high power cultures, people know their place in the hierarchy and acknowledge reciprocal obligations between those with and without power: inequality is the basis of social order. This section identifies three themes from applying a cultural values lens to inclusion: equality; public good; and education.

A significant cultural shift is required in Southeast Asia for people who have largely been seen as low in status, to be included as equal members of society. Why would one group, distinguished by one type of characteristics, be seen as equal and included, when all societies are divided and people are excluded by myriad other factors—e.g. geography, class, poverty, ethnicity, gender, religious or political affiliation? Through one lens, since identity may be more related to one’s membership of a group or perceived status, than one’s disability, inclusion may be less elusive than expected. Jackson et al. (2023) described leaders with disabilities being included in local government consultations as representatives of communities which led to a sense of belonging and being valued, even if other changes did not occur.

Collectivist cultures tend to have relatively lower interest in the ‘public good’ or national agendas, compared with individualist cultures. A great deal of energy is required to maintain group membership, so little energy remains for engagement at other levels, for most people.

Education is key in achieving disability inclusion, through educating communities about disability and including people with disabilities in education. Education systems generally reflect prevailing cultural values and changing sources of power. A report on inclusive education in ASEAN (Singh 2022) noted ‘culture impacts learning’ and ‘inclusive education requires transformation of not just policy but also culture’ (2022: 39).

Cultural values and how change happens

Change happens differently in different settings and cultural values have a major influence on the nature and scope of change (Hofstede and Hofstede 2005). Cultural values affect how power is wielded and yielded, how priorities are set, and levels of ambition in organizations and societies. In low power distance cultures, advocacy and democratic processes influence leaders. In high power distance cultures, people expect leaders to make decisions based on their authority and status, under social contracts: change occurs when leaders decide they are necessary. Power distance is relatively high in Southeast Asian countries, so citizens are more likely to emphasize respect for leaders, than efforts to influence them. For people with disabilities to make decisions about individual interests and priorities, a significant change from existing values is necessary. This is exemplified in Indonesia, where top-down changes have occurred (see “Focus on Indonesia” section).

Cultural dimensions provide a platform for considering change, including change-focused partnerships (Rhodes 2022): understanding how different cultures engage with change, enables people from different cultures to interact respectfully and jointly identify change processes for each setting. Many approaches can be used to lead and support sustainable change. Approaches which engage people who embody existing values in discussions about how values serve different groups unevenly, and how they can be changed to benefit everyone, are potentially useful. Imposing values on another’s culture is unacceptable, but working with and adjusting existing cultural values, when there are shared change agendas, is appropriate (Green 2016).

Focus on Indonesia

Applying a cultural lens to the recent history and implementation of Indonesian disability law is beneficial for illustrating its value in understanding change processes.

In 2011, the Government of Indonesia ratified CRPD through Law 19/2011. This represented a significant shift from previous laws (Priebe and Howell 2014), increased public discourse on disability rights and raised expectations of a shift to a less paternalistic approach (Wardana and Dewi 2017). In 2016, a major new law on people with disabilities known as Penyandang Disabilitas 8/2016 was passed (Government of Indonesia 2016), describing the Government’s obligations to remove discriminatory policies and protect rights. However, implementation of the law has been slow and encountered many complexities, including at provincial levels (Irwanto and Thohori 2017; Wardana and Dewi 2017; Dibley 2019; Dibley and Tsaputra 2019; Afrianty 2020). The contest between the prevailing charity model, deeply embedded in Indonesia’s culture (Afrianty 2020) and religious beliefs (Afrianty and Fenwick 2024), and the rights-based approach is the key challenge: charity is part of Indonesia’s collective consciousness.

The Government of Indonesia submitted its first progress report on CRPD in 2017, noting: ‘the government has taken fundamental steps to strengthen its legal and institutional frameworks to promote and protect human rights for all. In this context, the breakthrough in the effort to promote and protect the rights of persons with disabilities is by changing the mindset of policymakers and the community as a whole’ (Committee on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities 2017: 2) (italics added). The importance of ‘mindset’ or values is clear.

The role of cultural values is well understood by leaders and others with disabilities in Indonesia. Prior to CRPD, Komardjaja (2001) examined ‘whether ‘independence’—a cultural ideal at the heart of the Western barrier-free design notion—is a quality worth pursuing in the Asian context’ (2001: 78). She found ‘certainly, Western attitudes towards disabled people and advanced knowledge of disability cannot be directly translated to Indonesia’ (2001, 79). Describing barriers in Bandung, she noted: ‘Social barriers … are embedded in culture and affect people’s behaviour for long periods of time’ (2001: 79). Riany, Cuskelly, and Meredith (2016) noted ‘cultural beliefs about parenting have an important influence on parenting behaviours, including …. ways to parent children with autism’ (2016: 623) and ‘revealed five related themes about autism, including traditional cultural beliefs about appropriate behaviour during pregnancy, karma, and God’s plan, that are not usually reported in the literature from western countries’ (2016: 623).

Culture and religion are key to understanding disability in Indonesia (Afrianty and Fenwick 2024), confirmed by Chrysant Lily Kusumowardoyo, Director, ASB Regional Office, South and Southeast Asia. She said: ‘In Indonesia, everyone must have and practice a religion. Collectivist culture means neighbours and communities have expectations about others’ religious practice. Religious leaders commonly teach that disability is something to be fixed and a person with a disability is cursed for some reason. In Christianity, many people literally interpret the biblical story about lepers being healed and the blind seeing if they believe and follow the rules. When our family member developed a hearing impairment, neighbours and fellow church-goers insisted they attend a preacher’s rally where miracles are performed and cures provided. Neighbours concluded our family member’s absence from church (the sermon could not be heard) would result in them not ‘hearing faith’ and not going to heaven.’ [interview, November 2024].

High power distance values in Indonesia have underpinned the traditional use of a charity model, i.e. leaders have had responsibility for those who they lead, including people with disabilities. The charity model gave leaders a structure for providing services. In Indonesia, issues related to people with disabilities were previously the responsibility of provincial Social Affairs Offices and the national Ministry of Social Affairs, consistent with the charity model.

High power distance values have also supported implementation of CRPD and Law No 8/2016. Leaders in the disability movement have collaborated with political and public sector leaders to achieve progress on inclusion and rights (Jackson et al. 2023). For example, Dibley (2019) described the work of organisations in Jogjakarta, known as the epicentre of the disability rights movement. She highlighted Sentra Advokasi Perempuan Difabel dan Anak (SAPDA) (Advocacy Centre for Women and Children with Disabilities) and Sana Integrasi dan Advokasi Difable (SIGAB) (Place for Integration and Advocacy for People with Disabilities), which led changes. These organisations and other OPDs were given significantly greater impetus and status because of the new law. In Jogjakarta, activists established Komnas Difabel (National Disability Commission) which, supported by academics and officials, successfully campaigned for changed laws to recognise rights of people with disabilities. SIGAB conducted training for government officials in Jogjakarta, contributing to new provincial regulations (Dibley 2019). SAPDA, SIGAB and Komnas’ experiences illustrate that in a hierarchical culture, change is more likely if leaders can be persuaded to be part of the change. When leaders support people with disabilities, this legitimises challenges to prevailing values, enabling others to follow. Leaders’ actions are necessary to bring about systemic change, consistent with high power distance cultural values.

The rights-based approach implies changes in all aspects of power and government, inevitably encountering resistance from some leaders. Disability advocates considered CRPD and legal implementation to be cross-sectoral, requiring changes in all government departments, broadening responsibilities beyond social affairs offices. They also recognised that a rights- based approach to disability policy clashes with a charity model, so required leaders to make a fundamental change in beliefs to recognize all human beings are equal (Dibley 2019).

The disability movement, while focused on individual rights, is underpinned by collectivist values and has harnessed these values in Indonesian society to achieve momentum and progress. The movement has mobilized support from others, including other NGOs and senior officials. Collectivist values (which prioritize group harmony and shared goals) are strongest in small groups such as extended families, villages, and neighbourhoods, but are also strong in major population centres, where the movement collaborated with and was supported by others. Ongoing implementation of CRPD and the new law in Indonesia is not possible without collectivist action.

Indonesia’s score for motivation towards achievement and success is mid-range (Hofstede 2011), suggesting Indonesians are moderately disposed to change, and more likely to be measured in efforts to bring about change. This value has influenced actions taken by the disability movement and the extent of advocacy. The movement’s use of discussions, forums, conferences, and education to advance their cause has been moderate and measured, seeking not to cause embarrassment and loss of face to leaders, which could easily derail efforts.

Indonesia has a mid-range score for uncertainty avoidance (Hofstede 2011), suggesting comfort with taking moderate risks: this appears to be the case in the history of the disability movement. Following the end of the Suharto regime in the late 1990s, people with disabilities recognised opportunities for greater freedom and began to form groups, providing safety to advocate for change (Wardana and Dewi 2017; Afrianty 2020).

In summary, Indonesia’s deeply held cultural values shape perceptions and experiences of disability, as well as processes and prospects for change towards disability inclusion.

Cultural values in global and regional agreements and agendas

Limited references to ‘culture’ are found in global and regional disability rights agendas, suggesting absence of formal acknowledgement that cultural values require consideration or change. The few mentions of culture in these documents relate to objective aspects of culture (e.g. art, traditions) rather than cultural values. Article 30 of CRPD refers to accessibility of artistic and creative aspects of life. SDGs mention culture as a source of products for sustainable tourism (UN General Assembly 2015: 20). Neither the Policy Guidelines for Inclusive Sustainable Development Goals (UN OHCHR 2024), nor other supporting documents mention culture or cultural values.

At the regional level, the ‘Incheon Strategy’ (UN ESCAP 2012) makes one mention of ‘cultural,’ referring to ‘cultural diversity’ in a policy directions section (UN ESCAP 2012: 16). As the Strategy was developed after consultations with regional governments and civil society stakeholders, it is assumed Asian values informed the selection of ten agreed goals (i.e. not all CRPD articles). The ‘ASEAN Enabling Masterplan 2025’ mentions culture in relation to ‘socio-cultural and religious activities, sports and leisure’ (ASEAN 2018: 19). It refers to ASEAN Socio-cultural Community Blueprint 2025 (ASEAN 2016), which describes aspirations for the region’s people and institutions (2016: 19–20) and mentions ‘traditional cultural assets’ (2016: 20). References to values such collectivism and power distance are not obvious.

Progress on CRPD and disability inclusion in Southeast Asia

Reporting on CRPD and SDGs progress has been underway for nearly two decades and one decade respectively, but commentators report overall lack of progress globally (Modeer and Viera 2023). By early 2025, six of eleven Southeast Asian countries had submitted only one progress report since ratifying CRPD (Thailand, Indonesia, Laos, Myanmar, Singapore, and Vietnam) and Philippines had submitted two progress reports, leaving four without reports (Malaysia, Cambodia, Brunei and Timor-Leste) (UN OHCHR 2019). Reports describe national reviews of policies and some new legislation, but little substantive change. Explanations for low levels of reporting and progress may include cultural factors mentioned above.

Limited progress in Southeast Asia is reported in multiple sources. Crosta and Sanders (2021) found ‘while many regulatory frameworks have emerged …, in many cases recent legislation has yet to be implemented and enforced in full’ (2021: 1–2). They also found ‘although all 10 of the ASEAN Member States have ratified CRPD, certain Member States have adopted more comprehensive national legislation than others’ (2021: 7). A UN ESCAP regional progress report mentions changes to disability allowance in Philippines (UN ESCAP 2024: 7) but little other evidence of other change. UN DESA’s 2024 report on global progress on disability in realization of SDGs, makes no mention of cultural value differences that influence prospects for change (UN DESA 2024). UN OHCHR Guidelines and training materials for Inclusive Sustainable Development Goals (UN OHCHR 2024) do not acknowledge the role of cultural values in disability inclusion. The Asian Development Bank’s roadmap on disability inclusion refers to changing internal organizational culture, but not countries’ cultures (ADB 2022).

Partnerships and international cooperation between governments, OPDs and development organisations, feature in Southeast Asian efforts towards disability inclusion (Crosta and Sanders 2021), consistent with Article 32 in CRPD and SDG 17. Donors which provide funds for OPDs and disability inclusion include Governments of Australia and Norway, notably countries with relatively egalitarian and individualist values. International NGOs and UN agencies are increasingly involved in supporting disability inclusion: cross-cultural understanding is critical for partnerships’ success.

Bringing about policy change towards inclusion

Moves towards more disability-inclusive societies involve changing values which underpin policies, resource allocations, decision-making, motivation, and other behaviour. There are benefits in applying understanding of values in contexts where change is expected to occur, and engaging with positive aspects of prevailing cultural values, including motivation. Increasing motivation towards change can include engaging communities in conversations about how prevailing values serve groups differently, and promoting ways to ensure all society members are served better overall. Collectivist values are manifested in strong beliefs in Asian countries about ‘taking care of others’ which means many people could participate in conversations without undermining the importance of kindness. Importantly, people with disabilities with religious beliefs may experience tension if they value care provided, but are told this is deemed inappropriate through a rights-based lens. Charitable and religious organizations are a potential source of motivation towards greater inclusion, particularly if a social model of disability and/or a rights-based model of disability policy are incorporated.

Shifting a society’s cultural values is generally not a quick or linear process (Green 2016). Many factors need to line up and be in sufficient quantity to push changes ‘over the line.’ Good leadership is always necessary (Leftwich 2009), by powerful leaders in hierarchical cultures or charismatic leaders in egalitarian cultures. In an increasingly unequal and divided world, where most people live in hierarchical and collectivist cultures, considerably more leadership and work are needed than may be implied in the wording of global and regional agreements.

Useful lessons have been learned from other change processes across cultures. Participatory, context-based approaches, which recognise existing strengths and values in each setting and build on these towards agreed change, are more likely to contribute to positive change than problem-based, standardised, or centralised approaches (Green 2016; Winterford, Rhodes, and Dureau 2023).

Analysis in this article leads to three suggestions. First, change-makers in disability inclusion could increase understanding of cultural values’ role in conceptualising disability and prospects for inclusion in each place, and support others to recognise links. Facilitating respectful conversations about the impact of current values on citizens’ lives can contribute to open-ness to change. Finding opportunities to work with and expand inclusive elements of existing values has more potential than trying to overturn values. Second, valuing and increasing support for local leadership and collective efforts (i.e. OPDs) are important. This is already widely recognised, but core funding of OPDs and capacity-strengthening support remain insufficient. Third, use of a strengths-based approach and partnerships are recommended, as they have potential to expand existing Southeast Asian efforts and attributes. Revealing existing strengths, assets or highlights is a strong basis for moving towards preferred futures (Winterford, Rhodes, and Dureau 2023). Strengths-based partnerships are more likely to recognise positive aspects of existing values than those which see culture as a ‘blockage’ or source of problems.

Conclusion

CRPD is widely seen as the major catalyst in the global disability rights movement, but its implementation requires engagement with different cultural values across the world: values shape current attitudes and institutions as well as scope of change. In Southeast Asia, ensuring rights of people with disabilities are protected and no-one is left behind in development, requires understanding of existing values and ways to work in different contexts. Without this, while new policies may be developed, cultural values may prevent their implementation.

Finding ways to promote inclusion and the rights of people with disabilities within each context is crucial, illustrated in the example of Indonesia. There are ways to cultivate disability-inclusive societies, acknowledging the status quo does not currently serve people within societies evenly, and that values do not change overnight. Implementation of disability inclusive policies requires cultural values to flex much more than is implied in CRPD, SDGs and the Incheon Strategy. Principles and aspirational targets in global and regional agendas represent broad agreements, but the nature and scope of implementation inevitably varies. The mix of elements in each country interact differently: efforts require informed understanding of and engagement with values in every context.

This article confirms that cultural values influence ways people with disabilities are perceived, and perceive themselves, as well as potential for changes toward greater inclusion in Southeast Asia. When a cultural values lens is applied, the potential for change becomes highly nuanced: this understanding can contribute to further extending progress on legislative and policy change. By leveraging these insights, interventions can be better tailored to resonate with values, ensuring they are both effective and culturally appropriate.
