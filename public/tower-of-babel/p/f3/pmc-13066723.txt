Probing the Interaction Between Thioacetamide Fungicide and Bovine Serum Albumin Through Fluorescence, Electrochemical, and Docking Analyses
Authors: Zeynep Murathan, Hülya Silah, Merve Yıldırım, Ipek Kucuk, İsmail Çelik, Bengi Uslu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThioacetamide (TAA) is used in pharmaceutical and industrial applications and has also been reported as a fungicidal agent; however, it poses serious health risks due to its hepatotoxic and immunosuppressive effects. Despite this toxicological importance, the molecular interplay of TAA with serum albumins, major plasma proteins that regulate transport, distribution, and bioavailability, remains insufficiently defined. Here, we systematically examined the binding of TAA to bovine serum albumin (BSA) under physiological conditions. UV–visible absorption, steady‐state fluorescence spectroscopy (including synchronous and three‐dimensional fluorescence), dynamic light scattering (DLS), Fourier‐transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy, and differential pulse voltammetry (DPV) were integrated with molecular docking and molecular dynamics (MD) simulations. Fluorescence titration revealed concentration‐dependent quenching with temperature dependence and moderate affinity (Ka on the order of 103–104 M−1), with approximately one dominant binding site per BSA molecule. Thermodynamic analysis indicated a spontaneous process (ΔG < 0) with predominantly hydrophobic contributions. Synchronous and 3D fluorescence measurements showed pronounced microenvironmental changes around tryptophan residues, consistent with conformational rearrangements of BSA upon TAA binding. DLS further supported complex formation by showing an increase in the hydrodynamic diameter of BSA after TAA addition. DPV measurements exhibited a gradual decrease in the TAA oxidation current in the presence of BSA.

Introduction

Thioacetamide (TAA) has been employed as a fungicide, accelerator in rubber vulcanization, organic solvent, and motor oil stabilizer. Additionally, because of its pivotal pharmaceutical and physiological activities, TAA has been widely utilized in the synthesis of polymerization inhibitors, catalysts, stabilizers, photographic agents, electroplating additives, crosslinking agents, pesticides, etc. The TAA compound is extremely dangerous to health. TAA has been identified to cause immune suppression [1, 2, 3]. Furthermore, this compound has highly toxic properties, as it is rapidly metabolized by cytochrome P450 and flavin‐containing monooxygenases to reactive metabolites such as reactive oxygen species and TAA‐S‐oxide [4, 5]. Therefore, TAA can also be described as a highly vigorous hepatotoxic agent that supposes metabolic activation by multifunctional oxidases. The hepatotoxicity of TAA has been recognized since 1948 by Fitzhugh and Nelson. Following the use of TAA as a fungicide in these years and its subsequent detection in orange juice, it has since been used to model both acute and chronic liver injury, including hepatocarcinogenesis. TAA is a well‐established hepatotoxin used in rodent models to induce liver injury, including necrosis, fibrosis, and cirrhosis [4]. TAA exemplifies an important member of the thioamide compound group, described by a molecular formula of CH3CSNH2. TAA compound has a thiono‐sulfur group, and its chemical structure is shown in Figure 1.

Despite the extensive toxicological data available for TAA, information regarding its molecular‐level interactions with plasma proteins remains limited. Understanding such interactions is essential, as protein binding plays a critical role in determining the transport, distribution, bioavailability, and biological fate of toxic compounds in the bloodstream.

One of the most abundant proteins in blood is serum albumin. These proteins, such as bovine serum albumin (BSA) and human serum albumin (HSA), constitute approximately 55%–65% of total plasma proteins. Serum albumins have crucial functions, such as transporting biological substances such as drugs, hormones, and fatty acids, as well as, most importantly, maintaining plasma colloid osmotic pressure [6, 7]. BSA is composed of 583 amino acids and three homologous domains (I, II, and III) with varied structures, which are similar to HSA [8]. The structure of BSA is further stabilized by 17 disulfide bridges and represents a predominantly α‐helical conformation, making BSA an impeccable model compound for examining small‐molecule interplays such as those with drugs, pesticides, and metal ions [9]. Insight into the binding affinity of different pesticide molecules to these serum albumins is indispensable for brightening their pharmacokinetic behavior and systemic toxicity. Some pesticides have been specified to interplay predominantly with Site I (Subdomain IIA) of albumin through a combination of hydrogen bonding, hydrophobic interplays, and electrostatic forces. These interplays not only stabilize the pesticide compounds in circulation but also stimulate measurable conformational shifts in the albumin structure, including a reduction in α‐helix content and variances in surface hydrophobicity, which may impress the natural ligand transport function of albumin. Subsequently, binding toxic chemicals, such as fungicides, to plasma proteins has toxicological significance and notably influences the excretion and distribution of toxicants. The inclusions of such binding include extended plasma retention of pesticides, potential competition with therapeutic or endogenous ligands, and modulation of bioavailability, all of which are serious for toxicological risk assessments [10].

The fluorescence spectroscopy technique has been generally utilized to research the associations between small molecules and proteins [11]. This spectroscopic technique contributed to the exploration of molecule‐protein binding mechanisms in developing an understanding of the number of binding sites, binding constant, and thermodynamic variables [11, 12].

To the best of our knowledge, no previous study has systematically investigated the molecular interaction between TAA and BSA using an integrated spectroscopic, electrochemical, and computational approach. In particular, experimental evidence that correlates fluorescence‐based binding data with electrochemical behavior and molecular‐level simulations for the TAA–BSA system is still lacking. Therefore, the present study aims to provide a comprehensive investigation of the interaction between TAA and BSA under physiological conditions by combining UV–visible spectroscopy, steady‐state fluorescence spectroscopy (including synchronous and 3D fluorescence), dynamic light scattering (DLS), Fourier‐transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy, and differential pulse voltammetry (DPV) with molecular docking and molecular dynamics (MD) simulations. This multifaceted strategy enables clarification of the binding affinity, thermodynamic parameters, dominant interaction forces, binding site preference, and conformational as well as secondary structural changes in BSA induced by TAA, thereby offering molecular‐level insight into the biointeractive and toxicokinetic behavior of TAA.

The selection of TAA as the target compound in this exploration was based on a deliberate design strategy. TAA is a widely used industrial and agricultural chemical compound and has long been exploited as a reference hepatotoxic and immunosuppressive agent in experimental toxicology models [1, 2, 3, 4, 5]. Despite its prevalent application and well‐documented systemic toxicity, molecular‐level info regarding its interplay with plasma transport proteins remains limited. In addition, TAA possesses a small molecular size, a thioamide functional group, and moderate polarity, making it a convenient model compound for systematically investigating protein–ligand interplays without steric complexity [4, 5]. Therefore, TAA was intentionally selected as a single, well‐defined fungicidal toxicant to enable clear mechanistic interpretation and to establish a structure–interplay relationship with BSA.

From a practical perspective, illuminating the interplay between TAA and serum albumin is prominent because albumin binding can directly modulate the free (bioavailable) fraction of TAA in circulation, thereby influencing its distribution and persistence in biological systems. Moreover, the correlation of spectroscopic binding data with electrochemical behavior ensures an implementation‐oriented framework that supports the development of albumin‐inclusive analytical protocols and advances the interpretation of exposure and toxicokinetic data. These outcomes are particularly relevant for industrial environments in which TAA is manufactured, processed, or handled, as they contribute to safer exposure assessment and biomonitoring strategies.

Research Design and Methods

Reagents and Experimental Solutions

BSA of analytical grade—free from protease and globulin and fatty acids—warfarin (WFN), and ketoprofen (KTN) were products of Sigma Aldrich, USA, and were utilized without further purification. All chemicals employed throughout the experimental study were of analytical purity, and ultrapure water was employed in solution preparation. Experimental assays were conducted in a phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS) solution consisting of sodium phosphate, adjusted to pH 7.4, to imitate extracellular physiological conditions. The BSA stock solution was freshly prepared in PBS, and its concentration was determined spectrophotometrically using a molar extinction coefficient (ε) of 43,824 M−1·cm−1 at 280 nm. For the ligand TAA, a 1.0‐mg/mL (ppm) stock was initially prepared in ultrapure water and subsequently diluted with PBS to acquire working concentrations convenient for the experiments.

Methodology for UV–Vis Spectroscopy

UV–visible absorption spectra were obtained using a Shimadzu 1601PC double‐beam UV–vis absorption spectrophotometer equipped with a 1.0‐cm path length quartz cuvette. Spectral scans were conducted within the wavelength range of 280–500 nm. Measurements were performed for TAA solutions across concentrations ranging from 50 to 350 μM, for serum albumin BSA at concentrations of 3 μM, and for BSA–TAA complex mixtures prepared at varying molar ratios. All solutions were prepared in PBS (pH 7.4) and maintained at a constant temperature of 298 K to simulate physiological conditions during surveys.

Fluorescence Spectral Analysis

Fluorescence spectra for native BSA and the TAA–BSA complex were received making use of an Agilent Cary Eclipse spectrofluorometer equipped with a 1.0‐cm path length quartz cuvette housed in a temperature‐controlled Peltier cuvette holder connected to a circulating water bath. Fluorescence emission spectra were recorded within the range of 300–500 nm following excitation at 280 nm for BSA at a fixed concentration of 3 μM. Titration assays were carried out by incrementally adding TAA in the concentration range between 50 and 350 μM (intervals of 50 μM). Prior to each analysis, the mixed solutions (3.0‐mL total volume) were incubated at diverse temperatures (288, 298, and 308 K) for 30 min to maintain thermal equilibration.

Synchronous fluorescence investigations were also achieved to survey microenvironmental alterations in Tyr and Trp residues. Measures were conducted with a fixed BSA concentration of 3 μM and varying TAA concentrations (0–350 μM), using Δλ values of 15 and 60 nm to selectively probe Tyr and Trp residues, respectively. The corresponding emission ranges were adjusted at 250–350 nm for Tyr and 240–400 nm for Trp.

Three‐dimensional (3D) fluorescence spectra were obtained at 298 K using native BSA (3 μM) and the TAA–BSA complex at a 50:1 M ratio to elaborate and explore microenvironmental and conformational variances. Excitation wavelengths ranged from 220 to 350 nm with 5‐nm increments, whereas emission spectra were gathered from 220 to 550 nm, supplying insight into global alterations in the intrinsic fluorophores.

DLS Studies of TAA–BSA Interaction

The Zetasizer Nano ZS instrument was operated to conduct DLS measurements. A cuvette made of polystyrene was filled with 1 mL of the sample. Three samples were prepared with 3‐μM BSA for the intent of DLS measurements. There was no TAA in the first sample, but there was 20 μM in the second and 40 μM in the third. To warrant consistency, each measurement was carried out eight times [19].

Electrochemical Measurement

The voltammetric experiments were conducted using an AUTOLAB PGSTAT‐30 electrochemical workstation (Eco Chemie, Utrecht, Netherlands). The setup employed a conventional three‐electrode configuration, consisting of an Ag/AgCl reference electrode (BASi) immersed in 3 M NaCl, a glassy carbon working electrode (GCE) (3.0‐mm diameter), and a platinum wire serving as the counter electrode. Electroanalytical analyses were performed using the DPV method under the following optimized parameters: a potential window of 0.0 V to +1.60 V, a pulse amplitude of 25 mV, a step potential of 5 mV, and a scan rate of 0.0125 V/s. Before working by GCE, the electrode surface was carefully polished using an alumina slurry (0.05‐μm particle size) on a polishing cloth to ensure a smooth and clean surface. All electrochemical measurements were carried out at room temperature.

FTIR Studies

The interaction between TAA and BSA was further confirmed by FTIR spectroscopy (Perkin Elmer, Spectrum 100 model). The samples were scanned over a wavelength range of 400–4000 cm−1 with a resolution of 4 cm−1 using FTIR spectrophotometry with the ATR technique.

Competitive Drug Displacement Study

The binding region of TAA on BSA was surveyed through competitive displacement testing utilizing two site‐specific probes: WFN for Site I and KTN for Site II [20, 21]. Various concentrations of TAA (0–200 μM) were introduced into solutions containing BSA (3 μM) preincubated with 6‐μM amounts of KTN or WFN. The mixtures were allowed to equilibrate for 30 min at 298 K. The excitation wavelengths of WFN‐BSA and KTN‐BSA were 347 nm. The fluorescence quenching data were analyzed using the Stern–Volmer equation (Equation 2).

Molecular Docking Study

Two molecular docking tools, CB‐Dock2 (https://cadd.labshare.cn/cb‐dock2/) [22] and MzDOCK v.3 (https://mzlab.sjtu.edu.cn/mzdock) [23], were employed to predict the interactions between ligand and protein. CB‐Dock2 follows a blind docking approach by automatically identifying potential binding cavities on the protein surface without requiring prior knowledge of the binding site. In contrast, MzDOCK allows for structure‐guided docking by targeting known active sites, particularly those defined by co‐crystallized ligands. This complementary usage enabled both broad‐spectrum screening (via CB‐Dock2) and site‐specific validation (via MzDOCK) to be carried out in parallel.

The target protein, the crystal structure of BSA in complex with 3,5‐diiodosalicylic acid (PDB ID: 4JK4), was obtained in PDB file format from the Protein Data Bank [24]. The ligand, TAA, was downloaded from PubChem with CID: 2723949 in 3D SDF file format and converted to MOL format for further processing.

In CB‐Dock2, after uploading the protein and ligand structures, the platform identified the five most probable binding cavities on the protein surface. Docking simulations were carried out using the center coordinates of each cavity using the AutoDock Vina engine [25]. The resulting docked poses were ranked according to their binding affinities (kcal/mol), and the top‐scoring poses were retrieved for interaction analysis. Given the blind docking nature of CB‐Dock2, the predicted binding regions were interpreted in the context of the study's objectives. In particular, poses located near the drug‐binding Site 1 (DS1) of BSA were prioritized, and the best‐fitting pose within this site was selected for further evaluation. In contrast, MzDOCK was employed for targeted docking by utilizing structural information from the crystal complex. The active site defined by the co‐crystallized ligand, 3,5‐diiodosalicylic acid, was set as the binding region. The prepared TAA was directed toward this active site, and docking was conducted accordingly. Resulting poses were assessed based on their docking scores and conformational fit, allowing for structural validation of the predefined binding pocket and the ligand's binding potential. Docking results from both platforms were compared regarding binding energy, interacting amino acid residues, hydrogen bonds, and cavity locations. Top poses were analyzed collectively to infer potential binding modes. Visual representations of ligand–protein complexes were generated using PyMOL v2.4 and BIOVIA Discovery Studio Visualizer v2025.

Molecular Dynamic Simulation Study

The top‐ranking complexes obtained from CB‐Dock2 and MzDOCK were subjected to MD simulations to assess their dynamic behavior and structural stability. System setup was performed via the CHARMM‐GUI web server (http://www.charmm‐gui.org). The AMBER ff14SB force field was applied to the protein, whereas the ligand parameters were derived using the GAFF force field [26, 27]. Each complex was solvated using the TIP3P water model and neutralized with 0.15 M KCl. Following energy minimization and equilibration steps, conducted at 300 K and 1 atm for a total of 125 ps, production MD was carried out for 50 ns under the NPT ensemble with a 1‐fs time step. Postsimulation analyses were performed using the gmx script to calculate root mean square deviation (RMSD), root mean square fluctuation (RMSF), and hydrogen bond formation, providing insights into complex stability and ligand retention within the binding site. Graphical analysis was conducted using QtGrace v0.2.6, whereas 50‐ns structural snapshots were visualized via Discovery Studio Visualizer. A comparative evaluation of both complexes was made based on their dynamic trajectories.

DFT Calculations

Ground state geometric optimization calculations were conducted using density functional theory (DFT). Computational calculations and visualizations regarding the compounds were executed utilizing Gaussian 09 Rev. C.01 and GaussView 5.0.9, respectively. The hybrid B3LYP functional with a mixed basis set was utilized to optimize molecular geometries. 6‐311++G(d,p) was employed to TAA in the ground state at the gas phase.

Results and Discussion

TAA‐Induced Fluorescence Quenching of BSA and Quenching Mechanism

Fluorescence titration is an effective analytical approach for investigating the binding behavior between small molecules and proteins by tracking alterations in fluorescence emission upon incremental addition of the target molecule. This method provides qualitative insight into binding behavior and also facilitates quantitative analysis of binding affinities and the nature of the intermolecular forces involved easier [19].

When the interactions of different molecules with BSA are examined, it can be seen in general that although the intrinsic fluorescence of the BSA molecule arises from tyrosine, tryptophan, and phenylalanine groups, the main important residue contributing to quenching the steady‐state fluorescence of the BSA protein is the tryptophan group [28]. Among them, two tryptophan residues have a dominant role: Trp‐212, which resides within a hydrophobic pocket of the protein's core, and Trp‐134, which is exposed on the surface of the protein. These residues are both liable for the characteristic fluorescence emission profile of BSA [29, 30, 31].

In this work, fluorescence titration was conducted to investigate the binding mechanism of TAA with BSA. Incremental additions of TAA (0–200 μM, in 40‐μM steps) were introduced into 3‐μM BSA solutions. Emission spectra were recorded at 288, 298, and 308 K utilizing an excitation wavelength of 280 nm (Figure 2A–C). The fluorescence spectra of the BSA and BSA–TAA complexes were enrolled with excitation at 280 nm and emission in the wavelength range of 300–500 nm. In fluorescence studies, changes in protein emission intensity are typically evaluated at the maximum emission wavelength [12]. In experimental studies, the maximum emission wavelength for BSA was determined and used as 347 nm.

The concentration of BSA was held constant, whereas the TAA concentration was altered. As shown in Figure 2A–C, a decline in fluorescence intensity was monitored with the augmentation of TAA concentration. The observed signal loss indicates that TAA reduces the intrinsic fluorescence of BSA, most likely through nonfluorescent complex formation [29]. In addition, as shown in Figure 2A–C, the emission maximum of BSA exhibited a hypsochromic shift after the addition of TAA. Upon increasing the concentration of TAA, the fluorescence intensity of BSA declined by 46.70%, 49.41%, and 61.28% at 288, 298, and 308 K, respectively (Figure 3A), which indicates complex generation between the TAA molecule and BSA. The observed quenching suggests that TAA associates in the vicinity of Trp residues, thereby modifying their surrounding microenvironment [32].

Fluorescence quenching can occur through several physicochemical mechanisms, including collisional encounters, energy transfer, excited‐state reactions, and ground‐state complex formation [8, 33]. The fluorescence quenching mechanism of the protein can usually be classified into three groups, including dynamic quenching, static quenching, and combined static and dynamic quenching. In fluorescence quenching mechanisms, the dynamic quenching process occurs due to the collision of the protein molecule with the quencher molecule, whereas the static quenching process occurs due to the formation of a ground‐state complex between the protein molecule and the quencher. The hybrid‐based quenching mechanism, which comprises both dynamic and static quenching processes, occurs due to both collision and complex formation with the same quencher [34, 35]. Theoretically, dynamic and static quenching can be identified by their divergent dependency on the excited‐state lifetime and temperature. During the dynamic quenching process, as the molecules move faster as the temperature increases and accordingly the probability of collisions between them increases, fluorescence quenching increases. However, since the stability of the complex decreases with the increase of temperature, the formation of the ground‐state complex responsible for static quenching will decrease, and this will decrease fluorescence quenching [36, 37].

By utilizing the slope of the Stern–Volmer plot of F 0/F versus [Q] (Figure 3B), the values of K sv and K q were computed. The experimental results brought to light that the K sv ′ values increased from 0.59 × 104 to 1.03 × 104 L·mol−1·s−1, whereas the temperature was steadily raised. As the values of K sv increased with rising temperature, the quenching process was assigned to a dynamic quenching mechanism. In dynamic quenching, elevated temperatures enhance molecular diffusion and facilitate electron transfer, leading to an increase in the constant of K SV . Additionally, the K q values of the TAA–BSA system at three different temperatures were much lower than the diffusion‐limited quenching constant of biological molecules (2.0 × 1010 L mol−1 s−1), which propounded that the dynamic process had a prominent act in this interplay system [11, 29, 38, 39].

The Binding Affinity and Thermodynamic Parameters on the Binding Interaction of TAA–BSA Complex

The K a value is an essential parameter for understanding drug distribution in plasma, as weak interplays may ensue in limited distribution or rapid clearance, whereas overly potent binding can diminish the free biomolecule concentration available for therapeutic action.

According to Equation (3), the number of binding sites (n) and binding constant (K) were identified from the intercept and slope of the linear plot of log[(F 0 − F)/F] versus log[Q] (Figure 4), with the outcomes summarized in Table 2. An upward trend in K a values with rising temperature was demonstrated for TAA binding to BSA, suggesting augmented affinity and the generation of firmer TAA–BSA complexes at higher temperature values. The K a computed for the TAA–BSA system, ranging from 6.17 × 103 to 11.0 × 103 M−1, points out a moderate level of binding affinity. This moderate interplay may make possible the dissociation of TAA from its binding site, potentially elevating free TAA levels in the blood system, which could contribute to adverse effects. Moreover, the value of n ranged from 0.762 to 0.940. The n values for TAA interplays with BSA were close to 1.0, indicating a single class of binding sites. This suggests that TAA preferentially associates within the hydrophobic cavity of Subdomain IIA, in closeness to Trp‐214, consistent with the computational studies in the following sections [10, 40, 41].

Interactions between biological macromolecules and different molecules are generally described by four types of interactions/forces: Van der Waals, hydrophobic, electrostatic interactions, and hydrogen bonding [42]. The ΔS, ΔH, and ΔG values of this process are calculated with the help of the van't Hoff equation to determine the forces affecting the binding process between the drug molecule and BSA (Equation 5) [43]. ΔS° and ΔH° can be calculated from the intercept and slope of the linear plot of ln K a against 1/T. According to thermodynamic calculation results, negative values of ΔH 0 and ΔS 0 generally attribute to the copresence of hydrogen bonding and/or weak forces such as van der Waals forces that subscribe to the steadiness of the complex formed. Conversely, positive values of ΔH 0 and ΔS 0 normally remark on the existence of hydrophobic forces that preponderate over other forces [44]. Additionally, if ΔS is positive but ΔH is negative, electrostatic forces have a considerable act in binding [38].

As demonstrated in Table 2, the negative ΔG° value endorses the spontaneous nature of the binding process. The positive values of both ΔS° (+146.21 J mol−1 K−1) and ΔH° (+21.26 kJ mol−1) for the TAA–BSA interplay recommend that the binding is primarily driven by entropy, whereas the enthalpic contribution is unfavorable. These thermodynamic parameters mean that hydrophobic interplays predominantly govern the binding mechanism [45].

UV Spectra Measurements and Analysis

Although UV absorbance measurement is a simple method, it provides important information about complex formation mechanisms and is widely used in these studies. UV–Vis spectrophotometry proffers an informative and straightforward approach to analyzing conformational variances and complexation occurrences in biomolecular systems. To support the outcomes of the fluorescence experiment, UV–Vis spectra of BSA in the absence and existence of TAA were implemented [6, 46].

BSA ordinarily represents two considerable absorption bands, one near 210 nm associated with the peptide backbone and another around 280 nm, primarily attributed to aromatic amino acids such as Tyr, Trp, and phenylalanine (Phy) undergoing the π–π* transition. Alterations in absorbance at approximately 280 nm are diacritic of binding interplays with low molecular weight compounds. A red shift in this region of the spectrum frequently reflects an increment in the local polarity surrounding aromatic residues, whereas a blue shift recommends augmented hydrophobicity or a more nonpolar environment [6, 46]. As illustrated in Figure 5A, UV absorption spectra were recorded for TAA alone, BSA alone, and TAA–BSA mixtures. TAA displayed a characteristic absorbance peak approximately at 260 nm. In the UV absorption spectrum (Figure 5B), the intensity of BSA's absorbance increased progressively with the addition of TAA. A slight shift of the absorption maximum toward shorter wavelengths (blue shift; hypsochromic effect) was observed in the BSA–TAA complex. This shift in λ max recommends alterations in the microenvironment of the Trp residues, pointing out changes in the polarity and conformation of the peptide backbone. Additionally, a blue shift in the absorption maximum was also observed, likely due to complex formation between TAA and BSA.

Synchronous Fluorescence Spectroscopy Investigations

The synchronous fluorescence spectroscopic technique is an invaluable process for probing the local environment of intrinsic protein fluorophores, such as Tyr and Trp residues. By scanning excitation and emission wavelengths simultaneously with a constant wavelength offset (Δ λ ), this technique increases spectral selectivity and diminishes background interference, making it possible to maintain more detailed conformational and structural understanding about proteins [10, 47]. Synchronous fluorescence spectrophotometric examinations were enforced to come to a realization concerning the microenvironmental alterations surrounding protein fluorophores brought on by the binding of TAA molecules to BSA. When the wavelength interval (∆λ) is fixed at 15 or 60 nm, the synchronous fluorescence can differentiate the characteristics of Tyr and Trp residues of the BSA molecule, respectively [48].

When Figure 6 is examined, the decline in fluorescence intensity at Δλ = 60 nm was higher (64.49%) than at Δλ = 15 nm (27.94%). The fluorescence intensity clearly declines in the spectrum of Figure 6B. In this case, it displays that the quenching degree of Trp residues is higher than the quenching degree of Tyr residues. Consequently, the basis of the interplay between TAA and BSA is that the binding site on BSA is mainly focused on nearby Trp [22]. As the ligand (TAA) concentration increased, a bathochromic shift in the absorption spectrum was observed, along with a progressive increase in intensity. These changes indicate alterations in the microenvironment of BSA [49].

Especially, 3D fluorescence spectroscopy permits detailed inspection of conformational alterations in protein structures by capturing variations in emission behavior before and after ligand binding. In this context, comparison of 3D spectral profiles of BSA and TAA–BSA complexes (Figure 7) can provide valuable insights into structural alterations induced by drug binding.

Investigations of DLS Regarding the Interaction Between TAA and BSA

To elucidate the impact of TAA on the structural characteristics of BSA, the DLS technique was employed to accurately assess its hydrodynamic diameter, particle size distribution, and potential aggregation behavior. The DLS distribution results for solutions containing 3‐μM BSA are presented in the absence of TAA, as well as in the presence of 40‐μM TAA and 80‐μM TAA in Figures 8A‐C. The size distribution of the solution comprising solely BSA was 3.009 nm. The insertion of 40‐ and 80‐μM TAA resulted in a rise in the size distribution of BSA to 5.37 and 6.99 nm, respectively. The progressive enlargement of the hydrodynamic radius of BSA in response to increasing TAA concentrations demonstrates a concentration‐dependent interaction between the two molecules. This observation implies that TAA induces conformational rearrangements within BSA, ultimately promoting the aggregation and complex formation of the BSA–TAA system [19].

Electrochemical Assay of the Interplay of TAA With BSA

The electrochemical behavior of TAA was investigated in the nonexistence and presence of BSA to appraise their possible interplay at the electrode interface. DPV was employed to monitor the variations in oxidation reply upon consecutive additions of BSA to a fixed TAA concentration.

The DPV recorded for 7.5 ppm TAA with increasing BSA concentrations (5.0–15.0 ppm) exposes a gradual decline in oxidation peak current along with a slight shift of the peak potential toward more positive potential values (Figure 9). The continuous decrease in current indicates that the existence of BSA reduces the electroactive fraction of free TAA in solution, which can be attributed to complex formation between the TAA analyte and the protein BSA. The positive displacement of the oxidation potential recommends that the electron‐transfer process happens kinetically less favorably upon the addition of BSA. These results ensure a clear electrochemical argument for the interplay between BSA and TAA, as the complexation leads to a lower diffusion rate and hindered electron transfer of the bound TAA species at the electrode surface [50].

FTIR Results

FTIR spectroscopy assists as an important tool for probing molecular interplays between various small ligands and serum albumins, such as BSA and HSA, supplying data on characteristic vibrational modes associated with functional group alterations.

The IR spectrum of TAA (Figure 10) demonstrates characteristic bands at ~3300–3200 cm−1 (N–H stretching), 2960–2870 cm−1 (C–H stretching), 1620–1650 cm−1 (C=S stretching), 1230–1160 cm−1 (C–N stretching), and around 1040 cm−1 (C–S stretching). The shape and strong absorption near 1620 cm−1 support the existence of the thioamide group [51]. The FTIR spectrum of BSA (Figure 10) displays the characteristic vibrational properties of a typical globular protein. A broad absorption band noticed around 3280–3300 cm−1 is appointed to the amide (N–H stretching) vibration, specifying the existence of extensive intramolecular hydrogen bonding within the protein matrix. The most intense band at around 1650 cm−1 corresponds to the Amide I (C=O stretching) mode of BSA. The absorption near 1540 cm−1, attributed to the Amide II (N–H bending coupled with C–N stretching) vibration, further encourages the existence of a well‐defined peptide backbone stabilized by hydrogen bonds. A medium‐intensity band near 1390 cm−1 originates from the asymmetric stretching of carboxylate (COO−) groups of acidic amino acid residues, whereas additional peaks appearing near 1240 cm−1 (Amide III) and 1040 cm−1 (C–O and C–N stretching) arise from mixed vibrations of peptide linkages and amino acid side chains [52, 53]. The FTIR spectrum of the TAA–BSA complex (Figure 10) demonstrates distinct shifts relative to the spectra of the pure components, confirming molecular interplays between TAA and BSA. The Amide I band of BSA shifts from 1650 to ~1643 cm−1 with reduced intensity, indicating hydrogen bonding between peptide carbonyls and TAA's polar groups. The Amide II band broadens and moves to ~1532 cm−1, suggesting conformational rearrangements within the peptide framework, whereas the Amide A band (~3300 cm−1) shifts to lower frequencies on account of N–H···S=C interplays. The C=S vibration of TAA overlaps with Amide I, causing band broadening, and the C–S stretching (~1040 → 1050 cm−1) indicates a more polar environment after coordination. These spectral variations confirm that TAA binds to BSA mainly through hydrogen bonding and weak electrostatic forces, slightly perturbing the α‐helical structure of the protein.

Competitive Drug Displacement Results

Most small molecules known to bind with BSA tend to associate predominantly at Site I, whereas fewer interact at Site II. However, predicting the exact binding site solely based on the molecular structure is often arduous. Previous studies have indicated that Site I indicates preferential binding for compounds such as WFN, whereas KTN and similar agents typically target Site II. To determine the specific binding location of TAA on BSA, competitive displacement assays were conducted using site‐specific markers: KTN and WFN [54].

Figure 11 illustrates the titration profiles of BSA and its preformed complexes with WFN and KTN upon incremental addition of TAA. A progressive reduction in fluorescence intensity at RFI347 nm was recorded across all systems with increasing TAA concentrations. Specifically, the fluorescence quenching in the WFN–BSA complex was considerably less pronounced compared with native BSA, suggesting reduced accessibility or competitive displacement. Binding affinity analysis revealed that the K a values for the KTN–BSA and WFN–BSA complexes were 2.37 × 104 M−1 and 1.54 × 104 M−1, respectively, representing a reduction of approximately 24% and 50% relative to the unbound BSA (K a = 7.76 × 104 M−1). These indications emphasize that TAA competes with WFN for the same binding locus on BSA, namely, drug‐binding Site I, a conclusion further substantiated by molecular docking simulations.

Plots showing decline in the fluorescence signals at 347 nm of BSA as well as 1:1 WFN/KTN–BSA complexes with rising TAA concentrations: [BSA] = 3 μM; WFN = KTN = 6 μM; [TAA] = 0–200 μM with 40‐μM increments.

The competitive displacement tests ensure principal insight into the binding site preference of TAA on BSA. As demonstrated in Figure 11, the existence of WFN, a well‐known Site I (Subdomain IIA) marker, outstandingly reduced the binding affinity of TAA, whereas KTN, a selective Site II (Subdomain IIIA) probe, exerted a much weaker effect. This differential behavior remarks that TAA competes with WFN for the same binding region on BSA, while exhibiting minimal overlap with the KTN binding site. Drug‐binding Site I of serum albumin is characterized by a predominantly hydrophobic cavity surrounding Trp‐213 and is known to accommodate small polar and aromatic molecules through a combination of hydrophobic interplays and hydrogen bonding. The observed competitive displacement of WFN proposes that TAA preferentially associates within this area. In contrast, the restricted effect observed in the existence of KTN implies that Site II does not significantly contribute to TAA binding. This binding behavior is fully consistent with the spectroscopic and molecular docking results, which identified the vicinity of Trp‐213 in Subdomain IIA as the primary interaction region. Similar competitive displacement patterns, where Site I markers effectively inhibit ligand binding, whereas Site II probes show minimal influence, have been widely reported for small xenobiotic and toxic compounds interacting with serum albumins. These findings collectively confirm that TAA predominantly binds to Site I of BSA, providing a mechanistic explanation for its competitive interaction with WFN but not with KTN.

Molecular Docking and Molecular Dynamics Results

Molecular docking analyses of the BSA protein with the TAA were employed using CB‐Dock2 and MzDOCK tools. The binding poses with the lowest binding energy values obtained from each tool were selected for further evaluation, followed by MD simulations to assess the structural stability of the complexes. The CB‐Dock2, which operates through a blind docking approach, identified five potential binding pockets on the protein surface. Among these, the pose within the DS1 cavity exhibited a favorable binding affinity and conformational fit. In the MzDOCK platform, targeted docking was conducted based on the co‐crystallized ligand in the active site, revealing that TAA was suitably accommodated within the predefined binding pocket.

In this study, the docking pose obtained from MzDOCK was designated Pose 1 (Figure 12A), whereas the pose from CB‐Dock2 was referred to as Pose 2 (Figure 12B). For Pose 1, the calculated binding affinity was −7.1 kcal/mol, and the TAA was observed to interact with residues Arg194, Arg198, and Trp213 within the DS1 region. For Pose 2, the Vina score was calculated as −3.1 kcal/mol, with the docking pose located at coordinates (x, y, z = 79, 22, 29) within the DS1 region. In this pose, interactions were observed with residues His145 and Ser192. Redocking is performed to verify that the algorithms predict the binding poses correctly; the atomic deviation between a known reference pose and the docking pose is calculated as RMSD. The RMSD value for Pose 1 was measured as 1.36 Å, and for Pose 2 as 0.86 Å. The lower RMSD of Pose 2 indicates that it reproduces the experimental binding pose with higher accuracy. Both docking tools identified similar binding sites, supporting the DS1 domain as a potential binding site for TAA. Although the docking methodologies differed, both platforms converged on closely related regions, strengthening the consistency and reliability of the findings. Furthermore, previous experimental studies suggest that the compound has a remarkable affinity for the DS1 region of BSA. Consistent with these findings, in silico results indicated that the top‐ranked poses, including those identified via blind docking with CB‐Dock2, were predominantly located in the DS1 cavity. This agreement between experimental expectations and computational predictions strengthens the reliability of the identified binding site and highlights DS1 as a pharmacologically important site for TAA binding.

DFT Results

In molecular orbital (MO) theory, the highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) symbolizes the uppermost energy level that contains electrons in the ground state and is therefore closely associated with the electron‐donating ability of a molecule. Regions where the HOMO is localized are more prone to participate in oxidation processes, nucleophilic interplays, and electron transfer events. As a result, the spatial distribution of the HOMO ensures direct insight into the most reactive electron‐rich sites of a molecule. On the other hand, the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) corresponds to the lowest available energy level that can accept electrons and is related to the electron‐accepting capability of the molecular framework. Localization of the LUMO identifies molecular regions that are more susceptible to reduction processes or electrophilic interactions. Together, the HOMO and LUMO define the frontier orbitals that govern chemical reactivity and intermolecular interplays.

As shown in Figure 13, the HOMO of TAA is predominantly localized over the sulfur atom and the C=S bond, indicating that the thioamide sulfur constitutes the principal electron‐rich center of the molecule. This localization reflects the high polarizability of sulfur and the π‐electron density associated with the C=S functionality. In contrast, the LUMO is distributed over the C=S–NH2 framework, encompassing both the thioamide carbon and the amide nitrogen, suggesting that electron acceptance involves the conjugated thioamide system rather than a single atomic site.

Optimized structure (A) and HOMO–LUMO orbital distributions (B) of TAA calculated at the B3LYP/6‐311++G(d,p) level in the gas phase.

Using the computed frontier orbital energies (EHOMO = −0.24196 a.u. and ELUMO = −0.14484 a.u.), the resulting HOMO–LUMO energy separation of 0.09712 a.u. (≈2.64 eV) reflects a moderate energy gap between the occupied and unoccupied electronic states of TAA. Such an energy gap is characteristic of molecules with intermediate electronic softness, indicating that the redistribution of electron density does not require excessive energetic input. The relatively low‐lying HOMO suggests that electron donation predominantly originates from the thioamide sulfur‐centered region, whereas the accessible LUMO energy level makes easier acceptance of electron density within the conjugated C=S–NH2 framework. Therefore, this balanced HOMO–LUMO separation implies that the electronic structure of TAA can be readily modulated by its surrounding environment, underscoring the central role of the thioamide group in governing the intrinsic ground‐state electronic behavior of the molecule.

Conclusion

This study provides an exhaustive and integrative viewpoint on the molecular interplay landscape between TAA, a well‐acknowledged hepatotoxicant, and BSA, a pivotal plasma transport protein. By synergistically combining assorted spectroscopic and computational methodologies, our consequences bring to light the mechanistic nature of the binding behavior of TAA at the molecular level. Spectroscopic data divulged a temperature‐sensitive, dynamic quenching pattern with modest affinity, driven largely by hydrophobic interplays, and localized predominantly close to Trp‐213 within the Subdomain IIA of BSA. Remarkably, both 3D and synchronous fluorescence approaches underpinned that TAA engagement stimulates noteworthy alterations in the microenvironment and tertiary structure of the BSA molecule. Complemental molecular docking and MD simulations corroborated the experimental conclusions, showing site‐specific and stable binding within BSA's drug Site I, supported by permanent hydrogen bonding and minimal structural fluctuations over time. Additionally, thermodynamic studies remarked that hydrophobic forces have a primary role in the binding of TAA to BSA, as indicated by the positive ΔS and ΔH values, and the negative ΔG value shows that the interaction process between TAA and BSA occurs spontaneously. The gradual increase in the hydrodynamic radius of BSA in the DLS analysis indicated a concentration‐dependent interplay between the two molecules, confirming BSA–TAA aggregation and complex genesis. Additionally, the electrochemical findings, characterized by the gradual attenuation of the oxidation current and a positive shift in peak potential upon incremental BSA addition, further corroborate the formation of a TAA–BSA complex and the consequent reduction in electroactive free TAA fraction at the electrode interface, thereby reinforcing the spectroscopic and computational evidence for a stable binding interplay. Furthermore, combining data from these complementary techniques provides valuable insight into the intricate nature of ligand–BSA interplays, which are influenced by the structural flexibility of the protein and the diversity of its binding sites. Beyond the mechanistic understanding, the existing experimental results have practical implications, as albumin binding was demonstrated to diminish the electroactive free fraction of TAA. This interpretation is directly relevant for analytical monitoring approaches and for interpreting toxicokinetic behavior under physiological‐like conditions. As a consequence, the present study provides molecular‐level evidence that can support risk evaluation and exposure monitoring in industrial settings where TAA is used.
