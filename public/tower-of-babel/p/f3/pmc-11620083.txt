How do capable village cadres influence the effectiveness of rural living environment improvement? Empirical evidence from China
Authors: Yuyuan Yi, Ning Zhu, Yifu Zhao, Caiyan Liu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractRural Living Environment Improvement (RLEI) is crucial for constructing an ecological civilization and revitalizing rural areas. This study conducts an empirical analysis of the influence of capable village cadres on RLEI effectiveness, using microeconomic data from 173 villages across seven Chinese provinces between 2012 and 2019. The findings reveal a progressive enhancement in the RLEI among the surveyed villages over this timeframe. However, disparities persist in meeting farmers’ aspirations for an improved quality of life. Notably, village secretaries with business experience significantly contributed to advancements in domestic waste and sewage treatment (WST) and sanitary toilet coverage (STC). The robustness of these conclusions is upheld after adjusting the regression model and reassessing the sample data. Furthermore, capable village cadres can conveniently access government project support and promote the development of rural collective economy, thereby improving the RLEI. Comprehensive measures and substantial guarantees should be implemented to incentivize individuals with both integrity and competence to assume roles as village cadres. The monitoring system should be modernized, paving the way for a new paradigm of “strong state and strong society” in RLEI.Keywords: Capable village cadres, Rural living environment improvement, Business experience, Resilient governance

Introduction

Rural living environment improvement (RLEI) is pivotal for global economic and social development, and directly impacts the well-being of most farmers and the sustainable development of agriculture and rural areas [1,2]. Western countries have played a pioneering role in the process of industrialization and urbanization which has transformed human civilization. However, past practices of inefficient resource utilization and uncontrolled waste discharge have precipitated the ongoing degradation of rural ecological space and deterioration of the living environment [3,4]. Consequently, various developed nations and international organizations have implemented laws and regulations designed specifically for their regional circumstances: The United States established foundational environmental legislation, such as the Clean Water Act and Solid Waste Disposal Act [5,6]. Meanwhile, Japan enacted the Purification Tank Law and other waste management statutes [7], and the European Union instituted the 2007–2013 Rural Development Regulations [8]. These measures have led to significant improvements in the rural habitat in these countries and regions. By contrast, numerous developing nations are in the midst of industrializing and urbanizing. Rural areas in developing countries commonly experience sanitation and disorder issues, stemming from outdated environmental policies financial constraints, underdeveloped technology, and low rural community involvement [[9], [10], [11]]. These challenges significantly undermine public health and hinder rural development [[12], [13], [14]]. Consequently, investigating the governance of rural settlements in developing nations is imperative.

As one of the most populous developing countries in the world, China has consistently prioritized RLEI. In 2021, the “Five-Year Action Plan for the Improvement of Rural Human Settlements (2021–2025)” issued by the Ministry of Agriculture and Rural Affairs delineated key tasks: promoting sanitation, advancing waste management, and enhancing sewage treatment. This plan serves as a fundamental and actionable guide to ensure significant progress is made in RLEI. However, challenges, including disparities in regional development and inadequate basic amenities, still exist [15]. The National Bureau of Statistics of China reported that by the end of 2021, rural waste disposal rates reached 56.60 %, while sewage treatment lagged at 28 %. RLEI in China still has a long way to go [16].

Village cadres are the concrete facilitators of national policies and local government administrative tasks [17]. They form the main body of village public affairs management, playing the dual role of agents of the state and representatives of the residents [18]. Their capacity is critical in shaping the success of village governance [19]. Studies have indicated that village cadres with higher levels of education typically have a more profound understanding of national policies [20]. They actively seek suitable preferential policies or development opportunities to provide more income channels for farmers [21]. Village cadres with business experience usually possess strong economic capabilities, unique business philosophies, and social relationship networks, which help to enhance the village's ability to acquire resources and economic development capabilities, and are the core force in promoting neo-endogenous development of villages [22]. In addition, some scholars have focused on the role of village cadres' leadership in rural governance. The leadership of village officials plays an important role in stimulating farmers' enthusiasm for participating in public affairs, enhancing the village's collective action capacity, and improving the performance of rural governance [[23], [24], [25]]. With the establishment of livable and beautiful villages, the role of village cadres in RLEI has become particularly valued. Some scholars have pointed out that village cadres are the promoters, participants, and leaders in RLEI [26,27]. Therefore, the capabilities of village cadres largely determine the effectiveness of RLEI [17]. Overall, the importance of capable village cadres in RLEI has been widely recognized in academia [28,29]. However, opportunities for further research exist. First, previous studies on the internal mechanisms by which capable village cadres influence RLEI are still insufficient, and a “black box” still exists between the two. Second, constrained by data availability, related studies mostly rely on empirical summaries or theoretical deductions based on a few typical cases, with scarce literature empirically demonstrating the impact of capable village cadres on RLEI [28]. China is a typical “heterogeneous country”, and due to regional and socio-economic differences, studies utilizing small samples to reflect the actual situation in Chinese rural areas inevitably suffer from insufficient generalizability of their research conclusions [30]. In this study, rural microeconomic data from seven provinces in China from 2012 to 2019 were used to empirically analyze the impact of capable village cadres on RLEI, and the underlying mechanisms were examined.

This article is structured as follows: Section 2 outlines the theoretical foundation and presents the research hypotheses, clarifying the connection between village cadre competence and RLEI. Section 3 details the data sources, variables, and model selection. Section 4 presents the empirical results and their analysis. Section 5 summarizes the conclusions and policy implications.

Theoretical analysis and research hypothesis

Capable village cadres and the effectiveness of RLEI

In rural China, bridging the top-down governance goals of the state with the grassroots demands of villagers requires effective intermediaries [25]. Village cadres function as these key agents [17]. In accordance with the Chinese saying, “a thousand lines above and one needle below”, it is necessary to rely on village cadres to implement almost all higher-level policies relating to villagers [31]. Therefore, the capacity of village cadres is closely related to the effectiveness of policy implementation [32]. This is also true for the implementation of RLEI. In general, capable village cadres, especially those with business experience, often have a more defined development strategy and better understanding of RLEI's benefits for village progress and farmers' livelihood. This can mitigate the passive implementation of upper-tier government mandates by village cadres [33]. Additionally, capable village cadres can better mobilize villagers to engage in RLEI owing to their higher social prestige [26,34], and they are more proficient in deploying various governance tools [35]. For instance, some villages have strengthened the enforcement of village regulations and agreements [36]. Enhanced rules on classification and sanitation maintenance are now part of village ordinances, with specified penalties for non-compliance. Moreover, some villages have adopted a point-based system for RLEI and conduct inspections and evaluations consistently [37]. These approaches effectively foster intrinsic motivation among villagers to participate in RLEI. Based on the aforementioned analysis, this study proposes the following hypothesis.

Compared with ordinary village cadres, capable village cadres have a significant promoting effect on RLEI.

Impact mechanisms

RLEI is a typical public goods supply behavior [38]. Sustained and stable financial investment is critical for the success of RLEI. Presently, RLEI funding in China originates mainly from institutional and non-institutional sources [39]. Institutional supply is mainly provided through government projects and is the primary source of funding [28]. Non-institutional funding encompasses alternative financial channels beyond formal institutions, often realized as self-generated village funds [40]. For most villages, the key to sustained RLEI lies in improving the accessibility of government project funds and increasing the endogenous development capacity of villages [41]. Therefore, this research assesses the role of capable village cadres in RLEI through the lens of “external introduction” and “internal connections”.

First, the external introduction mechanism is examined. Since the tax reform in China, project-based funding has emerged as a crucial way to distribute resources and deliver public services to grassroots communities [42]. The success of RLEI in villages largely hinges on government project support, with resource acquisition resting on the governance skills and social networks of the village cadres [43]. Capable village cadres usually possess a deep comprehension of policies [20]. They can leverage their resources and abilities to compete for projects and funding for their villages. Additionally, capable village cadres usually possess substantial social capital, enabling them to efficiently consolidate various resources in the village and unite different stakeholders [22]. This facilitates more significant achievements in RLEI and establishes a foundation for ongoing government support. Furthermore, local authorities favor allocating resources to villages with robust development bases, promising futures, and exemplary cadres [44]. The execution of government projects can close the funding shortfall in RLEI, and through awards and incentives, effectively encourage villager participation in these efforts [45].

Second, fostering intrinsic development impetus in villages is also critical to advancing RLEI. Research has pointed out that three main sources of self-financing exist: contributions from village collective economic organizations, equal contributions from villagers, and donations from elite residents [46]. Considering that public fund-raising may aggravate the burden on farmers and may lead to the problem of “free-riding” [47]and that individual donations are unpredictable [48], this study argues that expanding the rural collective economy is the key to enhancing the endogenous development capacity of villages. In the development of China's rural collective economy, village cadres frequently function as the operators, managers, and decision-makers for collective assets, and their competencies significantly influence the collective economy's progression [46]. The neoclassical economic growth model posits that economic growth is the result of capital, labor, and technology synergistically contributing to production [49]. The endogenous growth theory highlights that economic growth is primarily driven by investment in human capital, innovation, and knowledge [50]. This study suggests that competent village cadres, with their exceptional entrepreneurial skills, enhance productivity and, by extension, spur economic growth in the collective rural economy. Practically, as the competencies of village cadres has increased, the rural collective economy has experienced swift growth. Data from the China Rural Management Statistics Annual Report (2015) and China Rural Policy and Reform Statistics Annual Report (2021) show a decrease in villages without a rural collective operating income nationwide, from 310,664 in 2015 to 115,469 in 2021. Capable village cadres can obtain more government financial support for a village, thereby improving RLEI.

Capable village cadres can promote the development and growth of a village's collective economy, thereby improving RLEI.

Material and methods

Data source

The data for this study were obtained from the Survey for Agriculture and Village Economy (SAVE) from 2012 to 2019. Initiated in 2011 by the Institute of Agricultural Economics and Development of the Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences, SAVE's data collection commenced in 2012. To ensure a representative sample, provinces across North, Northeast, East, Central South, Southwest, and Northwest China were ranked by the average net income per farmer from 2008 to 2012. Provinces with average incomes falling in the mid-range for each region were selected as survey subjects. In 2012, eight provinces (autonomies)—including Hebei, Shandong, Jilin, Fujian, Henan, Yunnan, Shaanxi, and Xinjiang—were selected (Fig. 2). Three counties, cities, or districts per province were selected via stratified random sampling, based on economic development levels, comprising three towns each, and then three villages within those towns (Xinjiang excluded). In Xinjiang, seven counties were selected, encompassing 54 villages. Since 2012, annual follow-up surveys have been conducted. Village questionnaire data were collected through face-to-face interviews with village cadres. The survey encompasses information on basic demographics, party-building, industrial growth, local culture, social governance, and ecological construction in the sample villages. Considering the inconsistent sampling method in Xinjiang compared with the other seven provinces, these data were excluded to eliminate potential bias in the regression results. After removing samples that were missing key variables, a mixed panel dataset of 173 villages with 1263 total observations over eight years was constructed.

Study areas map.

Geographically, the study spans Eastern, Central, and Western China, ensuring extensive area coverage. Additionally, the sampled villages represent diverse economic levels across provinces and regions, minimizing sample selection bias. To further validate the study data's representativeness, this study compared the annual per capita disposable income of the sampled villages with data from the National Economic and Social Development Statistics Bulletin by the National Bureau of Statistics. Subsequent analyses revealed an annual margin of error margin of less than 10 %, suggesting that the survey samples closely mirror national characteristics, thereby affirming their representativeness.

Variable description

Dependent variable

This study identifies RLEI effectiveness as the dependent variable. Considering that RLEI in China focuses on domestic waste, sewage, and sanitary toilets, this study employs village waste and sewage treatment status, alongside sanitary toilet coverage rates, as explanatory variables. Prior research has frequently utilized binary variables to measure waste and sewage treatment, (e.g., domestic waste sorting and centralized sewage treatment processes) [51]. Concurrently, some studies have gauged RLEI effectiveness through user satisfaction [52]. Additionally, comprehensive assessment indices for RLEI were developed using the entropy method [53]. This study suggests that reliance on binary variables for assessment could lead to oversimplification, which does not precisely capture the nuances of waste and sewage treatment levels in villages. Consequently, this study classifies village waste and sewage treatment status (WST) into four ordinal categories: no treatment, partially treated, mostly treated, and fully treated. The typical standard measure for sanitary toilets is represented by their coverage rate. Drawing from the literature [54], this study adopts the village sanitary toilet coverage rate (STC) as an indicator of toilet improvement effectiveness. Currently, toilets in China are primarily of three types: flush toilets, sanitary dry toilets (a sealed type of dry toilet with a small fecal treatment device that can control odor and prevent pollution), and regular dry toilets (open dry toilets that cannot control odor or prevent pollution). This study defines flush toilets and sanitary dry toilets as “sanitary toilets.”

Independent variables

Educational levels are frequently employed to evaluate the capabilities of village cadres. However, studies have identified a threshold under which education does not significantly increase the cadres' effectiveness [55]. Additionally, some scholars have investigated the impacts of non-agricultural experiences on village cadres, including business and military backgrounds [56,57]. This study proposes that particular forms of human capital, like business experience, derived from market activities, can be indicative of an individual's comprehensive quality. Village cadres possessing such experience are often characterized by traits of both political and economic elites, showing a significant focus on economic efficiency within governance practices. Hence, this study adopts the business experience of the village cadres (BEC) as an indicator of abilities, measured by their experience as a business owner or self-employed individual. Considering the village secretary's role as the primary authority and central figure in managing and making decisions about village collective assets, this study focuses on these individuals.

Mediating variables

Based on the aforementioned mechanism analysis, the mediating effect is examined from both external and internal perspectives. Government project investment undertaken by the village (logarithm) and village collective operating income (logarithm) are utilized as proxy variables. The former represents project-based government funds allocated to villages for RLEI, reflecting the village's capability to acquire project resources. The latter encompasses village collective operating income, contracting income, and investment income, which signify the income-generating and sustainable development capacity of village collective economic organizations.

Control variables

Other village attributes frequently influence the effectiveness of RLEI. Building on existing research, this study measures each village's natural endowment by examining the per capita cultivated land area and distance between the village and nearest county center. The number of farming households (logarithm) and number of natural villages were used to gauge village scale and dispersion. Whether it functions as a town government seat, and its location in a revolutionary base area are considered social characteristics of the village. The completion of rural collective property rights system reform and implementation of the “one shoulder pole” system (the village party secretary concurrently serves as the village director) are utilized to measure the village's institutional environment. The economic development characteristics of a village are assessed by logarithms of per capita disposable income, public transportation accessibility, and the number of self-employed businesses. Additionally, the age and years of education of the village secretaries are taken into account. BECit denotes the business experience of the village secretaries, serving as a crucial explanatory factor in our model. Xit symbolizes a set of control variables. μ indicates fixed effects at the individual village level, accounting for time-invariant factors, such as entrenched sanitation habits and geographic characteristics. v denotes time-fixed effects that mitigate the impact of unobservable temporal variables, such as evolving rural policies and media efforts in environmental governance promotion. εit is the stochastic error term.

This study treats WSTit as an ordinal categorical variable and utilizes an ordered logit model within a panel data framework for estimation. Existing literature frequently employs the random effects ordered logit model to regress panel data featuring ordinal discrete dependent variables. However, meeting this model's assumptions poses challenges in practice, as it necessitates the independence of explanatory variables from individual effects [58]. The fixed-effects ordered logit model is effective in removing endogeneity bias due to unchanging individual heterogeneity [59]. Additionally, this method circumvents the inefficiency in leveraging explanatory variable information, a problem that can render fixed-effects binary logit model estimations invalid and biased. Consequently, the study employs an ordered logit fixed-effects model to empirically assess the effect of capable village cadres on WST.

Considering that STCit is a bounded dependent variable, a panel Tobit model is used for estimations. The fixed-effects Tobit model cannot execute conditional maximum likelihood estimation due to insufficient data on individual heterogeneity μi, unlike its logit counterpart. The study utilizes a random-effects Tobit regression for the analysis and an OLS fixed-effects model for robustness verification.

Moreover, drawing on Iacobucci et al. [60], this study applies structural equation modeling (SEM) to assess if GPI and COI mediate BEC's impact on RLEI. Notably, SEM offers benefits over OLS regression by reducing standard errors, which may otherwise skew the estimated path coefficients.

Robustness test

Replacing regression models

Although the study accounts for specific individual and temporal variables that may influence the RLEI outcomes, it recognizes the potential for misreporting by respondents, such as the overreporting or underreporting of WST. To minimize the impact of measurement inaccuracies on findings, this study assigns a score of 1 to “mostly treatment” and “fully treatment,” and a score of 0 to “no treatment” and “partially treatment”, and then empirically analyzes them again using a binary logit fixed effects model. Additionally, this study employs an OLS fixed effects model to verify the supportive role of the BEC in the STC. The results demonstrate a notably positive impact of the village secretary's business experience on both WST and STC, consistent with the fundamental regression analysi (see Table 4). This provides additional validation of Hypothesis 1.

Propensity score matching

To mitigate the selection bias arising from systematic disparities between villages with business-experienced secretaries and those without, this study applies propensity score matching (PSM) to perform a robustness assessment of the baseline regression outcomes. As this study uses panel data, a time-by-time matching method is employed to select the control group samples, inspired by Böckerman and Ilmakunnas [61]. Balance test outcomes (Fig. 4) reveal a marked decrease in standardized variable bias post-matching. This indicates that PSM effectively rectifies bias in research results due to systematic variances and passes the balance test. Subsequently, the study conducted regression analysis on the matched sample set (Table 5). The regression results closely align with those of the benchmark regression, lending additional support to the empirical findings’ robustness.

Introducing external mechanism

The study utilizes the “medsem” function in Stata to estimate the parameters along each pathway of impact. Table 6 presents the outcomes of BEC's indirect effect on WST through GPI. The three-step Baron and Kenny procedure indicates partial mediation, identifying GPI as a mediator in BEC's impact on WST. This conclusion is also supported by the Zhao, Lynch, and Chen (ZLC) approach. Specifically, GPI explains 23.4 % of this indirect relationship, as evidenced by the proportion of the indirect effect relative to the total effect. Similarly, GPI acts as a mediator between BEC and STC, with an RIT of 30.4 %, as presented in Table 7. This indicates that seeking more financial support from government programs is still an important means of improving the habitat of the village. Compared with ordinary village cadres, capable village cadres are more competitive in this regard. In summary, Hypothesis 2 is supported.

Internal linking mechanism

Table 8 presents the indirect effects of BEC on WST via COI. Notably, BEC positively impacts COI and is significant at the 1 % level. In step (2), the p-value is 0.244, exceeding the 10 % threshold for significance. The result is reinforced by the indirect effect's insignificance across the Delta, Sobel, and Monte Carlo methods, with p-values of 0.245, 0.245, and 0.249, respectively. This indicates that COI does not serve as a mediator in BEC's impact on WST within the sample. The same applies to BEC's impact on STC (see Table 9). Thus, Hypothesis 3 is not supported. A possible explanation is that the governance of rural residential environments currently focuses on developing infrastructure such as sewage treatment pipelines and centralized garbage processing sites. These projects require substantial investment and are primarily funded by the government. In numerous villages, underdeveloped collective economies face insufficient funding for such infrastructure projects, with limited income more likely to be allocated to general daily expenditures. This study contends that the situation may not always be as it appears, for the following three reasons: First, with ongoing rural reforms, the rural collective economy is expected to grow, thereby enhancing the material base that enables village collectives to contribute to RLEI. Second, although the Chinese government is considerably proactive, the involvement of non-governmental organizations remains crucial, particularly when economic development slows down and fiscal pressures arise. Third, the governance of rural living environments will shift toward ongoing maintenance and management. Correspondingly, the demand for funds will also shift from the pursuit of scale to sustainability. The short-term nature of government project-based provisions may not fully satisfy this; however, the participation of village collectives can effectively address this gap. Accordingly, the role of village collective economic organizations in RLEI warrants attention.

Discussion

RLEI is contingent on the initial establishment of infrastructure as well as its ongoing operations and maintenance. Sustained and stable financial investment is critical for the success of RLEI. For a considerable period of time, the Chinese government has covered almost the entire cost of RLEI [28]. In this situation, the government not only faces enormous financial pressure [14], but is also prone to the challenges of mismatch between supply and demand [62], “the last kilometre” [63], and the emphasis on construction at the expense of maintenance [64]. Therefore, some scholars have pointed out the need to give full play to the role of villagers in the RLEI [38]. Its rationality lies in its ability to effectively enhance the efficiency of the funds utilization and effectively meet the actual needs of farmers [46], which is suitable for some small-scale investment areas. However, some studies have noted that farmers' financing will inevitably cause the problem of “free-riding” [47] and an increased burden on farmers [65]. In recent years, with the deepening of the reform of the rural collective property rights system, the development of the collective economy has once again gained importance. Funding from rural collective economic organizations can not only compensate to some extent for the short-term nature of the government's project-based system and the mismatch between supply and demand, but also avoid the problem of high transaction costs associated with fundraising from villagers. Consequently, some scholars have argued that enhancing the capacity of rural collective economic organizations and increasing the income of the village collective economy is considered one of the most important ways to sustainably break the dilemma of rural public goods provision in the future [66].Overall, the RLEI in China has experienced a shift from being “government-led” to “multi-participation”. However, regardless of whether it is “government-led” or “multi-participation”, RLEI is inseparable from the key group, village cadres. As a bridge between the state and residents, village cadres are responsible for promoting grass-roots democracy, developing the rural economy, and managing village public affairs [67,68]. Therefore, their abilities are a key factor impacting the effectiveness of RLEI. This study confirms the impact of capable village cadres on the governance of rural human settlements from two dimensions: external introduction and internal connections.

On the one hand, the project-based system is the most important means by which the Chinese government distributes resources to grassroots villages [69]. Therefore, whether or not they can secure government funding for projects or how much they can secure determines, to a certain extent, the level of infrastructure and public services in that village. Generally speaking, compared with ordinary village cadres, capable village cadres have a deeper understanding of policies [20], which can enable them to have an advantage in the fight for projects. At the same time, capable village cadres usually have a good public base and can be trusted by villagers [26]. Therefore, villages with capable village cadres usually have strong cohesion and organizational mobilization ability, which is an important reason for the successful implementation of government projects [47]. On the contrary, villages with weak village cadres usually have poor organizational mobilization ability and more cases of non-cooperation from villagers, which makes it very difficult to implement projects [69]. For grassroots governments, they usually tend to favor project funds to villages with better development prospects for reasons of political performance. Therefore, capable village cadres can help villages take an advantageous position in project acquisition, which is in line with Han et al. [22] research.

On the other hand, it is difficult to fully satisfy farmers' demand for public services by relying only on government project funds, as the temporary and phased nature of the project-based system dictates [70]. Therefore, it is necessary to play the role of the village itself [71]. This paper finds that capable village cadres, especially those with business experience, are able to significantly increase rural collective operating income and thereby improve the RLEI. The possible explanation is that village cadres with business experience usually have a sharper insight into market dynamics, resource allocation and business opportunities [72]. They are better able to identify potential opportunities for the development of the village collective economy and guide the village collectives to invest in promising industries or projects, thus promoting the rural collective economy in a more efficient and competitive direction, which is consistent with the research findings of [73]. [74]. In addition, business experience has enabled village cadres to accumulate practical experience in business management and operation, which helps them adopt a more scientific and professional approach in managing village collective assets and formulating development strategies [75]. For example, they can effectively promote the sustained growth of the rural collective economy by introducing modern enterprise management concepts, optimizing resource allocation, and improving operational efficiency.

The contribution of this study is reflected in three aspects. First, the study demonstrates the need to build a “strong state - strong society” governance pattern for RLEI. It is essential that any country or region cannot rely solely on the government or the spontaneous power of villages to effectively improve the rural human environment, and that multifaceted and cooperative governance is the only long-term solution. Secondly, it confirms the importance of capable village cadres in the RLEI, which provides empirical references for the RLEI in a wide range of developing countries, including China. Third, methodologically, the study employs fixed-effects ordered logit and random-effects Tobit models to empirically assess how capable village cadres influence rural human settlement improvements. It also utilizes a structural equation model to elucidate mechanisms, addressing the endogeneity of prior cross-sectional data to strengthen the validity of its conclusions and practicality of its policy recommendations.Despite its contributions, there are still limitations. (1) This study is limited in assessing cadres' capability solely on the presence of business experience. Capabilities are indeed multifaceted, with business acumen being important but not exclusive. For instance, cadres' military or political backgrounds may significantly impact village development, yet this aspect was overlooked due to data constraints. Moreover, self-employment experience and business owner experience are both business-related experiences, but their impact on RLEI may differ significantly. Due to data availability, this study was not able to explore this aspect further. (2) At present, rural domestic waste management, domestic sewage management, toilet feces management and village appearance enhancement are all important elements of the RLEI in China. However, this study only examined the first three due to data limitations, while village appearance improvement was not taken into account. (3) This research focuses on the village level, but RLEI involves farmers’ close participation. Incorporating farmer-oriented case studies would be pivotal to advancing future research.

Conclusion and implications

Main conclusion

Notably, RLEI is a comprehensive, long-term, and complex endeavor that necessitates the collaborative participation of various entities including the government, village cadres, villages, and other NGOs. Among them, village cadres—as promoters and advocates for the rectification of the rural living environment—are also direct participants and leaders, whose role should not be underestimated.

Based on the rural microeconomic data from seven provinces in China from 2012 to 2019, this study empirically analyzes capable village cadres’ impact on RLEI and its underlying mechanism, using the fixed-effects ordered logit model, random-effects Tobit model, and structural equation model. The main findings are as follows: First, the average value of WST in the sample villages from 2012 to 2019 increased from 2.3394 to 3.2453. Concurrently, the STR increased from 52.19 % to 74.01 %. This indicates a gradual improvement in RLEI, though the overall level has not yet reached a high standard. Second, village secretaries with business experience significantly positively impact WST and STC, indicating that capable village cadres are pivotal in enhancing RLEI. This conclusion still holds after conducting robustness tests by adjusting the regression model and reassessing the sample data. Third, the mediation analysis reveals that capable village cadres can improve RLEI by obtaining financial support from government programs. Although the mediating effect of COI is not significant, this study has demonstrated the contribution of capable village cadres to the COI. This is significant in enhancing the internal dynamics of village development and building a “strong state and society” governance pattern of RLEI.

Importantly, although effective village cadres are instrumental in RLEI, acknowledging the governance boundaries and limitations inherent in this arrangement is crucial. Proficiency in business does not necessarily translate to adept village governance. Electing entrepreneurs as village leaders can cause issues such as the conflation of public and private interests and corruption, undermining effective village governance. Consequently, establishing a robust appraisal and oversight mechanism is essential to ensure that these village cadres exercise their authority under the vigilant oversight of the villagers and in accordance with legal standards.

Policy implications

Based on this study's findings, the following policy recommendations are proposed.

(1)Multiple strategies and guarantees to motivate talented and honorable individuals to return and serve as village cadres should be implemented. A consistent talent engagement mechanism should be established to facilitate daily communication with external entrepreneurs and swiftly address their issues and doubts. Meanwhile, the incentives for grassroots village cadres who are responsible, efficient, and take initiative should be enhanced. This will ensure that they receive recognition, financial security, and promotion opportunities, thereby boosting their work enthusiasm.
