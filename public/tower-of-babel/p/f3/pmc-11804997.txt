Comparative Evaluation of Surface Roughness of Different Composite Restorative Materials after Thermocycling and Prolonged Immersion in Common Beverages Using Atomic Force Microscopy—In Vitro Study
Authors: Akanksha Malik, Anjali K. Kothari, Riya Dave, Vinay V. Rao, Kaushal Vyas, Deep P. Agrawal, Kishan Patel
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:Direct composite restorations face abrupt temperature changes, varying pH, and surface deterioration in the oral environment. Atomic force microscopy (AFM) provides visual and numerical data on surface roughness.Aim:This study evaluates and compares the surface roughness of composite restorative materials after simulating oral conditions.Materials and Methods:Three composite groups (n = 16 each) were tested. Group A: Microhybrid, Group B: Nanofilled, and Group C: Nanohybrid. Each group was divided into four subgroups (n = 4 each): distilled water (control), black coffee, Coca-Cola, and Red Bull. After thermocycling and immersion, surface roughness was measured using AFM.Results:Nanocomposites with uniform monomodal fillers showed the least surface disintegration and superior properties.Conclusion:Resin chemistry, including filler size and matrix composition, is crucial for composite properties and performance under function.

INTRODUCTION

Composite resins, long used as aesthetic restorative materials, are now preferred for posterior teeth. They consist of filler particles in a resin matrix linked by a silane coupling agent. Microhybrid, nanofilled, and nanohybrid composites are the most common types.[1]

Acidic sports and energy drinks cause acid erosion, degrading composite restorations. The mechanical properties of composites depend on their composition and chemistry. Research has improved these properties and aesthetics by reducing filler size, increasing inorganic filler load, and modifying the organic matrix.[2]

For clinical success, composites must endure long-term functionality, benefiting patients and reducing dentists’ workload.

Method

• Specimen Preparation:

Forty-eight composite specimens (5 mm diameter, 2 mm thickness) were prepared using Teflon-coated instruments and stainless-steel molds, covered with glass slides, and polymerized using an LED curing light. Stored in distilled water in lightproof bottles, they were finished with composite stones and polished with the Super-Snap Rainbow kit.

• Thermocycling Treatment:

Specimens underwent thermocycling between 5°C and 60°C for 3000 and 10,000 cycles, then washed and stored.

• Prolonged Immersion in Beverages:

Four specimens from each subgroup were immersed daily in 5 ml of various beverages for 2 minutes over 30 days, simulating saliva effects.

• Evaluation of Surface Roughness:

• Surface roughness was measured post-thermocycling and beverage immersion using an AFM.

DISCUSSION

Water acts as a plasticizer, degrading composites through hydrolysis at matrix-filler and silane-filler interfaces. Thermocycling mimics oral temperature changes, validating the combined effects of hydrolysis and thermal stress on composites.[3]

TEGDMA, a composite matrix component, is more prone to degradation due to its hydrophilicity, unlike Bis-GMA, which absorbs more water.[4] TEGDMA also undergoes greater enzymatic hydrolysis than aromatic monomers like Bis-EMA or Bis-GMA.[5] Replacing TEGDMA with less water-soluble Bis-EMA and UBis stabilizes and improves composites.[6]

Adding smaller filler particles increases the filler-to-matrix ratio, evenly distributing stress and reducing fatigue failure. Smaller fillers result in a smoother surface finish.[7] Our study found higher surface roughness in Polofil Supra (microfillers) compared to Estelite Sigma Quick (nanofillers).

Multimodal composites with irregularly sized, coarse filler particles have more gaps and protruding particles, resulting in inferior surface properties. This leads to stress concentration, “clumping,” and higher surface roughness compared to monomodal composites.[8] Uniform, small spherical filler particles are preferred for better performance. Nanofilled composites show higher wear resistance and lower roughness than nanohybrid composites.[910]

Acidic beverages like Coca-Cola, Red Bull, and Coffee[1112] erode composite surfaces, with Red Bull being more damaging due to its higher buffering capacity. Phosphoric acid in these drinks causes tooth erosion, nanoleakage, restoration failure, and secondary caries. Water facilitates acid penetration, causing microcracks.[2131415]

3D nanoscale images of composites using AFM [Figure 1] revealed peaks and valleys from thermocycling and beverage effects, providing detailed surface roughness data. AFM is a reliable technique for assessing surface textures of various materials.[15]

CONCLUSION

Temperature changes in the mouth can cause composite dental materials to break down through hydrolysis, increasing acid vulnerability.Carbonated and energy drinks accelerate the acid-induced deterioration of composite dental materials, increasing surface roughness.Nanocomposites have superior surface characteristics and durability, ideal for enduring challenging oral conditions.
