Effectiveness and safety of left atrial appendage electrical isolation in managing persistent atrial fibrillation: an updated meta-analysis of 3425 patients
Authors: Ahmed Elshahat, Ahmed Diaa, Rehab Adel Diab, Shrouk Ramadan, Youssef Barakat, Mohamed Ismail Elnady, Amr Arafa, Ahmed Mansour, Ahmed Sobhy, Ahmed Elmorsy Mohamed, Mohamed Yasser Elnaggar, Ahmed Yasser Shaban, Salwa Gharib, Ahmed Abdelaziz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and aimAtrial fibrillation (AF) is a substantial public health disorder associated with heightened morbidity and mortality. Pulmonary vein isolation (PVI) has limited efficacy in persistent AF (PAF), indicating additional strategies such as left atrial appendage electrical isolation (LAAEI). This study evaluates the efficacy and safety of LAAEI in managing PAF.MethodsAn extensive search of the literature was carried out on PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Cochrane Library. We included all the eligible studies comparing LAAEI plus PVI to PVI alone in managing PAF. Our primary endpoint was the incidence of recurrent atrial arrhythmia. Dichotomous data were pooled as risk ratios (RRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).ResultsTwelve studies involving 3,425 patients were included in our meta-analysis. LAAEI significantly reduced the incidence of recurrent atrial arrhythmia (RR = 0.57, 95% CI: [0.54 to 0.75], P = 0.001). Furthermore, the subgroup analysis focusing on the utilized anatomical approach showed that the ostial approach favored the LAAEI compared to the control group in reducing the incidence of recurrent atrial arrhythmias, while the linear approach did not favor either of the two groups. The radiofrequency strategy favored the LAAEI group rather than the control group, while the cryoballoon ablation and the LARIAT device did not prefer either of the two groups.ConclusionLAAEI plus PVI is a promising strategy when compared to PVI alone to reduce the incidence of recurrent atrial arrhythmia in patients with PAF.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12872-026-05607-0.Keywords: Atrial fibrillation, Left atrial appendage, Persistent AF, Catheter ablation, LAAEI

Introduction

Atrial fibrillation (AF) is a notable public health challenge with significant social, clinical, and economic adverse implications across the world [1, 2]. The incidence and prevalence of AF increase, with estimates reaching 50 million cases by 2020 [3, 4]. Common symptoms of AF include heart palpitations, dizziness, and chest pain with consequent major complications such as heart failure, myocardial infarction, and stroke [5, 6]. Therefore, developing effective treatment options has become a top priority for healthcare systems all over the world [7, 8].

The contemporary AF ablation approach, including the electrical isolation of pulmonary veins (PVs) from the left atrial chamber, was prompted by early research revealing that ectopic activity originating from the PVs commonly triggers AF [9]. Despite consistent success with pulmonary vein isolation (PVI) in treating paroxysmal AF, the results for persistent atrial fibrillation (PAF) are suboptimal [10].

PAF is caused by foci extending beyond the PVs [11, 12]. It often necessitates extensive catheter ablation (CA) that targets significant portions of the atrial myocardium as well as adjacent thoracic veins [13]. Nevertheless, the STAR AF II trial revealed that neither complex fractionated atrial electrogram ablation nor empirical linear ablation, when added to PVI, conferred any additional benefit in patients with non-paroxysmal AF [8]. Furthermore, the persistent success of PVI in treating long-standing PAF is approximately 20% [14].

Numerous reports have revealed that the left atrial appendage (LAA) acts as an arrhythmogenic trigger for AF [9, 15]. Consequently, several studies examined the efficacy of left atrial appendage electrical isolation (LAAEI) in managing patients with PAF. They found significant effectiveness in reducing atrial arrhythmia recurrence following this procedure [9, 16, 17]. Despite its potential advantages, LAAEI is not widely performed worldwide due to the risk of complications, encompassing myocardial perforation, damage to surrounding structures, and a heightened risk of thromboembolism from the loss of LAA function after isolation [16]. Therefore, our meta-analysis aims to assess the effectiveness and safety of LAAEI in a systematic approach for managing PAF.

Methods

This systematic review and meta-analysis was carried out in strict alignment to the guidelines declared in the Cochrane Handbook [18]. PRISMA statement guidelines were followed during the process of reporting this study [19]. This study was registered on PROSPERO (ID: CRD42024569523).

Eligibility criteria and endpoints

We included all studies, randomized controlled trials (RCTs), and observational studies investigating the efficacy and safety of LAAEI plus PVI compared to PVI alone in managing PAF patients. Our main endpoint was the incidence of recurrent atrial arrhythmias. Detailed definitions of recurrent atrial arrhythmia per study are provided in Supplementary Table 1. Our secondary outcomes were AF recurrence, death, stroke, bleeding, transient ischemic attack (TIA), LAA thrombus, and pericardial effusion. We also studied the procedural characteristics encompassing procedure time and fluoroscopy time.

Our exclusion criteria were as follows: reversible causes of AF (hyperthyroidism), LA thrombus, contraindication for anticoagulation, life expectancy less than 12 months, repeated cases, or AF rhythm during the left atrial angiogram. Conference abstracts.

Literature search and selection process

A comprehensive search of the literature was carried out on PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science (WOS), and Cochrane Library from origin till August 2024 using the following search strings: ((atrial fibrillation) OR (persistent atrial fibrillation) OR (long-standing persistent atrial fibrillation)) AND ((left atrial appendage) OR (catheter ablation) OR (pulmonary vein isolation) OR (LAA)). A detailed search strategy for each database is outlined in Supplementary Table 2.

Following the databases search, the gathered reports underwent a two-phase screening process by two blinded authors: A.D and M.E. First, the titles and abstracts were evaluated utilizing the Rayyan web [20]. Second, the full text of retrieved articles after the title and abstract screening phase was evaluated based on our pre-determined selection criteria. Duplicate records were eliminated by employing EndNote (Clarivate Analytics, PA, USA). Additionally, A manual screening was performed on the references of the studies chosen for inclusion. Any disagreement among the authors was addressed by a discussion with a third author.

Data gathering

Two blinded authors, A.M. and S.R., extracted the summary and baseline data employing a pre-designed data extraction sheet. The summary data involved study design, country, sample size, the type of AF, the type of catheter ablation used, inclusion criteria, primary outcome, follow-up period, and findings. The baseline features of studies population include age, male gender, body mass index (BMI), CHA2DS2-VASc score, left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), left atrial size, comorbidities, and procedural characteristics. Any conflict between the authors was addressed through a conversation with a third author.

Risk of bias evaluation

Two authors, Y.B. and A.D. evaluated the quality of randomized controlled trials (RCTs) using the Cochrane risk of bias two (ROB2) tool [21]. ROB2 assesses the risk of bias concerning several domains encompassing the randomization process, deviation from intended interventions, missing outcome data, outcome measurement, selection of the reported results, and other sources of bias. The authors classify the risk of bias within RCTs as high, low, or some concerns about risk of bias. Furthermore, the Newcastle Ottawa Scale (NOS) was utilized to assess the quality of observational studies [22]. NOS assesses the quality of observational studies regarding various items, including the selection process, comparability between the two cohorts, and outcome assessment. Any conflict among the review authors was addressed by a discussion with a third author.

Data synthesis

Dichotomous outcomes were pooled as risk ratios (RRs), and their 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and continuous outcomes were pooled as Cohen’s d with its 95% CI employing the restricted maximum likelihood estimator with Hartung–Knapp adjustmet. We identified the statistical significance when the p-value < 0.05. A notable heterogeneity was assumed if the chi-square p-value fell less than 0.05 or the I2 > 50%. Furthermore, we conducted subgroup analyses according to study design, short-term (12 months) and long-term (more than one year) follow-up periods, type of catheter ablation used, and the anatomical route of the procedure, either ostial or linear. A sensitivity analysis called the Leave-one-out test was conducted, excluding each study in each scenario to confirm that no study has a major effect on the pooled effect estimate. We imputed the Galbraith plot for further examination of the heterogeneity among studies. For further evaluation of the effect of various moderators on endpoint results, we conducted a meta-regression analysis to investigate the association between age, BMI, LVEF, LA size, or CHA2DS2-VASc score and the incidence of recurrent atrial arrhythmia employing a random-effect model. The funnel plot was constructed to assess any potential publication bias. We also applied the trim and fill method to adjust for any potential bias. All statistical analyses were executed employing STATA 18 BE and R version 4.3.2.

Results

Literature search

Our initial electronic search yielded 1284 records from four different databases. Of them, 420 records were eliminated as duplicates employing EndNote, resulting in 864 records eligible for the title and abstract screening stage. After strict application of our selection criteria, only 30 studies were retrieved and entered the full-text screening phase. In total, 12 studies were incorporated into our primary investigation. Characteristics of included studies

A total of 12 investigations were involved in our primary analysis, with a total of 3425 participants [9, 10, 16, 17, 23–30]. Three studies were RCTs [9, 10, 28], and nine studies were observational studies [16, 17, 23–27, 29, 30]. Three studies were conducted in the USA, two in Germany, two in Turkey, two in the USA and Italy, one in the UK, one in Russia, and one in Korea. The most frequent catheter ablation type used was ablation (eight studies) followed by LARIAT device ablation (Two studies) and cryoballoon (two studies). The average follow-up duration was 21 months. The mean age of our study participants was 63.69 (4.7) years, with a total of 2196 (64%) male participants. All RCTs exhibited a low risk of bias based on the ROB2 tool, as illustrated in Fig. 2. An In-depth description of the quality of observational studies is presented in Supplementary Table 3.

Risk of bias graph of the randomized controlled trials

Primary outcome

Atrial arrhythmia recurrence

The incidence of recurrent atrial arrhythmias was assessed in all included studies. The pooled RR showed that patients who underwent LAAEI significantly experienced lower atrial arrhythmia recurrence relative to the control group (RR = 0.57, 95% CI: [0.54 to 0.75], P = 0.001; I2 =76.9%, P = 0.001), as illustrated in Fig. 3.

Forest plot of the atrial arrhythmia recurrence

The Leave-one-out test was executed, and it revealed that no single study had a significant impact on the pooled analysis, as illustrated in Fig. 4.

The leave-one-out plot

We executed a subgroup analysis according to the study design, of which the pooled estimate from observational studies favored LAAEI over the control group (RR = 0.51, 95% CI: [0.37 to 0.72], P = 0.001; I2 =79.7%, P = 0.001). However, the pooled estimate from RCTs showed a non-notable variation between the two groups (RR = 0.75, 95% CI: [0.45 to 1.24], P = 0.27; I2 =42.8%, P = 0.17), as presented in Fig. 3.

Regarding the specific LAAEI anatomical technique, The ostial approach (RR = 0.53, 95% CI: [0.37 to 0.76], P = 0.0001; I2 =81.5%, P = 0.001) showed a notable benefit for LAAEI approach over the control group while the linear approach (RR = 0.78, 95% CI: [0.55 to 1.11], P = 0.12; I2 =21.3%, P = 0.29) revealed a non-significant benefit for LAAEI than the control group in reducing recurrent atrial arrhythmias, as illustrated in Fig. 5.

Forest plot of the atrial arrhythmia recurrence subgroup based on the LAAEI anatomical technique

Furthermore, according to the specific catheter ablation strategy utilized, the radiofrequency (RR = 0.62, 95% CI: [0.55 to 0.68], P = 0.001; I2 =82.6%, P = 0.001) ablation strategy showed a substantial benefit for the LAAEI group over the control group, while the cryoballoon (RR = 0.56, 95% CI: [0.04 to 7.64], P = 0.67) and LARIAT device (RR = 0.71, 95% CI; [0.07 to7.18], P = 0.77) strategies exhibited a non-notable variation between the two groups, as illustrated in Fig. 6.

Forest plot of the atrial arrhythmia recurrence subgroup based on the LAAEI ablation technique

Concerning the follow-up period, individuals who were managed by LAAEI showed significantly lower all-atrial arrhythmia recurrence instances relative to the control group within the short-term (RR = 0.49, 95% CI: [0.33 to 0.72], P = 0.001; I2 =76.9%, P = 0.001) and long-term (RR = 0.71, 95% CI: [0.55 to 0.93], P = 0.001; I2 =44%, P = 0.14) follow-up periods, as illustrated in Fig. 7.

Forest plot of the atrial arrhythmia recurrence subgroup based on the follow up period

The Galbraith plot was constructed, and upon inspection, only the Biase et al. study [17] was located outside the 95% CI of the precision area, indicating potential heterogeneity from the other studies, as illustrated in Fig. 8.

Galbraith plot of the atrial arrythmia recurrence

Finally, the funnel plot was constructed, and upon examination, there was asymmetry around the pooled effect estimate. This was statistically confirmed by Egger’s linear regression test (P = 0.03), indicating the presence of potential publication bias, or small-study effects, especially with the inclusion of several single-center studies, which were likely underpowered. We applied the trim and fill method to adjust for the publication bias and identify the missing value. We found that three studies were imputed to restore the symmetry of the plot with no significant change in the pooled effect estimate, with a new pooled RR of 0.764 (95% CI, [0.633 to 0.921], as illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 1.

In our meta-regression analysis, There was no significant association between age (Coefficient = 0.016, P = 0.602), body mass index (Coefficient = 0.0266, P = 0.633), left ventricular ejection fraction (Coefficient = -0.010, P = 0.74), left atrial size (Coefficient = 0.101, P = 0.106), CHA2DS2-VASc (Coefficient = -0.2017, P = 0.224) and the incidence of recurrent atrial arrhythmia, as presented in Supplementary Tables 4 and Supplementary Fig. 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, respectively. The lack of significant findings in our meta-regression should be interpreted with caution. The explanatory power of these analyses may have been constrained by the relatively narrow range of baseline clinical characteristics across the included studies, which limits the ability to detect a linear relationship between these covariates and the incidence of recurrent arrhythmia.

Secondary outcomes

The LAAEI group showed notably lower atrial fibrillation recurrence than the control group with a pooled RR of 0.47(95% CI: [0.27 to 0.80], P = 0.01) with associated substantial heterogeneity (I2 =63.28, P = 0.03). Nevertheless, no notable variations were observed between the two studied groups in terms of all-cause mortality (RR = 1.27, 95% CI: [0.53 to 3.04], P = 0.59; I2: 0%), stroke (RR = 1.67, 95% CI: [0.43 to 6.49], P = 0.46; I2: 43.75%), bleeding (RR = 0.71, 95% CI: [0.21 to 2.39], P = 0.58; I2: 0%), TIA (RR = 0.71, 95% CI: [0.22 to 2.28], P = 0.57; I2: 0%), LAA thrombus (RR = 2.39, 95% CI: [0.71 to 8.06], P = 0.16; I2: 12.73%), and pericardial effusion (RR = 2.09, 95% CI: [0.82 to 5.35], P = 0.12; I2: 0%), as illustrated in Table 3.

Procedural characteristics

Furthermore, LAAEI showed a significantly higher procedure time relative to the control group (Cohen’s d = 0.64, 95% CI: [0.13 to 1.15], P = 0.01; I2: 96.18%, P < 0.001). Notably, Cryoballoon and LARIAT device ablation strategies exhibited a higher procedure time with LAAEI compared to the control group. In contrast, the radiofrequency strategy did not favor any of the two procedures (Cryoballoon: Cohen’s d = 1.68, 95% CI: [1.42 to 1.94], P = 0.01; LARIAT: Cohen’s d = 0.44, 95% CI: [0.10 to 0.78], P = 0.01; RFA: Cohen’s d: 0.30, 95% CI [-0.16 to 0.75], P = 0.20), as illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 7.

Our pooled analysis of five studies did not favor either of the two procedures regarding fluoroscopy time (Cohen’s d: 0.30, 95% CI [-0.17 to 0.77], P = 0.22; I2: 94.94%, P < 0.001). Cryoballoon ablation showed a significantly higher fluoroscopy time with the LAAEI group relative to the control group (Cohen’s d: 0.94, 95% CI [0.75 to 1.13], P = 0.01). In contrast, the fluoroscopy time was notably lower in the LAAEI group than the control group when utilizing the LARIAT device (Cohen’s d: -0.56, 95% CI [-0.90 to -0.22], P = 0.01) While the RFA did not favor any of the two procedures (Cohen’s d: 0.18, 95% CI [-0.29 to 0.65], P = 0.45), as illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 8.

Discussion

In this meta-analysis, 12 studies were included, encompassing a total of 3,425 patients with PAF, to compare the efficacy and safety of incorporating LAAEI with standard ablation versus standard ablation alone. This study found that LAAEI notably decreased the incidence of recurrent atrial arrhythmia compared to the PVI alone group regarding both short- and long-term follow-up. Regarding subgroup analyses of atrial arrhythmia recurrence according to study design, RCTs showed a non-substantial difference between the LAAEI and control groups, while observational studies favored LAAEI over the control, which might be influenced by residual confounding and selection bias.

Specifically, utilizing the ostial LAAEI anatomical technique, there was a significant benefit for LAAEI over the control group in reducing the incidence of recurrent atrial arrhythmias, while the linear approach did not favor either of the two groups. Regarding LAAEI ablation techniques, radiofrequency and Cryoballoon ablation strategies show significant advantages for LAAEI over the control group. In contrast, the LARIAT technique revealed a non-significant difference between the two approaches.

In comparison to the control group, the LAAEI group demonstrated a significantly lower rate of AF recurrence. Regarding procedural characteristics, LAAEI revealed a notably higher procedure time relative to the control group. How, the pooled effect estimated did not favor either of the groups concerning fluoroscopy time. On the other hand, no significant differences were noted regarding the incidence of bleeding, TIA, death, pericardial effusion, LAA thrombus, and stroke.

Isolation of LAA has changed our understanding of AF management, recognizing the LAA as a prominent trigger for AF. The literature showed that isolating the LAA can dramatically affect AF recurrence rates, but it raises concerns about potential effects on LAA function and increased thromboembolic risks, necessitating continuing anticoagulation. Various isolation approaches, such as radiofrequency and cry balloon ablation, are being investigated, with the current study expected to offer additional information about optimizing these treatments and improving patient outcomes.

This study suggests that LAAEI could be a viable option for the management of AF, depending on the significant association between LAAEI and a reduced risk of atrial arrhythmia recurrence for short and long-term follow-up periods. This finding is supported by several studies that highlight the role of the LAA as a potential trigger site for AF. For instance, one study found that in 27% of patients with recurrent AF after a previous ablation procedure, the LAA was involved [26], and in 8.7% of cases, the LAA was the only source of arrhythmia [17]. However, the fact that the results were primarily driven by the observational studies, and the non-significance difference between the two groups in RCTs highlights the need for larger, well-designed RCTs to confirm the effectiveness of LAAEI in preventing AF recurrence.

Ostial LAAEI techniques were more efficient in reducing atrial arrhythmia recurrence than linear LAAEI techniques. LAA is well-known to have complex anatomy, which includes its thin walls and multilobed shape, therefore it can be difficult to achieve persistent linear LAAEI, and intraprocedural reconnection is common, particularly in the anterior and superior areas [9, 26]. AF maintenance may also be influenced by the function of Bachmann’s bundle, a significant electrical channel close to the LAA [27]. Linear LAAEI may not always break this connection, which could allow AF triggers to continue. Furthermore, different results are caused by variations in linear ablation procedures, meaning that more research is required to identify the best practices for long-lasting LAA isolation. RFA and cryoballoon ablation are the preferred techniques. for LAAEI, based on this study’s results. RFA is well-known for its dependence on totally isolating the LAA electrically, which directly addresses the LAA’s function as an AF trigger [16]. In the same context, Cryoballoon ablation achieves circumferential electrical isolation by freezing tissue, creating a lesion that blocks erratic electrical signals. This approach targets both the pulmonary veins and LAA, which are known to be critical in maintaining AF. By isolating these structures, cryoballoon ablation prevents electrical impulses from re-entering the heart, significantly reducing the likelihood of atrial arrhythmia recurrence [29, 30]. However, with only two studies that used cryoballoon ablation in our study, it lacked statistical significance.

Although the LARIAT procedure does not directly handle these points. LARIAT technique is largely focused on mechanical closure, compared to RF, which prioritizes addressing the electrical activity of the LAA, which is in line with the primary goal of preventing AF recurrence [16, 29].

Despite its effectiveness, the LAAEI approach can lead to electromechanical dissociation of the left atrial appendage, which has been presumed to increase the risk of LAA thrombus formation and subsequent thromboembolism [31]. However, our pooled analysis did not reveal any significant difference between the LAAEI and control groups regarding LAA thrombus, stroke, or TIA. These findings were aligned with several studies in the literature [9, 10, 28]. However, it’s worth mentioning that the events of TIA, stroke, and LAA thrombus were self-reported and observational in nature, which might imply possible limited power. In contrast, Heeger and his colleagues found that the LAAEI group was associated with higher rates of LAA thrombus and thromboembolic events encompassing stroke and TIA compared to the control group despite the prescription of oral anticoagulants [24]. Their findings could be attributed to their utilization of wide-area LAAEI compared to circular LAAEI used in other studies, which can impair blood flow not only in the LAA but also in surrounding regions. The disruption of flow dynamics and the creation of more significant regions where blood can stagnate further exacerbate the risk of clot formation compared to more localized, targeted ablation strategies [9].

Most of the published studies in the literature support the efficacy of LAAI in reducing AF recurrence, particularly when combined with other procedures such as pulmonary vein isolation. Di Biase et al. discovered a 74% AF recurrence rate in patients when LAA firing was not ablated, compared to just 15% when LAAI was done, establishing the LAA as a significant trigger for AF in many repeat surgeries [17]. Di Biase et al. supported this by demonstrating that LAA electrical isolation enhanced long-term freedom from arrhythmias in individuals with chronic AF [9]. Similarly, Yorgun et al. found that using cryo-balloon technology for LAAI in conjunction with pulmonary vein isolation dramatically reduced AF recurrence over a year, without raising thromboembolic risks [29]. Panikker et al. found that combining LAAI with LAA occlusion during ablation resulted in higher freedom from AF at 12 months compared to ablation alone, implying that this technique could manage both AF and thromboembolic risk [26]. However, this positive effect is not universally observed. For example, Romanov et al. discovered no significant reduction in AF recurrence when LAA excision was coupled to surgical ablation, implying that the comprehensive nature of surgical lesions may already target important regions involved in AF [28]. These data suggest that, while LAAI is generally effective, its success can be impacted by variables such as the type of AF, the specific ablation technique used, and the overall breadth of the surgery. The aMAZE trial showed that LAAI did not meet the prespecified efficacy criteria of freedom from atrial arrhythmia at 12 months [10]. The lack of significant improvement in rhythm control, despite successful mechanical closure, likely stems from a disconnect between physical ligation and electrical isolation. Ligation at the appendage neck often leaves the ostium (base) intact, potentially failing to eliminate arrhythmogenic triggers that persist even after pulmonary vein isolation. Additionally, the study’s focus on early persistent AF rather than long-standing cases where the appendage is a more dominant driver may have limited the observed clinical benefit. Consequently, mechanical exclusion alone appears insufficient to suppress arrhythmias without addressing the specific electrical substrate at the appendage base.

Friedman et al. conducted a meta-analysis of seven studies, including 1,037 patients, to figure out the relationship between LAAI and recurrent atrial tachycardia (AT) or AF following an AF ablation operation [32]. Our study showed that patients in the LAAI group had a significantly higher chance of remaining free from atrial arrhythmia recurrence, with a reported OR of 1.37, indicating that those in the LAAI group had higher odds of remaining free from atrial arrhythmia. Friedman et al. found that patients who underwent LAAI were 62.5% less likely to have recurrent AT/AF, with a particularly high reduction in cases where the LAAI was administered percutaneously, with a reported OR of 0.375 [32]. Friedman et al. do not directly evaluate the efficacy of different LAAI strategies (ostial vs. linear) but acknowledge difficulties due to the varied approaches to LAAI, including variances in ablation procedures [32]. Friedman et al. revealed no significant relation between LAAI and thromboembolism when paired with LAA blockage or excision. However, in patients lacking LAA blockage or excision, the study remained equivocal due to large confidence intervals, suggesting the need for additional research [32].

Romero et al. did a meta-analysis on LAAI in AF patients with 930 patients. The study found that 75.5% of patients in the LAAI group experienced no arrhythmia, while 43.9% in the control group did [33]. According to Romero et al. RFA was the most employed ablation method for LAAEI in the research they reviewed (71%). However, it does not distinguish between studies utilizing RFA for LAAEI and those using other ablation procedures (cryoablation, LARIAT) [33]. This indicates that the reported 75.5% independence from atrial arrhythmia recurrence at 12 months follow-up in the LAAEI group cannot be attributable only to RFA [33]. Romero et al. conducted a meta-analysis, including 2336 over nine studies, limited to patients with non-paroxysmal AF (persistent AF, long-standing persistent AF, or both) [34]. Its primary outcome is freedom from all-atrial arrhythmia recurrence during the study period. It calculates this as a 69.3% success rate in the LAAEI group against 46.4% in the control group, standard ablation alone. This translates to a 46% relative risk reduction and a 22.9% absolute risk reduction, favoring LAAEI. It also reports on secondary outcomes like stroke and acute procedural complications and found no statistically significant difference between the LAAEI and control groups for these events, which was aligned with our findings. Also, it used the Mantel-Haenszel risk ratio random effects model, together with the intention-to-treat principle. They conduct sensitivity analyses using the study design, LAAEI strategy, and LAAEI technique.

Strength points and limitations

This is the most updated meta-analysis, including the largest sample size, addressing this topic and testing the efficacy of different techniques and approaches that are not discussed in previously published studies. Additionally, studies included in our review were performed in various geographical regions within different populations, which enhances the generalizability and reliability of our findings. The studies note a few drawbacks, such as differences in patient demographics, study designs, inconsistent endpoint monitoring, and differing follow-up durations, which make direct comparisons more difficult and emphasize the necessity of standardized research methodologies; this is reflected in the analysis in the form of increased heterogeneity. It is more difficult to thoroughly assess the long-term safety and effectiveness of LAAEI because of certain research’s relatively short follow-up periods. Furthermore, due to the technical requirements of LAAEI, operator skill and technique have a major impact on results, which affects the procedure’s effectiveness as well as the rates of complications. In addition, meta-regression analyses demonstrated limited ability to explain between-study heterogeneity. The variables accounted for only a small proportion of the observed heterogeneity. This limited explanatory power is likely due to the relatively narrow range and low between-study variability of baseline characteristics across the included studies, as well as the modest number of studies available for meta-regression. Consequently, the results of these analyses should be interpreted cautiously. Furthermore, although no statistically significant differences were observed between groups for thromboembolic outcomes, the pooled relative risk for LAA thrombus exceeded 2.0 with wide CI, suggesting limited statistical power due to the low number of events rather than definitive equivalence between groups. Therefore, conclusions regarding the long-term thromboembolic safety of LAAEI should be interpreted cautiously. Finally, the majority of the included studies were observational and single-center in design, which may introduce selection bias and limit causal inference. These studies were also relatively small and likely underpowered, raising the possibility of small-study effects. This was supported by the presence of funnel plot asymmetry and a statistically significant Egger’s regression test, suggesting potential publication bias and we emphasize that the findings should be interpreted with appropriate caution.

Advances in clinical practice

With the significant reduction in atrial arrhythmia recurrence associated with the LAAEI plus PVI, this technique can be added to the standard protocol, especially for persistent atrial fibrillation and long-standing persistent atrial fibrillation. However, we still have to investigate the thromboembolism risk and validate that there’s no significant risk of it. In addition, with the different techniques used (Ostial vs. linear LAAEI), there can be a tailored choice of approach depending on the patient’s anatomy and characteristics, as both techniques showed significant results. While our study found no significant increase in thromboembolic events, there is still a concern about developing LAA thrombus, so clinicians may need to develop protocols for anticoagulation management and regular monitoring of LAA function after the procedure to reduce risks. Finally, as the role of LAA in AF evolves, educational programs for cardiologists and electrophysiologists can be developed to ensure the best practices and update treatment protocols according to the latest evidence.

Recommendations for future research and clinical practice

Large-scale, randomized controlled trials with prolonged follow-up periods are necessary for future researchers to have a better understanding of the long-term risks and benefits of LAAEI. Standardized protocols are also required for the selection of patients, procedural methods, and post-procedural care, which includes anticoagulation strategies. To improve LAAEI results, there is also an emphasis on investigating emerging technologies, including imaging and innovative ablation energy sources. Future large-scale RCTs should consider the potential impact of sex differences, as they have been shown to influence outcomes in other patient populations [35]. Recently, Pulsed field ablation (PFA) has emerged as an innovative non-thermal technique for left atrial appendage (LAA) isolation, and has shown promising potential in paroxysmal atrial fibrillation [36], offering a potentially safer alternative to traditional thermal methods. While the procedure demonstrates high immediate success, achieving acute LAA isolation in 100% of cases with very low rates of dormant conduction (1.7%) following adenosine challenge, long-term durability remains a significant challenge. Follow-up remapping at three months revealed that LAA isolation was maintained in only 4.6% of patients. These findings suggest that while PFA is acutely effective for LAA exclusion, the lesions produced may not yet provide the permanent transmurality required for long-term electrical isolation in extra-pulmonary vein substrates [37].

Conclusion

Pooled results from studies evaluating LAAEI suggest a potential reduction in recurrent atrial arrhythmia during both short- and long-term follow-up when compared with pulmonary vein isolation alone. However, these findings should be interpreted with caution, as the observed benefit appears to be largely driven by observational studies, while analyses restricted to RCTs showed a smaller and non-significant effect.

In addition, uncertainties remain regarding long-term safety, particularly the risk of thromboembolism, with variable effects on stroke risk, especially among patients not receiving oral anticoagulation. Therefore, careful patient selection, individualized anticoagulation strategies, and meticulous procedural techniques remain essential. Future large-scale randomized controlled trials with standardized protocols and longer follow-up are needed to reduce heterogeneity and better define the clinical role of LAAEI.
