Epidemiology, clinical characteristics, and outcomes for heart failure with improved ejection fraction by chronological age
Authors: Kishan Srikanth, Keane Lee, Alan S Go, Rishi V Parikh, Andrew P Ambrosy, Thida C Tan, Kyung Min, Steven A Hamilton, Jana Svetlichnaya, Scott D Solomon, Muthiah Vaduganathan, Riccardo M Inciardi, Orly Vardeny, Elena Vasti, Alexander T Sandhu, Ivy A Ku, Sirtaz Adatya, Ankeet S Bhatt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and AimsHF incidence increases with age. Novel treatments have led to more patients recovering LVEF, now classified as HF with improved ejection fraction (HFimpEF). However, the age-related epidemiology and prognosis of HFimpEF remain unclear.Methods and ResultsWe identified adults ≥18 years with incident HFrEF (LVEF ≤40%) between 2013 and 2022 within Kaiser Permanente Northern California (KPNC), a healthcare delivery system serving 4.5 million members. HFimpEF was defined as baseline LVEF ≤40% with improvement to ≥40%. HFimpEF incidence was assessed by baseline age categories (<50, 50–64, 65–79, ≥80 years [yrs]). Rates of worsening HF (WHF) events (i.e. HF-related hospitalization, ED, or outpatient visits) were compared between HFimpEF and persistent HFrEF patients across age using Cox proportional hazards models adjusted for sex, race, and EF at the time of incident HFrEF, including evaluation of a potential interaction by age.DataAmong 28 292 adults with incident HFrEF, 35% experienced HFimpEF, with lower rates at older ages: 43% (<50 yrs), 42% (50–64 yrs), 39% (65–79 yrs), and 21% (≥80 yrs) (P < .001 for trend). WHF incidence rates (per 100 person-years) increased with age: 10.6 [95% CI, 9.5–11.7] (<50 yrs), 14.5 [13.7–15.3] (50–64 yrs), 20.8 [20.0–21.6] (65–79 yrs), and 31.7 [29.9–33.5] (≥80 yrs). Patients with HFimpEF, compared to those with persistent HFrEF, had lower adjusted rates of WHF, a finding more prominent at lower ages (pinteraction by age <0.001).ConclusionsHFimpEF is common within a year of incident HFrEF, more prevalent in younger patients, and is associated with continued residual risk. Compared to persistent HFrEF, improvements in LVEF are associated with lower risk of WHF or death across the age spectrum. Additional research is needed to understand HF symptom burden and medical therapy use in this population to inform shared decision-making.

Introduction

Heart failure (HF) incidence increases with age, making it a growing concern in an ageing population, with significant implications for treatment and prognosis; risk scores for incident HF often include age as a strong predictor. With advancements in modern treatments, a growing number of patients are recovering their left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), newly classified in the 2022 ACC/AHA guidelines, as heart failure with improved ejection fraction (HFimpEF). HFimpEF is a new classification that includes patients who initially present with heart failure and a reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF; LVEF <40%) but later show a significant improvement in LVEF, defined as an increase to >40%.1 Despite increasing recognition, our understanding of HFimpEF remains limited, particularly regarding the factors that predict which patients are most likely to experience such improvements.

Age is an important factor in HFimpEF, as younger patients may have a higher likelihood of reversible aetiologies of heart failure. Age is a well-established risk factor for many clinical conditions, but it has not been well-studied in patients who experience significant improvements in LVEF. In this analysis, using diverse, longitudinal datasets from the Kaiser Permanente Northern California (KPNC) integrated healthcare system, we examine the epidemiology, clinical outcomes, and medical therapy of HFimpEF according to age.

Methods

Setting and source population

KPNC is a large integrated healthcare delivery system with 21 hospitals and >260 freestanding clinics, serving >4.6 million members with comprehensive care across both outpatient and inpatient settings. KPNC membership is highly representative of the regional population with respect to age, gender, race/ethnicity, and socioeconomic status, ensuring the generalizability of our study.2–4 This study was approved by the KPNC institutional review board (IRB), and a waiver of informed consent was granted, as it is a retrospective, data-only study.

Study overview and cohort assembly

The sample study included all adults ≥18 years with incident HFrEF between 1 January 2013 and 31 December 2022 within Kaiser Permanente Northern California (KPNC). Incident HFrEF was defined as a recorded LVEF measurement of ≤40% without any prior LVEF measurements ≤40%. To ensure the population had a reduced ejection fraction due to HF, we excluded patients without a qualifying HF encounter at any time before or within 90 days after the LVEF measurement. Qualifying HF encounters were defined as either a hospitalization with a primary discharge diagnosis code for HF, a hospitalization with a secondary discharge diagnosis code for HF, and meeting diagnostic criteria for WHF (described below), or the first of ≥3 ambulatory visits with a diagnosis code for HF in the absence of hospitalization. Patients with <12 months of prior continuous KPNC membership and pharmacy benefit were excluded to ensure complete capture of baseline covariates. We also excluded patients with <1 day of follow-up due to in-hospital death or loss of KP membership, and those with prior end-stage kidney disease (ESKD), defined as receipt of chronic dialysis or kidney transplant from a comprehensive health plan ESKD treatment registry.5 As qualifying LVEF measurements could be ascertained within either ambulatory, emergency department, or inpatient encounters, the HFrEF index date was set as the discharge date from the index encounter (i.e. the same date as LVEF measurement for ambulatory and ED visits, and potentially after the LVEF measurement for hospitalizations).

Patients with HFimpEF were defined as those with a subsequent LVEF measurement >40% after the incident HFrEF measurement. Patients with persistent HFrEF were identified as those with no LVEF measurements >40% within 1 year after incident HFrEF. As a sensitivity analysis, we used a stricter definition of HFimpEF requiring a follow-up LVEF >40% with ≥10% absolute improvement from the baseline LVEF. To compare outcomes between those with HFimpEF and persistent HFrEF, we conducted a landmark analysis beginning one year after the incident HFrEF diagnosis. For this analysis, we excluded patients who died or disenrolled from the KP health plan prior to one year. Patients who were alive at 1 year after incident HFrEF without receiving a subsequent LVEF measurement were categorized as having persistent HFrEF.

Definition of worsening heart failure events

Patients were followed until death, loss of KP membership, or 31 December 2023. WHF events (inpatient, emergency, outpatient) were identified using a combination of symptoms, signs, objective criteria, and changes in HF therapy ascertained from structured and unstructured EHR data using rule-based NLP algorithms. This has been previously described and has been validated in detail.6 These diagnostic criteria are based on a standardized definition of inpatient and outpatient WHF previously developed and validated by a consensus panel of trialists with expertise in clinical endpoint classification in collaboration with the US Food and Drug Administration.7,8 We have previously shown that NLP algorithms based on this definition were highly accurate for identifying worsening HF compared to diagnosis codes alone. We observed a sensitivity of 98% and specificity of 95% for hospitalizations, sensitivity of 96% and specificity of 95% for emergency department visits and observation stays, and a sensitivity of 80% and specificity of 95% for outpatient visits compared to manual physician adjudication.6

Data sources and covariates

Data for this study were primarily derived from the KPNC Epic-based EHR system, which provided hospitalization data, patient progress notes, and cardiac imaging reports, including echocardiograms and chest X-rays. Additional data were extracted from the KPNC Virtual Data Warehouse (VDW), which includes validated information on comorbidities, outpatient medications, and laboratory results.9,10 Demographic details such as age, self-reported gender, and race/ethnicity were also obtained from the EHR. Comorbid conditions were identified within the five years prior to hospitalization. Laboratory results, excluding B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP) levels, were collected from the most recent outpatient tests conducted between 7 and 365 days before hospital admission. Baseline medication use was determined from outpatient prescription records within 120 days preceding the hospitalization. LVEF was assessed using structured echocardiogram data, as well as alternative methods like radionuclide scintigraphy, nuclear imaging, or left ventriculography, where applicable. In cases where structured data were unavailable, LVEF values were extracted from echocardiogram reports using natural language processing (NLP) algorithms. Inpatient medication use, BNP levels, vital signs, and procedures were also extracted from the EHR. To investigate how age influences HFimpEF, we stratified patients into four age categories at the time of their incident diagnosis: <50 years, 50–64 years, 65–79 years, and ≥80 years.

Among those with HFimpEF, we describe adherence to GDMT using dispensed outpatient medications at the time of HFimpEF (i.e. time of qualifying improved LVEF measurement), 6 months after HFimpEF, and 12 months after HFimpEF. GDMT medications included angiotensin converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEi), angiotensin II receptor blockers (ARB), angiotensin-neprilysin inhibitors (ARNi), evidence-based beta blockers (carvedilol, bisoprolol, or metoprolol succinate), mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (MRA), and sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors (SGLT2i).

Statistical analysis

We first describe characteristics of the cohort at the time of incident HFrEF, stratified by age categories. Among patients with HFimpEF, we describe adherence to GDMT medications at the time of HFimpEF and at 6 and 12 months after HFimpEF by age.

In the landmark analysis cohort starting one year after initial HFrEF, we calculated crude incidence rates (per 100 person-years) of WHF events and death through the end of follow-up, by HFimpEF (vs persistent HFrEF) and age categories. We also plot Aalen-Johansen cumulative incidence estimates of composite WHF events and death across follow-up, stratified by HFimpEF and age. Finally, we evaluated whether the association between HFimpEF versus persistent HFrEF and clinical outcomes varied by age. We used a Cox proportional hazards model for the composite of WHF events and death with an interaction term between HFimpEF and age categories and tested the interaction using the likelihood ratio test. We included sex, race (Asian, non-Hispanic Black, Hispanic, non-Hispanic White, Other), and baseline LVEF (i.e. at incident HFrEF) as covariates. To more flexibly model the relationship between age and the hazard ratio for HFimpEF, we conducted the same model replacing age categories with a restricted cubic spline for age. All analyses were conducted using R version 4.3.2 (R Core Team, 2023).

Results

Population

We examined 28 292 patients with incident HFrEF diagnosed between 2013 and 2022. The mean age of the cohort was 70 (sd:14.6) years, and the population was predominantly male (65%). Of these patients, 60% of patients were White, 14% were Hispanic, 13% were Asian, and 11% were Black. The mean index LVEF at the time of diagnosis was 31%.

In this cohort, there was a substantial prevalence of cardiometabolic comorbidities, including diabetes (38%), with the highest rates observed in the 65–79 age group (42%) and the lowest in those <50 years (28%). Hypertension and hyperlipidaemia were present in >70% of patients, with older patients having higher rates than younger patients. Characteristics of study population at time of incident HFrEF

Of the 28 292 patients diagnosed with incident HFrEF, 9962 (35%) developed HFimpEF in the year following diagnosis. The incidence of HFimpEF was highest in patients under 50 years of age (41%) and age 50–64-years (42%), followed by 65–79-years (39%), and ≥80-years (21%) (trend P < .001). Follow-up status of all incident HFrEF patients is also presented in Table 1.

Among the 21 907 patients who were alive, maintained active KP membership, and remained in the study period at one year after incident HFrEF (i.e. landmark cohort), 8603 (39.3%) had experienced HFimpEF.

Guideline directed medical therapy use

Among patients with HFimpEF, there was a high utilization of evidence-based beta-blockers (BB) and ACE inhibitors/angiotensin receptor blockers (ACEi/ARB) at the time of HFimpEF diagnosis. Beta-blocker use at incident HFimpEF ranged from 88% in patients under <50 years to 74% in those aged ≥80 years. Similarly, ACE/ARB use ranged from 80% in the <50 group to 73% in the ≥80 group. Use of angiotensin receptor-neprilysin inhibitors (ARNi) and sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 (SGLT2) inhibitors was comparatively lower across all age groups. ARNi use at incident HFimpEF ranged from 13% in patients <50 years to 6.5% in those aged ≥80 years, with minimal change over time. SGLT2 inhibitor use ranged from 11% in the youngest group to 6.2% in the oldest group. Medication use was lower in older patients compared to younger patients across all GDMT categories (Figure 1). After diagnosis of HFimpEF, GDMT use declined at 6- and 12-months follow-up, consistent across age strata (Figure 1). These patterns were similar in the subset of patients with ≥10% absolute improvement in EF (Supplementary Table S1). GDMT trends among patients diagnosed after 2018 (N = 16 102) reflecting contemporary practice with higher rates of ARNi and SGLT2 inhibitor use, are presented in Supplementary Figure S1.

Clinical outcomes

In both persistent HFrEF and HFimpEF groups, the cumulative incidence of worsening heart failure (WHF) or death increased with advancing age (Figure 2). Among patients with persistent HFrEF, rates of WHF were: 29.9 events per 100 person-years [95% CI, 28.2–31.7] in patients <50 years, 29.4 [95% CI, 28.39–30.43] in those aged 50–64 years, 33.9 [95% CI, 33–34.8] in the 65–79 age group, and 44.9 [95% CI, 43.5–46.4] in patients ≥80 years. Among patients who experienced HFimpEF, WHF and death rates were lower compared to those with persistent HFrEF but also increased with age. In adjusted analyses, compared to patients with persistent HFrEF, HFimpEF was associated with a significantly lower risk of WHF or death, with age-stratified hazard ratios (HR) of 0.49 [95% CI, 0.41–0.59] for patients <50 years, 0.54 [95% CI, 0.49–0.59] for ages 50–64, 0.63 [95% CI, 0.59–0.67] for ages 65–79, and 0.64 [95% CI, 0.58–0.70] for >80 years (interaction P = 0.004). HFimpEF was also associated with a lower risk of clinical adverse events or death across the age spectrum when modelled continuously (P for interaction by age splines <0.001; Figure 3). Discussion

In a contemporary cohort of nearly 30 000 patients diagnosed with incident HFrEF, we found a substantial portion developed HFimpEF within 12 months of diagnosis: while older patients were less likely to develop HFimpEF, nearly 1 in 5 patients aged ≥80 still had improvement in EF over time. Risk of adverse outcomes increased with advancing age; however, development of HFimpEF was associated with reduced clinical outcomes as compared to persistent HFrEF across the age spectrum. Guideline directed medical therapy declined in the 12 months following HFimpEF consistently across age strata. To the best of our knowledge, this is the largest, most diverse, and contemporary assessment of the age-related epidemiology and outcomes of HFimpEF in the USA.

Overall, younger patients demonstrated a higher likelihood of LVEF recovery compared to older cohorts. This aligns with prior literature suggesting that younger individuals with HFrEF are more likely to have reversible underlying aetiologies, such as myocarditis, tachycardia-induced cardiomyopathy, or substance-related cardiomyopathy, which may respond more favourably to reverse remodelling.11 The higher rates of HFimpEF observed in younger patients may also be partly due to their greater tolerance for and adherence to GDMT, which plays a key role in improving LVEF.12,13 In contrast, older patients tend to have a higher prevalence of fixed aetiologies such as ischaemic heart disease or chronic ventricular dysfunction and lower absolute rates of GDMT.14 These concerns—such as frailty, polypharmacy, and competing comorbidities—may lead to hesitation in initiating or intensifying GDMT in older patients, which may in turn limit the potential for LVEF recovery. Despite these limitations, it is important to note that nearly 1 in 5 patients aged 80 or above experienced HFimpEF, suggesting that EF is a modifiable entity with early GDMT even among the elderly. Baseline predictors of HFimpEF in this cohort—including younger age, alcohol use, atrial fibrillation, and absence of ischaemic risk factors—have been characterized in a companion analysis from our group.15

Importantly, older patients remain a particularly vulnerable population for adverse events, even when LVEF improves. Our study highlights that older patients continue to experience higher rates of worsening heart failure events and mortality, even after incident HFimpEF, suggesting that LVEF recovery does not equate to complete myocardial recovery. Age-related factors likely play an important role in shaping long-term outcomes. However, GDMT use fell precipitously after HFimpEF diagnosis among this age group, suggesting that there may be clinician aversion to aggressive and continued treatment of HF in this vulnerable patient population with a large residual risk burden, representing potentially yet another treatment-risk paradox.16 In fact, landmark clinical trials have found no attenuation of benefit for HFrEF GDMT across the age spectrum and potential incremental utility in treatment intensification even after HFimpEF.17–19 Further research is needed to determine whether older HFimpEF patients are at risk of relapse due to premature GDMT de-escalation.

Current ACC/AHA guidelines acknowledge that although GDMT can lead to improvements in symptoms, functional capacity, LVEF, and reverse remodelling, the majority of patients do not achieve full and sustained recovery, with structural abnormalities often persisting.20 The TRED-HF trial demonstrated that nearly 40% of patients with dilated cardiomyopathy who had recovered LVEF experienced recurrence of heart failure within 6 months of GDMT withdrawal.21 This relapse led to significant declines in LVEF, increased left ventricular volumes, and worsening symptoms, reinforcing the importance of ongoing therapy. Further evidence is needed to understand if the risk of relapse is influenced by underlying age at the time of incident HFrEF and HFimpEF to enhance shared decision making around pharmacotherapy and device-based therapy intensification. Additionally, in older populations, clinicians should also consider initial de-implementation of non-evidence-based or potentially harmful therapies (e.g. anticholinergics or sedatives) when clinically appropriate to create therapeutic space for potentially life-prolonging HF-directed therapies.

Although HFimpEF was linked to a lower risk of adverse outcomes compared to persistent HFrEF across all age groups, our data demonstrated that this association is attenuated in older adults. This shows the need to better understand the cardiovascular and non-cardiovascular contributions to morbidity and mortality in these elderly populations, to tailor medical therapy regimens that prioritize addressing modifiable risk factors and balancing patient and family preferences, goals of care, feelings regarding polypharmacy, and symptom burden.

This study should be considered in the context of several limitations. First, we defined HFimpEF based on the 2022 ACC/AHA/HFSA guideline criteria, identifying patients with an initial LVEF <40% and subsequent LVEF ≥40%; alternate definitions of HFimpEF may yield different estimates.22 Second, we restricted our ascertainment of HFimpEF to the first 12 months following the initial HFrEF diagnosis. This time window was chosen pragmatically, but shorter or longer follow-up periods could impact the proportion of patients identified as HFimpEF. The precise timing of LVEF recovery is difficult to establish, as follow-up imaging was obtained at varying intervals in routine clinical care. Third, the high use of beta-blockers and ACEi/ARB at the time of HFrEF diagnosis may not be solely due to optimization of guideline-directed medical therapy but rather may have already been used at baseline for other comorbidities such as hypertension, atrial fibrillation, or chronic kidney disease. Finally, the relatively low uptake of ARNI and SGLT2i during the study period likely reflects the evolving nature of heart failure treatment, as parts of the cohort were enrolled before these therapies were widely adopted into clinical practice. Additionally, NT-proBNP and NYHA functional class are not routinely documented in structured form within our EHR, precluding their inclusion in the analysis. Available BNP measurements, key echocardiographic parameters, and signs and symptoms of heart failure ascertained via validated NLP algorithms are presented in Supplementary Table S3.

Conclusion

In this large, real-world cohort of patients with incident HFrEF, we observed that HFimpEF occurred in over a third of the population and was common even among elderly patients. The development of HFimpEF compared to persistent HFrEF was associated with improved clinical outcomes across the full spectrum of age. Our findings highlight the need for more precision with regards to shared decision making and implementation/de-implementation practices among elderly patients with HFimpEF.
