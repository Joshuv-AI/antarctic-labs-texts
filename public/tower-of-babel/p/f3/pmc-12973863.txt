Transcranial magnetic stimulation mitigates perioperative neurocognitive disorders by regulating the function of the glymphatic system
Authors: Xuanran Feng, Jiehui Liu, Zhanping Liang, Xue Du, Lijuan Zhao, Ning Xie, Changqi Chen, Lu Ding, Xiaohuan Xia, Jialin C. Zheng, Jianhui Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The incidence of perioperative neurocognitive disorders (PND) increase with age, especially within those countries facing great challenge of aging population. However, the mechanism of PND remains elusive, and the lack of precautions has resulted in extended recovery among the elderly. Transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) has shown promising therapeutic potential in many neurological disorders such as depression and Alzheimer’s disease. This study aimed to explore the therapeutic potential of TMS on PND mouse model and aged patients. PND mice model were established through exploratory laparotomy on aged mice. We performed Y maze test and novel object recognition test to evaluate the cognitive function after TMS treatment. Intracisternal injection and immunofluorescence staining were conducted to assess the glymphatic system function. We used ELISA, immunofluorescence staining, Western-blotting and TUNEL assay to detect neuroinflammation and neuronal loss. We examined the therapeutic effect of TMS on patients in a clinical trial. PND mice showed improved cognitive functions after TMS treatment. TMS abrogated glymphatic system dysfunction and aquaporin-4 (AQP4) translocation, the key pathological changes that lead to cognitive decline, in PND mice. Moreover, TMS alleviated neuroinflammation and apoptosis in PND mice. We further found that inhibition of glymphatic system function blocked the ameliorative effects of TMS on postoperative cognitive function, neuroinflammation, and neuronal damage. Patients who received TMS showed improved memory function 7 days after surgery and obtained better Abbreviated Mental Test Score 30 days post-operation. Our study indicates the great therapeutic potentials of TMS in anesthesia- and surgery-induced cognitive impairment and pathological changes. Introduction

Perioperative neurocognitive disorders (PND) defined as all perioperative cognitive changes that occur in the preoperative and postoperative period, including postoperative delirium (POD) and postoperative cognitive dysfunction (POCD) [1]. The pathogenesis of PND is complex, which includes neuroinflammation and neuronal damage. However, the underlying etiology of PND is not well understood, resulting in ineffective treatments for PND [2]. Recent studies have implied the dysfunction of glymphatic system as one potential contributor to PND [3]. The glymphatic system is a fluid clearance pathway in the central nervous system that non-selectively clears the interstitium of metabolic waste [4]. Aquaporin-4 (AQP4), a water channel located in the endfeet of astrocytes, is considered a central driver of the glymphatic system [5]. The disruption or loss of perivascular polarity distribution of AQP4, induced by deregulation of MMP-9-mediated β-dystroglycan (β-DG) cleavage, results in defective glymphatic drainage, which triggers and exacerbates neuroinflammation and neuronal loss, leading to proteinopathies and cognitive dysfunction [5–10]. Enhancement of AQP4 polarization and glymphatic functions have emerged as novel therapeutic strategies for neurological diseases [6].

Recently, a non-invasive brain stimulation technique, transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) has been proposed as a new approach to improve cognitive performances in patients with Alzheimer’s disease (AD), Parkinson's disease, and vascular dementia [11]. Inspiringly, TMS has been reported to correct glymphatic system dysfunction, therefore promoting synaptic plasticity of neurons and inhibiting apoptosis and neuroinflammation in neuropathological conditions including AD and stork [12–15]. However, the therapeutic effects and the underlying mechanisms of TMS on PND remain largely unknown. Since recent clinical studies have reported great therapeutic potentials of intermittent theta-burst stimulation (iTBS) on cognitive function in patients with AD, we therefore followed the iTBS protocol to investigate the efficacy of TMS on PND in both clinical trials and animal studies [16, 17]. In this study, we found that postoperative TMS treatment significantly improved cognitive performances in elderly patients and PND animal models after anesthesia and surgery. Moreover, TMS treatment suppressed glymphatic dysfunctions, AQP4 depolarization, neuroinflammation, and neuronal loss, and the therapeutic effects of TMS were abrogated by inhibiting the functions of glymphatic system. Hence, our results suggested TMS as a promising therapeutic strategy for PND presumably through enhancing glymphatic system functions.

Methods

Animals and treatment

Eighteen-month-old male C57BL/6 J mice were used in this study to avoid variability caused by estrus cycle. Mice were housed in a controlled environment (25 ± 1 °C, 40 ~ 70% humidity, and 12 h ligh/dark cycle) with free access to food and water. The cages were checked daily to ensure animal welfare. All animal procedures were conducted in compliance with protocols approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at Tongji University (Approval No. TJAA06723108). This work has been reported in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines [18].

Laparotomy

Mice were randomly divided into anesthesia/surgery (A/S) group or control (Ctrl) group. Mice of the A/S group underwent open abdominal surgery induced by 2% isoflurane anesthesia and maintained by 1.4% isoflurane anesthesia. A longitudinal midline incision was made at the skin, abdominal muscle and peritoneum from the xiphoid process to 0.5 cm proximal to the pubic symphysis, and then an exploratory operation was performed. The incision was closed layer by layer with 5–0 Vicryl thread and anesthesia was maintained with 1.4% isoflurane in 40% oxygen for two hours. At the end of the procedure, EMLA cream (2.5% lidocaine and 2.5% procaine) was applied to the incision and then applied every 8 h until 72 h post procedure. After the mice recovered from anesthesia, they were placed back into their cages and provided with food and water. Control mice were given 40% oxygen for two hours.

TMS procedures

Mice were randomly divided into four groups: the Ctrl + sham TMS group, the Ctrl + TMS group, the A/S + sham TMS group, and the A/S + TMS group. Mice in the Ctrl + TMS group and the A/S + TMS group received TMS (iTBS mode, ten 50 Hz bursts with 3 pulses each repeated 20 times at 5 Hz intervals, with the total duration of the procedure of 200 s) starting at 24 h after laparotomy for three consecutive days, twice a day at 10 a.m. and 4 p.m. Stimulation intensity was set at 24%. The mice were non-anesthetized during the stimulation process and were immobilized using a homemade device. All administrations were performed with a magnetic stimulator (CCY-II, Yiruide Medical Equipment, Wuhan, China) connected to a round coil (diameter: 2 cm). The heads of the mice were pressed against the center of the coil. The Ctrl + sham TMS group and the A/S + sham TMS group received sham stimuli with the coil 15 cm away from the head of the mice at the same time.

Drug administration

Mice were randomly divided into six groups: Ctrl + sham TMS group, A/S + sham TMS group, A/S + TMS group, A/S + TMS + Melatonin group, A/S + TMS + AER-271 group and A/S + TMS + TGN-020 group. Mice in the A/S + sham TMS, A/S + TMS, A/S + TMS + Melatonin, A/S + TMS + AER-271, and A/S + TMS + TGN-020 groups were subjected to Laparotomy. Mice in the A/S + TMS, A/S + TMS + Melatonin, A/S + TMS + AER-271, and A/S + TMS + TGN-020 groups received TMS treatment twice daily for 3 days, starting on postoperative day 1. Prior to the first TMS session, the A/S + TMS + Melatonin, A/S + TMS + AER-271 and A/S + TMS + TGN-020 groups were intraperitoneally administered melatonin (10 mg kg−1), AER-271 (5 mg kg−1), or TGN-020 (250 mg kg−1), respectively. The Ctrl + sham TMS and A/S + sham TMS groups received sham stimuli at the same time.

Y-maze test

The Y-maze had three arms (20 cm long × 10 cm wide × 20 cm high) at 120° angles. The three arms included the start arm (always open); the novel arm (blocked at the training phase, but opened at the test phase); and the old arm (always open). During the training phase, the novel arm was blocked and the mice were allowed to explore the rest two arms for 10min. The test phase was conducted 24 h after the training phase. In the test phase, the novel arm was opened. The mice were allowed to explore all of the three arms for 5 min and the entrance to each arm is recorded. A video camera connected to the Any-Maze animal tracking system software is mounted above the maze to record the movements of the mice for analysis. The spatial recognition memory ability of the mice was evaluated by calculating the discrimination index and recognition index of the old and novel arms. The experimental environment was kept quiet, light, and slightly dark and each arm of the Y maze was cleaned with 70% ethanol solution between tests. Discrimination Index = (Exploration Time Novel Arm—Exploration Time Old Arm)/(Exploration Time Novel Arm + Exploration Time Old Arm); Recognition Index = Exploration Time Novel Arm/(Exploration Time Novel Arm + Exploration Time Old Arm).

Novel object recognition test

The novel object recognition test was conducted in a square space of 60 cm × 60 cm. The whole process was divided into a training stage and a testing stage. During the training stage, two identical objects were placed equidistant from the edge of the space and the mice were allowed to freely explore the space for five minutes. The test stage was conducted 24h after the training stage. During the testing stage, one of the original objects was replaced by a novel object with different color and shape at the same position. The mice were allowed to freely explore the space for 5min, and the number of times they explored the old and novel objects as well as the time duration were recorded. The recognition memory ability of the mice was assessed by calculating their discrimination index and recognition index. The experimental environment was kept quiet, bright, and slightly dark, and the whole space was cleaned with 75% ethanol solution between experiments. Discrimination Index = (Exploration Time Novel Arm—Exploration Time Old Arm)/(Exploration Time Novel Arm + Exploration Time Old Arm); Recognition Index = Exploration Time Novel Arm/(Exploration Time Novel Arm + Exploration Time Old Arm).

Intracisternal injection

Intracisternal injection was performed according to published protocol [5]. Evans Blue (Sigma), Texas Red-dextran-3 (Invitrogen), and ovalbumin-conjugated Alexa Fluor 647 (Invitrogen) were injected into the subarachnoid space using a Hamilton microinjector Mice were anesthetized and perfused transcardially with 4% paraformaldehyde 30 min later for subsequent DAPI (Thermo Scientific) staining.

Immunofluorescence staining

Mice were anesthetized and perfused with PBS and then 4% paraformaldehyde. Primary antibodies used were provided in Supplementary Table S1. For polarization index, the average fluorescent intensity of AQP4 −15 μm to 15 μm centered on blood vessels were quantified using Image J. The polarization index was defined as the difference between the peak and baseline fluorescence intensity of AQP4 divided by the peak fluorescence intensity. Peak fluorescence intensity was defined as the mean of fluorescence intensity from −2 µm to 2 μm, and baseline fluorescence intensity was defined as the mean of fluorescence intensity from −15 µm to −10 μm and from 10 µm to 15 μm.

Western blotting

Brain tissues were lysed in RIPA lysis buffer (ThermoFisher) containing a protease inhibitor cocktail (Sigma). Lysates containing equal amounts of protein were separated by 7.5–15% sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride membranes (Bio-Rad). Proteins were incubated with purified primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C followed by a HRP-conjugated secondary anti-rabbit or anti-mouse antibody (Cell Signaling Technologies) incubation. Antigen–antibody complexes were visualized by Pierce ECL Western Blotting Substrate (Epizyme). Primary antibodies used were provided in Supplementary Table S2. Acquired images were analyzed using Image J.

Enzyme-linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA)

Mouse cortical tissue lysates were collected and commercially available ELISA kits were used to measure the levels of IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, and iNOS (UpingBio) according to manufacturer’s protocols.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, USA). Data are presented as mean ± standard error (SEM) from at least two independent experiments. Group comparisons were performed using unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-tests for two-group analyses. For multiple comparisons, ordinary one-way or two-way ANOVA (Tukey’s multiple comparison test) was employed. Statistical significance was defined as follows: *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, and ****p < 0.0001.

Clinical investigation

Human study design and participants

This study is a randomized, double-blinded, single-center, sham-controlled, preliminary exploratory clinical trial. The study consists of two groups: the TMS group and the sham TMS group. The study schedule has been presented in Figure S1.

Study population

The elderly patients undergoing decompression surgery for lumbar spinal stenosis were recruited. All patients underwent cognitive screening using Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE), with a higher score indicating better function [19].

The inclusion criteria and exclusion criteria were performed as described in previous studies with minor modifications [20].

Randomization and blinding

Informed consents were obtained from all participants before surgery. At the start of the study, the rehabilitation physician who administers the TMS was responsible for randomization and allocation. Each participant was randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to either TMS group or sham TMS group based on an allocated randomization number generated using Excel for Windows. The rehabilitation physician was not involved in anesthesia management and the follow-up of postoperative outcomes. The researchers responsible for patient recruitment, data collection, and follow-up evaluation, along with the anesthesiologists and participants, were blinded to group assignment. Unblinding was permitted only if clearly required for clinical purposes.

TMS interventions

TMS was performed using a commercial magnetic stimulator (MagPro X100, MagVenture Medical Equipment, Denmark) equipped with a MCF125 magnetic stimulation coil. The stimulation site was the left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC), and the magnetic stimulation coil was tangential to the scalp. Each participant was given three sessions (stimulation program: intermittent theta burst stimulation (iTBS) mode, 50 Hz, 80% resting motor threshold (RMT), 3 pulses as a cluster, intercluster frequency: 5 Hz, number of intercluster: 10, number of repeats: 20, intermittent time: 8 s, total 600 pulses per session, 1 session per day) in 1–3 days after surgery. Sham TMS was conducted by placing the magnetic stimulation coil vertically (90°) to the skull.

Data collection and measurements

Baseline data included patient characteristics, educational level, history of anesthesia, preoperative comorbidities, and baseline cognitive function.

All patients underwent standardized perioperative monitoring and management. General anesthesia was induced with cis-atracurium, etomidate, sufentanil, and maintained with infusion of remifentanil, propofol, and 0.6–1.2 minimal alveolar concentration sevoflurane until the end of surgery. All patients received intermittent cis-atracurium infusion as required. The bispectral index was maintained between 40 and 60. Fluid infusion and blood transfusion were performed based on routine practice. Routine management for intraoperative hypotension included fluid infusion, reducing anesthetic depth, and administration of vasopressors (e.g., phenylephrine and ephedrine). Patient-controlled analgesia was initiated after tracheal extubation. The analgesic drug was sufentanil 2 ug kg−1 (maximum 200 ug), diluted with normal saline to 100 ml. The analgesia pump was set with a background infusion rate of 2 ml h−1 and 2 ml boluses with a lockout interval of 10 min.

At the end of surgery, subjects were monitored in the postanesthesia care unit until the modified Aldrete score was ≥ 9 [21]. Then all patients were sent back to surgical ward. Interventions were performed by the rehabilitation physician according to the prespecified protocol 1–3 days after surgery.

POD was evaluated twice daily until the 7th day after surgery by a well-trained investigator with Confusion Assessment Method (CAM) based on 4 basic features of delirium, namely acute onset and fluctuating course, inattention, and either disorganized thinking or altered level of consciousness [22]. The interval between two evaluations daily was at least 6 h.

Neurocognitive function was evaluated with the International Study of Postoperative Cognitive Dysfunction (ISPOCD) test battery 1 day before surgery (baseline), and either 7 days after surgery or at discharge from the hospital [23]. The test battery consisted of 9 tests: Digits Symbol Substitution Test (DSST), Trail Making Test-A (TMT-A), Judgment of Line Orientation Test (JLOT), Rey's Auditorial Verbal Learning Test, first to third presentation of words (RAVLT 1–3), Rey's Auditorial Verbal Learning Test, long-term memory (RAVLT LT), Stroop Color Word Test (STROOP), Digit Span test-Forward (DST-F), Digit Span Test-Backward (DST-B), and Semantic Fluency Test (SFT), which primarily focus on memory, attention, and executive function.

POCD was defined if the preoperative to postoperative difference exceeded more than one standard deviation in 2 or more neuropsychological tests [1, 24]. At 1 month after surgery, the global cognitive function was evaluated by the Abbreviated Mental Test score (AMTS) including 11 questionnaires through telephone communication. Scores range from 0 to 11, with higher scores indicating better function [25].

Other prespecified outcomes included feasibility assessment, TMS-related adverse event within postoperative 3 days, postoperative duration of hospitalization, and pain severity during the first 48 h after surgery.

Results

TMS treatment improves cognitive function in aged mice after anesthesia and surgery

We induced PND-like phenotypes on 18-month-old male aged mice through exploratory laparotomy (Figure S2). TMS treatment was conducted the second day after surgery twice a day for three consecutive days, followed by Y-maze test and novel object recognition test (Fig. 1A). In both tests, we found that the discrimination index and recognition index of mice in the A/S + sham TMS group were significantly reduced compared with the control group, indicating a cognitive decline. Moreover, TMS treatment significantly increased the discrimination index and recognition index of the A/S mice versus sham group, and no significant difference was observed between the A/S + TMS group and the control one (Fig. 1C-D, J). The average speed and total distance of each group did not show significant difference in both tests (Fig. 1E-F, K-L), indicating negligible effects of A/S and TMS on the motor ability of aged mice. Moreover, we found that TMS treatment given before anesthesia and surgery equally improved cognitive function in aged mice (Figure S3). TMS treatment alleviates the glymphatic system dysfunction in aged mice after anesthesia and surgery

Next, we examined the changes of the glymphatic system post anesthesia and surgery, and the effects of TMS on these changes in vivo. After injecting fluorescent tracer ovalbumin-conjugated Alexa Fluor 647 (OV-647) into the cisterna magna (Fig. 2A), we observed significantly reduced fluorescence intensities in the brain parenchyma of aged mice after anesthesia and surgery versus control ones (Fig. 2B and C), which was reversed by TMS treatment (Fig. 2B and C). We then used two more fluorescent tracers Evans blue (EB) and Texas Red-dextran-3 (Dex-3) to confirm our findings. Similarly, the fluorescence intensities of EB and Dex-3 reduced in the brain parenchyma of aged mice after anesthesia and surgery, and TMS treatment abrogated the inhibitory roles of anesthesia and surgery in EB and Dex-3 diffusion in the brain parenchyma (Figure S4A-D). Afterwards, we examined the fluorescence intensities of tracers in the deep cervical lymph nodes, an important transit station for the drainage of cerebrospinal fluid from the glymphatic system to the systemic circulation. We observed lowered OV-647 and Dex-3 intensities in the deep cervical lymph nodes of aged mice after anesthesia and surgery versus control ones, and found significantly increased OV-647 and Dex-3 intensities in that of mice received TMS treatment (Fig. 2D and E, Figure S4E and F). Moreover, we examined the effects of anesthesia and surgery, as well as TMS, on the perivascular polar distribution of AQP4 in astrocytes by immunofluorescence staining (Fig. 2F, Figure S4G). Our results showed that AQP4 perivascular polarity was inhibited in mice in the A/S + sham TMS group, compared with controls (Fig. 2F). TMS treatment enhanced polar perivascular distribution of AQP4 versus sham controls (Fig. 2F). We further found that TMS treatment reduced the protein level of matrix metalloproteinase-9 (MMP-9) and attenuated the cleavage of β-DG (Figure S5A-C), which may be the mechanism by which TMS ameliorates AQP4 polarity damage [8]. Overall, our results suggest that TMS treatment restores the function of the glymphatic system and enhances AQP4 perivascular polarity in aged mice after anesthesia and surgery.

TMS treatment inhibits neuroinflammation and neuronal damage in aged mice after anesthesia and surgery

We next examined the effects of TMS on neuroinflammation and neuronal damage, two downstream pathological changes of glymphatic dysfunction.

We examined the levels of inflammatory factors in the cortex, ELISA results showed that the levels of IL-6, TNF-α, IL-1β and iNOS increased after anesthesia and surgery compared with the control group and tended to decrease after TMS treatment compared with the A/S + sham TMS group (Fig. 3A-D). Anesthesia and surgery induced an inflammatory state in age mice, marker by elevated number of Iba1+ cells, less microglial branches, shorter branch length and CD68 expression (Fig. 3E-G, Figure S6A-D). TMS treatment was able to restore these changes (Fig. 3E-G, Figure S6A-D). Western blotting and RT-qPCR results showed that the expression of CD86, a marker of pro-inflammatory microglia, were increased in the A/S + sham TMS group compared with the control one. TMS treatment reduced the expression levels of CD86 proteins and transcripts in the brains of mice after anesthesia and surgery to comparable levels as that in the control group (Figure S6E-G). Western blot results also showed that compared with the control group, the protein levels of astrocyte activation marker GFAP and C3 of the A/S + sham TMS group was increased, and TMS treatment reduced the GFAP protein to the levels of the control group (Fig. 3H-J). AQP4 inhibitors blocked the improvement of TMS on cognitive function, neuroinflammation and neuronal damage in aged mice after anesthesia and surgery

To investigate whether TMS treatment ameliorated anesthesia- and surgery-induced cognitive dysfunction, neuroinflammation, and neuronal damage by enhancing the function of the glymphatic system, we blocked the function of the glymphatic system before TMS treatment using the AQP4 inhibitors AER-271 and TGN-020, and used Melatonin as a glymphatic system agonist (Fig. 4A). Behavioral results showed that the discrimination index and recognition index of mice in the A/S + TMS + AER-271 and A/S + TMS + TGN-020 groups were significantly reduced compared with the Ctrl + sham TMS, A/S + TMS, and A/S + TMS + Melatonin groups in the Y-maze test and the novel object recognition test (Fig. 4B-E), and there was no significant difference between the Ctrl + sham TMS group, A/S + TMS group and A/S + TMS + Melatonin group (Fig. 4B-E). No significant differences were found in the average speed and total distance traveled by the mice in each group (Figure S7A and B). TMS ameliorated the neurocognitive performance in patients

A total of 135 patients scheduled for spinal stenosis surgery were admitted from March 2022 to September 2023. Among these, 74 patients were enrolled in this study and divided into the TMS group and the sham TMS group (Figure S1). 72.5% (74/102) of eligible patients consented to participate. Patients had a high acceptance for postoperative TMS treatment. All 74 patients completed three sessions of TMS treatment or sham stimulation intervention. Subjects adhered well to the study protocol. 67.6% (50/74) patients were satisfied with this study, and 63.5% (47/74) patients were willing to participate in this study again in the next surgery.

The two groups were well balanced on baseline characteristics including age, education level, preoperative comorbidities, and preoperative cognitive function (Supplementary Table S3). Intraoperative data, including durations of anesthesia and surgery, medications during anesthesia, fluid balance, blood loss and transfusion, urine output, and anesthesia depth, did not differ between the two groups (Supplementary Table S4).

Seventy-four patients completed the neuropsychological tests on the 7th day after surgery or prior to discharge. Of these, 25 patients (33.8%) exhibited POCD. There was no statistical difference in the incidence of POCD 7 days after surgery between the TMS group and the sham TMS group (28.9% vs 38.9%, relative risk, 0.74; 95% CI, 0.39 to 1.42, p = 0.336) (Table 1). Importantly, patients in the TMS group showed higher cognitive performance in two cognitive tests (RAVLT 1–3, DST-F) versus patients in the sham TMS group, implying that postoperative TMS treatment improved patients' postoperative short-term memory (Table 1, Figure S9).

Simultaneously, 56 patients completed telephone AMTS assessment 30 days post-surgery. The primary causes of loss to follow-up were identified as non-responsiveness to phone calls and refusals from patients or their families to participate in follow-up. The two groups were well balanced on baseline characteristics (Supplementary Table S5). Patients receiving TMS demonstrated higher cognitive scores 1 month after surgery compared to the sham group (8.0 [IQR 1.8] vs. 7.0 [IQR 1.7], p = 0.007), indicating superior cognitive recovery (Table 1).

No severe adverse events including epileptic seizure and syncope was observed in all patients received TMS or sham TMS intervention, suggesting that TMS treatment administered 1–3 days after surgery is overall safe and feasible. TMS treatment did not reduce the pain intensity at 24 and 48 h after surgery. The length of postoperative hospital stay and total hospital stay were also similar between the two groups (Supplementary Table S4).

Discussion

TMS has been commonly used in brain function modulation in clinical and animal studies, due to its advantages in safety, high stimulation efficiency, and low cost [26]. For instance, Marwan Sabbagh et al. reported that TMS improved AD assessment scale-cognitive (ADAS-Cog) in AD patients in a multicentered RCT study [27]. Similarly, Giacomo Koch et al. also found that TMS treatment decelerated cognitive and function decline, and increased local gamma oscillations in AD patients [28]. The clinical observations have been confirmed in AD animals that TMS enhanced hippocampal long-term potentiation (LTP) through facilitating large conductance calcium-activated potassium channels [29]. However, very limited studies have been conducted to evaluate the therapeutic potentials of TMS in PND. In a recent small-sample-size prospective pilot study that involved elderly patients with POD, TMS was found to cure 90% of POD patients within three sessions of treatment [30]. In this study, we for the first time demonstrated that TMS alleviated PND-induced cognitive dysfunctions in a combination of animal experiments and clinical research. Hence, our observations suggest TMS as a promising therapeutic strategy for cognitive impairment in PND.

To date, TMS has been reported to exert therapeutic effects through various mechanisms. TMS significantly reduces neuroinflammation by inhibiting the excessive activation of microglia and astrocytes [31, 32], and effectively inhibits neuronal pyroptosis and apoptosis to reduce neuronal damages [32, 33]. Recent studies have found that TMS alleviates behavioral and pathological changes in animal models of AD and intracerebral hemorrhage via reversing AQP4 polarity loss-induced glymphatic system dysfunction [15]. In our study, we identified glymphatic system dysfunction and reduced perivascular polarity of AQP4 as key pathological features of PND. More importantly, TMS restored glymphatic function and AQP4 perivascular polarity in PND mice, implying glymphatic system as a central downstream target of TMS in PND. It is worth-noting that the dysfunction of the glymphatic system contributes to neuroinflammation and neuronal damage [34–37], and the improvement of glymphatic system function significantly suppresses neuroinflammation and neuronal damage [34, 38, 39]. However, it remains unknown that whether TMS alleviates neuroinflammation and neuronal damage in PND by modulating the function of the glymphatic system, which needs to be elucidated in subsequent studies. Furthermore, although this study employed AQP4 inhibitors AER-271 and TGN-020 to demonstrate that AQP4-mediated glymphatic dysfunction constitutes a key pathological mechanism in PND and a critical therapeutic target for TMS, it remains unable to directly prove that glymphatic dysfunction is the primary upstream driver of PND. Future studies employing conditional gene knockout models could help establish its pivotal role. Nevertheless, recent research has demonstrated that AQP4 overexpression alleviates glymphatic dysfunction and cognitive impairment induced by prolonged isoflurane anesthesia [40], providing support for the critical role of the glymphatic system in PND.

The present study assessed cognitive changes in mice only within the first 7 postoperative days. However, as the clinical definition of PND encompasses cognitive impairment persisting for 30 days or longer after surgery, this constitutes a limitation of the current model. Future studies will incorporate extended behavioral testing up to 30 postoperative days and beyond to evaluate the long-term therapeutic effects of TMS intervention. Additionally, while this investigation was conducted exclusively in male mice, emerging evidence suggests potential sex differences in both TMS responsiveness and glymphatic system function. Thus, subsequent studies will include female subjects to systematically examine sex-dependent effects of TMS treatment. Our study identified dysfunction of the glymphatic system in a PND animal model. Subsequent research will involve clinical trials integrating advanced neuroimaging modalities to evaluate preoperative and postoperative quantitative metrics of glymphatic system function in PND patients. This approach aims to further validate the correlation between PND pathogenesis and glymphatic system dysfunction, while establishing a theoretical foundation for developing preventive and therapeutic strategies. Additionally, we will explore the clinical utility of glymphatic system functional parameters as diagnostic and prognostic biomarkers for PND.

Notably, while the TMS group showed trends toward lower incidences of POD (2.6% vs. 8.3%) and POCD (28.9% vs. 38.9%) compared with the sham TMS group, these differences did not reach statistical significance. This limitation likely stems from the small sample size inherent to this exploratory pilot study, with post-hoc analysis indicating low statistical power and high Type II error risk. Besides, the influence of aging and learning effects on neurocognitive tests could not be analyzed due to lack of control group that did not undergo anesthesia/surgery. Therefore, future multi-center randomized controlled trials with larger sample sizes and more diverse controls are warranted to conclusively evaluate the effects of TMS on PND.

In conclusion, our animal study found that TMS treatment alleviates cognitive declines of PND mice, presumably through mitigating glymphatic system dysfunctions and its associated neuronal damage and neuroinflammation. Our clinical studies further demonstrated the therapeutic effect of postoperative TMS treatment on postoperative short-term memory in elderly patients after non-cardiac surgery. Overall, our study suggest TMS as a novel and safe therapeutic strategy for preventing or alleviating PND, particularly for elderly patients.
