Caudal esophageal achalasia in a Quarter Horse colt
Authors: Genner Josue Alvarado Soto, Mikaela Maldonado, Amy Armentrout, Kevin Woolard, Monica Aleman, Paula Giaretta, Andrew T Willis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAchalasia is the most common motility disorder of the esophagus in humans and has been diagnosed in cats and dogs. We describe a 4-month-old Quarter Horse colt with failure to thrive, recurrent colic episodes, and aspiration pneumonia, in which fluoroscopic evaluation identified a caudal esophageal motility disorder consistent with achalasia. Necropsy examination confirmed achalasia.

Introduction

Esophageal disorders are relatively well described in horses and include a variety of conditions, with esophageal obstruction being the most common.1 Regarding esophageal motility disorders, esophageal hypomotility is the most prevalent dysfunction of the esophagus in horses and can ultimately result in esophageal dilatation or megaesophagus.2 Although megaesophagus in horses is considered an acquired condition, idiopathic megaesophagus in young horses likely is congenital.1 Congenital megaesophagus is more common in Friesian horses compared with other breeds and often is associated with smooth muscle hypertrophy in the distal esophagus.1

Most disorders of the cervical esophagus in horses present with a similar range of clinical signs, including nasal discharge containing ingesta and saliva, dysphagia, odynophagia, and coughing. Less frequent signs associated with esophageal obstruction include neck extension, sweating, restlessness, and anorexia, which may occur secondary to acute, chronic, or recurrent impairment of ingesta and saliva transit. In contrast, esophageal motility disorders often have an insidious onset and may manifest as weight loss or failure to gain weight, which was consistent with the history in our case.3

Esophageal achalasia is a disease of unknown cause that interferes with esophageal emptying4. The term achalasia refers to a failure to relax. Among esophageal disorders, achalasia represents a primary motility disorder characterized by degeneration of the esophageal myenteric plexus and vagal nerve fibers at the lower esophageal sphincter (LES).4 Achalasia is the most commonly recognized motor disorder of the esophagus in humans.5 Although relatively uncommon in humans, this degeneration results in impaired peristalsis and failure of LES relaxation. In humans, achalasia is a well-recognized cause of dysphagia, regurgitation of undigested food, chest pain, weight loss, and aspiration pneumonia, with less specific signs including chronic cough and pyrosis.4

Although extensively documented in the human medical literature, esophageal achalasia is rarely reported in veterinary medicine. In the 1970s and 1980s, the syndrome of esophageal achalasia was described in the veterinary literature, with isolated cases reported in dogs and cats.5,6 In particular, achalasia of the upper esophageal sphincter (cricopharyngeal achalasia) has been well described as a congenital condition in dogs, whereas lower esophageal achalasia appears to be a rarely acquired condition.5

Esophageal achalasia has not been reported previously in horses. We describe a Quarter Horse colt with failure to thrive, recurrent colic episodes, and aspiration pneumonia, in which fluoroscopic evaluation identified a caudal esophageal motility disorder consistent with achalasia. Necropsy examination confirmed the diagnosis.

Case

The colt was born in hospital to an apparently healthy dam, but precise gestational age was unknown. The colt was small in stature, weighed 18 kg, and was slow to stand, requiring approximately 3 h with assistance. No other clinical features suggestive of prematurity or dysmaturity were noted. Parturition was normal. Despite an adequate suckle reflex, the foal initially was unable to latch, presumably because of the mare’s small udder and limited milk production. A nasogastric feeding tube was placed to provide enteral nutrition, and an IV catheter was placed for administration of 1 unit of plasma. The foal subsequently nursed appropriately, urinated, and passed meconium without complication. The patient was discharged as an apparently healthy colt weighing 23 kg at 6 days of age.

At approximately 4 months of age, the colt was presented for evaluation for signs of recurrent colic as evidenced by straining to defecate. At this time, the colt weighed 53 kg. Past pertinent medical history included failure to thrive with low body condition and muscle mass, delayed shedding and hypertrichosis, and reportedly normal blood test results (CBC and biochemical profile) obtained by the referring veterinarian 1 month earlier. The colt had been treated with a single dose of fenbendazole (5 mg/kg PO once) 1 month earlier and recently had completed a more extended course of anthelmintic treatment (fenbendazole, 10 mg/kg PO once daily for 5 days) 1 week before presentation.

Abnormal findings at presentation were mildly increased rectal temperature (102.5°F), low body condition score of 3/9, moderate diffuse symmetrical muscle atrophy, and a dry haircoat with overlong guard hairs. The remainder of the physical examination was normal. Initial blood testing consisted of CBC, serum biochemistry, venous blood gas analysis, and triglyceride, fibrinogen, serum amyloid A, and electrolyte concentrations. Clinically relevant abnormalities included decreased hemoglobin concentration (6 g/dL; reference interval [RI], 10.0-17.0 g/dL); moderate microcytic (mean cell volume, 24.1 fL; RI, 37.0-59.0 fL) normochromic (mean cell hemoglobin concentration, 36.3 g/dL; RI, 30.0-38.0 g/dL) anemia (hematocrit 16.6%; RI, 30.0%-49.0%); and mild hypochloremia (93 mmol/L; RI, 95-108 mmol/L). Abdominal radiographs and ultrasonography identified no clinically relevant abnormalities. The next day, the foal’s failure to thrive prompted a comprehensive diagnostic evaluation, including thoracic radiographs, thoracic ultrasonography, and serum vitamin E, whole blood selenium, trace element, glucose, leptin, insulin, adrenocorticotropic hormone, free l-thyroxine (T4; equilibrium dialysis), total T4, baseline T3, bile acid, cortisol, serum iron, vitamin B₁₂, folate, anti-Müllerian hormone, testosterone, inhibin, progesterone, and estrone sulfate concentrations. Results of these diagnostic tests were returned at a later date and identified no clinically relevant abnormalities. The colt was managed symptomatically in hospital and did well with no signs of colic. The patient was subsequently discharged 4 days later.

The colt was re-presented 2 days later for recurrent colic. Physical examination identified no clinically relevant changes compared with the previously performed examination and abdominal ultrasonography was again normal. Repeat blood test results were similar to the previous evaluation. The next day, gastroscopy was performed under standing sedation. Despite persistent efforts, the gastroscope could not be advanced beyond the gastric cardia, suggestive of an esophageal stricture. Therefore, nasogastric intubation was attempted but similarly could not pass beyond the cardia. Barium contrast radiography then was performed and identified a focal narrowing near the LES. Positive contrast cervical, thoracic, and abdominal computed tomography was performed under anesthesia. No clinically relevant findings were visualized, but incidental asymmetrical thyroid glands were noted.

At approximately 5 months of age, the colt was presented for acute hypersalivation. At this time, it weighed 60 kg. Clinical examination, aside from hypersalivation, identified no clinically relevant changes compared with previous examinations.

Blood test results were similar to those previously obtained. Thoracic ultrasonography identified a small section of hepatized lung in the right cranioventral thorax, most consistent with aspiration pneumonia. Oral examination identified no abnormalities. Repeat gastroscopy was performed, and the endoscope initially could not be passed through the cardia, consistent with the findings of the previous examination, but a small foal nasogastric tube was successfully passed with insufflation. After nasogastric intubation, hypersalivation resolved. Repeat gastroscopy was attempted and, with insufflation and careful manipulation, passage of the gastroscope through the cardia was achieved. On gastroscopic evaluation, diffuse squamous mucosal hyperkeratosis was noted, but no additional abnormalities (glandular, pyloric, or duodenal) were noted. Based on the history, clinical findings, and response to interventions (ie, resolution of hypersalivation after intubation), an esophageal motility disorder was suspected. After several days of treatment with sulfadiazine-trimethoprim (24 mg/kg PO q12h) for aspiration pneumonia, a fluoroscopic swallow study was recommended.

The colt was restrained in lateral recumbency for fluoroscopy and syringe fed 2 different barium feed materials: liquid barium and a pelleted feed and barium mixture. Each substrate was administered individually PO via syringe and captured by fluoroscopy passing from the oropharynx to the esophagus and entering the stomach. This study confirmed gastroesophageal reflux with both liquid and small bolus feed material passing slowly distally, with small amounts of feed material remaining in the distal esophagus and additional material traveling retrograde from the stomach orad to the caudal esophageal sphincter (Figures 1 and 2).

Fluoroscopy confirmed a caudal esophageal motility disorder at or near the LES. Prognosis was considered poor and euthanasia with necropsy was recommended. The horse was euthanized using pentobarbital overdose. No clinically relevant findings were identified on necropsy. Histopathology of the distal esophagus indicated that the myofibers within the inner muscular layer, muscularis externa, and outer muscular layer were segmentally hyalinized, hypereosinophilic and contained fragmented nuclear debris. The myofibers were separated by increased clear space (presumably edema) and often disrupted. Within the wall of the esophagus, the number of ganglia neurons was subjectively decreased and extant neurons exhibited dispersal and loss of Nissl substance, as well as being multifocally surrounded by satellite cells. These findings were most consistent with acute esophageal segmental myonecrosis and myenteric plexus multifocal neuronal chromatolysis with atrophy, loss, and mild satellitosis.

Discussion

We believe this case represents a congenital and progressive esophageal motility disorder, consistent with what has been termed esophageal achalasia. Particularly interesting is the severe and acute myonecrosis of the lower esophageal musculature in contrast to the more chronic neuronal lesions indicated by the lack of neurons within the myenteric plexus. Evidence supporting esophageal achalasia includes the limitation of this lesion to the lower esophagus, but the myonecrosis is notably atypical. Although chronic esophageal motility disorders can present with acute esophageal necrosis, we would have expected the mucosa also to be affected, which did not occur in this colt. Rather, the necrosis was restricted to the esophageal musculature. The muscle necrosis however was very acute and not associated with much inflammation, suggesting that it was secondary to either pressure or a vascular event, rather than either primary myositis or an immune-mediated event. Regardless, the damage to the esophagus was severe and recovery of function was considered impossible in this colt. Given the more chronic neuronal lesions, we speculate that the longstanding esophageal dysmotility was primarily caused by neuronal dysfunction, and the acute myonecrosis resulted from luminal impaction and caused the more recent exacerbation of clinical signs.

From a clinical standpoint, the foal’s failure to thrive can be attributed to the inability of the esophagus to move ingesta effectively down the esophageal lumen. The esophagus does not absorb nutrients, and the persistent dysfunction in motility ultimately prevented adequate nutrition. As a secondary effect, complications (ie, aspiration pneumonia and recurrent regurgitation) further compromised the colt’s condition and likely led to inappetence. Similar to other species, such as humans, dogs, and cats, poor growth, and poor body condition are the most common clinical signs in patients diagnosed with esophageal achalasia.4–6 Horses with chronic esophageal disorders also are predisposed to electrolyte and acid–base imbalances, likely associated with chronic salivary losses, as observed in our case.2

We believe that the hypomotility caused by achalasia (similar to what happens in megaesophagus) predisposed this foal to esophagitis, most likely reflux esophagitis1.Although esophagitis was not confirmed on endoscopy in our case, mild or intermittent reflux esophagitis may occur without visible mucosal lesions, depending on factors such as chronicity, timing of examination, and diet. Reflux esophagitis also may help explain the observed hypersalivation. Ongoing gastric acid exposure and repeated reflux likely contributed to gastric acid loss, contributing to the hypochloremia observed in this case. Hypochloremia in horses is commonly linked to gastroesophageal reflux, and accompanying electrolyte abnormalities have been documented in foals with reflux, including metabolic alkalosis and hyponatremia.7 These abnormalities align with the pathophysiology of chronic esophageal disease, where ongoing fluid and electrolyte loss through reflux and salivation may contribute to electrolyte and acid base abnormalities, even when chloride depletion is mild.

Esophageal achalasia should be included as a differential diagnosis for failure to thrive, persistent hypochloremia, hypersalivation, and recurrent colic in young horses. Contrast fluoroscopic evaluation should be considered in foals with such clinical findings.
