Human papillomavirus awareness in a digital world: The intersection of faith and online media
Authors: Alfu Laily, Soojung Jo, Robert J Duncan, Randolph D Hubach, Kathryn J LaRoche, Natalia M Rodriguez, Monica L Kasting
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjective: This study examines the association between Human Papillomavirus (HPV) awareness and internet use, social media use, and trust in health information from religious leaders.Methods: We analyzed national data, collected March–November 2022 in the U.S. The final sample included 3444 adults ages 18–65 years. The outcome was HPV awareness (being aware of both HPV and the HPV vaccine). Weighted chi square and multivariable logistics regression were used to assess the outcome and each predictor, including internet and social media use, trust in health information from religious leaders, and demographic factors.Results: Among respondents, 62.3 % were aware of HPV. Those who used the internet had higher odds of HPV awareness (aOR = 2.31, 95 %CI = 1.58,3.39), whereas those reporting perceived online health information from social media as accurate had lower odds of HPV awareness (aOR = 0.40, 95 %CI = 0.16,0.98). Social media use and trust in health information from religious leaders were not significantly associated with HPV awareness.Conclusion: Internet use, but not social media use or trust in health information from religious leaders, may improve HPV awareness. This highlights the need for interventions to guide individuals find reliable sources online and for future research to assess accuracy of online HPV information to facilitate vaccination decisions.Keywords: Human papillomavirus, Internet, Online media, Religious leaders, Social media

Introduction

In the United States (U.S.), Human Papillomavirus (HPV) diseases are the most common sexually transmitted infection, causing approximately 48,000 cancer cases annually (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2024; Elissa et al., 2021). The primary risk factor for HPV infection is skin-to-skin anogenital contact and many infections are asymptomatic (Elissa et al., 2021). The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention recommends initiating HPV vaccination at age 11–12, with the option to start as early as age nine (Center for Disease Control and Prevention, 2023). Catch-up vaccination is recommended up to age 26, and adults ages 27–45 may benefit from the vaccine and should consult their healthcare provider (Center for Disease Control and Prevention, 2023). Despite effective HPV vaccines capable of preventing 85–95 % of HPV-related cancers and 90 % of genital warts (Luxembourg and Moeller, 2017), HPV vaccination rates in the U.S. adults remain low. In 2022, only 57.7 % of women and 34.8 % of men who are eligible (ages 19–26) reported receiving more than one HPV vaccine dose (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2022).

Although HPV vaccination is routinely recommended for adolescents, parental awareness is a key driver of their children's HPV vaccine uptake (Aragaw et al., 2023), underscoring the need to identify factors linked to HPV awareness. In addition, cervical cancer screening guidelines currently recommend screening with HPV testing up to age 65 (US Preventive Services Task Force et al., 2018). Therefore, awareness of HPV in general, not just the HPV vaccine, is important for people beyond those who are in the age group who can personally be vaccinated against HPV. The most commonly cited sources of HPV awareness and information are school health programs, healthcare providers, and internet/social media spaces (Schwendener et al., 2022; Jo et al., 2022). The internet/social media use aligns with 2022 data showing that about 58.5 % of Americans adults turn to the internet to look for health or medical information (Wang and Cohen, 2022). However, the relationship between online media exposure to HPV-related information and vaccine uptake remains unclear. Some studies show that internet and social media exposure improves awareness and knowledge, but this does not always translate to higher vaccination rates (Dib et al., 2021; Ortiz et al., 2019). This suggests that online information can be a powerful tool for promoting positive HPV vaccine decision when credible information is prioritized.

Relying upon online health information to navigate HPV vaccine decision making anonymously may be more prevalent in some religious communities where discussions about HPV prevention can be sensitive and stigmatized (Tizard, 2023; Powell et al., 2011). Concerns about the link between HPV vaccination promotes more risky sexual behavior have made accepting HPV vaccine a contentious issue within some religious communities (Tizard, 2023), while studies found there was no riskier sexual behaviors after HPV vaccination (Kasting et al., 2016). Additionally, some religious values promoting abstinence before marriage and monogamy afterward may create a perception that these practices provide sufficient protection, reducing motivation to receive the HPV vaccine (Birmingham et al., 2019). However, a commitment to monogamy does not guarantee monogamous behavior, and in North America, approximately 23.2 % of men and 19.2 % of women reported engaging in sexual activity outside their primary relationships (Mark et al., 2011). These patterns highlight that individuals in these groups may remain susceptible to HPV infection despite their religious beliefs and tenets, underscoring the importance of HPV vaccination.

Trusted sources of information about HPV may play a role in shaping HPV awareness and eventually HPV vaccination decisions, particularly in some religious communities where conversations about sexual behaviors are taboo. In today's digital age, online media is a major source of health information (Wang and Cohen, 2022) offering the added benefit of confidentiality (Powell et al., 2011), which is important for individuals whose religious beliefs may stigmatize HPV prevention. Religious leaders also noted as trusted sources of health information (Viskupič et al., 2025), providing an additional channel for those whose religious beliefs shape their vaccine decisions. These factors intersect, as religious leaders have the opportunity to act as a trusted health messenger and can help counter misinformation/disinformation found online and reinforce credible health messages. Given this complex interplay between online information sources and some religious influences, understanding how these factors are associated with HPV-related awareness is essential. Building on these insights, this study aims to investigate the association between HPV awareness and internet use, social media use, and trust in health information from religious leaders.

Methods

Procedures and participants

This secondary data analysis study used the Health Information National Trends Survey (HINTS), a national survey conducted by the U.S. National Cancer Institute and the dataset was publicly available (National Cancer Institute, 2023). The HINTS dataset includes information related to cancer, including awareness, knowledge, attitudes, and preventive health behaviors. This study utilized data extracted from the 2022 HINTS 6, collected March–November 2022. Although newer HINTS data (2024 HINTS 7) was available, this survey did not assess HPV-related awareness. HINTS 6 employed a dual-mode approach, allowing respondents to choose between completing a traditional paper survey or an online option. The sampling frame was divided into four strata, stratifying high and low concentrations minority (Hispanic/African American) adults in both rural and urban settings. The 2015–2019 American Community Survey data were used to classify census tracts as high-minority if the population proportion of Hispanic and African American adults was 34 % or greater, and all others were classified as low-minority. The sampling strategy followed a two-stage design. First, the Marketing Systems Group generated random address samples. In the second stage, one adult was randomly chosen from each sampled household. HINTS used a stratified sample of U.S. adults and provided weighting procedures to ensure nationally representative estimates (National Cancer Institute, 2023). Furthermore, Preferred Reporting Items for Complex Sample Survey Analysis for this study was available to report weighted analysis using HINTS's complex sampling data (Supplement 1). This was an observational study that utilized publicly available data and does not necessitate ethical approval.

A total of 6252 responses were collected in the final dataset for HINTS 6, with an overall response rate of 28.1 %. For our study, only participants ages 18–65 years old were included. Ineligible participants due to age (n = 2057, 32.90 %) and responses with missing data (n = 751, 12.01 %) were excluded in this study, resulting in a final sample of 3444 complete cases included in the analysis.

Variables and measures

We created the awareness variable based on two questions assessing awareness of HPV “Have you ever heard of HPV? HPV stands for Human Papillomavirus. It is not HIV, HSV, or herpes.” and HPV vaccine “A vaccine to prevent HPV infection is available and is called the HPV shot, cervical cancer vaccine, or GARDASIL®. Before today, have you ever heard of the cervical cancer vaccine or HPV shot?” with two response options each: “Yes” and “No”. Responses were categorized as “aware” if participants answered “Yes” of both HPV and the HPV vaccine, and “unaware” if they lacked awareness of either or both. There were 236 participants who knew of the HPV vaccine but not HPV, and 411 knew of HPV but not the vaccine, showing that awareness of one does not always predict awareness of the other. Thus, the combined variable offers a more inclusive indication of general HPV-related awareness.

Our main predictor variables were internet use, social media use, and trust in health information from religious leaders. Other covariates included age, race/ethnicity, sex assigned at birth, marital status, education level, household income, rurality, and perceived inaccuracy of social media health information. Internet use was measured using a question “Do you ever go on-line to access the Internet or World Wide Web, or to send and receive e-mail?” with binary options “Yes” and “No.” For social media we used “In the last 12 months, how often did you visit a social media site?” with five options: “Almost every day,” “At least once a week,” “A few times a month,” “Less than once a month,” and “Never.” Trust in health information from religious leaders was measured using “In general, how much would you trust information about cancer from religious organizations and leaders?” with the following options “A lot”, “Some”, “A little”, and “Not at all” and was coded as a binary variable, categorized as “Yes” for any level of trust and “No” for “not at all.”

Age was categorized into three groups, 18–26, 27–45, and 46–65 years old. The age range of up to 26 years aligns with the catch-up HPV vaccination period for adults (Center for Disease Control and Prevention, 2023), the range up to 45 years corresponds to the U.S. Food and Drug Administration licensing for HPV vaccination eligibility in men and women (U.S. Food and Drug Administration, 2020), and the range up to 65 years corresponds to the United States Preventive Services Task Force recommendations of the latest age for cervical cancer screening (National Cancer Institute. Cervical Cancer Screening, 2025.). Race/ethnicity was categorized into five groups: Non-Hispanic White, Non-Hispanic Black, Hispanic, Non-Hispanic Asian, and Non-Hispanic Other. Sex was determined by what was listed on individuals' birth certificate: male or female. Marital status was classified into married, separated (including divorced and widowed), and never married. Education level was divided based on the highest level of education completed: ≤ high school, some college/college degree, and postgraduate. Income was assessed as total combined household gross income in the past year: $0–49,999, $50,000-99,999, and ≥ $100,000. The rurality variable was available through HINTS's variable deriving from United States Department of Agriculture 2010 Primary Rural-Urban Community Area Code with four categorizations: metropolitan, micropolitan, small town, and rural. Perceived inaccuracy of social media health information was measured with “How much of the health information that you see on social media do you think is false or misleading?” and coded with five options “A lot”, “Some”, “A little”, “None”, and “I do not use social media”.

Data analysis

To account for sampling variability in population estimates, replicate weights were applied using the jackknife replication method. We performed chi-square tests to summarize the sample characteristics based on total sample and HPV awareness groups (aware/unaware) by reporting the weighted percentage in each sub-category and its corresponding p-value. Then, we conducted multivariable logistic regression analysis to examine the associations between HPV awareness and the predictors. Statistical significance was determined at p < 0.05, with 95 % CI calculated for each adjusted odds ratio (aOR). All analyses were conducted using SAS software (version 9.4).

Results

The total sample included 3444 adults. In the weighted sample, the majority (44.1 %) were between 46 and 65 years old, 62.3 % were aware of HPV, while 37.7 % were not. Most participants were non-Hispanic White (58.5 %), assigned male at birth (50.5 %), and married (55.4 %). The majority also reported using the internet (91.6 %), using social media almost daily (67.3 %), and trusting cancer information delivered by religious leaders (58.0 %). In bivariate analyses, almost all the variables were significantly associated with HPV awareness, with the exception of rurality (p = 0.40) and trusting health information from religious leaders (p = 0.06). Discussion

Our study highlights key insights into factors associated with HPV awareness, particularly focusing on internet use, social media use, and trust in health information from religious leaders. We found that individuals who used the internet had significantly higher odds of HPV awareness. We also observed that social media use and trust in cancer information from religious leaders were not significantly associated with HPV awareness in the adjusted model. HPV awareness is critical, as parental awareness is closely linked to HPV vaccine uptake among adolescents (Aragaw et al., 2023). Additionally, since HPV testing is recommended for cervical cancer screening up to age 65 (US Preventive Services Task Force et al., 2018), HPV awareness remains important well beyond the typical age for vaccination.

One notable finding of our study is the significant association between internet use and higher HPV awareness. In our sample, approximately 91.6 % of participants reported using the internet. With internet widespread usage, seeking health information online has become increasingly common. Prior research reported that electronic sources, including information accessed through the internet, have become the most common sources of HPV knowledge in some communities rather than information provided by physicians (Adjei Boakye et al., 2017). Many individuals do not only passively consume online health information, but they also participate in online spaces where health information is actively shared and discussed. However, the open and unregulated nature of the internet raises concerns about the credibility of the information being circulated (British Medical Journal, 2000; Battineni et al., 2020). Given the findings in our study and others, it is important that public health efforts focus on teaching individuals how to cross-reference information from multiple reputable sources, including established health organizations websites and physician offices to avoid relying on misleading or inaccurate online health information (Lapidus and Dryankova-Bond, 2014; Tan and Goonawardene, 2017).

Our results indicated that people who used social media were more aware of HPV, but this association was no longer significant in the multivariable regression model while controlling for other covariates. This shift in significance suggests that other variables included in the regression model altered this association, which is consistent with other research. Previous literature found that women (compared to men), urban (compared to rural) residents, and Non-Hispanic White participants were more likely to use online media as source of health-related and medical information (Wang and Cohen, 2022; Chen et al., 2019; Hou and Shim, 2010). Moreover, using social media does not necessarily imply exposure to or active engagement in HPV-related information, which is more closely associated with HPV awareness and vaccination promotion.

We explored the role of perceived inaccuracy of health information on social media and HPV awareness. Our findings suggested that those who perceived social media health information as accurate tended to have lower HPV awareness. When individuals rely on social media as their primary source for health information, the brief nature and potential for information that is not fact-checked can lead to erroneous beliefs (Keim-Malpass et al., 2017). Furthermore, social media users may have skewed or incorrect information from persuasive personal stories, which is concerning because research shows that social media platforms' personal stories posts about HPV tend to be more negative than positive (Llavona-Ortiz et al., 2022).

Trust in cancer information from religious leaders was not associated with HPV awareness in either bivariate or multivariable regression analyses. This was contrary to what has been found in other research examining religious leaders as trusted messengers for health information. However, the recent studies have mostly examined vaccines in general or the COVID-19 vaccine (Viskupič et al., 2025; Meyer et al., 2022). While these studies did show people trusted religious leaders for information and recommendations, there has been less research on whether these findings are similar for the HPV vaccine specifically. Even though religious leaders often hold significant influence within their communities, our findings suggest that the influence of religious leaders on health awareness may not always materialize in the context of HPV-related information. Therefore, the possibility that religious leaders could either improve or hinder HPV vaccination uptake is unclear and the implications of these findings need to be further explored in future research.

Despite these important insights, our study has several limitations. First, the use of secondary data means that we did not develop the specific questions we used about HPV awareness or the trust in health information delivered by religious leaders, limiting the clarity of association we were examining. Relatedly, given the small numbers of people in some of our analytic groups (e.g. those who did not use the internet, those who perceive social media information as accurate, etc.) it is possible these factors might be explained by unmeasured confounding factors and results should be interpreted with caution. The online/social media use may not always correspond to online exposure to HPV-related content, constraining the context of the observed associations. The HINTS 6 survey did not provide a variable measuring HPV vaccine uptake, preventing us from analyzing this health outcome which may have provided a clearer picture of the associations of interest. Furthermore, because HINTS data is observational and not an experiment, we cannot draw causal conclusions from the associations observed. Additionally, due to the self-reported nature of the study, participant responses are subject to recall and social desirability biases, and they may not have accurately reported their HPV awareness. Furthermore, this voluntary survey may have been subject to selection bias as certain groups, such as those from minority religious affiliations, might be underrepresented. However, the weighting procedures and statistical analyses were intended to limit the effect of this bias. Finally, part of our sample falls outside the recommended age for HPV vaccination, which may limit direct implications for increasing vaccine uptake. Despite these limitations, this study contributed to body of research work to understand association between HPV awareness and online media use and trust in heath information from religious leaders.

Conclusion

This study provides valuable insights into the potential factors associated with HPV awareness, particularly the role of internet and social media use, as well as information trust from religious leaders. Our findings indicated that internet users tend to have higher HPV awareness, while social media use and trust in health information from religious leaders were not significantly associated with HPV awareness. Based on these results, public health interventions could focus on guiding individuals to find trusted sources of health information online. Given that trust in health information from religious leaders was not found to be significant, it may be more effective to situate these interventions outside of religious settings. There are needs for future studies with a primary data collection to assess predictor variables of HPV awareness to ensure accuracy and reliability of the responses. Furthermore, additional variables to examine accuracy of HPV awareness should also be assessed to better understand the complex relationships between HPV awareness and internet use, social media, and trust in health information from religious leaders.
