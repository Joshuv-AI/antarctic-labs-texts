Endometrial Cancer of the Uterine Isthmus: A Cohort Study on Clinicopathological Features and Prognosis
Authors: Hao Wang, Peijie Xiao, Chendi Zhu, Jinxia Zhou, Chunhua Yang, Paulo Moreira, Li Yan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveThe study compared the clinicopathologic characteristics and prognosis of patients with uterine isthmus cancer to those with uterine fundus cancer. The aim was to identify risk variables associated with the prognosis of patients with isthmus cancer.MethodsIn the SEER cohort, 584 endometrial cancer patients were included: 91 in the isthmus group and 493 in the fundus group. Data for this retrospective study were collected from the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database and the registry of a provincial hospital. SEER included individuals diagnosed with endometrial cancer between 2000 and 2018. Patients were divided into two groups according to the primary site code for endometrial cancer: Isthmus and fundus. Chi‐square tests were used to compare the population characteristics. STROBE Protocol was followed.ResultsIsthmus cancers were younger, more advanced, often non‐endometrioid adenocarcinomas, and more likely to receive adjuvant therapy (p < 0.05). The 3‐year overall survival rate for isthmus cancer was significantly lower than for fundus cancer (p = 0.016). Uterine isthmus cancer in the hospital cohort accounted for 2.0% (9/455) of the diagnosed endometrial cancers. All patients survived without recurrence or metastasis and were in good physical condition.ConclusionThe research assessed the prognostic impact of the uterine isthmus and the clinical and pathological features of uterine isthmus cancer, and the associated risk factors affecting prognosis. Uterine isthmus cancer demonstrates a higher stage, a stronger tendency for adjuvant therapy, and a more unfavorable prognosis when compared to fundus carcinoma. The international evidence is diverse on this issue.Keywords: endometrial cancer patients, metastatsis, myometrial infiltration, oncology, survival rates, uterine isthmus cancer

Introduction

Endometrial cancer (EC) is one of the most common malignancies in gynecology [1], with an increasing incidence and a tendency for patients to be younger [1, 2]. Most of the lesions are located in the uterine fundus and body of the uterus, but some cases originate in the isthmus of the uterus, known as the uterine isthmus. The uterine isthmus is the narrowest part between the uterine corpus and the cervix and is pathologically the area between the mucous glands of the cervix and the endometrial glands [3, 4, 5].

The definition of lower uterine segment involvement remains unclear. Most of the literature defines lower uterine segment involvement as diffuse involvement limited to the lower uterine segment or extending from the fundus/uterine body to the lower uterine segment [6, 7]. Some scholars believe it should only be defined as confined to the lower uterine segment [3, 8]. Some studies have shown that isthmus cancer accounts for 3%–8.1% of all endometrial cancer cases [6, 7]. Tumor location is not considered a reference in the updated FIGO 2023 staging system [9]. Meanwhile, although FIGO stage defines interstitial cervical infiltration as stage II9, it is still inconclusive whether the involvement of the lower uterine segment can be considered an independent risk factor affecting the recurrence and prognosis of endometrial cancer or as a criterion for deciding on adjuvant therapy [10, 11].

In this study, we investigated the clinicopathological characteristics, prognosis, and prognostic factors of patients with isthmus endometrial cancer using the SEER database. In addition, we collected clinical data from patients with endometrial cancer diagnosed at a comprehensive tertiary hospital between January 2010 and December 2023 to validate the clinicopathological characteristics of isthmus cancer.

Methods

Our data were obtained from the SEER17+ registry database submitted in November 2021 (2000–2019 dataset) [12]. Inclusion criteria were as follows: Primary site including Uterine isthmus (C540) and Uterine fundus (C543); given that the scope of this study was limited to uterine isthmus cancer in a narrow sense, patients with tumor size ≤ 4 cm were selected to differentiate the primary site. Exclusion criteria were as follows: Unknown number of months of survival, non‐primary malignancy, and unknown other clinical data. The clinical data used in this part of the study were obtained from the publicly accessible SEER database; therefore, ethical approval and informed consent were not required. In addition, patient data included in the hospital dataset were approved by the hospital's institutional review board. As this was a retrospective study, patient‐informed consent was not required. All patient data were analyzed anonymously.

We extracted several variables from the SEER database, including age at diagnosis (reclassified as < 60 and ≥ 60 years), stage (included patients were evaluated according to the AJCC 6th and 7th), primary site, treatment modality (surgery, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy), and patient survival status and survival time. In addition, we classified histological subtypes into well‐differentiated, moderately differentiated, poorly differentiated, and undifferentiated.

The chi‐square test was used to compare the demographic characteristics of the isthmus and fundus groups. Univariate survival analyses were performed using the log‐rank test to identify risk factors affecting patient survival. The Kaplan–Meier method was used to describe survival curves as indicated by international literature [13]. Multivariate survival analyses were performed using the Cox proportional hazards model to identify independent prognostic factors affecting patient survival, and hazard ratios (HR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were reported. All statistical analyses were performed using R version 3.4.4 and SPSS version 26.0, and p values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant. STROBE Protocol was followed.

Results

A total of 584 EC patients were included in the SEER database, ranging in age from 30 years to over 85 years. There was a statistically significant difference between 91 patients with isthmus cancer (47 (51.6%) of whom were less than 60 years old) and 493 patients with fundus cancer (196 (39.8%) of whom were less than 60 years old) (p = 0.034). Forty‐six (50.5%) of the patients with isthmus cancer were in stage I, 19 (20.9%) were in stage II, 17 (18.7%) were in stage III, and 9 (9.9%) were in stage IV. In contrast, 443 patients (89.9%) in the fundus cancer group were in stage I, 12 (2.4%) in stage II, 23 (4.7%) in stage III, and 15 (3.0%) in stage IV, which is a statistically significant difference (p < 0.001). Patients with isthmus cancer were diagnosed at an advanced stage than patients with fundus cancer (p < 0.001). There was no significant difference between the two groups in terms of grade and tumor size (p > 0.05), indicating a balance between the two groups without addressing potential bias.

In terms of treatment modalities, only 6 of the 584 patients did not receive surgical treatment, and all of them had isthmus cancer, of which 4 were advanced. In terms of adjuvant treatment, 45 cases (49.5%) of patients with isthmus cancer did not receive chemotherapy or radiotherapy, 22 cases (24.2%) received only radiotherapy, 7 cases (7.7%) received only chemotherapy, and 17 cases (18.7%) received combined radiotherapy and chemotherapy (Table 1).

Of the 584 EC patients, 117 died within 3 years of surgery, including 26 patients with isthmus cancer and 91 patients with basal cancer. To compare the prognostic outcomes of the two groups of patients, we performed a Kaplan–Meier survival analysis, calculated the 3‐year OS, and plotted a Kaplan–Meier survival curve (Figure 1).

Kaplan–Meier curve comparing 3‐year OS between uterine isthmus cancer and fundus cancer individuals.

The 3‐year survival rate was 71.4% for patients with isthmus cancer and 81.5% for patients with fundus cancer, and the difference between the two groups was statistically significant (p < 0.05). Log‐rank univariate survival analysis showed that age, tumor site, pathological stage, grade, surgery, and chemotherapy were risk factors for 3‐year OS in EC patients (p < 0.05). In isthmus cancer, pathological stage and grade were significant risk factors for 3‐year OS (p < 0.05), whereas age, tumor size, surgery, and adjuvant therapy were not associated with prognosis (p > 0.05). Multivariate analysis by incorporating significant factors from log‐rank analysis into the Cox proportional hazards model showed that tumor location was not an independent risk factor for survival in patients with endometrial cancer. However, pathological stage and grade were considered independent risk factors for 3‐year survival in patients with endometrial cancer of the isthmus (Tables 2 and 3).

In addition, screening data were obtained from the hospital, where 455 cases of endometrial cancer that underwent staging surgery between 2012 and 2021 were collected after excluding cases with lesions larger than 4 cm and where the primary tumor site could not be identified. Of these, only 9 cases were identified as uterine isthmus cancer and were analyzed descriptively. The age of the patients ranged from 29 to 75 years, with a median age of 51 years. Body mass index ranged from 22 to 36.9 years, and age at menarche ranged from 13 to 18 years, with a median age of 15 years. None of the patients had a family history of cancer. There were 5 cases of endometrioid carcinoma, 1 case of clear cell carcinoma, and 3 cases of plasma adenocarcinoma. Among them, 4 cases were highly or moderately differentiated, and 5 cases were poorly differentiated. All cases had myometrial invasion, and 2 out of 9 cases had deep myometrial invasion. One case presented with lymphovascular space invasion without pelvic lymph node metastasis. Four of them were FIGO stage I, and five were stage II. In addition, follow‐up data were successfully obtained for all 9 patients, ranging from 11 to 108 months, with a mean follow‐up of 44 months. Five patients received no adjuvant therapy, 2 patients received chemotherapy only, and 2 patients received both chemotherapy and radiotherapy. All nine patients are alive, without recurrence or metastasis, and in good general condition (Table 4).

Discussion

Principal Findings

Our research utilized the publicly accessible SEER database to investigate the pathological characteristics, prognosis, and risk factors of patients with endometrial cancer of the isthmus of the uterus. Unlike single‐institution studies [3, 6], our study leveraged a large national dataset, minimizing bias from external factors. The main findings revealed that patients with uterine isthmus cancer were more likely to exhibit type II endometrial cancer pathology: Younger at onset, high pathological stage, and pathological type of non‐endometrioid adenocarcinoma. Notably, most patients with isthmus cancer received adjuvant therapy, unlike patients with endometrial cancer of the uterine fundus. However, age, tumor size, and adjuvant therapy were not associated with the 3‐year OS of uterine isthmus cancer. Pathological stage and grading are independent risk factors that influence the prognosis of uterine isthmus cancer.

Comparison With Existing Literature

The definition of lower uterine segment involvement can be categorized into narrow and broad definitions. The broad definition includes lesions originating in the body of the uterus and extending to the lower uterine segment [7], whereas the narrow definition refers specifically to tumors originating in the lower uterine segment [3]. Endometrial cancers are generally categorized as estrogen‐dependent (type I) or non‐estrogen‐dependent (type II) [14]. Type I tumors are associated with continuous estrogen stimulation without progesterone opposition, while type II tumors, often unrelated to estrogen, arise from endometrial atrophy. The isthmus of the uterus is the narrowest part between the uterine corpus and the cervix, with a thin mucosal layer, fibrotic stroma, underdeveloped glandular structures, and a weak response to hormonal stimulation [3]. Uterine isthmus carcinoma typically lacks type I characteristics but shares features with type II tumors [8, 15].

Consistent with reports by Westin [3] and others [6, 16], patients with isthmus cancer tend to be younger, with a mean age nearly 10 years lower than those with uterine corpus cancer, a higher incidence under 50 years of age, more advanced tumor stages, and a poorer prognosis. The results of Madom's study are in agreement with this study, showing that carcinoma of the uterine isthmus had a younger median age, higher histological grade, worse histological type, deeper myometrial infiltration, and more frequent lymphovascular interstitial infiltration compared to carcinoma of the uterine corpus [17], which had a worse impact on the prognosis. In our study, all these features seem to suggest a type II endometrial cancer. Uterine isthmus cancer is frequently reported as a poor prognostic factor [17, 18]; however, several studies have shown that lower segmental involvement does not necessarily lead to a poorer prognosis [19, 20].

In this study, the 3‐year OS for isthmus cancer was 71.4%, compared to 81.5% for the fundal group, and the difference in 3‐year OS between the two groups was statistically significant (HR = 1.70, 95% CI = 1.10–2.62, p = 0.024). One study suggests that uterine isthmus tumors have a higher propensity for lymphovascular invasion and a higher risk of lymph node metastasis [21]. Miyoshi [20] and Lavie's [22], study also confirmed previous findings showing that isthmus cancer was associated with more advanced stages, deeper myometrial infiltration, and higher rates of lymph node metastasis. Meanwhile, the Kaplan–Meier survival analyses showed that patients with isthmus cancer had a poorer prognosis, but multivariate Cox proportional hazards models did not find tumor location to be an independent prognostic factor for endometrial cancer, which is similar to our results. Brown [23] found no significant association between isthmus involvement and recurrence in a multivariate analysis controlling for other risk factors. This discussion is summarized in Table 5.

Irwin's research has proposed that isthmus cancer might warrant adjuvant radiotherapy, even in the absence of other high‐risk factors [24]. Meanwhile, Mayr suggested that involvement of the lower uterine segment increases the risk of pelvic recurrence and proposed that postoperative radiotherapy may enhance local control in this patient [5]. However, Phelan [19] found that lower segment involvement is not linked to a poorer prognosis or increased risk of pelvic recurrence when adverse pathological features are absent, and thus should not be an indication for adjuvant radiotherapy. Similarly, Westin [3] and Phelan [19] do not support the routine use of postoperative adjuvant therapy. Our study suggests that, despite more aggressive postoperative adjuvant therapy, patients with isthmus cancer may have a poorer prognosis compared to those with fundal cancer. Notably, the evidence generated in our study suggests adjuvant therapy did not show a significant correlation with prognosis in isthmus cancer patients, which is similar to the study by Lavie et al. [22]. Although more patients with isthmus endometrial cancer received postoperative radiotherapy, radiotherapy was not an independent factor affecting prognosis. However, other studies suggest that paracervical lymph node metastasis is one of the reasons for treatment failure in patients with isthmus‐involved endometrial cancer, implying that adjuvant radiotherapy may be useful in reducing local recurrence [5, 16]. The isthmus of the uterus is an anatomical transition zone, and its proximity to the cervix may influence the tumor's biological behavior, resulting in invasiveness similar to cervical‐involved endometrial cancer. All of these studies point to the possibility that isthmus cancer may have an impact on patient prognosis, albeit not as much as histologic grading and staging. However, when all of these factors are considered together, they can assist guide treatment options.

Understanding the risk factors associated with uterine isthmus cancer increasingly requires looking beyond traditional clinicopathological features [25, 26]. Genetically, there is already a strong recognized association with Lynch syndrome, which is suspected in up to 29% of these patients [3]. Furthermore, recent advancements emphasize that integrating novel molecular markers, such as L1CAM expression, is highly effective in predicting poor outcomes and tailoring patient‐specific adjuvant therapies [27, 28]. While it would be highly valuable to assess how isthmus involvement interacts with such specific molecular targets, the SEER database currently lacks this granular data. Consequently, a key direction for future research will be exploring the intersection of anatomical factors, like isthmus location, with modern molecular profiling.

Limitations

Finally, our study has several limitations. First of all, as a retrospective study, this study collected information from a dataset and excluded patients with missing data from the variables collected, which may lead to selection bias. Since there was no lymphovascular infiltration in the data extracted from the SEER database and only one case with vascular metastasis in the affiliated hospital, it is not possible to directly judge the correlation with this risk factor, but the analysis of the data showed that the proportion of advanced, moderately poorly differentiated and non‐endometrioid adenocarcinomas in patients with isthmus cancer was higher than that of fundus cancer, which could also explain the poor prognosis of the uterine isthmus from the point of view of the pathognomonic features. In addition, due to the specific location of the lesions and the limited number of cases in the hospitals' dataset, we were only able to characterize their pathology without being able to make further comparisons of differences. Also, because the SEER database reflects data from US residents, there may be potential racial or ethnic differences. These factors should be explored in different clinical populations using high‐quality individual data.

Conclusion

Our findings and recent research [29, 30, 31, 32] imply that patients with uterine isthmus cancer generally have a poorer prognosis. These patients typically develop the disease at a younger age, present with a more severe stage at diagnosis, and have a poorer histological grade. Tumors located in the isthmus are more likely to be aggressive, demonstrating deep myometrial invasion and lymphovascular space involvement. Although this study suggests a higher propensity for administering adjuvant therapy in these patients, it appeared to have no meaningful impact on prognosis in our cohort. Because this is a retrospective investigation limited by the absence of genomic data, large‐scale prospective studies that integrate anatomical features (such as isthmus involvement) with modern molecular profiling are required to definitively refine risk stratification.

Some practice recommendations that evolve from this study include: Firstly, isthmus endometrial cancers should be considered an initial consideration in clinical practice when encountering patients with a young age of onset, a site between the uterine body and cervix, and a highly aggressive tumor; secondly, because isthmus tumors carry a poorer prognosis and exhibit invasive behaviors similar to cervical involvement, traditional parameters such as tumor stage and histological categorization—ideally alongside emerging molecular risk factors—must be carefully integrated when determining the optimal, individualized therapy regimen.
