Hyperresponse during ovarian stimulation despite profoundly suppressed ovarian reserve markers in a patient with long-term combined hormonal contraceptive use
Authors: Shelun Tsai, Ahmad Hasan, Steven R. Lindheim, Nigel Pereira
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo report a case of unanticipated hyperresponse during controlled ovarian stimulation (COS) despite profoundly suppressed ovarian reserve markers in a patient with long-term combined hormonal contraceptive (CHC) use and discuss the possible mechanisms contributing to this clinical conundrum.DesignCase report.SubjectsA 33-year-old gravida 0 woman presented to the office to discuss planned oocyte cryopreservation. She had been using a vaginal contraceptive ring containing etonogestrel 0.12 mg/day and ethinyl estradiol 0.015 mg/day for the past 12 years. Her antral follicle count and antimüllerian hormone (AMH) at the initial consultation was 1 and 0.17 ng/mL (1.2 pmol/L), respectively. Discontinuation of CHC was recommended. Her cycle day 2/3 follicle-stimulating hormone, luteinizing hormone, and AMH were 5.9 IU/L, 8.1 IU/L, and 0.66 ng/mL (4.7 pmol/L), respectively, 6 months after discontinuing CHC.ExposureOocyte cryopreservation and oocyte retrieval for planned oocyte cryopreservation approximately 4 weeks after her most recent ovarian reserve testing.Main Outcome MeasuresTotal number of oocytes retrieved and cryopreserved after COS.ResultsThe patient began estrogen priming in the luteal phase of the prior menstrual cycle. Her antral follicle count at the start of COS was 9. The COS was initiated with 300 IU of recombinant follicle-stimulating hormone and 150 IU of human menopausal gonadotropin with a gonadotropin-releasing hormone antagonist protocol. An unanticipated robust response was observed as highlighted by her estradiol levels of 5,992.4 pg/mL (22,000 pmol/L) on the 9th day of COS. Her gonadotropin doses subsequently were decreased over the next 2 days of COS with peak estradiol levels of 7,817.3 pg/mL (28,700 pmol/L). The cumulative gonadotropin dosage during COS was 4,125 IU. She received a gonadotropin-releasing hormone agonist trigger on day 11 of COS; of 55 oocytes retrieved, 50 mature oocytes were cryopreserved. Repeat AMH testing 11 months after discontinuing CHC was 22.4 ng/mL (160 pmol/L).ConclusionCombined hormonal contraceptive may result in profound suppression of ovarian reserve markers even after 6 months of discontinuation; however, rapid reversal of ovarian suppression and hyperresponse may occur once exogenous gonadotropins are administered.

Combined hormonal contraceptive (CHC) methods are used widely by reproductive-aged women for contraceptive and noncontraceptive purposes (1). These hormonal contraceptives comprise estrogen and progestin, and are administered in various forms including oral pills, transdermal adhesive patches, and vaginal rings. Long-term CHC does not seem to have a permanent impact on future fecundity but can cause a transient and reversible decrease in antimüllerian hormone (AMH) and antral follicle counts (AFCs) (2, 3, 4). Upon discontinuation of CHC, ovarian suppression generally reverses but may require up to 6 months before ovarian reserve parameters return to precontraceptive levels (5, 6, 7).

The suppressive effect of CHC on ovarian reserve is especially relevant in patients undergoing controlled ovarian stimulation (COS) for planned oocyte cryopreservation (OC) where ovarian suppression can be associated with an attenuated response to exogenous gonadotropins and lower oocyte yields (5, 6). Therefore, it is not uncommon for clinicians to advise patients to stop CHC before planned OC (5, 7, 8, 9). The benefits of this strategy have been supported by studies highlighting increased AFCs and oocyte yields in patients undergoing COS after a CHC break compared with those without (5, 7). Yet, the optimal duration of contraceptive cessation to improve ovarian reserve markers or ovarian response particularly in patients with long-term CHC use remains unclear. Prior publications have demonstrated that long-term CHC can cause persistent ovarian suppression for several months even after discontinuation, resulting in a false positive diagnosis of diminished ovarian reserve, thereby accelerating the timeline of COS for planned OC (6, 10). The resultant COS attempt invariably is suboptimal despite high dose gonadotropins because of a suppressed AFC (6, 10). Consequently, these patients may request additional COS attempts a few months later, which has been reported to be associated with a disparately higher oocyte yield presumably because of the normalization of ovarian reserve parameters (6, 10, 11). We describe a patient with profoundly suppressed ovarian reserve markers even after a 6-month break from CHC, but who exhibited an unanticipated hyperresponse during COS for planned OC. Unlike prior cases (6, 10), our patient experienced rapid reversal of ovarian suppression during COS once exogenous gonadotropins were administered.

Case report

The current submission was exempt from Review Ethics Board approval as per Mount Sinai Hospital policy. Patient consent was obtained for the publication of this case report.

A 33-year-old gravida 0 woman presented to the office to discuss planned OC. Her medical history was significant for Hashimoto’s thyroiditis for which she was on oral levothyroxine 50 mcg once daily. She was using a vaginal contraceptive ring (etonogestrel 0.12 mg/day and ethinyl estradiol 0.015 mg/day) for the past 12 years. Her menstrual cycles were regular occurring every 28 to 30 days both before and while using CHC. She was 5 feet 4 inches (162.6 cm) tall and weighed 145 pounds (65.8 kg), for a body mass index of 24.9 kg/m2 and did not exhibit any clinical signs of hyperandrogenism. Transvaginal pelvic ultrasonography demonstrated a retroverted uterus measuring of 48.1 × 72.0 × 40.4 mm with a volume of 73.3 mL (cm3). The right ovarian dimensions and volume were 26.7 × 29.8 × 21.7 mm and 9.0 mL (cm3), respectively. No antral follicles were visualized in the right ovary (Fig. 1A). The left ovary was posterior to the uterus and poorly visualized; its dimensions and volume were 14.2 × 14.6 × 10.0 mm and 1.1 mL (cm3), respectively. Only one antral follicle was seen in the left ovary (Fig. 1A). Her initial AMH level was 0.17 ng/mL (1.2 pmol/L). She returned 6 months later; her repeat follicle-stimulating hormone, luteinizing hormone, and AMH were 5.9 IU/L, 8.1 IU/L, and 0.66 ng/mL (4.7 pmol/L), respectively. Her menstrual cycles remained regular off CHC. The patient underwent further testing given her persistently low AMH, which included a karyotype (46,XX) and FMR1 gene mutation analysis (31 CGG repeats). She opted to initiate COS for planned OC with her next menstrual cycle. Luteal estrogen priming with 4 mg oral 17-β-estradiol (E2) tablets daily was started approximately 1 week before her next anticipated menstrual cycle. The COS was initiated on CD 3 with evening doses of 300 IU of follitropin α (Gonal-f EMD Serono Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany) and 150 IU of human menopausal gonadotropin (Menopur Ferring Pharmaceuticals, Toronto, ON, Canada). Her AFC at the start of COS was 9 (Fig. 1B). Once-daily gonadotropin-releasing hormone antagonist (ORGALUTRAN, Organon Canada Inc., Kirkland, QC, Canada) was introduced on the 6th day of COS. Because of the exaggerated ovarian response, she received only 300 IU of follitropin α without any human menopausal gonadotropin that evening. Her E2 levels on the day 10 of COS were 7,272.5 pg/mL (26,700 pmol/L) after which her follitropin α dose was decreased to 225 IU. On day 11 of COS, there were 6 follicles ≥17 mm (Fig. 2); her E2 levels were 7,817.3 pg/mL (28,700 pmol/L). Ovulation was triggered with 0.5 mg buserelin acetate (SUPREFACT, Xediton Pharmaceuticals Inc., Mississauga, ON, Canada). Transvaginal oocyte retrieval was performed under conscious sedation 36 hours after the trigger. A total of 55 oocytes were retrieved, of which 50 were mature and cryopreserved. Her immediate post-retrieval course was uncomplicated with no signs or symptoms of ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome. She resumed vaginal CHC approximately 11 months after her initial consultation. An AMH of 22.4 ng/mL (160 pmol/L was noted before restarting CHC. Discussion

This case report illustrates the suppressive effects of long-term CHC on ovarian reserve markers, which did not appear to normalize despite a prolonged break, yet resulted in unanticipated hyperresponse during COS.

Previous studies have highlighted that CHC can decrease AFC and AMH values within 4 weeks of use (12). These changes in ovarian reserve parameters may occur across different hormonal contraceptive preparations and routes of administration (12). It also is well established that these values can increase and then normalize after cessation of CHC (5, 7). Most data suggest that a CHC break of up to 2–4 months may be adequate to optimize ovarian response and oocyte yield during COS when compared with those undergoing COS immediately after discontinuation (5, 7). However, patients coming off long-term or higher doses of CHC may have a protracted recovery from ovarian suppression. Two independent reports exemplify this point. Fox et al. (10) reported on a 36-year-old G0 woman using CHC via a vaginal ring for 14 years. Her initial assessment revealed an AFC of 0 and undetectable AMH (<0.015 ng/mL, <0.1 pmol/L). Repeat AMH testing 6 weeks after CHC cessation was 0.114 ng/mL (0.81 pmol/L). Her first planned OC cycle was initiated 4 months after stopping CHC. Her baseline AFC was 12; of 8 oocytes retrieved, 6 were cryopreserved. Her second planned OC cycle was 2 months after the first cycle (>6 months after stopping CHC) where her AFC and AMH was 28 and 3.45 ng/mL (24.6 pmol/L), respectively. A total of 29 oocytes were retrieved and 27 were cryopreserved. In another case report (6), a G0 woman in her early 30s had been using oral CHC (ethinyl estradiol 10 mcg-norethindrone acetate 1 mg) for over 15 years. An AFC of 5 and an AMH of 0.06 ng/mL (0.43 pmol/L) was noted initially. Two months after discontinuing CHC, her AFC and AMH remained low at 5 and 0.02 ng/mL (0.14 pmol/L), respectively. She underwent a planned OC cycle resulting in 4 retrieved oocytes, of which 2 were mature and cryopreserved. Five months after stopping CHC, her AMH rose to 1.64 ng/mL (11.7 pmol/L). Her second planned OC cycle 2 months later (7 months after stopping CHC) yielded 44 oocytes; 34 were mature and cryopreserved.

Our patient had an exaggerated ovarian response despite profoundly suppressed ovarian reserve markers after stopping long-term CHC. Moreover, our patient required only one COS cycle that resulted in a high oocyte yield, whereas the aforementioned patients (6, 10) required two separate COS cycles a few months apart given the low oocyte yield in the first COS cycle. Perusal of the current case report raises important considerations. First, distinguishing between suppressed AFC and AMH levels due an extended course of CHC and a genuine diagnosis of diminished ovarian reserve because of the patient’s own biological potential can be extremely challenging (5, 6). Because most patients do not undergo AFC and AMH measurements before initiating CHC, it is almost impossible to identify the normalization of ovarian reserve parameters once long-term CHC is discontinued. One study suggested that women with an initial AFC at the median for age or above while using CHC may initiate COS for planned OC without necessarily taking a break from CHC (5). In contrast, for women with low initial AFC, waiting up to 6 months after discontinuing CHC can increase AFC and AMH levels, and potentially improve overall oocyte yield (5). An alternate strategy is to perform AFC measurements every 1–2 months and begin COS once the AFC rises to the expected median for age or after the third AFC measurement, if there is a plateau (5). However, this approach requires multiple visits and may cause unnecessary patient anxiety and financial costs. Second, the use of AFC and AMH to formulate gonadotropin dosing or predict ovarian response may be altered by long-term CHC use. Specifically, decreased AMH levels seen in CHC users reflects the suppression of small antral follicles, which in turn is due to the CHC-mediated suppression of follicle-stimulating hormone (12). Further, various studies suggest that AMH levels may fluctuate more than initially thought (12, 13). For example, states of prolonged gonadotropin deficiency, as in the case of gonadotropin-releasing hormone agonist use, pregnancy, and idiopathic hypogonadotropic hypogonadism are associated with a decline in AMH levels (12, 14). In these scenarios, gonadotropin exposure subsequently can increase AFC and AMH markers. For example, Tran et al. (14) described a patient with idiopathic hypogonadotropic hypogonadism where the AFC and AMH initially were low at 0 and 0.20 ng/mL (1.43 pmol/L), but subsequently rose to 6 and 1.26 ng/mL (9 pmol/L), respectively, after undergoing treatment with gonadotropins.

We posit that the normalization of our patient’s ovarian reserve coincided with her COS cycle, which in combination with high dose gonadotropins, could have resulted in the unexpected hyperresponse seen during COS. Although there is clear evidence that AFC and AMH measurements will normalize after a sufficient break from long-CHC, it currently is unknown whether administration of gonadotropins can expedite this recovery. Our patient demonstrated an AMH increase from 0.17 (1.2 pmol/L) to 0.66 (4.7 pmol/L) ng/mL after a 6-month break from CHC. However, her AMH increased from 0.66 (4.7 pmol/L) to 22.4 (160 pmol/L) ng/mL 5 months after her planned OC cycle. A similar increase in AFC from 12 to 28 and AMH from 0.02 (0.14 pmol/L) to 1.64 (11.7 pmol/L) ng/mL over 2 months was observed in the patient described by Fox et al. (10) and our previous patient (6), respectively after their planned OC cycles. It is difficult to ascertain whether the timelines of AFC and AMH normalization in the current and previous patients (6, 10) could have been the same without high dose gonadotropin administration.

Conclusion

Altogether, this case highlights the challenges of accurately assessing ovarian reserve and response to COS in patients with long-term CHC use. The optimal gap between discontinuation of CHC and initiation of COS remains elusive and may range from 2–4 months in most patients to >6 months in patients with significant ovarian suppression (5, 6, 7, 9). Whether reversal of ovarian suppression can be expedited by the administration of exogenous gonadotropins remains unanswered. Despite the ostensible benefits of AFC and AMH as ovarian reserve markers (15), studies are needed to identify more accurate and novel biomarkers of ovarian reserve that are not impacted by CHC (16, 17). These efforts will optimize the timing of COS, dosing of gonadotropins, and prevention of iatrogenic hyporesponse or hyperresponse in patients with long-term CHC use.
