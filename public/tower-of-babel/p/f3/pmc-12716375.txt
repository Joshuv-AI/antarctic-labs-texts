Duodenal Volvulus Causing a Small Bowel Obstruction in the Setting of Superior Mesenteric Artery Syndrome
Authors: Erik E Rabin, Samantha Betman, Ryan Kuhn, Melissa Goczalk, Kristine U Makiewicz, Steven R Bonomo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroduction:Small bowel obstruction is a common surgical problem. Volvulus as a cause of obstruction is rare, and duodenal volvulus has only been described in case reports.Case Description:A 60-year-old male presented with abdominal pain and emesis in the setting of significant weight loss. He was identified to have a spontaneous duodenal volvulus with massive gastric outlet obstruction, due to superior mesenteric artery (SMA) syndrome. He underwent laparoscopy with successful reduction of the volvulus and creation of a duodenojejunostomy.Discussion:Here we review all published cases of duodenal volvulus and present the first case of duodenal volvulus in the setting of SMA syndrome, treated with a minimally invasive approach.Keywords: Duodenal obstruction, Duodenal volvulus, Intestinal volvulus

INTRODUCTION

Small bowel obstruction (SBO) is a common general surgery problem, accounting for 12–16% of all surgical admissions in patients with acute abdominal conditions.1,2 Surgical intervention is indicated when conservative management fails or when bowel compromise is suspected. Adhesive disease is the most common etiology, and accounts for an estimated 60–70% of all SBOs.3 Volvulus, rotation of the bowel around itself or its mesentery is rare,4 accounting for only 1% of bowel obstructions. The duodenum has four segments, and except for the first portion, it is retroperitoneal, and fixed to the posterior abdominal cavity.5 A spontaneous duodenal volvulus is exceedingly rare, only existing in a small number of case reports. Here we present a case of duodenal volvulus following rapid weight loss in the setting of a suspected superior mesenteric artery (SMA) syndrome and a review of all published cases of duodenal volvulus to date.

CASE DESCRIPTION

The patient is a 60-year-old male with a history of necrotizing myositis, interstitial lung disease on home oxygen and immunosuppressant therapy, and recent 80-pound weight loss, who presented with five days of intermittent epigastric pain, nausea, and emesis. On presentation, he was tachycardic and tachypneic, but normotensive. His abdomen was soft, distended and tympanitic, but nontender. Labs were notable for leukocytosis of 21.5, creatinine 1.9, bicarbonate 42, chloride 70, and lactate 8.5 which came down to 2.6 with fluid resuscitation. Computed tomography (CT) of the abdomen and pelvis demonstrated a large, fluid-filled stomach and distended first and second portions of the duodenum with subsequent decompression of the third and fourth portions. There appeared to be swirling of the mesentery near a transition point in the duodenum and the entirety of the small bowel was located on the right side of the abdomen. There also appeared to be an acute angle of the SMA branching off the aorta of 25.8°F and a significantly decreased aortomesenteric distance (AMD) of 3.9 mm (Figures 1 and 2).

The patient was given intravenous fluid resuscitation, and a nasogastric (NG) tube was placed with immediate removal of 2.5 liters of feculent appearing fluid. Because of the patient’s severe electrolyte derangements, benign abdominal examination, and improvement in his lactic acidosis, he was admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU) for resuscitation with plans for operative exploration the following day.

OPERATIVE TECHNIQUE

The abdomen was entered via a Veress needle and optical trocar at Palmer’s point, then additional laparoscopic ports were placed inferior to the umbilicus. The stomach and proximal duodenum were dilated with decompression of the distal duodenum and the entirety of the small bowel. After lifting the transverse colon cephalad, it appeared that the distal duodenum and ligament of Treitz had been rotated under the SMA into the right hemiabdomen. Because the duodenum did not cross midline, the entirety of the small bowel remained on the right side of the abdomen. There was also significant laxity of the retroperitoneal structures. The third and fourth portions of the duodenum were grasped and brought under the SMA which allowed for rotation of the small bowel into the patient's left hemiabdomen, relieving the obstruction. The small bowel was then run distally from the ligament of Treitz to the cecum and all bowel appeared viable. The posterior leaflet of the small bowel mesentery was then sutured to the overlying retroperitoneum to prevent future recurrence (Figure 3).

(A) Volvulized duodenum to the right hemiabdomen. (B) Complete reduction of small bowel. (C) Anatomic defect between mesentery and retroperitoneum after reduction of small bowel. (D) Closure of anatomic defect. (E) Closed defect. (F) Stapled duodenojejunostomy creation.

Because the etiology of this volvulus was thought to be secondary to SMA syndrome, the decision was made to perform a stapled duodenojejunostomy, which would allow enteric contents to bypass the portion of the duodenum that was compressed by the SMA. The abdomen was then desufflated, incisions were closed, and the patient was transferred back to the surgical ICU in stable condition.

The patient tolerated the procedure well, however, his postoperative course was complicated by an ileus requiring NG tube decompression and total parenteral nutrition (TPN). He was ultimately discharged home on postoperative day 13 after tolerating a general diet and having bowel function. He was seen in the outpatient clinic multiple times and recovered well. He remained stable at his five month follow up visit.

DISCUSSION

Here we present a case of a spontaneous duodenal volvulus, thought to occur in the setting of SMA syndrome. Also known as Wilkie syndrome or arteriomesenteric duodenal compression syndrome, SMA syndrome is the combination of clinical and radiographic features associated with compression of the duodenum by the SMA, resulting in duodenal obstruction. This often occurs in the setting of rapid weight loss due to loss of the retroperitoneal fat pad. Clinical features of SMA syndrome include postprandial epigastric pain, nausea, emesis, weight loss, and anorexia. Radiographically, there is a decrease in the acute angle between the SMA and the aorta, known as the aortomesenteric angle (SMA-Ao). Standard definition of this angulation varies, but a normal SMA-Ao is described between 38°F and 65°F,6 but has been reported as low as 28°F, with a normal AMD of 10–34 mm.7 CT measurements in SMA syndrome describe a SMA-Ao of 6–22°F and AMD between 2 and 8 mm.7 Dedicated CT angiography is the gold standard, with barium X rays, Ultrasound, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), endoscopy, and endoscopic ultrasonography (EUS) as additional modalities.6 Leading up to presentation, our patient had three abdominal CTs which demonstrated a progressive loss of SMA-Ao and AMD. Two months prior to presentation, he had an SMA-Ao of 36.6°F and an AMD of 7.6 mm. Two weeks prior to presentation, he had an SMA-Ao of 29.9°F and AMD of 7.2 mm, and at presentation, his SMA-Ao measured 25.8°F and the AMD was 3.9 mm, with clear loss of the retroperitoneal fat pad. In this time, the patient remained clinically symptomatic, had reduced AMD distance, and demonstrated a progressive narrowing of the SMA-Ao angle, suggestive of an SMA syndrome. It was not until the day of admission that the SMA angle was very narrow and the duodenum had shifted to the right. We suspect the patient’s pathology developed after a period of weight loss resulting in significant laxity of the retroperitoneal structures, including the ligament of Treitz and narrowing of the SMA-aorta angle. As SMA syndrome developed, he had significant gastric distention, and the lack of fixation from the ligament of Treitz caused the duodenum to eventually rotate under the SMA. This rotation of the duodenum caused all of the small bowel to move into the right hemiabdomen, and this ultimately caused the obstruction and volvulus.

Comparing multiple CT scans helped confirm the acuity and underlying etiology of this patient’s duodenal volvulus. Diagnostic criteria for duodenal volvulus are based on clinical suspicion in the setting of an acute obstruction with a whirl or “corkscrew” of the distal duodenum on CT. This is accompanied by proximal duodenal dilatation with a transition point at the distal duodenum.7 All of these findings were present in our patient, with the addition of operative confirmation of a rotation of the duodenum around the SMA axis and return to normal anatomy following untwisting and reduction of the duodenum. Within the variations of duodenal anatomy, one may think the etiology of this pathology could be due to a right paraduodenal hernia (RPDH). This variation usually develops in the setting of a congenital defect where both the jejunum and colon herniate, and the duodenum stays fixed anteriorly to the sac.8 CT scans demonstrating a RPDH hernia often have an encapsulated cluster of jejunum to the right of midline with mesenteric vessels stretched along its anterior margin. Operatively, RPDH hernia reduction often requires widening or dissection of the defect/sac.6 Unlike a right para-duodenal hernia, there was no hernia sac behind the SMA, no encapsulated portion of jejunum or colonic mesentery involvement and the obstructing structure was the twisted distal duodenum itself (Figure 4). In our case, the radiographic and operative findings meet our definition of a spontaneous duodenal volvulus, and the acute change from normal prior imaging confirms this. Therefore, we can maintain that this is a duodenal volvulus, likely precipitated by significant weight loss, thinning of the mesenteric fat pad, and laxity of the ligament of Treitz.

To date, seven cases of duodenal volvulus have been reported in the literature (Table 1). Of these cases, five occurred in elderly patients between the ages of 79 and 100.9–12 Previous case reports attribute the cause of the duodenal volvulus to progressive downward migration of the duodenum, age related laxity of the connective tissues, and loss of intra-abdominal fat.9–11 The youngest patient with a reported duodenal volvulus, discussed in Ribeiro et al, was 18 years old and had a 22-km weight loss prior to the development of the volvulus.14

Shared among these reports is advanced age and/or weight loss, establishing predisposing factors for the development of duodenal volvulus in the susceptible patient. In previous reports, duodenal volvulus in the absence of ischemia, has been treated either nonoperatively with NG tube decompression or with an exploratory laparotomy and surgical fixation of the small bowel mesentery to the retroperitoneum. Here we report the first case of a duodenal volvulus treated with laparoscopic fixation of the duodenum in its anatomic position and creation of a duodenojejunostomy to treat the inciting SMA syndrome.

Spontaneous duodenal volvulus is an extremely rare phenomenon, only occurring in a small number of case studies. Most patients present with obstructive symptoms in the setting of significant weight loss or advanced age. In the absence of bowel ischemia, nonoperative management can be considered. However, if surgery is indicated for an acute duodenal volvulus, a laparoscopic approach can be utilized as first line surgical treatment.
