Mechanisms of synergistic action between olaparib and KPT-330 in pancreatic cancer
Authors: Yuntao Yang, Yuying Zhang, Yajing Zhang, Hang Su, Huixiang Dong, Xiaohan Jin, Shiqiang Tai, Zhengchao Shen, Zhiyuan Chen, Hao Xie, Da Fu, JiaBin Jin, Xiaoming Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) is a highly aggressive and prognostically poor cancer driven by multifaceted mechanisms, notably immune evasion and drug resistance. The majority of PDAC patients present with unresectable locally advanced or metastatic disease, emphasizing a critical unmet need for effective therapies against advanced-stage pancreatic cancer. Despite the proven efficacy of the PARP inhibitor Olaparib against pancreatic cancer in patients carrying BRCA mutations, its practical use is limited by the development of acquired resistance. The clinical application of the PARP inhibitor Olaparib is limited by acquired resistance, making combination therapy a promising strategy to improve treatment responses. Exportin 1 (XPO1) is frequently overexpressed in PDAC cells, representing a therapeutic target, and its inhibitor KPT-330 has demonstrated antitumour potential. Therefore, we aim to combine Olaparib with KPT-330 to enhance its therapeutic effect against PDAC.ObjectiveThis study aimed to investigate the synergistic antitumour effects of the combination of Olaparib and KPT-330 against PDAC and to elucidate the underlying molecular mechanisms.MethodsWe applied RNA sequencing to pancreatic cancer cell lines following combination therapy to identify differentially expressed genes. Separately, Western blotting was performed to measure both the expression and phosphorylation status of key proteins within the involved signaling cascades.ResultsThe combination of KPT-330 and Olaparib significantly suppressed PDAC cell viability and tumour growth. Mechanistic investigation revealed that the combination specifically reduced SGPP2 levels, leading to inhibition of the IL-6/JAK2/STAT3 signaling axis. Evidence included diminished p-JAK2 and p-STAT3, with no alteration in total JAK2 or STAT3. This SGPP2-associated synergistic effect may overcome olaparib resistance by modulating the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment.ConclusionThis study reveals that the combination of Olaparib and KPT-330 enhances antitumor efficacy in pancreatic cancer by downregulating SGPP2, a tumor-associated protein, thereby inhibiting the IL-6/JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathway. SGPP2 is proposed as a key molecular node linking the two agents. Collectively, these findings provide preclinical evidence that the Olaparib and KPT-330 combination acts synergistically via the SGPP2/STAT3 axis, supporting its potential application in pancreatic cancer combination therapy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s10565-026-10219-7.

Introduction

PDAC is a highly lethal cancer. Across all clinical stages, the median overall survival is roughly 4 months (Jiang et al. 2025; Zhang et al. 2025). This poor prognosis stems from its highly aggressive biology and asymptomatic early course, such that more than half of patients have distant metastases at diagnosis (Jiang et al. 2025). Approximately 80–85% of cases are deemed unresectable at presentation, and local or distant recurrence is common (Daamen et al. 2019). Even in cases that are not locally advanced, early systemic dissemination is common, and effective systemic control remains challenging because of the limited efficacy of conventional chemotherapy. Current clinical interventions for PDAC—including surgery, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy regimens such as gemcitabine or FOLFIRINOX—exhibit limited efficacy in advanced-stage disease, with median survival outcomes remaining unsatisfactory. This therapeutic challenge largely originates from PDAC’s pronounced tumor heterogeneity, the physical and biochemical barrier posed by its dense fibrotic stroma, and the consequent profoundly immunosuppressive microenvironment. Together, these factors undermine the effectiveness of conventional chemotherapy and single-agent targeted therapies, while also driving rapid acquisition of drug resistance. Therefore, moving beyond monotherapy toward synergistic combination strategies that concurrently target tumor cells, stromal architecture, and immune dysregulation has emerged as a vital direction and an urgent unmet need in overcoming therapeutic resistance in PDAC. According to the 2022 Chinese national guidelines on pancreatic cancer diagnosis and management (Zhou et al. 2022), patients who carry germline BRCA1/2 mutations, maintain a good performance status, and show no disease progression after at least 16 weeks of initial platinum-based chemotherapy are eligible for maintenance treatment with the PARP inhibitor Olaparib. Although treatment advances, including neoadjuvant chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and improved surgical techniques, have been introduced, outcomes remain dismal.Monochemotherapy yields objective response rates less than 20%, which is far from adequate for meeting clinical needs.Preclinical studies have demonstrated that Olaparib combined with gemcitabine (GMZ) significantly suppresses tumour growth and prolongs survival in mouse models (Quiñonero et al. 2022).

The PARP inhibitor Olaparib prevents the repair of DNA single-strand breaks, raising the risk of double-strand breaks. In BRCA-mutant cells lacking functional homologous recombination repair, these unresolved double-strand breaks lead to cell death. This mechanism makes Olaparib particularly valuable for pancreatic cancer patients with BRCA mutations, and clinical evidence confirms its strong efficacy in those with unresectable locally advanced or metastatic disease (Park et al. 2021). According to the study, among patients resistant to gemcitabine, the response rate reached 50%, 25% achieved disease stabilization lasting at least 8 weeks, and the median overall survival was 9.8 months (Golan et al. 2019). Phase III trial results showed that Olaparib maintenance significantly extended median progression-free survival to 7.4 months, versus 3.8 months with placebo (Zhu et al. 2020). Consequently, the FDA approved Olaparib for BRCA-mutant pancreatic cancer patients (Louvet et al. 2019). For this subset of PDAC patients, Olaparib remains the only targeted agent approved for use. However, when given alone, its therapeutic effect is limited; it fails to provide a clear overall survival benefit in individuals without BRCA mutations. Both de novo and acquired resistance occur frequently, and in over 80% of cases, combination chemotherapy is hampered by pronounced intolerance (Javle et al. 2021). Nonetheless, Olaparib represents a foundational therapy, forming a basis for establishing rational combinations with immunomodulators or signalling pathway inhibitors.

KPT-330 (selinexor) – a newly developed agent that inhibits the nuclear export protein XPO1 – has demonstrated activity against PDAC and may serve as a valuable addition to Olaparib therapy. This compound exerts its antitumor effects specifically by blocking XPO1 (Uddin et al. 2023). XPO1 is frequently overexpressed in PDAC and other malignant cells, where it regulates the nucleocytoplasmic transport of proteins involved in tumour proliferation and survival (Wang et al. 2024). Preclinical studies have demonstrated that KPT-330 effectively suppresses PDAC cell proliferation and induces apoptosis in a concentration-dependent manner (Marijon et al. 2021). Mechanistically, KPT-330 promotes the nuclear retention of tumour suppressor and proapoptotic proteins, leading to growth arrest and cell death (Azmi et al. 2020). Although clinical data on KPT-330 in PDAC remain limited, its unique mechanism of action and robust efficacy in vitro support its exploration as a promising therapeutic strategy (Azmi et al. 2017; Liu et al. 2021; Gravina et al. 2014). Given the aggressive nature and poor treatment outcomes of PDAC, novel approaches are urgently needed. Therefore, investigating the combination of Olaparib with KPT-330 is a rational approach, and elucidating the underlying mechanisms is essential for improving patient outcomes.

SGPP2 is as a key enzyme in the sphingolipid metabolic pathway and is responsible for maintaining the intracellular balance between sphingosine-1-phosphate (S1P) and sphingosine. Growing evidence indicates that sphingolipid metabolism is closely associated with multiple malignant cancer phenotypes (Corsetto et al. 2023). We propose a plausible mechanism underlying the synergy between Olaparib and KPT-330: their combined action may disrupt the nucleocytoplasmic transport of SGPP2-related transcription factors or mRNAs, leading to coordinated downregulation of SGPP2 expression. Reduced SGPP2 levels disturb the metabolic equilibrium of S1P, which has been identified as a potent activator of several pro-survival signalling pathways, including the IL-6/JAK/STAT3 pathway (Lee et al. 2010). We therefore hypothesize that SGPP2 downregulation attenuates the activity of the IL-6/JAK/STAT3 signalling axis by altering the levels of S1P and related lipid signalling molecules.

Whether Olaparib and KPT-330 exhibit synergistic antitumour effects and increase the sensitivity of cancer cells to Olaparib are current focuses of research. KPT-330 together with Olaparib has been found to act synergistically in preclinical ovarian cancer research, as shown by in vitro and in vivo experiments. Nonetheless, the detailed molecular basis for this interaction is not yet fully clear (Handley et al. 2021). A recent glioblastoma study reported that KPT-330 suppresses the repair of DNA double-strand breaks caused by Olaparib, magnifies DNA damage, and boosts Olaparib sensitivity, culminating in a synergistic anticancer effect (Wang et al. 2024). Whether this combination exerts synergistic activity specifically in PDAC cells has not been reported before.

In our study, we confirmed strong synergistic effects (CI < 1) between the two agents in PDAC cell lines. In vitro experiments further revealed that combination treatment downregulated SGPP2 gene expression. Reducing SGPP2 expression led to decreased cell proliferation, induction of apoptosis, and blockade of the downstream IL-6/JAK2/STAT3 signaling axis. In agreement with these observations, animal experiments showed that the combination therapy achieved greater inhibition of tumor growth and extended survival relative to either single-agent or vehicle groups. Mice receiving control shRNA (NC) developed larger subcutaneous tumors and had shorter lifespans than those in the shSGPP2 group. Taken together, these results indicate that SGPP2 plays an essential role in mediating the cooperative anticancer effect of Olaparib plus KPT-330, and suggest a viable approach to circumvent Olaparib resistance in PDAC.

Materials and methods

Clinical samples

FFPE tumor samples and clinicopathological data were gathered from PDAC patients at the First Affiliated Hospital of Wannan Medical University (2014–2023). Postoperative pathology confirmed each diagnosis. Patients with prior radiotherapy, chemotherapy, or immunotherapy were ineligible. Further exclusions covered coexisting malignancies or severe systemic illness. The ethics committee of the university approved the protocol. Histopathological evaluation relied on immunohistochemistry (IHC).

Cell lines

We acquired the human PDAC cell line PATU-8988 and the murine PDAC line Pan02 from the Chinese Academy of Sciences’ Shanghai Cell Bank. All lines were maintained according to the suppliers’ instructions. With informed consent, we isolated circulating tumor cells (CTCs) from a PDAC patient, named SIC 002, and cryopreserved them at Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine. Routine culture used RPMI-1640 containing 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin.

RT–qPCR

We extracted RNA with the RNeasy Plus Kit (Qiagen) as directed. cDNA was synthesized using SuperScript IV Reverse Transcriptase (Invitrogen). For qPCR, we employed Brilliant III Ultra-Fast SYBR Green Master Mix (Agilent Technologies) with primers targeting SGPP2 and GAPDH. Relative expression levels were calculated by normalizing to GAPDH and applying the 2-ΔΔCt formula. Each experiment was repeated three times.

Western blotting

We lysed cells in RIPA buffer (ABclonal, Wuhan, China) containing 1% protease/phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (MCN Biotech). The lysates were chilled on ice for 10 min, then heated at 95 °C for another 10 min. Protein samples were separated via 10% SDS-PAGE, transferred onto PVDF membranes, and subsequently blocked. The membranes were then incubated at 4 °C overnight with primary antibodies specific for SGPP2, IL-6, JAK2, p-JAK2 (Tyr1007/1008), p-STAT3 (Tyr705), GAPDH (Desclone, A10011), and β-actin (Desclone, AC004). After treatment with HRP-linked secondary antibodies, signals were developed using an ECL system. Each assay was performed three times.

Gene knockdown and overexpression

For transient knockdown, SGPP2-targeting shRNA (sense: 5′-GCCATGGCGGAGCTGCTGC-3′) and negative control shRNA (5′-CCTAAGGTTAAGTCGCCCT-3′) were synthesized by GenePharma (Shanghai, China). Transfection used Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 10 nM shRNA, with cell harvest after 48 h. For stable knockdown, two independent shRNAs against human SGPP2 (NM_152386.4) were cloned into pLKO.1 lentiviral vector (Addgene #8453) using AgeI/EcoRI: shSGPP2-1 (coding region, near start codon): CCGGGCCATGGCGGAGCTGCTGCGGCTCGAGCCGCAGCAGCTCCGCCATGGCTTTTT.shSGPP2-2 (3′-UTR): CCGGACAGATACCAGTATCCATTTGCTCGAGCAAATGGATACTGGTATCTGTTTTTT.

Scrambled shNC (5′-CCTAAGGTTAAGTCGCCCTCGCTC-3′) served as negative control. Lentivirus was produced by co-transfecting HEK293T cells with shRNA, psPAX2 and pMD2.G plasmids (Addgene). SIC 002 and PATU‑8988 cells were transduced with lentivirus plus 8 μg/mL polybrene, then selected with puromycin (2 μg/mL) for 7 days. Knockdown efficiency (> 70% mRNA reduction) was confirmed by qPCR and Western blotting (Fig. 5A–C). To minimize off‑target effects, all assays used both shSGPP2 constructs; only consistent phenotypes were considered on‑target. For overexpression, the full coding sequence of SGPP2 (NM_152386.4, nt 61‑1260) was inserted into pcDNA3.1(+) (Invitrogen). Transient transfection used Lipofectamine 3000, and overexpression was verified by Western blotting (Fig. 8E).

RNA sequencing

PATU-8988 cells in the logarithmic growth phase were divided into four treatment groups: DMSO control, 0.19 μM selinexor (KPT-330), 18.29 μM Olaparib, and their combination. After 72 h of treatment, the cells were washed twice with cold PBS, trypsinized, and pelleted by centrifugation. Total RNA was isolated using TRIzol reagent. Each group consisted of three biological replicates. RNA samples were stored at −80 °C and submitted to Wuhan MetWare Biotechnology Co., Ltd., for quality control, library construction, and high-throughput sequencing.

Inhibitors

We purchased Olaparib and selinexor from MedChemExpress (MCE, USA). HPLC analysis confirmed that both compounds had a purity of less than 99% (i.e., > 99%). Each drug was dissolved in high-purity DMSO to generate 50 mmol/L stock solutions. These stocks were filter-sterilized through 0.22 μm membranes, separated into aliquots, and stored at −80 °C to minimize freeze–thaw damage.

Growth inhibition assay

We plated 5,000 cells per well in 96-well plates. After the cells attached, we exposed them to selinexor alone (0–6.4 μmol/L), Olaparib alone (0–128 μmol/L), or a combination of both for 72 h. To measure cell viability, we used the CCK-8 assay (Dojindo, Japan) and recorded absorbance at 450 nm. IC₅₀ values were derived by fitting a nonlinear regression model in GraphPad Prism. CI values were obtained with CompuSyn software (version 1.2.0). Each condition was tested in triplicate.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

We used IHC to measure SGPP2 and Ki67 in xenograft tumors from four treated groups, one NC group, and two SGPP2-knockdown groups. After deparaffinizing 4-μm sections in xylene, we rehydrated them through a graded ethanol series. Citrate buffer was applied for antigen retrieval, and 1.5% H₂O₂ blocked peroxidase activity. The sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies to SGPP2 and Ki67, then with an HRP-conjugated secondary antibody for 30 min at room temperature. Staining was visualized with DAB, followed by hematoxylin counterstaining, dehydration, and mounting.

Colony formation assay

PATU-8988 and SIC 002 cells were plated in 6-well plates at 1,000 cells per well and cultured for two weeks. Colonies were then fixed and stained with 0.1% crystal violet (Beyotime, China), and those larger than 1 mm in diameter were counted. Each experiment was repeated three times.

Wound healing assay

We seeded PATU-8988 and SIC 002 cells in 6-well plates (1 × 10⁶ cells/well). Once the cultures reached full confluence, we switched to a low-serum medium (5% FBS) to limit the influence of cell division on the assay. A scratch was introduced with a 200-μL pipette tip, and non-adherent cells were removed by PBS washing. Images were taken at the start (0 h) and after 24 h using a phase-contrast microscope. In the mitomycin C-treated group, cells received 10 μg/mL mitomycin C in 1% FBS medium for 2 h before scratching, were then washed twice with PBS, and replenished with fresh 1% FBS medium. Supplementary Fig. S1A–D displays representative images of wound closure under these low-serum, mitomycin C-pretreated conditions. We quantified the remaining wound area with ImageJ. Each condition was tested three times.

Cell cycle analysis

We treated PATU-8988 and SIC 002 cells for 48 h with DMSO (control), selinexor (0.19 μM), Olaparib (18.29 μM), or their combination. After treatment, we collected the cells, fixed them in 70% ethanol, and stained them with propidium iodide (PI). A BD FACScan flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, USA) was used to determine cell cycle distribution. Each experiment was performed in triplicate.

Bioinformatics analysis

We explored gene expression alterations across multiple datasets. To identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in PATU-8988 cells, we applied the DESeq2 R package to normalized RNA-seq data. Public expression data were retrieved from three sources: TCGA (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/), GEO (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/), and the EMBL-EBI Expression Atlas (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/gxa/home). The TCGA-PAAD cohort contributed 150 histologically proven PDAC tumor specimens (“pancreas-adenocarcinoma ductal type”) alongside 4 normal pancreatic tissue samples. RSEM-derived level 3 RNA-seq counts were log₂-transformed and median-centered. The GEO entry GSE62452, based on the Affymetrix Human Gene 1.0 ST Array (GPL6244), includes 69 PDAC tumors and 61 paired non-cancerous tissues. Transcripts showing zero or very low abundance in more than 20% of samples were filtered out; the remaining data were normalized using the Robust Multi-array Average (RMA) approach. Expression profiles of 46 pancreatic cancer cell lines came from the EMBL-EBI Cell Expression Atlas, processed by the standard Atlas pipeline (log₂-transformed values). Survival analysis additionally employed the GSE131027 dataset (25 PDAC samples). When needed, cross-dataset batch effects were corrected with the ComBat algorithm. DEGs were identified using both DESeq2 and edgeR. Functional annotation of DEGs was carried out by GO and KEGG pathway enrichment via ClusterProfiler. GSEA was run with the MSigDB hallmark (H) and curated (C2) gene sets. Heatmaps were drawn using the ComplexHeatmap package.

xenografts and/or CTC-derived xenografts (CDX/CTCX)

Pan02 cells (5 × 105) or SIC 002 NC/shSGPP2 cells (2 × 10⁶) were resuspended in 100 μL of PBS and injected subcutaneously into the right flank of 5- to 6-week-old female BALB/c nude mice.

Ten days post-injection, the animals were allocated to the following groups: Experiment 1: control, Olaparib alone, KPT-330 alone, and combination (n = 6 per group).Experiment 2: NC, shSGPP2-1, and shSGPP2-2 (n = 6 per group).

An additional six mice per group were kept for survival observation until spontaneous death or when the predefined ethical endpoint was met.

Both Olaparib and KPT-330 were prepared at 5 mg/mL in a vehicle containing 4% DMSO, 30% PEG 300, and ddH₂O. The drugs were injected intraperitoneally every other day for 20 days, at doses of 100 mg/kg (Olaparib) and 25 mg/kg (KPT-330). Body weight and tumor dimensions were recorded twice weekly.

Twenty-four hours after the last dose, mice were humanely euthanized by carbon dioxide overdose followed by cervical dislocation. Tumors were dissected, weighed, and then fixed in formalin, embedded in paraffin, sectioned, and processed for immunohistochemistry.

All animal procedures followed the guidelines of the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC, www.iacuc.org) and were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Shanghai Jiao Tong University (Approval No. A2025094-002) before the study commenced.

Statistical analysis

We plotted Kaplan–Meier curves and compared them using the log-rank test. Multiple-group comparisons employed one-way or two-way ANOVA, whereas unpaired t-tests were used for other comparisons. The threshold for statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Data are shown as the mean ± SD.

Ethical approval and informed consent

Participants signed written informed consent before joining the study, adhering to the Declaration of Helsinki. The protocol was approved by the IRB of the First Affiliated Hospital of Wannan Medical University (Approval No. 2024223).

Results

IC₅₀ and combination index (CI) values of olaparib and KPT-330 in pancreatic cancer cells

We used the CCK-8 method to evaluate how Olaparib and KPT-330 affect the viability of three PDAC cell lines (SIC 002, PATU-8988, PANC-1). For Olaparib, the IC₅₀ ranged from 19 to 40 μM; for KPT-330, it ranged from 0.02 to 3 μM (Fig. 1A–F). Given that Olaparib monotherapy often yields suboptimal anticancer efficacy, we next assessed the combined effect of Olaparib and KPT-330 on cell viability. Subsequently, we examined their synergistic impact on PDAC cell proliferation. The results demonstrated that both drugs inhibited cell proliferation in a dose-dependent manner. Importantly, the combination of Olaparib and KPT-330 significantly enhanced this inhibitory effect compared with either agent alone in SIC 002 (Fig. 1A), PATU-8988 (Fig. 1C), and PANC-1 (Fig. 1E) cells. Compusyn synergy analysis confirmed a synergistic interaction between the two drugs in all three cell lines. Combination olaparib and KPT-330 synergistically impairs PDAC cell functions

Cell growth, motility, and invasiveness play critical roles in tumor development and progression. We thus tested the impact of Olaparib and KPT-330, alone and in combination, on these behaviors. Colony formation assays comparing the control, single-drug, and combination groups showed that the combined treatment was more effective at inhibiting cell proliferation than monotherapy (Fig. 2A, B). To limit the influence of proliferation on the assay, we performed wound healing experiments in low-serum medium (1% FBS) after treating cells with 10 μg/mL mitomycin C for 2 h (Supplementary Fig. S1A–D). Under these conditions, the combination of Olaparib and KPT-330 significantly slowed wound closure compared to each drug alone, pointing to an inhibitory effect on migration (Fig. 2C–F). Cell cycle analysis also showed that the combined treatment caused a larger G1 arrest in SIC 002 cells than either single agent (Fig. 2G–J). A similar increase in G2 and S phase arrest was observed in PATU-8988 cells after combination treatment. Integrated analysis of five independent RNA-seq datasets revealed a common list of 12 downregulated candidate genes (Fig. 3A). The cluster heatmap (Fig. 3B), PCA scatter plot (Fig. 3C), and volcano plot (Fig. 3D–H) supported consistent transcriptional changes. On the basis of their clinical relevance in PDAC, SGPP2, OASL, and STAC3 were selected as potential downstream regulators. Prediction and validation of downstream pathways of SGPP2 in PDAC cells

To further investigate how the downregulation of SGPP2 expression after combination treatment affects therapeutic efficacy in PDAC, we performed bioinformatics analysis on genes related to downstream pathways of SGPP2. Identifying SGPP2 as a crucial molecular node linking the synergistic effects of Olaparib and KPT330 necessitates understanding the downstream network it influences to uncover the fundamental mechanism by which this combination therapy suppresses tumors. These analyses aimed to identify SGPP2-associated biological processes, thereby directly explaining how its downregulation enhances antitumor effects. We performed a bioinformatics analysis of genes associated with pathways downstream of SGPP2. Volcano plot analysis revealed significant SGPP2 downregulation, with log₂FC ≈ −2.5 (fourfold downregulation) and -log₁₀ (P.adj) ≈ 20 (FDR < 1 × 10⁻2⁰), which were well above the significance threshold, indicating robust suppression of SGPP2 expression in the experimental group (Fig. 8A). A bubble chart of KEGG pathways associated with SGPP2 highlighted the IL-6–JAK–STAT3 signaling axis as particularly relevant. GSEA revealed significant enrichment of IL-6/JAK/STAT3-related genes in the high-SGPP2 expression group (ES ≈ 0.6; p = 0.0027; FDR = 0.0057), suggesting that SGPP2 knockdown potently suppresses this pro-tumor pathway (Fig. 8B–C) Garris et al. (2013). Western blotting confirmed that SGPP2 silencing markedly inhibited the activity of the IL-6/JAK2/STAT3 cascade in both SIC 002 and PATU-8988 cells. Specifically, SGPP2 knockdown reduced p-JAK2 and p-STAT3 levels compared with control, whereas total JAK2 and STAT3 remained unchanged (Fig. 8D). To examine whether SGPP2 overexpression counteracts the synergistic effect of the combination on cell survival and whether the combined regimen still suppresses p-JAK2/p-STAT3, we overexpressed SGPP2 in SIC 002 and PATU-8988 cells. Overexpression was confirmed by Western blot (Fig. 8E–G). SGPP2 overexpression did not abolish the combination-induced decrease in cell viability (Fig. 8F, G), and the combination still reduced p-JAK2 and p-STAT3 levels (Fig. 8E), further supporting the importance of this signaling axis in PDAC aggressiveness. Together, these computational and experimental results demonstrate that SGPP2 knockdown selectively blocks phosphorylation within the IL-6/JAK2/STAT3 pathway without affecting total protein levels. To further explore whether pharmacological modulation of STAT3 influences the synergistic effect of Olaparib and KPT-330, we treated SIC 002 and PATU-8988 cells with a STAT3 inhibitor (Stattic, 2 μM) alone or in combination with the two drugs. CCK-8 and colony formation assays indicated that the STAT3 inhibitor alone produced a modest reduction in cell viability. However, when Olaparib and KPT-330 were added together with the STAT3 inhibitor (triple combination), the suppression of cell proliferation was significantly enhanced compared with the two-drug regimen (Supplementary Fig. S1E–G). Western blot analysis confirmed that the STAT3 inhibitor effectively lowered p-STAT3 levels; nevertheless, adding the STAT3 inhibitor to the two-drug combination did not reduce p-STAT3 below the level achieved by the two drugs alone, consistent with the observation that the combination already potently inhibited STAT3 phosphorylation (Supplementary Fig. S1H). These results suggest that although STAT3 activation contributes to the synergistic antitumor effect, it is not the sole mechanism, as other signaling pathways are likely involved.

Discussion

Though the specific pathogenesis of PDAC remains unknown, it is known to be influenced by multiple mechanisms, including immune evasion and drug resistance mediated by posttranslational modifications (Gong et al. 2024). Research shows that the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment in pancreatic cancer, marked by low T-cell infiltration and mutational burden, grants immune privilege, allowing evasion of host immune surveillance (Pu et al. 2021). A substantial proportion of PDAC patients present with unresectable disease, approximately 50% with locally advanced disease and 30% with metastatic tumours, resulting in an overall unresectability rate exceeding 85% (Gilbert et al. 2017). These statistics underscore the urgent need to develop effective treatments for advanced and metastatic PDAC. Nevertheless, improving therapeutic outcomes in patients with PDAC remains a profound clinical challenge.

Olaparib is indicated as first-line maintenance for patients with germline BRCA1/2 mutations who show no disease progression after at least 16 weeks of platinum-based chemotherapy. However, most patients derive only modest benefit from Olaparib as a single agent because of acquired resistance. One promising strategy to overcome this limitation is combination therapy.In the phase III POLO trial, compared with placebo, maintenance Olaparib nearly doubled the median progression-free survival time to 7.4 months versus 3.8 months (Kindler et al. 2022). In light of the complex pathophysiology of PDAC, Olaparib is frequently used in combination regimens. Preclinical studies have shown that Olaparib combined with gemcitabine significantly suppresses PDAC cell function and tumour growth both in vitro and in vivo (Quiñonero et al. 2022). TOPBP1 could act as a predictive marker to improve the response to Olaparib and AZD6738 combination therapy (Tang et al. 2025). Additionally, Olaparib boosts radiation-triggered type I interferon signaling and adaptive immunity, thereby rendering PDAC more sensitive to αPD-L1 treatment — an approach currently being tested in clinical trials (Valvo et al. 2025). FDI-6 overcomes adaptive resistance to Olaparib through the suppression of genes involved in cell cycle control and DNA damage repair, such as FOXM1, PARP1/2, BUB1, CDC25A, and BRCA1 (Wu et al. 2022). Using PDX models with BRCA1/2 mutations and defective homologous recombination, we found that the combination of Olaparib, radiotherapy, and AZD6738 resulted in significantly greater suppression of tumor growth compared with other treatment arms (Parsels et al. 2021). Given these findings, we focused on identifying a novel agent that synergizes with Olaparib to enhance its efficacy against PDAC.

KPT-330 exhibits advantageous pharmacological characteristics, such as low toxicity, oral bioavailability, and blood–brain barrier penetration (Landes et al. 2023; Miao et al. 2024). XPO1, a key mediator of nuclear export, is highly expressed in many malignant cells, including those of pancreatic cancer, positioning it as an attractive drug target. In a preclinical PDAC study, the specific XPO1 inhibitor KPT-330 demonstrated efficacy as a single agent and also in combination with gemcitabine. Moreover, KPT-330 and its structural analogs exhibit broad anticancer activity across various tumor types in clinical settings (Pai et al. 2021; Santiago-O'Farrill et al. 2020). The effects of these compounds on tumor stroma, angiogenesis, immune regulation, as well as their ability to synergize with chemotherapy and targeted drugs have been explored. In multiple myeloma, KPT-330 has been reported to interfere with tumor microenvironment communication by suppressing osteoclastogenesis (Tai et al. 2014). Across different tumor cell lines, dose-dependent cell killing by KPT-330 has been repeatedly observed in preclinical studies. Furthermore, the compound exhibits strong antitumor activity in both xenograft and orthotopic mouse models (Sun et al. 2014; Zheng et al. 2014; Chakravarti et al. 2024). Both translational and clinical evidence back the combination of selinexor with gemcitabine and nab-paclitaxel, which is considered an effective therapy for metastatic pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (Azmi et al. 2020). In our study, the combination of KPT-330 and Olaparib yielded CI values less than 1 in SIC 002, PATU-8988, and PANC-1 cells, indicating a synergistic interaction. We observed that combination treatment significantly suppressed PDAC cell proliferation, migration, and cell cycle progression in vitro. Consistent with these findings, in vivo experiments demonstrated that compared with the other treatments, the dual-drug regimen reduced subcutaneous tumour weight and volume, improved survival, and decreased Ki67 expression.

Previous studies have reported that elevated SGPP2 expression promotes glioblastoma cell proliferation, survival, and invasion and tumour angiogenesis by facilitating S1P production (Abuhusain et al. 2013). A 5-methylcytosine-related prognostic signature consisting of SGPP2 and six other genes has been shown to significantly correlate with hepatocellular carcinoma outcomes (Pan et al. 2022), which aligns with our current findings. At the level of fundamental cell functions, SGPP2 has been implicated in vitamin D-dependent regulation of lung function (Reardon et al. 2013). In addition, another study reported that NUDT21 directly regulates SGPP2, thereby promoting gastric cancer cell growth and spread and acting as a mediator of NUDT21’s oncogenic effects (Zhu et al. 2021). On the basis of these insights, we analysed RNA sequencing data and identified SGPP2 among the downregulated genes. Subsequent validation confirmed that SGPP2 is a downstream target of the synergistic interaction between Olaparib and KPT-330. We conducted bioinformatics analysis to explore the impact of SGPP2 downregulation on PDAC treatment efficacy, identifying the IL-6/JAK/STAT3 signaling axis as a crucial downstream pathway. Our findings indicated that SGPP2 expression, assessed through RNA, protein levels, and immunohistochemical analysis of mouse xenografts, was notably reduced in the combination treatment group compared to other groups.Experiments conducted both in vitro and in vivo demonstrated that knocking down SGPP2 inhibited the development and progression of PDAC.Mechanistic studies demonstrated that SGPP2 influences PDAC via the IL-6/JAK/STAT3 pathway, and its downregulation inhibits this signaling axis.

This study provides the first evidence that Olaparib plus KPT-330 synergistically improves PDAC outcomes by reducing SGPP2 levels and suppressing the IL-6/JAK/STAT3 cascade. These results not only present a new strategy to counteract chemotherapy resistance and the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment in PDAC but also reveal a previously unrecognized molecular mechanism. Moreover, our data position SGPP2 as a critical connector between the drug combination and the pro-survival IL-6/JAK/STAT3 axis, which plays a central role in PDAC pathogenesis, immune escape, and therapy resistance (Huo et al. 2022; Wu et al. 1861). We further demonstrated that the combination treatment markedly reduced the phosphorylation of JAK2 and STAT3, confirming the effective blockade of this key pathway. Importantly, we identified SGPP2 downregulation as the upstream event mediating this inhibitory effect. The SGPP2-associated synergistic mechanism may have potential therapeutic implications. Notably, this combination regimen appears to suppress multiple pathways: while Olaparib induces DNA damage, KPT-330 may concurrently disrupt another crucial survival mechanism in tumour cells through inhibition of the SGPP2/STAT3 axis. This multitarget strategy, resembling a “synthetic lethal” approach, could provide new avenues to overcome resistance caused by compensatory activation of individual pathways. Moreover, suppression of the IL-6/JAK/STAT3 pathway is thought to have favourable effects on the tumour microenvironment. Given that persistent STAT3 activation is recognized as a key driver of immunosuppression in PDAC, as it is typically associated with elevated levels of immunosuppressive cytokines and impaired T-cell function, our combination therapy might not only directly eliminate tumour cells but also indirectly promote a shift towards a more favourable immune milieu. This modulation could create opportunities for future combinations with immune checkpoint inhibitors and other immunotherapeutic agents.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. The precise biochemical processes through which olaparib and KPT-330 downregulate SGPP2, as well as the specific role of SGPP2 in regulating the IL6/JAK/STAT3 pathway, have yet to be completely understood.Second, the proposed SGPP2S1PSTAT3 axis presents a potential mechanistic paradox: given that SGPP2 encodes an S1P phosphatase, its downregulation would be expected to increase S1P levels, which typically activate STAT3 via S1P receptors. We did not directly measure S1P concentrations or perform epistatic validation (e.g., S1PR1 blockade, IL6 neutralization); therefore, the causal directionality of this pathway remains speculative and requires direct lipidomic and functional validation in future studies. Third, the precise effects of this combination therapy on tumour immune cell populations in vivo, and the utility of SGPP2 expression levels as predictive biomarkers for patient response, are critical questions that warrant further investigation. Our study identifies targeting SGPP2 to inhibit the IL6/JAK/STAT3 pathway as a novel and essential mechanism for the synergistic anti-PDAC effects of olaparib combined with KPT330, despite certain limitations. This discovery expands our understanding of cooperation between nuclear export inhibitors and PARP inhibitors and provides a theoretical foundation for developing novel combination therapies against refractory PDAC.
