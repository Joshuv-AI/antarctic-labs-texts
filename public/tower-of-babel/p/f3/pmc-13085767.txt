Unveiling the unexpected: refractory rickets as an uncommon presentation of tyrosinemia type I
Authors: Meenakshi B. Ramanna, Mahesh Kamate, Bhavana Koppad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTyrosinemia type I is a rare autosomal recessive inborn error of metabolism caused by deficiency of fumarylacetoacetate hydrolase (FAH), an enzyme essential for the final breakdown of tyrosine. The enzyme defect leads to accumulation of toxic metabolites, primarily affecting the liver, kidneys, and brain. Typically, diagnosed in infancy due to acute or chronic liver dysfunction, refractory rickets is an uncommon presentation. We report a child with tyrosinemia type I presenting with refractory rickets and hepatosplenomegaly but with preserved liver function.Case presentationA three-year-old child presented with bilateral limb deformities unresponsive to multiple courses of vitamin D and calcium. The child also exhibited floppiness, polyuria, and polydipsia. Evaluation revealed hepatomegaly and hypophosphatemic rickets secondary to Fanconi syndrome, later confirmed to be due to tyrosinemia type I. The child was treated with phosphate supplementation, nitisinone, and a tyrosine- and phenylalanine-restricted diet, with favorable follow-up.ConclusionTyrosinemia type I may present beyond infancy with renal-predominant manifestations such as refractory rickets and preserved hepatic function. Introduction

Tyrosinemia type I is a rare inborn error of tyrosine metabolism caused by deficiency of the enzyme fumarylacetoacetate hydrolase (FAH), leading to accumulation of fumarylacetoacetate, maleylacetoacetate, and succinylacetone [1]. These metabolites cause hepatic and renal toxicity and predispose to hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). The incidence is approximately 1 in 100,000 live births [2]. Clinical presentations range from acute hepatic failure in infancy to chronic, insidious renal manifestations later in childhood [3]. Rarely, the disease presents with hypophosphatemic rickets due to renal tubular dysfunction before overt hepatic failure.

We report a child with tyrosinemia type I presenting with refractory rickets and hepatosplenomegaly but with preserved liver function, underscoring the importance of early biochemical diagnosis and timely initiation of nitisinone therapy.

Case presentation

A three-year-old female child, born to non-consanguineous parents, presented with bony deformities in the lower limbs despite multiple therapeutic courses of vitamin D and calcium. The child later developed floppiness and inability to stand without support. History revealed polyuria and polydipsia without tetany, seizures, or family history of similar illness.

Focused examination revealed classical rachitic deformities, including wrist widening, genu varum, anterior bowing of legs, and enamel hypoplasia (Figs. 1 and 2). In view of hepatosplenomegaly, secondary causes were investigated, and urine succinylacetone was markedly elevated, confirming tyrosinemia type I (Tables 1 and 2). Whole-exome sequencing identified a pathogenic FAH variant in Exon 1 (NM_000137.4: c.1A > G; p.Met1Val), classified as pathogenic per ACMG criteria and a second variant in Exon 14(NM_000137.4:c.1222 T > G;p.Cys408Gly), classified as variant of uncertain significance per ACMG criteria.

A diagnosis of tyrosinemia type I with secondary Fanconi syndrome was established. Nitisinone was initiated at 0.5 mg/kg twice daily, with alkali therapy (potassium citrate) at 10 mEq/kg/day in three divided doses and elemental phosphorus at 30 mg/kg/day in six divided doses. Under dietitian supervision, a tyrosine- and phenylalanine-restricted diet was initiated with the target of maintaining plasma tyrosine level below 500 µmol/L. Urinary succinylacetone and plasma tyrosine levels were monitored every four weeks and the dose of nitisinone was subsequently titrated to a maintenance of 1 mg/kg/day in a single daily dose, once urine succinyl acetone levels were undetectable (Table 2). Child underwent ophthalmological screening prior to initiation of nitisinone therapy. Regular ophthalmologic evaluations were performed to monitor for corneal keratopathy; total leukocyte, platelet counts and metabolic parameters were reviewed quarterly. Dietitian oversight ensured appropriate tyrosine- and phenylalanine-restricted diet.

Serial monitoring demonstrated undetectable urinary succinylacetone, plasma tyrosine levels maintained below 500 µmol/L, and normalization of phosphate with serial lowering of alkaline phosphatase levels. Liver function tests, albumin, and coagulation profile remained within reference ranges (Table 1). Alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) remained normal, and abdominal ultrasound showed hepatomegaly(liver span 13 cm) with normal liver echogenicity and no focal lesions.

The child continues to be on regular follow-up with urinary succinylacetone, plasma tyrosine, liver function test, coagulation profile, AFP and ultrasound every six months and biennial Magnetic Resonance Imaging(MRI) of the liver for HCC surveillance. With early initiation of nitisinone, child’s liver functions have remained normal with no evidence of HCC.

Discussion

Refractory rickets represents a diagnostic challenge in pediatric practice and warrants evaluation beyond nutritional vitamin D deficiency. Failure to respond to adequate vitamin D and calcium supplementation should prompt consideration of renal phosphate wasting, renal tubular disorders, and inherited metabolic diseases. This report highlights tyrosinemia type I (HT1) as an uncommon but important cause of refractory hypophosphatemic rickets, presenting with predominant renal involvement and preserved hepatic function.

A structured approach is essential in children with refractory rickets with comprehensive biochemical assessment including serum calcium, phosphate, alkaline phosphatase, parathyroid hormone, and 25-hydroxyvitamin D levels [4, 5]. The presence of hypophosphatemia with normal vitamin D concentrations should prompt evaluation for renal phosphate wasting through measurement of urinary phosphate excretion, tubular maximum reabsorption of phosphate (TmP/GFR), and assessment of acid–base status. Concomitant findings such as normal anion gap metabolic acidosis, glycosuria, aminoaciduria, or tubular proteinuria are suggestive of proximal renal tubular dysfunction and Fanconi syndrome [6, 7]. In such scenarios, secondary causes, including inherited metabolic disorders, must be actively investigated, particularly when clinical features such as hepatomegaly, polyuria, growth failure, or neuromuscular weakness suggest a systemic etiology and guide targeted investigations.

HT1 is classically described as a disorder of infancy, most commonly presenting with acute or chronic liver failure [8]. In most reported series, hepatic manifestations dominate the clinical picture at diagnosis, while renal involvement is usually concurrent or secondary. Megdadi et al. reported that the majority of patients presented within the first six months of life with hepatic disease, and delays in diagnosis were associated with adverse outcomes [8]. Late-onset presentations are less common and are typically characterized by insidious liver disease with variable renal manifestations [9].

Renal tubular dysfunction and pathogenesis of rickets in tyrosinemia type I

The pathogenesis of rickets in HT1 is multifactorial and primarily driven by proximal renal tubular dysfunction. Deficiency of fumarylacetoacetate hydrolase leads to accumulation of toxic metabolites, particularly fumarylacetoacetate and succinylacetone, which exert direct cytotoxic effects on renal proximal tubular cells. Succinylacetone inhibits multiple tubular transport processes, resulting in Fanconi syndrome characterized by phosphaturia, bicarbonaturia, glucosuria, aminoaciduria, and tubular proteinuria [6, 7].

Chronic renal phosphate wasting is the central mechanism leading to hypophosphatemic rickets in HT1. Phosphate is essential for hydroxyapatite formation and normal mineralization of the growth plate; sustained hypophosphatemia impairs chondrocyte apoptosis and endochondral ossification, leading to widening, cupping, and fraying of the metaphyses [10].

Metabolic acidosis, secondary to proximal renal tubular acidosis, further contributes to skeletal demineralization. Chronic phosphate wasting impairs growth plate mineralization, while metabolic acidosis further exacerbates bone demineralization by increasing calcium and phosphate efflux from bone [10]. Unlike nutritional rickets, serum calcium levels are often normal, and 25-hydroxyvitamin D concentrations are typically within reference ranges, explaining the lack of response to vitamin D supplementation alone. This underscores the importance of biochemical evaluation, including assessment of phosphate handling and tubular function, in children with treatment-resistant rickets.

The combined effects of phosphate depletion, metabolic acidosis, and impaired vitamin D activation result in severe, treatment-resistant rickets and muscle weakness, as observed in the present case.

Clinical relevance and literature context

While rickets is a recognized complication of HT1, it is rarely the dominant presenting feature. Zengin et al. reported rickets in several patients with HT1; however, all cases were associated with biochemical liver dysfunction [11]. Similarly, previously reported late-onset cases have demonstrated hepatic involvement at diagnosis [9]. In contrast, the present case is notable for a renal-dominant presentation, with refractory rickets and Fanconi syndrome occurring in the absence of biochemical liver dysfunction, despite hepatosplenomegaly.

This atypical presentation may lead to diagnostic delay, particularly in regions where nutritional rickets is common. Recognition of hypophosphatemia, normal vitamin D levels, and evidence of proximal tubular dysfunction should prompt evaluation for metabolic disorders, including HT1.

Early diagnosis of HT1 is critical given the availability of effective disease-modifying therapy. The introduction of nitisinone has transformed the prognosis of Tyrosinemia type I, improving survival to over 85% when initiated early [10, 12, 13]. Nitisinone inhibits 4-hydroxyphenylpyruvate dioxygenase, preventing the formation of toxic downstream metabolites and leading to rapid suppression of succinylacetone. Early initiation of therapy has been shown to reverse renal tubular dysfunction, improve phosphate handling, and significantly reduce the risk of hepatocellular carcinoma [10, 13].

In the present case, prompt initiation of nitisinone, dietary tyrosine and phenylalanine restriction, and phosphate and alkali supplementation resulted in normalization of biochemical parameters and improvement in skeletal manifestations. This underscores the importance of early metabolic evaluation in children with refractory rickets.

Clinical implication

This report expands the phenotypic spectrum of HT1 and emphasizes refractory hypophosphatemic rickets as a potential early manifestation. Measurement of urinary succinylacetone should be considered in children with unexplained Fanconi syndrome or treatment-resistant rickets, particularly when hepatomegaly is present, even in the absence of overt liver dysfunction. Early recognition and treatment are essential to prevent irreversible hepatic and renal complications.

Conclusion

Tyrosinemia type I, though rare, carries high morbidity and mortality. Hypophosphatemic rickets occurring as part of Fanconi Syndrome when associated with hepatomegaly albeit with normal liver function tests, should prompt evaluation for Tyrosinemia type I. Early diagnosis through urinary succinylacetone screening and prompt initiation of nitisinone with dietary management are crucial for improved prognosis and prevention of hepatic complications.
