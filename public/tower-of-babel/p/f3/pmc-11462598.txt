Examining the effects of the HIV‐1 protein Tat and morphine on antiretroviral accumulation and distribution within the brain
Authors: Austin M Jones, Kara M Rademeyer, Elias P Rosen, Silas Contaifer, Dayanjan Wijesinghe, Kurt F Hauser, MaryPeace McRae
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractDespite combination antiretroviral therapy effectively suppressing HIV within the periphery, neuro‐acquired HIV (neuroHIV) remains a significant problem and approximately half of people living with HIV will experience HIV‐associated neurocognitive disorders (HAND). Concurrent opioid use exacerbates neuroHIV by promoting neuroinflammation, neuronal injury and synaptodendritic culling, viral replication, and potentially altering antiretroviral concentrations within the brain. The present study examined the effects of HIV and morphine co‐exposure on the accumulation and spatial distribution of antiretroviral drugs across multiple regions within the brain in an HIV‐1 Tat transgenic mouse model by using infrared‐matrix‐assisted laser desorption electrospray ionization mass spectrometry imaging (IR‐MALDESI MSI). Morphine exposure uniquely decreased antiretroviral concentrations in anterior cerebral (primary motor and somatosensory) cortices, corpus collosum (anterior forceps), caudoputamen, nucleus accumbens, and posterior regions including the hippocampus, corpus callosum (main body), cerebral cortex (somatosensory and auditory cortices), thalamus, and hypothalamus. Interestingly, male mice experienced greater morphine‐associated decreases in antiretroviral concentrations than females. The study also assessed whether changes in antiretroviral concentrations were linked with inflammation in astroglia, assessed through the measurement of astroglial activation using glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) as a marker. Alterations in antiretroviral concentrations co‐registering with areas of astroglial activation exhibited sex‐specific treatment differences. This study highlights the intricate interplay between HIV, opioids, and antiretroviral drugs within the CNS, elucidating distinct regional and sex variations in responsiveness. Our findings emphasize the identification of vulnerabilities within the neural landscape and underscore the necessity of carefully monitoring opioid use to maintain the efficacy of antiretroviral therapies.

INTRODUCTION

Although combination antiretroviral therapy (cART) effectively suppresses HIV within the periphery and improves health outcomes, up to one‐half of people with HIV experience manifestations of neuro‐acquired HIV infection (neuroHIV) including HIV‐associated neurocognitive disorders (HAND). 1 , 2 HAND encompasses a spectrum of disorders ranging from asymptomatic neurocognitive impairment to mild neurocognitive disorder to HIV‐associated dementia. 1 NeuroHIV is characterized by blood–brain barrier (BBB) integrity dysfunction, infiltration of HIV‐infected macrophages within the brain, persistent infection of CNS‐resident glia, and exposure to virotoxins, which result in inflammation, bystander neuronal injury, and the loss of synaptodendritic connectivity. 3 , 4

The HIV and opioid epidemics are interlinked, with up to 50% of initial HIV infection attributable to injection drug use. 5 , 6 Drugs with abuse liability, specifically opioids, exacerbate neuroHIV and cognitive deficits in people with HIV through enhancing neurotoxicity, increasing pro‐inflammatory cytokines, increasing recruitment of immune cells into the brain, worsening CD4 counts, and decreasing antiretroviral adherence. 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 Morphine, the major bioactive metabolite of heroin, potentiates neuroHIV through aggravating CNS inflammation, 13 , 14 increasing viral replication and viral loads, 6 , 15 and altering cell signaling ultimately leading to cellular dysfunction and death. 12

HIV‐1 Tat (transactivator of transcription) is produced and secreted by HIV‐infected cells. In addition to being required for efficient HIV replication, Tat also is capable of being internalized by, and affecting, neighboring uninfected cells. Tat is an important mediator in the development of neuroHIV, contributing to glial activation, neuroinflammation, and neuronal injury, 10 , 13 , 16 , 17 , 18 in part through increasing pro‐inflammatory cytokine release, enhancing monocyte recruitment into the brain, and decreasing BBB integrity and function. 13 These mechanisms underscore the clinical relevance of Tat, as evidenced by studies demonstrating that even in virally suppressed patients on chronic antiretroviral therapy, active Tat viral proteins remain elevated within the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and there is a significant association with a history of illicit substance use. 19

Our lab has previously reported that morphine, and to a lesser extent Tat, disrupts the BBB, resulting in an increased leakage of fluorescently labeled dextrans into the brain and decreased tight junction protein expression. 16 , 20 Additionally, Tat and morphine independently increase the recruitment of immune cells into the brain, 13 , 16 , 20 further contributing to neuroinflammation. We also have reported that morphine exposure decreases the overall brain accumulation of antiretroviral drugs in a drug‐ and region‐specific manner, 20 potentially limiting effectiveness in viral suppression. The prior work, however, only examined two brain regions. Having a better understanding of how HIV and morphine impact the concentrations and regional distribution of antiretrovirals across multiple brain structures will help develop better therapeutic strategies for the treatment of neuroHIV in the setting of opioid use/misuse by optimizing drug dosing to ensure therapeutic levels are achieved in critical brain regions.

The purpose of the present study was to examine the impact of HIV‐1 Tat and morphine on antiretroviral penetration and distribution within the brain across eight different brain structures known to be impacted by HIV. HIV preferentially infects brain regions such as the hippocampus, striatum (basal ganglia), caudoputamen, cerebral cortex, nucleus accumbens, corpus callosum, thalamus, and hypothalamus. 21 , 22 The hippocampus, crucial for declarative and spatial memory, and both the striatum and caudoputamen, essential for habit formation, are notably impacted by HIV, contributing to neuroinflammation and neuronal injury. 7 , 16 , 20 , 23 The cerebral cortex and nucleus accumbens, important for cognitive functions and reward‐seeking behavior, respectively, also show significant alterations in response to HIV/opioid exposure. 21 , 22 , 24 The corpus callosum, closely related to balance and gait function, and the thalamus and hypothalamus, both responsible for executive function and behavioral control, have also been implicated in the pathogenesis of the clinical manifestations of neuroHIV. 22 , 24 Infrared‐matrix‐assisted laser desorption electrospray ionization (IR‐MALDESI) mass spectrometry imaging (MSI) was used to measure the spatial distribution of antiretroviral drugs sampled from discrete locations across an entire tissue section. 25 MSI evaluation of the spatial distribution of drugs in tissue is critical because understanding the distribution of antiretrovirals in HIV reservoirs, where low‐level viral replication may still be occurring in the face of clinically effective antiretroviral therapy, may help guide eradication strategies. 26 The study also evaluated whether the observed changes in antiretroviral concentrations co‐localized with areas of astroglial reactivity to evaluate whether the Tat‐ or morphine‐induced astrogliosis and inflammation alter the accumulation of antiretroviral drugs.

METHODS

All studies were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at Virginia Commonwealth University, and the experiments were conducted in accordance with ethical guidelines defined by the National Institutes of Health (NIH Publication No. 85‐23).

Study design

The doxycycline‐inducible HIV‐1 Tat‐1 transgenic mice, and their appropriate controls, were used for the present studies (20 males, 19 females). Tat transgenic mice are on a C57 background and express HIV‐1 Tat1‐86 controlled by a reverse tetracycline transactivator (rtTA)‐driven glial‐specific GFAP promoter. The tetracycline responsive element (TRE) promoter triggers Tat expression by a specially formulated chow containing 6 mg/g doxycycline. Tat(+) transgenic mice express both GFAP‐rtTA and TRE‐tat genes, while control Tat(−) transgenic mice express only the GFAP‐rtTA genes. 7 Genotype‐confirmed Tat(+) and Tat(−) mice (~4 months of age, ~25 g) were placed on doxycycline chow to induce HIV‐1 Tat transcription, if applicable. The Tat transgenic model is well characterized and mimics key aspects of the HIV‐opioid interplay. 7 , 16 Tat(+) and Tat(−) mice were placed on doxycycline chow for 14 days (to induce Tat expression), followed by a 5‐day washout (no doxycycline) to minimize potential doxycycline‐antiretroviral‐morphine drug interactions. Long‐lasting effects (up to 1 month) of Tat on learning and memory after doxycycline completion have previously been demonstrated. 27

Drug administration

Triumeq®, a combination antiretroviral tablet containing abacavir (600 mg), dolutegravir (50 mg), and lamivudine (300 mg) was purchased from the VCU Health System Pharmacy. Dosing was calculated based on interspecies allometric scaling from the recommended dose in humans to mice, 28 based on an average 25 g mouse and was as follows: abacavir 2.5 mg/day (123.5 mg/kg/day), dolutegravir 0.2 mg/day (10.3 mg/kg/day), and lamivudine 1.2 mg/day (61.7 mg/kg/day); drugs were prepared as previously described. 20 Briefly, the tablets were crushed and resuspended in normal saline. After centrifugation to pellet excipients and sterile filtration, the drug solution was loaded into the ALZET® osmotic pump (Model 2001). For morphine groups, morphine salt pentahydrate powder, at a concentration sufficient to deliver 2.5 mg/day, was directly diluted into the antiretroviral solution prior to loading the pump. Drug preparations were made in batches to minimize dosing variability.

Surgical manipulation

Mice were anesthetized with isoflurane (4% induction, 2% maintenance). A small mid‐scapular entry was made in the skin and the osmotic pump, containing the antiretroviral drugs and morphine (or saline), was subcutaneously implanted. Bupivacaine was applied to surgical sites immediately post‐operation to provide local anesthesia. During tissue harvesting, mice were briefly (~2 min) transcardially perfused with cold 1× PBS to clear the brain vasculature of blood but minimize drug leaching from tissue. Brain tissue was harvested and the tissue was carefully transferred to a protective foil barrier and placed atop dry ice to freeze. Once frozen (~5 min), samples were stored at −80°C until MSI analysis.

Sample preparation

Brain samples were placed in a brain matrix (Zivic Instruments, BSMAS001‐1) to reproducibly access coronal regions that included the anterior and posterior regions. Tissues were sectioned from these two regions (Leica CM1950, Leica Biosystems, Nussloch, Germany). Serial sections (10 μm) were thaw‐mounted onto glass slides and stored at −80°C until analysis by MSI and immunohistochemistry (IHC). See Figure 1 for the sectioning plan.

MSI Analysis

MSI analyses using an IR‐MALDESI source is described in more detail elsewhere. 25 , 29 , 30 Tissue sections were placed on a thermally controlled stage in the source chamber and cooled to −9°C, allowing an ice film to form on the sample surface and remain stable under controlled humidity (RH = ~15%). Samples were ablated step‐wise in 100 μm increments by an IR OPO laser (Opotek, Carlsbad, CA; λ = 2.94 μm) and desorbed material was ionized by a perpendicular electrospray. The resulting ions were sampled into the Orbitrap QE Plus mass spectrometer (ThermoFisher Q Exactive Plus, Bremen, Germany). Analysis was conducted in positive mode, with resolving power set to 140,000 at m/z 200. The mass accuracy of better than 1 part per million (ppm) was confirmed by weekly calibration of the mass spectrometer with the calibration solution recommended by the manufacturer. Antiretrovirals were evaluated in a single tissue section using full‐scan MS mode, with mass spectra acquired with a mass range m/z 150–600, with an electrospray solvent of water and methanol (50:50 v/v) and 0.2% formic acid. Automatic gain control (AGC) function was turned off to match trapping conditions required by IR‐MALDESI MSI, and ion injection time was fixed at 11 ms. Calibration of antiretroviral response on brain tissue was performed by spiking 100 nL of standards directly onto mouse brain tissue sections collected in the same region from mice not dosed with the drug. The detection limits with MSI for each drug were estimated to be: abacavir (103 ng/g), lamivudine (1535 ng/g), and dolutegravir (11,733 ng/g).

LC–MS/MS analysis

For LC–MS/MS analysis of antiretroviral concentrations, tissue between the anterior and posterior sections was homogenized in 1 mL of 70:30 acetonitrile:1 mM ammonium phosphate (pH 7.4) using a Precellys 24 tissue homogenizer (Bertin Technologies, Montigny‐le‐Bretonneux, France). A Shimadzu HPLC system performed chromatographic separation and an AB SCIEX API 5000 mass spectrometer (AB SCIEX, Foster City, CA, USA) equipped with a turbo spray interface as the detector. The samples were analyzed with calibration standards (0.025–50.0 ng) and quality control samples. Precision and accuracy of the calibration standards and quality control samples were within the acceptance criteria of 15%.

Immunohistochemistry

Chromogenic IHC was performed on OCT‐embedded tissue sections mounted on positively charged slides. IHC was carried out using the Leica Bond III Autostainer system. Slides were hydrated in Bond Wash solution (AR9590). Heat‐induced antigen retrieval was performed at 100°C in Bond‐Epitope Retrieval solution 1 pH‐6.0 (AR9961) for 20 min, followed by a 5‐min peroxide blocking step (Biocare, IPB5000L). After pretreatment, slides were incubated with a GFAP primary antibody (Dako, Z0334) at 1:1500 followed by Novolink Polymer (Leica, RE7260‐CE) secondary. Antibody detection with 3,3′‐diaminobenzidine (DAB) and hematoxylin counterstain was performed using the Bond Intense R detection system (Leica, DS9263). Stained slides were dehydrated and coverslipped with Cytoseal 60 (23‐244256, Fisher Scientific). Positive and negative controls were included. Slides were digitally imaged with the Aperio AT2 (Leica Biosystems) using a 20× objective.

Image analysis

Following IHC, manual histological annotation was performed on brain regions within the anterior and posterior sections. To facilitate this process, each brain slice was aligned with the corresponding image in the Allen Mouse Brain Atlas (Version 2). Specifically, for the anterior sample sections, coronal images 44–53 of the Allen Atlas were used to identify four regions of interest: the corpus callosum (anterior forceps), caudoputamen, cerebral cortex (primary motor and somatosensory cortices), and nucleus accumbens. For the posterior sample sections, coronal images 66–85, depending on the individual section, were used to identify five regions of interest: the hippocampal formation, corpus callosum (main body), cerebral cortex (somatosensory and auditory cortices), thalamus, and hypothalamus. MSConvert 31 was used to convert raw signal intensity data to .mzXML files, which were loaded into the MSI program MSiReader (MATLAB application; Mathworks, Natick, MA) to generate ion distribution maps. MSiReader was used to export pixel intensity matrices for antiretrovirals, morphine, and endogenous ions of interest across the entire tissue slice into MATLAB. IHC microscopy data and annotations were downsampled to match MSI data resolution (pixel size: 100 × 100 m) and color thresholded to isolate staining from counterstain. Using MATLAB, several series of coding were performed to co‐localize both MSI and IHC images to generate drug concentrations in several regions of interest. Image co‐localization was performed by manually selecting a minimum of 8 control points between the MSI and IHC images. Once selected, the control points were used to align the images atop one another to generate regionally specific ion intensities. Additional analyses were conducted to determine if glial activation (measured by GFAP immunoreactivity) was associated with antiretroviral signal(s). This was done by masking antiretroviral signal onto the distribution of GFAP and was used to determine the proportion of drug that was co‐localized with both GFAP(+) and GFAP(−) cell populations.

Statistical analysis

Only abacavir was quantifiable by MSI, while lamivudine and dolutegravir were below detection limits. Both mean MSI abacavir concentrations in the various brain regions and LC–MS/MS data were analyzed using two‐way ANOVA for drug treatment and Tat status, with Tukey's post hoc tests for pairwise comparisons. Because the original data showed heteroscedasticity of residuals, we estimated the fitted two‐way ANOVA models with log‐transformed responses for each region in the anterior and posterior brain sections for abacavir. For the LC–MS/MS data, the dolutegravir responses were log‐transformed. The residuals of each model appear approximately normally distributed, as indicated by the diagonal alignment against normal quantiles in the QQ plot, and exhibit approximately constant variance across the fitted values in the scatterplot (Figures S1–S4).

Data and figures presented reflect the combined analysis of both sexes. An exploratory analysis was also conducted to examine sex differences by separately analyzing males and females using two‐way ANOVA. Given the small sample sizes for these separate analyses, the results should be interpreted with caution, as assumptions of normality and homoscedasticity may not be fully met. These analyses were intended to provide preliminary insights into potential sex‐specific responses rather than definitive conclusions.

Changes in abacavir concentrations within areas of gliosis

To evaluate whether abacavir concentrations were preferentially localized to regions of activated glial cells and whether Tat and/or morphine exposure influenced drug accumulation in these areas, abacavir concentrations within areas of gliosis were analyzed. Glial activation was evaluated using IHC, where brain slices immediately adjacent to the slices used for MSI were stained for GFAP, a marker for astrogliosis. Morphine and Tat effects on abacavir accumulation within areas of gliosis were determined by comparing abacavir concentrations in both GFAP+ and GFAP− cells. Areas where the ratio of [abacavir concentration in GFAP+]/[abacavir concentration in GFAP−] was greater than one represent locations where abacavir accumulation was increased within areas of gliosis. Significance was set at α = 0.05, and all analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism Version 9.4.0.

RESULTS

Only abacavir concentrations were above the MSI limits of detection, so spatial analysis of antiretroviral exposure across multiple brain regions focused on abacavir. LC–MS/MS, which has greater sensitivity, was also performed on tissue homogenates, from the tissue located between the anterior and posterior sections sampled, which allowed for quantification of all three antiretrovirals within brain tissue.

Abacavir distribution across multiple regions was measured by MSI on coronal brain sections representing two distinct areas. Abacavir concentrations in the “anterior” sections [corpus callosum, caudoputamen, cerebral cortex, and nucleus accumbens] and the “posterior” sections [hippocampal formation, corpus callosum, cerebral cortex, thalamus, and hypothalamus] were measured, with the cerebral cortex and corpus callosum being analyzed in both sections (Figure 1).

Morphine significantly reduces abacavir concentrations across multiple brain regions

Morphine significantly reduced abacavir concentrations across the anterior and posterior brain sections. In the anterior brain, abacavir concentrations were decreased in all regions, including the corpus callosum, caudoputamen, cerebral cortex, and nucleus accumbens (Figure 2). Similarly, in the posterior brain, morphine decreased abacavir concentrations across all regions, including the hippocampal formation, corpus callosum, cerebral cortex, thalamus, and hypothalamus (Figure 3). Sex differences in abacavir concentrations

An exploratory analysis was performed to examine sex differences in abacavir concentrations across both brain sections. This analysis revealed notable sex differences in the effects of morphine, but not Tat, exposure on abacavir accumulation. Morphine significantly reduced abacavir concentrations in males throughout all regions of both anterior and posterior sections. In females, however, morphine significantly reduced abacavir concentrations only in the corpus callosum (in both anterior and posterior regions), hippocampal formation, and thalamus (Figure 4). Morphine and HIV‐1 Tat exposure reduce dolutegravir concentrations in non‐region‐specific tissue homogenate analysis using LC–MS/MS

Because MSI was only able to quantify abacavir concentrations, traditional LC–MS/MS analysis was performed on tissue homogenates from the tissue between the anterior and posterior sections (Figure 1). While this approach provided the increased sensitivity needed to measure dolutegravir and lamivudine concentrations, it does not allow for the assessment of regional specificity. The median concentration and range for the antiretroviral drugs were 181.83 ng/g (0–700.16 ng/g) for abacavir, 19.10 ng/g (2.55–48.85 ng/g) for lamivudine, and 3.12 ng/g (1.03–8.24 ng/g) for dolutegravir. The primary analysis revealed a significant effect of morphine [F (1, 35) = 6.218, p = 0.0175] on dolutegravir concentrations (Figure 5). When examining sex differences, morphine decreased dolutegravir concentrations [F (1, 15) = 5.628, p = 0.0315] only within the brains of male mice. There were no significant effects of Tat or morphine on abacavir or lamivudine concentrations using this non‐region‐specific approach.

Sex‐based differences on the effects of HIV‐1 Tat ± morphine‐induced astroglial reactivity on abacavir accumulation

The influence of Tat and/or morphine on abacavir accumulation within areas of high density of activated astroglia was assessed by the ratio of immunoreactive GFAP+ cells/GFAP− cells. When males and females were grouped together, neither Tat or morphine significantly influenced abacavir distribution within areas of astroglial reactivity (Figure 6a,c). Additional analyses performed to assess potential sex differences revealed that across all brain regions within the anterior section, morphine‐treated males, irrespective of Tat status, had increased abacavir accumulation in areas of astrogliosis, (F (1, 11) = 7.369, p = 0.0201) compared with saline‐treated mice. In contrast, in females, morphine had no effect, but Tat exposure resulted in higher accumulation of abacavir in areas of gliosis compared with Tat(−) mice (F (1, 15) = 5.504, p = 0.0331) (Figure 6a–c). Thus, in male mice, morphine (but not Tat) increased abacavir accumulation in activated astroglia; while in female mice, Tat (but not morphine) increased the accumulation of abacavir within these areas of astrogliosis.

DISCUSSION

Morphine decreases abacavir concentrations across multiple brain regions

This study highlights that morphine can alter brain concentrations of antiretroviral drugs and that there are sex‐specific effects of morphine on abacavir concentrations in the brain, with more pronounced effects in male mice, and that there are some brain region‐specific variations in these effects. These findings emphasize the need for careful consideration of opioid use in individuals living with HIV, as it may reduce the efficacy of antiretroviral therapy. Morphine exposure significantly lowered abacavir concentrations across all brain regions examined. We have previously reported that within the striatum, both dolutegravir and abacavir concentrations are decreased by morphine. However, in that study, we did not find morphine‐mediated changes in abacavir within the hippocampus. 20 The prior data were generated from tissue lysates using LC–MS/MS. This method of analysis, while capable of determining drug concentrations with a high degree of accuracy, lacks the spatial resolution of MSI making it less effective in measuring drug concentrations among discrete brain regions. This limitation may partially explain the variations in results between the two studies performed in our lab.

The exploratory analyses of potential sex effects revealed that male mice appear to be more susceptible to the morphine‐mediated decreases in abacavir concentrations. Visual inspection of the data suggests abacavir concentrations in female mice are more variable than in male mice, which could be due to individual females being at different stages of their estrous cycle, 32 though it was not controlled for in this study. Furthermore, studies have reported sex differences in the pharmacokinetics of antiretroviral drugs, including abacavir, zidovudine, and lamivudine, with females exhibiting significantly higher plasma concentrations. 33 , 34 , 35 , 36 There are also sex differences in the pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics of opioids. Preclinical studies have reported lower morphine levels in females, which may contribute to poorer acute and chronic pain control by morphine in females, 37 while clinical studies suggest women progress from opioid use to dependence more quickly than men. 32 , 38 These results highlight the importance of considering sex when evaluating antiretroviral therapy and opioid use, as the observed decrease in abacavir concentrations in male mice and higher variability in female mice underscores the complex interplay between sex hormones, drug metabolism, and antiretroviral efficacy. Future studies with larger sample sizes and controlled variables, such as estrous cycle, are necessary to confirm these findings and further elucidate the impact of sex on drug response.

Morphine exposure reduces dolutegravir concentrations in non‐region‐specific tissue homogenate analysis using LC–MS/MS

Of the three antiretroviral drugs used in this study, only abacavir concentrations were above the limit of quantitation by MSI. The inability to quantify lamivudine and dolutegravir using MSI is likely attributed to their limited brain penetration due to the drugs' physical and chemical properties. The ability of a drug to penetrate the brain depends on certain factors, such as lipophilicity, molecular size, and protein binding. Although lamivudine has low protein binding (<36% protein‐bound), it is also more hydrophilic than abacavir, which may impede lamivudine's ability to cross the lipid bilayers of endothelial cells comprising the BBB. 39 Abacavir, despite being more protein‐bound (~50%), is also more lipophilic than lamivudine, which may increase its potential to penetrate the CNS. 39 Dolutegravir, however, is highly protein‐bound (> 98%), which markedly reduces the free, unbound fraction of the drug, which is essential for detection and quantification by MSI. 40 Additionally, to achieve high spatial resolution, MSI requires sampling very small and closely spaced areas of tissue. This limitation on sample size inherently restricts the amount of analyte and therefore reduces sensitivity. 41 The lipid‐rich environment of the brain can lead to substantial matrix effects, which decrease the signal‐to‐noise ratio and potentially interfere with the detection of peak concentrations. This interference can also suppress the ionization of targeted analytes, thereby diminishing MSI's relative sensitivity compared with the more robust and targeted detection capabilities of LC–MS/MS. 41 The lower sensitivity of MSI prevented a full evaluation of Tat and morphine effects on the regional accumulation of the other two antiretrovirals in this study. To better evaluate the impact of Tat and morphine on the concentrations of all the antiretrovirals administered, the mid‐portion of the brain (between the anterior and posterior brain sections examined in this study; see Figure 1) was measured using traditional LC–MS/MS. Using LC–MS/MS, the only antiretroviral drug that was significantly impacted by Tat or morphine exposure was dolutegravir. These findings are consistent with previous work conducted in our lab, where morphine exposure decreased dolutegravir in the striatum and hippocampus and decreased abacavir concentrations in the striatum of HIV‐1 Tat transgenic mice. 20 The decreases in dolutegravir and abacavir concentrations within the CNS were attributed to morphine‐induced increases in the expression and function of the efflux transporter, P‐glycoprotein (P‐gp); dolutegravir and abacavir are known substrates for P‐gp. 20 Several other studies have similarly reported increases in P‐gp protein and/or mRNA expression in response to chronic opioid exposure, including morphine and oxycodone. 42 , 43 , 44 However, some studies have observed no significant changes in P‐gp expression following morphine exposure, 45 , 46 highlighting the variability in the effects of opioids on efflux transporter regulation.

The lack of changes in abacavir concentrations using LC–MS/MS in the current study may be because we used tissue homogenates, which included multiple brain regions, potentially diluting (or negating) any regional differences in the actions of morphine. This underscores the presence of regional differences in antiretroviral concentrations and responsiveness—nuances that might otherwise be overlooked when combining regions.

HIV‐1 Tat and morphine cause preferential accumulation of abacavir within areas of astrogliosis

Astrocytes quickly become activated following a variety of insults, and the ensuing reactive astrocytosis triggers and facilitates inflammatory responses, which can result in neuronal injury and death. 47 Upon activation, astrocytes secrete cytokines and other inflammatory mediators such as reactive oxygen and nitrogen species, pro‐inflammatory lipids, and express heightened levels of the astroglial marker, glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP). 47 Increased GFAP levels are an indicator of astroglial reactivity. Morphine and HIV‐morphine co‐exposure activates astrocytes and increases GFAP levels. 7 , 14 , 35

We previously reported that morphine decreases the intracellular concentrations of the antiretroviral cocktail of tenofovir, emtricitabine, and dolutegravir in human astroglia in vitro 48 —suggesting heightened astrogliosis might reduce antiretroviral concentrations and promote latent viral reservoirs. 49 Accordingly, we anticipated astrocytes would become more reactive in mice exposed to morphine or Tat‐morphine, 7 , 14 , 35 and we predicted that morphine ± Tat‐induced astrogliosis would result in lower antiretroviral concentrations. Surprisingly, we found the opposite; abacavir accumulation was actually increased within areas of gliosis. Furthermore, Tat and morphine influenced the abacavir concentrations differently between males and females. Both had areas of accumulation of antiretrovirals, but responses varied by treatment. In males, morphine increased abacavir accumulation in areas of astrogliosis. In contrast, in females, there was preferential accumulation of abacavir in the Tat and Tat‐morphine co‐exposed mice.

A possible explanation for the observed sex‐dependent findings could be related to the differences in morphine concentrations and metabolism between sexes. Female rats have increased peripheral morphine metabolism compared with males, which may contribute to lower concentrations of active morphine in the brain, 37 , 50 although this was not tested experimentally. Accordingly, the modulatory effects of morphine and HIV‐1 Tat on abacavir concentrations could potentially impact the effectiveness of antiretroviral therapy in suppressing HIV replication within the brain and result in differential inflammatory and possibly neurobehavioral consequences.

Although this exact question has not been experimentally tested before, others report sex differences in astroglial numbers, morphology, gene expression, and function in various regions of the developing and adult CNS. 51 , 52 Recent studies have elucidated sex‐specific disparities in immune cell activation in response to nociceptive stimuli, evidencing enhanced microglial activation in males and predominant T‐cell activation in females. 53 , 54

These findings indicate the possibility of differential mechanisms governing the interactions between immune cells and astrocytes in male and female subjects, potentially influencing pain perception or having other neurobehavioral consequences. It has also been reported that sex differences in glial activation may be related to phagocytosis. An in vitro study demonstrated that inflammation stimulated phagocytosis in male, but not female, astroglia, suggesting sex‐specific differences in the role of astroglia to reestablish tissue homeostasis. 55

CONCLUSION

Collectively, the data suggest that antiretroviral concentrations within the brain are not uniform and that pathophysiologic insults, such as HIV and/or morphine exposure cause drug redistribution within the brain, which may result in preferential accumulation within specific brain regions, and may also depend on the extent of glial reactivity and the particular brain regions affected. Thus, the ability of antiretrovirals to suppress viral replication or latency may be context‐dependent, with region‐specific, sex‐specific responses to different pathophysiologic insults, such as comorbid opioid use disorder. Future research should explore the mechanisms behind these interactions and also examine how different opioids might affect HIV treatment, with the goal of improving care for HIV patients who use opioids.
