Correlation of Contusion Morphology and Volume With Functional Recovery and in Hospital Mortality in Traumatic Brain Injury
Authors: Rajnish Kumar, Aamir Hussain Hela, Kaiser Karim, Mohsin Fayaz, Aizul Khursheed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTraumatic cerebral contusions are one of the most clinically significant parenchymal lesions and contribute to great morbidity and mortality. Early identification of radiological factors associated with poor outcome can be helpful in early surgical intervention. To identify the association between contusion morphology and contusion volume and functional outcome and in-hospital mortality of patients suffering from traumatic cerebral contusions.MethodsThis prospective observational cohort study included 50 patients with traumatic brain injury and radiologically confirmed cerebral contusions. The Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) was used and functional outcome at 6-month follow-up was measured as the Glasgow Outcome Scale (GOS). Contusions were evaluated on non-contrast computed tomography for morphology, anatomical location, depth, and volume. Associations between clinical and radiological variables and outcomes were reported using Spearman correlation, Kruskal-Wallis test, χ2 test, and Fisher’s exact test.ResultsThe mean age was 32.2±11.5 years and 64.0% of patients were male. Fracture-associated contusions occurred in 54.0%, coup contusions in 66.0%, and herniation contusions in 20.0%. The frontal lobe was most commonly involved (22.0%). The mean contusion volume was 12.8±5.5 mL and the average admission GCS was 8.4±3.0. Decompressive craniectomy was also performed in 48.0% of the patients. Median GOS at follow-up was 3.0 and in-hospital mortality was 16.0%. Contusion volume was significantly associated with GOS (r=−0.760, p<0.001) and mortality.ConclusionIn patients with traumatic cerebral contusions, a large volume of lesion, herniation-type appearance, and an unfavorable anatomical location were associated with poor functional outcome and in-hospital mortality.Keywords: Brain injuries, traumatic; Cerebral hemorrhage, traumatic; Brain contusion; Tomography, X-ray computed; Glasgow Outcome Scale; Hospital mortality

INTRODUCTION

Traumatic brain injury (TBI) is still a leading cause of death and disability worldwide, as well as one of the main causes of trauma-related death and long-term neurological morbidity.9,13) In addition to its immediate lethality, TBI also causes severe physical, cognitive, behavioral, and emotional impairment in patients, families, and health systems.15) Among the intracranial lesions after TBI, traumatic parenchymal mass lesions are of greatest clinical significance, occurring in up to 8.2% of all TBI cases and in 13%–35% of severe injuries, and accounting for a significant proportion of surgically treated intracranial lesions.5,10)

Cerebral contusions are among the most clinically significant traumatic parenchymal lesions and are focal areas of cortical tissue injury with varying levels of hemorrhage, edema, and necrosis, mainly involving the frontal and temporal lobes because of the impact on the irregular skull.10,11,14) Their frequency is important, as well as their association with neurological deterioration, persistent neurocognitive deficits, and poor functional outcome.11,24)

A defining feature of cerebral contusions is that they tend to enlarge after the initial injury. Most progression occurs within 24 hours, but the enlargement may continue over the following days in some patients.1,4,17,19) This process is known as hemorrhagic progression of contusion or contusion blossoming and is important in clinical practice because increasing lesion size can cause worsening mass effect, neurological decline, a greater need for surgical intervention, and higher mortality.1,2,4,17,19,20,22,23,25)

Non-contrast computed tomography (NCCT) is still the imaging modality of choice for the initial evaluation of cerebral contusions and for the detection of interval progression on repeat imaging.4,19) The Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) is currently the standard tool for assessing level of consciousness and injury severity in patients with TBI.17) In addition, lower admission GCS scores, larger lesion volumes, and radiological evidence of progression are associated with poorer outcomes, longer hospitalization, and a higher likelihood of operative management.2,3,6,7,12,18,20,22,23,25,26) In addition, radiological features such as contusion location, volume, depth, mass effect, and associated intracranial lesions may provide important prognostic information beyond clinical examination alone.1,2,3,12,20,26)

Current recommendations for surgical management of traumatic parenchymal lesions are based on a combination of clinical and radiological findings (low GCS score, large volume, contusion location, neurological deterioration, mass effect, cisternal compression, and associated hematomas).11,16,21) In routine neurosurgical practice, however, the decision to operate is often individualized, particularly in patients with evolving contusions or borderline lesion characteristics. A clearer understanding of the relationship between contusion morphology, lesion volume, and clinical outcome may therefore improve early risk stratification and support more timely surgical decision-making.

We have therefore undertaken the study to investigate the correlation of contusion morphology and contusion volume with functional outcome and in-hospital mortality in patients with traumatic cerebral contusions. We have also sought to assess the relationship between clinical and radiological deterioration and to examine the factors influencing surgical management in this patient population.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design and setting

This prospective observational cohort study was designed to investigate the relationship between cerebral contusion morphology and contusion volume and functional outcome and in-hospital mortality among patients with TBI. The study also examined the association of clinical and radiological deterioration and surgical intervention for patients with cerebral contusions. It was conducted at the Super Speciality Hospital, Government Medical College, Srinagar over a 2-year period.

Study population

The number of patients studied were 50 with TBI and radiologically confirmed cerebral contusions. All patients were enrolled at the time of admission to the hospital (FIGURE 1).

Inclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria were: (1) history of TBI; (2) presence of cerebral contusion on initial NCCT of the head; (3) availability for in-hospital observation and follow-up outcome assessment.

Exclusion criteria

The patients were excluded if they had: (1) inadequate clinical or radiological information; (2) non-traumatic intracerebral lesions; (3) prior neurological disease that was important to outcome assessment; or (4) lack of follow-up and outcome documentation.

Clinical assessment

At the time of admission, all patients were evaluated for health and demographic information including age and sex. Neurological severity at the time of admission was assessed using the GCS. Patients were assessed for pupillary status, neurological deterioration in hospitalization, and surgical intervention. All patients were monitored throughout the hospital stay by serial neurological examination and neurosurgical assessment.

Radiological assessment

All patients were examined by NCCT scan of the brain at admission. The contusions were characterized for morphological pattern and classified as fracture-related contusions, coup contusions, counter-coup contusions, gliding contusions, intermediary contusions, and herniation contusions.

Fracture-related contusions

Parenchymal hemorrhagic lesions occurring in direct anatomical relation to an overlying skull fracture, typically at the site of impact.

Coup contusions

Cortical hemorrhagic lesions situated directly beneath the point of cranial impact.

Counter-coup contusions

Contusions located at the brain surface diametrically opposite to the point of impact, classically in the orbitofrontal and temporal poles.

Gliding contusions

Hemorrhagic lesions in the parasagittal white matter, typically at the grey-white matter junction, occurring as a result of differential rotational acceleration.

Intermediary contusions

Lesions located in the deep white matter or corpus callosum, between the cortex and the brainstem, representing intermediate-depth injury.

Herniation contusions

Hemorrhagic cortical lesions that occur secondary to mechanical compression of brain tissue against bony ridges or dural folds during herniation, most characteristically at the parahippocampal gyri, uncus, cingulate gyrus, or cerebellar tonsils.

The location of each lesion was recorded (frontal, temporal, parietal, occipital, bilateral frontal, and combined lobar involvement). The depth of the contusions and the presence of extra-axial hematomas (subdural hematoma [SDH] and epidural hematoma [EDH]) were also noted.

Contusion volume was measured on computed tomography (CT) imaging, expressed in mL (volume measured by ABC/2 formula). Radiological evidence of progression, mass effect, or herniation was recorded when the patient was seen. These measures were then considered for functional outcome and in-hospital mortality.

Management protocol

The patient was either managed conservatively or surgically based on their clinical condition and radiological findings. Conservative treatment was focused on intensive neurological examination, serial and frequent examinations, and medical management of TBI. The surgical treatment consisted mainly of decompressive craniectomy for patients with neurological deterioration, significant mass effect, large contusion volume, herniation, or other radiological and clinical indications as determined by the treating neurosurgical team.

Outcome measures

The main outcome measure was the functional outcome at 6-month follow-up, which was assessed using the Glasgow Outcome Scale (GOS). The secondary outcome was in-hospital mortality. Contusion morphology and volume, location of lesion, contusion depth, and radiological findings were compared with the outcomes with respect to GOS and mortality.

Statistical analysis

Data were collected in MS Excel sheets and analyzed with the statistical tool SPSS v28 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive and distribution statistics were calculated. The ordinal outcome data were expressed as median with range where appropriate. The association between predictor variables and GOS was analyzed using Spearman rank correlation for binary and continuous variables. For variables with multiple groups—contusion location and contusion depth—the Kruskal-Wallis test was used to measure association with GOS. Associations between predictor variables and in-hospital mortality were analyzed using χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test as relevant. A p-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethical considerations

The study was conducted in line with GMC Srinagar institutional ethical standards (Educational Credential Assessment obtained protocol number 87/2023). The patient or patient’s legally authorized representative was informed of the study and consent was obtained as appropriate.

Admission GCS and contusion morphology

Herniation contusions were associated with significantly lower admission GCS scores compared with patients without this subtype. Similarly, gliding contusions and intermediary contusions were associated with significantly lower admission GCS compared with their respective absent groups. Fracture-related, coup, and counter-coup contusion subtypes did not demonstrate a statistically significant difference in admission GCS. Admission GCS showed a very strong positive correlation with functional outcome across the full cohort and a clear gradient in GOS and mortality was observed across GCS severity strata (TABLE 5).

Management and outcomes

Twenty-four (48.0%) of the patients in our study underwent decompressive craniectomy while 26 (52.0%) were treated conservatively. The functional outcome based on the GOS was reported to be 3.0 (range, 1–5). In-hospital mortality was observed in 8 patients (16.0%) and 42 patients (84.0%) survived to discharge (TABLE 6).

Association of clinical and radiological variables with GOS and in-hospital mortality

Clinical and radiological variables were found to have a weak relationship with GOS and in-hospital mortality (r=−0.144, p=0.320). A slight association with in-hospital mortality was seen (p=0.055). Coup contusions (r=−0.177, p=0.219) and counter-coup contusions (r=−0.014, p=0.924) did not significantly correlate with functional outcome or mortality.

Gliding contusions (r=−0.379, p=0.006) had a moderate negative relationship with GOS, indicating that these patients had worse functional outcome. However, there was no statistically significant association with in-hospital mortality (p=0.718). Intermediary contusions had a borderline negative association with GOS (r=−0.265, p=0.063) and no significant association with mortality (p=1.000).

Herniation contusions emerged as one of the most clinically important lesion subtypes, showing a strong negative correlation with GOS (r=−0.560, p<0.001) and significantly higher in-hospital mortality (p=0.005). Contusion volume is the most strongly associated continuous factor with outcome, with larger lesions having a very strong negative correlation with GOS (r=−0.760, p<0.001) and a significant association with mortality (p=0.006).

Contusion location is a significant factor of functional outcome (Kruskal-Wallis χ2=34.903, p<0.001) and in-hospital mortality (χ2=37.847, p<0.001). Contusion depth is also a significant factor of GOS (χ2=16.502, p<0.001), but its association with mortality was not significant (p=0.297). The association between SDH and EDH in this group was not significant (p=1.000) (TABLE 7).

DISCUSSION

In this prospective cohort of 50 patients with traumatic cerebral contusions, contusion volume, herniation morphology, and anatomical location were most strongly associated with poor functional outcomes and in-hospital mortality, but fracture-associated, coup, and counter-coup contusions were not associated with either endpoint. The findings of this study support the idea that in traumatic parenchymal lesions, prognostic importance lies less in the presence of a contusion and more in the biological severity (lesion size, mass effect, and involvement of the most vulnerable brain regions). This is in clear agreement with previous studies that have shown that larger contusions, early radiological progression, and intracranial mass effect are associated with neurological worsening and worse outcomes.2,3,7,11,20)

The strongest signal in this study was the inverse relationship between contusion volume and GOS. Larger lesions were associated with lower functional recovery and higher in-hospital mortality. This finding is in line with the natural history work of Alahmadi et al.,2) who concluded that contusion progression is more common in larger lesions and that patients with large contusions and low initial GCS are more prone to delayed deterioration. Similarly, Chang et al.7) identified large initial intraparenchymal hemorrhage size as a risk factor for early progression and emphasized that these lesions should be regularly monitored from a radiological viewpoint. Recent work has also found support in this direction. In a study on bifrontal contusions, M et al.16) found that contusion volume above 51 mL was associated with poor GOS, and even intermediate volumes were associated with worse outcomes. Together with the very strong correlation in our cohort, these findings support the notion that lesion volume is not only a descriptive radiological feature of the brain, but rather a clinically meaningful marker of tissue destruction, evolving edema, and impending mass effect.

Our findings that herniation-type contusions are associated with poor GOS and increased mortality are also clinically relevant. While the majority of the literature has more frequently reported cisternal effacement, midline shift, and mass effect rather than “herniation contusion” as a formal subtype, the biological message is the same: lesions that reflect or produce mechanical distortion of intracranial structures are associated with worse prognosis.5,7) Chang et al.7) reported that effacement of the basal cisterns on the initial CT scan strongly predicted the progression of traumatic intraparenchymal hemorrhage. Likewise, Bullock et al.5) emphasized that low GCS, significant lesion burden, midline shift, and cisternal compression should prompt serious consideration of operative management in traumatic parenchymal lesions. In this context, the present finding that herniation morphology carried both functional and mortality implications appears pathophysiologically plausible and surgically relevant.

Admission GCS was significantly lower in patients with herniation-type contusions compared with those without (mean 5.4±2.2 vs. 9.4±2.5; p<0.001), and correlated strongly with functional outcome across the full cohort confirming its role as an important confounding variable. Although herniation morphology remained associated with poor GOS and mortality in our unadjusted analysis, these contusions also showed lower admission GCS and larger volumes; without multivariable adjustment we cannot determine whether the association is independent of these factors, and it should be interpreted as an association rather than an independent prognostic effect.

Location of the lesion was also a key determinant of outcome in our series. We found that lesion location was significantly associated with both GOS and in-hospital mortality, indicating that anatomical distribution is associated with outcome beyond simple lesion presence. Prior studies have similarly shown that topography influences outcome because certain locations are more prone to secondary swelling, mass effect, and clinically important neurological deficits.7,11,16) Frontal contusions were the most common lesions in our cohort, which agrees with the established predilection of traumatic contusions for the frontobasal and temporal regions owing to the mechanism of impact and the relationship of the brain to the skull base.11) However, our data suggest that not all frontal or mixed-lobar contusions are equivalent; rather, unfavorable locations likely reflect a combination of lesion burden, bilateral involvement, proximity to critical structures, and greater risk of radiological evolution. The poor prognosis associated with bifrontal and deep contusion patterns reported by M et al.16) further supports the importance of lesion topography in outcome assessment.

The significant relationship between contusion depth and GOS, despite the absence of a significant association with mortality, is also noteworthy. This may indicate that deeper lesions contribute more to persistent neurological disability than to early death. One possible explanation is that deeper contusions may represent greater transmission of traumatic force, more diffuse parenchymal disruption, or involvement of eloquent subcortical networks, all of which may impair recovery even when patients survive the acute hospitalization. Although depth as an isolated variable has been less consistently studied than volume or progression, our findings suggest that it deserves further attention in future prognostic models, particularly those focused on disability rather than mortality.11)

Gliding contusions showed a moderate but significant association with worse GOS in our cohort, whereas intermediary contusions showed a borderline relationship with functional outcome and no association with mortality. These findings are interesting because such morphological subtypes are often underreported in outcome studies compared with more traditional categories such as lesion size, hemorrhagic progression, or associated extra-axial hematoma. A plausible interpretation is that gliding lesions may reflect a higher-energy mechanism with greater shearing stress and more diffuse axonal-parenchymal injury, thereby impairing neurological recovery even when early survival is preserved. Because the available literature has not extensively validated these morphology-based subgroups, these results should be interpreted cautiously; however, they suggest that morphology may provide additional prognostic information beyond volume alone and merit further study in larger cohorts.3,11)

In contrast, fracture-associated, coup, and counter-coup contusions were not significantly associated with outcome or mortality in the present analysis. This suggests that these descriptors, although useful for mechanistic characterization, may be less prognostically informative than markers of lesion severity such as size, mass effect, and radiological evolution. Some prior studies have also shown that anatomical and volumetric features outperform mechanism-based descriptors when outcome is modeled clinically.2,3,11) Interestingly, M et al.16) reported worse prognosis in patients with contrecoup injury patterns in bifrontal contusions, whereas we did not observe a significant association for counter-coup lesions in the broader cohort. This discrepancy may reflect differences in sample size, lesion selection, outcome timing, and the fact that their study focused on a more anatomically restricted and surgically relevant subgroup.

The present findings also have practical implications for monitoring and surgical decision-making. Cerebral contusions are dynamic lesions, and several studies have shown that early hemorrhagic progression is common, particularly during the first 24 hours after injury.2,3,20) Oertel et al.20) demonstrated that progressive hemorrhage after head trauma occurs frequently, especially in cerebral contusions, and is associated with intracranial pressure elevation. Alahmadi et al.2) likewise emphasized that not all radiological progression is accompanied by immediate clinical deterioration, which supports the need for routine repeat imaging in selected patients. The systematic review by Baldon et al.3) further summarized that progression of contusion hematoma is associated with neurological decline, need for surgery, higher mortality, and poorer long-term outcomes. As such, our finding that larger volume lesions and herniation-type contusions had the worst outcomes suggests a low threshold for repeat imaging and treatment for high-risk radiological profiles, even before clinical deterioration becomes imminent.

Our management results should be interpreted with reference to current neurosurgical protocols. More than half of the patients in the group did have decompressive craniectomy, indicative of the severity of the injury. Current guidelines call for surgery in patients with frontal or temporal contusions of at least 20 cm3 with midline shift of at least 5 mm and/or cisternal compression in patients with low GCS, and for any lesion greater than 50 cm3.5) The surgical threshold in many current cohorts is based on these guidelines.26) The results of the current study do not support surgery on morphology alone but suggest that morphology, especially herniation pattern, may complement standard operative criteria for those patients most likely to deteriorate or have poor neurological recovery.

The mortality rate in our group was 16% in the hospital and 84% survived to discharge. This mortality rate is within the broad range reported in studies of traumatic parenchymal injury, but comparison should be made cautiously because mortality rates vary according to admission severity, lesion location, imaging timing, and whether outcome is measured in hospital or in longer-term follow-up.2,3,11,12,16,20,26) We focused on both functional and mortality outcomes, and the factors influencing these 2 endpoints were not the same. For example, depth and gliding morphology were related to functional recovery but not to mortality. In other words, short-term survival and neurological disability may be related but are not identical.

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size was too small to provide sufficient statistical power and may be the reason why some morphology subgroups were only borderline associated. Second, this was a single-cohort observational study, and the analyses were mainly univariate, so the results should be interpreted as associations and not independent predictors. Third, herniation-type contusions were associated with both larger volumes and lower admission GCS, and because our analysis was univariate, we cannot determine whether their poorer outcome reflects the morphology itself or simply the greater lesion burden and injury severity within this subgroup. Fourth, at follow-up, the outcome was measured using GOS, but the long-term neurocognitive recovery was not addressed. Fifth, contusion morphology and depth were classified on the admission NCCT without formal blinded multi-reader adjudication, and no formal measure of inter-rater reliability (e.g., Cohen’s or Fleiss’ kappa) was calculated. This may affect the reproducibility and classification reliability of the morphology-based subgroups. Sixth, the ABC/2 formula used for contusion volume estimation may underestimate or overestimate volume in lesions with complex morphology. Finally, treatment selection bias in contusions is unavoidable, as the sickest patients are more likely to undergo surgery and it is difficult to separate the impact of procedures and interventions on outcome. Moreover, with a cohort of 50 patients only, the study was powered for descriptive and univariate association analyses only, and the absence of multivariable modelling means that no independent prognostic factor can be identified from the current dataset.

Despite these limitations, the study provides important clinical information, as it indicates that volume, herniation morphology, and anatomical location are the radiological features most strongly associated with poor GOS and in-hospital mortality in traumatic cerebral contusions. These findings support early risk stratification on admission CT, require close monitoring of high-risk lesions, and suggest that morphology-based characteristics are a useful addition to standard volumetric and clinical assessment. Larger prospective studies using multivariable models and standardized imaging definitions are required to assess whether these morphology-based features remain independent prognostic tools and how they may help inform surgical decisions in traumatic brain contusion.

CONCLUSION

As shown in our prospective group of 50 patients with traumatic brain contusions, lesion volume, herniation-type morphology, and anatomical location were the radiological factors most strongly associated with poor functional outcome and in-hospital mortality. Larger contusions were associated with poor GOS, highlighting the need for early volumetric assessment on admission CT. Herniation contusions were the most clinically significant morphological subtype and were associated with worse neurological recovery as well as higher mortality.

Contusion location and depth had a significant impact on outcome, suggesting that topographic features are more important than lesion size alone. Fracture-associated, coup, and counter-coup contusions were not associated with outcome or mortality. These findings suggest that detailed radiological analysis of brain contusions may aid early risk stratification and enhance clinical and surgical decisions. More prospective studies with multivariable analysis and standardized imaging criteria are needed in order to confirm these associations and improve prognostic models for TBI.
