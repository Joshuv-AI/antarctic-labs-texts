Self-limiting recovery from subacute haemorrhagic pericardial tamponade following early ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction: a case report
Authors: Yiting Cai, Jing Yang, Guangyao Zhai, Amir Khalifa, Andrea Freixa-Benavente, Alexandru Patrascu, Deepti Ranganathan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHaemorrhagic pericarditis following an early ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) is commonly associated with intensive antithrombotic therapy, which may necessitate pericardiocentesis or surgery due to haemodynamic compromise. However, the natural progression of haemorrhagic pericarditis remains poorly documented.Case summaryA 54-year-old man underwent primary percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) of the left anterior descending artery for acute extensive anterior myocardial infarction. During the perioperative period, a combination of multiple antithrombotic agents was administered. The following day, the patient experienced pleuritic chest pain, and transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) revealed a small-to-moderate pericardial effusion (PE), consistent with post-infarction pericarditis. Five days later, he developed dyspnoea and jugular venous distension, with TTE indicating progression to a large PE. Cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) imaging demonstrated diffuse pericardial enhancement and similar T1 values between the effusion and the right atrium, suggestive of haemorrhagic pericarditis while excluding cardiac rupture. Considering the mild haemodynamic compromise, emergency pericardiocentesis was deferred. Discontinuation of the anticoagulant agent and ticagrelor resulted in gradual PE resolution, with complete absorption achieved after 1.5 months.DiscussionThis case highlights the potential development of haemorrhagic pericardial tamponade from small-to-moderate PE following early STEMI, particularly under intensive antithrombotic therapy in patients with transmural myocardial infarction. Introduction

Moderate to large pericardial effusion (PE) occurring early after acute myocardial infarction, defined as a diastolic diameter > 10 mm, poses a substantial risk of progressing to pericardial tamponade (PT). These patients warrant thorough evaluation to exclude subacute myocardial rupture or iatrogenic coronary perforation.1–4 Intensive antithrombotic therapy may contribute to haemorrhagic PE, a condition frequently overlooked. The natural progression of haemorrhagic PE induced by antithrombotic therapy remains poorly documented, as haemodynamic compromise often necessitates urgent pericardiocentesis or surgical intervention.5

Case presentation

A 54-year-old male with a history of hypertension presented to our emergency department with severe chest pain lasting 3.5 h. On physical examination, there were no abnormalities in his cardiovascular or pulmonary findings. Initial electrocardiogram revealed following ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) involving the anterolateral wall (Figure 1A). The patient received a loading dose of oral aspirin (300 mg) and ticagrelor (180 mg) before the primary percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI).

(A) Admission electrocardiogram. (B) Electrocardiogram when pleuritic chest pain resembling post-infarction pericarditis.

Two drug-eluting stents (3.5 × 14 mm and 4.0 × 29 mm) were implanted in the infarct-related proximal segment of the left anterior descending (LAD) artery (Figure 2A; Supplementary material). Additionally, a 90% diffuse stenosis in the right coronary artery (RCA) was identified and scheduled for elective intervention (Figure 2B; Supplementary material).

Coronary artery angiography. (A) Total occlusion of the proximal left anterior descending artery. (B) Severe stenosis (90%) in the mid and distal segments of the right coronary artery.

Anticoagulation was initiated with bivalirudin during the procedure and continued for 1 h post-procedure. Due to the high thrombus burden, manual aspiration thrombectomy and intracoronary tirofiban administration were performed, followed by a 24 h tirofiban infusion at a rate of 0.01 µg/kg/min. Low molecular weight heparin at 5000 units was administered subcutaneously twice daily, beginning12 h post-procedure.

Despite symptoms relief, only approximately 30% of the ST-segment elevation resolved, suggesting suboptimal microvascular perfusion. On Day 2, the patient experienced recurrent chest pain, exacerbated by inspiration. Electrocardiogram showed low QRS voltages, ST elevation, and PR depression in most limb leads, with reciprocal changes in lead aVR, as well as R-wave loss, QS complexes, and ST elevation in reciprocal leads (Figure 1B). Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) revealed a small-to-moderate PE measuring about 10 mm, consistent with post-infarction pericarditis (PIP). Intravenous aspirin and lysine effectively alleviated the pain by the following day.

Five days later, the patient developed nocturnal dyspnoea, tachycardia, and Kussmaul’s sign (Video 1), though blood pressure remained consistently above 90/70 mmHg. Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated progression to a large PE (15 mm) with heart swinging and mild early-diastolic right ventricular collapse (Figure 3 A and B). The absence of low-velocity colour Doppler flow from the left ventricle into the effusion and the lack of signs of free wall rupture (FWR) suggested that subacute cardiac rupture was unlikely (Video 2). On Day 7, 3.0T cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) imaging was performed, though only partial sequences were obtained due to poor breath-holding. The imaging revealed a large PE on T1-weighted (T1W) and T2-weighted (T2W) images, with no evidence of FWR (Figure 4).

(C) Transthoracic echocardiography on Day 29 demonstrating significant pericardial effusion absorption, with pericardial enhancement (white arrow).

Considering the mild haemodynamic compromise, emergency pericardiocentesis was not performed. Subsequent TTE demonstrated progressive PE, peaking at 25 mm on Day 13 (Figure 3), without further haemodynamic deterioration. Anticoagulant therapy and ticagrelor were discontinued and substituted with clopidogrel, while aspirin was maintained. Following this adjustment, the PE gradually subsided and was completely absorbed after 1.5 months.

A follow-up CMR on Day 18 revealed diffuse pericarditis and transmural infarction with extensive pericardial and transmural enhancement (Figure 5). The PE volume had decreased to 11 mm, with hypokinesia in the anteroseptal and apical regions, and a left ventricular ejection fraction of 45% (Figure 4) on steady-state free precession (SSFP). On T2-weighted short tau inversion recovery (T2-STIR) images, increased pericardial signal intensity was observed, consistent with oedema, together with a heterogeneous PE suggestive of haemorrhagic content (Figure 5). Complementary findings on T1-weighted fast spin-echo (FSE) images demonstrated that the effusion exhibited increased and heterogeneous fluid signal intensity, indicative of an exudative or haemorrhagic process (Figure 4). Moreover, comparable T1 values between the effusion and the right atrial blood pool were observed, further supporting the diagnosis of haemopericardium (Figure 4). The CMR study showed no morphological evidence of FWR, such as pericardial fat discontinuity, wall rupture, thrombus within the infarcted region, or pseudoaneurysm formation.

Three months post-discharge, the patient underwent RCA angioplasty with a drug-coated balloon to address recurrent angina. At the 1-year follow-up, he remained clinically stable, with TTE confirming absence of effusion recurrence.

Discussion

To our knowledge, the natural progression of haemorrhagic pericarditis under conservative management is rarely reported. This case highlights the potential development of haemorrhagic PT in patients with small-to-moderate PE following early STEMI, particularly under intensive antithrombotic therapy.

The patient initially presented with pleuritic chest pain resembling PIP, followed by progressive PE and mild haemodynamic compromise. Post-infarction pericarditis occurring early after STEMI is typically benign and self-limiting, with tamponade being a rare complication. It is generally attributed to pericardial exudation resulting from direct stimulation following a transmural infarction, reported in 5%–20% of cases but rarely identified clinically.6

In contrast, moderate to severe PE during early STEMI is most frequently associated with haemopericardium, with 65% potentially developing tamponade and 41% experiencing FWR according to the literature.7 Additionally, large PE following PCI often necessitates exclusion of iatrogenic coronary complications, while haemorrhagic pericarditis, although rare, remains an under-recognized cause.8 In our case, all three aetiologies were plausible. However, angiographic review during PCI showed no signs of perforation, and follow-up evaluation with both CMR and TTE revealed no morphological evidence of FWR or pseudoaneurysms. Furthermore, no abnormal pericardial or thoracic masses were detected, and no signs of aortic dissection were identified on CMR imaging. The spontaneous resolution of haemorrhagic pericarditis upon cessation of partial antithrombotic therapy supports the feasibility of conservative management in patients with mild haemodynamic compromise. This approach diverges from conventional strategies advocating immediate drainage to prevent haemodynamic deterioration. Although the specific antithrombotic agent responsible could not be identified, the combined use of two anticoagulants and triple antiplatelet therapy likely contributed to haemorrhagic pericarditis, consistent with previous reports.2,9 The Academic Research Consortium–High Bleeding Risk (ARC-HBR) score of this patient indicated a non–high bleeding risk profile. The patient had no history of bleeding diathesis, easy bruising, or familial coagulopathy. Routine laboratory tests—including platelet count, coagulation studies, and liver and renal function tests—were all within normal ranges, and targeted testing for inherited bleeding disorders such as haemophilia or von Willebrand disease was not indicated.

This case also underscores the diagnostic value of CMR imaging in differentiating haemorrhagic effusion from other aetiologies and in guiding management decisions. Although not routinely recommended for pericardial diseases, CMR offers invaluable diagnostic information in complex cases of PE with stable haemodynamics. T2W and late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) provide essential insights into the extent and stage of pericarditis by detecting pericardial inflammation and oedema,10 with LGE aiding in quantifying disease severity.11

Cardiac magnetic resonance can also characterize the nature of PE through T1W, T2W, and T1 mapping. Exudative effusions, rich in protein and cellular content, typically exhibit high T1W signal intensity and low T2W signal intensity,12 whereas haemopericardium presents as high T1W signal intensity with inhomogeneous low SSFP signal intensity.13 Studies have demonstrated that native T1 mapping values of pericardial fluid correlate with protein concentration, aiding in differentiating transudates, exudates, and haemorrhagic effusions.14–16 This capability may reduce the need for invasive procedures like pericardiocentesis when diagnostic clarification is the primary goal. Additionally, CMR provides detailed anatomical information when subacute FWR with associated pericardial bleeding is suspected.

Transthoracic echocardiography also played a crucial role in this case, from initial PE detection to monitoring disease progression. It remains the first-line imaging technique for pericardial diseases and serves as a guide for pericardiocentesis. While TTE can detect FWR through myocardial tears and low-velocity Doppler flow from the left ventricular cavity into the effusion (Video 2B), its ability to characterize pericardial tissue is limited by resolution and restricted acoustic windows.

In conclusion, this case illustrates the self-limited progression of subacute haemorrhagic PT associated with antithrombotic therapy during early STEMI. Intensive antithrombotic therapy during early STEMI may increase the risk of haemorrhagic pericarditis, particularly following extensive transmural infarction. The decision to pursue invasive pericardiocentesis for significant PE should be guided by a comprehensive assessment of both the underlying aetiology and the patient’s haemodynamic status. When feasible, CMR imaging provides critical insights by accurately determining the aetiology of PE and additional anatomical information and aiding in risk stratification. This case therefore supports the potential role of conservative management for haemorrhagic pericarditis in selected patients with haemorrhagic pericarditis and mild haemodynamic compromise, challenging the conventional preference for immediate drainage.
