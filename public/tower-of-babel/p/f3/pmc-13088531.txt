Attitudes and knowledge of South African healthcare professionals towards blood flow restriction training
Authors: Georgia Torres, Kameran Jacobsohn, Philippe Gradidge
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveBlood Flow Restriction Training (BFRT) has been shown to have similar benefits to high-intensity exercise conditions without high resistance loads. This training methodology is not included in traditional healthcare professional training, as it is an emerging training tool. Moreover, the attitudes and knowledge of healthcare professionals towards BFRT remain unknown in South Africa. The purpose of this exploratory study was to elucidate the attitudes and knowledge of selected healthcare professionals registered with the Health Professions Council of South Africa regarding BFRT.ResultsA total of 100 healthcare professionals completed an online questionnaire, including exercise physiologists (46%) and physiotherapists (13%), with medical practitioners (14%), occupational therapists (14%), and other professions (13%). Most had heard about BFRT. Exercise physiologists and physiotherapists agreed that BFRT is a feasible clinical tool and that they possess sufficient knowledge of BFRT methodology, whereas other practitioners were indifferent. Most healthcare professionals were significantly indifferent on the role of BFRT in post-surgical interventions and in improving physiological outcomes. Physiotherapists disagreed with the statement that BRFT improves health outcomes in older individuals. At the same time, all healthcare practitioners agreed that BFRT can be used in the healthy adult population, but is contraindicated for pregnant women. A very high proportion of health practitioners agreed on the need for more education and training in BFRT.ConclusionOverall, health care providers demonstrated attitudes that were generally positive toward BFRT, but were indifferent regarding using the tool in rehabilitation and post-surgery. There was low confidence in the application of BFRT, and most participants desired more training and education. Our findings emphasise the need for the adoption and education of BFRT.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13104-026-07743-4.Keywords: Blood flow restriction, Training, Healthcare professionals, Attitudes, Knowledge, Rehabilitation

Introduction

Blood Flow Restriction training (BFRT) is an alternative method used by clinicians that has been extensively researched and shows promising results in patients undergoing physical therapy [1–3]. The BFRT method involves partially restricting arterial blood flow and restricting the vein of the working skeletal muscle, allowing blood to still enter the muscle but causing lactate to accumulate [4]. Increased lactate during BFRT indirectly contributes to the recruitment of fast-twitch muscle fibres by creating a metabolic environment that favours their activation [5]. The accumulation of lactate, along with other metabolites, triggers the exercise pressor reflex, which stimulates the nervous system to activate more motor units, including those that primarily innervate fast-twitch fibres [6]. The BFRT technique uses a cuff or band placed on the most proximal aspect of the limb being trained [7]. The device is tightened to cause blood pooling distal to the cuff, mimicking the physiological conditions found during high-intensity exercise, but without the need for high resistance loads.

Research has demonstrated that BFRT is beneficial for anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction [8] and total knee arthroscopy rehabilitation. Positive outcomes have also been shown with the functional recovery in the elderly [9] and for pain relief following physical therapy sessions that included kinetic chain knee extensions [10]. BFRT has been shown to increase cardiorespiratory capacity, when combined with aerobic training [11] and enhance bone formation and resorption [12]. In addition, a systematic review and meta-analysis showed that low-load BFRT alone and walking combined with BFRT both increased muscle hypertrophy and improved muscle strength in older adults [13].

The advantages of BFRT compared to traditional training are increases in muscle size, strength, and endurance capacity that can be attained with low exercise intensities [14–16]. Furthermore, adaptations from BFRT occur within shorter time spans when compared to traditional training [17]. However, it must be noted that some research did not support the use of BFRT and cited the need to adjust for the confounding variables such as age and gender [18–20].

Despite the numerous beneficial outcomes associated with BFRT as a modality in physical therapy, it is not widely implemented in the clinical setting. The knowledge and attitude of clinicians are important components of BFRT adoption, especially regarding the safety of the approach, which may partly account for whether they use the technique or tool [20]. Earlier evidence has demonstrated that clinicians’ readiness to use BFRT are driven by how they perceive the tool’s safety, available of context-specific protocol and accessibility of appropriate training [21, 22]. A deeper understanding of these factors, particularly in low-resourced settings is important to determine the gaps and optimal approaches to integrate BFRT into rehabilitation.

Given the limited evidence on South African healthcare professionals’ attitudes and knowledge of BFRT, and that the technique is emerging in clinical practice, the aim of this study was to explore these perceptions to inform future implementation of the tool in South Africa.

Methods

This study employed a quantitative, exploratory cross-sectional design for its investigation. A self-administered questionnaire was designed and housed online using the Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) data management platform hosted by the University of the Witwatersrand on encrypted servers (Supplement A). All data were password protected on REDCap and kept confidential by anonymising responses. The questionnaire was comprised of questions that included queries on demographic details, attitudes, beliefs towards BFRT, and knowledge of BFRT. It had been pre-tested on ten healthcare professionals, not included in the analysis. The target population included active healthcare professionals registered with the Health Professions Council of South Africa (HPCSA) boards that have a scope in exercise prescription and rehabilitation. At the time of data collection, the HPCSA registry was an estimate of approximately 71 559 in 2020. The relevant HPCSA boards and professional associations provided permission to circulate the online survey. The questionnaire was based on expert panel input, including content validity, from South African clinicians [23] and on former BFRT surveys [21, 22]. Pilot testing was conducted with 10 healthcare professionals, and this resulted in minor adjustments the wording to improve clarity. It was distributed via official email lists and closed mobile app groups to selected HPCSA professionals from boards relevant to BFRT, instead of circulating to all HPCSA professionals to ensure relevancy. Practitioners, who are registered with the HPCSA, include biokineticists (hereafter referred to as ‘exercise physiologists’ to reflect the strong alignment between their clinical scope and the physiological principles underlying BFRT in rehabilitation), including biokinetics interns, physiotherapists, medical doctors, medical researchers, dieticians, occupational therapists, and orthotists. These practitioners were invited through their professional associations to participate in the research questionnaire (Association for Dietetics in South Africa (ADSA), Occupational Therapy Association of South Africa (OTASA), South African Society of Physiotherapy (SASP), and the South African Sports Medicine Association (SASMA). Sports Scientist were also included in the study as BFRT is used in their profession. Additionally, participants were encouraged to share the questionnaire with their colleagues to create a snowballing effect. Practitioners in training were excluded from the study to ensure that participants’ response were a reflection of qualified healthcare professionals’ perspectives. Participants provided written informed consent before participating. The Human Research Ethics Committee (Medical), University of the Witwatersrand (M191194) provided ethical clearance for the study.

All data were analysed using IBM SPSS Statistics, version 26. Descriptive data were presented as frequencies. The responses to attitude and knowledge questions were captured on a Likert scale and collapsed into three categories for ease of interpretation, including ‘agree’, ‘indifferent’, and ‘disagree’. The term ‘indifferent’ was used to reflect the ‘neutral’ option on the questionnaire rather than ‘uncertain’ participant responses. The differences between healthcare professional groups were assessed using Pearson Chi-square tests. Statistical significance set at 5% (see Table 1).

Results

A total of 100 healthcare professionals participated in this study, mostly exercise physiologists (46%), followed by medical practitioners (14%), physiotherapists (13%), occupational therapists (14%), and 13% were grouped as other and included orthotists, podiatrists, dieticians, interns in training, radiographers, and sports scientists. The mean age of the sample was 33.8 ± 9.9 years, and most participants worked in private clinical practice (67%) or in hospital settings (24%).

The overall familiarity with BFRT as a treatment modality was moderate at 53%. Table 2 demonstrates that while exercise physiologists and physiotherapists agree that BFRT is a feasible clinical tool and that they have sufficient knowledge on BFRT methodology, most other healthcare professionals (65%) were indifferent about the role of BFRT in post-surgical rehabilitation. A high percentage of exercise physiologists (93.5%) and physiotherapists (92.3%) agreed that they needed more training in BFRT. The other health practitioners also supported this notion.

Exercise physiologists were more confident than other healthcare professionals that BRFT enhances muscle hypertrophy. However, it was observed that all healthcare professionals were significantly indifferent to the role of BFRT in post-surgical interventions (p = 0.02). Only 19% of participants indicated that they would recommend BFRT as a treatment approach. These findings support the significant differences that were noted for the need to include further BFRT education and training, despite the low desire for its application in practice (p = 0.04). Physiotherapists disagreed with the statement that BRFT improves health outcomes in older individuals, while all healthcare practitioners agreed that BFRT can be used in the healthy adult population, but is contraindicated for pregnant women.

Discussion

A high proportion of South African health practitioners who participated in this study were not confident of their comprehensive knowledge of the BFRT technique (54%) and that BFRT is a feasible tool for clinical rehabilitation (55%). In addition, most were indifferent as to whether BRFT increases muscle hypertrophy without excess joint stress; helps reduce the time required for post-surgical rehabilitation; enhances muscle hypertrophy, strength, protects joints, improves cardiovascular health, and whether it is beneficial for older adults. Yet, more than half did agree that BFRT can be used in healthy individuals, is contraindicated in certain populations such as pregnant women and those with hypertension, and that the cost of BFRT needs to be considered. There was inter-disciplinary variation in responses, yet the only significant differences for inter-disciplinary responses were with the questions of BFRT reducing the time required for post-surgical rehabilitation, and the need for more training.

The question with the highest proportion (85%) of agreement was the one that identified the need for more training in BFRT. Finally, there seemed to be a lack of confidence among health professionals in BFRT, as most practitioners expressed that they would not recommend BFRT as a treatment modality.

Our study provides the first evidence on attitudes and knowledge of BFRT in registered South African healthcare professionals, and these data address a gap in the literature as most previous empirical data focused on high-income countries. In comparison, the study of Patterson et al. [21], also developed a questionnaire for BFRT to investigate the usage of BFRT. Contrary to the results of this study, the use of BFRT was widespread amongst practitioners of that study. Most practitioners performed the technique for ~ 10 min each training session, 1–4 times per week, and 80% of practitioners rated the use of BFRT as very good-excellent. The difference in results may be due to the low percentage (8%) of African participants in the Patterson et al., study. The Patterson et al. (2015) study, however, highlighted the technical applications of BFRT including the variations in cuff widths and restriction pressures that practitioners use, and the lack of consistent use of limb occlusion measurements to determine pressures, while we focused on the attitudes and knowledge of practitioners in our study.

Rolnick et al. [22], found additional aspects such as understanding appropriate pressure application guidelines, the variety of BFR technologies, safe implementation in practice, and the importance of perceived exertion in training to foster long-term compliance that may be further barriers to adoption of BFRT.

In 2022, in preparation for a formal submission to the Health Professionals Council of South Africa (HPCSA) and to gain better insight into the use and perception of BFRT in South Africa, an expert panel of medical professionals was asked a series of questions [23]. The roundtable discussion demonstrated consensus on the safety, screening, efficacy, and appropriate use of BFRT amongst health professionals in South Africa. The BFRT relative loading and occlusion pressure protocols in the different phases of rehabilitation were less clear. It was noted that BFRT was a relatively new modality, and detailed knowledge of its application in South Africa is lacking. The panel highlighted the need for standardised training and the development of evidence-based protocols across different health professionals to ensure good clinical practice. Furthermore, the rapid dissemination of BFRT skills to health professionals in South Africa was viewed as a novel and effective tool that could bridge the acute and longer-term rehabilitation of numerous patients. The comments of the panel discussion align with the main finding of our study, highlighting the need for standardised BFRT protocols in South Africa and education. However, our study demonstrated that participants’ confidence in BFRT was low, indicating that there is a clear gap between clinical practice and the expert panel’s opinion.

BFRT has shown promise as a methodology to improve muscle mass, strength, and function without exposing joints to high loads. However, BFRT does not seem to be widely used in the South African setting as most healthcare professionals in our study reported low confidence and reluctance to recommend it in clinical practice, despite some clinicians indicating active usage. This study aimed to explore the attitudes and perceptions of these professionals toward BFRT. Most healthcare professionals were not confident regarding the benefits, use, and outcomes of BFRT, although most agreed that it is a beneficial training method for healthy individuals. A high proportion of healthcare professionals highlighted the need for education and training in BFRT.

Limitations

Although rigorous methodology was followed, including obtaining ethical clearance and piloting, the exploratory characteristic of this study reflects the absence of formative BFRT data. The sample was limited due to voluntary nature of participation during the hard lockdown period during the Covid-19 pandemic, resulting in limited representativeness of the eligible population.
