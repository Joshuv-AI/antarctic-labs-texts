Mechanistic Basis of Cholesterol Binding and Transfer in NPC2: Insights From Molecular Dynamics Simulations
Authors: Smit Patel, Nadia Elghobashi‐Meinhardt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe Niemann–Pick type C (NPC) disease is characterized by impaired function of the lysosomal proteins NPC1 or NPC2. The soluble NPC2 protein is believed to transport cholesterol to the larger, membrane‐bound NPC1, thereby maintaining cholesterol homeostasis; disruption of this trafficking results in sterol accumulation and disease. Numerous NPC2 variants have been reported in NPC patients and cell‐based assays. However, the underlying connection between mutated primary amino acid sequence and disrupted sterol trafficking is not understood. To advance our understanding of mechanistic behavior in NPC2, here we present results of extensive all‐atom molecular dynamics simulations of wild‐type NPC2 and prominent NPC2 variants. The behavior of cholesterol‐bound (holo) and unbound (apo) NPC2 was analyzed using several descriptors, including binding‐pocket volume, entrance‐gating metrics, and principal component analysis. These analyses reveal that cholesterol binding in most variants restricts the protein's conformational freedom. Furthermore, we identify coordinated loop motions that modulate pocket closure, and we propose a mechanistic basis for how variation in protein sequence perturbs cholesterol trafficking.Keywords: cholesterol transfer, gating, ligand binding, molecular dynamics simulations, Niemann–Pick type C2 protein

Introduction

Animal cells maintain their cholesterol levels largely by extracting cholesterol from the bloodstream, mostly in the form of low‐density lipoprotein (LDL), or “bad cholesterol”, a process referred to as forward cholesterol transport [1, 2]. LDL particles latch onto specific receptors on the cell surface and enter the cell through clathrin‐mediated endocytosis [3, 4]. Once inside the cell, LDL travels through a series of cellular compartments until it reaches the late endosomes (LE) and lysosomes. There, an enzyme called acid lipase breaks down the cholesterol esters that were carried in by the LDL [3, 4, 5]. The exact mechanism by which the freed cholesterol moves within the lysosome and exits to other parts of the cell is still not fully understood.

Abnormal cholesterol accumulation within LE and lysosomes is a hallmark of the Niemann–Pick type C (NPC) disease, which is characterized by impaired cholesterol egress [6]. This disruption results in a range of neurological and nonneurological problems, including splenomegaly, hepatomegaly, cognitive decline, and, in severe cases, neonatal mortality. NPC disease can occur at any age: infantile, late‐infantile, juvenile, and adult stage [7]. NPC disease is classified as rare, with an estimated incidence of 1 in 100,000 to 120,000 [7, 8, 9]. However, emerging evidence suggests that this prevalence may be underestimated [10].

NPC disease is further classified into two subtypes, NPC‐1 and NPC‐2, which arise from pathogenic variants in NPC1 and NPC2 genes. The two genes, NPC1 and NPC2, encode two proteins by the same name, NPC1 and NPC2, which localize to the lysosome. NPC1 is a large (1278 amino acids), membrane‐bound protein localized at the limiting membrane of lysosomes [11], whereas NPC2 is a smaller (152 amino acids) soluble protein present in the lysosomal lumen [12]. Due to the larger size of NPC1 relative to NPC2, ≈95% of known NPC mutations map to NPC1, while only 5% are associated with NPC2 [6].

The mechanism of cholesterol transfer between NPC1 and NPC2 is still not fully understood. Based on in vitro binding assays [13], sterol transfer experiments [14] and the structural data of both NPC2 and NPC1 [14, 15] a hydrophobic hand‐off model has been proposed [13, 16]. In this model, NPC2 accepts cholesterol liberated by acid lipase following cholesteryl ester hydrolysis and subsequently transfers it to the N‐terminal domain (NTD) of NPC1, without exposure to the aqueous solvent [16]. Supporting this hand‐off model, alanine‐scanning mutagenesis of both proteins identified residues critical not only for sterol binding but also for transfer, many of which are located at the predicted interface of the NPC1‐NPC2 complex [16]. Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations have also provided insight into the possible pathway for cholesterol transfer from NPC2 to NPC1 [17, 18, 19, 20, 21]. After receiving cholesterol, NPC1 is believed to facilitate the transport of cholesterol across the glycocalyx‐coated endolysosomal membrane, thereby mediating its export from LE and lysosomes [16]. Interestingly, NPC2 is also able to transfer cholesterol to the lysosomal membrane without any interaction with NPC1 [22].

The crystal structures of bovine NPC2 (PDB ID: 2HKA [23]) and human NPC2 (PDB ID: 6W5V [15]), determined by X‐ray crystallography, reveal a compact immunoglobulin‐like β‐sandwich containing two β‐sheets [12] (Figure 1A,1B). The bovine structure 1NEP [12] identifies an apolar rim composed of six hydrophobic residues: V59, V64, F66, Y100, P101, and I103) [23] (Figure 1C). This rim delineates an aperture that remains closed in the apo state and opens upon cholesterol binding [23]. Gate opening is mediated by concerted reorientation of the Tyr101 and Phe66 side chains, and mutations at either position have been shown to reduce cholesterol affinity and disrupt function [12, 23, 25]. Sequence analyses and structural data of human NPC2 indicate that key residues and the overall 3D fold are conserved (79% sequence identity) relative to the bovine protein [26, 27] (Figure 1C,1D).

A naturally occurring human mutant of NPC2, P120S fails to accommodate cholesterol within the NPC2 binding cavity and to transfer the sterol from NPC1(NTD) to liposomes [28, 29]. Moreover, homozygosity for P120S in an NPC2 patient leads to a phenotype of severe dystonia and intellectual disability [28]. In vitro sterol‐binding assays show that P120S abolishes affinity, whereas cell‐based studies demonstrate an almost complete block in lysosomal egress, suggesting that proline residue at position 120 is critical for protein behavior [30].

Three further residues have been identified through alanine‐scanning mutagenesis as crucial for transferring cholesterol to NPC1(NTD): M79, V81, and V83 [16, 31]. V81 and V83 create a hydrophobic environment for interactions with G199 and Q200 in NPC1(NTD) [14]. In vitro binding assays with radio‐labelled [3H]‐cholesterol have demonstrated that V81A and V81D variants retain wild‐type (WT) binding affinity and kinetics, and in vitro transfer experiments have revealed a partial defect for V81A and a severe defect for V81D [16]. Met79 constitutes part of the M79/V81/V83 ‘transfer patch’ [17]. Structural analysis of the NPC1 middle lumenal domain (MLD) in complex with NPC2 shows that the M79 side chain forms extensive hydrophobic contacts with Y423 and Y506 on NPC1(MLD) loops [14]; accordingly, substitution at M79 is found in transfer assays to impair cholesterol transfer while leaving binding largely intact. Further evidence that this ‘transfer patch’ is significant for sterol trafficking comes from in vitro experiments [32]; mutations within the region 76–83, also referred to as a ‘hydrophobic knob’ [22], prevent cholesterol transfer from NPC2 to LBPA‐enriched egg phosphatidylcholine membranes [22]. Consistent with these results, modeling of NPC2 with a lipid bilayer [33, 34] predicts that residues 76–83 engage directly with the lipid bilayer [32]. Mutagenesis studies have further shown that two additional NPC2 variants, P100A and G76A, inhibit cholesterol binding and transfer, respectively [16]. Interestingly, P100 is nearly 40 Å from the binding aperture and has no direct contact with the sterol ligand (Figure 1B).

Here, we focus on the behavior of NPC2 with and without cholesterol to understand the influence of the sterol on protein conformation prior to cholesterol hand‐off to NPC1. To investigate the mechanisms of cholesterol binding and transfer in NPC2, we have used extensive all‐atom MD simulations to compare the behavior of cholesterol‐bound NPC2 to the behavior of apo NPC2 for WT and mutants. We focus on the following variants, which have been identified in biochemical assays or in clinical phenotypes to be critical for sterol binding and transport: G76A, L78A, M79A, V81A, V81D, V83A, V83D, P100A, and P120S (Figure 1B). Comparison of the WT species with these mutants provides insight into connecting structural changes with the biochemical and clinical phenotypes observed in NPC disease (Table S1).

Methods

The atomic coordinates of structures analyzed in the study are taken from the RCSB Protein Data Bank [35]. The human NPC2 structure has been solved in a cholesterol‐bound “open” conformation [14, 15], but structural data of the apo state are not available. The structure of WT human NPC2 was taken from the pH 5.5 NPC1‐NPC2 complex (PDB ID: 6W5V [15]) in the cholesterol‐bound state (4.0 Å resolution). The structures were refined using the bovine NPC2 structure (PDB ID: 1NEP [12]) that is obtained by X‐ray diffraction with a resolution of 1.70 Å with the help of the matchmaker tool from ChimeraX [36]. The corresponding apo model was produced by deleting the ligand from the binding pocket. The molecular topology and coordinate files for cholesterol‐bound and apo models were generated with CHARMM36m force field [37], and hydrogen atoms were added using HBUILD as implemented in the program CHARMM‐GUI [38]. The apo variants G76A, L78A, M79A, V81A, V81D, V83D, P100A, and P120S and holo variants M79A, V81A, V81D, and V83D were prepared using CHARMM‐GUI [39] from the refined NPC2 structure. The disulfide bonds (C27‐C140, C42‐C47, and C93‐C99) were constructed by the CHARMM‐GUI. Each protein was centered in an octahedral simulation box that extended at least 1.2 nm from any solute atom, solvated with TIP3P water [40], and neutralized with Na+ and Cl− ions. The CHARMM36m force field [37] was used for all subsequent energy minimization and simulations. Energy minimization of the system was performed using the steepest descent method with a maximum force cut‐off of 1000 kJ mol−1 nm−1 to remove steric clashes and to relax the geometry of the structures. The solvent and ions around the proteins were then equilibrated using NVT (canonical or isothermal‐isochoric) ensemble for 100 ps with a reference temperature of 310.15 K. The long‐range electrostatic interactions were evaluated using the particle mesh Ewald (PME) method [40]. The short‐range van der Waals interactions were computed using Verlet neighbor list calculation with a cut‐off of 10 Å. The P‐LINCS algorithm was used to constrain the bond lengths, and a time step of 2 fs was used to integrate the equations of motion [41]. A velocity‐rescaling thermostat with a coupling constant of 0.1 ps was used to control the reference temperature at 310.15 K [42]. The reference pressure and coupling constant were set to 100 kPa (1 bar) and 2 ps, respectively, and were controlled using the C‐rescale barostat [43].

Production MD simulations were performed for WT and each mutant in both ligand‐free (apo) and cholesterol‐bound (holo) states. For each system, three independent replicas were run for 500 ns each, initiated with different initial velocity seeds, yielding 1.5 μs total sampling per system. All subsequent analyses are performed on three independent replicas per system; reported values are replica averages, and variability is reported across replicas.

MD simulations were performed using the package GROMACS 2023.2 [44]. Coordinates of the systems were collected every 10 ps for a total of 10 000 frames for each run. Trajectory analyses were carried out with built‐in GROMACS utilities and the MD Analysis [45] library in Python. Visual inspection employed VMD [46] and PyMOL [47], and plots were generated with Matplotlib [48] library in Python.

Pocket volumes are calculated using analysis of null areas (ANA) [49]. Pocket‐lining atoms are specified, a convex hull is constructed, and the surrounding space is divided into a mesh of 3D wedges (tetrahedra) by Delaunay triangulation [50]. At each MD frame, only tetrahedra (or their clipped portions) that lie inside the hull and exceed the probe threshold, that is, accessible to a virtual spherical probe of user‐defined radius (1.4 Å by default), are retained; atomic van der Waals volumes are subtracted, and the cavity volume is obtained by summing the retained tetrahedral volumes. In low‐precision mode, entire intersecting tetrahedra are counted (subject to the probe cutoff), whereas in high‐precision mode, each intersecting tetrahedron is clipped to its exact intersection with the hull before summation. In this work, the high‐precision option is used for all volume calculations. To suppress algorithmic noise from loop fluctuations, where triangulation can amplify small atomic displacements into spurious variance, a moving average filter is applied to the pocket‐volume time series before analysis, reducing frame‐to‐frame jitter while preserving longer‐timescale trends.

To analyze and compare the conformational changes occurring in the simulations of the apo and holo forms of each protein, we conducted principal component analysis (PCA) of protein Cα carbon atoms on a concatenated trajectory of the apo and holo forms of the wild type and its variants using the scikit‐learn package [51]. Prior to PCA, the Cα coordinates were least‐squares‐fitted to the average structure to eliminate global translation and rotation. Subsequently, a 3 N × 3 N covariance matrix of positional fluctuations was constructed and diagonalized to yield eigenvectors (collective motions) and eigenvalues (amplitudes). The first few principal components, ranked by descending eigenvalue, capture the dominant conformational changes. Upon examination of the resulting principal components, we focused on the first principal component, PC1 which exhibits the highest variance.

To calculate the diameter of rim residues, the distance from the center of geometry (COG) of each of the six rim residues was measured, and the averaged value from the six radii yields the diameter of the aperture (Figure 2A). To measure gate closure, the centroid‐to‐centroid distance between the aromatic rings of Y119 and F85 was calculated (Figure 2B). In addition, loop separation at the pocket rim was evaluated as the distance between the Cα centers of mass of residues on the opposing gate loops (Figure 2C).

(A) The aperture diameter is computed per frame as twice the mean distance from the side‐chain COGs of the six apolar‐rim residues (L78, V83, F85, Y119, P120, I122) to their common center of geometry (black triangle). (B) The distance between the side‐chain heavy‐atom COMs of Y119 and F85 is indicated with a dotted line. (C) The interloop separation is measured between the COM of Cα atoms on the βE‐βF gate (Y119, P120, I122; red sphere 1) and the COM of Cα atoms on the opposing βC‐βD gate (L78, V83, F85; red sphere 2). Red spheres denote COMs; distances are reported in Å and evaluated for every frame of the trajectories.

Results and Discussion

To investigate how cholesterol binding influences NPC2 protein flexibility, we focus on variants recognized as critical to cholesterol binding, G76A, L78A, P100A, and P120S [16] as well as variants critical for sterol transfer [16, 22] M79A, V81A, V81D, V83A, and V83D. Positions 79, 81, and 83 in NPC2 are important because they constitute a “transfer patch” that the protein relies on for establishing contact with NPC1(NTD) loop region containing L175, G199, Q200, Y506, Y423 through electrostatic and hydrophilic interactions [14]. Position 120 is critical as it is known to engage with NPC1(NTD) residues K179, D180, and D182 [14]. Indeed, in vitro biochemical assays show that substitution at position 120 abolishes cholesterol binding [16]. Position 100 is located distal to the binding pocket (Figure 1B), yet substitution at this site is found in biochemical assays to disrupt cholesterol binding [16], suggesting that, despite its distance from the binding pocket, position 100 is critical for maintaining a binding‐competent pocket conformation.

Structural Stability of WT and Mutants‐Apo and Cholesterol‐Bound RMSD Analysis

To assess the overall protein structural stability, backbone RMSD values of WT and variants were calculated for all three replicates of 500 ns trajectories (Figure 3, S1–S3). Overall, in absence of sterol, NPC2 is less conformationally restricted than in the cholesterol‐bound state. Cholesterol‐bound NPC2 shows a reduced RMSD profile (1.3 ± 0.1 Å) compared to apo NPC2 (1.6 ± 0.1 Å) (Figure 3A, Table S3). Apo trajectories exhibit higher backbone variability than holo trajectories (Figure 3, S1), consistent with the general observation that cholesterol binding reduces the protein's structural mobility [52], a pattern consistently observed across WT and variants. Overall, while ligand binding typically reduces backbone mobility and leads to lower RMSD values, cholesterol‐bound V81A, V81D and V83D exhibit elevated RMSD values relative to cholesterol‐bound WT (Figure S3 and Table S3). The insertion of a negatively charged side‐chain at position 81, introducing favorable electrostatic interactions at the aqueous solvent‐exposed aperture, likely increases conformational mobility. These observations are consistent with mutagenesis data indicating that substitutions at or near rim residues reduce cholesterol affinity [31].

Root‐Mean‐Square Fluctuation (RMSF) Mapping Identifies Local Dynamic Hotspots in Apo and Holo NPC2 Variants

Next, to identify protein regions that demonstrate notable backbone changes, we performed a RMSF analysis across the NPC2 sequence (Cα) of each variant for apo and cholesterol‐bound states (Figures 4 and S4, respectively). Residues with RMSF values exceeding 2 Å are considered as highly flexible, and five clusters of elevated RMSF values are identified, centered at S29, T44, G80, Y119, and K134 (Figure 4A). In general, prominent RMSF peaks primarily occur in disordered loop regions, though loop flexibility varies among mutants. Apo WT and nearly all mutants (P120S, V81A, V81D, M79A, L78A, and P100A) exhibit elevated flexibility around residues 79–81 (Figure 4A, S4A, Table S4), which lie within the region commonly referred to as the ‘transfer patch’ (residues M79, V81, and V83). This patch is believed to facilitate transient loop rearrangements that enable docking to NPC1 and subsequent sterol hand‐off [17, 19, 20, 21]. In the cholesterol‐bound state, WT NPC2 shows a flattened RMSF profile relative to apo WT, with fluctuations at the transfer patch falling below the high‐mobility threshold (2 Å), consistent with sterol‐induced damping of loop motions (Figure 4B and S4B, Table S4). In the holo state, transfer‐defective variants M79A, V81A, V81D, and V83D continue to exhibit elevated RMSF at or near the transfer patch, indicating that ligand binding does not fully suppress entrance‐loop dynamics (Figures 4B and S4B, Table S4). The gating loop, therefore, remains dynamic in transfer‐defective variants, whereas its motion is attenuated in holo WT (Figure S4B). Overall, sterol binding stabilizes the entrance loop in WT but not in the mutants. Enhanced loop flexibility at or near rim residues in NPC2 variants therefore, likely compromises formation of the transient docking geometry required for NPC1 engagement and subsequent sterol transfer.

Cα RMSF analysis of NPC2 and its variants in (A) apo and (B) holo forms. Per‐residue RMSF profiles averaged over three 500 ns replicates. Dashed circles indicate regions showing enhanced flexibility across multiple variants.

Impact of Mutations on Cavity Volume Dynamics

To explore the impact of mutations on the volume of the cholesterol binding pocket, the cavity volume of the NPC2 binding pocket was analyzed for each trajectory. The NPC2 ligand binding pocket is composed exclusively of hydrophobic amino acids [25] (Figure 5). The collective dynamics of these residues were tracked over the total 1.5 µs trajectories for both WT and mutant NPC2 structures using the cavity analysis tool ANA [49] (Figure 6A‐J).

In the absence of cholesterol, WT and nearly all variants exhibit an average binding pocket volume of ~60 Å3, and this cavity volume remains relatively stable throughout the 500 ns simulation (Figure 6A, Table S6).

PCA Identifies Collective Motions at the NPC2 Binding Rim

Given that the RMSF primarily reflects the plasticity of local regions, we conducted a PCA to examine the impact of mutations on large‐scale motions. We performed PCA on the concatenated trajectories, as this method is effective in capturing the dominant, large‐scale motions in protein conformations observed during the simulations [53]. For consistency in this analysis, holo simulations of binding‐defective variants were also conducted, even though these mutants in their holo form do not represent true thermodynamic minima. Through this approach, we identify the protein regions experiencing the most significant collective motion (Figure 7, 8). Our analysis identifies the first principal component (PC1), which accounts for most of the variance in the dynamics, averaging 31% across all variants and the wild type (WT) in our dataset (Figure S5, Table S6).

Superimposed protein structures (gray: position 1 and red: position 2) reveal the range of prominent protein motions, particularly in loop and rim regions. Black arrows represent the direction of displacement calculated using the PyMOL [46] modevectors script.

The collective protein motion of PC1 correlates with an opening and closing of loop and rim areas, thus controlling the plasticity of NPC2. This motion is concentrated in five protein segments: L1, the N‐terminus of βB (region 28–32), the βC‐βD rim (region 78–84), the βE‐βF rim (region 115–122), and L5 (region 133–136) (Figure 1B, 7). Consequently, the dominant collective mode primarily influences the entrance region and loops rather than the β‐sandwich core. We observe bending of βB, βC, βD, and βE at the rim region toward the binding pocket (Figures 8A‐8I), leading to the closure of the pocket (Figure 8A‐I). The pattern across all variants is similar, with the βC‐βD rim exhibiting the largest range of motion. P120S demonstrates enhanced PC1 amplitude at 115–120 (Figure 7, 8J), indicating that substitution of P120 increases motion at the βE‐βF edge and can alter entrance geometry during closure. Only one variant, P100A, displays relatively low PC1 contributions over the rim segments, consistent with an open protein conformation that lacks the coordinated motions required for pocket closure and binding (Figure 7, 8I).

To further investigate the role of cholesterol‐binding residues, we analyzed the noncovalent interactions that stabilize cholesterol recognition in NPC2. In sterol‐bound NPC2 crystal structures, the cholesterol iso‐octyl side chain is buried within an apolar cavity, while the 3β‐hydroxyl group points towards the pocket opening and solvent. The binding pocket is primarily hydrophobic, suggesting that van der Waals and hydrophobic interactions are the dominant forces behind cholesterol recognition. We quantified the interactions between the pocket residues and cholesterol by calculating per‐residue cholesterol contact occupancies, which represent the fraction of trajectory frames in which any residue lies within 4.5 Å of any cholesterol heavy atom. The analysis (Figure S6) reveals frequent, long‐lived contacts with several hydrophobic pocket residues (V39, L49, V57, V74, L78, V83, F85, Y119, P120, I122, L124, Vl126, W128, W141, I143, Vl145, and I147). These residues suggest a recognition mode driven by cholesterol shape complementarity and apolar packing interactions. In contrast, residues like N58 show lower contact occupancy, consistent with a binding mode dominated by hydrophobic rather than polar interactions.

To distinguish between “static” and “dynamic” residues, we analyzed RMSF (Cα) values for residues lining the binding pocket in both apo and cholesterol‐bound NPC2 trajectories. This analysis was complemented by PCA, performed on the concatenated (apo+holo) trajectory, to capture ensemble‐wide variability. Residues exhibiting both low RMSF values and small contributions to the primary component (PC1) were classified as the most static residues (57–59, 74,76, 126,128,145,147). Conversely, residues showing higher RMSF values and/or dominant PC1 contributions were classified as the most dynamic (78, 83, 85, 113–115, 119−120, 122).

Averaging across three independent replicas yielded consistent results, with the largest RMSF values and PC1 contributions clustering at residues adjacent to the pocket entrance. These residues primarily modulate access to the binding pocket and local rearrangements in the rim. In contrast, deeper cavity residues remain relatively rigid, contributing to the hydrophobic packing that stabilizes cholesterol in the cavity. This pattern of dynamics is consistent with the view that entrance‐adjacent residues, such as V64, F66, K97, Y100, and I103, become more rigid upon cholesterol binding, supporting the concept of a “locked” configuration upon ligand binding [23].

Dynamic Gating Metrics Reveal Mechanistic Insights of Rim Opening and Sterol Transfer

To understand how the binding cavity aperture is dynamically gated, the positions of rim residues were investigated. Analogous analyses of bovine NPC2 crystallographic studies show that sterol binding widens the tunnel mouth through the displacement of key rim residues V59, V64, F66, Y100, P101, and P103 [13]. Accordingly, we mapped the equivalent positions in human NPC2, that is, L78, V83, F85, Y119, P120, and I122, respectively, and treated these six hydrophobic amino acids as a fixed apolar rim. Three quantitative descriptors are defined to monitor the gating motion during the simulations: (1) The instantaneous diameter of the apolar rim that frames the tunnel mouth (Figure 2A); (2) The distance between the center of mass (COM) of heavy atoms of the aromatic side chains of Y119 and F85, whose separation changes upon cholesterol binding in bovine NPC2 [23] (Figure 2B); (3) The distance between the COM of two opposing gate loops (βC‐βD, Cα atoms L78‐F85, and βE‐βF, Cα atoms Y119‐I122) that collectively “zip” the entrance (Figure 2C).

The rim diameter of apo WT and variants, averaged over the triplicates of 500 ns trajectory, is between ~10–12 Å (Figure S7 and Table S7). Interestingly, the largest rim diameter (12.3 ± 1.3 Å) is observed for apo V83D (Figure 9A and Table S7). V83, part of the transfer‐patch, is situated in the apolar rim on the βD strand; substitutions at this site modulate aperture spacing. Replacement of valine by aspartic acid at this site introduces a charged sidechain, so the βC‐βD region is displaced away from the βD‐βE side, correlating with RMSF and PCA analysis with high peaks around these regions (Figure 7B). Apo P100A slightly expands the rim relative to WT (10.9 ± 0.2 Å) (Figure 9B and Table S7), as P100, located on loop L4, is positioned at the βD‐βE hinge.

Time‐resolved aperture diameter (shaded traces) is computed each frame from the six apolar‐rim residues (L78, V83, F85, Y119, P120, and I122); horizontal lines denote mean across three replicas.

For cholesterol‐bound NPC2 variants, the sterol widens the rim diameter compared to apo variants (Figure S8 and Table S7). The V81 and V83 positions are sensitive to substitution, in agreement with in vitro experiments that show a partial transfer defect for V81A and a severe defect for V81D [16]. The mutant V83D exhibits the largest diameter (15.2 ± 1.2 Å) relative to WT (Figure S8 and Table S7). A broadening of the cavity entrance may prevent recognition and/or precise loop alignment with NPC1 required for sterol transfer.

In addition to rim diameter, the orientation of gating residues Y119 and F85, is a metric that reveals structural differences between NPC2 variants. The π‐π gating of aromatic side chains of these two residues regulates sterol passage to enter or exit the cavity (Figure 1C,D). To interpret the ring motions, we extracted structures at the beginning and end of each trajectory and superposed these on the starting frame (Figure 10A‐J). The average distance between Y119 and F85 is ≈6–7 Å across most variants, a pattern observed in both the apo and holo forms (Figures S9, S10, and Table S8). The distance between the side chain rings was found to be comparable to that of the wild type (WT). However, the orientation of the side chains varies among the mutants (Figure 10). Relative to WT, V81A demonstrates a significant change during simulation; Y119 flips outward toward the solvent, consistent with the increased spacing and the βD displacement toward βE (Figure 10E). These data suggest that altered orientation of side chain rings disturbs π‐π gating in binding‐defective mutants (G76A, L78A, P100A, and P120S), thereby affecting the mechanism of cholesterol binding. In transfer‐defective variants (M79A, V81A, V81D, V83A, and V83D), the altered rim behavior likely affects NPC1‐dependent sterol hand‐off, a conclusion consistent with binding assays [13] and in silico studies [19] reported for these substitutions.

A metric related to the rim geometry, the interloop separation distance (βC‐βD ↔ βE‐βF) was also measured to study the behavior of WT and variant models. In apo trajectories for WT, this distance fluctuates around 10–11 Å (Figure S11, Table S9). Among binding‐defective variants, some, like L78A, cause the aperture to narrow, while others, such as P100A and P120S, lead to its widening. In cholesterol‐bound systems, the interloop distance is larger on average, fluctuating around 12–13 Å (Figure S12, Table S9), indicating that sterol binding reduces the range of motion of these loops. The collective motion of loops βC‐βD and βE‐βF may be a mechanism by which the protein signals its availability for docking and transfer.

Overall, the combined analyses of the rim aperture, Y119‐F85 gating metrics, and interloop distance reveal trends that may explain deficiencies in cholesterol binding and transfer for NPC2 mutants. Previous experimental work established a clear functional distinction between NPC2 variants that are defective in sterol binding (e.g., G76A, L78A, P100A, P120S) [16, 28] and variants that retain sterol binding but fail to transfer cholesterol efficiently to NPC1 (e.g., M79A, V81A, V81D, V83A, and V83D) [16]. Cellular and biochemical assays further demonstrated that even under conditions favoring transfer, such as increased concentrations of negatively charged lysosomal lipids, alterations at rim positions (e.g., G76, V81, and V83) significantly reduce the rate of cholesterol export from NPC2 to lysosomes [22]. While these studies firmly established the functional importance of rim and surface residues, they were based on endpoint measurements and therefore did not provide a dynamic, structural explanation for how these defects arise. Complementary computational studies on bovine NPC2‐NPC1 complexes demonstrated which residues are important for making a transient complex and how transfer‐defective mutations weaken the binding energetics of the protein–protein interface [14, 17, 19, 20, 21, but these studies did not directly resolve how NPC2 internal dynamics contribute to this loss of function.

The simulations presented here provide this missing dynamical context. At the level of backbone fluctuations and collective motions, both binding‐defective and transfer‐defective variants perturb a common structural element of NPC2: the rim and entrance‐adjacent loop regions that gate access to the hydrophobic cavity. RMSF and PCA analyses consistently identify residues 78, 83, 85, 113–115, and 119–122 as dominant contributors to conformational variability across all variants that accompany the apo‐to‐holo transition. These observations suggest that the transfer patch is not a static interaction surface but a dynamic gate whose coordinated motions allow cholesterol to be transferred from NPC2 to NPC1 without exposure to aqueous solvent. Thus, in mutant NPC2 species, altered behavior of rim residues leads to both binding‐defective and transfer‐defective phenotypes.

These results reconcile prior experimental observations by showing that NPC2 function depends on a finely tuned dynamic balance: a relatively rigid hydrophobic core that stabilizes cholesterol binding, combined with a flexible but coordinated rim that enables controlled access and productive transfer. Mutations that perturb this balance can destabilize sterol capture or sterol handoff. Both of these cholesterol handling mechanisms ‐ transfer and binding ‐ are therefore highly sensitive to critical single point mutations.

Conclusion

Extensive all‐atom MD simulations performed on multiple variants of human NPC2, both with and without cholesterol, reveal differences in the conformational landscape associated with each variant. Mutations in the apolar rim residues (M79, V81, V83, F85, Y119, P120, and I122) directly influence the rim geometry, thereby likely affecting cholesterol entry into the binding pocket. Variations in aperture rim geometry are observed for apo and cholesterol‐bound NPC2 states. The sequence segment encompassing residues 78–81, which includes two of the three residues (79, 81, 83) comprising the ‘transfer patch,’ emerges as the locally most flexible region in the variants, irrespective of whether cholesterol is present in the pocket or not. Indeed, mutation‐induced changes occur predominantly in the hydrophobic region, which is known to interact with the lipid bilayer or with NPC1‐NTD.

PCA consistently shows that the dominant collective motion (PC1, ~30%–38% variance) is concentrated at the two entrance‐gating segments: βC‐βD (region 79–83) and βE‐βF (region 116–120). In all variants these loop modes are altered relative to WT behavior.

Binding pocket analyses identify structural features that affect protein dynamics. In binding‐defective variants (G76A, L78A, P100A, P120S), Y119‐F85 π‐π stacking behavior drives movements of the βC‐D and βE‐F loops, resulting in either constriction or widening of the aperture rim with respect to WT geometry.

Proline substitutions at P100 and P120 produce distinct structural perturbations. Relative to WT, P100A shows enhanced mobility in βC‐D loops and distinct Y119‐F85 ring flipping motion, indicating that mutations far from the rim allosterically modulate the rim geometry. How the entrance responds as the ligand approaches a closed conformation remains unresolved.

In the P120S mutant, the volume of the binding cavity is reduced compared to that of the WT protein. Although the overall pocket dynamics resemble those of WT, including the characteristic Y119‐F85 ring‐flipping motion, the residues near position 120 display increased flexibility, as indicated by elevated RMSF and higher PC1 contributions relative to WT. This enhanced mobility results in local distortions of the rim geometry. Overall, these perturbations likely make the protein environment thermodynamically less favorable for sterol transfer and binding.

Mutations of residues in the ‘transfer patch’ (M79A, V81A, V81D, V83A, and V83D) perturb loop motion that perturb the gating metrics. These results suggest that successful cholesterol hand‐off relies on the loops forming stable connections with NPC1. Indeed, these variants follow WT‐like pocket closure when cholesterol is not present.

Overall, these in silico studies further our understanding of structure features of NPC2 that may influence cholesterol binding and transfer. Although the structure of apo human NPC2 has not yet been resolved, a cholesterol‐free model, constructed from structural data of holo NPC2, provides us with an approximation of the protein in the absence of the sterol. Structural data of apo human NPC2 would provide further insight into the mechanism of cholesterol binding and transfer and may help further elucidate the role of gating residues.
