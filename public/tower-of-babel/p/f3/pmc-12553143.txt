Introduction

Acute lumbosacral radiculopathy from disc herniation is common and particularly painful [1, 2]. Except in rare cases of motor deficit or cauda equina syndrome, management consists primarily of conservative medical treatment combining physical measures, antinociceptive analgesics, systemic anti-inflammatory drugs and epidural steroid injections. The challenge is to provide sufficient relief to the patient until the natural regression of the herniated disc allows them to heal. In the majority of cases, acute lumbosacral radiculopathy from disc herniation disappears within a few weeks, and surgery is only offered if the symptoms persist, generally after three months. The problem is that during this time, pain relief measures currently available are often insufficient, with only partial and uncertain efficacy [1–5].

Acute lumbosacral radiculopathy from disc herniation is usually considered to be induced by excessive nociception linked to local inflammation. For this reason, and because the pain is acute, neurotropic analgesics are not used. However, this pain likely has different causes, including a combination of neuropathic phenomena resulting from nerve damage. Indeed, when a nerve root is compressed by a herniated disc, chemical phenomena rapidly occur, leading to inflammatory reactions, but also functional and histological changes, resulting in hyperexcitability of the root and ectopic electrical activity. Therefore, nerve root impingement is likely to cause acute neuropathic pain [6–8].

Current painkillers only help control the nociceptive component of pain. A more global approach could be to concomitantly prescribe neurotropic analgesics acting on the neuropathic component, taking into account all the mechanisms generating pain, to obtain more effective pain relief while limiting the use of strong opioids. This concept is in fact fairly similar to the multimodal analgesia approach currently used for anaesthesia.

Gabapentin is a neurotropic analgesic that is recommended for first-line treatment of neuropathic pain [9, 10]. It has anti-hyperalgesic properties which are exerted via its effect on the medullary presynaptic calcium channels [11]. Hyperalgesia results in greater sensitivity to pain, which sets in quickly, and can increase the intensity and duration of acute pain. The antihyperalgesic action of gabapentin has been demonstrated in postoperative pain, and taking it briefly in the perioperative period helps to reduce the intensity of postoperative pain and the use of strong opioids over the subsequent days. These data show that the analgesic effect induced by gabapentin in the context of mixed pain can be rapid and continue even after discontinuing administration [12, 13]. We therefore hypothesise that taking gabapentin for a short time could help reduce the intensity of pain in acute lumbosacral radiculopathy from disc herniation.

The data on the efficacy of neurotropic analgesics in radicular pain relate almost exclusively to chronic neuropathic pain [14–16]. The short-term efficacy of a treatment like gabapentin has never been evaluated in acute lumbosacral radiculopathy from disc herniation. A very preliminary case study suggests its benefits [17]. Only one randomised trial has studied the usefulness of a neurotropic analgesic in acute radicular pain, and it concluded that it was ineffective [14]. However, this study does not answer the question asked: pregabalin was used (whose recommendation level is lower than gabapentin in neuropathic pain) [9, 10]; all radicular pain was considered whatever the cause (with very different pathophysiological mechanisms, radiculalgia linked to referred pain of posterior articular origin for example, having little in common with acute lumbosacral radiculopathy from disc herniation); radicular pain was included regardless of duration (meaning that patients with acute pain represented only a portion of the population studied); pain was assessed in the medium and long term (and not in the short term, in the early critical period of acute lumbosacral radiculopathy from disc herniation, a condition in which the pain is not expected to become chronic and for which the priority is to bring fast relief to the patient until the natural progression of the condition leads to healing).

We therefore wish to set up the first prospective, multicentre, randomised, controlled, double-blind superiority study evaluating the efficacy of gabapentin versus placebo for short-term management of acute lumbosacral radiculopathy from disc herniation. We believe that gabapentin, when combined with the usual analgesic treatments, could reduce pain intensity and secondary hyperalgesia in acute lumbosacral radiculopathy from disc herniation. The primary objective of the study is to assess the short-term analgesic efficacy of gabapentin compared to the placebo in acute lumbosacral radiculopathy from disc herniation.

Study design

This is a prospective, multicentre, randomised, placebo-controlled, patient and study personnel-blinded superiority study.

Intervention

Randomisation

Patients will be randomised by the physician on the day of their inclusion, after their signed informed consent is obtained by the investigator (physician), with no plans of ancillary studies.

Randomisation will be centralised by an electronic randomisation and treatment arm allocation module and will be based on a list pre-compiled by computer program and verified according to the sponsor’s procedures. The randomisation list will be drawn up by a statistician from the GHICL Clinical Research and Innovation Division Biostatistics Unit before starting the study, according to a 1:1 ratio to the gabapentin or placebo groups, balanced in blocks of variable size, and stratified according to initial intake of strong opioids (step 3 analgesics). The statistician drawing up the randomisation list will store it on a secure system. Once the eligibility of a participant is determined, randomization will be obtained by the investigator using the electronic randomization module included in the eCRF.

Stratification according to intake of strong opioids will make it possible to balance the severity of the LSR pain between the groups, and also avoid introducing bias in the event of a different gabapentin-related synergistic or pharmacokinetic effect for strong opioids and other antinociceptive analgesics.

Blinding methods

The study is double blind, meaning that investigators, study personnel and patients will remain blinded to treatment allocation. To adhere to double blind conditions, the electronic randomisation module will give a treatment number.

Randomisation data will remain strictly confidential and will be accessible only to authorised personnel until unblinding. Authorised personnel may include the statistician who made the randomisation list, an unblinded statistician, and unblinded programmers. The randomisation list will be stored on a secure system.

Investigational treatments will be labelled to protect blinding. The coding system will enable the treatment to be rapidly identified (in case of a medical emergency).

Unblinding method

The investigator should only request unblinding in the event of a medical emergency, when knowledge of the allocated treatment is needed for the patient’s care.

Unblinding may also be requested by pharmacovigilance in the event of a SUSAR when the investigator and/or the sponsor considers a causal link possible and the sponsor considers the event as unexpected.

Unblinding can be done 24/7 via the CleanWeb (IWRS) electronic CRF unblinding module. In order to be able to confirm unblinding, the investigator must provide the reason for their request. A reminder of the conditions for unblinding is provided by the IWRS module, and the investigator must confirm that they have read these conditions.

The randomisation arm can then be provided to the investigator.

A summary of the information is sent to the investigator who made the unblinding request.

The investigator will have to provide the sponsor with the exact reasons for unblinding and document them.

An unblinding procedure will be drafted and available in the Investigator’s Brochure.

Patients who are unblinded will no longer receive the study treatment, but will be monitored for up to seven days as described in the procedure for study patients.

In accordance with the dosage and method of administration of oral gabapentin.

The investigational treatment may be administered during or outside meal times and should be swallowed whole with a sufficient amount of liquid (e.g. a glass of water).

The dose chosen in the protocol follows the GABAPENTIN dosing regimen, discontinuing treatment after three days (Table 1). Indeed, our hypothesis is that taking GABAPENTIN for a brief period is sufficient to rapidly reduce the intensity of pain, with benefits lasting several days after discontinuing treatment. This is based especially on the results observed in studies on postoperative pain and its proven ability to reduce primary and secondary hyperalgesia [13].

Discontinuation of the investigational treatment

Treatment discontinuation will be proposed for intolerance in the event of a grade ≥ 2 adverse event on the NCI scale.

Precautions for use in case of concomitant use of opioids

Patients requiring concomitant treatment with opioids should be closely monitored for signs of central nervous system (CNS) depression, such as somnolence, sedation and respiratory depression.

Overdose

No acute life-threatening toxicity has been observed in cases of gabapentin overdoses up to 49 g.

In the event of an overdose, the sponsor should be contacted to discuss the details of the overdose and formulate a patient care plan.

Authorized and prohibited treatments

Antinociceptive analgesic treatments

The treatment (gabapentin/placebo) will be combined with antinociceptive analgesics (non-opioids, weak opioids, strong opioids, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs) prescribed at the clinician’s discretion, according to the usual treatment. No interactions between antinociceptive analgesics and gabapentin have been described.

To measure only the effect of the treatment (gabapentin/placebo), the antinociceptive analgesic treatment must be continued systematically (maintenance therapy, generally extended release, at a set dosage over one day) and must not be modified between D1 and the end of the evaluation on D4 (product, dosage, number of doses, route of administration).

This systematic treatment may be combined with on-demand treatment, in the form of intermediate doses (fast and short-acting analgesic action) which will be recorded in the CRF, to manage transient breakthrough pain. The aim of an intermediate dose is to counteract breakthrough pain to return to the “basal” level of pain intensity, which will be measured by VAS at the different evaluation times.

Prohibited treatments/procedures

Prescribing pregabalin (neurotropic analgesic with a mechanism of action similar to gabapentin) and administering epidural steroid injections between the first capsule on D1 and the end of the evaluation on D4 will not be authorised.

After D4, the investigator may change the antinociceptive analgesics (increase or decrease in pain relief) and give steroid injections if deemed useful.

Data analysis plan

Calculation of the number of subjects

The number of subjects is calculated based on the power of the test comparing the mean changes in the radicular pain VAS on D4. In the literature, there is no study on short-term analgesic efficacy in acute LSR, only in chronic LSR, and in a model of acute postoperative pain. In this latter context, the mechanism of pain induced by surgery is very different to the radicular pain we are studying. Since there is no literature indicating the expected standard deviation associated with these mean changes, we based our calculation on the expected “effect size” (= difference in means/standard deviation of pain VAS change). According to Cohen’s guidelines (1988), for an effect size that we hope is clinically at least “moderate”, i.e. 0.5, a power of 80%, a type 1 risk of 5%, and 10% of patients lost to follow-up, 72 patients are needed per group, i.e. 144 in total.

General procedure

Personnel involved in the study analysis will remain blinded until the database is locked and protocol deviations are identified. Statistical analyses will be carried out by the GHICL Clinical Research and Innovation Division Biostatistics Unit using R software.

The statistical analysis plan (SAP) will be approved before the study database is locked and the study data is unblinded. The SAP will provide a detailed description of the statistical methods and develop the protocol content. The SAP will be provided upon request.

Descriptive analysis of population characteristics

We will first carry out a descriptive analysis of the data: the means and standard deviations will be calculated for the quantitative data, the medians and interquartile ranges for the discrete data, and the numbers and frequencies for the qualitative variables.

All data will be presented for the general population and by treatment group.

Baseline characteristics are defined as the last non-missing measurement before or on the day of the first study treatment dose.

Populations analysed

The population included will be all patients who have given informed consent. This population will be used to describe study withdrawals.

The intention-to-treat (ITT) population will include all randomised patients.

The modified intention-to-treat (mITT) population will include all patients who receive at least one dose of treatment, and will be analysed based on the treatment assigned by randomisation. This population will be the primary set used to analyse the primary efficacy endpoint, and for all secondary efficacy endpoints.

The safety population will include all patients who receive at least one dose of treatment, and will be analysed based on the treatment actually taken. This population will be used to analyse tolerance and safety data.

The per protocol population will include all mITT patients who have taken at least four of the six treatment doses foreseen, and who do not have any significant protocol deviation that could affect the primary endpoint. Major protocol deviations that could affect the primary endpoint will be defined and agreed prior to unblinding. This population will be used for sensitivity analyses.

Checking group comparability

The comparability of the two groups will be checked using Student’s t-test for the quantitative data in case of normality; otherwise, the Mann-Whitney U test; for discrete data the Mann-Whitney U test will be used; for qualitative data, the chi-square test will be used or Fisher’s exact test in case of insufficient numbers.

If a significant difference is found for one of the tested variables, the bivariate analyses below will be replaced by multivariate analyses (regression models) with an adjustment for the non-comparable data.

Comparability will be checked for the ITT, mITT and per protocol populations in case of major deviations.

We will consider that the significance level is 5%.

Analysis of the primary endpoint

To meet the main objective, we will calculate the changes in the radicular pain VAS between D1 and D4 for each group. These changes will be compared using Student’s t-test if the data are normal, otherwise the Mann-Whitney U test.

In addition, the responder rate on D4 (= radicular pain VAS < 40/100) in the groups will be compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. However, only changes in the radicular pain VAS will be used to conclude on the efficacy of the two treatments (gabapentin or placebo).

These analyses will be carried out for the ITT, mITT and per protocol populations in case of major deviations.

We will consider that the significance level is 5%.

Analysis of secondary endpoints

The number of patients with at least one adverse event (all types and grades according to the NCI-CTCAE scale) in the groups will be compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test.

This analysis will be carried out for the safety population. We will consider that the significance level is 5%.

The following analyses will be carried out for the ITT, mITT and per protocol populations in case of major deviations. We will consider that the significance level is 5%.

Changes in the low back pain VAS between D1 and D4 in the groups will be compared using the same analysis as the primary endpoint. The responder rate on D4 (= low back pain VAS < 40/100) will be analysed in the same way as the responder rate for the radicular pain VAS.

The changes in the radicular pain and low back pain VAS between D1 and D7 in the groups will be compared using the same tests as for the changes between D1 and D4.

A more overall analysis incorporating all the measurements (D1, D2, D3, D4 and D7) will be implemented by establishing a repeated measures ANOVA model for each VAS. We will plot the mean VAS curves for each group. Depending on their appearance, the appropriate model will be chosen (mixed model with random effects, splines, etc.) and the application conditions verified. Establishing this type of model will allow us to analyse the group × time interaction.

The changes in the NPSI in each group will be compared using the same analysis as for the VAS.

The impact of the initial positivity of the DN4 will be analysed using a multiple linear regression model explaining changes in the radicular pain VAS between D1 and D4, then between D1 and D7, and incorporating the group and the positivity of the DN4 as explanatory variables. The conditions for applying the regression will be verified graphically on the residuals (normality and homoscedasticity).

A transformation may be applied if these conditions are not met. Also, the positivity of the DN4 can be incorporated into the repeated measures ANOVA model analysing the overall changes.

The numbers of intermediate doses of antinociceptive analgesics in the two groups will be compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. The number of patients requiring at least one intermediate dose will be compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test.

The same tests will be used to compare the number of patients reducing the concomitant antinociceptive analgesic treatment between D4 and D7.

Within the gabapentin group, if the distribution of antidepressant and antiepileptic drug use allows, a Student’s t-test will be used in case of normality, otherwise a Mann-Whitney U test, to compare the mean or median radicular pain VAS on D4 between the two use groups (yes/no).

Analysis of safety data

The safety population will be used to analyse the safety data.

The AEs will be coded with the Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities using the NCI scale. Treatment-emergent adverse events (TEAE) are defined as AEs occurring on or up to seven days after the date of the first treatment dose. They will be reported to an independent safety data monitoring committee (“pharmacovigilance”). They will be presented by organ class and characteristics in the frequency tables. Patients with multiple AEs will only be counted once in each category. Key information on patients who have an AE leading to death, patients experiencing an SAE, and patients with an AE leading to treatment discontinuation will be listed. The characteristics, management, and clinical course of AEs will be compared between groups using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. AE duration will be compared using the Mann-Whitney-Wilcoxon test. The statistical unit will be the AE, with patients experiencing multiple AEs counted only once in the comparison of proportions, with an overall rate of patients experiencing at least one AE compared using the chi-square test.

No specific provisions are made for post-trial care, as all participants are required to be affiliated to the French National Health Agency, which provides free healthcare for all.

Handling of missing values

Every effort should be made by the investigator to provide comprehensive data.

This will enable the criteria to be analysed for the data without imputation.

Otherwise, exploratory analyses incorporating the multiple imputation method or replacing with the last available value may be used. The robustness of the analysis findings will be explored by means of sensitivity analyses.

Missing safety data will not be imputed.

Interim analysis

No interim analysis is foreseen in this study. Study interruption will be guided by safety considerations if they occur. No auditing is planned for the trial.
