Goal setting as a facilitator of motor learning in postural control task
Authors: Kazunori Akizuki, Kosuke Takeuchi, Ryohei Yamamoto, Kazuto Yamaguchi, Jun Yabuki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study explored the combined effects of specific goal setting and augmented feedback on motor learning and intrinsic motivation in a postural control task. A sex-stratified, randomized, and non-blinded study was conducted among 34 healthy young adults assigned to a goal-setting or a control group. Participants performed a postural control task using an unstable board. The goal-setting group received performance goal values prior to each trial and feedback after each trial, whereas the control group received feedback only. Balance performance was measured using an overall stability index, and intrinsic motivation was assessed using the Intrinsic Motivation Inventory. The experiment included a pretest, practice session, and retention test conducted 24 h later. The goal-setting group outperformed the control group in the practice session and retention test. Significant improvements were observed in interest/enjoyment and perceived competence but not in effort/importance. The results suggest that combining specific, challenging, yet attainable goals with performance feedback enhances motor performance and facilitates motor learning and intrinsic motivation. The results provide practical and clinical implications for designing practice protocols aimed at motor-skill acquisition in sports and rehabilitation contexts.

Introduction

A goal is the object or aim of an action an individual is trying to accomplish1. A goal represents a desired outcome or state and serves as a cognitive focal point that directs human behavior. Accordingly, goals are considered crucial determinants of the direction and intensity of one’s actions. Goal setting is a widely used strategy for enhancing motivation and regulating behavior. It has demonstrated effectiveness across various domains, including industry2,3, education4,5, sports6,7, and rehabilitation8,9. In particular, specific, challenging, yet attainable goals have been shown to enhance learners’ self-efficacy and contribute to improvements in skill acquisition and task performance in sports and rehabilitation contexts10–13.

Goal setting theory (GST), proposed by Locke and Latham14, provides a systematic framework for explaining how goal setting influences behavioral change. According to GST, goals influence task-related performance through four primary mechanisms. First, the directive function channels attention and effort toward goal-relevant activities and away from goal-irrelevant ones. Second, the energizing function increases the intensity of effort exerted. Third, the persistence function enables sustained effort over time, led by more challenging goals. Finally, the discovery and/or utilization of task-relevant knowledge and strategies facilitate effective performance. Moreover, four moderators of the relationship between goals and performance have been identified: performance feedback, goal commitment, ability, and situational factors3,14–16.

Empirical evidence supports this theory in the context of motor-skill acquisition. Gauggel and Fischer17 showed that individuals with brain damage who received specific and challenging goals outperformed those given general “do your best” instructions in a manual dexterity task, with enhanced retention performance. In the domain of sports performance, Kyllo and Landers12 conducted a meta-analysis revealing that goal specificity and goal difficulty positively influenced motor-performance outcomes. Additionally, Costa et al.18 investigated the effects of the goal difficulty level on motor learning and whether perceived competence played a role in this process. They found that goals of higher difficulty enhanced skill acquisition more effectively than those of lower difficulty, and that perceived competence moderated the learning process. Furthermore, Tzetzis et al.19 examined the effects of combining goal setting with augmented feedback on the learning of basketball skills of varying complexity. Their findings indicated that the combination of goal setting and augmented feedback facilitated skill acquisition more effectively than feedback alone.

However, some studies have reported findings that diverge from the predictions of GST. Corrêa and de Souza20 examined the effects of five goal-setting conditions on the learning of the Bachman ladder task: (1) generic (“do your best”); (2) long-term, difficult, and specific; (3) long-term, easy, and specific; (4) short- and long-term, difficult, and specific; and (5) short- and long-term, easy, and specific goals. Their findings indicated that the specificity, difficulty level, and temporality of goal setting did not affect performance during practice or motor learning. Iwatsuki and Regis21 examined the effects of success criteria (i.e., goal difficulty) on motor learning. In their experiment, two levels of success criteria were established for the same task: a relatively easy success (RES) group and a difficult success (DS) group. The results showed that participants in the RES group demonstrated greater motor learning and higher perceived competence compared to those in the DS group. These findings suggest that setting easier goals may facilitate motor learning and motivation. Similarly, Mohammadi et al.22 reported that easy goal setting was more beneficial for the acquisition and retention of basketball free throws in children with intellectual disability compared to difficult goal setting. Thus, the effects of goal setting on motor learning remain inconclusive. Furthermore, while augmented feedback is critical in motor learning23,24, the combined effects of goal setting and augmented feedback, as well as the underlying mechanisms of their interaction, have yet to be fully clarified.

Therefore, this study aimed to clarify the effects of combining specific goals with augmented feedback on motor learning using a postural control task25,26. Postural control and balance skills are essential for performing daily activities and preventing falls27 and serve as fundamental motor skills that underpin athletic performance in sports settings28. Moreover, based on previous research findings18,21, this study examines the mechanism underlying the combined effects of goal setting and augmented feedback from the perspective of motivation29–31. We hypothesized that goal setting combined with augmented feedback would result in higher motivation and performance during practice and greater motor learning, compared to the condition in which augmented feedback was provided alone.

Methods

Ethical considerations

The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Kobe International University (approval number: G2022-168). All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations and principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and its future amendments. Before conducting the study, we explained the experiment procedures to potential participants and obtained their written informed consent.

Participants

A power analysis was conducted using G*Power (Heinrich Heine University, Düsseldorf, Germany) to determine the required sample size. We conducted a mixed-design repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) with a within-between interaction, setting α = 0.05 and power = 0.80. Since no prior studies provided an empirical effect-size estimate relevant to our design, we assumed a medium effect size (f = 0.25)25,32. A total of 34 individuals completed the experiment (mean age = 21.0 years, SD = 0.17; 15 women and 19 men). Prior to enrollment, none reported neurological or vestibular disorders, or orthopedic conditions. All participants had no previous experience with the experimental task, and the specific purpose of the study was not explained.

Apparatus and task

The apparatus configuration and task instructions were based on previously published unstable-board procedures25,26. Postural instability was assessed using an unstable board (SAKAI Medical Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) instrumented with a compact three-axis accelerometer and three-axis gyroscope (multi-sensor θ; SAKAI Medical Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). The board (300 × 500 × 30 mm) included a semicircular boss attached to the underside (φ 160 × 60 mm). The device allowed multidirectional tilting, with maximum angles of 14° from horizontal in the medial–lateral direction and 25° in the anterior–posterior direction. Recorded signals were relayed to a personal computer via a dedicated data logger (Data Logger; SAKAI Medical Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). In this equation, x represents the tilt angle around the medial–lateral axis, y represents the tilt angle around the anterior–posterior axis, and n indicates the number of samples (100 Hz). OSI captures the time-dependent change in board inclination; thus, larger OSI values reflect greater postural instability and poorer performance. Participants stood on the board with feet shoulder-width apart and arms crossed over the chest, while fixating a point at eye level located 2 m ahead. Under these constraints, they were instructed to keep the board as level as possible throughout each trial.

Experimental design and procedures

The study design and procedures were adapted from our previous work25,26, with modifications as described below. A sex-stratified, randomized, and non-blinded study was conducted. First, we divided the participants into groups by sex to ensure a balance in the number of participants per group and the female-to-male ratio33. Next, we randomly assigned participants to either the goal-setting group (8 women and 9 men) or the control group (7 women and 10 men) (Fig. 1A).

The experiment started with the control group. Measurements for the goal-setting group began only after all data collection for the control group was completed. For participants assigned to the goal-setting group, the average performance of the control group in each corresponding trial was presented as the goal value prior to each trial. Additionally, after each trial, participants in the goal-setting group were provided with feedback on their performance (i.e., OSI). Conversely, participants in the control group were informed only of their performance after each trial (Fig. 1B).

The experiment comprised a pretest, practice session, and a retention test. During the pretest, participants completed three trials without any information regarding goal values or feedback. Immediately after completing the pretest, participants completed a psychological assessment using a nine-item questionnaire comprising the interest/enjoyment, perceived competence, and effort/importance sub-scales of the Intrinsic Motivation Inventory (IMI)26,34. This assessed participants’ subjective experiences in performing the experimental task. The items were scored on a seven-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (“strongly disagree”) to 7 (“strongly agree”).

After completing the psychological assessment, the practice session began. The practice session comprised four blocks of three trials each (twelve trials in total). The inter-trial interval was set to 30 s, and a 3-minute rest period was provided between blocks. After completing all practice trials, participants completed the IMI again. Twenty-four hours after the practice session, a retention test was conducted under the same conditions as the pretest.

Data analyses

To evaluate whether goal setting improved performance and intrinsic motivation relative to the control condition, we conducted mixed-design ANOVAs. For OSI during practice, group (goal-setting vs. control) was treated as a between-subject factor and block (four blocks of three trials) as a within-subject factor, yielding a 2 × 4 mixed ANOVA with repeated measures on block. For OSI in the test phase, we applied a 2 (group) × 2 (test: pretest vs. retention) mixed ANOVA with repeated measures on test. For the IMI, each subscale score and the total score (assessed before and after practice) were analyzed separately using a 2 (group) × 2 (timing: before vs. after practice) mixed ANOVA with repeated measures on timing. When sphericity was violated, Greenhouse–Geisser corrections were applied. Post-hoc comparisons with Bonferroni adjustment were performed if significant main effects or interactions were observed. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 29; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), with a two-sided significance level of p < .05.

Results

Stability index

Participants in the goal-setting group exceeded the goal value in an average of 82.4% (SD = 25.7%) of the trials. The analysis of the practice session revealed significant main effects of block (F[2.37, 75.78] = 5.07, p = .006, ηp2 = 0.137) and group (F[1, 32] = 6.46, p = .016, ηp2 = 0.168). However, the interaction effect of block and group (F[2.37, 75.78] = 2.12, p = .119, ηp2 = 0.062) was not significant. Post hoc analysis for the group factor indicated that the goal-setting group (M = 7.68, 95% confidence interval [CI] = [6.76, 8.59]) performed significantly better than the control group (M = 9.29, 95% CI = [8.37, 10.20]) across the practice session. Post hoc comparisons between blocks showed that the average OSI in Block 4 (M = 7.95, 95% CI = [7.27, 8.63]) was significantly lower than that in Block 1 (M = 8.82, 95% CI = [8.10, 9.53]) (p = .010).

In the test phase, the main effects of test (F[1, 32] = 23.88, p < .001, ηp2 = 0.427) and group (F[1, 32] = 9.65, p = .004, ηp2 = 0.232) were significant. The interaction effect of test and group (F[1, 32] = 4.75, p = .037, ηp2 = 0.129) was also significant. Post hoc analysis for the interaction of test and group indicated that the goal-setting group was significantly superior to the control group in the retention test (goal-setting group: M = 7.02, 95% CI= [6.09, 7.96], control group: M = 9.51, 95% CI = [8.58, 10.45], p < .001). Meanwhile, no significant difference was found between the groups in the pretest (goal-setting group: M = 9.27, 95% CI= [8.30, 10.23], control group: M = 10.37, 95% CI = [9.41, 11.34], p = .108). Moreover, a significant improvement in performance between the pretest and retention test was revealed in the goal-setting group (p < .001); no significant difference was observed in the performance of the control group (p = .065) (Fig. 2).

Intrinsic motivation

The analysis of the total IMI score revealed significant main effects of timing (F[1, 32] = 19.46, p < .001, ηp2 = 0.378) and group (F[1, 32] = 5.12, p = .031, ηp2 = 0.138). However, the interaction effect of timing and group was not significant (F[1, 32] = 2.10, p = .157, ηp2 = 0.062). Post hoc comparison for the group factor indicated that the goal-setting group reported significantly higher intrinsic motivation than the control group. Additionally, the total IMI score significantly increased from before to after the practice session (Table 1).

Regarding the interest/enjoyment subscale, significant main effects of timing (F[1, 32] = 13.26, p < .001, ηp2 = 0.293) and group (F[1, 32] = 5.93, p = .021, ηp2 = 0.138) were observed. However, the interaction effect of timing and group was not significant (F[1, 32] = 0.10, p = .752, ηp2 = 0.003). Post hoc comparison for the group factor indicated that the goal-setting group reported a significantly higher interest/enjoyment score than the control group. Additionally, the interest/enjoyment score significantly increased from before to after the practice session (p < .001).

For the perceived competence subscale, a significant main effect of timing was observed (F[1, 32] = 25.12, p < .001, ηp2 = 0.440), as well as a significant interaction of timing and group (F[1, 32] = 5.10, p = .031, ηp2 = 0.137). The main effect of the group was not significant (F[1, 32] = 2.99, p = .094, ηp2 = 0.085). Post hoc analysis for the interaction revealed that the goal-setting group had significantly greater perceived competence than the control group after the practice session (p = .011); meanwhile, no significant difference was found between the groups before practice (p = .698). A significant improvement in perceived competence from before to after the practice session was revealed in the goal-setting group (p < .001), whereas no significant change was found in the control group (p = .060).

Regarding the effort/importance subscale, neither the main effect of timing (F[1, 32] = 0.90, p = .351, ηp2 = 0.027) nor the main effect of group (F[1, 32] = 0.27, p = .870, ηp2 = 0.001) was significant. Additionally, the interaction effect of timing and group was not significant (F[1, 32] = 0.297, p = .590, ηp2 = 0.009).

Discussion

This study examined the combined effects of goal setting and augmented feedback on motor learning in a postural control task. The goal-setting group performed the task with a goal value provided for each trial and received feedback on their performance after each trial. Conversely, the control group received feedback only following task performance. Consistent with our hypothesis, participants in the goal-setting group demonstrated significantly superior motor performance during both the practice session and retention test compared to the control group. Furthermore, the goal-setting group reported higher levels of intrinsic motivation, particularly in the subdomains of interest/enjoyment and perceived competence. These findings provide novel evidence showing that goal setting, when combined with augmented feedback, facilitates motor learning and intrinsic motivation in the context of postural control tasks.

This study’s results are consistent with those of previous studies17,19 and provide further support for GST14. This theory posits that specific, challenging goals enhance performance by directing attention, increasing the intensity of effort, sustaining persistence, and encouraging the discovery and/or utilization of task-relevant strategies. The goal-setting group in this study was provided with specific performance goals based on reference data from the control group and trial-by-trial performance feedback. These conditions likely supported the directive and energizing functions of goal setting, enabling participants to monitor their performance better and adjust their strategies accordingly15,16. Consequently, the goal-setting group demonstrated superior motor performance compared to the control group during the practice session.

Furthermore, although no significant differences were observed between groups at the pretest, the goal-setting group exhibited significantly superior motor performance during the retention test. Moreover, only the goal-setting group showed a significant improvement from the pretest to the retention test. These results suggest that the observed benefits of goal setting were not limited to transient performance gains but extended to motor learning. This finding is consistent with that from previous studies showing that specific and difficult goals enhance immediate performance and long-term retention of motor skills17,18. Participants in the goal-setting group evaluated the quality of their performance by comparing the performance feedback received after each trial with the performance goal provided prior to task execution. This process likely afforded learners increased opportunities to reflect on their performance. When they recognized that they were not meeting the goal, they tended to adjust the level or direction of their effort or modify their performance strategies to align with the goal requirements15. Furthermore, when participants’ performance exceeded goal values, the resulting increase in perceived competence may have promoted intrinsic motivation, facilitating motor learning31. Previous studies using positive social-comparative feedback have shown that comparisons with others can increase perceived competence and, in turn, facilitate motor learning26,35. The performance goals provided to the goal-setting group in this study may have served as a form of social comparison, thereby enhancing learners’ perceived competence.

The motivational findings further support the utility of combining goal setting with feedback. The goal-setting group reported significantly higher scores on the IMI, especially in interest/enjoyment and perceived competence. According to self-determination theory29,30, intrinsic motivation is fostered when individuals experience satisfaction of basic psychological needs: autonomy, competence, and relatedness. In this study, the provision of a specific, challenging, yet achievable goal and experience of success may have enhanced participants’ perceived competence and promoted motor learning31. Notably, perceived competence significantly increased in the goal-setting group after practice, whereas no such change was found in the control group. Some studies have shown that high goal difficulty enhances performance18; however, others have found that easier goals may be more beneficial for promoting perceived competence and motor learning2,21. The performance goals in this study were based on the mean values from a normative sample (i.e., the control group), which may have provided an optimal level of challenge: neither too easy to cause boredom nor too difficult to induce discouragement. Additionally, effort/importance scores neither differed between groups nor changed over time. One possible interpretation is that both groups had high levels of engagement in the task, likely because all participants were unfamiliar with the postural control task using the unstable board and perceived it as novel and challenging. This lack of group difference may also be attributed to a ceiling effect induced by the use of the 7-point Likert scale. Future research should consider employing alternative indicators that may be more sensitive to detecting the effects of goal setting on participants’ exerted effort.

This study has several limitations. First, the sample comprised young adults without neurological or orthopedic impairments. Therefore, caution is warranted when generalizing the findings to other populations, such as individuals with stroke, Parkinson’s disease, or community-dwelling older adults. Second, as this study employed a postural control task involving an unstable board, the generalizability of the results to other types of motor skills (e.g., fine motor tasks or complex sport-specific skills) may be limited. Third, individual psychological characteristics were not assessed. Akizuki et al.26 reported that the effects of social-comparative feedback on motor learning could vary depending on an individual’s level of competitiveness. Accordingly, the effects of goal setting observed in this study may be influenced by individual psychological characteristics. Future research should explore the underlying mechanisms of how goal setting affects motor learning from multiple perspectives, including learners’ psychological characteristics. Finally, this study used the mean value of the control group as a benchmark rather than directly manipulating goal difficulty. Consequently, this study’s goal-achievement rate was relatively high. However, because we did not manipulate goal difficulty, we could not examine its effects, or those of achievement rate, on motor learning and motivation. Future research should investigate the optimal level of goal difficulty for enhancing motor learning. Additionally, effective goal-setting strategies from the perspective of motor learning must be explored.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated that combining specific goals with augmented feedback enhanced both motor learning and intrinsic motivation in a postural control task. These findings support GST and self-determination theory by indicating that such interventions are effective not only for improving task performance but also for fostering motor learning and motivation. The results provide practical and clinical implications for designing practice protocols aimed at motor-skill acquisition in sport and rehabilitation contexts.
