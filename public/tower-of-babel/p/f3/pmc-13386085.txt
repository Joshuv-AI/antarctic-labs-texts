When immigration policy meets the bedside: operationalizing care and compliance
Authors: David Zonies
Published: 2026

Hospitals are grounded in a fundamental social promise to our communities: that care will be available to anyone who needs it, without hesitation or fear. The increasing presence of immigration enforcement activity in clinical spaces challenges that premise and introduces operational complexity. For hospital administrators, the responsibility is clear: ensure strict adherence to the law while preserving an unwavering focus on patient care.

The framework outlined in Sakran’s article appropriately anchors this issue within established legal obligations.1 Patient protections under existing federal law governing patient information (Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA)), EMTALA’s (Emergency Medical Treatment and Active Labor Act) requirement to provide emergency care regardless of status, and the distinction between public and nonpublic clinical spaces are not discretionary.24 Rather, they are binding and guide our institutional responses. At the same time, compliance alone is insufficient. The presence of enforcement activity may undermine patient trust and deter individuals from seeking necessary care, directly affecting individual outcomes. This dual obligation—legal compliance and preservation of access—demands a prescriptive and system-level response.

Health systems can eliminate ambiguity at the point of care by establishing policies that govern interactions with law enforcement. They should be explicit, concise, and universally understood. Frontline clinicians should not be asked to interpret legal authority or engage directly with enforcement personnel.5 These expectations must be reinforced through training, scripts, and routine integrated workflows. Further, we must hold clinicians accountable to such policies regardless of personal conviction.

It is incumbent that hospitals assert operational control of the clinical environment, particularly in high-acuity settings such as emergency departments, trauma resuscitation areas, and operating rooms. Administrators should clearly define that clinical leaders will retain authority over patient care spaces, and law enforcement engagement will be managed exclusively through legal or administrative channels (eg, presentation of a judicial warrant to enter private areas). Because patient care must proceed uninterrupted regardless of external presence, hospital executives must assure clarity or risk fragmented decision-making that unnecessarily burdens frontline staff.

Institutions must embed confidentiality protections into system design. This includes minimizing unnecessary documentation of sensitive social or legal information, ensuring routine safeguards such as workstation auto-lock, and reducing visibility of protected health information in public areas. These are not immigration-specific precautions but fundamental practices that should be consistently enforced.

Administrators must take an active role in supporting both patients and staff during these encounters. Patients should receive clear, reassuring communication that their care will continue and that their health information remains protected. This can be reinforced using private care spaces, interpreters, and patient advocacy resources. Simultaneously, staff must be supported with clear expectations, visible leadership, and assurance that adherence to policy will be backed institutionally.
