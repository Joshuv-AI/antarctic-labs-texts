Unraveling the Coupling Mechanism of Viscous and Capillary Forces in Tight Sandstone: An Integrated Study Using Online NMR and Phase-Field Simulation
Authors: Zhe Zhang, Rongjun Zhang, Jian Sun, Xinyu Zhong, Le Qu, Xiaolei Zheng, Zhipeng Miao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
For tight sandstone reservoirs, dynamic imbibition is critical for enhancing oil recovery but involves complex multiscale capillary-viscous interactions. This study integrates real-time online nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) experiments with pore-scale phase field numerical simulations to elucidate these mechanisms in tight sandstone. The results indicate that critical nonmonotonic dependence of imbibition efficiency on displacement velocity, identifying an optimal regime showing positive correlation with permeability. It maximizes the synergy between capillary suction in microthroats and viscous displacement in macro-channels. Higher reservoir quality indices correspond to improved pore-throat connectivity, accelerated imbibition recovery equilibrium, and enhanced imbibition displacement efficiency. The progressive attenuation of matrix capillary forces combined with reservoir heterogeneity results in limited recovery enhancement during late-stage dynamic imbibition, whereas effective oil mobilization in poorly developed (micro and small) pores requires extended soaking time. Pore-scale analysis reveals distinct saturation anomalies driven by structural heterogeneity. In homogeneous matrices, a transient “reverse oil migration” into micropores occurs during early-stage displacement, which occurs when early-stage, high-velocity viscous forces create local hydraulic gradients in macro-pores that temporarily overpower the capillary entry pressure of adjacent micropores, forcing oil backward. Conversely, in fractured systems, while fractures enhance the fluid-matrix contact area and accelerate early recovery by 46.74%, they trigger a late-stage “water-locking” phenomenon. Microscopically, the high conductivity contrast promotes capillary snap-off in narrow throats, where deformed oil ganglia face severe resistance (the Jamin effect), leading to localized oil entrapment. The numerical models visually capture these counter-current flow topologies and interface evolutions, confirming the experimental saturation profiles. These findings provide a mechanistic understanding of dynamic imbibition, offering theoretical guidance for optimizing injection strategies in unconventional reservoirs.

Introduction

The efficient development of tight oil resources is pivotal for global energy security. Unlike conventional reservoirs, tight sandstone formations are characterized by ultralow porosity, extensive micronano pore systems, and complex fracture networks. Recent exploration efforts continental sedimentary basins in China, including the Ordos, Junggar, and Songliao basins, have revealed extensively developed tight oil and gas reservoirs, demonstrating substantial exploration potential. , While horizontal drilling and multistage hydraulic fracturing have enabled commercial extraction, the rapid production decline and low ultimate recovery factors (<30%) remain primary bottlenecks. − Consequently, the advancement of efficient extraction technologies for tight resources represents a strategic imperative to restructure China’s energy security landscape and reduce reliance on foreign energy imports.

Water flooding is a standard method to maintain reservoir pressure. However, in tight formations, the substantial conductivity contrast between hydraulic fractures and the matrix often leads to early water breakthrough and severe ″fingering″ effects, leaving a significant amount of oil trapped in the matrix. Since the early 1950s, Caudle, Aronofsky, and many other researchers have initiated systematic investigations into the role of imbibition in enhancing oil recovery from water-wet reservoirs. , Laboratory-scale imbibition-displacement simulations now guide field implementation strategies aimed at enhancing oil recovery in low-permeability tight reservoirs. In this context, dynamic imbibition- a process where wetting fluids spontaneously enter smaller pores under capillary action while displacing oil into larger channels or fractures during continuous flow - has emerged as a key mechanism for enhanced oil recovery (EOR). Unlike static spontaneous imbibition, dynamic imbibition involves a complex coupling of capillary forces and external viscous pressure gradients. −

Studies indicate that spontaneous imbibition contributes only approximately 7% to total oil recovery. Xu et al. conducted imbibition under forced pressure experiments on tight cores, revealing that the enhanced displacement efficiency compared to spontaneous imbibition arises from the synergistic effects of confining pressure-induced compaction and intensified capillary-driven fluid redistribution. Yang et al. studied large-scale physical models to investigate cocurrent and counter-current imbibition processes. Their results show that lower permeability reservoirs exhibit stronger imbibition effects but require extended equilibration periods, with recovery efficiency inversely correlated to permeability. Cao et al. revealed through high-temperature tight reservoir experiments that surfactants enhance imbibition recovery through interfacial tension reduction (from 30 mN/m to <5 mN/m) and wettability alteration, while minimizing adhesion work improves displacement efficiency. Dou et al. analyzed interfacial tension effects on dynamic imbibition under different scale of pores through low-field NMR displacement experiments. By comparing the oil-displacement characteristics of three types of flow regimes, he put forward a method to combine the two imbibition modes to optimize the oil recovery. Furthermore, systematic investigations have quantified the impacts of reservoir factors (fluid viscosity, fracture density, wettability) and operational parameters (injection volume, soaking time) on imbibition performance. − The advancement of pore-scale computational and numerical simulation techniques has opened new avenues for accurately capturing two-phase flow dynamics in complex porous media. , This progress has fostered the adoption of approaches such as the Lattice Boltzmann Method (LBM), , the Phase Field (PF) method and the Level Set (LS) method , to investigate microscopic flow mechanisms. The PF method, by coupling the Cahn–Hilliard and Navier–Stokes equations, provides a robust framework for accurately describing fluid flow in porous media while ensuring mass conservation. This approach is particularly effective for complex media characterized by heterogeneous grain distributions or fractal features, as it adeptly resolves the dynamics of complex moving interfaces within intricate pore structures. Nevertheless, consensus remains elusive regarding the dominance of specific mechanisms in dynamic imbibition processes.

Despite extensive research on spontaneous imbibition, the pore-scale dynamics governing the synergy between displacement (viscous-dominated) and imbibition (capillary-dominated) are not fully understood. While NMR experiments provide macroscopic, volumetric quantification of fluid redistribution across different pore sizes, the phase-field numerical simulations offer the microscopic, spatiotemporal visual evidence (such as interface deformation and snap-off) that physically explains the macroscopic anomalies observed in the NMR spectra. Furthermore, the specific mechanisms of “reverse migration” or oil retrapping during dynamic processes lack direct visual and quantitative evidence. In response to these issues, this study employs online Nuclear Magnetic Resonance (NMR) coupled with pore-scale phase field simulations to investigate dynamic imbibition in tight sandstone cores from the Ordos Basin. We aim to (1) precisely characterize fluid saturation distributions across hierarchical pore systems (large: > 10 μm, small: 0.6–10 μm, micro: < 0.6 μm); (2) quantify the sensitivity of dynamic imbibition efficiency to injection parameters; (3) elucidate the distinct pore-scale fluid behaviors in fractured versus unfractured matrices; and (4) reveal the physical mechanisms behind saturation anomalies, such as capillary snap-off.

Dynamic imbibition displacement experiments were conducted using a custom-built system consisting of a high-precision syringe pump (ISCO-500D, Kexing Technology Co., China), high-pressure piston accumulator, core holder, confining-pressure tracking pump, NMR spectrometer, and automated temperature–pressure acquisition module (Figure ). Coupled with temperature–pressure sensing modules, the NMR system enabled accurate, real-time monitoring of pore-scale fluid behavior.

Experimental Procedures

The preliminary preparations related to the experiments included placing the core samples in an oil-washing device, followed by deep cleaning for 10 days using a solution prepared by mixing n-hexane and ethanol at a 1:2 ratio. The cores were then dried in an oven at 85 °C for 24 h, labeled, and weighed. Formation water was simulated by preparing a mixed solution of CaCl2, KCl, and NaCl, with a salinity of 22000 mg/L. In addition, a MnCl2 solution with a mass fraction of 40% was prepared to suppress the hydrogen signal in the fluid, thereby eliminating interference during NMR signal testing. The viscosity of the simulated crude oil was measured to be 5.17 mPa·s at the actual reservoir temperature of 47 °C. Prior to the experiments, all the above fluids were subjected to NMR calibration. The detailed experimental procedure is described below:

The permeability of each core was first measured using the steady-state method. The cores were then saturated with simulated formation water under vacuum pressurization. After 24 h, the cores were weighed, porosity was calculated from the mass difference, and the NMR T 2 spectrum was measured. Next, the cores were placed in the core holder, and the confining pressure was kept 2 MPa higher than the injection pressure. A shielding solution containing Mn2+ was injected at 0.10 mL·min–1 until about five times the pore volumes (PV) had been produced, and the injection was stopped.

The temperature was maintained at 47 °C. Simulated crude oil was injected at 0.10 mL·min–1 until only oil was observed at the outlet. Both valves were then closed, and the cores were aged for 48 h to allow the oil–water distribution to stabilize. NMR T 2 scanning was carried out afterward. Water flooding was then performed at 2 MPa and 0.05 mL·min–1 until no oil appeared at the outlet. The recovered oil was collected and recovery efficiency was calculated. After flow stabilization, the outlet valve was closed to simulate a 24 h shut-in, followed by stepwise pressure depletion to atmospheric pressure. Oil production was recorded and the NMR T 2 spectrum was measured again.

New cores were used after washing and drying for 24 h. The same procedure was repeated under different injection rates, followed by NMR T 2 measurements. Finally, the recovery performance at different injection rates was compared to determine the optimum condition, and additional dynamic imbibition experiments were conducted under varied reservoir quality and fracture conditions.

Fluid Mobility Evaluation Based on NMR Detection

Principles of NMR Detection

With rapid advancements in nuclear spin magnetic resonance imaging, NMR technology became widely adopted in the petroleum industry during the late 20th century, particularly for studying fluid mobility in unconventional reservoirs. When reservoir fluids (crude oil or formation water) are subjected to a static magnetic field, Zeeman splitting occurs. Hydrogen nuclei’s magnetic moments align coherently, generating a macroscopic magnetization vector whose intensity correlates linearly with hydrogen content. The degree of nuclear magnetic signal decay is generally characterized by measuring the faster transverse relaxation time (T 2 ), consisting of surface relaxation of rock particles, bulk fluid relaxation and diffusive relaxation due to internal magnetic field gradients. 8 1T2=1(T2)S+1(T2)B+1(T2)D where T 2 , (T 2 ) S , (T 2 ) B , and (T 2 ) D represent the transverse relaxation time, surface relaxation time, bulk relaxation time, and diffusion relaxation time, respectively. Under homogeneous external magnetic fields, the influence of diffusion relaxation on T 2 can be minimized by optimizing (T 2 ) D parameters. In tight reservoir porous media, strong fluid–solid interactions induce prolonged bulk relaxation (T 2 ) B , rendering fluid-property-related relaxation signals negligible in laboratory NMR measurements. Consequently, eq can be simplified to 9 1T2=NsT2sHSV=ρsSV where N s , T 2s , and H are fluid-dependent parameters, while ρ s denotes the surface relaxivity coefficient governing pore-fluid interactions.

Equation establishes the correlation between T 2 relaxation time and the pore surface-to-volume ratio. This relationship implies that longer T 2 values correspond to larger pore sizes within the rock matrix. The signal amplitude (vertical axis) in T 2 spectra directly reflects hydrogen proton density in fluids. However, as reservoirs contain both oil and water phases, hydrogen signal suppression from formation water is essential for accurate oil-phase characterization.

Quantitative Evaluation Methodology for Dynamic Imbibition-Displacement

In tight oil and gas reservoirs, the development of micro- to nanoscale pores results in highly complex pore structures. Oil stored in these tiny pores cannot be displaced by conventional water flooding and can only be produced under the combined effects of capillary force, gravity, and pressure gradients. To address this, NMR imaging is often used to obtain T 2 spectra at key stages. These spectra not only provide a direct view of the dynamic changes during imbibition-driven recovery but also enable the development of a quantitative evaluation method through dynamic displacement-imbibition experiments. Previous studies have confirmed the correlation between T 2 relaxation time and pore-throat size, with longer relaxation times indicating larger pore volumes. By combining statistical data from mercury injection capillary pressure (MICP), NMR, and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) with practical engineering needs, appropriate T 2 cutoff values were selected to achieve a refined classification of micro to nanoscale pores. Analysis of changes in T 2 spectra then allows a quantitative evaluation of the contribution from different pore-size ranges to oil recovery during dynamic imbibition and displacement. Pore throats play a dominant role in fluid mobilization within reservoirs. High-pressure mercury injection experiments directly describe pore-throat size by reflecting the relationship between pressure and pore diameter. , Although this reflects a different physical phenomenon compared with NMR, both are fundamentally governed by the geometry of the pore structure in reservoir rocks. Results and Discussion

Analysis of Influencing Factors in Displacement-Dynamic Imbibition

Imbibition has long been regarded as an important approach to enhance oil recovery in low-permeability, ultralow-permeability, and tight reservoirs. The unique spontaneous imbibition mechanism is mainly driven by capillary pressure and gravity, which enable the injected fluid to overcome viscous resistance in the matrix-fracture system, penetrate into finer pores, displace the trapped oil, and eventually move it into fractures for production. This process is jointly controlled by multiple factors, including fluid properties, displacement media, and matrix-fracture characteristics. , To assess the influence of different factors on dynamic imbibition recovery in tight reservoirs, we compared displacement velocity, RQI, fracture properties, and operational parameters. Effect of Displacement Velocity

Displacement velocity is a key factor in dynamic imbibition-displacement recovery. Figure illustrates the oil recovery efficiencies of water flooding and imbibition under different velocities. For samples with different permeabilities, the recovery of water flooding first increases with injection rate and then fluctuates within a narrow range, suggesting that beyond a certain threshold, higher rates do not lead to further improvement. In contrast, imbibition recovery first rises and then declines as the rate increases. The optimal displacement rates for samples S1, S2, and S3 were determined as 1.25, 1.85, and 3.05 mL·min–1, respectively. Notably, this trend correlates positively with permeability (0.072 × 10–3 μm2, 0.209 × 10–3 μm2, and 0.251 × 10–3 μm2), indicating that each permeability level has an optimal displacement rate that maximizes recovery, and that this rate increases with permeability.

Relationship between displacement velocity and recovery in displacement-dynamic imbibition processes.

The optimal displacement velocities identified for samples S1, S2, and S3 must be contextualized using dimensionless fluid mechanics scaling to ensure scientific generalizability. The fundamental competition between external viscous driving forces and internal capillary resistance is rigorously quantified by the macroscopic Capillary Number (Ca). The dynamic balance is given by 14 Ca=μvγ⁡cos⁡θ where the symbol μ is the displacement phase viscosity, mPa·s; v is imbibition front velocity, m/s; γ is oil–water interfacial tension, N/m; θ is the contact angle, °.

At suboptimal injection rates (Ca ≪ 10–6), the viscous pressure gradient is mathematically insufficient to overcome the threshold capillary entry pressure of the surrounding micropore network. Under these low-velocity conditions, the injected aqueous phase preferentially channels through the paths of least resistance (macro-pores and microfractures), leaving the matrix oil largely undisturbed and yielding poor overall sweep efficiency. As the velocity increases and the system approaches the critical Capillary Number transition zone (Ca ≈ 10–4 to 10–3), a stable hybrid displacement regime emerges. In this optimal regime, viscous forces provide sufficient supplementary energy to assist capillary suction in mobilizing oil from medium-to-small pores without triggering severe viscous fingering. Consequently, residual oil previously trapped in submicron pores is steadily displaced into larger channels and rapidly produced, confirming the synergy between viscous displacement and capillary imbibition. By combining the Laplace equation with Darcy’s law, the displacement velocity v is derived as 15 v=kμ(2γ⁡cos⁡θrL+∇P) where v represents the actual fluid migration velocity. When the system approaches equilibrium ∇P≈2γ⁡cos⁡θrL , a stable linear displacement regime emerges. This hybrid displacement regime-neither purely capillary-driven spontaneous imbibition nor viscosity-dominated high-speed flooding-features synergistic force interactions. Residual oil in micropores is gradually displaced into large pores and fractures via imbibition, then rapidly produced under external viscous-driven pressure. As shown in Figure , concurrent attenuation of long-T 2 (large pores) and short-T 2 (micro- pores) NMR signals confirms effective multiscale pore utilization, resulting in maximized oil recovery efficiency.

NMR T 2 spectra of sample S1 under 1.25 mL·min–1.

On the other hand, as the displacement velocity increases, the pressure also increases gradually, which means that there generate a stronger viscous gradient. This gradient becomes the main force driving the fluid to overcome capillary resistance and displace oil. In practice, higher injection velocities can stabilize the displacement front, but excessive velocities may cause the fluid to channel through low-resistance paths, leading to fingering or high-permeability flow. This results in uneven advance of the injected fluid, rapid breakthrough at the production well, and residual oil accumulation in some regions, thereby lowering overall recovery. Thus, higher rates do not always improve recovery efficiency and may even be detrimental, as shown by the bar data in the lower part of Figure .

Effect of Reservoir Quality Index

Due to strong heterogeneity, low-permeability and tight oil reservoirs usually exhibit low permeability (<0.1 × 10–3 μm2) and complex pore-throat structures, which strongly affect oil recovery through imbibition. To evaluate this effect, we introduced the reservoir quality index (RQI), which integrates porosity and permeability, to characterize the influence of reservoir properties on dynamic imbibition recovery. Figure shows the variation of recovery efficiency for samples with different RQI values, while the final recovery factors are summarized in Table . Compared with the high-quality sample S3 (final oil recovery of 35.30%), the recovery factors of S4 and S6 were reduced by 12.01% and 14.96%, respectively. This indicates that recovery efficiency increases with RQI.

Relationship between recovery and IRR for samples with varying RQI.

For all samples, recovery rose rapidly in the early imbibition stage and slowed down later, suggesting that oil displacement efficiency decreases over time. This observation divides the process into two stages: rapid imbibition-driven recovery at the beginning and slower recovery at later times. To better capture these changes, we introduced the concept of imbibition recovery rate (IRR), defined as the change in recovery per unit time under the same experimental conditions, given by 16 IRR=ΔηimbΔt where Δηimb is the incremental imbibition recovery, %, and Δt is the time interval, h.

Intuitively, all samples exhibited low IRRs (<0.5%/h) when the imbibition time reaches 24 h. Notably, the highest-RQI sample (S3) showed a drastic IRR decline from 2.96%/h (1 h) to 0.048%/h (24 h), indicating significantly diminished late-stage imbibition efficiency. Figure reveals that higher-RQI samples (S3, S4) plateaued beyond 24 h, whereas lower-RQI sample S6 demonstrated a relative IRR increase of 50.92% (rising from an absolute rate of 0.026%/h at 60 h to 0.040%/h at 120 h). Although it may not be possible to visualize a significant change numerically, the IRR of S6 increased by 50.92% compared to that at 60 h. This implies that capillary-driven imbibition requires extended durations in low-RQI reservoirs with underdeveloped pore-throat systems. , Field evidence confirms capillary pressure as the dominant driver in water-wet reservoirs. The Bond Number (B o ) quantifies the gravitational-to-capillary force ratio in porous media, and based on this concept expressions closely related to specific physical property parameters (K and φ), as given by 17 BO=ΔρgK/φσL2 where Δρ is the density difference between wetting/nonwetting phases, g/cm3; g is the denotes gravity acceleration; K and φ represent permeability and porosity; L is the sample length, mm; σ is the oil–water interfacial tension, mN/m. Imbibition is considered capillary-dominated only when the Bond number (B O ) is much less than 1. However, as RQI increases, bond number also rises, indicating that gravity and external viscous forces begin to overshadow capillary dominance. The flow resistance in high-RQI reservoirs decreases, allowing viscous forces to preferentially connect larger pores and form fast-flow channels, which limits the growth of imbibition recovery efficiency at later stages.

Effect of Fracture

For unconventional reservoirs, hydraulic fracturing is commonly applied to create a stimulated volume. The purpose is 2-fold: (1) creating high-conductivity fracture networks to accelerate fluid displacement, although the resulting complex fracture networks often trigger fluid channeling, (2) enhancing imbibition efficiency by increasing matrix-fluid contact area and transmitting external pressure to inaccessible matrix pores.

Actually, field operations inherently involve coupled displacement and imbibition processes. To assess the role of fractures, we treated different samples with induced fractures and recorded their recovery during both processes Figure . The results show that fractured sample S2 achieved much higher displacement recovery than unfractured S7, with a difference of 46.74%, and the highest total recovery (85.06%). By contrast, the two samples showed similar recovery under imbibition­(minimal difference <6%). This divergence not only confirms that cracks play a role in expanding the contact area of spontaneous imbibition fluids with the substrate and enhancing the capillary effect, but underscores the dominance of capillary forces in fractured systems. In contrast, displacement depends on external pressure, which drives fluids preferentially through fractures (S2 conditions) while bypassing the matrix (S7 conditions), leading to lower overall recoverya phenomenon consistent with the “fingering effect” described by.

Pore-Scale Behavior of Dynamic Imbibition

Mobilization Characteristics across Pore Scales

Figure compares the incremental recovery factors (IRF) across pore scales under fractured and unfractured conditions, where 0 h represents the postwater flooding status. At this stage, oil recovery from large pores is substantial (42.44% and 58.83%), while recovery from micropores (<10 μm) shows a strong contrast: 23.59% in the unfractured matrix versus only 3.45% in the fractured matrix. This indicates that conventional water flooding mobilizes oil mainly from larger pores, leaving much of the micropores oil inaccessible. Contrary to the conventional expectation of monotonic saturation decline, a transient increase in oil saturation (manifesting as negative IRF) was observed in specific pore scales. In the unfractured core, this phenomenon occurred during the early stage (Figure a), whereas in the fractured core, it appeared during the late stage (Figure b). Rather than experimental anomalies, these saturation inversions reflect complex pore-scale fluid redistribution mechanisms governed by the shifting dominance between viscous and capillary forces. In the homogeneous unfractured matrix (Figure a), the early stage negative IRF in micropores is attributed to viscous-driven crossflow. During the initial high-velocity displacement, the injected water preferentially occupies the high-conductivity large pores, creating a transient hydraulic pressure differential between the macroscopic flow channels and the surrounding micropores system. Before spontaneous capillary suction can effectively mobilize fluids in the micropores, this local pressure gradient forcibly squeezes the oil from the large pores into the connected micropores. This phenomenon is herein formally defined as “forced backfilling”. Specifically, this transient pore-scale process occurs when early-stage viscous forces temporarily overcome local capillary entry pressures, physically driving fluids into micropores or dead-end geometries that would otherwise resist spontaneous uptake. This results in an anomalous, temporary increase in oil saturation within the micropore domain before capillary equilibrium is re-established. Surfactant effects can be ruled out, as the experimental brine composition matched that of the formation water. Conversely, for the fractured system (Figure b), the late-stage saturation increase in micro- pores is driven by capillary trapping and the snap-off mechanism. Forced Backfilling. Fractures act as high-speed conduits that rapidly surround the matrix blocks with water. As imbibition proceeds into the depth of the matrix, the high aspect ratio of the pore bodies to throats facilitates the snap-off of oil ganglia. The water phase creates hydraulic bridges in the narrow throats, inducing a water-locking effect (Jamin effect) that entraps the migrating oil. Consequently, the oil that was mobilized from larger pores becomes retrapped in the micropore throats, leading to the observed rise in residual oil saturation. − This illustrates the evolving fluid transport pathways during dynamic imbibition.

Contribution of Dynamic Imbibition to Oil Recovery

Figure quantifies and shows the pore-scale contributions of different pore types to total recovery during dynamic imbibition. large pores (>10 μm) dominate recovery, followed by small pores (0.6–10 μm), with micropores (<0.6 μm) contributing little. Comparison of residual oil changes before and after dynamic imbibition indicates that large pores achieved 85–92% displacement efficiency due to the combined effects of capillary and viscous forces. This appears inconsistent with earlier studies on spontaneous imbibition, ,, which suggested that nanometer-scale pores exhibit the strongest imbibition efficiency. In principle, those kind of research viewpoints are valid because spontaneous imbibition tests are designed without external pressure, where capillary forces dominate and become stronger as pore size decreases.

Residual oil saturation variations across pore-throat types before and after dynamic imbibition.

However, these findings do not preclude the efficacy of dynamic imbibition in small pores; rather, the observed behavior is governed by two critical mechanisms. First, T 2 spectra show that oil saturation is initially much higher in large pores, meaning the volume of oil available for displacement in micro- pores is inherently limited. Second, external pressure and flow rate play a critical role in dynamic imbibition, because this will significantly lead to the enhancement of viscosity. As viscous forces strengthen, the injected phase preferentially invades larger pores with lower flow resistance, while capillary-driven imbibition in micropores remains incomplete within the experimental time frame. Reservoir heterogeneity further amplifies this effect: large pores usually connect to high-permeability throats that facilitate continuous flow, whereas micropore throats (<0.6 μm) impose high viscous resistance that can exceed capillary suction. For instance, in sample S1, oil saturation in large pores dropped from 88.56% to 47.15%, while micro- pores decreased by only 0.33%, supporting this interpretation.

Notably, fractured samples (S2 and S4) exhibited improved oil utilization (0.14–16.99%) in small and micro-pores, confirming that dynamic imbibition can still enhance recovery from smaller pores when fracture-matrix connectivity increases the effective contact area.

Figure shows the changes in dynamic imbibition recovery over time. Samples S2, S3, and S4 all display a pattern of rapid oil recovery in the early stage, followed by a slower increase later. Among them, S2 had the fastest recovery rate and the highest final recovery (51.54%), followed by S3 and S4. In addition, sample S1 showed a marked 8.71% increase in recovery between 12 and 24 h, and its final recovery even exceeded that of S4. These results further indicate that in low-permeability matrices, where capillary forces dominate, imbibition requires a longer time to achieve significant recovery.

Pore-Scale Simulation of Countercurrent Imbibition Mechanisms

Based on the numerical model established in Section , the simulations compare oil–water interface within the fracture-matrix system across early, intermediate, and late stages, revealing phenomena distinct from spontaneous imbibition. It is important to note that the inherent time scale difference between the physical experiments (monitored in hours, capturing macroscopic equilibrium) and the phase-field simulations (measured in milliseconds, capturing rapid, transient pore-scale interface dynamics). By analyzing the velocity and pressure fields at 5 ms (Figure a,b), we find that sustained high-velocity and low-pressure characteristics emerge at pore-throat constrictions and along the displacement front. These conditions suggest that sustained imbibition drives fluid transport in these constricted areas. The migration direction of the expelled oil also indicates that counter-current imbibition dominates (Figure b).

Figure illustrates the temporal evolution of oil recovery during dynamic imbibition within a single-fracture model. The curve clearly identifies three characteristic stages: rapid growth, slowing down, and steady state. Based on our previous analysis, two primary mechanisms drive the initial surge in recovery. First, high capillary pressure differentials force the fracturing fluid into micropores, effectively displacing the oil. Second, the external pressure gradient overcomes viscous resistance, propelling the injected water into the pore network to displace oil. Due to their high permeability and low flow resistance, the larger pores on the left act as preferential flow paths for pressure transmission and are rapidly saturated. This synergy between capillary forces and pressure gradients accelerated recovery, which reached 17.40% within 28 ms. The middle stage is characterized by competition between capillary and viscous forces. A relatively well-defined main imbibition channel forms, and the displacement front advances into more structurally complex, smaller pores. However, pore throat tortuosity and viscous resistance increasingly trap larger oil droplets. Further oil mobilization requires overcoming interfacial tension and deformation energy, resulting in only a 3.82% increase in recovery and a slowing growth trend. In the late stage, external driving forces reach dynamic equilibrium with the viscous resistance of residual oil in isolated pores, causing imbibition to nearly stagnate. This indicates that under the current driving force, a large amount of oil has been extracted. The residual oil may be trapped in smaller pores, requiring higher pressure gradients to force water entry and sustain displacement. These simulation results agree well with the conclusions from our prior physical experiments.

Further Discussion

A comprehensive investigation of imbibition and its recovery mechanisms is crucial for the efficient development of unconventional reservoirs, though it also presents significant challenges. Since the discovery of imbibition, numerous studies have been devoted to unraveling its complexity. Unlike the spontaneous imbibition observed in conventional reservoirs, our waterflooding-imbibition experiments on tight cores revealed new phenomena. The interactions between displacement velocity, RQI, and fracture networks with pore-scale recovery mechanisms strongly control oil production during dynamic imbibition. Pores act as the microscopic carriers of this process: their structure, wettability evolution, and fluid distribution directly govern the efficiency of displacement-imbibition coupling. Compared with field-scale tests that typically last weeks or months, our short laboratory experiments raised a question of whether limited experimental duration restricts the observable response of smaller pores. Previous studies also suggest that complex fracture networks reduce the effective matrix-fracture contact area, thereby slowing imbibition in cases of linear flow geometry. Furthermore, while the 2D phase-field simulations effectively visualize fundamental interface dynamics (e.g., snap-off), they inherently lack the third spatial dimension. This absence of out-of-plane connectivity tends to exaggerate the magnitude of capillary trapping and the ’water-locking’ effect compared to true 3D porous media.

As highlighted above, dynamic imbibition differs fundamentally from spontaneous imbibition, functioning instead as a continuous “injection-displacement-production” cycle. This process is further shaped by the coupled effects of multiphase fluid behavior, pore–throat structure, heterogeneous wettability evolution, and dynamic boundary conditions. Based on these insights, future research could benefit from integrating molecular simulations to deepen understanding of heterogeneous pore-scale dynamics and to better resolve the governing mechanisms of dynamic imbibition.

Conclusions

In this study, the pore-scale mechanisms of dynamic imbibition in tight oil reservoirs are systematically investigated using online NMR experiments coupled with microscopic numerical simulations. The effects of displacement velocity, reservoir quality, and fracture-matrix interactions were quantitatively evaluated. The main conclusions are as follows:(1)Dynamic imbibition is distinct from spontaneous imbibition; it is a coupled process governed by the competition between capillary driving forces and viscous resistance. An optimal displacement velocity exists (1.25–3.05 mL·min–1 for the tested samples) where the synergy between capillary suction in microfractures and viscous displacement in the matrix is maximized. Deviating from this range leads to either insufficient pressure gradients to overcome capillary barriers or viscous instability fingering.(2)Higher RQI reservoirs can improve pore-throat connectivity, accelerate imbibition recovery equilibrium (initial IRR > 2%/h), and enhance imbibition displacement efficiency. However, the characteristics of capillary force decay and heterogeneity suppress late-stage performance (IRR < 0.05%/h). Low-RQI samples showed limited early recovery but exhibited a 50.92% increase in IRR after 120 h, highlighting the need for long-term capillary-driven imbibition in smaller pores.(3)Fractures enhance capillary effects by enlarging the contact area between the imbibition fluid and the matrix, while simultaneously providing high-speed channels that accelerate displacement. This dual effect improves matrix oil–water exchange efficiency by 46.74%. Imbibition dominates oil recovery in small and micro- pores, while fracture-driven displacement targets larger pores, and their synergy significantly boosts overall recovery.(4)For unfractured matrix cores, viscous-driven backflow during early imbibition caused reverse oil migration into micro-pores, producing complex pore-scale responses. In fractured samples, fractures enhanced the large pores mobilization during waterflooding and early imbibition, but in later stages, the snap-off effect led to residual oil accumulation in micro- pores.(5)Both experimental and numerical results confirm that dynamic imbibition evolves through three stages: rapid growth (dominated by pressure difference), slowing down (capillary-viscous competition), and steady state (equilibrium). Numerical simulations visually verified the counter-current imbibition mechanism and the phenomenon of residual oil trapping due to droplet snap-off in complex throat constrictions.
