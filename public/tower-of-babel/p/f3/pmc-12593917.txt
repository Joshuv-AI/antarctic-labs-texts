Costs of medical treatment of advanced heart failure: a scoping review
Authors: Livia A Goldraich, Daniela Meirelles do Nascimento, Lucas Porto Santos, Dayanna Machado Lemos, Miriam Allein Zago Marcolino, Nadine Clausell, Ana Paula Beck da Silva Etges
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAn estimated 10–15% of patients with heart failure (HF) progress to an advanced disease stage. The majority of patients with advanced HF, who are ineligible and/or have no access to expensive, specific therapies such as heart transplant and left ventricular assist devices, are medically managed. The cost of medical treatment for advanced HF is largely unknown. The objective of this scoping review is to summarize the evidence on costs of advanced HF patients who are not treated with advanced therapies, to understand the main settings of this health expenditure, and to map the main cost drivers in this disease stage.MethodsThe PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, Scopus, and BVS were used as data sources for literature searches evaluating direct costs of patients with advanced HF who were not treated with advanced therapies. Cost methodological categorization, such as micro or macrocosting, payment perspectives, and cost information reported, including the total cost per patient and the main cost drivers, were extracted. Cost methodology heterogeneity was also observed. Despite this, in most studies, cost information was derived from observational cohorts or clinical trials and used either macrocosting or microcosting methods from a public payer perspective.ResultsA total of 8157 records were identified, and 16 were included in the review. Criteria for advanced HF varied among studies. Hospitalizations emerged as the main cost driver in the majority of reports. The mean total annual cost per patient was 48,309 international dollars (SD 34,874). Background

Patients with heart failure (HF) may progress to advanced stages, representing an estimated 10–15% of those living with the condition [1]. In the course of the disease, this phase is characterized by repeated hospital admissions, use of multiple resources, and advanced HF therapies [1]. Given the variety of settings and complexity of HF presentation, obtaining accurate data on the financial burden of the advanced HF spectrum of care is challenging. While considering the overall HF patient population, it is estimated that US $39.2 billion and almost GBP 1 billion are spent every year in the United States (US) and the UK, respectively [2, 3].

Highly complex treatments, in particular heart transplant and left ventricular assist devices (LVAD), are important to selected patients with advanced HF, as they both may increase survival and quality of life [3, 4]. Heart transplant is limited by organ shortage, and, although the number of procedures has been rising globally, only a limited proportion of patients that may benefit from this therapy are transplanted [5]. Likewise, the implant of LVAD is still scarcely available from both public and private healthcare systems in most countries, mainly due to its elevated costs [6]. Thus, only a minority of patients with advanced HF receive either of these advanced therapies, whereas the majority of them rely solely on medically based treatment, for which costs are largely unknown. The number of patients with advanced HF who are treated conservatively is not modest; in turn, understanding the cost of their medical care may be relevant given its magnitude globally.

While financial information is scarce regarding medical treatment in the advanced HF scenario, accurate cost information may be valuable for the establishment of effective and sustainable health policies to improve the care for the condition, particularly in low- and middle-income economies. By using detailed cost data, the chances of introducing equitable models that allow increased access to advanced therapies as well as improved medical care are amplified, resulting in favorable implications for survival rates and quality of life for patients with HF.

The objective of this scoping review was to summarize the evidence on costs of medical management for patients with advanced HF, including both inhospital and outpatient settings, as well as long-term facilities and end-of-life stages. More specifically, we aimed at addressing the costs of patients who were not treated with advanced therapies, the type of settings where those costs were predominantly studied, and the main drivers of health costs involved in their care.

Methods

The present protocol was registered on the Open Science Framework using the identifier 10.17605/OSF.IO/54GQN. The scoping review was conducted in accordance with the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) methodological framework for scoping studies and reported in accordance with guidance provided in the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) [7, 8].

Study eligibility criteria

This review included reports focused on patients with advanced HF not treated with advanced therapies—heart transplant or LVAD—due to their clinical profile, contraindications, or limited access to treatments. Patients with advanced HF managed at inpatient, outpatient, and long-term care facility levels were included, as well as those in end-of-life stages. Studies reporting solely on patients with specific etiologies or comorbidities (i.e., amyloidosis, muscular dystrophy, sleep apnea), patient subgroups in which overall grouping did not adequately represent the population with advanced HF, or patients with worsening HF were not included.

Data of interest included direct or indirect costs, collected from primary or secondary sources, prospective or retrospective, involving patients with advanced HF who were medically treated. Preprints, conference abstracts, and editorials or comments, as well as narrative, scoping, or systematic reviews, were excluded. Articles evaluating exclusively indirect costs focused on cost-effectiveness metrics, secondary cost sources (i.e., costs extracted from other studies), and cost projections (no actual measurement of resource metrics, such as days of hospitalization, tests, and treatments) were also excluded. No language restrictions were applied. For eligible studies published in languages other than Portuguese or English, translations were sought through professional services, institutional support, or consultation with fluent collaborators to ensure accurate data extraction.

Information sources

The literature search included original, peer-reviewed full manuscripts. To account for contemporary studies, a literature search was conducted until April 2024 across PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, Scopus, and BVS databases. If needed, authors were contacted to obtain full-text papers not available in usual databases.

Search strategy

The search string contained a combination of the following medical subject heading terms: “heart failure,” “transplant-ineligible,” and “costs” and “cost analysis.” All searches were simultaneously conducted, and the results were exported into Rayyan® software [9] on the same day to ensure consistency across databases. Duplicates were removed by an experienced researcher (M. M. Z.) and further refined through team discussion. The final search strategy for the databases can be found in Additional file 1.

Study selection

Two pairs of authors (D. M. N./D. L. and N. C./L. S.) independently screened the titles and abstracts of all studies identified in the search. Within each pair, reviewers conducted blinded assessments, meaning that each individual completed the screening without access to the other’s decisions, based on the review’s inclusion criteria. Abstracts that did not provide sufficient information about the inclusion and exclusion criteria were retrieved for full-text evaluation. Full-text articles retained from the first stage were reassessed independently by the same pairs of authors. Any disagreements within each pair were solved through discussion and/or with the involvement of a third reviewer (L. G.). Studies that were not original papers did not study advanced HF patients, did not measure direct costs, or reported only specific therapies other than medical management were excluded. Reasons for the exclusion of full texts were recorded and reported.

Data items

For each included study, the following variables were extracted: title, author names, year of publication, country, data source, study setting (outpatient, inpatient, both inpatient and outpatient, or long-term care facilities), advanced HF definition as stated by the authors, number of participants, age, sex, major comorbidities, cost methodology (microcosting, macrocosting, or ratio of costs), perspective (public, private, or mixed), cost data source (primary or secondary), costs by period analysis, period unit used for cost analysis, cost components [emergency visits, intensive care unit (ICU) hospitalizations, non-ICU hospitalizations, medications, personnel, and exams], main cost drivers, currency year, and mean total cost per patient.

Data extraction

Relevant data were extracted from all included studies by two pairs of independent reviewers using a structured data extraction in Microsoft Excel, with blinded assessments to ensure impartiality.

Any disagreements between the reviewers were fixed through discussion or with the opinion of an additional reviewer. When appropriate, authors were contacted once by the reviewer responsible for assessing the paper’s eligibility to request missing or additional data. If no response was received within 1 week, the study was excluded from the scoping review.

Data analysis and presentation

The extracted data was structured on descriptive Tables 1 and 2.. Cost analyses were performed to compile the cost information identified in each individual study into a mean total annual cost per patient. Also, variability in methods and financial information across the studies was reported. All the financial data is presented in international dollars (Int$) to account for purchasing power parity, according to the World Bank conversion factor from each country for 2023. For the conversion, firstly, the mean cost per patient reported in each study was updated considering the local inflation rates from the study year to 2023, and then the present value of the reference year was converted to Int$.

Results

A total of 8157 records were identified from the searched databases. After removing duplicates, the titles and abstracts of 4617 studies were reviewed, and ineligible studies were excluded. The 164 reports sought for retrieval were reassessed, and 16 full-text articles were included for review. Most of the studies excluded in the final stage did not meet the eligible criteria or did not report cost information. Figure 1 depicts the study selection flow diagram.

Characterization of the included studies

The selected reports [10–25] comprised the period between 1995 and 2024, but approximately half of them were published in the last 10 years. Most cost studies (50%) were developed in the USA using the perspective of the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS). There were also reports from Spain [18], Greece [19], Canada [21], China [22], Singapore [25], and Italy [24]; no studies from Latin America or Africa were identified. Regarding the patient population evaluated in the studies, different inclusion criteria for advanced HF were observed.

Patients included in the studies were usually severely symptomatic and either referred for assessment for advanced therapies or ineligible for heart transplant. Many studies (68%) evaluated patients while hospitalized, and some included subjects that were on intravenous inotropes. A few reports were focused on a retrospective review of patients who died of or with HF. Approximately, a third of the reports were conducted with real-world data, while the majority were linked to observational cohorts or clinical trials. The largest studies were from the USA and had CMS datasets as their information source. Table 1 describes geographic identification and clinical characteristics of the patient population that were evaluated in the included studies.

Costs information

There was heterogeneity of cost methodologies across studies; nine (56%) studies were macrocosting based, five (31%) were microcosting based, one (6%) utilized mixed-method approaches, and one (6%) used ratio of costs. Primary cost data sources were identified in most of the studies. The cost perspective was most commonly public, with a large volume of data from the USA, reflecting the financial context of that country. Only two studies were developed with a private cost perspective. Hospitalizations were often reported as the major cost driver, being cited by 10 articles as the most representative cost, encompassing both ICU and non-ICU hospitalizations. Secondary components of the total cost composition were scatteredly described and included personnel, exams, and nursing care, among others, with a great variability observed among studies. Table 2. contains the cost information extracted from individual studies.

While considering the 16 studies included, the calculated mean total annual cost per patient was Int$ 48,309 (SD 34,874). When computing only the studies which evaluated inpatient costs, it accounted for Int$ 57,056 (SD 35,209). One study evaluated exclusively out-of-pocket costs, accounting for an annual amount of Int$ 6730.

Variability across countries

When comparing studies across countries, those performed with data from the USA reported the highest costs for patients with advanced HF being medically managed; the mean total annual cost in the USA was Int$ 65,749 (SD 35,899), while in other countries it averaged Int$ 25,887 (SD 11,461). Discussion

In this scoping review, our aim was to identify cost values and to understand the nature of costs involved in the care of patients with advanced HF not treated with either heart transplant or LVAD. We included studies that reported costs across different settings, representing the spectrum of medical management for this disease stage. Also, we identified major cost drivers and payment sources in most studies reflecting different practices worldwide. As a major finding, we observed heterogeneity of cost methodologies across studies yet showing that expenditure values were not trivial, irrespective of patient setting or payer perspective.

Among the 16 studies included in this review, the definitions of advanced HF were quite heterogeneous. That finding, to some extent, may reflect the wide period of time covered by the selected studies (from 1995 to 2024), during which the terms and characterization of clinical stages of HF may have changed [26]. Although focused on advanced HF, some reports derived from administrative databases may have led to the inclusion of patients that did not fulfill contemporary clinical criteria of advanced HF [5, 13, 17, 20].

Despite the methodological heterogeneity among the studies, it is possible to state that patients with advanced HF accounted for an expressive resource consumption in all countries represented in this review, particularly in the USA. Irrespective of a public or a private payer perspective, considerable expenditure was recorded in all clinical settings, suggesting that advanced HF represents a burden in healthcare systems, which is yet more relevant given the increasing number of patients with HF worldwide [2]. Interestingly, we observed data indicating that the final months of life could be even more costly [2, 17]. Finally, even though cost components varied significantly among studies, hospitalizations were the main cost driver in most of them.

Consolidating the information retrieved makes this scoping review valuable to guide policymakers in defining strategies to sustain and reimburse providers who are delivering clinical care for patients with advanced HF who do not have access to highly expensive technologies. The present cost information can also support economic analysis to evaluate the cost-effectiveness of offering technologies such as LVADs in universally covered systems, as well as heart transplant, also a costly treatment. Processes for the incorporation of complex health technology, such as expensive cardiovascular devices, are emergent worldwide and require different assessment practices when compared to medications. Recently, the World Health Organization reported a public consultation about their guidelines for health technology assessment of medical devices [27]. Although their final document is not yet published, it clearly demonstrates that accurate regional data is required to subside sustainable decision-making processes, in particular in low- and middle-income countries. In this context, generating cost information associated with access to, or lack of access to, expensive devices is a field that deserves further research efforts.

Some limitations of our study should be acknowledged. Conference abstracts were excluded as these reports usually lack sufficient details that could add meaningful information to this review. Also, we did not perform a formal quality assessment of the included studies, which could contribute to a deeper understanding of the body of literature collected. We included studies from the late 1990 s, which could have posed variations in definitions and practices for advanced HF, both of which have evolved in the last decades. However, both the long period covered and the international perspective may provide a broader view on how expenditures on advanced HF care have been characterized globally over time.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this scoping review summarizes the available evidence of cost information of advanced HF and shows a vast heterogeneity of methodologies applied to measure costs in a field of high expenses. Given the wide variation in the way costs were measured in the literature, it remains challenging to draw a unified concept to provide insights into the building of robust healthcare policy to sustain reimbursement for the care of patients with advanced HF. Further contemporaneous studies, preferentially collaborative, with well-characterized advanced HF patient definition and comprehensive cost methodology are warranted to better guide investments in the management of complex chronic conditions, such as advanced HF.
