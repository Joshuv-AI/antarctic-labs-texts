High prevalence of unrecognized postoperative delirium in elderly patients: a prospective cohort study from a resource-limited country
Authors: Arinze Duke George Nwosu, Edmund Ndudi Ossai, Cyprian Chukwuebuka Nganwuchu, Francis Ndubuisi Ahaotu, Ndubuisi Ebere Duru
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPostoperative delirium is a frequent complication following surgery in elderly patients. Despite its strong association with poor health outcomes the literature in Nigeria has been limited to a few case-reports. A major barrier to improving delirium care in elderly individuals is its poor detection, particularly in surgical populations. We aimed to determine the incidence, detection rate and risk factors for postoperative delirium in our cohort of elderly surgical patients.MethodsWe conducted a prospective cohort study in a regional orthopedic and plastic surgical center in Enugu, Southeast Nigeria, between April 2020 and March 2024. The participants were patients aged 65 years and above, who underwent surgical procedures. Each patient was visited daily by a trained nonpsychiatrist physician during the first three days after surgery and was assessed for delirium during each visit. The “Confusion Assessment Method” algorithm was used for the diagnosis of delirium. The motor subtypes were classified on the basis of the predominant psychomotor disturbance (hyperactive, hypoactive, mixed, or none). Multivariate analysis via binary logistic regression was used to determine the predictors of delirium. The level of statistical significance was determined by a p value of < 0.05.ResultsData from 304 patients (mean age, 71.7 ± 6.4 years) were analyzed. The incidence of postoperative delirium was 24.0% (73/304). The delirium subtype manifestations were “hypoactive” 35.6%, “hyperactive” 35.6%, “mixed” 20.5%, and “none” 8.2%. Approximately 92% (67/73) of the delirious cases presented within 48 h following surgery. Among the delirious cases, 90.4% (66/73) were undetected by the managing surgical teams and nursing personnel. The predictors of delirium were perioperative anaemia (AOR = 4.6; 95% CI: 1.3–16.5), impaired preoperative cognitive status; (AOR = 4.5; 95% CI: 2.0-9.9), perioperative blood transfusion (AOR = 2.1; 95% CI: 1.1–4.2), and surgery lasting between 120 and 179 min (AOR = 0.3; 95% CI: 0.1–0.8).ConclusionPostoperative delirium was grossly underrecognized by the managing surgical teams and nursing personnel. Education of the healthcare providers, with implementation of routine monitoring for postoperative delirium using validated tools is recommended. Perioperative anaemia and blood transfusion were modifiable risk factors for postoperative delirium in these patients, and improvements in patient blood management offer great potential for safer care.Keywords: Anesthesia, Delirium, Elderly, Resource-limited country, Surgery

Introduction

Postoperative delirium is a common neuropsychiatric syndrome that is more likely to develop following surgery in older patients, than in younger patients [1]. It is associated with several adverse health outcomes, including the development of new dementia and the acceleration of existing dementia in older adults [2, 3]. Compared with that of non-delirious patients, the length of hospital stay is longer in those who develop delirium, with a much greater risk of institutionalization and death following discharge [4]. Its association with poor outcome is independent of confounders such as age, comorbid illness or illness severity and baseline dementia [5]. Poor detection and management of delirium in elderly patients is widespread in clinical settings, globally [6–8].

In Nigeria, postoperative delirium receives scanty attention, with the few documented studies being limited to case reports [9, 10]. Postoperative delirium is an acute brain dysfunction characterized by concurrent disturbances of consciousness and attention, perception, thinking, memory, psychomotor behavior and the sleep-wake cycle. During hospitalization several postoperative delirium-related adverse events may occur. Among these, whereas self-removal of the endotracheal tube, intravenous access, oxygen delivery devices, nasogastric tubes and indwelling catheters tend to occur more often with the ‘hyperactive’ and ‘mixed’ subtypes, pressure ulcers and falls are more common with the ‘hypoactive’ subtype [11]. These events in turn result in an increased risk of thromboembolism, prolonged hospital stay, delayed functional recovery, functional decline and increased mortality.

The pathophysiology of delirium is not clearly understood but diverse pathobiological mechanisms are thought to be involved, including; neurotransmitters imbalance, neuroinflammation, and impaired cerebral energy metabolism [12].

Primary prevention through targeted control of modifiable risk factors such as anaemia, dehydration, fever, intraoperative hypotension, hypothermia, and anticholinergic drugs, with adequate pain control remains the mainstay of delirium management. Earlier use of anesthesia-based intervention programs has proven very successful in significantly reducing the prevalence of postoperative delirium [13]. Treatment of diagnosed postoperative delirium is aimed at addressing the underlying causes and managing the symptoms. The use of antipsychotics for the prevention or treatment of postoperative delirium is of unproven benefit in improving outcomes [14]. However, they may be used for patient safety indications at low doses and for a very limited course. Thus, with a better understanding of the risk factors and the routine use of validated tools for the detection and diagnosis of postoperative delirium, appropriate management can be instituted.

The incidence of delirium in hospitalized patients varies widely depending on the sample population, the study design and the diagnostic tool used. Among elderly patients undergoing orthopedic procedures an incidence of 55% has been reported following hip fracture repair [15]. Instruments for detecting delirium are not in short supply with as many as 24 scales identified in a review by Adamis et al. [16], and many more exist. However, a small number of scales are considered robust and widely used. In general, the confusion assessment method (CAM) and CAM for the intensive care unit (CAM-ICU) are preferred for the diagnosis of delirium [16, 17]. The Neelon and Champagne (NEECHAM) Confusion Scale and Delirium Observation Screening Scale (DOSS) appear to be the most suitable screening instruments for patients with delirium [16, 17]. Despite performing well as delirium screening tools in adult ICU patients, a comparative study assessing their performance as screening tools for postoperative delirium in elderly patients revealed that both the CAM-ICU and the Nursing Delirium Screening Scale (NuDESC) had poor sensitivity in this setting [18].

With the increase in both life expectancy and access to surgery, the morbidity and mortality associated with postoperative delirium portend considerable public health concerns among elderly patients. Consequently, perioperative physicians should pay attention to the impact of postoperative delirium in this patient population to address this issue. Our study aimed to determine the incidence, detection rate and risk factors for postoperative delirium among elderly patients undergoing surgical procedures in our health facility.

Methods

Research ethics

The research protocol was approved by the institutional review board before the commencement of the study; NOHE: IRB/HEC No- S.313/iv/ (Protocol No. 207). Informed consent was obtained from all prospective participants before recruitment in the study.

Study type and setting

This single-center prospective cohort study was conducted in a regional trauma and burn center in Enugu, Southeast Nigeria, between April 2020 and March 2024. The facility offers a vast range of orthopedic and plastic surgical surgeries including limb arthroplasties, instrumented spine procedures, body contouring surgeries, repair of orofacial clefts, contracture release and amputation of gangrenous extremities.

Inclusion criteria

All patients aged 65 years and above, scheduled for elective or emergency surgical procedures.

Exclusion criteria

Patients with a history of psychiatric illnesses and those with altered consciousness were considered ineligible. Other patients whose cognitive status could not be evaluated with the SBT because of severe communication handicap were also deemed ineligible. Patients for day-case procedures and non-consenting eligible patients were excluded from the study.

Description of instruments

The confusion assessment method (CAM) [19]

This instrument was developed from a geriatric patient population. The CAM algorithm for the diagnosis of delirium requires the presence of both acute-onset mental change with fluctuating course and inattention, with either disorganized thinking or altered levels of consciousness. It was designed for use by nonpsychiatry-trained physicians and takes approximately 5 min to administer. In a systematic review exploring 21 delirium screening tools across a broad range of inpatient settings the CAM was the most widely used instrument to identify delirium [20]. In another review of 25 prospective studies by Wong et al., the CAM was rated as the best bedside delirium instrument considering its ease of use, test performance and time of administration [21]. The performance of CAM is dependent on the skill of the rater and for the best results rater training has been recommended by Ryan et al. [22].

The delirium motor subtype scale (DMSS) [23]

The DMSS is an 11-item scale used to classify delirium into several motoric subtypes. The ‘hyperactive subtype’ is assigned if the patient shows at least two of the following symptoms over the previous 24 h: (1) increased amount of motor activity, (2) loss of control of motor activity, (3) restlessness, and (4) wandering. The ‘hypoactive subtype’ is assigned if the patient shows at least two of the following symptoms over the previous 24 h: (1) decreased amount of activity, (2) decreased speed of action, (3) reduced awareness of surroundings, (4) decreased amount of speech, (5) decreased speed of speech, (6) listlessness, and (7) reduced alertness/withdrawal. At least two out of four DMSS hyperactive items, or at least two out of seven DMSS hypoactive items must be present for the hyperactive and hypoactive subtypes, respectively, to be diagnosed. The ‘mixed subtype’ is assigned if the patient shows both hyperactive and hypoactive symptoms. ‘None subtype’ is assigned if there is neither the hyperactive nor hypoactive symptomatology.

The studied outcomes were the presence of delirium and its motor subtype, as determined by the Confusion Assessment Method (CAM) and Delirium Motor Subtype Scale (DMSS), respectively. Two nonpsychiatrist physician research assistants were given training in the use of assessment tools for cognitive status (Short Blessed Test), delirium detection (CAM) and delirium subtyping (DMSS) prior to the commencement of the study. The training of the research assistants in the use of CAM was of special importance in enhancing the performance of the instrument, promote inter-rater reliability and reduce observer/detection bias [19, 22]. All patients aged 65 years, or above, who were scheduled for emergency or elective surgeries were deemed eligible for participation in the cohort study, except those otherwise excluded. Patients were recruited consecutively during the preoperative anesthetic assessment. For patients who had multiple procedures during hospitalization only the first procedure was considered, and the patient was enrolled only once. In each case the baseline cognitive status assessment was performed using the ‘Short Blessed Test’ (SBT) [24]. After surgery, a trained nonpsychiatrist physician who was trained in the use of the CAM algorithm visited and assessed each patient once daily for delirium evaluation, during the first three postoperative days. If delirium was present subtyping was performed according to the DMSS, and the postoperative day of the first episode of delirium was recorded. The following data were collected: age, gender, educational attainment, comorbidities, American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status grade, presence and motor subtype of postoperative delirium, indication for surgery, type of surgery, duration of surgery and technique of anesthesia. Others were; the presence of perioperative anaemia, perioperative blood transfusion, perioperative use of benzodiazepines or anticholinergic drugs, recognition of postoperative delirium by attending physicians or nursing staff, and treatment offered in cases of detected delirium.

Statistical analysis

Data entry and analysis were performed via the International Business Machine, Statistical Product and Service Solutions (IBM-SPSS) statistical software version 25.0. Categorical variables are presented using frequencies and proportions, whereas continuous variables are represented as means and standard deviations. The chi-square test of statistical significance was used to compare the differences in proportions between two categorical variables. Multivariate analysis via binary logistic regression was used to determine the predictors of the outcome measure of the study. The level of statistical significance was determined by a p value of < 0.05.

The primary outcome measure of the study was the presence of postoperative delirium. This was determined by clinical assessment using the Confusion Assessment Method (CAM). To examine the factors associated with postoperative delirium, the sociodemographic, clinical and perioperative characteristics of the respondents were cross-tabulated with the outcome measures. The variables that had a p value of < 0.2 in the bivariate analysis were entered into the logistic regression model to determine the predictors of postoperative delirium. The results of the logistic regression analysis were represented using adjusted odds ratio (AOR) and 95% confidence interval (CI) and the level of statistical significance was determined by a p value of < 0.05.

The preoperative cognitive status was categorized into three groups on the basis of the SBT score. Scores of 0–4 were designated ‘normal’; scores of 5–9 were regarded as ‘questionable’; and scores of 10 and above were classified as ‘impaired cognition’. Similarly, the duration of surgery was categorized into three groups. First, the surgeries that lasted for less than two hours, then 120 min to 179 min and then those that lasted for three or more hours.

Discussion

The incidence of postoperative delirium in our cohort of elderly patients was 24%. Among the 14 perioperative risk factors that were investigated, only four (preoperative cognitive status, perioperative anaemia, perioperative blood transfusion, duration of surgery) were independently associated with postoperative delirium. Another study recently undertaken in Ethiopia reported an incidence of 33.7% among elderly elective orthopedic patients, with increasing age, perioperative opioid or anticholinergic use and blood transfusion being predictive of postoperative delirium [25]. Both prospective studies used the CAM algorithm for the diagnosis of postoperative delirium over a 3-day period postoperatively. However, while the raters in our study were trained physicians the raters in the Ethiopian study were trained nurses. How the skill of the raters could impact the accuracy of CAM has been highlighted previously [22]. Furthermore, increasing age was a predictor of postoperative delirium in the Ethiopian study sample in which patients aged 75 years and above constituted 58% of the sample, compared with approximately 28% in our study. Thus, the older age of the patients in the former group could have contributed to the higher incidence. Similarly, another prospective study that investigated postoperative delirium in elderly hip fracture patients in China using the CAM algorithm reported a 21% incidence [26]. General anesthesia was administered to all their patients, and the anesthesiologist rated the patients twice daily during the three consecutive days following surgery. In their cohort, older age, higher blood glucose, lower albumin, longer surgery duration and red blood cell transfusions were predictors of postoperative delirium. It is conceivable that the exclusion of demented patients in their study sample could have significantly reduced the incidence reported, with cognitive impairment having been widely reported as a strong risk factor for postoperative delirium in diverse settings [27–29]. Most postoperative delirium studies are retrospective, with the actual incidence likely to be higher than that reported because of underrecognition and poor documentation. Postoperative delirium is generally underrecognized during routine care [6–8]. This shortcoming limits the validity of incidence rates derived from retrospective studies. Hence, incidence rates of 1.2%, 1.9% and 6% respectively, were reported in retrospective studies undertaken in the United States of America [30], New Zealand [31] and South Korea [32]. Notably, these studies were also conducted on the general adult patient population rather than elderly patients, with the latter being more prone to postoperative delirium [25, 26].

In contrast, studies of postoperative delirium conducted among adult patients in the intensive care unit (ICU) project a much higher incidence, with an overwhelming predominance of the hypoactive subtype. Robinson et al. investigated postoperative delirium among adult patients ≥ 50 years old, who were electively admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU) following a diverse range of surgical procedures. They administered the delirium tool; Confusion Assessment Method for the Intensive Care Unit (CAM-ICU) daily throughout the patients’ ICU stay [11]. Delirium occurred in 43% of the patients. Furthermore, Pandharipande et al. reported 70% incidence of postoperative delirium among mechanically ventilated adult surgical and trauma ICU patients who were monitored for delirium for as long as 10 days [33]. The high incidence of delirium observed in ICU patients has been partly attributed to the common use of benzodiazepines for sedation in this setting [34].

We investigated the preoperative cognitive status of our patients as a risk factor for postoperative delirium. Our study revealed that impaired cognition was associated with a 4- to 5- fold greater risk of experiencing postoperative delirium than was normal cognition. This finding is consistent with other research papers on the subject [35, 36]. Weiss et al. also reported that elderly patients with cognitive impairment, but without preexisting dementia had a 3-fold greater risk of postoperative delirium [35]. Similarly, Kim et al. reported a 9-fold greater risk of postoperative delirium in elderly patients with preexisting dementia following total knee arthroplasty [36]. These findings corroborate the 4-fold increased risk of postoperative delirium in elderly patients with preoperative cognitive impairment reported in the systematic review and meta-analysis of 53 studies by Chen et al. [29]

Delirium subtyping is deemed useful in prognosticating among patients with postoperative delirium, with the hypoactive subtype implying worse outcomes in terms of mortality and functional recovery [11, 37, 38]. Evidence from a systematic review and meta-analysis suggested that hypoactive delirium is the more common subtype in elderly patients [39]. Other sources also indicate that hypoactive delirium tends to occur comparatively more in patients with greater vulnerability and disability [37]. The motor subtypes of delirium in our patients were predominantly the ‘hypoactive’ and ‘hyperactive’ types in equal proportions, whereas the ‘mixed’ and ‘none’ subtypes were progressively less common. However, the motor phenomenology of delirium may be a dynamic rather than a stable, static state. Hence, a study undertaken by Scholtens et al. on the variability of subtypes in elderly patients who underwent hip fracture surgery revealed that the motor subtype changed over time in 62% of patients with postoperative delirium [40]. This may explain the preponderance of hyperactive delirium in some studies of elderly patients with delirium, such as reported by Gual et al. [37]. However, two studies of postoperative delirium conducted in the ICU setting showcase a large preponderance of hypoactive motor subtype whereas the hyperactive subtype was rare. In the study by Robinson et al., the proportions were 68% hypoactive, 31% mixed and 1% hyperactive [11], whereas in the study by Pandharipande et al., the proportions were 88% hypoactive delirium, and 12% mixed, with none of the delirious patients experiencing the hyperactive subtype [33]. Moreover, among acutely-ill patients (mean age of 80 ± 9 years) admitted to a geriatric medical unit in Brazil, the hypoactive subtype was predominant (53%), followed by the mixed (30%) and hyperactive subtypes (17%) [38].

There have been conflicting claims regarding the role of anesthetic techniques in predisposing patients to postoperative delirium. Our study agrees with the findings of the RAGA Study Investigators [41], who reported that the technique of anesthesia (regional versus general anesthesia) does not influence the incidence of postoperative delirium in elderly surgical patients. An and colleagues reported a greater risk of postoperative delirium in patients who had undergone surgery under general anesthesia [42]. Our study further supports the findings of the research undertaken by Bushan et al. to unravel the role of anesthesia in postoperative delirium incidence among elderly surgical patients [43]. Their systematic review and meta-analysis revealed no significant difference in postoperative delirium incidence between surgeries conducted under general anesthesia and those conducted under regional anesthesia.

Postoperative delirium is generally acknowledged to occur early in the postoperative period. Over 90% of the delirious cases in our patients were observed within 48 h following surgery. The time course of delirium manifestation among elderly orthopedic patients in South Korea revealed a similar pattern; with 38.9%, 38.9%, 16.7%, and 5.5% respectively, manifesting on days 1, 2, 3 and 4 following surgery [44]. In another study, the onset times for postoperative delirium in elderly patients that underwent orthopedic procedures in the Netherlands were 21.2%, 48.5%, 21.2%, and 9% on the day of surgery or early the day after surgery, on the second day, on the third, and on the fourth days after surgery, respectively [45].

In our study, delirium was grossly underrecognized by the healthcare providers, with only 9.6% of the cases (7/73) documented in the patients’ medical records. An earlier study undertaken by Han et al. revealed that Emergency physicians in the United States of America missed 76% of delirium cases that presented in the acute care setting [6]. The inadequacy and poor sensitivity of informal delirium assessment by both physicians and nurses have been attested to by Grossmann et al. [46]. This inadequacy persists even after a teaching intervention, if a validated tool is not applied. With the prevailing low awareness, delirium surveillance has not been considered part of routine care for our teeming population of elderly surgical patients. Presently, there is no institutionalized protocol for postoperative delirium detection or management in the hospital by either the managing surgeons or the nursing staff. Inconsistency in the existence and implementation of delirium management framework had also been reported in earlier studies conducted in Belgium [7] and Canada [8]. However, the recent increase in awareness and the development of delirium management guidelines have seen remarkable evolution in the care of elderly individuals. Consequently, simple institutional protocols that improve delirium detection have been successfully implemented elsewhere [47, 48]. Such institutionalized tool-aided monitoring mitigates the burden of postoperative delirium and promotes patient safety in the healthcare delivery to elderly individuals.

In managing patients diagnosed with postoperative delirium the evaluation should include a complete history and physical examination, medication review, laboratory evaluation and infection work-up. Oral bromazepam tablets were the therapeutic intervention offered in the management of one of the patients with postoperative delirium. Benzodiazepines arguably have no role in the management of postoperative delirium, especially in elderly individuals. Selected benzodiazepines may only be indicated in combination therapy with antipsychotics for palliative sedation of refractory agitated delirium in terminal settings or in patients with delirium tremens [49]. Reassurance was the only intervention offered to another patient with postoperative delirium, whereas for another patient it was just a clinical psychologist consultation request. Reassurance for the family members of patients with postoperative delirium, as well as relevant education regarding the phenomenon is recommended. However, the reassurance alone offered to a patient with postoperative delirium is not sufficient intervention. As part of the interdisciplinary team, a clinical psychologist may help with orientation programs for patients with postoperative delirium, as well as guide nurses and other caregivers regarding the behavioral management of delirium symptoms. Nevertheless, these alone would not suffice for the management of diagnosed postoperative delirium in elderly individuals.

The recently updated evidence-based guidelines on postoperative delirium in adult patients recommend that postoperative delirium be assessed with a validated screening tool, at least once per day for the first 3 consecutive days following surgery [50]. While recognizing that some of the risk factors for postoperative delirium are not modifiable the guidelines recommend multi-component preventive measures targeted at modifiable risk factors for the team-based approach in postoperative delirium management. One such program, the Hospital Elder Life Program (HELP) has been shown to reduce the risk of delirium by more than 50% in elderly patients [51].

The limitations of the study include a high likelihood that the actual incidence of postoperative delirium in our cohort is higher than the figure we obtained. Whereas postoperative delirium is known to occur early in the postoperative period limiting our monitoring of postoperative delirium to the first three postoperative days could have resulted in some missed cases that presented later. For example, a prospective study by Robinson et al. reported that the mean time to initial presentation in delirious patients following surgery was 2.3 ± 1.8 days postoperatively [11]. Other studies cited earlier in the text also revealed that as many as 5.5% [44] and 9% [45] of delirious cases presented on the 4th postoperative day. Hence, the steep decline in the incidence that we observed on days 1, 2 and 3 does not rule out late presentation in some patients beyond the third day following surgery. The available evidence in the literature, however, suggests that this is of a very small magnitude. Furthermore, the rating of delirium in our study was conducted only once a day such that subtle delirious incidents with their fluctuating course could have passed unnoticed even in the presence of untrained caregivers. Hence, despite the methodological compliance with the Society of Anaesthesiology and Intensive Care Medicine guidelines for postoperative delirium detection in adult patients [50], the aforementioned lacunae could still impair the accuracy of our findings.

Conclusion

Postoperative delirium in elderly surgical patients was grossly underrecognized by the managing surgical and nursing personnel in our resource-limited environment. The identified risk factors for postoperative delirium suggest that improvements in patient blood management deserve more attention in the multicomponent approach to preventing delirium in our elderly patients. These interventions should begin prior to surgery and extend well into the postoperative period. Furthermore, healthcare provider education and the implementation of routine delirium surveillance using validated tools are recommended for improved detection and treatment of postoperative delirium.
