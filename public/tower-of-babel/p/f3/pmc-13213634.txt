No patient left behind: Developing an interdisciplinary transition clinic for young adult patients with congenital heart disease
Authors: Khadija Shah, Samantha J Klaver, Sara R Ford, Suzanne McLaughlin, Toi Spates, Barbara Jandasek, Marisa Waycuilis, Janet Anderson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAdvances in pediatric cardiology care have improved survival rates among children with congenital heart disease (CHD), resulting in a growing number of adults with CHD (ACHD). Many of these individuals encounter difficulty maintaining cardiac follow-up as they transfer into adult care. Transition clinics have emerged to support continuity of care, though best practices for their design remain unclear, and implementation varies widely across institutions.ObjectiveThis article describes the development and implementation of an interdisciplinary Cardiology Transition Clinic at an academic medical center, detailing replicable processes, highlighting challenges and successes, and reviewing implications for models of care.ResultsCollaboration amongst the Divisions of Adult and Pediatric Cardiology, as well as Departments of Psychology and Social Work, led to the development of a monthly Transition Clinic at the Pediatric Heart Center (PHC). Our clinical model and standardized intake forms, used to assess patients’ transition readiness, psychosocial needs, and potential transition barriers, evolved from literature review and interdisciplinary planning. Patient transition visits include assessments from an ACHD cardiologist and a pediatric psychologist. Clear communication between clinicians and medical staff has proven vital to maintaining a smooth transition and transfer process.ConclusionsPHC’s Transition Clinic offers a structured, interdisciplinary approach to assessing readiness and addressing barriers to the transfer to adult cardiac care. Our model of transition services may contribute to the further development of standardized, evidence-based approaches, helping to navigate this critical stage in patient care across institutions.Keywords: Congenital heart disease, Healthcare transitions, Transition readiness, Clinical psychology, Multi-disciplinary clinic, Psychosocial assessment, Adolescent health services

Introduction

Globally, an estimated 1.3 million children per year are born with congenital heart disease (CHD), a diagnostic category that encompasses a variety of different anatomical or physiological cardiac-related abnormalities.1 Within the United States, approximately 40,000 children per year are born with CHD.2 Though forms of CHD span a spectrum of “mild” to “severe”, all forms of CHD have been associated with an increased risk of mortality.3 Fortunately, recent advancements in diagnostic and treatment procedures focused on pediatric cardiology concerns have shifted the prognosis for this vulnerable population, with data showing significant increases, both globally and within the United States, in survival rates for children impacted by CHD.4, 5 At present, it is estimated that more than 90% of children diagnosed with CHD worldwide survive into adulthood.6

This survival shift has resulted in a growing population of adults with congenital heart disease (ACHD). Worldwide, approximately 13 million individuals are living with ACHD.7 These individuals face unique and complex medical, developmental, and psychosocial challenges that require specialized coordinated care. However, transition from pediatric to adult cardiology care is often fragmented, with up to 62.7% of patients failing to meet follow-up standards consistent with the guidelines set forth by the American College of Cardiology and the American Heart Association (AHA).8 Absent effective transition, adolescents and young adults with CHD are at risk for preventable medical deterioration and significant psychosocial consequences, including anxiety, depression, and maladaptive health behaviors.9, 10, 11 There is a clear and growing need for specialized clinics designed to support effective pediatric-to-adult healthcare transition (HCT).

Development and gaps in HCT clinics

In 1993, the Society for Adolescent Medicine called for healthcare that facilitates “a smoother transition for adolescents with chronic health conditions from the child to adult healthcare system”, asserting a need to focus on developing more intentional support.12 The position paper cautioned that adolescents and young adults with chronic conditions are at heightened risk for unnecessary dependency, psychosocial delay, and disrupted development. Their summary emphasized the importance of healthcare systems that promote autonomy, personal responsibility, and self-management for this population. These principles were reaffirmed in 2003, with strong support for the need to address both medical and psychosocial aspects of adolescent care in effective HCT programs.13

The American Academy of Pediatrics, the American Academy of Family Physicians, and the American College of Physicians introduced a structured framework to assist with this challenge, reporting on the “Six Core Elements” of HCT.14 These components (policy development, tracking, readiness assessment, planning, transfer of care, and transfer completion) were updated in 2018 and 2023, and have been widely adopted within HCT programs across various health subspecialities, including cardiology.15 Still, studies evaluating the design and implementation of HCT clinics have highlighted significant variation from institution to institution, including differences in staffing, readiness evaluation tools, education practices, provider collaboration, and follow-up processes.16, 17, 18, 19

This inconsistency is pronounced in subspecialty care settings, including pediatric cardiology. Professional organizations, including the American Heart Association, have emphasized the need for coordinated, multidisciplinary transition programs for adolescents with CHD,20 however, many pediatric cardiology clinics lack the necessary infrastructure. The impact of inconsistency in cardiology-specific HCT programs is reflected in population-level data. According to the 2019 National Survey of Children’s Health, overall HCT readiness remains low, hovering around 20.5%.21 Adolescents and young adults with cardiac conditions report similarly low rates of key transition conversations: 26.4% recall discussing insurance, 49% have talked about adult care needs, and only 22.9% have addressed the transition to adult providers.22 A systematic review found that 3.6–62.7% of adolescents and young adults with CHD experience lapses in cardiac follow-up, with the highest dropout rates occurring in U.S. populations and among youth with less severe or complex conditions.8

Structured HCT programs appear to mitigate this risk, reducing loss to follow-up from 36.2% to 12.7%16 and improving relevant outcomes such as medical knowledge, self-management capabilities, social connectedness, and overall quality of life.23, 24 Significant variability remains in the design and implementation of these programs, particularly as they relate to interdisciplinary collaboration (including strong pediatric-adult provider partnerships), personalized education, and systematic readiness tracking.25, 26

Despite awareness that adolescents and young adults with CHD are at risk for elevated rates of depression, anxiety, and broad behavioral difficulties,27and research identifying these psychosocial challenges as a barrier to effective HCT28, the integration of mental healthcare providers, including psychologists, is an underdeveloped area in HCT program planning and evaluation. In a study of North American pediatric cardiology clinics, only a small percentage of HCT clinics include a designated mental healthcare provider or psychologist within their integrated team and had yet to incorporate psychosocial screenings into routine cardiac care.29 Participating cardiology clinics reported the lack of behavioral health personnel such as psychologists and social workers was a significant hindrance to being able to provide the called for psychosocial and developmental care.29 Importantly, research has shown that HCT clinics that have incorporated mental health and psychological services report improved identification of unmet needs, streamlined referrals, and reduced stigma.30 This research supporting the effect of these co-located services on the quality of life for adolescents and young adults with CHD led to the release of a Scientific Statement by the American Heart Association calling for large scale research on initiatives that embed mental health professionals within pediatric and adult cardiology interdisciplinary teams.31 Yet, barriers to integration such as funding constraints, staffing limitations, and lack of institutional prioritization continue to impede widespread implementation.

As highlighted above, despite decades of calls for improved transition processes13, 15 and a growing body of literature emphasizing positive outcomes associated with specialized HCT, there remains no unified model or best-practice standard. HCT clinics vary widely in structure, age of transfer, degree of interdisciplinary collaboration, and engagement with patients and families.16, 17, 20 This inconsistency has the potential to negatively impact patients and families, particularly in fields such as pediatric cardiology, where poor care continuity can lead to worsening cardiac function, emergence of new complications, and in some cases, life-threatening cardiac or pulmonary events.10, 32 In addition to an increase in physical health-related risks, the transition period can exacerbate or lead to additional psychological challenges, including anxiety, depression, and the occurrence of significant maladaptive health-risk behaviors.9, 11

Aims

This paper presents the development logistics and process outcomes of integrating behavioral health components in a transition-focused cardiology clinic. Our interdisciplinary Cardiology Transition Clinic, embedded within a pediatric cardiology practice incorporated integrated mental health services and of the use of psychosocial screenings as a primary outcome of a multi-year quality-improvement initiative. By describing the structure, rationale, and operational strategies of the clinic, including barriers that we encountered and solutions that we employed to overcome them, we hope to offer a replicable, evidence-informed model that other institutions might adopt or adapt. We report process outcomes supporting the value and effectiveness of an integrated behavioral health model for a subspecialty HCT program.

Clinic development

Institutional and HCT clinic context and inception

The Transition Clinic was developed within the Pediatric Heart Center (PHC) at Hasbro Children’s Hospital of Brown University Health (BUH), a large, urban academic medical center in the Northeast serving a racially and socioeconomically diverse pediatric and young adult patient population. Prior to its creation, patients aging out of pediatric cardiology were routinely referred to our institution’s ACHD Clinic, though gaps in follow-up were common.

Clinic development was led by directors from BUH’s Divisions of Adult and Pediatric Cardiology in collaboration with an ACHD-trained cardiologist. Decisions regarding clinic structure, including the integration of behavioral health, emphasis on continuity of care, and clinic frequency, were informed by multiple factors. First, as previously noted, a growing body of literature demonstrates that adolescents and young adults with CHD exhibit higher rates of anxiety, depression, and psychosocial vulnerabilities27, which have been linked to poorer transition readiness and greater likelihood of lapses in follow-up care28, suggesting that behavioral health integration may be a critical component for HCT models. Second, national guidelines and consensus statements emphasize the importance of multidisciplinary, developmentally informed HCT models that address both medical and psychosocial needs14, 20, 33, further supporting this critical HCT component. Third, a substantial body of literature highlights the high rates of loss to follow-up during the transition from pediatric to adult care (particularly among patients with CHD) and identifies disruptions in established patient-provider relationships as a key contributing factor.8 These findings were likewise reflected in clinical observations from key clinic development members, including the ACDH-trained cardiologist, with patients and families frequently reporting anxiety related to transfer, limited self-efficacy in navigating adult systems, and logistical barriers that interfered with successful follow-up.

These considerations resulted in the decision to host frequent (e.g., monthly) Transition Clinic visits, incorporating a “warm handoff” model, in which direct, in-person introduction of the patient to the adult provider occurred within a familiar pediatric setting, thereby promoting continuity, trust, and engagement. In order to effectively integrate behavioral health, the Transition Clinic visits were likewise structured to function within a “tandem visit” model, with each 90-minute Transition Clinic visit being divided equally between a ACHD cardiologist and a pediatric psychologist. A pediatric psychologist was selected as the embedded behavioral health provider due to their specialized training in pediatric medical populations, including cardiology, expertise in evidence-based assessment and intervention, and experience in interdisciplinary and family-based care. Specific responsibilities of the ACHD cardiologist and the pediatric psychologist are described in Section 2.3.4. It was determined that, following a patient’s visit, the ACHD cardiologist and pediatric psychologist would meet together to integrate findings from validated screening tools and clinical interviews to determine the patient’s overall readiness for transfer.

Once this general Transition Clinic structure was identified, the interdisciplinary planning group (which included the ACHD cardiologist, several pediatric psychologists, a pediatric cardiology nurse, and a research assistant) met repeatedly over a six-month period and to develop pre-visit materials, screening tools, and workflow, as well as to identify possible eligible patients. The emerging workflow is outlined in Appendix A.

HCT clinic referral and pre-visit workflow

In developing the design and model of our Transition Clinic, the team utilized the tenets of the Six Core Elements of Health Care Transitions. These elements include: (1) discuss with the patient/family about a transition of care process/policy; (2) track and monitor progress of youth in preparation for transition; (3) assess/conduct readiness assessments and orient to adult practice; (4) develop transition plan with integration into adult practice; (5) transfer of care including initial visit with adult provider; and (6) transition completion with adult providers15, 34 as guiding principles. Each core element has been considered as we developed and implemented our clinic and will be noted throughout.

Eligibility

Patient age (18 +) is a useful metric for providers to “flag” patients eligible for our Transition Clinic. However, preparation for this process starts early in the provider/patient relationship by defining for families the concepts of transition and transfer of care. We advise parents to start preparing for this transitional phase when a child is about 10–12 years old, with more emphasis in the teen years. Transfer of care usually occurs between ages 18–21 years.

In the case of CHD, conversations between the family and provider start when the child is very young to involve the family in understanding the child’s condition: what a CHD diagnosis means and what to expect in the future. Our providers begin teaching basic clinical concepts to school-age patients to help ease the future transition. We encourage participation in our annual Heart Camp (ages 8–16 +), which further enriches and reinforces patient and family health literacy.

Several weeks prior to the start of our Transition Clinic, our cardiology providers met as a group to achieve consensus on patient eligibility. We agreed that in addition to age, complexity and clinical status of the patient have a direct impact on timing of the referral to Transition Clinic. Eligible patients should have a stable cardiopulmonary status and be current on all testing including imaging, ambulatory ECG monitoring, cardiopulmonary metabolic testing, and medication management. A patient who has active/evolving hemodynamic issues, such as need for cardiac catheterization in the next 6–12 months, would not be a good candidate for our Transition Clinic. In individual cases, the psychological benefits of our Transition Clinic and transfer to adult CHD care may outweigh the active clinical complexity of the patient’s CHD. In this scenario, the pediatric and adult CHD provider will collaborate with the psychologist and involve the patient and family in a shared decision-making discussion about transfer.

Referral Process

At present, our Transition Clinic only accepts referrals from providers in our institution’s Division of Pediatric Cardiology. Pediatric cardiologists can refer eligible patients in one of two ways: (1) during a routine appointment or (2) via medical chart review and outreach. In the first pathway, the pediatric cardiologist will spend time during the routinely scheduled appointment with the patient discussing the need for adult care transition and transition support and provide information on what to expect during their Transition Clinic appointment and provide the patient with the Transition Clinic flyer as an overview (Appendix B). In the second pathway, the pediatric cardiologist will examine their broader patient list for those who are approaching adulthood and require transition support. Specifically, we leveraged our institutional electronic medical record (EMR) and BusinessObjects software to create a Web Intelligence (Webi) report of potential eligible patients based on age and diagnosis, sorted by provider. The provider then contacts confirmed-eligible patients to discuss our new model and clinic for adult care transition support, and provides information on what to expect during their Transition Clinic appointment. Regardless of the specific referral method, the pediatric cardiology provider then coordinates scheduling with the trained medical assistant (MA).

The pediatric cardiologist provides information regarding what to expect during their visit within our Transition Clinic, including explanation of the concept, purpose and structure. The importance of ongoing adult care, transition support, and evaluation of overall HCT readiness is emphasized. The pediatric cardiologist also spends specific time highlighting the integrated nature of the Transition Clinic, including the “tandem visit” model and encouraging the patient to be an engaged, active participant in both segments of the visit. A trained MA calls to confirm patient appointments, re-emphasizing the “tandem visit” model. Repetition of the Transition Clinic structure and purpose occurs over a period of weeks-to-months to build patients and families’ understanding of the model, reinforce the importance of attendance, allow them ample time to ask questions, and make informed decisions about their care. Discussion of transition plans and policy is Core Element #1 of HCT.

Scheduling challenges

Initial Transition Clinic sessions had challenges with maintaining a full patient schedule, with many scheduled patients who did not attend their appointments. To better understand these challenges, the psychologist and a research assistant (RA) attended the weekly cardiology case management conference and provider meetings to discuss recruitment and retention strategies. MA’s were also involved in identifying scheduling and communication barriers; their outreach role in visit reminders contributed to early identification of any potential patient concerns. To improve consistent recruitment for Transition Clinics, our pediatric cardiology colleagues now routinely assess their panels for patients approaching or at age for eligibility (18 + years old) who could benefit from the Transition Clinic. Transition Clinic recruitment became a standing provider meeting agenda item, and resulted in development of strategies including a “look forward” on existing provider schedules to identify eligible patients and consider changing their appointment to the Transition Clinic. Another strategy was to create a “Patient Reminder” in the EMR, in real time during the patient visit, to flag patients with eligibility for the Transition Clinic in the next six to 24 months, structuring a a future delivery date for a provider prompt to initiate the Transition Clinic scheduling process for that patient.

Despite these efforts, in early sessions, we noted some patients arrived at their appointment with little understanding of the purpose of the PHC’s Transition Clinic visit. A subsequent improvement cycle saw creation of a visual flyer for the pediatric cardiologist to share with their patient, in person, at the time of their referral (Appendix B). The flyer provides information to the patient regarding the purpose/goals of an HCT clinic, the model and expectations of PHC’s Transition Clinic, and contact information for the PHC’s Transition Clinic team. The flyer was developed with input from critical partners within the Divisions of Adult and Pediatric Cardiology, the Department of Psychology, and our institution’s Marketing and Communications Department. Importantly, the structure of the Transition Clinic does not include any additional costs; clinical visit services are reimbursed through insurance coverage. Use of standard clinic procedures allows for efficient and consistent program operations.

HCT clinic visit workflow

Initial arrival and pre-visit intake packets

When patients arrive for their Transition Clinic appointment, they are approached by a trained RA, who welcomes them to the Transition Clinic, confirms the names and credentials of the two providers they will be meeting with (e.g., the ACHD cardiologist and the transition-trained pediatric psychologist), and provides patients with a pre-visit intake packet administered on iPads via REDCap. Study data arecollected and managed using REDCap electronic data capture tools hosted at Brown University Health.34, 35 REDCap (Research Electronic Data Capture) is a secure, web-based software platform designed to support data capture for research studies, providing (1) an intuitive interface for validated data capture; (2) audit trails for tracking data manipulation and export procedures; (3) automated export procedures for seamless data downloads to common statistical packages; and (4) procedures for data integration and interoperability with external sources. This data collection process allows us to track and monitor preparation and transfer of patients seen in the clinic (Core Element #2)

There are five measures within the pre-visit intake packet: a demographics form, a cardiology-specific health knowledge assessment, cardiology-specific self-care assessment, and brief screeners for anxiety and depression used to assess readiness for transition (Core Element #3).The pre-visit intake packet was created with the intention of providing the two primary clinicians with a “snapshot” of the patient’s broad physical and mental health functioning, as well as their HCT readiness, in order to better guide the foci of the Transition Clinic visits. The pre-visit intake packet was created with input from the Transition Clinic team, including adult and pediatric cardiologists, pediatric psychologists and interns, MAs, RAs, and office administrators. The pre-visit intake packet forms have been tailored to best suit the needs of adolescent and young adult patients with CHD. These forms are described here.

Demographics. The demographics form asks patients to describe their age, sex assigned at birth, gender identity, sexual identity, race, and ethnicity. In addition, items assessing social determinants of health were integrated, including housing, food insecurity, insurance, or transportation issues. These items were edited with the input from our department social workers to ensure that we accurately identify r patients at risk of losing their public health insurance based on local eligibility criteria. A comprehensive list of questions for social work indication can be found in Appendix D. This form is modeled on a formalized intake questionnaire utilized by our institution’s Department of Psychology for new CHD patients.

Cardiology-Specific Health Knowledge Assessment. To assess for cardiology-specific health knowledge (e.g., a patient’s capacity to manage their own health and healthcare, specifically related to their cardiac condition), patients complete a 16-item health knowledge measure, modeled on a clinical assessment tool (the “General Health Knowledge Assessment for Youth”).36 Patients are able to choose from three response options (“Yes, I know this,” “I need to learn more,” and “Not applicable”). Example items include, “I understand the long term potential issues associated with my heart condition,” and “I know what cardiac symptoms require more urgent medical attention.”

Cardiology-Specific Self-Care Assessment. To assess for cardiology-specific self-care capabilities (e.g., specific self-care skills a patient may have specifically related to their cardiac condition and ensuing healthcare needs, patients complete a 17-item self-care measure, modeled on a clinical assessment tool (the “Transition Self-Care Assessment for Young Adults with Congenital Heart Disease”).37 Patients are able to choose from three response options (“Yes, I know this,” “I need to learn more,” and “Not applicable”). Example items include, “I know or can find the name and contact information for my heart doctor (cardiologist),” and “I carry important health information with me every day (e.g., insurance card, allergies, medications, emergency contact information, medical summary).”

Of note, the cardiology-specific health knowledge and self-care assessments were adapted by the American College of Cardiology (with input from the American Academy of Pediatrics and the American College of Physicians) from existing validated transition readiness tools. While these adapted measures were developed through an interdisciplinary process and informed by established measures, no formal psychometric validation studies have been conducted at this time.

Anxiety Screener. To screen for possible symptoms of anxiety, patients complete the General Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7),38 a 7-item measure of possible anxiety symptoms occurring over the previous two weeks. Patients are able to choose from four response options, on a Likert-type scale ranging from 0 (Not at All) to 3 (Nearly Every Day). Example items include, “Feeling nervous, anxious, or on edge,” and “Not being able to stop or control worrying.” The GAD-7 has established reliability and validity across a variety of samples.38, 39, 40

Depression Screener. To screen for possible symptoms of depression, patients complete the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9),41 a 9-item measure of possible depressive symptoms. Patients are able to choose from four response options, on a Likert-type scale ranging from 0 (Not at All) to 3 (Nearly Every Day). Example items include, “Feeling down, depressed, or hopeless” and “Thoughts that you would be better off dead or of hurting yourself in some way.” The PHQ-9 has established reliability and validity across a variety of samples.42, 43, 44

Though patients within the Transition Clinic often attend the visit with one or more members of their family, the RA explains to the patients and their family members that the intake packet is to be completed only by the adolescent or young adult patient, as it will be a tool utilized to accurately assess the patients’ individual readiness for HCT. If available space in the clinic waiting room allows, patients may be asked to sit away from their families to ensure independent completion.

On completion of the pre-visit intake packet, responses are automatically recorded and saved in REDCap to be reviewed by an RA, who creates a summary topsheet (Appendix C) that is given to the two primary providers. The topsheet highlights key data points and follow-up needs, and specifically includes any scores that are automatically calculated in REDCap (e.g., for the GAD-7 and PHQ-9), any automatically flagged responses that may indicate a positive need for mental health or social work follow-up, and whether or not the patient is currently enrolled in the online medical portal, where they are able to access critical medical information and communications from their past visits with providers in the Division of Pediatric Cardiology and future visits with providers the Division of Adult Cardiology.

Patients with intellectual/developmental delay

Throughout the implementation of the Transition Clinic, it was noted that there was a subset of patients with co-occurring developmental and/or intellectual delays. It is important to note that CHD often co-occurs with developmental or intellectual delays, with many patients presenting with complex symptomologies that span the developmental spectrum.29 Thus, it became critical for the Transition Clinic to expand our original pre-visit intake forms to better address the needs of these patients. To do so, our Transition Clinic team developed two additional versions of our original demographics form: one to be filled out by patients themselves, which included developmentally-appropriate language, and one to be filled out on behalf of the patient by a parent, caregiver, or other guardian. In addition, our Transition Clinic team made two additional versions of the cardiology-specific health knowledge assessment and the cardiology-specific self-care assessment: one to be filled out by the patient themselves, which included developmentally-appropriate language, and one to be filled out on behalf of the patient by a parent, caregiver, or other guardian. These adjusted measures were heavily modeled on formalized assessment tools (e.g., the “Transition Readiness Assessment for Youth with Intellectual Disabilities or Developmental Disabilities,” the “Transition Readiness Assessment for Parents and Caregivers of Youth with Intellectual Disabilities or Developmental Disabilities,” the “Self-Care Assessment for Young Adults with Intellectual Disabilities or Developmental Disabilities” and the “Self-Care Assessment for Parents or Caregivers of Young Adults with Intellectual Disabilities or Developmental Disabilities”).38 Ther Transition Clinic team made the decision to exclude the anxiety and depression screeners from the pre-visit intake packets for patients with co-occurring developmental and/or intellectual delays, given the lack of well-established measures for this specific population45, opting instead to rely on clinical interview to accurately identify these concerns. We are monitoring ongoing research in this field to identify promising measures for future integration into our clinic procedures46, 47.

In addition to altering the pre-visit intake packets for patients with co-occurring mild developmental and/or intellectual delays, parental questionnaires were created for patients with more severe delays who are unable to complete screeners on their own. This is the only circumstance in which parental screeners are used.

Questionnaire scoring

During the first two Transition Clinic days, paper copies of the intake packet were administered to patients. By the third Transition Clinic, the intake packet was fully copied into REDCap and available to fill out online via iPads.Administering the intake packet via iPads has several advantages, including automated scoring of the GAD-7 and PHQ-9, flagging of safety items and questions indicating a need for social work referrals, which were previously manually done by the RA. Calculated scores of the GAD-7 and PHQ-9 (i.e., minimal to severe anxiety or minimal to severe depression) are semi-automated with input from the RA. Automated flagging and scoring decreases potential error and increases efficiency of clinic flow.

Within the Transition Clinic, and in compliance with our institution's standard operating procedures, completed pre-visit intake packets are stored both physically and electronically. After being printed and attached to the summary topsheet, completed physical copies of the intake packet are given to the office administrator to be scanned into the patient chart. After this, the RA stores them in a locked cabinet. Electronic copies of the intake packets are stored in REDCap, as data instantly uploads into REDCap after submission. It is important to note that the summary topsheet is not documented as a separate measure in REDCap, but rather dispersed within the same survey completed by patients. However, patients are unable to see if questions are flagged, or their overall scores, as these fields are programmed to be hidden when the intake packet is distributed as a REDCap survey. The only summary topsheet field found on a different instrument is the post-visit review indicating if a social work or psychology referral was necessary.

As the pre-visit intake packets for patients with co-occurring developmental or intellectual delays were created as a separate REDCap survey, submitted data is stored in a separate REDCap project. Similar to the original pre-visit intake packet, the summary topsheet fields are dispersed throughout the instrument and hidden from patient view, though the version for individuals with developmental delay does not include any fields for scoring the GAD-7 and PHQ-9 since it is not included in that version of the packet. Social work and outpatient psychology referrals are also stored in another instrument within the REDCap project. Printed, physical copies of the completed form and the summary topsheet are stored in a locked cabinet by the RA.

Tandem visits with pediatric psychology and ACHD cardiology

As discussed earlier, and as illustrated in the sample schedule (Fig. 1), Transition Clinic patients are scheduled in alternating “cross over” time slots across the two primary providers' calendars, allowing for smooth transitions between appointments that decrease time and resource burden for patients. A patient may be scheduled to see the pediatric psychologist first and the ACHD cardiologist second or vice versa. On any given Transition Clinic day, up to 8 patients can be seen.

Sample schedule depicting the “cross over” approach to tandem visits. Each set of two patients are scheduled in four slots. This design achieves a consistent cadence of visit intervals for each provider.

The initial visit with the pediatric psychologist includes a review of the overall goal of the Transition Clinic and the reasoning behind the assessment tools completed. These are reviewed with both parent and patient during the initial part of the visit. Documentation of the patient’s visit with the pediatric psychologist evolved over time, as the Transition Clinic was being implemented. Initially, the documentation in the EMR was an adaptation of an initial evaluation template used by providers within our institution’s Department of Psychology. The template involved questions related to the patient’s demographics, psychosocial history, and current emotional/behavioral functioning. The template was adapted to include cardiology-specific (e.g., knowledge of cardiac condition, past and current medications, limitations, and interventions) and transition-specific questions (e.g., confidence in ability to engage in self-management of care, concerns related to ability to engage in self-management of care). Attention is paid to level of efficacy in self-management of the patient’s cardiac condition, awareness and compliance with any restrictions or medication regimes, history of mental health diagnoses or treatments and psychosocial factors that may support successful transition to adult care vs the need to obtain further psychological services/supports in order to develop additional self-efficacy skills prior to the transition (Core Element #3).

On the morning of Transition Clinic day, each pediatric cardiologist will identify their own patients scheduled that day and have a brief face-to-face conversation with the ACHD cardiologist to provide background, context, and personal insights about the patient and family. Whenever possible, the pediatric cardiologist may enter the exam room with the ACHD cardiologist to make a personal introduction for the family (“warm hand-off”). The visit typically begins with an introduction explaining why a formal transition process was considered beneficial and outlining the Transition Clinic’s goal of ensuring that patients and their families feel equipped with the tools needed to navigate the transition to adult medicine. The ACHD cardiologist then asks the patient to describe what they know about their diagnosis in their own words. The ACHD cardiologist confirms the accuracy of their understanding, and, if needed, uses pictorial descriptions to further support comprehension. The ACHD cardiologist reviews the patient’s EKG results, as well as their pre-visit intake paperwork, and addresses each topic thoroughly with the patient. Special attention is given to ensure the patient understands the importance of lifelong monitoring, any unique patient-specific recommendations, considerations around contraception and pregnancy, and general principles of primary prevention. A decision is made regarding whether any diagnostic studies are needed to address specific cardiac concerns or as part of anatomy-specific follow-up. Before concluding the visit, the ACHD cardiologist addresses any issues related to MyChart access, including downloading the EMR phone application or assisting with troubleshooting for patients transitioning from a child profile to their own independent account (Core Elements #4 & 5).

Following the completion of both the psychology and cardiology visits, the two Transition Clinic providers meet briefly to collaborate, review their clinical perceptions of the patient's and parent's readiness for transition. During these consultations, completed assessment tools as well as clinical interview results are discussed. At this time, planning is done to identify those patients who may need psychological intervention and/or psychosocial support (e.g. issues related to health insurance or housing insecurity) that may require a return visit to the Pediatric Heart Center (PHC) prior to readiness to transition to the adult clinic. This process is completed for both parents and patients. When a patient is deemed ready, based on both written and clinical assessments, adult follow-up appointments are facilitated through the administrative staff from the Adult Congenital Cardiology Clinic if a direct referral is deemed appropriate. Patients typically only have one appointment at our Transition Clinic, though in some cases, an additional Transition Clinic visit may be recommended for the patient, the parent, or both, and is scheduled prior to check out at the PHC.

Results

Since our initial Transition Clinic, we have stored completed screeners to better understand the population served at PHC and their specific needs. Data are collected and managed using REDCap hosted at BUH.34, 35

The Institutional Review Board of Rhode Island Hospital approved a chart review of the Transition Clinic. To date, over eighty patients have been offered an appointment at our Transition Clinic. Most patients had completed a high school degree/equivalent or higher (76.4%), with 13.7% describing themselves as full-time students and nearly a quarter engaged in full-time work. Almost half (45.1%) of patients had public health insurance at the time of their Transition Clinic visit, though it is important to note that insurance is required before Clinic visits. Discussion

CHD is a chronic condition requiring lifelong medical care, and young adult patients graduating from pediatric services are more at risk of losing access to that care. The development of HCT clinics aimed at supporting this transition are critical. Research shows that formal transition processes for pediatric patients with chronic illnesses, including CHD, greatly improves follow-up with adult medical services and healthcare outcomes. Despite this, no unified model or best-practice standard exists and many models fail to emphasize integrated cardiac and behavioral health care. Several recent studies are exploring psychosocial factors that affect successful transition such as self-efficacy,48 while others emphasize multidisciplinary teams but most identify multiple medical specialties49 in the absence of psychology or integrated behavioral health. Our institution’s Transition Clinic was created with the intention of addressing gaps in the HCT from pediatric to adult services in patients with CHD. Through a series of meetings between pediatric cardiologists, ACHD cardiologists, and behavioral health providers, we developed a monthly Transition Clinic in which patients see both a pediatric psychologist and an ACHD cardiologist. A detailed description of the steps involved in developing our integrated cardiology HCT embedded within an academic medical center specialty clinic was presented. We offer an implementation model for an effective and supportive HCT clinic that incorporates both medical and psychological evaluation.

In 2022 the American Heart Association revised its construct of cardiovascular health, expanding the age spectrum to include the entire life course. The foundational contexts of social determinants of health and psychological health were addressed as crucial factors in optimizing and preserving cardiovascular health.50 HCT for pediatric patients with CHD focuses on a key inflection point in their lifelong cardiovascular health. Our Transition Clinic incorporates validated psychological screeners, and an assessment of social determinants of health incorporating broadly accepted content areas51 and reflecting the AHA’s scientific statement of the importance of social determinants in addressing inequities in cardiac care.52

Critical to the ongoing successful development of the clinic, a review process has been established between the cardiologist, psychologist, and members of the Transition Clinic team. Attention to feedback on service glitches and exploration of ways to improve service delivery were emphasized. Within this context, we learned that key elements requiring attention for successful transition included: preparing the patient/family, ensuring enough lead time, offering the service within the safe and familiar location of the pediatric cardiology clinic, the benefit of warm hand-off between the pediatric and ACHD cardiologist, and between the ACHD cardiologist and psychologist, ensuring effective engagement, and recognizing that ambivalence by both patient and parent play a role in their motivation to attend the Transition Clinic. The benefit of a practice in which psychology is already embedded in the cardiology clinic cannot be overstated, allowing for our unique model of patient care delivery and contribution to the literature on effective transition models. We strive to emphasize the significance of the collaborative role of psychology as an integral part of the transition from pediatric to adult care within a specialty clinic serving patients with CHD.

Conclusion and future directions

Future directions

We plan to utilize our ongoing data collection to improve our current clinic flow and procedures, to assess patient satisfaction, and to determine transition readiness before and after Transition Clinic appointments. Given our experiences to date, we are discussing a new Clinic visit process to screen younger patients for transition readiness. We are exploring interventions which will provide additional preparation for transition, identify CHD patients at an earlier age, and target support for parents. Parents have been engaged with their Pediatric Cardiologist since their child’s birth or even prenatal diagnosis. Our first year’s experience with a Transition Clinic, balancing the roles of an ACHD specialist and pediatric psychologist, more clearly defined the challenge that it is often the parent of the young adult with CHD who may find the “leaving” more emotionally challenging. Recognizing parental readiness as an element affecting successful transition to ACHD clinics, we plan to explore additional parental support such as parenting groups or a limited number of sessions with the psychologist to address transition issues as a parent. Finally, we will be tracking the number of adult cardiology appointments made and kept by the young adults who have transitioned through our Transition Clinic (Core Element #6).

Our institution is one of the first clinics to implement a transition clinic model that integrates visits with both cardiology and psychology, allowing for a holistic assessment of transition readiness for patients and their families. Recognizing the value of this model, an area of future development is to establish a long-term integrated behavioral health approach with onsite psychosocial support in the adult cardiology clinic to match the model present within the Pediatric Heart Center. The barriers to implementing this integrated care structure remain to be addressed, such as the need for foundational negotiations to develop integrated behavioral services at the ACHD Clinic and challenges with ensuring financial reimbursement for patients arriving at academic medical centers from across state lines.

As it has been less than a year since launching the Transition Clinic, no patients have been seen for their scheduled one year follow-up at the ACHD Clinic. Our goal is to continue to collect data on the adult side for empirical research on outcomes, including completion of case studies for longitudinal outcomes and the effectiveness of this Transition Clinic model once this milestone has been reached.

Conclusion

Questions about the components of effective transition services for young adults with chronic conditions have been asked for decades. Recurrent questions include: what preparation is necessary, what steps need to be taken in the transfer of medical care, what are some effective strategies, and are the components of effective models.12 The conception and early development of the Transition Clinic for CHD patients described here may provide insight on these points and guidance for other programs considering development of transition services. The foundation of our Transition Clinic is built upon close collaboration between all individuals involved in patient care across the psychology and cardiology departments. We have strived to provide individualized support to patients and their families as they prepare to enter the adult system. A key element is hosting the Transition appointments in an environment where patients and their parents are able to connect with their new cardiologist and a behavioral health specialist in a familiar setting with the opportunity to say ‘good-bye’ to their pediatric cardiology team. As this is an especially vulnerable time, the addition of connecting patients with social work, if indicated, can be invaluable to a patient’s ability to follow-up with adult services. We have outlined the strengths and challenges faced in the development and implementation of our institution’s Transition Clinic tailored to the needs of patients with congenital heart disease. The more we learn about the transition process at other institutions, and with repetition and improvement of our own Transition Clinic, best practices will continue to evolve giving direction for the highest standard of patient care.
