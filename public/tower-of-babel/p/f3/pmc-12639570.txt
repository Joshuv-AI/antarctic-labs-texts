Evaluating Research Productivity Trends Among Orthopaedic Adult Reconstruction Fellowship Applicants: A Bibliometric Study
Authors: Emily Tufford, Andrew H. Kim, Peter F. Monahan, Peter Tortora, Luke B. Elias, Andrew B. Harris, Gregory J. Kirchner, Mark W. Mason
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe research benchmarks necessary for a successful adult reconstruction fellowship match remain unclear. This study aimed to characterize and identify trends in the academic profiles of adult reconstruction fellows in the United States.MethodsThe American Association of Hip and Knee Surgeons fellowship directory was used to identify U.S. adult reconstruction fellowships programs that listed fellows graduating from 2019 to 2023. For each fellow, total number of publications, authorship position, number of publications with an adult reconstruction focus, and number of citations were collected using Scopus. Only publications accepted or published before the fellow’s match year were compiled. Research productivity was compared across medical degree, sex, fellowship year, and residency program geographic region.ResultsA total of 247 fellows from 46 programs were identified. Fellows published an average of 5.5 ± 9.7 publications during residency, with 66.6 ± 190.4 citations. There was no significant change in research productivity from 2019 to 2023. Average number of publications per fellow was significantly greater for fellows with an MD degree compared with a DO degree (5.8 ± 10.0 vs 2.1 ± 4.2; P < .05). Fellows who completed residency in the northeast had the highest average number of publications per fellow (9.9 ± 16.3) and average number of first author publications per fellow (2.6 ± 4.0) among all regions.ConclusionsThere was no change in research output among adult reconstruction fellows graduating from 2019 to 2023, suggesting that research may not be driving the increased competitiveness of adult reconstruction fellowships.

Introduction

Over 90% of orthopaedic surgery residents plan on pursuing postgraduate fellowship training [1,2]. In addition to improving a surgeon’s marketability for job opportunities, [3,4] subspecialization also generates positive financial returns [5,6] and provides residents additional training to further develop their surgical skills in their desired subspecialty [2,7,8]. Among all orthopaedic surgery fellowships, adult reconstruction has had the largest significant increase in the percentage of positions filled [9]. This trend has resulted in increased selectivity, as adult reconstruction had the lowest match rate in 2017 [10].

When evaluating fellowship applicants, orthopaedic surgery fellowship program directors consider the interview, letters of recommendation, residency program attended, and personal connections as the most important factors when ranking candidates [[11], [12], [13], [14], [15], [16]]. For adult reconstruction fellowship program directors, research experience was ranked among the top six variables in ranking criterion [16]. Despite the importance of academic productivity, the research productivity benchmarks of successfully matched applicants in adult reconstruction fellowships remain unknown.

While studies have examined the research output from adult reconstruction fellowships and their faculty, [17,18] information regarding the academic productivity of successfully matched adult reconstruction fellows is limited. Thus, the purpose of this study was to 1) characterize the research profiles of recent adult reconstruction fellows in the United States during their residency training and 2) identify publication trends by fellow sex, degree, and residency program attended. We hypothesize that research output will increase overtime during the period of study, indicating that research output could be a driver of the increased competitiveness of the subspecialty.

Material and methods

Fellowship program data

The American Association of Hip and Knee Surgeons fellowship directory [19] was used to identify all adult reconstruction fellowship programs in the United States. Programs were excluded 1) if their fellows were not listed on the fellowship website and 2) if the program was located outside the United States. Included programs were then categorized geographically by U.S. region [20]. Each website was accessed to compile a list of adult reconstruction fellows from the previous five fellowship cycles (2019-2023). Fellows were excluded if their research or academic profiles could not be verified or were not publicly available.

Data collection

Fellows identified in the initial data collection were then searched in Scopus, [21] a publicly available abstract and citation database of peer-reviewed literature, to obtain their academic profiles. This methodology has been previously used in bibliometric studies within orthopaedic surgery [[22], [23], [24], [25]]. To confirm fellow profiles, names and educational backgrounds were cross-referenced with publicly available information from departmental websites and professional profiles. After verification, total number of publications, authorship position, number of publications with an adult reconstruction focus, and number of citations were collected for each fellow. These metrics were included from publications published or accepted up to December 31 of the year prior to the fellow’s match year to reflect their resident research profile during the fellowship application cycle. Nonindexed research experiences, such as abstracts, presentations, letters, and posters were excluded from analysis.

Statistical analysis

All continuous data were reported as means and standard deviations. Comparisons between research productivity and fellow medical degree and sex were performed using unpaired Mann-Whitney U tests. Fellowship year and residency program region were compared with publication metrics using one-way analysis of variance on ranks with Dunn’s post hoc comparisons between groups. P values <0.05 were considered significant.

Research productivity by fellow

In comparing research productivity between male and female adult reconstruction fellows, there was no significant difference in the average number of publications per fellow (5.7 ± 10.0 vs 2.5 ± 2.8) or the average number of first author publications per fellow (1.7 ± 2.8 vs 1.6 ± 2.0). Fellows with an MD degree compared to those with a DO degree had a significantly greater average number of publications per fellow (5.8 ± 10.0 vs 2.1 ± 4.2) and average number of first author publications per fellow (1.8 ± 2.8 vs 0.2 ± 0.5) (P < .05) (Table 2).

Discussion

Our investigation found no difference in academic productivity from 2019 to 2023 among matched adult reconstruction fellowship applicants. Additionally, fellow sex had no impact on the average number of publications per applicant. However, fellows with an MD degree had a significantly greater number of publications compared to those with a DO degree. Finally, fellows completing their residency training in the northeast region had a significantly greater number of publications and first author publications when compared to other regions of the United States.

There is an increasing interest in characterizing the academic productivity of orthopaedic surgery trainees [26,27]. In 2017, DeFroda et al. found that sports medicine fellows had an average of 5.4 publications during the 2016–2017 academic year [28]. This was comparable to foot and ankle fellows examined between 2017 and 2023, where applicants were found to have 5.3 publications per fellow [23]. The present study found that matched adult reconstruction applicants had similar levels of research productivity, with an average of 5.5 publications per fellow. This is notable, considering the increasing competitiveness of matching into an adult reconstruction fellowship [9,17]. However, given that we did not find a difference in research output over the study period, it is possible that the increased competitiveness is being driven by something other than a change in research productivity. It is worth mentioning that almost one-third of applicants did not have any publications, which would also support the conclusion that research may not be one of the most important factors in the adult reconstruction match.

These publication trends were lower than reported values for residents applying for spine fellowships, who produced an average of 7.9 publications per fellow from 2018 to 2023 [25]. A 2021 survey of spine fellowship directors found that research was the fourth most important factor when evaluating applicants [11]. While our study found that matched adult reconstruction fellows produced an average of 5.5 total publications, this aligns with the lessened emphasis placed on research when evaluating applicants for adult reconstruction fellowships. In their 2020 survey of adult reconstruction fellowship directors, Cotter et al. reported that research productivity was considered the sixth most important factor in fellow selection [16]. These data further support that among adult reconstruction fellowship programs, factors such as letters of recommendation, geographical ties, and residency program reputation may have greater importance over research experiences [16].

Our study also found that research productivity was similar for men and women who successfully matched into an adult reconstruction fellowship. However, females have a lower adult reconstruction fellowship match rate than their male counterparts, [29] which is corroborated by the number of male vs female adult reconstruction fellows in our study. Despite this, a bibliometric analysis examining research trends among spine fellowship applicants found that males had a significantly greater number of publications than females [25]. The results of our study may be attributed to an increasing number of female members in American Association of Hip and Knee Surgeons, promoting more research interest and productivity in the field of adult reconstruction [29].

Matched adult reconstruction applicants who completed their residency training at programs in the northeast region of the United States were found to have the highest average number of publications when compared to other regions of the country. Although this may be attributed to the greater number of top National Institutes of Health funded medical schools located in the northeast United States, [30] factors such as faculty research productivity and the presence of research fellows may also contribute to these geographic differences. Additionally, academic institutions are not evenly distributed throughout the United States. This finding is consistent to what has been reported in sports medicine, foot and ankle, and spine subspecialty literature [3,4,7].

Although research productivity is not considered among the most important criteria when evaluating fellowship applicants, the aspiring adult reconstruction fellow should not neglect research in favor of other aspects of their application. Acevedo et al. found that orthopaedic surgeons publish more after the completion of their training rather than during residency and fellowship [31]. With prior studies demonstrating a strong correlation between prior and current productivity, conducting research is imperative for a successful career in academics, regardless of subspecialty choice [[31], [32], [33]]. Future studies are warranted to examine whether students and residents are receiving adequate training in research methodology and manuscript production throughout their academic training [27].

The results of our study should be interpreted in the context of its limitations. Given that data collection was performed using publicly available websites, there are no controls to ensure applicant information is updated or complete. This was evident by the limited number of fellowship programs that listed their adult reconstruction fellows online. Second, while this analysis examined research productivity trends among residents who applied for an adult reconstruction fellowship, our study was only able to evaluate candidates who have successfully matched. There is no publicly available record of applicants who were unsuccessful in the match. Though we attempted to obtain additional data on unmatched applicants by contacting the San Francisco Match, the organization that governs the orthopaedic surgery fellowship match, no response was received. Therefore, we are unable to comment on the research productivity of unmatched applicants or provide a comparison. Additionally, while bibliometric data were collected through December 31 of the year prior to the start of an applicant’s fellowship, this cutoff point may not accurately represent a resident’s complete academic profile. Furthermore, Scopus is a database that only includes indexed publications, which may underestimate the true research productivity of a resident applicant as it does not contain manuscripts in process, rejected manuscripts, and noncitable publications. Only Scopus was used to collect data about publications because if multiple databases were used, manuscripts that appear on more than one database would have been counted more than once, leading to falsely inflated publication counts. This methodology has also previously been used in similar orthopaedic surgery bibliometric studies that analyzed the research output of sports medicine, spine, and foot and ankle fellowship applicants [23,25,28]. Further research is needed to integrate multiple databases and more comprehensively capture the research productivity of adult reconstruction fellowship applicants. Finally, our study was unable to control for time allocated for research. Discrepancies in dedicated research blocks in residency program curricula or residents enrolled in T32 Training Programs may impact research output. Despite these limitations, to our knowledge, this is the first study to characterize the academic profiles and identify publication trends among recent adult reconstruction fellows in the United States.

Conclusions

There was no change in research output among adult reconstruction fellows graduating from 2019 to 2023. Research productivity was significantly higher among fellows with an MD degree and those who completed their residency training at programs in the northeast region. Over half of all adult reconstruction fellows had at least one publication and first authorship article. Characterizing these research trends can help orthopaedic surgery residents evaluate their competitiveness when applying for adult reconstruction fellowships.
