Primary pure small cell neuroendocrine carcinoma of the endometrium: a case report
Authors: Si Gao, Xiaodan Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSmall cell carcinoma is a highly aggressive neuroendocrine malignancy that most commonly arises in the lungs, gastrointestinal tract, or pancreas. Primary small cell neuroendocrine carcinoma of the endometrium is exceedingly rare, and its clinical characteristics, diagnostic criteria, and optimal treatment strategies remain poorly defined.Case presentationWe report the case of a 74-year-old postmenopausal Chinese woman (gravida 4, para 4) who presented with a 20-day history of irregular vaginal bleeding. Transvaginal pelvic ultrasound performed at an outside institution 1 week prior to admission revealed an intrauterine mass with internal vascularity, suggestive of an endometrial neoplasm. Pelvic magnetic resonance imaging demonstrated an intrauterine mass invading the deep myometrium, without definite evidence of extrauterine metastasis. Serum tumor markers were within or near normal ranges. Diagnostic curettage revealed a malignant neoplasm, and immunohistochemistry confirmed a diagnosis of small cell neuroendocrine carcinoma of the endometrium. The patient subsequently underwent laparoscopic extrafascial total hysterectomy with bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy and pelvic and para-aortic lymphadenectomy. Histopathological examination confirmed a 3.0 cm × 2.0 cm × 2.5 cm small cell neuroendocrine carcinoma of the endometrium with invasion of more than half of the myometrium and diffuse lymphovascular space invasion but without lymph node metastasis. According to the 2009 International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics (FIGO) staging system, the tumor was classified as stage IB. Given the aggressive histological features, the patient received six cycles of adjuvant chemotherapy with etoposide and cisplatin. To date, corresponding to 18 months after completion of chemotherapy, no evidence of disease recurrence or metastasis has been observed.ConclusionPrimary pure small cell neuroendocrine carcinoma of the endometrium is an exceptionally rare and highly malignant neoplasm associated with an unfavorable prognosis. Accurate diagnosis relies on histopathological and immunohistochemical evaluation. In this single-case observation, comprehensive surgical staging followed by platinum-based chemotherapy was administered. However, owing to the limited follow-up duration and the absence of adjuvant radiotherapy, definitive conclusions regarding treatment efficacy cannot be drawn. Larger case series and longer-term follow-up are required to establish optimal management strategies for this rare tumor.

Introduction

Neuroendocrine neoplasms (NENs) are classified into low- and high-grade categories. Low-grade neoplasms include typical and atypical carcinoid tumors, whereas high-grade neoplasms comprise small- and large-cell neuroendocrine carcinoma. Small cell neuroendocrine carcinoma most frequently originates in the lungs, gastrointestinal tract, and pancreas, but it may also arise in the female reproductive system, including the cervix, ovaries, and endometrium [1, 2]. Primary small cell neuroendocrine carcinoma of the endometrium (SCNEC) is extremely rare, accounting for only about 0.8% of all malignant endometrial tumors [3]. In this report, we describe a case of primary pure SCNEC treated at our institution and review the relevant literature to summarize the clinical features, diagnostic approach, and treatment considerations associated with this rare malignancy. This case underscores the diagnostic complexity and aggressive nature of SCNEC and contributes additional evidence to the limited body of literature on primary pure SCNEC.

Case presentation

A 74-year-old Chinese woman (gravida 4 para 4 [G4P4]) who had been postmenopausal for 19 years presented to our department on 9 January 2024 (T = 0), with a 20 day history of irregular vaginal bleeding. The patient was a farmer and had been living independently prior to admission. She reported no personal or family history of malignancy and had no significant comorbidities, including hypertension, type 2 diabetes mellitus, or cardiovascular disease. No psychosocial factors, financial constraints, language or cultural barriers, or limitations in access to healthcare were identified during the diagnostic process. She had no history of prior gynecologic disease, procedures, or interventions relevant to the current presentation.

On admission, the patient was alert, and her vital signs were stable and within normal limits. General physical examination revealed no pallor, lymphadenopathy, or peripheral edema. Abdominal examination was unremarkable, with no tenderness or palpable masses. Gynecological examination showed normal external genitalia. Speculum examination revealed normal vaginal walls and cervix without visible lesions, although minimal dark-brown vaginal bleeding was noted. Human papillomavirus polymerase chain reaction testing was negative. Bimanual pelvic examination demonstrated a slightly enlarged uterus with a smooth surface, firm consistency, and good mobility. No adnexal masses or tenderness were palpable. No obvious abnormalities were detected on combined speculum and bimanual examination, and the examining glove showed no blood staining.

Transvaginal pelvic ultrasound performed at an outside institution 1 week prior to admission (T = −1 week) demonstrated an anteverted uterus measuring approximately 4.0 cm × 3.6 cm. Moreover, a heterogeneous, moderately hypoechoic lesion measuring approximately 3.6 cm × 2.8 cm × 3.4 cm was identified within the uterine cavity, with punctate internal blood flow signals observed on color Doppler imaging, raising suspicion for endometrial lesion. On initial presentation (T = 0), pelvic magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was subsequently performed (Fig. 1A–D), which demonstrated an enlarged uterus with an expanded uterine cavity containing a mass-like lesion that appeared slightly hyperintense on both T1-weighted and T2-weighted images. Diffusion-weighted imaging showed high signal intensity with corresponding low apparent diffusion coefficient values, indicating restricted diffusion. The lesion demonstrated deep myometrial invasion, with suspected involvement of the serosal surface and indistinct borders relative to the left adnexa. Small lymph nodes were noted adjacent to the bilateral iliac vessels, with the largest measuring approximately 0.4 cm in short-axis diameter. These imaging findings suggested an intrauterine mass, with possible involvement of the left adnexa and a high likelihood of malignancy, although they were not specific, and definitive diagnosis and staging required histopathological confirmation. Computed tomography of the chest, abdomen, and head revealed no evidence of distant metastasis. The patient’s serum calcium levels were within normal limits. Serum tumor markers were as follows: carbohydrate antigen 125 (CA125), 10.30 U/mL; carbohydrate antigen 19–9 (CA199), 45.44 U/mL; human epididymal protein 4 (HE4), 93.13 pmol/L; premenopausal risk index, 25.67; and postmenopausal risk index, 15.88.Fig. 1Magnetic resonance imaging showing an intrauterine space-occupying lesion with deep myometrial invasion, suspected serosal involvement, and an indistinct boundary relative to the left adnexa: A sagittal view; B coronal view; C, D axial view.

Diagnostic curettage was performed, and histopathology revealed a malignant neoplasm. Immunohistochemical analysis supported a diagnosis of SCNEC. Given the known aggressive biological behavior of SCNEC and its propensity for early lymphatic and hematogenous dissemination, even at an apparently early clinical stage, comprehensive surgical staging was considered necessary. Preoperative imaging suggested possible left adnexal involvement, and small pelvic lymph nodes were noted, raising concern for occult metastasis. As the uterine volume was not markedly enlarged and removal via the vaginal route was feasible, a laparoscopic approach was selected. On 13 January 2024 (T = 4 days), the patient underwent laparoscopic extrafascial total hysterectomy with bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy and pelvic and para-aortic lymphadenectomy. Postoperative histopathology confirmed a malignant tumor consistent with SCNEC, measuring approximately 3.0 cm × 2.0 cm × 2.5 cm. The tumor invaded more than half of the myometrium and demonstrated diffuse lymphovascular space invasion (LVSI), involving more than five vessels, but did not involve the endocervical canal, serosal surface, or parametrial tissues. The cervix showed only chronic inflammatory changes. Both adnexa were free of tumor involvement. No metastatic disease was identified in the lymph nodes examined, including para-aortic (0/1), left pelvic (0/10), or right pelvic (0/11) nodes. The Ki-67 proliferation index was high in both the diagnostic curettage specimen (approximately 80%) and the postoperative surgical specimen (approximately 70%), with this minor variation likely attributable to intratumoral heterogeneity and sampling differences. The cervix was entirely submitted for histological examination and showed no evidence of tumor involvement, supporting an endometrial rather than a cervical primary. According to the 2009 International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics (FIGO) staging system for endometrial carcinoma, the tumor was classified as stage IB on the basis of invasion of more than half of the myometrium in the absence of cervical stromal, serosal, adnexal, or lymph node involvement. Notably, although preoperative MRI suggested possible serosal involvement, postoperative pathological examination definitively excluded serosal and adnexal invasion, confirming that the imaging findings represented suspected changes only. Despite the early anatomical stage, the presence of LVSI and high-grade SCNEC histology indicated a biologically aggressive disease. Given the high proliferative index and presence of LVSI, adjuvant systemic chemotherapy was recommended. In the absence of standardized treatment guidelines for SCNEC, therapeutic decisions were extrapolated from management strategies for small cell lung carcinoma and other gynecologic neuroendocrine malignancies. Etoposide combined with cisplatin, which has demonstrated activity in prior case series, was selected as the adjuvant regimen.

At T = 1 month, the patient initiated adjuvant chemotherapy with etoposide and cisplatin and completed six cycles by T = 6 months (June 2024). Following completion of chemotherapy, she was monitored at 3-month intervals. Follow-up evaluations included physical and gynecological examinations, serum tumor marker assessment, and imaging studies, such as transvaginal ultrasound, MRI, and computed tomography of the chest, full abdomen, and head, as clinically indicated. As of December 2025, corresponding to 18 months after completion of chemotherapy, there has been no evidence of disease recurrence or metastasis, and the patient remains under close surveillance. Discussion

The histogenetic origin of primary SCNEC remains a subject of debate. Some investigators propose that SCNEC arises from preexisting neuroendocrine cells within the endometrium or through neuroendocrine differentiation and malignant transformation of endometrial adenocarcinoma cells [1, 2]. Others suggest an origin from Müllerian epithelial cells or multipotent endometrial stem cells capable of divergent differentiation [4–6]. SCNEC most commonly occurs in women aged over 50 years [7, 8] and has been reported more frequently in multiparous patients [9]. The clinical characteristics of the present patient, including advanced age and multiparity, are consistent with previously reported epidemiological patterns.

The most common presenting symptoms of primary SCNEC are irregular vaginal bleeding, lower abdominal pain, and pelvic mass [9, 10], although paraneoplastic syndromes have also been reported in a minority of cases [11, 12]. Chuang et al. [13] described a patient who initially presented with persistent pelvic inflammatory symptoms, emphasizing that refractory pelvic inflammation in postmenopausal women should prompt consideration of rare malignancies such as SCNEC. Preoperative imaging modalities, including pelvic CT and contrast-enhanced MRI, can provide valuable information regarding tumor size, depth of myometrial invasion, and the presence of lymph node or distant metastases [14, 15]. Chest CT may assist in excluding an extrauterine primary site. Serum tumor markers, including CA125, CA199, and NSE, may be elevated and can offer supportive diagnostic information [13]. Histologically, SCNEC closely resembles small cell neuroendocrine carcinoma arising at other anatomical sites and readily invades blood vessels, forming tumor emboli [16–18]. Neuroendocrine granules may be observed under microscopy [19]. The tumor may present as pure small cell neuroendocrine carcinoma or coexist with adenocarcinoma, adenosquamous carcinoma, or mesenchymal components [20, 21], with pure small cell neuroendocrine carcinoma generally associated with a poorer prognosis than mixed types [22]. Notably, most patients with SCNEC present with advanced-stage disease and/or lymph node involvement at diagnosis and generally have a poor prognosis. In contrast, the present case involved a node-negative, early stage tumor, with no evidence of disease recurrence during a follow-up period of 18 months after completion of chemotherapy. This outcome differs from the poor prognosis typically reported in literature, suggesting potential heterogeneity in clinical outcomes and underscoring the importance of individualized management strategies. Immunohistochemical staining typically demonstrates positivity for neuroendocrine markers, such as NSE, CD56, synaptophysin (Syn), chromogranin A (CgA), and insulinoma-associated protein 1 (INSM1) [23, 24]. Tumor cells in the present case also demonstrated thyroid transcription factor-1 (TTF-1) positivity (Fig. 3F). TTF-1 expression is not site-specific and is commonly used clinically as a marker for pulmonary small cell neuroendocrine carcinoma or thyroid primary tumors. However, after excluding primary lesions in the lung or thyroid by imaging, its expression, in combination with other immunohistochemical markers, further supported neuroendocrine differentiation of the tumor rather than metastatic disease. In our case, ultrasound effectively served as a screening modality, while MRI raised suspicion for adnexal involvement that was ultimately not confirmed pathologically, highlighting the importance of surgical staging. Although serum NSE was only mildly elevated preoperatively, postoperative pathology revealed diffuse LVSI and strong expression of neuroendocrine markers, reinforcing the importance of histopathological and immunohistochemical evaluation for definitive diagnosis.

Given the extreme rarity of SCNEC, no standardized treatment strategy has been established. Current therapeutic approaches are largely extrapolated from protocols used for small cell lung carcinoma and other gynecologic neuroendocrine malignancies. Surgical resection is generally considered the foundation of treatment, with extrafascial total hysterectomy and bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy commonly performed in early stage disease, while cytoreductive surgery may be indicated in advanced-stage cases [2, 17, 25]. Platinum-based chemotherapy regimens, particularly etoposide combined with cisplatin, are most frequently employed, although reported outcomes vary widely [26–29]. Published case reports illustrate this heterogeneity. Katahira et al. [17] described an early stage patient treated with surgery followed by high-dose medroxyprogesterone acetate who remained disease-free for 9 years. In contrast, Verschraegen et al. [30] reported a refractory case treated with leuprolide acetate, tamoxifen, and octreotide, with a survival of only 1 year. Su et al. [31] described a patient with advanced-stage mixed-type SCNEC and high tumor mutational burden who experienced rapid disease progression after palliative surgery but subsequently achieved complete remission and prolonged progression-free survival following chemotherapy combined with immunotherapy. For patients with early stage SCNEC, particularly those with FIGO stage IB disease, radical surgery remains the cornerstone of treatment. Published reports indicate that some early stage patients achieved favorable outcomes with radical surgery combined with platinum-based chemotherapy, whereas others have undergone observation alone or received adjuvant chemoradiotherapy [2, 32]. These findings suggest that postoperative individualized radiotherapy may improve survival; however, their roles remain controversial. In the present case, comprehensive surgical staging followed by six cycles of etoposide and cisplatin was feasible and well tolerated, with no evidence of recurrence observed to date. Although adjuvant radiotherapy was not administered, this treatment strategy was largely consistent with previously reported approaches for node-negative, early stage disease and was considered reasonable given the complete surgical staging and absence of residual tumor. For this early stage, completely resected, node-negative case, the decision to omit radiotherapy was based on reports in the existing literature indicating that the benefit of radiotherapy remains uncertain. Nevertheless, the relatively short follow-up period and the absence of adjuvant radiotherapy preclude definitive conclusions regarding long-term treatment efficacy.

Conclusion

Primary SCNEC is a rare and highly aggressive malignancy, with most reported cases relapsing within 2 years and an overall poor survival outcome. Prognosis is influenced by tumor stage, depth of myometrial invasion, lymph node involvement, and histological type, with pure SCC associated with worse outcomes [33]. Owing to the limited number of reported cases, the optimal management of SCNEC remains unclear, and current treatment strategies are primarily based on retrospective case series and extrapolation from other neuroendocrine carcinomas. For early stage cases such as ours, radical surgery followed by platinum-based chemotherapy may represent a viable approach, although the role of adjuvant radiotherapy remains undefined [3]. In contrast, the therapeutic roles of endocrine therapy and octreotide remain under investigation. Emerging evidence suggests that patients with a high tumor mutational burden may benefit from immunotherapy [31, 34, 35]. In addition, mismatch repair protein expression analysis, as reported by Cady et al. in their clinicopathologic study of 25 endometrial neuroendocrine carcinoma cases, may help identify patients who could benefit from immunotherapy and provide additional diagnostic and therapeutic guidance [36]. The findings from this case should be interpreted with caution. Longer follow-up periods and larger case series are required to further clarify the role of adjuvant therapies, including radiotherapy, endocrine therapy, and immunotherapy, in the management of SCNEC. More comprehensive genomic profiling is also recommended to guide subsequent treatment decisions.
