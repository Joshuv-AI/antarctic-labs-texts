ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundThe shortage of pathologists presents a significant bottleneck in delivering timely and accurate diagnoses. To address this challenge, we developed the POLyp Artificial Intelligence-based RISk classifier (POLARIS), a prescreening tool designed to assist pathologists in handling the increasing volume of colorectal biopsies.MethodsUsing 15,079 whole-slide images (WSIs) from 2993 patients in the UK bowel cancer screening program between 2014 and 2018 and the histological diagnoses as ground truth, POLARIS was developed applying the open-source foundation model H-optimus-0, multiple instance learning, and a training scheme designed to provide robust models. Each WSI was categorised into one of five classes based on the diagnosis, reflecting increasing risk of malignancy, and regrouped into two broader categories for clinical interpretation. One category includes only samples not requiring pathological review, specifically those classified as normal tissue or tubular adenomas with low-grade dysplasia (LGD). The second category includes all other types of polyps, which are polyps recommended for review by a pathologist. A prespecified validation protocol defined how a specific model should be evaluated on a geographically external dataset comprising 10,842 WSIs from Cheltenham General Hospital between 2008 and 2019 and acquired using two scanners, Leica Aperio AT2 and Hamamatsu NanoZoomer XR. After validation, three experienced pathologists independently assessed the cases in which model prediction differed most from clinical diagnosis and, blinded to model output, reached a consensus.FindingsIn the external validation, POLARIS correctly identified 98.94% (95% CI 96.92–99.78%) of polyps with high-grade dysplasia (HGD) and adenocarcinoma as POLARIS-positive, and correctly classified 83.04% (81.66–84.36%) of normal and tubular adenomas with LGD as POLARIS-negative. The overall balanced accuracy was 86.65% for this prespecified primary analysis. In the tuning set, balanced accuracy was 87.64%, the sensitivity for polyps with HGD and adenocarcinoma was 100% (95% CI 97.14–100), and the specificity for normal and tubular adenomas with LGD was 81.89% (79.94–83.70). POLARIS achieved an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) in external validation of 0.9449 (95% CI 0.9384–0.9507) for distinguishing normal and tubular adenomas with LGD from polyps recommended for review by a pathologist and 0.9788 (0.9718–0.9844) for polyps with HGD and adenocarcinoma cases specifically. The model predicted the same class for both scanners in 97.93% (97.49–98.32%) of the cases. In the review of selected cases, the pathologists agreed with the model and not the clinical diagnosis in 92.5% of the cases. Heatmaps highlighting regions where the model indicates high-risk features closely correlated with areas annotated by expert pathologists as high-risk.InterpretationPOLARIS has the potential to enhance diagnostic workflows in colorectal pathology by reliably identifying high-risk lesions and highlighting high-risk regions while substantially reducing the number of slides requiring pathologist review.FundingThe Norwegian Cancer Society.

Research in context

Evidence before this study

We searched PubMed without language or date restrictions using the search terms “colorectal”/“large-bowel”, “deep learning”/“artificial intelligence”, “whole slide image” and “classification”/“grading”/“screening”, yielding 203 results. We found 25 original research studies that applied deep learning to automatically classify colorectal polyps. All were validated on datasets smaller than 1600 WSIs and only 12 of them reported evaluation on an independent external cohort. To our knowledge, no one prespecified the experimental protocol or the primary analysis. Several of the studies performed multi-class classification, but only 2 directly evaluated the impact of their methods on reducing pathologist workload. 5 studies included tubular adenoma or low-grade dysplasia (LGD) as one of several classes, but none focused on identifying tubular adenomas with LGD as a main task.

Added value of this study

We have developed POLARIS, an artificial intelligence (AI)-based pre-screening tool for classification of colorectal biopsies, trained using a weakly supervised learning approach relying solely on slide-level labels. Validated on one of the largest colorectal polyp datasets to date using a prespecified primary analysis, our model demonstrates robust performance and high accuracy. It offers a substantially greater potential reduction in pathologist workload compared to existing colorectal pre-screening tools, without compromising diagnostic safety, and can be used to assist pathologists also by highlighting high-risk regions.

Implications of all the available evidence

The implementation of colorectal screening programs has significantly increased the workload for pathologists, creating an urgent need for efficient support tools. POLARIS identifies cases recommended for pathological review and supports decision-making by highlighting regions of polyps warranting closer examination by a pathologist. This allows specialists to focus on diagnostically challenging cases, potentially reducing patient waiting times and enabling timely intervention for cases with advanced neoplastic features. With effective integration into clinical workflows, POLARIS could improve diagnostic quality by reducing pathologists’ workload and interobserver variability as well as ensuring early detection of high-risk lesions.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer is the third most frequently diagnosed cancer worldwide and the second leading cause of cancer-related death.1 Organised screening programs have been implemented in several countries and have been shown to significantly reduce both the incidence and mortality of the disease.2,3 Screening programs bring an immediate need for more endoscopists and pathologists, in particular specialised gastrointestinal pathologists.4 At the same time, the global pathology workforce is under growing pressure. Many countries report a declining number of practicing pathologists despite a consistently growing volume of cases due to ageing populations, more specimens per patient, and expanding screening programs.5,6 This pressure emphasises the need for automated and assistive decision support tools to sustain diagnostic capacity and accuracy. Furthermore, the intra- and inter-observer variability in polyp histopathology diagnostics is significant, underpinning the need for accurate and objective methods.7

Several recent studies have explored artificial intelligence (AI)-based systems for colorectal histopathology and shown promising results for workload reduction and triage in pathology, either by pre-screening slides to rule out normals, prioritizing suspicious cases, or providing visual decision support tools such as heatmaps or gland segmentation to improve accuracy and efficiency.8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14 Despite encouraging findings, most of the existing studies were validated on relatively small datasets, few included evaluation on independent external cohorts, and only two studies specifically examined the impact of their methods on pathologist workload.

Using the largest polyp datasets to date, we here present the development and validation of the POLyp Artificial Intelligence-based RISk classifier (POLARIS), designed to distinguish colorectal biopsies that are recommended for expert pathological review from those that can be safely excluded from specialist evaluation. In particular, POLARIS aimed to identify normal samples and tubular adenomas with low-grade dysplasia (LGD) with high confidence, while ensuring that all cases with high-grade dysplasia (HGD) or adenocarcinomas were flagged for specialist evaluation. By predicting multiple classes, POLARIS also allows highlighting of high-risk regions to assist pathological review.

Methods

Materials

This retrospective study included four cohorts, with sample size determined by data availability. Two cohorts were used for model training and tuning. The University College London (UCL) cohort included 2993 patients from a consecutive series in the UK bowel cancer screening program between 2014 and 2018. The tissue sections were stained with haematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and digitised using Aperio AT2 (Leica Biosystems, Germany) and NanoZoomer XR (Hamamatsu, Japan) scanners. The UK Expert Board cohort comprised 746 H&E stained Aperio AT2 whole-slide images (WSIs) from 586 patients submitted to the national experts board for a second opinion as they were considered particularly difficult to diagnose within the bowel cancer screening program. After exclusions, 8374 NanoZoomer XR WSIs and 6705 Aperio AT2 WSIs from the two cohorts were included in the model development (Fig. 1A; Appendix pp 5–6). 70% of the development set was used for training, while the remaining 30% was used for tuning. We ensured that no patients were present in both the training set and the tuning set. A cohort with 495 polyp sections from 308 patients diagnosed at University Medical Centre Utrecht, the Netherlands, was used as an external test set. The sections were selected to reflect a high proportion of cases with HGD, stained with H&E and digitised with the Aperio AT2 and NanoZoomer XR scanners. The only exclusions were 3 NanoZoomer XR slides due to poor image focus (Fig. 1A; Appendix p 7).

The external validation cohort consisted of 2514 patients diagnosed at Cheltenham General Hospital between 2008 and 2019, also part of the UK screening program. The tissue sections were stained with H&E and digitised using the Aperio AT2 and NanoZoomer XR scanners. After exclusions, a total of 5276 NanoZoomer XR WSIs and 5566 Aperio AT2 WSIs remained for validation (Fig. 1).

All polyp sections have been classified according to histological type when they were diagnosed at the respective pathology departments or by the national experts board, using the clinical information routinely available in clinical practice. Patient-level race and ethnicity data were not collected, as this information was unavailable to the investigators because of prior anonymisation. The cohorts used for development and validation are representative of the intended clinical population, whereas the cohort from Utrecht was chosen to assess performance particularly for HGD cases and used only as an external test set.

This study was conducted in accordance with Norwegian research regulations. The Regional Committees for Medical and Health Research Ethics in Norway (REK) evaluated the protocol and determined that the project falls outside the scope of the Health Research Act (ref. no. 747827) and could be conducted without formal REK approval. Institutional approval was obtained from the local data protection authority (ref. no. 25/07908). Because all samples were fully anonymized prior to analysis and the study did not involve identifiable human participants, informed consent was not required.

Class labelling

Histopathological diagnosis was used as the reference standard, and polyp slides were categorised into five disjoint classes based on their histological features and associated risk of progression to colorectal cancer (Fig. 1A). Based on discussions with two expert gastrointestinal pathologists (MML, MN) and one experienced general pathologist (MP), the class structure, including the number of classes and the mapping of diagnostic categories to classes, was defined to represent increasing histological complexity and clinical significance, ranging from normal tissue (class 0) to invasive carcinoma (class 4).

For clinical use, these five classes were further grouped into two categories based on the need for pathological review. Polyps in class 0 or 1 were considered not to require expert examination. This group includes normal tissue samples and tubular adenomas with LGD, both of which are commonly encountered, morphologically consistent, and associated with long surveillance intervals when the tubular adenoma is less than 10 mm.15, 16, 17 The other main category consisted of polyps in class 2, 3 or 4, representing increasingly abnormal, diagnostically challenging, or clinically significant findings that are recommended for review by a pathologist. Class 2 includes tubulovillous and villous adenoma with LGD in addition to non-neoplastic polyps that still warrant expert evaluation, as some benign diagnoses (e.g., inflammatory polyps or epithelial misplacement) can mimic more serious pathology or indicate underlying inflammatory bowel disease.17,18 Class 3 comprises serrated polyps, including hyperplastic polyps, sessile serrated lesions, and traditional serrated adenomas. Although hyperplastic polyps are considered benign, they can be difficult to distinguish from sessile serrated lesions and may indicate serrated polyposis syndrome, a hereditary condition associated with increased cancer risk.19,20 Class 4 includes lesions with advanced neoplastic features, such as adenocarcinoma and high-grade dysplasia. Accurate identification in this class is critical, as these findings directly influence treatment decisions and prognosis.17

Preprocessing

All WSIs were processed using DeepFocus, and those showing poor focus were excluded from all datasets (Appendix p 24).21 For slides in the training dataset scanned with both NanoZoomer XR and Aperio AT2, image registration was performed to align the corresponding WSIs. This registration process was carried out using elastix and enabled extraction of tiles from both scanners that represented the same physical area.22 Using these matched tiles, the model was trained using a method designed to increase the robustness against non-biological information represented by scanner variations (Appendix pp 28–29).

The WSIs were tiled at a 20× magnification (0.5 μm/pixel) using 224 × 224 pixels, corresponding to the training of H-optimus-0.23 Tiles with more than 50% background were discarded (Appendix p 22). The training dataset was augmented by including multiple synthetically distorted variants of each tile, generated with the same colour distortions and randomly sampled noise, before extraction of a feature vector with the pretrained foundation model H-optimus-0 (Appendix p 25).

Training

A five-class classification model was trained to categorise entire WSIs by adopting a weakly supervised training approach using attention-based multiple instance learning.24 The model was trained using the feature vectors extracted with H-optimus-0 to represent image tiles (Appendix p 25). Each WSI was processed independently by the model and assigned an individual prediction. H-optimus-0 features were transformed by a three-layer feedforward network, aggregated into a slide-level representation using attention-weighted pooling, and classified with a final linear layer. To increase robustness against non-biological information, two loss terms were added to the normal classification loss to penalise differences between scanners; one term minimised the difference between the representations of the matched tiles, the other minimised the difference between predicted score on corresponding WSIs. The classification network was trained for 20 000 optimisation steps with a batch size of 16 and a bag size of 4096. After every 500 steps, the models were compared using the tuning set, and the one maximising the specificity within strict criteria for sensitivity after dichotomisation was selected.

An ensemble model was obtained by averaging the class scores across the selected model from 10 independent training runs. A more detailed description is provided in the protocol (Appendix pp 28–29).

Classification

Two approaches were applied to convert the 5 continuous output values from the ensemble model into a classification. Clinical information, including the ground truth, was not available to the model.

The main approach maximised the specificity within strict criteria for sensitivity on the tuning set, which was the same approach as used to select a model from each training run. This allowed errors with more severe clinical implications to be weighted more than other errors. The resulting classifier was termed POLARIS. It is a binary classifier that outputs POLARIS-positive or POLARIS-negative, reflecting the two main categories of the ground truth labels. The alternative approach to obtain a classification maximised the multiclass balanced accuracy on the tuning set, using the definition of multiclass balanced accuracy implemented in scikit-learn v1.3.25 The direct output of this approach was one of the 5 classes, and this output was further converted into a classification reflecting the two main categories by applying the same definitions as for the ground truth labels; recommended for review by a pathologist if class 2, 3 or 4, and does not require expert examination if class 0 or 1.

Heatmaps

To visualise the predictions of POLARIS, we generated a heatmap for each WSI by blending the tile-level class prediction scores from POLARIS into a single RGBA overlay. For each tile, the prediction score for class 1 to 4 were mapped to the distinct base colours green, yellow, orange, and red, respectively, and combined using risk-based weights to make higher classes contribute more to the RGB value of the tile (Appendix pp 3–4). Tiles predicted as normal (class 0) were set to be fully transparent, and the overlay's alpha channel was scaled by the total risk weight to further emphasise high-risk regions. This heatmap was then overlaid on the original WSI to facilitate comparison between POLARIS prediction and histopathological features. In the example shown in Fig. 2G, the HGD region annotated by a pathologist closely matches the region predicted to be class 4 (HGD or adenocarcinoma) by POLARIS and therefore highlighted as such in the POLARIS-generated heatmap.

Statistical analysis

Primary and secondary analyses were predefined before evaluating the validation cohort and described in the protocol in accordance with the Protocol Items for External Cohort Evaluation of a deep learning System (PIECES) recommendations.26 All analyses were performed at the WSI level, with no aggregation across multiple slides from the same patient. The primary analysis evaluated POLARIS using balanced accuracy. Sensitivity and specificity were calculated as secondary analyses to evaluate POLARIS’ ability to differentiate between polyps not requiring review by a pathologist and other polyps. These metrics were further computed for each of the five classification categories. Confidence intervals for sensitivity and specificity were estimated using the Clopper-Pearson method. All analyses were conducted separately on both Aperio AT2 and NanoZoomer XR WSIs.

In addition, exploratory analyses included generating receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves to examine POLARIS's performance across a range of classification thresholds. The area under the ROC curve (AUROC) was calculated in the case where class 2, 3 and 4 are considered positives and in the case where only class 4 is considered positive.

All statistical analyses were performed in Python 3.8.

Results

External test dataset

Similar sensitivity for class 4 was observed in the external test dataset evaluated prior to the validation (98.14%, 95% CI 95.99–99.31). This dataset was designed to assert performance on polyps with HGD, resulting in a higher proportion of class 4 cases (65.0%) than in the validation dataset (5.1%). A relatively small proportion of other cases was also included, with observed sensitivities of 97.30% (95% CI 85.84–99.93) for class 3, 96.30% (81.03–99.91) for class 2, and 77.06% (68.03–84.57) for class 1 in the external test dataset (Appendix p 11).

Tuning dataset

In the tuning set, comprising 30% of the 15,079 WSIs in the development dataset, the model achieved similar performance as in the validation dataset. In particular, the balanced accuracy was 87.64%, the sensitivity for polyps with HGD and adenocarcinoma was 100% (95% CI 97.14–100), and the specificity for normal and tubular adenomas with LGD was 81.89% (79.94–83.70). See Appendix p 8 for class-wise performance.

ROC analysis of the validation dataset

ROC analysis was used to evaluate the performance of POLARIS across decision thresholds. In the validation dataset, POLARIS achieved an AUROC of 0.9449 (95% CI 0.9384–0.9507) for distinguishing normal and tubular adenomas with LGD from polyps recommended for pathologist review and 0.9788 (0.9718–0.9844) for polyps with HGD and adenocarcinoma cases specifically (Fig. 3B). At the operating point achieving the maximum balanced accuracy of 87.15%, the specificity for normal and tubular adenomas with LGD was 90.89% (95% CI 89.82–91.89) and the sensitivity for polyps with HGD and adenocarcinoma was 97.87% (95% CI 95.43–99.22).

Robustness against non-biological information

For the 4897 NanoZoomer XR WSIs in the validation dataset, POLARIS showed similar performance as for the Aperio AT2 WSIs (Appendix p 10). The balanced accuracy was 86.29%, and POLARIS correctly classified 98.80% (95% CI 96.55–99.75) of polyps with HGD and adenocarcinoma as POLARIS-positive and 83.45% (82.00–84.83) of normal and tubular adenomas with LGD as POLARIS-negative. Class-wise observed sensitivities were 62.64% (51.87–72.56) for class 0, 84.17% (82.72–85.54) for class 1, 83.03% (79.93–85.83) for class 2, and 90.37% (88.61–91.94) for class 3.

To evaluate the robustness of POLARIS against non-biological information, we compared the classification results between the corresponding Aperio AT2 and NanoZoomer XR WSIs in the validation dataset. The confusion matrices showed a 97.93% (95% CI 97.49–98.32) agreement for the binary classification and a 97.71% (97.25–98.11) agreement across the five classes when comparing the class predicted to be the most likely (Fig. 3C).

All noted architectural complexity and mildly increased cytonuclear atypia within the region highlighted by POLARIS, but differed in whether these features met the criteria for HGD. Two pathologists classified the case as LGD, while the last was inclined to report HGD.

In the consensus evaluation, all eight cases originally labelled as normal in the ground truth were reclassified by the pathologists into other classes. This prompted an additional exploratory analysis to further investigate this discrepancy. All 101 Aperio AT2 WSIs in the validation dataset labelled as normal in the ground truth were re-evaluated by the experienced general pathologist. In 84 out of the 101 cases, the pathologist's classification matched the class with highest POLARIS score (Fig. 4C). If this updated pathologist classification was used as the ground truth, the resulting specificity of POLARIS for class 0 (normal) would be 68.32% (95% CI 58.31–77.22).

Alternative POLARIS classifier

POLARIS was designed to maximise specificity while maintaining strict sensitivity criteria. As a secondary analysis, an alternative classifier was defined by maximising the multiclass balanced accuracy. This alternative POLARIS classifier also showed strong performance (Appendix pp 13–15). When evaluating Aperio AT2 WSIs in the validation dataset, the sensitivity for detecting polyps with HGD and adenocarcinoma was slightly lower (98.58% (95% CI 96.41–99.61)), but more normal and tubular adenomas with LGD were correctly identified (85.67% (84.37–86.89)). Notably, the alternative POLARIS classifier demonstrates a sensitivity of 87.13% (79.00–92.96) for class 0 after the re-evaluation.

Discussion

In this study, we present POLARIS, an AI-based risk classifier for colorectal polyp slides, which demonstrates very high sensitivity for detecting HGD and adenocarcinoma. With an 83% specificity for correctly identifying normal and low-grade dysplastic polyps, POLARIS may exclude more than 50% of all colorectal polyp slides from pathological review without compromising sensitivity for high-risk lesions. This enables a substantial improvement in diagnostic efficiency by allowing pathologists to focus on the most critical samples. In parallel, POLARIS-generated heatmaps highlight regions warranting closer examination, including small high-risk areas that may not dominate the overall slide-level classification. To ensure safe clinical usage, trained laboratory personnel might review all or some of the normal cases and low-grade dysplastic polyps. Trained on more than 15,000 WSIs and externally validated on more than 10 000 cases, POLARIS offers a robust decision-support tool designed to assist pathologists with the increasing number of samples submitted for evaluation. The development and validation cohorts represent the intended clinical population of participants in organized bowel cancer screening programs, while the Utrecht cohort was included to specifically test the model's performance on HGD cases.

The ROC analysis clarifies the practical trade-offs in threshold selection, and illustrates how POLARIS can be adapted to local clinical priorities and to shifts in histology class distribution. We deployed a high-sensitivity operating point to minimise missed cases with HGD and adenocarcinoma. The ROC curve shows that if thresholds are constrained to maintain 100% sensitivity for HGD and adenocarcinoma after re-evaluation, specificity for normal and low-grade dysplastic polyps can be kept at 80%. Alternatively, selecting thresholds that maximise balanced accuracy yields an even higher specificity and only slightly lower sensitivity.

POLARIS showed lower specificity for class 0 (normal tissue) than for other classes. Exploratory expert review suggested that misclassifications in the validation ground truth may contribute to the observed misclassification. Although relatively few validation cases were labelled as “no evidence of polyp,” this label accounted for more than half of class 0 in the development set. As this category may include a broad range of histological appearances, this could partly explain the observed performance.

Expert review of model misclassifications revealed strong agreement between POLARIS predictions and pathologist consensus in cases with large discrepancy between POLARIS and the original diagnosis. This indicates that some of the original misclassifications reflect incorrect original diagnoses. After the blinded re-evaluation by the pathologists, only one case with HGD or adenocarcinoma was misidentified as POLARIS-negative. This was a highly borderline lesion with substantial inter-observer disagreement, ranging from LGD to subtle features of HGD. Although a consensus of HGD was ultimately established in the blinded re-evaluation, the heatmap showed that the model successfully localised the relevant area. In the final unblinded re-evaluation of this case, the pathologists remained in doubt about whether the case should be classified as LGD or HGD, but the majority reported it as LGD, which is consistent with the POLARIS classification. Notably, the pathologists considered the classification of such very borderline cases to be of minuscule importance compared to the high intra- and inter-pathologist variation observed in routine clinical practice, reflected by variation in HGD rates between centres in the UK bowel cancer screening program.27

Moreover, the pathologists reached a consensus of HGD in the blinded re-evaluation for one case initially diagnosed as normal, and POLARIS predicted this case to be HGD or adenocarcinoma with high probability. This finding illustrates that the model may in some cases detect subtle areas that are missed during routine diagnostic review, and underscores the potential of POLARIS as a complementary assistance tool to enhance diagnostic safety.

The pathologists most often disagreed on cases with small, localised areas of high-grade morphology. This variability highlights the challenge of consistency in polyp histopathology assessment and emphasizes the need for more objective methods.7 While they all recognised the high-grade features, they differed on whether these were sufficient for a HGD diagnosis. Because these borderline cases are especially prone to inter-observer variability and may not carry the same clinical significance as more extensive involvement, current guidelines emphasize that such isolated or minimal high-grade changes should be interpreted with caution.28

Several recent studies have proposed AI-based solutions for classifying colorectal biopsy slides. Bilal et al. developed CAIMAN, a deep learning model to identify typical, non-neoplastic, and neoplastic biopsies.8 Graham et al. introduced a similar tool, IGUANA, a graph neural network model that aims to rule out normal colorectal biopsies while providing interpretable visual feedback.9 Both models were developed using the same dataset of approximately 6500 WSIs, and evaluated on around 1500 WSIs from three independent external datasets. At a sensitivity of 99%, CAIMAN achieved specificities ranging from 26% to 56%, while IGUANA obtained an average specificity of 55% across the external datasets. Mayall et al. conducted a prospective study showing that AI-assisted triage of colorectal biopsies can significantly reduce turnaround time for high-risk cases.10 The AI classified each WSI based on significant pathology, and cases flagged as neoplasia or inflammation were pushed to the front of the reporting queue. Ho et al. proposed a two-stage deep learning pipeline that flags high-risk colorectal biopsies and outlines dysplastic or malignant glands on the WSI, enabling pathologists to focus on those areas.13 Their model was validated on 150 WSIs, showing a sensitivity of 97% and a specificity of 60%. Aswolinskiy et al. introduced NIC-A, an attention-based model that pre-screens colorectal and cervical biopsies into normal, LGD, HGD and cancer.29 On an external set of 76 WSIs, it identified every cancer case while the overall agreement with the pathologists were 48%.

Compared to these approaches, POLARIS demonstrates several key advantages. It applied one of the largest datasets to date for both training and validation, and most slides were imaged on two different scanners. The balance between safety and efficiency surpasses the performance of CAIMAN and IGUANA, which reported high sensitivity but relatively modest specificity.8,9 Although Aswolinskiy et al. achieved 100% sensitivity for cancer detection, overall agreement with the pathologists was 48%.29 Unlike studies based solely on binary classification, POLARIS was trained using a five-class approach and subsequently grouped into a binary output reflecting clinical priorities. This multiclass framework enables the application of progressively stricter thresholds for polyps with increasing neoplastic potential, enhancing both safety and diagnostic precision. While CAIMAN and IGUANA focus on excluding only normal samples from pathologist review, POLARIS extends this exclusion to include tubular adenomas with LGD as this is the most common type of polyp, shows characteristic morphology and allows extended surveillance intervals.15, 16, 17 As a result, the potential reduction in pathologist workload is substantially greater for POLARIS than for previous models, without compromising diagnostic safety.

Although POLARIS was validated on a large external dataset according to a predefined protocol, additional studies are warranted to confirm its real-world utility and safety in routine clinical practice. The workload estimates are based on retrospective data and represent an idealised scenario. Direct evaluation of time savings as well as usage and impact of POLARIS in clinical practice requires dedicated studies, such as reader studies or clinical utility assessments. These studies should also evaluate the risk of diverting complex cases to less-experienced readers and determine whether the triage system can safely allow for independent review of low-risk cases by trained personnel, or if senior review remains necessary. While the misclassification of adenocarcinoma and high-grade dysplasia was minimal in the validation set, a clinical implementation should include strict protocols for human oversight. Cases with diagnostic uncertainty or atypical morphology should be escalated to a senior pathologist, regardless of the initial POLARIS classification. In this context, the generated heatmaps may serve as a safety feature by helping less-experienced readers to identify suspicious regions that warrant senior consultation. Furthermore, validations across diverse geographical locations, preparations and scanning methods of slides are important to verify precision and robustness. Future work should also explore real-time integration with pathology systems used in routine clinical practice, including the assessment of user acceptance, impact on turnaround times, and cost-effectiveness.

Our results demonstrate the potential of POLARIS to enhance diagnostic workflows in colorectal pathology by reliably identifying high-risk lesions while substantially reducing the number of cases requiring pathologist review. Its strong performance in large, diverse datasets and on multiple scanner types underscores the robustness and accuracy of POLARIS. However, local validations and prospective studies should be performed before integrating POLARIS into national screening programs.
