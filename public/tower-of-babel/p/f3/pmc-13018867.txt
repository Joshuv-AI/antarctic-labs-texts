Regulatory BC200 RNA promotes breast carcinogenesis by repressing BRCA1 gene expression
Authors: Valerio Berardi, Ilham A. Muslimov, Dan Sun, Giuseppe Militello, Prem Premsrirut, Henri Tiedge
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryDownregulation of the BRCA1 gene expression has been implicated in hereditary and sporadic breast cancer. Expression of regulatory BC200 RNA, which is normally restricted to neurons, is dysregulated in invasive breast carcinoma cells, giving rise to high cellular RNA levels. However, the molecular mechanisms by which BC200 RNA contributes to breast cancer malignancy remain poorly understood. Here, we show that atypical BC200 RNA expression in breast epithelial cells results in malignant transformation. By repressing the translation of BRCA1 mRNA, BC200 RNA reduces BRCA1 protein expression in breast epithelial cells, leading to increased DNA damage and carcinogenesis. RNAi-mediated downregulation of BC200 RNA restores the normal, non-malignant phenotype. Tumor innervation stimulates BC200 RNA expression, indicating a role of the nervous system in tumor formation. Thus, in a hitherto unreported molecular mechanism, neuronal BC200 RNA promotes breast carcinogenesis by repressing the expression of the tumor suppressor BRCA1. These findings indicate the utility of BC200 RNA in breast cancer therapy.

Introduction

Cancer is one of the leading causes of mortality, and breast cancer is among the most common cancers occurring worldwide, accounting for about 30% of new cancer diagnoses in women in the United States.1 Female breast cancer incidence rates have been slowly increasing every year, and for women in the US, the lifetime risk of being diagnosed with breast cancer is 1 in 8.1

Despite improvements in early detection and therapeutic interventions, breast cancer has remained a leading cause of malignancy-associated death among women.1 A large majority of breast cancers occur sporadically, whereas only 5 to 10% are inheritable.2 While mutations in the breast cancer-associated gene 1 (BRCA1) account for more than half of the hereditary breast cancer cases,3 decreased BRCA1 expression levels are often found in sporadic breast cancer,4,5 including triple-negative breast cancer.6 BRCA1 is a multifunctional and ubiquitously expressed protein participating in important cellular processes such as DNA damage repair, transcription regulation, and cell cycle checkpoint control.7 Such universal functions of BRCA1 have been suggested to underlie cancer susceptibility, although other reports have described roles of BRCA1 that are specific to breast tissue.8 Alterations in BRCA1 functionality lead to DNA instability and consequently to cell-cycle arrest, apoptosis, loss of chromosomal integrity, and a high rate of mutations.9 BRCA1 over-expression results in a reduction of tumor growth10 whereas conversely, significantly diminished BRCA1 protein expression has been observed in sporadic breast cancer.11 Currently, therapies for BRCA1-associated breast cancer focus on poly-ADP ribose polymerase (PARP) inhibitors, taking advantage of already impaired DNA repair mechanisms in BRCA1-deficient cells to cause cell death by blocking PARP.12

Among the many factors associated with cancer, recently emerging evidence has implicated non-protein-coding RNAs as key players in various human malignancies13 including breast cancer.14 Noncoding RNAs are RNA subtypes that are not translated into proteins but often play important mechanistic-regulatory roles in eukaryotic cells. Noncoding RNAs associated with breast cancer include circular RNAs,13 small noncoding RNAs,15 micro RNAs16 and long noncoding RNAs.14 Some of these RNAs have been discussed as promising therapeutic targets in breast cancer.17

Brain cytoplasmic 200 (BC200) RNA is an abundant non-protein-coding RNA that operates as a translational regulator in human neurons18,19 by interacting with eukaryotic initiation factors (eIFs) 4A and 4B.20,21 Specifically, by binding to eIF4A, BC200 RNA inhibits this factor’s helicase activity, which is essential for the unwinding of complex structures in the mRNA 5′ untranslated region (5′ UTR) that could otherwise interfere with the interaction between the mRNA and the translational machinery.22 eIF4B, on the other hand, acts as a bridge between the mRNA and ribosomal 18S rRNA.21 The interaction of eIF4B with BC200 RNA is regulated by the phosphorylation of the initiation factor at serine 406, which thereby acts as a molecular switch for the repressive activity of BC200 RNA.21 Binding of BC200 RNA to eIF4A and eIF4B effectively inhibits the activity of these two initiation factors, resulting in translational repression of target mRNAs. Typically, BC200 RNA is expressed in a neuron-specific manner and is not found in other somatic cell types.23 An important exception to this neuronal restriction is exemplified by human invasive breast carcinoma cells in which BC200 RNA was detected at high levels.19,24 The consequences of high BC200 RNA expression in cancer cells have been investigated, and BC200 RNA has been implicated in cancer cell survival and proliferation,25,26 cell migration,27 and pathogenesis.28 To date, however, there has been little published evidence concerning BC200 RNA molecular mechanisms that underlie functional-causal roles in breast cancer malignant transformation and progression.

Here, we report that BRCA1 mRNA is a specific target of BC200 RNA translational control, an interaction that results in diminished BRCA1 protein expression. BC200 RNA binds eIF4A and eIF4B and inhibits their mRNA unwinding and ribosome recruitment activities, as a result suppressing the translation of target mRNAs. BRCA1 mRNA contains a long and highly complex 5′ UTR that requires eIF4A and eIF4B to promote efficient translation initiation, and it is this translational mechanism that is inhibited by the action of BC200 RNA.29,30 Thus, by significantly reducing BRCA1 protein levels in breast epithelial cells, high-level BC200 RNA expression is directly and causally associated with malignant transformation. Consequently, BC200 RNA knockdown in breast carcinoma cells curtails the manifestation of the malignant phenotype, a result to indicate the utility of BC200 RNA as a therapeutic target in the treatment of invasive breast cancer.

Results

BC200 RNA represses expression of BRCA1 in MCF-10A cells

BC200 RNA is expressed at high levels in invasive breast cancer cells.24 However, a direct link between expression of BC200 RNA and tumor formation has not been established to date. BC200 RNA is a translation regulator, and in the basal default state in neurons, it represses the translation of target mRNAs.20,21,23 The question, therefore, arises as to how an inhibitor of protein synthesis can promote carcinogenesis. To resolve this apparent conundrum, we hypothesized that BC200 RNA may negatively regulate the expression of a tumor suppressor. The foremost tumor suppressor associated with breast cancer malignancy is BRCA1.3,5 Consequently, we decided to test the hypothesis that BRCA1 is a target of BC200 RNA translational control. In support of this hypothesis, BC RNAs (including BC1 RNA and BC200 RNA) are known to repress the translation of mRNAs with structured 5′ UTRs through interactions with initiation factors eIF4A and eIF4B.20,22 As a result, eIF4A’s helicase function is prevented from unwinding higher-order structural elements in mRNA 5′ UTRs, and eIF4B is prevented from interacting with the 40S small ribosomal subunit. BRCA1 mRNA contains a long and highly complex 5′ UTR that requires eIF4A and eIF4B for efficient translation initiation, and mutations in this region have been associated with breast cancer.29,30 It is thus hypothesized that BC200 RNA inhibits the translation of BRCA1 mRNA through repression of these essential initiation factors rather than through interactions between BC200 RNA and BRCA1 mRNA.

We used MCF-10A cells in an initial test of the above hypothesis. MCF-10A cells are normal, non-malignant mammary epithelial cells31 which do not express BC200 RNA.32 We transfected MCF-10A cells with increasing amounts (7.5, 15, and 30 nM) of full-length, wild-type BC200 RNA and performed western blot analysis after 72 h. We observed a significant dose-dependent decrease of BRCA1 expression levels in MCF-10A cells transfected with BC200 RNA (Figure 1). Transfection with 30 nM BC200 RNA reduced BRCA1 expression by more than 90%. U4 and U6 RNAs are small nuclear RNAs (snRNAs) that have previously been used as controls in work directed at BC RNA functionality: similar in size to BC RNAs, they operate in the nucleus where they play functional roles unrelated to translational control in the cytoplasm.20 In a further control, we used a mutant BC200 RNA in which the BC200 RNA central domain was modified by substituting adenosine (A) residues with uridine (U) residues.20 The 5′ GA targeting motif and the 3′ C-loop motif structure remain intact.20 This central domain mutant is unable to bind eIF4A and, as a result, does not repress translation initiation.20 The translational control-incompetent BC200 RNA mutant U+ had no effect on BRCA1 levels in MCF-10A cells (Figure 1).

Finally, to assess the specificity of the BC200 RNA regulation of BRCA1 expression, we tested the expression of a different tumor suppressor under the same experimental conditions. We again transfected MCF-10A cells with 30 nM BC200 RNA and performed western blot analysis after 72 h to examine expression of retinoblastoma protein (Rb), a tumor suppressor protein that is dysfunctional in various types of cancers.33 We established that transfection with BC200 RNA does not affect Rb protein expression levels in MCF-10A cells (Figure 1C).

To assess expression of BC200 RNA in parental MCF-10A cells and in transfected cells, we performed RT-qPCR analyses. The results showed a significant increase in BC200 RNA levels following transfection (Figure 1D). Co-transfection with a Dicer-substrate small interfering RNA (DsiRNA) that specifically targets BC200 RNA effectively reduced its expression (Figure 1D).

In summary, the data shown in Figure 1 indicate that BC200 RNA negatively and specifically controls expression levels of BRCA1, a tumor suppressor implicated in breast cancer.

BC200 RNA inhibits BRCA1-mediated DNA repair

An important role of the BRCA1 protein as it relates to malignancy is its function in DNA repair mechanisms.7 Compromised DNA repair can result in chromatin damage, and BRCA1 acts as a tumor suppressor through its DNA repair functionality.7,9 Loss of BRCA1 expression or function leads to genetic instability and reduces chromosome integrity, as a result increasing mutation rates and malignant transformation.7,9 We used the comet assay to ascertain the impact of BC200 RNA on BRCA1 DNA repair (Figure 2). The comet assay is a gel electrophoresis-based technique that is commonly used to visualize and evaluate DNA damage.34 DNA damage is measured by quantifying dispersion of DNA material from the nucleus (the “head of the comet”) to a resulting “tail.” DNA damage is expressed as “extent tail moment,” which takes into consideration both the extent of migration as well as the relative amount of the DNA in the tail. In our experiments, MCF-10A breast epithelial cells were transfected with BC200 RNA (alone or in combination with a BC200 RNA-specific DsiRNA), and after 48 h, were exposed to UV light for 5 min. The comet assay was performed 4 h after UV light exposure. Following transfection with BC200 RNA, we noted an increase in DNA damage in MCF-10A cells (Figures 2B and 2E). The damage is evidenced by a significant increase in the extent of the tail moment (by about 300%; Figure 2E). Significantly less DNA damage was observed after co-transfection with a DsiRNA specific for the 3’ domain of BC200 RNA (Figure 2C). The combined data of Figures 1 and 2 indicate that BC200 RNA specifically impacts BRCA1, reducing its expression and therefore its DNA repair capability. Thus, through its repression of BRCA1 synthesis, BC200 RNA causes increased DNA damage, which in turn is a key cancer determinant.

BC200 RNA promotes cell proliferation

A hallmark of cancer cells is their ability to multiply uncontrollably without external stimuli.35 We therefore examined the role of BC200 RNA in the proliferation potential of MCF-10A cells, introduced above as a non-malignant breast epithelial cell type that does not express BC200 RNA31,32 and adheres to a normal non-proliferating cell cycle.31 We found that the transfection of MCF-10A cells with BC200 RNA causes a dose-dependent increase in cell proliferation (Figure 3A) with an increase of over 400% after transfection with 30 nM BC200 RNA. This abnormal cell proliferation of MCF-10A cells was blocked if a DsiRNA specific for BC200 RNA was co-transfected (Figure 3A). We next worked with breast cancer cell type MCF-7,36 which expresses BC200 RNA at high levels.32 When MCF-7 cells were transfected with the above DsiRNA directed at BC200 RNA, we observed a significant reduction in cell proliferation (Figure 3C). Transfection with 20 nM DsiRNA specific for BC200 RNA led to a 40% reduction in the proliferation rate 72 h after transfection. Again, this effect increased over time, progressing from around 15% reduction after 24 h to over 40% reduction 72 h after transfection with 30 nM BC200 RNA (Figure 3D).

In summary, the above results link the expression of BC200 RNA to cell proliferation. They are congruous with previous work investigating the role of BC200 RNA in cell cycle regulation and cell survival.25,27,28

Expression of BC200 RNA induces malignant transformation

The above data show that BC200 RNA negatively regulates the expression of BRCA1, thereby causing reduced DNA repair activity and increased DNA instability. As a result, cell proliferation rates are significantly increased.

Next, we addressed the question of whether the introduction of BC200 RNA leads to malignant transformation. We performed a set of cell transformation experiments to answer this question. The transformation of a normal cell into a neoplastic one is an essential step in tumorigenesis.35 A further hallmark of tumor cells is their ability to grow without being attached to a surface.35 In cell transformation assays, we therefore grew cells in soft agarose and measured their ability to develop clonal colonies while not surface-attached.37

In a first approach (Figures 4A and 4B), 40,000 MCF-10A cells were plated and transfected with 30 nM BC200 RNA. Newly formed colonies were quantified in comparison with untreated cells after 10 days in soft-agarose medium. When cells undergo neoplastic transformation, they acquire the ability to grow and develop into spheroid-like clonal colonies. The results show that BC200 RNA expression led to significant malignant transformation (Figures 4A and 4B), and as a result, the surviving fraction increased by more than 7-fold (Figure 4B). The surviving fraction is a measure of the number of colonies formed per cell seeded, with a correction factor relative to the specific cell type and growing conditions. As shown in Figures 4A and 4B, the increase in colony formation after BC200 RNA transfection is strong and significant. The clonogenic assay relies on microscopic visualization and direct counting of the clonal colonies formed. In a second approach, the anchorage-independent growth of seeded cells was measured as a function of the conversion of tetrazolium salt to formazan by cellular mitochondrial dehydrogenases. MCF-10A cells were grown in soft agarose for 8 days following transfection with BC200 RNA, and colony formation was measured by absorbance at 450 nm.

The experimental data indicate that transfection with 30 nM BC200 RNA significantly increased the ability of MCF-10A cells to grow in a soft-agarose environment, in comparison with untransfected cells (Figure 4D). In contrast, transfection with BC200 RNA mutant U+ did not result in an increased growth of seeded MCF-10A cells in soft agarose (Figure 4D). We also note that after transfection with wild-type BC200 RNA, few clonal colonies were formed within the first 48 h; however, strong neoplastic transformation was observed beginning after day 4 (Figures 4C and 4E).

The combined results thus indicate that the introduction of BC200 RNA into MCF-10A cells leads to malignant transformation via repression of BRCA1 expression.

Co-transfection with the BRCA1 coding sequence rescues the BC200 RNA-induced phenotype

To further establish a direct link between BC200 RNA-mediated repression of BRCA1 translation and the resulting oncogenic transformation, we conducted rescue experiments (Figure 5A). MCF-10A cells were co-transfected with 30 nM BC200 RNA and 1.5 μg of a plasmid containing the BRCA1 coding sequence. The cells were then plated in soft agarose for an additional 6 days. In this set of experiments, the newly formed colonies were quantified in comparison with untransfected cells, with cells transfected with BC200 RNA alone, and with cells co-transfected with BC200 RNA and a BC200 RNA-specific DsiRNA. Reversal of malignant transformation through the reduction of BC200 RNA cellular levels

The data presented above indicate that BC200 RNA is driving the malignant transformation of healthy breast epithelial cells. The question, therefore, arises as to whether BC200 RNA can be used as a therapeutic target. To address this question, we developed a malignant cellular system in which the expression of BC200 RNA in MCF-7 human adenocarcinoma cells can be reduced by the administration of doxycycline (dox) to trigger expression of shRNAs directed at BC200 RNA. MCF-7 cells are human breast cancer cells that express BC200 RNA at high levels.32

Using the Mirimus SplashRNA algorithm,38 we examined shRNAs with a range of predicted BC200 RNA knockdown efficacies. Three shRNAs with the highest predicted relative knockdown efficacies were then selected, generated, and subcloned into a TRE-based, dox-inducible system packaged in an all-in-one lentiviral vector39 for introduction into MCF-7 cells. All shRNAs were designed to target the 3′ domain of BC200 RNA. The reason for this choice was to specifically target the 3′ domain C-loop motif, which is responsible for interactions with eIFs 4A and 4B and thus for translational repression competence.20,21

RT-qPCR was used to measure the level of BC200 RNA after 72 h of treatment with 1 μg/mL of doxycycline (Figure 6A). MCF-7 cells expressing shRNA1 (MCF7-shRNA1) or shRNA3 (MCF7-shRNA3) showed a robust knockdown of BC200 RNA levels (67% and 71%, respectively; Figure 6A), whereas MCF7-shRNA2 produced a more limited effect (16%, Figure 6A). The relative knockdown efficacies of the three shRNAs appear to be a function of what sequence of nucleotides in the BC200 RNA 3′ domain is being targeted, and the roles that these nucleotides play in the formation of noncanonical nucleotide interactions in the C-loop motif and in adjoining short A-form helical stems. These structural determinants are presented and discussed in Figures 6B and 6C. The C-loop motif is the functional domain for the translation repression competence of BC200 RNA,20 and shRNAs which target both the noncanonical C-loop motif structure and the adjoining A-helical basal stem were of the highest relative efficacies. For independent corroboration, we performed transformation assays using a different breast cancer cell type. In this case, we used the triple-negative breast cancer cell line SUM229PE. The SUM229PE cells have been derived from a pleural effusion of a patient with estrogen receptor-negative (ER−negative), progesterone receptor-negative (PR−negative), and HER2-negative breast cancer. Triple-negative breast cancer cells are known to be growth-aggressive and therapy-resistant.6 Our results (Figure 7F) demonstrate that over a period of 10 days, transfection with 30 nM of a DsiRNA specific for BC200 RNA significantly reduced tumor formation. Taken together, the above results underscore the role of BC200 RNA in malignant transformation, demonstrate the reversibility of the transformation process by the application of shRNA or DsiRNA, and establish BC200 RNA as a potential molecular target in breast cancer therapeutic interventions.

Nerves and tumors: neuronal BC200 RNA in breast cancer

Increasing evidence points to the importance of the nervous system in tumorigenesis.40,41 Neurons have been identified as contributors to tumor microenvironments that are important for the development and progression of malignancies.42 Of particular interest are the roles of nerve growth factor (NGF) and its receptors (TrkA and p75NTR) in cancer formation and progression,43 especially in breast cancer.44 The majority of breast cancers appear to express NGF, and NGF expression has been shown to stimulate cell proliferation and tumor cell survival.45,46

Here, we hypothesize that the presence of NGF in breast cells can trigger expression of BC200 RNA, and that the therapeutic effects observed by targeting NGF45,46 can therefore be explained by a downstream effect on BC200 RNA expression. To test this hypothesis, we asked if the presence of NGF results in an increase in BC200 RNA expression. MCF-10 A cells, non-malignant breast epithelial cells that do not express BC200 RNA,32 were treated with NGF, and expression of BC200 RNA was assessed by RT-qPCR after 24 h and 48 h (Figure 8A). The results show that treatment with NGF triggered expression of BC200 RNA in MCF-10A cells. Expression of BC200 RNA was dependent on NGF concentration and exposure time (Figure 8A). Discussion

Increasing evidence points to important roles of non-protein-coding RNAs in human cancer development13,14,48 and to the therapeutic potential of targeting such RNAs.49 Recent work by Bozzoni and colleagues50 describes a regulatory mechanism in which the circular RNA circHIPK3 directly binds to BRCA1 mRNA, enhancing its translation by competing with fragile X mental retardation protein (FMRP). FMRP is a translational repressor that normally participates in the regulation of mRNA translation in neurons51 but has also been associated with cancer.52 FMRP and BC200 RNA are both translational repressors implicated in the control of local protein synthesis at the synapse.19,53 However, while other noncoding RNAs have been implicated in cancer,13,14 the role of BC200 RNA in breast cancer appears to be unique. BC200 RNA is a non-protein-coding RNA that is expressed specifically and at robust levels in human neurons, where it operates as a translational regulator by repressing the translation of target mRNAs at synapses.18,23 High-level expression of BC200 RNA in human invasive breast carcinoma cells constitutes an exception to the typical neuronal specificity rule.23,24 Several studies have focused on BC200 RNA in breast cancer,25,26,27,28 but to date, molecular mechanisms underlying its role in mammary carcinogenesis remain poorly understood. Here we report that BC200 RNA is causal in the malignant transformation of healthy MCF-10A mammary epithelial cells, and that conversely, MCF-7 breast cancer malignancy can be reversed by targeting BC200 RNA with shRNAs or DsiRNA.

Following transfection with BC200 RNA, MCF-10A cells significantly increased their proliferation rate and acquired the ability to grow in culture in an anchorage-independent manner, akin to the unregulated growth of malignant cells in tumors.54 Moreover, our data indicate that BC200 RNA exerts its tumorigenic effect by suppressing the expression of BRCA1, a key tumor suppressor. Decreased BRCA1 expression levels and ensuing reduced DNA repair capability have been implicated as an underlying cause of breast cancer.5,6,7 We found that the suppression of BRCA1 expression by BC200 RNA leads to increased DNA damage and malignant transformation. No suppression of BRCA1 expression was observed with a mutant BC200 RNA that is lacking the wild-type BC200 RNA translational control functionality.20 In this BC200 RNA mutant U+, the central domain A-residues have been replaced with U residues, resulting in an inability of the mutant BC200 RNA to engage eIF4A and thus to repress translation initiation.20,22

In our DsiRNA and shRNAs strategies, we focused on the 3′ domain of BC200 RNA and decided to specifically target the resident C-loop motif with adjoining basal and apical helical stem structures (Figures 6B and 6C). The interaction of the BC200 RNA 3′ domain with initiation factor eIF4B is indispensable for the RNA’s translational repression functionality.20,22 The three shRNAs that we designed were of differential silencing efficacies, which in turn were consonant with the targeted BC200 RNA structures: an effective destabilization of the basal stem and the C-loop motif in the 3′ domain was commensurate with strong BC200 RNA silencing (Figure 6). Thus, shRNA1 and shRNA3 had strong BC200 RNA silencing efficacy, as did DsiRNA, whereas shRNA2 was less impactful (Figure 6).

Several studies have used siRNA strategies to examine the role of BC200 RNA in cancer. Shin and colleagues27 designed siRNAs that targeted BC200 RNA and identified potential gene targets in cancer. Specifically, they discovered that knocking down BC200 RNA reduced cell mobility and migration by targeting a calcium binding-protein that had previously been implicated in cancer.27 Using a similar siRNA approach, Booy and colleagues25 reported on the role of BC200 RNA in cell proliferation and survival. Our present work is therefore also relevant in the context of the role of BC200 RNA in cell cycle regulation and tumor cell survival.25,27,28

We here identify BRCA1 mRNA as a translational control target of BC200 RNA. BC200 RNA does not physically interact with BRCA1 mRNA. Rather, it represses its translation by inhibitory binding to eIF4A and eIF4B, translation initiation factors that are required for the successful initiation of mRNAs, such as BRCA1 mRNA, with complex higher-order structures in their 5′ UTRs. We show that expressing BC200 RNA in breast epithelial cells that normally do not express the RNA (i.e., MCF-10A cells) is sufficient to cause malignant transformation, as BRCA1 expression and its DNA repair activity are significantly reduced due to impaired translation initiation. We used DsiRNA and shRNAs to illustrate their potential therapeutic utility and report that they effectively reversed the malignant features of MCF-7 breast cancer cells. Our data show that by silencing BC200 RNA, DNA repair activity can be restored, and cell proliferation can be reduced. Moreover, clonogenic and transformation assays revealed that knockdown of BC200 RNA is sufficient to mitigate the malignant growth phenotype of MCF-7 breast cancer cells. This conclusion is further corroborated by results from rescue experiments in which BRCA1 expression was restored in BC200 RNA-transfected MCF-10A cells. Here, we used the BRCA1 mRNA coding sequence, which lacks the highly structured 5′ UTR. Its translation does therefore not require eIF4A and eIF4B activity and is thus not subject to BC200 RNA-mediated translational repression. As a result, BRCA1 is expressed in the presence of BC200 RNA, and malignant transformation by BC200 RNA was averted. These results further support the notion that BC200 RNA-induced carcinogenesis is mediated by suppression of BRCA1 expression.

Finally, a noteworthy aspect of cancer formation and development that has increasingly been investigated in recent years is the role of innervation in malignancies,41,43 especially in breast cancer.45,46 Through neurogenesis, neurons and nerve fibers infiltrate primary solid tumors and thus become essential in tumor initiation and progression.41 Especially in breast cancer, increasing neurogenesis is associated with aggressive subtypes and poor outcomes.41

Our work has identified BC200 RNA as a potential interlocutor between neurons and breast cancer. In neurons, BC RNAs are heterogeneously expressed, typically at high levels.18 Here we introduce a functional role for BC200 RNA in malignant transformation that results from its abnormal but high-level expression in breast cancer cells. NGF is an important determinant of the impact that neurons have on breast cancer development,44,46 and in our current work, we uncover a direct link between NGF stimulation and abnormal BC200 RNA expression in breast cancer cells. Further work will be needed to confirm potential therapeutic benefits of inhibiting NGF signaling,46 even though such effects may be achievable with higher specificity by directly targeting BC200 RNA downstream of the NGF pathway. In addition, future work in vivo will be needed to corroborate the role of BC200 RNA in de novo tumorigenesis in a natural environment.

In summary, our work introduces (1) a hitherto unreported BRCA1 mechanistic pathway in breast cancer development and (2) BC200 RNA as a potential target in therapeutic interventions. We establish the causal role that abnormal BC200 RNA expression plays in the reduction of BRCA1 expression levels and ensuing increased DNA damage. Of therapeutic relevance, we demonstrate that suppression of BC200 RNA is effective in mitigating breast cancer malignancy. Last but not least, we uncover a mechanistic link between neurons and cancer cells: the NGF-triggered abnormal expression of normally neuron-specific BC200 RNA in breast epithelial cells acts as an instigator of malignancy initiation.

Limitations of the study

The main caveat of our work is its nature as an in vitro study with cells in culture. Although these experiments provide valuable mechanistic insights into BC200 RNA functionality, they cannot fully recapitulate the complex tumor microenvironment and systemic responses in living organisms. Notably, while we partially addressed clinical relevance by including patient-derived breast cancer cells, the study still lacks validation in clinical cohorts and in vivo human-relevant systems. To increase the analytic power of our approaches and to confirm the potential of BC200 RNA as a possible therapeutic target, it will be essential to expand the study to an animal model. Patient-derived xenograft mice55 are an excellent model to verify the therapeutic impact of BC200 RNA and to assess potential toxicity and off-target effects in vivo.

Moreover, in this work, we decided to focus on the actions of BC200 RNA on BRCA1 expression, mostly because of the importance of this tumor suppressor gene in DNA repair mechanisms and in breast cancer development. Nevertheless, we are aware of the repressive activity of BC200 RNA in the cell cycle, and of the roles of eIFs 4A and 4B in cell cycle regulation.49,56 In addition, because our study did not employ unbiased proteomic approaches, it remains possible that BC200 RNA regulates additional downstream effectors beyond BRCA1 that were not captured here. Future studies will therefore investigate the potential broader impact of BC200 RNA on other tumor suppressor genes and oncogenes to fully elucidate its regulatory network.

Experimental model and study participant details

Cell culture and reagents

MCF-10A cells and MCF-7 cells were purchased from ATCC and cultured following the manufacturer’s guidelines. MCF-10A cells were cultured in MEBM with MEBM basal medium, BPE, hEGF, insulin, hydrocortisone, GA-1000 (Gentamicin Sulfate-Amphotericin) and 100 ng/ml cholera toxin. MCF-7 cells were cultured in Eagle’s Minimum Essential Medium, with 0.01 mg/ml human recombinant insulin and 10 % fetal bovine serum. SUM229PE cells are patient-derived triple-negative breast cancer cells. These cells were purchased from BIOIVT and cultured following the manufacturer’s guidelines. SUM229PE cells were cultured in Ham’s F12 medium, enriched with fetal bovine serum (final concentration 5%), 10 mM HEPES, 1 μg/ml hydrocortisone and 5 μg/ml insulin. Cell lines were tested for mycoplasma. Cell lines were authenticated by the vendors. Cell lines are female-derived.

Plasmids

The EGFP-BRCA1 plasmid was from Dr. Rob Parton (Addgene plasmid #115491; http://n2t.net/addgene:115491; RRID: Addgene_115491).57

Method details

Cell proliferation

MCF-10A and MCF-7 cells were plated in a 96-well format and transfected with RNA (BC200 RNA or DsiRNA specific for BC200 RNA) for 24-72 h. MTT colorimetric assay was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. In brief, the assay measures cell viability in terms of cell metabolic activity. It is based on the living cells’ conversion of a tetrazolium compound into water-insoluble formazan crystals by dehydrogenases. The MTT reagent was added to each well and the plates were incubated at 37°C for 2 h. Kit detergent was added, and plates were incubated in the dark at room temperature for 2 h. Absorbance of the solution was measured at 570 nm to obtain a standard cell count.

RNA transfection

Transfections were performed by using Lipofectamine 2000 CD Transfection Reagent. The ratio between RNA and Lipofectamine was kept at 1:2.5.22 When the RNA was titrated, the amount of RNA was kept constant by supplementation with random-sequence RNA to a combined total of 30 nM.

To our knowledge, no reliable data are available on the absolute concentration of BC200 RNA in breast cancer cells (e.g., expressed as nM, copies per cell, or mass per volume) as most studies report only relative expression levels such as fold changes between tumors and normal tissues or differences across cell lines.32 In neurons, BC200 RNA abundance is approximately 106 copies per ng of total RNA25 which, assuming an average RNA content of 10–30 pg per cell,58 corresponds to roughly 1 x 104 to 3 x 104 molecules per cell. In our experiments, transfection of 7–9 million cells with 30 nM BC200 RNA corresponds to ∼1.8 x 1016 molecules per liter. Given an average mammalian cell volume of ∼2 pL, the total cellular volume of 8 million cells is approximately 1.6 x 10−5 L, yielding an estimated 2.9 x 1011 BC200 molecules in total, or about 3.6 x 104 copies per cell. Considering the transfection efficiency of Lipofectamine 2000 (approximately 50–80%), the effective intracellular concentration is likely lower, suggesting that the experimental BC200 RNA levels remain below physiological concentrations.

Generation of doxycycline-inducible BC200 RNA knockdown MCF-7 cells

MCF-7 mammary adenocarcinoma cells were engineered using shRNAs with BC200-specific sequences to create three BC200 RNA doxycycline (dox)-inducible knockdown cell lines. Using Mirimus’s proprietary SplashRNA algorithm pipeline,38 the top three shRNAs with a range of predicted knockdown efficiency of BC200 RNA were selected and subcloned into a TRE-based, dox-inducible system packaged in an all-in-one lentiviral vector,39 The shRNAs targeted the BC200 RNA 3′ C-loop motif region which is responsible for translational control, but not the 5′ stem-loop domain (with its resident GA motif) which is responsible for synapto-dendritic transport. For each shRNA, the respective target segment within the 3′ domain of BC200 RNA was cloned into the 3′ UTR of a GFP reporter gene. To establish the BC200 knockdown efficacy of each shRNA, we used quantitative RT-PCR and inducible GFP fluorescent signals via flow cytometry analysis 72 h post-dox induction, in comparison with a validated control shRNA against Renilla luciferase (shRenilla.713, MCF-7-Renilla).

Query sequences for shRNAs guide strands (per DNA nucleotides):

shRNA 1: 5′ GTAACTTCCCTCAAAGCAACAA 3’

shRNA 2: 5′ CGTAACTTCCCTCAAAGCAACA 3’

shRNA 3: 5′ AGCGTAACTTCCCTCAAAGCAA 3’

The RT-qPCR primer sequences were published in previous work.32

Western blotting

MCF-10A cells were plated and transfected with BC200 RNA for 72 h. Proteins were extracted from MCF-10A cell pellets, using 1% Triton X-100. Protein extracts were resolved on 10% SDS-PAGE gels and transferred to nitrocellulose membranes. Membranes were blocked (5% milk/5% BSA) and incubated with primary antibodies. Primary antibodies were directed against the following proteins: BRCA1(dilution 1:750), Retinoblastoma (dilution 1:1000), and γ-tubulin (dilution 1:1000). For primary antibodies, molecular masses of antibody targets have been established by the vendor (and are given in Figure 1). Independent assessment of commercial antibodies is also provided by CiteAb. After incubation with secondary antibodies, the SignalFire ECL Reagent was used for visualization. Restore Plus Western Blot Stripping Buffer was used for membrane stripping. For quantitative analysis, band intensities were normalized to those of γ-tubulin.

RNA isolation and RT-qPCR

Total RNA was isolated from cells using TRIzol Reagent according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The reverse transcription step was performed using random hexamer primers and Superscript III as follows: 5 min at 25°C, 1 h at 50°C, and 15 min at 70°C. For qPCR, the following conditions were used: 30 s at 95°C, 30 s at 58°C, 30 s at 72°C for 35 cycles. qPCR was performed with the IQ SYBR Green Supermix dye kit, using a CFX96 Real-Time PCR System (BioRad). For BC200 RNA amplification, the following primers were used:

BC200 forward primer: 5′ CCTGGGCAATATAGCGAGAC 3’

BC200 reverse primer: 5′ GGGTTGTTGCTTTGAGGGA 3’

The primers amplified nt 91–188 in the 3′ region of BC200 RNA.32

The mRNA encoding acidic ribosomal protein (ARP) was used as internal standard for qPCR. For ARP mRNA amplification, the following primers were used:

ARP forward primer: 5′ ACTGTGCCAGCCCAGAACAC 3’

ARP reverse primer: 5′ GCAGATGGATCAGCCAAGAAG 3’

q-PCR data were analyzed using the cycle threshold (Ct) method. The Ct for BC200 RNA was normalized to the Ct for ARP mRNA, resulting in a ΔCt that reflects the relative level of BC200 RNA in the sample.

Cell transformation assay and clonogenic assay

Cell transformation assays37 were performed using a Cell Transformation Assay Kit (Colorimetric) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Two layers of agarose were generated, and after solidification of the base layer, 1-5 x 104 MCF-10A cells or MCF-7 cells were mixed with the top agarose layer and plated in a 96-well plate, in triplicates along with blank wells. MCF-10A and MCF-7 cells were transfected with RNA (BC200 RNA or DsiRNA specific for BC200 RNA). Cells were then cultured for 8 to 10 days at 37°C. Cells were monitored for colony formation, and at the end of day 8, WST Working Solution was added to each sample and the cell number was measured using spectrophotometry at 450 nm. In this method, non-adherent cells proliferation is measured as a function of the conversion of a tetrazolium salt (WST) into formazan by cellular mitochondrial dehydrogenases. Cells were imaged after addition of the Staining Solution.

In the transformation assay used to assess the effects of NGF exposure, cells were initially treated with a high concentration of NGF and subsequently embedded in agarose medium for an additional eight days. Because the semisolid agarose prevents repeated supplementation, it was not feasible to maintain a stable physiological NGF level throughout the assay. To compensate for NGF degradation over time and to ensure adequate pathway activation, we therefore initiated the assay with an elevated initial dose.

For clonogenic assays,59 a modified protocol was used since the number of clonal colonies was counted. The plating efficiency (PE, the ratio of the number of clonal colonies formed in the well to the number of cells seeded) was determined, and the surviving fraction of treated cells (ratio of colonies formed to cells seeded x PE/100) was calculated. Experimenters were blind to genotype and treatment at the point of analysis.

RNA preparation

All constructs used for in vitro transcription in this work have been described previously.20,60 Plasmids were linearized, and transcripts were generated using MEGA shortscript T7 according to manufacturer’s instructions. We designed a DsiRNA61 that is specific for the 3′ domain of BC200 RNA, and Integrated DNA Technologies (IDT) generated this anti-BC200 DsiRNA with the following sequence.5′ GGGUUGUUGCUUUGAGGGAAGUUACGC 3’.

5′ GGGUUGUUGCUUUGAGGGAAGUUACGC 3’.

5′ GGGUUGUUGCUUUGAGGGAAGUUACGC 3’.

Comet assay

A comet assay was used for assessing cellular DNA damage.34 Cells were transfected with BC200 RNA, harvested, and combined with pre-heated comet agarose (37°C) at a 1/10 ratio (v/v). Cells were spread on a slide and immersed in ice-cold lysis buffer for 60 min at 4°C in the dark. The lysis buffer was then replaced with ice-cold alkaline solution, and the slides were subjected to electrophoresis in cold alkaline electrophoresis solution (electrophoresis condition: 1 V/cm for 10–15 min). Subsequently, slides were washed with ice-cold water and were immersed in 70% ice-cold ethanol and let dry in the dark. Photomicrographs were obtained using a Microphot-FXA microscope (Nikon; purchased from Morrell Instruments).

The extent of DNA damage is expressed as the Extent Tail Moment which takes into consideration both the migration as well the relative amount of the DNA in the tail:

Extent Tail Moment = Tail DNA % x length of tail.

The percentage of DNA in comet tails was calculated as:

Tail DNA % = 100 x tail DNA intensity/cell (head of the comet) DNA intensity.

Quantification of the above parameters was based on at least 10 images (i.e., fields) with a total of at least 100 cells analyzed for each treatment. ImageJ processing was used for these measurements. Endpoint experimenters were not cognizant of sample types.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using R and GraphPad Prism 8.4.3. Statistical analyses were performed on raw data obtained from typically 8 independent experiments (this number being expressed as n). Results were visualized as normalized mean ± standard deviation (SD). The Mann-Whitney U test was used was used for comparisons of means of two groups. One-way ANOVA (followed by Dunnett’s multiple comparison analysis) or the Kruskall-Wallis test (followed by Dunn’s test for pairwise comparisons) were used for comparisons of three or more groups. n refers to the number of biological experiments performed. Levels of significance are indicated as follows: ∗p < 0.05; ∗∗p < 0.01; ∗∗∗p < 0.001; ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001. Results of statistical analyses are reported in figures and figure legends in this paper.
