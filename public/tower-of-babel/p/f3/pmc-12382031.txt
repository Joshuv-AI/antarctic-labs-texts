Differential gene expression and immune profiling in Parkinson’s disease: unveiling potential candidate biomarkers
Authors: Xiuping Yao, Peng Wang, Zhenqiang Huang, Lingyun Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundParkinson’s disease (PD) represents a common neurodegenerative disorder characterized by a multifaceted interaction with immune infiltration. Despite a well-defined clinical diagnosis, the misdiagnosis rate of PD remains around 20%. The aim of this study is to discover new diagnostic biomarkers for PD and investigate their pathogenesis to improve early intervention and effective management of patients with PD.MethodsFive PD-related GEO datasets were used: four for training (GSE7621, GSE8397, GSE20186, and GSE20292) and one for validation (GSE26927). Gene expression analysis included batch correction and “RobustRankAggreg” (RRA) methods. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were linked to functions via Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG). Hub genes were identified using CytoHubba in Cytoscape and validated with ROC analysis. Real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) confirmed hub gene expression in PD patients’ substantia nigra. CIBERSORT, along with the Wilcoxon test and Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression, analyzed differences in immune cell abundance between PD patients and healthy controls (HC). Spearman’s rank correlation in R explored the link between biomarkers and immune cells.ResultsThe intersection of two methods identified 124 DEGs in PD. GO analysis revealed enrichment in neurotransmitter transport, while KEGG analysis identified involvement in the dopaminergic synapse pathway. Three hub genes (DDC, NEFL, and SLC18A2) were identified using the “UpSet” R package, and their expression was significantly lower in PD patients than in the HC group (all p < 0.05), as confirmed by RT-qPCR. LASSO regression and ROC analysis demonstrated that SLC18A2 could diagnose PD with high specificity and sensitivity in both training (0.85 and 0.84) and validation sets (1.00 and 0.75). CIBERSORT analysis showed increased memory B cells, activated mast cells, NK cells, and CD8+ T cells in PD, with notable differences in the abundance of memory B cells and activated mast cells between PD and HC.ConclusionThe study identifies SLC18A2 as a potential candidate biomarker for PD and emphasizes the involvement of memory B cells and activated mast cells in the onset and progression of the disease.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12883-025-04388-x.Keywords: Parkinson's disease, SLC18A2, Diagnostic biomarkers, Memory B cells, Bioinformatics

Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is recognized as the second most prevalent neurodegenerative disorder, affecting approximately 1% of individuals over the age of 60 and 3% of those aged 80 and above [1]. Globally, over 6 million individuals are afflicted by PD, with projections suggesting a potential doubling of this number by 2040 [2]. Similar to other neurodegenerative diseases, the pathophysiology of PD is not fully understood. However, substantial evidence suggests that inflammation, occurring both within the central nervous system (CNS) and peripherally, plays a role in the initiation and progression of the disease [3]. The precise triggers and origins of this inflammatory response remain unclear [3]. The pathology of PD is characterized by the loss of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra, the aggregation of α-synuclein in Lewy bodies and neurites, and neuroinflammation within the brain [4]. The primary motor symptoms associated with PD include bradykinesia, rigidity, resting tremor, and impaired posture and gait [2]. Currently, there are no clinically viable diagnostic markers for the early or late-stage detection of PD, and its pathogenesis continues to be an area requiring extensive research.

Previous studies have reported the crucial role of dysregulated immune microenvironment in the development of PD [5]. The development of PD is associated with the activation of both microglia [6, 7] and T cells [8, 9], indicating the involvement of innate and adaptive immune responses. Mediators such as α-synuclein [10, 11], gut microbiota [12, 13], dopamine [14, 15], and short chain fatty acids (SCFA) [13, 16] can trigger T cell-driven inflammation, culminating in brain inflammation. Astrocytes contribute to immune system regulation in the brain, with disruptions noted in PD patients [17]. Recent studies on the immune system’s connection to PD have identified potential immune-related biomarkers for the condition [18]. For example, glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) and major histocompatibility complex II (MHCII) are recognized biomarkers for activated astrocytes, showing increased levels in the substantia nigra of postmortem PD brain tissues compared to healthy controls (HC) [19, 20]. Immune cell infiltration biomarkers PLOD3 and LRRN3 have been established and validated as reliable diagnostic biomarkers for PD using blood samples [5]; however, they remain in the experimental phase. Given the limited availability of clinically validated biomarkers for PD and the misdiagnosis rates among movement disorder specialists, which range from 16 to 20% due to clinical overlap with other parkinsonian disorders [1], there is an urgent need to continue the identification of reliable and specific biomarkers for the diagnosis of PD.

This study aimed to identify new diagnostic biomarkers associated with the abundance of immune cells in PD using bioinformatics, real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR), and machine learning techniques. We conducted an extensive analysis of four GEO datasets (GSE7621, GSE8397, GSE20186, and GSE20292) to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between PD and HC. Functional enrichment analysis was performed using GO and KEGG, and hub genes were identified with the R package “UpSet”. The predictability of these biomarkers was evaluated through ROC logistic regression analysis. CIBERSORT was employed to assess the relative abundance of immune cells in patients with PD. We used the Wilcoxon test and LASSO regression to identify significant differences in immune cell abundance between PD patients and HC. The study utilized Spearman’s rank correlation in R software to analyze the relationship between the three identified hub genes and the significantly different immune cell abundances. This study identified new potential candidate biomarkers for PD and analyzed the relative abundance of immune cells in the substantia nigra of individuals with PD. These findings offer important insights for future research aimed at identifying new diagnostic and immunotherapeutic targets for PD.

Materials and methods

Data collection and processing

Gene expression profiling for PD was analyzed using the GEO database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). The criteria for selecting datasets from the GEO database are specified as follows: the “disease state” must be “Parkinson’s disease” and the “sample source” is required to be the “postmortem human brain substantia nigra”. Five eligible datasets were selected, namely GSE7621, GSE8397, GSE20186, GSE20292, and GSE26927. Refer to Table 1 for details of all the data. Supplementary Table S1 presents the clinical information related to five gene expression matrices.

Datasets used in this study

The probe expression matrix was extracted from the raw data and normalized using the Robust Multiarray Average (RMA) method with the “affy” R package. The platform annotation file was then used to transform the probe expression matrix into a gene expression matrix. In instances where multiple probes corresponded to a single gene, the mean value was calculated. Subsequently, four gene expression matrices (GSE7621, GSE8397, GSE20186, and GSE20292) were merged using a Perl script. The R package “sva” was then employed to mitigate heterogeneity arising from variations in experimental batches and platforms. Ultimately, we acquired a single merged normalized gene expression matrix and utilized the R package “limma” for analysis. For the series matrix files, a Perl script was employed to individually extract each probe expression matrix, which were subsequently converted into gene expression matrices. Each gene expression matrix was then analyzed using the “limma” package. The validation RNA-Seq dataset, GSE26927, was acquired in the form of a gene expression matrix, and the R package “edgeR” was utilized for further analysis.

Screening of DEGs

Two distinct methods were applied to extract accurate DEGs from multiple microarray datasets. In the first method, called “Batch correction”, the four raw datasets (GSE7621, GSE8397, GSE20186, and GSE20292) were merged into an expression matrix (see Supplementary Table S4), and the DEGs were analyzed with the R package “limma” after performing batch correction and normalization. The second method, “RobustRankAggreg” (RRA), employed the “limma” R package to analyze DGEs in the downloaded gene expression matrices. The DEGs from each dataset were integrated using the “RRA” R package. The final DEGs were obtained by intersecting the results of the two methods using a Venn diagram [21]. DEGs were identified using threshold criteria of an adjusted p-value less than 0.05 and an absolute log fold change (FC) greater than 0.585.

Functional enrichment analyses

Gene IDs for DEGs were obtained using the “org.Hs.eg.db” R package. DEGs were analyzed for GO and KEGG using the “clusterProfiler” R package. GO terms and KEGG pathways were deemed statistically significant if the adjusted p-value was less than 0.05. The results were visualized using the “enrichplot” and “ggplot2” R packages.

Screening of hub genes

The protein-protein interaction (PPI) network of DEGs was analyzed using STRING (https://string-db.org). The database key setting parameter was set to high confidence 0.7 and the maximal number of interactors was 50. The network file was generated using Perl from the downloaded string_interactions_short.tsv interaction file. Cytoscape (v 3.9.1) and the cytoHubba plugin were employed to evaluate each node gene using the top 10 algorithms: Maximal Clique Centrality (MCC), Density of Maximum Neighborhood Component (DMNC), Maximum Neighborhood Component (MNC), Degree, Edge Percolated Component (EPC), BottleNeck, EcCentricity, Closeness, Radiality, Betweenness, and Stress. The R package ‘UpSet’ was used to identify hub genes from the top 15 node genes scored by each algorithm. The identified hub genes were validated using the RNA-Seq dataset GSE26927.

Clinical validation of hub genes by RT-qPCR analysis

From July 2021 to September 2024, tissue samples and clinical data (Supplementary Table S2) were obtained from normal and PD patients at Lishui City People’s Hospital. We employed RT-qPCR to confirm the expression of hub genes identified via bioinformatics in substantia nigra tissue from postmortem brains of normal and PD patients. RNA was extracted using the TRIzol reagent from a total of 16 samples, including 6 control samples and 10 samples from individuals with Parkinson’s disease, representing both genders. cDNA synthesis was achieved utilizing the HiScript IV RT SuperMix kit. RT-qPCR was performed employing the ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix kit. The PCR amplification protocol consisted of an initial denaturation step at 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 10 s and annealing/extension at 60 °C for 30 s. The threshold cycle (Ct) values for each reaction were recorded. The Ct values of the target genes were normalized to those of GAPDH, and relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method. Analysis of the predictive value of hub genes

ROC analysis evaluated the diagnostic effectiveness of three hub genes for predicting PD in both the training set and the GSE26927 dataset. The area under the ROC curve (AUC) assessed the ability to distinguish PD from controls in these datasets.

Construction of a diagnostic model based on three hub genes

A diagnostic model predicated on the expression levels of three hub genes was developed utilizing the training datasets (GSE7621, GSE8397, GSE20186, and GSE20292). The expression levels of the hub genes were extracted and subjected to LASSO regression analysis to determine the coefficients necessary for constructing the diagnostic model. To assess the model’s efficacy, all samples within the training set were classified as either disease (Treat) or control. The accuracy of this classification was verified through the construction of a confusion matrix and the plotting of ROC curves. Furthermore, these analyses were replicated using GSE26927 as an external validation dataset.

Analysis of the relative abundance of immune cells

The CIBERSORT algorithm was employed to assess the relative abundance of immune cells in the substantia nigra of individuals with PD and HC using immune cell–type–like gene expression signatures. The algorithm can transform the normalized gene expression matrix (GSE7621, GSE8397, GSE20186, and GSE20292) into the relative abundances of immune cell–type–like gene expression signatures. The CIBERSORT web portal (http://CIBERSORT.stanford.edu/) was used to submit the data, utilizing LM22 as the reference expression signature with 1000 permutations. The LM22 signature matrix delineates 22 immune cell components, encompassing various subsets of T cells (CD8+ T cells, naïve CD4+ T cells, memory resting CD4+ T cells, memory activated CD4+ T cells, T follicular helper cells, regulatory T cells, and gamma delta T cells), macrophages (M0, M1, and M2 macrophages), natural killer (NK) cells (resting and activated NK cells), mast cells (resting and activated mast cells), B cells (naïve and memory B cells), dendritic cells (resting and activated dendritic cells), as well as monocytes, plasma cells, neutrophils, and eosinophils.

For each expression file in CIBERSORT, p-values and root mean squared errors were calculated. Only data with a CIBERSORT p-value of less than 0.05 were selected and retained for subsequent analysis. In this study, a total of 47 samples were retained for the analysis of the relative abundance of immune cells. The resulting data were directly integrated to construct a comprehensive matrix of immune cell fractions. Utilizing the CIBERSORT algorithm, the study identified the proportions of 22 immune cell types based on tissue gene expression profiles, which were employed to ascertain immune cell proportions in PD and HC groups. The analysis of relative immune cell abundances was conducted using the “Cibersort” R package. A box plot was employed to visualize the proportion of each immune cell type across different samples, while a heatmap was utilized to display the relative abundance of the 22 immune cell subpopulations. The Wilcoxon test was applied to compare immune cell proportions between the PD and HC groups, with the results depicted through a violin plot. Additionally, a heatmap depicting the correlation among the 22 types of infiltrating immune cells was generated using the “corrplot” R package [22]. Furthermore, the results of principal component analysis (PCA) were visualized using the “ggplot2” package to elucidate the differences between PD and HC samples.

The LASSO regression analysis was employed to identify immune cells that exhibited significant differences between the PD and HC groups. Immune cell subgroups with P-values less than 0.05, as determined by the Wilcoxon test, were intersected with the characteristic immune cell subgroups identified through LASSO regression to derive the most precise characteristic subgroups possible. The Wilcoxon test was conducted using the “wilcox.test” function in R, while the LASSO regression analysis was performed utilizing the “glmnet” package.

Analysis of the relationship between hub genes and immune cells

Using R software, Spearman’s rank correlation analysis was conducted to investigate the association between three hub genes and two immune cells with differential abundance. The results were visualized using the “ggplot2” package in R.

Statistical methods

Statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.2.3, with a significance threshold of p < 0.05. RT-qPCR results are presented as mean ± SD. Comparisons between the two groups were carried out using an independent Student’s t-test.

Functional enrichment analysis

We performed GO functional and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses to thoroughly understand the biological functions and processes linked to the DEGs. The DEGs were categorized into three main categories: biological process (BP), molecular function (MF), and cellular component (CC). The BP analysis indicated significant enrichment of DEGs in key processes such as neurotransmitter transport, vesicle-mediated transport in synapses, and the synaptic vesicle cycle. Furthermore, the DEGs demonstrated enrichment in key cellular components such as the synaptic membrane, distal axon, and glutamatergic synapse. The MF analysis of the DEGs predominantly identified links to gated channel activity, including voltage-gated ion channel and channel activities (Fig. 3A-B). KEGG pathway analysis revealed that the DEGs were predominantly enriched in pathways associated with dopaminergic synapse, synaptic vesicle cycle, and serotonergic synapse signaling (Fig. 3C-D).

Identification and validation of hub genes

The PPI network results of the DEGs were obtained using STRING (Supplementary Figure S1) and visualized using Cytoscape (Fig. 4A). Subsequently, ten algorithms were employed to calculate the score of each node gene. Three hub genes, namely SLC18A2, NEFL, and DDC, were identified using the R package “UpSet”. To provide a clearer presentation of the findings, the expression levels of these three hub genes were visualized in a merged microarray data heatmap (Fig. 4C). To ensure the robustness of the results, we conducted validation using the RNA-Seq dataset GSE26927. The expression levels of SLC18A2, NEFL, and DDC were illustrated in another heatmap (Fig. 5A). Significantly lower expression of all three genes was observed in PD compared to HC (p < 0.05), as demonstrated in Fig. 5B-D. Furthermore, RT-qPCR analysis revealed that the mRNA expression levels of three hub genes were significantly reduced in the substantia nigra tissues of patients with PD compared to HC (Fig. 5E-G and Supplementary Table S6). Hence, SLC18A2, NEFL, and DDC may hold promise as potential therapeutic targets for PD.

We evaluated the predictive significance of three hub genes (SLC18A2, NEFL, and DDC) for PD in both the training and validation datasets. An AUC value exceeding 0.8 was considered indicative of excellent specificity and sensitivity for PD diagnosis. As illustrated in Fig. 6A, the AUC values of the ROC curves for NEFL, DDC, and SLC18A2 were 0.739, 0.876, and 0.906, respectively. Additionally, we performed a comparative analysis to validate the predictive efficacy of eight PD diagnostic biomarkers—SNCA [23], SYT1 [24], NEFM [24], GAP43 [24], GRIA1 [24], AGTR1 [25], GBE1 [25], and TPBG [25]—cited in existing literature. Our findings revealed that, with the exception of NEFL, the AUC values for SLC18A2 and DDC surpassed those of the eight diagnostic genes (Fig. 6A). Subsequently, a diagnostic model was constructed using the hub gene SLC18A2 via LASSO regression analysis. The optimal model derived from the training data exhibited a lambda.min of 0.021504, with gene coefficients of −1.109882 (Fig. 6B-C). This model achieved a specificity of 0.85, a sensitivity of 0.84, and an AUC of 0.906 (p = 3.989e-12) (Fig. 6D-E). The diagnostic efficacy of these biomarkers for PD was further validated using the GSE26927 dataset through ROC analysis. As depicted in Fig. 6F, the AUC values for NEFL, DDC, and SLC18A2 were 0.812, 0.833, and 0.917, respectively. Validation using the GSE26927 dataset revealed comparable specificity (1.00) and sensitivity (0.75) (Fig. 6G-H). Notably, the model demonstrated strong discriminatory power with an AUC of 0.917 in the GSE26927 dataset (p = 0.000532) (Fig. 6G). Consequently, SLC18A2 exhibited excellent specificity and sensitivity in the diagnosis of PD.

The proportions of naive B cells, gamma delta T cells, resting mast cells, and regulatory T cells (Tregs) showed little change. The findings revealed that memory B cells, activated mast cells, activated NK cells, and CD8+ T cells demonstrated a markedly increased relative abundance in PD.

We also evaluated the correlation between the 22 immune cell types in PD substantia nigra tissues (refer to Fig. 7C). Diverse correlations were identified among various immune cell types in PD substantia nigra tissues. Neutrophils were positively associated with plasma cells, and regulatory T cells (Tregs) showed a positive correlation with naive B cells. Resting dendritic cells exhibited a positive association with CD8+ T cells. Memory B cells and naive B cells exhibited a negative correlation. Activated mast cells were inversely correlated with both resting mast cells and M2 macrophages. It’s important to highlight that Spearman associations from deconvoluted data are not direct evidence of functional immune crosstalk, and future experiments are needed for confirmation. However, in the context of HC, the aforementioned positive correlations among immune cells were diminished or reversed, whereas the negative correlations remained largely consistent (Supplementary Figure S2). Furthermore, an analysis of the relative abundance of immune cells in substantia nigra tissues revealed that principal component analysis (PCA) did not show a clear differentiation between PD and normal HC (see Fig. 7D).

We employed the Wilcoxon test and LASSO regression to identify immune cells with significant abundance differences between PD patients and HC. Figure 8A presents a violin plot illustrating the results of the Wilcoxon test, which indicated a significant increase in the relative abundance levels of memory B cells and activated mast cells in the substantia nigra tissues of PD patients compared to HC (p < 0.05). Figure 8B and C depict the results of the LASSO regression analysis, highlighting four immune cell types with significant differences: memory B cells, activated memory CD4+ T cells, activated dendritic cells, and activated mast cells. The two intersecting immune cell types identified by both methods (Wilcoxon test and LASSO regression), namely memory B cells and activated mast cells, were subsequently extracted for further analysis.

Discussion

PD is a prevalent neurodegenerative disorder among the elderly, closely associated with immune infiltration [24]. The progressive degeneration of dopaminergic neurons in PD is attributed to a combination of genetic, environmental, aging-related, and oxidative stress factors. Among these, aging is regarded as the primary risk factor for the development of PD, although the precise mechanisms remain elusive [26]. Increasing evidence indicates that neuroinflammation and immune dysregulation play a pivotal role in the pathogenesis and progression of PD. Furthermore, the intricate interplay between immune dysregulation and neurodegeneration during the course of PD involves alterations in the peripheral immune system, brain-gut interactions, and brain-immune interfaces [27]. Despite extensive efforts to identify specific biomarkers for PD, there remains a paucity of effective diagnostic markers, particularly for the early detection of the disease prior to the onset of motor symptoms.

In 1997, the SNCA gene, responsible for encoding α-synuclein (AS), was identified as the first biomarker linked to PD development [23]. Following this discovery, several other genes have been linked to the disease, including parkin (PARK2), UCH-L1 (PARK5), PINK1 (PARK6), DJ-1 (PARK7), LRRK2 (PARK8), and ATP13A2 (PARK9). Furthermore, risk genes such as PARK16, GBA1, VPS35, and EIF4G1 have been implicated in PD through family-based linkage analysis [2]. Genetic mutations in genes such as SNCA, LRRK2, GBA1, PARK2, PARK6, and PARK7 are key factors in the degeneration of dopaminergic neurons in genetically-linked PD [28]. To identify potential novel biomarkers and investigate the immune mechanisms involved in the development of PD, this study utilized four data sets from the GEO database. The study analyzed DEGs between PD samples and normal controls, identifying hub genes for further investigation.

Similarly, Zhang et al. [24] screened 319 DEGs from three substantia nigra expression microarray datasets. The KEGG enrichment analysis results revealed that these DEGs are primarily associated with synaptic vesicle cycle, dopaminergic synapse, GABAergic synapse, and other pathways. NEFL was recognized as a key biomarker for differentiating PD from HC [24]. Additionally, Bao et al. [25]. screened 27 DEGs in PD and control samples from four substantia nigra expression microarray datasets. KEGG pathway enrichment analyses revealed that DEGs were significantly associated with pathways such as cocaine addiction, dopaminergic synapse, amphetamine addiction, synaptic vesicle cycle, and serotonergic synapse [25]. Previous studies have reported that synaptic dysfunction has been identified as an early neuropathologic event in PD [29]. Dopaminergic projections influence numerous brain functions and are associated with various neuropsychiatric disorders [30]. Al-Rafiah et al. [31]. showed that amphetamine addiction may increase the risk of PD. These results indicate that the screened DEGs are involved in the pathogenesis of PD. However, Chen et al. [32]. screened 154 DEGs closely related to PD from the GSE110720 dataset. KEGG enrichment analysis revealed that the DEGs are significantly associated with cytokine-cytokine receptor interactions, Th17 cell differentiation, and pathways regulating stem cell pluripotency [32]. We speculate that this may be related to the fact that a single GEO database has a higher false-positive rate and one-sided results compared to merged microarray datasets, and the sample size is too small.

In this study, three hub genes (DDC, NEFL, and SLC18A2) were identified and validated using the independent GSE26927 dataset, along with the RT-qPCR method. ROC analysis indicated that DDC, NEFL, and SLC18A2 showed strong predictive efficacy for PD in both training and validation sets, achieving an AUC greater than 0.8.

Aromatic L-amino acid decarboxylases (DDC) are crucial enzymes that convert aromatic L-amino acids into aromatic monoamines, facilitating the synthesis of catecholamines, indolamines, and trace amines. DDC is essential for converting L-3,4-dihydroxyphenylalanine (DOPA) to dopamine in PD treatment [33]. Rutledge et al. [34] found that DDC is consistently elevated in the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and urine of untreated PD, prodromal PD, and GBA or LRRK2 carriers across three proteomics methods. They also demonstrated that CSF DDC levels correlate with symptom severity in untreated PD patients and can accurately diagnose PD and prodromal PD. Neurofilaments, key elements of the neuronal axonal cytoskeleton, are associated with neurodegenerative diseases due to their presence in pathological inclusions [35]. Neurofilaments typically comprise three intermediate filament proteins: NEFL, NEFM, and NEFH, which are commonly used as reliable biomarkers for neurodegenerative pathology [36]. Jiao et al. [37] discovered that elevated NEFL levels were linked to future PD events, while reduced TPK1 levels posed potential PD risks. The combination of NEFL and TPK1 significantly boosted the predictive accuracy of clinical models, with the full-cohort AUC rising from 0.784 to 0.842 and the 5-year model from 0.780 to 0.908. In this investigation, as detailed in Supplementary Table S1, the duration of illness among PD patients exceeded five years, based on the GSE26927 dataset. Furthermore, all ten clinical specimens collected from PD patients were in stages three to five of disease progression, as indicated in Supplementary Table S2. Consequently, this study suggests that DDC and NEFL may serve as promising candidates for late-stage biomarkers and therapeutic targets in PD patients. Nonetheless, the potential of these two genes as early diagnostic markers for PD requires further validation through the collection of specimens from patients in the first and second stages of the disease.

Research indicates that the vesicular monoamine transporter 2 (SLC18A2, also known as VMAT2) is impaired in the brains of individuals with PD [38]. Adjusting SLC18A2 levels or function could enhance levodopa-derived dopamine efficacy, boost dopamine transmission from surviving neurons, and offer neuroprotection [38]. Yang et al. also discovered that higher levels or function of SLC18A2 have a neuroprotective role in PD [39]. Additionally, it has been identified as a hub gene associated with cuproptosis, which could potentially be utilized for the diagnosis of PD [40]. Similarly, research by Hinkle et al. [41] presents preliminary yet persuasive evidence that SLC18A2 expression may serve as a bidirectional biomarker of motivational dysregulation in PD. This study employed LASSO regression and ROC analysis, revealing that SLC18A2 possesses exceptional specificity and sensitivity in diagnosing PD, as illustrated in Fig. 6. Notably, when compared to other established biomarkers [23–25], SLC18A2 demonstrates superior diagnostic efficacy for PD (Fig. 6A). Collectively, these findings support the involvement of SLC18A2 in the pathogenesis of PD and propose its potential as a promising candidate for a late-stage biomarker and therapeutic target.

The earliest evidence of immune system involvement in PD was discovered through the identification of reactive/activated microglial cells in postmortem brain tissues of PD patients [6]. Previous studies have reported a significant increase in T cells (CD8+ and CD4+) in the substantia nigra of PD patients compared to control subjects, showing a connection between T-cell responses and dopaminergic neuron cell loss [42, 43]. Chen et al. [32]. found that immune inflammatory responses and immune cell regulation are implicated in the pathogenesis of PD. They identified five hub genes (NPBWR2, CXCL10, CXCL5, S1PR5, and GALR1) involved in the immune infiltration process of PD through bioinformatics and RT-qPCR validation. Zhang et al. [24]. demonstrated a significant increase in the proportion of neutrophils in the PD group compared to HC. They constructed a nomogram that exhibited reliable predictive capability for PD (AUC = 0.905) based on four immune-related DEGs (SYT1, NEFM, GAP43, and GRIA1).

In our study, we also observed that the relative abundance of CD8+ T cells was markedly increased in PD. Additionally, the relative abundance levels of memory B cells and activated mast cells in PD patients were significantly elevated (p < 0.05). However, the gene biomarkers DDC, NEFL, and SLC18A2 did not demonstrate a significant association with these highly abundant immune cells. This lack of significance may be attributed to the small sample size and the amalgamation of multiple datasets, which resulted in no clear distinction between PD patients and HC as indicated by PCA (Fig. 7D). This underscores the need for a larger sample size for further validation. Furthermore, the CIBERSORT algorithm exhibits several limitations: (1) It lacks the ability to distinguish between genuine immune cell infiltration and tissue-resident immune populations, such as brain-resident T cells. (2) CIBERSORT estimates the cellular composition of complex tissue samples based on transcriptomic data, which poses challenges in accounting for the tissue heterogeneity of regions like the substantia nigra. The local transcriptional upregulation of immune-related genes by brain cells, including astrocytes and microglia, may further compromise the accuracy of CIBERSORT’s analytical outcomes. (3) It is crucial to recognize that the immune crosstalk results derived from Spearman correlation analysis do not necessarily indicate direct biological interactions or co-infiltration. Consequently, the immune-related findings presented in this study require further validation through experimental techniques, such as immunohistochemistry or flow cytometry.

Conclusion

This study identified three hub genes (DDC, NEFL, and SLC18A2) linked to PD pathology, with SLC18A2 demonstrating high diagnostic accuracy. The findings highlight potential biomarkers and therapeutic targets but lack analysis on the applicability of these genes across different stages of PD or similar diseases. Further clinical validation is needed.
