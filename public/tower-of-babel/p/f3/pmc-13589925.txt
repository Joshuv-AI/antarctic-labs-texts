Neutrophil-mediated BDNF delivery for the treatment of moderate to severe Alzheimer’s disease
Authors: Feng Qin, Ni Zhang, Yanjiao Jiang, Kunyao Xu, Yang Xiong, Yuhang Liu, Qiuhong Ouyang, Haxiaoyu Liu, Yanping Dai, Linhong Jiang, Yue Zhao, Hongchun Li, Liang Wang, Yaxing Chen, Liqiang Hu, Qian Bu, Yinglan Zhao, Weihong Kuang, Meng Qin, Xiaobo Cen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlzheimer’s disease (AD), especially its moderate-to-severe stage (MSAD), remains a global health challenge with no disease-modifying therapies. Here, we identified a dual pathological signature in MSAD patients and murine models: a systemic pro-inflammatory milieu and critically reduced brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) levels in both the hippocampus and serum. To mitigate the decrease in BDNF, we developed an engineered neutrophil capsule, in which neutrophils carrying lipid nanoparticles encapsulating BDNF penetrated the brain and ameliorated cognitive capacity deficits in MSAD mice. The pro-inflammatory state in the MSAD brain facilitated neutrophil passage through the blood–brain barrier, and inflammatory mediators triggered the release of BDNF from the engineered neutrophils. Collectively, our findings show that engineered neutrophil-mediated brain delivery of BDNF efficiently reduces the amyloid-β plaque deposition in the hippocampus, leading to significantly improved learning and memory in MSAD mice. This study establishes neutrophil-mediated delivery as a paradigm strategy for MSAD, merging cell biology with nanotechnology to reverse neurodegeneration.Key words: Alzheimer’s disease, Brain-derived neurotrophic factor, Neutrophil, Blood–brain barrier, Amyloid-β, Neuroinflammation, Hippocampus, Learning and memory abilities

Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD), a progressive neurodegenerative disorder characterized by cognitive decline and neuronal death, affects over 50 million individuals globally, with projections exceeding 150 million by 20501. While current therapies, such as acetylcholinesterase inhibitors and N-methyl-d-aspartate receptor antagonists, transiently alleviate symptoms, they fail to halt disease progression2. Notably, approximately 40% to 60% of AD patients progress to moderate-to-severe stages (MSAD), with this population representing over 65% of long-term care residents and contributing disproportionately to the societal and economic burden of the disease3,4. Recent breakthrough drugs, such as Lecanemab, an anti-amyloid-β (Aβ) monoclonal antibody, are efficacious in mild AD but remain ineffective for MSAD, underscoring an urgent need for disease-modifying therapies targeting its pathology5,6.

Brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) represents a promising yet incompletely understood therapeutic target in AD7. Although isolated clinical studies have reported paradoxical upregulation8,9, the overwhelming evidence, including meta-analyses and cohort studies, confirms a significant reduction of BDNF in both blood and cerebrospinal fluid during mild AD10, 11, 12, 13. However, a critical gap remains: the trajectory of BDNF levels and its stage-specific neuroprotective effects in MSAD are systematically understudied. Clarifying this progression is vital to establishing BDNF’s relevance as a treatment target for MSAD. Preclinical interventions demonstrate that exogenous BDNF restoration rescues Aβ-induced cognitive deficits14, yet clinical translation faces insurmountable pharmacological barriers: its short serum half-life (<1 h), negligible blood–brain barrier (BBB) permeability, and rapid enzymatic degradation15,16. Conventional delivery strategies, including intracerebral infusion, viral vectors, and biomaterial scaffolds, are further limited by invasiveness, immunogenicity, or poor spatiotemporal control17, 18, 19. These challenges underscore the urgent need for a minimally invasive, BBB-penetrant platform capable of sustaining neurotrophic support across AD stages, particularly in understudied MSAD populations.

Neutrophil (NE)-based drug delivery systems offer a potential paradigm-shifting solution. As the most abundant circulating leukocytes (50%–70%), NEs inherently bypass the BBB via chemokine receptor-mediated transmigration, a process amplified in neuroinflammatory milieus20. Although neuroinflammation is frequently implicated in AD pathogenesis, whether it occurs in mild AD or MSAD remains controversial21, 22, 23. While elevated cerebrospinal fluid levels of interleukin (IL)-1β, IL-8, and tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α) have been reported24,25, their persistence in MSAD remains unverified. Critically, the therapeutic potential of NE-mediated drug delivery in MSAD requires further validation.

Here, we report an engineered NE capsule designed for the targeted delivery of BDNF to the MSAD brains. First, we identified a dual pathological hallmark in MSAD patients and murine models: systemic inflammation coexisting with critically depleted BDNF levels in the hippocampus and serum. To address the neurotrophic factor deficit, we developed lipid nanoparticles (LNP) encapsulating BDNF, which were phagocytized by NEs to create a cellular therapeutic (NE-LNP@BDNF, Fig. 1). We show that these engineered NEs selectively migrate into the inflamed AD brain, where they undergo inflammation-triggered degranulation and release BDNF to restore neuronal function. This work establishes NE-mediated delivery as a transformative strategy for treating MSAD, synergizing nanotechnology with innate immune cell biology to overcome the BBB barrier and reverse neurodegeneration.

Materials and methods

Gene expression profiling of brain tissue in MSAD patients

Transcriptomic data from AD patients and healthy controls were retrieved from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GSE48350), a publicly available repository hosted by the National Center for Biotechnology Information. This dataset, originally curated by Berchtold and Cotman at the University of California26,27, Irvine Institute for Memory Impairments and Neurological Disorders, comprises microarray profiles of four brain regions (hippocampus, entorhinal cortex, superior-frontal gyrus, and post-central gyrus) from three cohorts: (1) MSAD patients, (2) Age-matched controls, and (3) Young controls, sourced from seven U.S. AD research brain banks. Patients were classified as having MSAD based on concurrent clinical-pathological criteria: Mini-Mental State Examination scores ≤19 and Braak stage ≥ IV, reflecting advanced neurofibrillary tangle pathology28. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using the GEO2R analytical tool by applying thresholds of fold change (FC) > 1.25 and P-adjusted values (P-adj.) < 0.05. Principal component analysis: To assess global transcriptional variance across cohorts. Heatmaps: Hierarchical clustering of DEGs (Z-score normalized) to highlight region-specific dysregulation. Volcano plots: To illustrate the distribution of DEGs by statistical significance (–log10 P-adj.) and effect size (log2FC).

Clinical data and biospecimen collection in MSAD patients

This study was conducted with approval from the Biomedical Ethics Committee of West China Hospital (No. 2022-845), in accordance with the World Medical Association Declaration of Helsinki and the Ethical Review Measures for Biomedical Research Involving Human Subjects issued by the National Health Commission of China. Between December 2022 and June 2023, we recruited older adults (60–95 years) from the Mental Health Center at West China Hospital and local community-dwelling aged populations. All clinical records were de-identified to protect participant confidentiality, with research data stored in a password-protected institutional database accessible only to authorized investigators for follow-up and analysis. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their legal guardians when cognitive impairment precluded autonomous consent.

Inclusion criteria: (1) Age 60–95 years; (2) Capacity to complete assessments; (3) Availability of reliable caregiver (≥4 days/week); (4) Signed informed consent.

Exclusion criteria: (1) Participation in other clinical trials within 30 days; (2) Unstable systemic diseases (e.g., uncontrolled angina, active gastrointestinal bleeding); (3) Severe sensory impairment or substance abuse history; (4) Incomplete baseline data or serum samples; (5) Missing cognitive assessment results.

Clinical and demographic data were systematically collected to characterize the study cohorts. Epidemiological variables, including age, sex, education level, and marital status, were recorded along with medical histories (hypertension, diabetes, and coronary artery disease) and physical examination findings. Cognitive function was assessed using standardized neuropsychological instruments: the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) scores, Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR) scores, and Mini-Mental State Examination scores, administered by trained personnel under controlled conditions. Aged controls, mild AD, and MSAD participants were classified using culturally validated thresholds for Chinese populations29, 30, 31: Aged controls (education-adjusted MoCA ≥22, and CDR = 0); Mild AD (education-adjusted MoCA 10–16, and CDR = 1); MSAD (education-adjusted MoCA ≤13, and CDR ≥2). Peripheral blood samples (3 mL) were obtained via antecubital venipuncture using non-anticoagulant tubes and processed within 2 h post-collection. After clot formation (30 min, room temperature), samples were centrifuged (3000×g, 15 min, 4 °C) to isolate serum, which was aliquoted into barcoded cryovials and stored at −80 °C until analysis. This protocol ensured minimal pre-analytical variability and preserved biomarker integrity for downstream assays.

Serum biomarkers in MSAD patients

Serum levels of Aβ40 and Aβ42 were quantified using commercial Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay kits (Cat. NBP2-69909 and NBP2-69913; Novus Biologicals, CO, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocols. Multiplex cytokine analysis was performed using the Luminex platform with a customized Human Premixed Multi-Analyte Kit (Lot: L151846; R&D Systems, MN, USA), which enabled simultaneous quantification of: pro-inflammatory cytokines: Interferon-γ (IFN-γ), IL-1β, IL-2, IL-6, IL-12, TNF-α; anti-inflammatory cytokines: IL-4, IL-10, TNF-β; Chemokines: C–C chemokine ligands 2 (CCL2), C–X–C motif chemokine ligand 10 (CXCL10); neurotrophic factors: BDNF, glial cell line-derived neurotrophic factor (GDNF), nerve growth factor (NGF), neurotrophin-3. The analyte panel of inflammatory cytokines was selected based on established evidence from clinical studies demonstrating their pathological relevance to Alzheimer’s disease progression32,33. All assays included manufacturer-provided quality controls and were performed in technical duplicates with a coefficient of variation <15%. Serum samples were processed within 2 h of collection and stored at −80 °C until batch analysis to minimize pre-analytical variability.

Mice

Male amyloid precursor protein/presenilin 1 (APP/PS1) double transgenic mice (8-month-old and 18-month-old) representing AD phenotypes, along with age-matched wild-type controls, were obtained from Beijing HFK Bioscience Co., Ltd. (Certification SCXK 2014-0004). Adult C57BL/6J mice (6–8 postnatal weeks, 20–25 g) were also acquired from Beijing HFK Bioscience Co., Ltd. All animals underwent 7-day acclimatization in IVC systems under standardized conditions: 23 ± 3 °C, 55 ± 5% humidity, and 12:12 light–dark cycle with zeitgeber time synchronized to human circadian patterns (lights on 7:00–19:00). Animal welfare protocols followed the NIH Guide for Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (8th edition) and were approved by West China Hospital of Sichuan University’s Institutional Animal Care Committee (20220318011). Humane endpoints were established according to Association for Assessment and Accreditation of Laboratory Animal Care international guidelines.

Isolation of murine NEs from bone marrow

Bone marrow was harvested from the femur and tibia bones of euthanized C57BL/6J mice under sterile conditions. Marrow cells were flushed with ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), centrifuged (250×g, 10 min), and resuspended in 1 mL PBS. A discontinuous Percoll (Pharmacia, NJ, USA) density gradient was prepared by sequential layering of 75%, 65%, and 55% (v/v) solutions. The single-cell suspension was overlaid onto the gradient and centrifuged (250×g, 10 min), with NEs collected at the 65%–75% interface. Residual Percoll was removed by three washes with ice-cold PBS. Isolated NEs were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 1% penicillin/streptomycin at 37 °C under 5% CO2. For purity assessment, cells were stained with FITC-conjugated anti-mouse Ly-6G/Ly-6C (Gr1) and PE-conjugated anti-mouse MAIR-IV (CLM-5) antibodies (1:200 dilution in PBS). Flow cytometry analysis was conducted using a BD Fortessa X20 flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, CA, USA) and the BD FACS Diva™ software version 8.0 (BD Biosciences). The resulting raw data were subsequently analyzed using FlowJo software, version 10.8.1.

Preparation and characterization of LNP@BDNF

Liposome nanoparticles (LNP@BDNF) were prepared using microfluidics. In brief, an ethanolic lipid mixture containing hydrogenated soy phosphatidylcholine (HSPC), 1,2-distearoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine-N-methoxy-poly (ethylene glycol 2000) (DSPE-PEG2000), 1,2-dioleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine (DOPE), and cholesterol was rapidly mixed with an aqueous BDNF (Absin, Shanghai, China) solution at predetermined molar ratios (4:3:2:1, 5:2:2:1, and 6:1:2:1) in a microfluidic chip device, maintaining a 3:1 volume ratio. The resulting LNPs were then dialyzed against PBS using a 3 kDa molecular weight cut-off ultrafiltration device at 4 °C. Following the formulation process, the LNPs were stored at 4 °C and briefly sonicated (30 s) before use. The hydrodynamic diameter and surface charge of LNP@BDNF were measured by dynamic light scattering using BeNano 90 Zeta (Bettersize, Dandong, China) at room temperature. The surface elemental composition and chemical states of LNP and LNP@BDNF were analyzed by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, Thermo Scientific, MA, USA). The molecular structure and functional groups of LNP and LNP@BDNF were verified by proton nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy (1H NMR, Bruker ARX 400 MHz, Bremen, Germany). Concentration of BDNF within LNPs was determined using the BCA protein assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA). Free BDNF was separated from LNP@BDNF using ultrafiltration tubes with a molecular weight cutoff of 14 kDa. The concentration of BDNF in the dialysate was measured. Triplicate measurements were performed for each batch (n = 3). Morphological evaluation was conducted using a JEM-1400 microscope (JEOL, Tokyo, Japan) operated at 80 kV. LNPs were diluted to 0.05 mg/mL in deionized water, deposited onto carbon-coated copper grids, and negatively stained with 2% (w/v) phosphotungstic acid (pH 6.8). Grids were air-dried for 24 h before imaging.

Preparation and characterization of NE-LNP@BDNF

NE-LNP@BDNF were engineered by co-culturing NEs with LNP@BDNF. Fresh NEs were adjusted to a density of 1 × 106 cells/mL in sterile tubes and pre-conditioned in serum-free medium for 1 h to minimize nonspecific protein interactions. The cells were subsequently exposed to LNP@BDNF (75 μg BDNF/mL) at 37 °C for 2 h. Following incubation, unbound nanoparticles were removed by three sequential washes with ice-cold PBS. The resulting NE-LNP@BDNF were resuspended in fresh PBS and utilized immediately to ensure optimal cellular activity. Cellular uptake efficiency was quantified using a BD Fortessa X20 flow cytometer (BD Biosciences) controlled by BD FACS Diva™ software (v8.0). Data analysis was performed with FlowJo software (v10.8.1). To assess NE morphology post-engineering, 100 μL of NE-LNP@BDNF suspension was cytocentrifuged onto glass slides. Slides were fixed in methanol for 30 s, stained with Wright-Giemsa solution (Sigma–Aldrich, MO, USA) for 10 min, and then gently rinsed in deionized water. Air-dried samples were imaged with an Olympus BX53 microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). Morphological integrity was evaluated by three independent observers blinded to experimental groups.

To explore the availability rates of NE-LNP@BDNF after 24 h, the NE-LNP@BDNF were adjusted to 1 × 106 cells/mL in complete medium (RPMI-1640 medium with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin–streptomycin dual antibiotics) and incubated in 24 wells at 37 °C under 5% CO2. After incubating for 24, 48, and 72 h, cells were collected, centrifuged at 200×g for 3 min, and then resuspended in pre-cooled PBS. Then, 1 μL of FVS700 (BD Biosciences) viability stain was added to 1 mL of cell suspension, and the mixture was incubated for 10 min. After staining, the cells were washed once with PBS and finally resuspended in PBS. The availability rates of NE-LNP@BDNF were quantified using a BD Fortessa X20 flow cytometer (BD Biosciences) controlled by BD FACS Diva™ software (v8.0). Data analysis was performed with FlowJo software (v10.8.1). Meanwhile, NE and NE-LNP@BDNF were treated with the inflammatory inducer WKYMVm (10 nmol/L) and co-cultured as control groups. Triplicate measurements were performed for each batch (n = 3).

Evaluation of physiological functions of NE-LNP@BDNF

To assess the inflammatory responsiveness of NE-LNP@BDNF, CD11b surface expression was quantified via flow cytometry. Briefly, native NE or NE-LNP@BDNF were stimulated with graded concentrations of the formyl peptide receptor agonist WKYMVm (0, 1, and 10 nmol/L) for 30 min at 37 °C. Cells were washed thrice with ice-cold PBS, stained with PE-conjugated anti-CD11b antibody (20 ng/mL; BioLegend) for 30 min, and analyzed using a BD Fortessa X20 flow cytometer (BD Biosciences). Fluorescence intensity was normalized to unstimulated controls.

NE migratory capacity was evaluated using a transwell system (3 μm pore polycarbonate membrane, Corning, NY, USA). NE-LNP@BDNF or native NE (1 × 105 cells) were seeded in the upper chamber, while the lower chamber contained serum-free medium supplemented with WKYMVm (0, 1, and 10 nmol/L). Superoxide generation was measured using dihydroethidium (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). BDNF release from NE-LNP@BDNF in inflammatory microenvironments

The spatiotemporal release profile of BDNF from NE-LNP@BDNF was systematically characterized under physiological and inflammation-mimicking conditions. To simulate chemotactic recruitment to inflammatory foci, we employed the formyl peptide receptor agonist WKYMVm (10 nmol/L) in serum-free medium. NE-LNP@BDNF (1 × 106 cells/well in 24-well plates) were subjected to timed stimulation (0, 4, and 8 h), with subsequent quantification of intracellular versus extracellular BDNF pools using fluorescence spectrometry (Synergy H1, Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Real-time vesicular release dynamics were visualized using a dual-labeling strategy: Dir-labeled NE (NE/Dir) carrying LNP containing FITC-labeled BDNF were used to characterize these dynamics. Following phorbol myristate acetate (PMA, 100 nmol/L) activation in serum-free conditions, time-lapse confocal imaging (Leica SP8) captured the subcellular redistribution process. Cells plated in glass-bottom dishes (1 × 106 cells/dish) were counterstained with propidium iodide (PI, 5 μg/mL) for 30 min post-stimulation (0, 4, and 8 h). Three sequential PBS washes were performed before image acquisition under standardized laser power and detector gain settings.

Cell culture

Murine brain microvascular endothelial cells (bEnd.3, RRID: CVCL_0170) and human neuroblastoma cells (SH-SY5Y, RRID: CVCL_0019) were procured from Pricella Biotech (Wuhan, China); both cell lines were confirmed to be free of contamination. Cells cultured in DMEM and RPMI-1640 medium (Gibco, Grand Island, NY) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Cells were routinely screened for mycoplasma contamination using the MycoAlert PLUS Kit (Lonza, Walkersville, USA).

Biocompatibility and efficacy assays in vitro

Cytotoxicity screening: SH-SY5Y cells (1 × 104 cells/well in 96-well plates) were cultured for 24 h and exposed to BDNF formulations (free BDNF, LNP@BDNF, or NE-LNP@BDNF) for 24 h at 37 °C. Cell viability was determined using Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8, Cat# CK04, Dojindo Laboratories, Kumamoto, Japan): 10 μL CCK-8 reagent (0.5 mg/mL final concentration) was added per well, incubated for 3 h at 37 °C, absorbance was measured at 450 nm (Synergy H1, Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Aβ42-induced neurotoxicity modeling: Cells were treated with increasing concentrations of Aβ42 oligomers (0–30 μmol/L; A9810, Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) for 48 h. CCK-8 assays were performed as described above. Neuroprotective efficacy evaluation: To assess BDNF-mediated neuroprotection, SH-SY5Y cells were preconditioned with 20 μmol/L Aβ42 oligomers (48 h) to establish a toxicity model. Subsequently, cells were treated with BDNF formulations for 8 h, followed by CCK-8 analysis. All experiments included blank control (PBS only) and vehicle controls (LNP, or NE-LNP), with n = 4/group.

BBB penetration

The in vitro BBB model was constructed with bEnd.3 cells using a transwell culture system34. Briefly, bEnd.3 cells (1 × 105 cells/well) were seeded onto the upper chamber of the gelatin-coated (2% w/v) 3 μm pore transwell inserts (Corning, NY, USA), and cultured with complete medium (DMEM, with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin) for 7 days. Barrier integrity was monitored daily using a Millicell ERS-2 Voltohmmeter (Millipore, MA, USA), with transepithelial electrical resistance values ≥ 300 Ω cm2 considered indicative of mature tight junctions35. The formation of tight junctions was further confirmed by immunofluorescence staining for the key tight junction protein ZO-1 and for actin filaments using phalloidin. Three BDNF formulations were tested in triplicate: Free FITC-BDNF (0.75 μg/mL), LNP@FITC-BDNF (BDNF-equivalent dose), NE-LNP@FITC-BDNF (1 × 105 cells; BDNF-equivalent dose). Formulations were applied to the apical chamber, while the basolateral chamber contained serum-free medium with or without 10 nmol/L WKYMVm (Tocris, MN, USA), a formyl peptide receptor agonist. After 3 h incubation (37 °C, 5% CO2), compartmental analysis was performed: Basolateral medium BDNF levels quantified via fluorescence spectrometry using a FITC-BDNF standard curve (0.1–10 μg/mL, R2 > 0.99). Membrane-associated FITC intensity measured by BD Fortessa X20 flow cytometer (BD Biosciences) after trypsinization, normalized to unstained controls. Extravasated neutrophils in basolateral chambers were counted using a Countess II FL Automated Cell Counter (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and viability was confirmed by trypan blue exclusion (>95%).

In vivo and ex vivo imaging

Brain targeting efficiency of BDNF formulations was evaluated in 18-month-old male APP/PS1 double-transgenic mice (n = 3/group), a model of MSAD. BDNF was conjugated with Cy3 NHS ester (Lumiprobe) at a molar ratio of 1:3 (BDNF: dye). Mice received intravenous injections via tail vein: Free Cy3-BDNF (0.25 mg/kg); LNP@Cy3-BDNF (equivalent BDNF dose); NE-LNP@Cy3-BDNF (1 × 106 cells, BDNF-matched payload). Whole-body fluorescence was monitored using an IVIS Lumina III imaging system (PerkinElmer, MA, USA) at 0, 1, 2, 4, 8, 24, 48, 72, 120, and 168 h post-injection. Imaging parameters: Anesthesia: 2% isoflurane/oxygen mixture; Acquisition: Auto-exposure mode (typical exposure 0.5–5 s); Spectral unmixing: 570 nm excitation/620 nm emission filters; Spatial resolution: 150 μm. At 8 h post-injection (peak NE-LNP@BDNF accumulation phase), mice were euthanized by gradual filling of the chamber with CO2 at a displacement rate of 30%–70% of the chamber volume per minute, and perfused with ice-cold PBS (pH 7.4) to clear circulating nanoparticles. Major organs (brain, heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) were excised, rinsed in PBS, and imaged ex vivo under standardized exposure settings.

Isolation and characterization of human NEs-LNP@BDNF

Human peripheral blood NEs were isolated from EDTA-anticoagulated whole blood using a commercial human neutrophil isolation kit (STEMCELL Technologies, BC, Canada) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, 3 mL of whole blood was layered onto the separation medium and centrifuged at 400×g for 30 min at room temperature. Human NEs (hNEs) were harvested from the granulocyte layer, followed by erythrocyte lysis using ACK lysis buffer (150 mmol/L NH4Cl, 10 mmol/L KHCO3, 0.1 mmol/L EDTA, pH 7.4) for 5 min on ice. Cells were washed twice with PBS and resuspended in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 1% penicillin/streptomycin. hNE yield was quantified via trypan blue exclusion using a hemocytometer, with 1 × 106 NEs typically isolated per 1 mL of whole blood. hNEs purity was confirmed by flow cytometry using APC-conjugated anti-CD16 and FITC-conjugated anti-CD66b antibodies. All procedures involving human blood samples were approved by the Institutional Review Board of West China Hospital, Sichuan University (Protocol No. 2022-845) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained from all donors.

Culture and characterization of 3D cerebral organoids

Human induced pluripotent stem cells (hiPSC, RRID: CVCL_A4EF) verified to be uncontaminated, were cultured and differentiated into cerebral organoids using a modified protocol based on our previously established methods36. Briefly, hiPSCs were maintained in mTeSR™1 medium (STEMCELL Technologies, BC, Canada) on Matrigel-coated plates (1:100 dilution; Corning, NY, USA) under feeder-free conditions. Cells were passaged using ReLeSR™ at 80% confluency.

Embryoid bodies were generated by dissociating hiPSC into single cells (Accutase, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and seeding them in AggreWell™800 plates (STEMCELL Technologies, BC, Canada) at 3 × 104 cells per well in neural induction medium (DMEM/F12, 20% KnockOut Serum Replacement, 1% non-essential amino acids, 1% GlutaMAX, 4 ng/mL bFGF, and 3 μmol/L CHIR99021). On Day 5, embryoid bodies were transferred to ultra-low attachment plates and patterned toward forebrain identity using dual SMAD inhibition (10 μmol/L SB431542 and 100 nmol/L LDN193189) and 2 μmol/L XAV939 (Wnt inhibitor) for 7 days. Organoids were embedded in Matrigel (8 mg/mL protein concentration) on Day 12 to promote cortical layering and maintained in cerebral organoid differentiation medium (Neurobasal™-A, 1% N2 supplement, 2% B27 supplement minus vitamin A, 1% penicillin–streptomycin, 0.5% GlutaMAX, and 0.1 mmol/L β-mercaptoethanol). Medium was supplemented with 20 ng/mL EGF and 20 ng/mL FGF2 (PeproTech, NJ, USA) from 12 to 30 days, followed by gradual withdrawal to promote spontaneous neuronal maturation.

Organoids were cultured for 42 or 91 days (with medium refreshed every 3–4 days) to model early and mature neuronal networks, respectively. Organoid integrity and cortical structure were validated at 42 days by immunofluorescence staining for neural markers: astrocytes, neural progenitor cells, immature neurons, and cortical neurons.

Delivery of hNE-LNP@BDNF in 3D cerebral organoids

BDNF was labeled with FITC (Thermo Fisher Scientific) following the manufacturer’s protocol. FITC-conjugated BDNF was encapsulated into lipid nanoparticles (LNP@BDNF-FITC) and further engineered into human NEs-mediated carriers (hNE-LNP@BDNF-FITC) using the methodologies described in previous sections. Cerebral organoids (cultured in 6-well plates) were treated with: Free BDNF-FITC: 1.6 μL of BDNF-FITC (75 μg/mL) in 3 mL organoid medium; LNP@BDNF-FITC: 1.6 μL of LNP@BDNF-FITC (75 μg/mL BDNF equivalent) in 3 mL medium; hNE-LNP@BDNF-FITC: 1.6 μL of hNE-LNP@BDNF-FITC (75 μg/mL BDNF equivalent) in 3 mL medium. Organoids were incubated at 37 °C under 5% CO2 with gentle agitation for 8 h to ensure uniform distribution. Post-incubation, organoids were transferred to a 96-well plate using wide-bore pipette tips (to minimize shear stress) and imaged with a high-content imaging system (PerkinElmer, MA, USA). Fluorescence intensity was quantified to assess BDNF penetration and retention within organoid layers. Z-stack imaging enabled 3D reconstruction of BDNF distribution.

Animal grouping and interventions

18-month-old male C57BL/6J mice and 18-month-old male APP/PS1 transgenic mice (MSAD) were utilized. MSAD mice were randomized into five groups: WT + Saline: aged WT mice received intravenous (IV) tail vein injections of Saline (100 μL); MSAD + Saline: MSAD mice received IV Saline (100 μL); MSAD + NE-LNP: MSAD mice received NE-LNP (1 × 106 cells/mouse, 100 μL); MSAD + BDNF: MSAD mice received BDNF (0.25 mg/kg free BDNF, 100 μL); MSAD + LNP@BDNF: MSAD mice received LNP@BDNF (equivalent to 0.25 mg/kg BDNF, 100 μL); MSAD + NE-LNP@BDNF: MSAD mice received IV NE-LNP@BDNF (1 × 106 cells/mouse, equivalent to 0.25 mg/kg BDNF, 100 μL). All treatments were administered once weekly for four consecutive weeks. The BDNF dosage (0.25 mg/kg) was selected based on prior preclinical efficacy studies16,37. To ensure dosing accuracy, injections were performed by certified technicians at the national chengdu center for new drug safety evaluation, in accordance with standardized protocols.

Behavioral tests

All behavioral tests were conducted between 09:00 and 17:00 under controlled light conditions. Experimenters were blinded to mouse genotypes, and mice were randomized across groups. To eliminate olfactory cues, apparatuses were sanitized with 70% ethanol and super hypochlorous water between trials.

Nest-building behavior was evaluated as described previously with modifications38. Individually housed mice were provided three pre-weighed paper squares (5 cm × 5 cm, sterile kitchen towels) in their home cages. After 24 h, nests were scored blindly using a validated 5-point scale: 0: No paper shredding; 1: Scattered paper without bite marks; 2: Concentrated paper without bite marks; 3: Partially shredded paper with localized bite marks; 4: Fully shredded paper forming a cohesive nest.

The Novel object recognition (NOR) test was conducted as the reported procedure39. NOR test was performed in a rectangular arena (48 cm × 48 cm × 31 cm) under EthoVision 7.0 tracking (Noldus, USA). Protocol: Habituation (Day 1): Mice were allowed to explore the empty arena for 10 min. Familiarization (Day 2): Two identical objects (positions A and B) were introduced; exploration time was recorded over 10 min. Test (Day 3): Object B was replaced with a novel object. Exploration times for familiar and novel objects were measured over 10 min. Preference index was calculated according to Eq. (2):

The Morris water maze (MWM) test was conducted as the reported procedure39. Spatial learning and memory were assessed in a circular pool (150 cm diameter) containing a submerged platform (12 cm diameter, 1 cm below the water surface). Water temperature: 23.0 ± 0.5 °C. Training: 4 trials/day for 5 days (90 s/trial), with the platform fixed in the southwest quadrant. Mice that failed to locate the platform within 90 s were guided to it and given 15 s of reinforcement. Probe test: On Day 6, the platform was removed, and the mice were allowed to explore the pool for 60 s. Analysis: Escape latency, swimming velocity, and time spent in the target quadrant were tracked using ANY-maze software (Stoelting, IL, USA).

PET/CT imaging of cerebral Aβ deposition

The Aβ-specific radiotracer 18F-AV45 was synthesized and validated by the Department of Nuclear Medicine, West China Hospital, Sichuan University. The final injectate was confirmed to be sterile, pyrogen-free, and suitable for intravenous administration. Mice were randomly selected 24 h post-Morris water maze testing. Body weights were recorded for dose normalization. 18F-AV45 (6.5–7.5 MBq per mouse) was administered via tail vein injection. Pre- and post-injection syringe radioactivity was measured using a calibrated γ-counter (PerkinElmer, MA, USA) to calculate the precise delivered dose. Mice were anesthetized with 2% isoflurane post-injection. Animals were secured in a prone position on the scanner bed using medical adhesive tape. Positron emission tomography-computed tomography (PET/CT) scans were performed on a small-animal PET/CT system (Mediso, Budapest, Hungary) with the following parameters: PET: 12–13 min static acquisition, energy window 400–600 keV. CT: 50 kVp, 300 ms exposure, 720 projections for anatomical co-registration. PET data were reconstructed using a 3D ordered-subset expectation maximization algorithm (4 iterations, 6 subsets) with CT-based attenuation correction. Aβ plaque burden was quantified in the hippocampus and cortex using PMOD v4.2 (PMOD Technologies, Zurich, Switzerland). Standardized uptake value ratios (SUVRs) were normalized to cerebellar reference regions.

Genome-wide RNA-sequencing

MSAD mice were treated with either NE-LNP@BDNF or Saline as a control. Treatments were administered intravenously every 7 days for 4 weeks (n = 3 per group). Mice were sacrificed 7 days after the final dose. Hippocampus and prefrontal cortex were flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen. To evaluate the safety profile of hNE-LNP@BDNF, 42-day 3D brain organoids were treated with either hNE-LNP@BDNF (1 × 105 cells/organoids, equivalent to 0.75 μg/mL BDNF) or a Saline control. Therapeutic agents were added to the organoid culture medium once every 4 days for a total of four administrations. 3D brain organoids were flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen.

For both mouse brain tissues and 3D brain organoids, RNA sequencing was uniformly performed by Majorbio Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) upon treatment completion. Briefly, total RNA was extracted using TRIzol, followed by library preparation with the NEBNext Ultra RNA Library Prep Kit. Paired-end sequencing (150 bp) was conducted on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform, generating ∼20 million reads per sample. DEGs were identified using DESeq2 (|log2FC| > 1.5, FDR-adjusted P < 0.05), followed by Gene Ontology (GO) term and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses.

Immunofluorescence analysis

Mice were transcardially perfused with ice-cold PBS followed by 4% paraformaldehyde. Brains and 42-day cerebral organoids were cryoprotected in 30% sucrose, embedded in OCT compound, and sectioned at 12 μm thickness using a cryostat (Leica CM1950, Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany). For antigen retrieval, sections were incubated in citrate buffer (pH 6.0) at 95 °C for 20 min, followed by blocking with 5% donkey serum/0.1% Triton X-100/PBS. The detailed information on the antibodies used is shown in Supporting Information Table S1. Species-matched secondary antibodies were incubated for 2 h with DAPI nuclear counterstaining (1 μg/mL). Images were collected by confocal microscopy (Leica SP8, Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany).

Western blots

Mice were anesthetized with an overdose of isoflurane and transcardially perfused with PBS. Following dissection, the liver, heart, lung, spleen, kidney, and various mouse brain subregions were instantly frozen with liquid nitrogen. The tissue proteins were extracted using the total protein extraction kit (BC3710, Solarbio, Beijing, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions. After determining protein concentration with Bradford reagent (E211-01, Vazyme, Jiangsu, China), equal amounts of proteins were loaded and fractionated on SDS-PAGE and transferred onto 0.45 or 0.2 μm PVDF membrane (IPVH00010 or ISEQ00010, Millipore), immunoblotted with antibodies, and visualized by Western ECL Substrate (WBKLS0500, Millipore). Band intensity was quantified using Fiji. The detailed information on the antibodies used is shown in Supporting Information Table S2.

Safety evaluation

Healthy aged male C57BL/6 mice (18-month-old, n = 4/group) were randomly assigned to receive either NE-LNP@BDNF or Saline via tail vein injection. Animals were intravenously administered 100 μL of respective formulations once weekly for four consecutive weeks. Blood was collected 24 h after the final administration via cardiac puncture. Plasma was isolated by centrifugation (3000×g, 15 min, 4 °C) and analyzed for hepatic/kidney function markers: alanine aminotransferase, aspartate aminotransferase, alkaline phosphatase, urea, uric acid, and creatinine using commercial kits (Sigma–Aldrich). Major organs (brain, heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned at 5 μm thickness for hematoxylin–eosin staining. Histopathological scoring was performed by two blinded pathologists using a semi-quantitative scale (0: normal; 4: severe injury).

Statistical analysis

Data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) unless otherwise noted. For in vitro studies, experiments were independently replicated at least twice with 3–10 technical replicates per condition. Randomization was performed using the R package blockrand, stratified by baseline body weight, for murine studies. Blinding was implemented during data acquisition (e.g., behavioral scoring, histopathology) but not during statistical modeling due to treatment-specific biomarker assays. Statistical analysis was performed as described in the figure captions with GraphPad Prism 10.0.0 (GraphPad). Statistical analysis was performed using unpaired Student’s t-tests for two groups and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) or two-way ANOVA for multiple groups. Significance thresholds: P < 0.05 (∗), P < 0.01 (∗∗), P < 0.001 (∗∗∗), P < 0.0001 (∗∗∗∗).

Results

Inflammatory cytokines and neurotrophic factor in MSAD patients

We analyzed gene expression profiles from four brain regions (hippocampus, entorhinal cortex, superior-frontal gyrus, and post-central gyrus) using the Alzheimer’s disease dataset GSE 48350, which includes 16 MSAD patients (74–94 years), 23 aged controls (74–99 years), and 22 young controls (20–52 years) after rigorous quality control (Fig. 2A, Supporting Information Table S3). Screening for DEGs was performed across the four brain regions using thresholds of FC > 1.25 and P-adj. < 0.05. The results revealed that the hippocampus is the most dysregulated region in MSAD patients, with 1014 upregulated genes and 1616 downregulated genes compared to aged controls (Fig. 2B, Supporting Information Fig. S1). Among these DEGs, genes encoding inflammatory factors (IL-5, IL-11, and IL-15), CC chemokine ligands (CCL7, CCL17, and CCL22), and inflammasomes (NLRP3 and NLRP7) were significantly up-regulated (Supporting Information Table S4). Strikingly, BDNF exhibited significant downregulation in MSAD hippocampus (log2FC = −1.80, P-adj. = 0.0184), while expression of the genes encoding NGF and GDNF remained unchanged (Fig. 2C, Supporting Information Table S4). A subgroup analysis revealed no significant sex differences in the expression patterns of these inflammatory and neurotrophic factors in the MSAD hippocampus (Supporting Information Fig. S2). In a comparative analysis between aged and young controls across the four brain regions, we observed that the superior-frontal gyrus harbored the most prominent differential gene signatures (Fig. 2B). While inflammatory mediators showed consistent upregulation across all examined brain regions, BDNF expression was specifically reduced in the superior-frontal gyrus (log2FC = −0.77, P-adj. = 0.0064) compared to young controls, with no significant alterations detected in other neurotrophic factor-encoding genes across the remaining brain regions (Supporting Information Fig. S3 and Table S5). Critically, this study identifies a dual pathological hallmark in the hippocampus of MSAD patients: a hyperinflammatory microenvironment coexisting with critically depleted BDNF levels, distinguishing MSAD from age-related neuroinflammation.

Under approval from the Biomedical Ethics Committee of West China Hospital, Sichuan University (No. 2022-845), we established a clinical cohort of 41 mild AD patients, 44 MSAD patients, and 42 aged controls (60–99 years; Fig. 2D, Supporting Information Table S6). Serum Aβ42/Aβ40 ratios were significantly elevated in MSAD patients compared to aged controls (P < 0.01), correlating inversely with MoCA scores when age was accounted for as a covariate (r = −0.22, P < 0.05; Supporting Information Fig. S4). Inflammatory cytokines profiling of MSAD patients revealed elevated serum levels of TNF-α (1.29-fold; P < 0.0001), IL-6 (1.64-fold; P < 0.0001), and CXCL10 (1.90-fold; P < 0.0001, Fig. 2E, Supporting Information Fig. S5). TNF-α and IL-6 levels correlated inversely with MoCA scores (both P < 0.01; Supporting Information Fig. S5). Critically, MSAD patients exhibited markedly reduced serum BDNF (0.76-fold, P < 0.001), NGF (0.89-fold, P < 0.05), and GDNF (0.91-fold, P < 0.05) compared to aged controls, with BDNF levels significantly correlating with cognitive decline (P < 0.05; Supporting Information Fig. S6). Notably, BDNF concentrations demonstrated strong inverse correlations with pro-inflammatory mediators (all P < 0.05; Fig. 2F, Supporting Information Fig. S7), suggesting a reciprocal relationship between BDNF deficiency and systemic inflammation. These results demonstrate that the dual pathological signature in MSAD, heightened peripheral inflammation coexisting with critically depleted BDNF levels, mirrors the hippocampus deficits observed in MSAD patients.

Inflammatory cytokines and neurotrophic factors in MSAD mice

To investigate the interplay between neuroinflammation, neurotrophic deficiency, and cognitive decline in MSAD, we utilized a male APP/PS1 double transgenic mouse model with appropriate aging to model progressive AD pathology (Fig. 2G). We applied the nesting test to assess general health, and the NOR and the MWM tests to examine spatial learning and memory. Nesting ability as well as learning and memory were mildly impaired in 8-month-old mice, while these characteristics were severely impaired in 18-month-old APP/PS1 mice (Supporting Information Fig. S8). Based on the extent of cognitive impairment, the 8-month-old APP/PS1 mice were determined to be displaying mild AD neuro-behaviors, whereas the aggravated manifestation of cognitive impairment indicated that the 18-month-old APP/PS1 mice displayed MSAD characteristics.

Luminex profiling of hippocampal and prefrontal cortical tissue and serum was performed to investigate MSAD-specific pathophysiology, with respect to the levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6, IFN-γ, and CXCL-10), anti-inflammatory cytokine (IL-4, IL-10), and neurotrophic factors. Compared to 8-month-old wild-type (young WT) mice, 18-month-old WT (aged WT) mice exhibited elevated IL-6 levels in the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex (P < 0.05). Mild AD mice showed an increase in cortical IL-6 compared to young WT mice (P < 0.05). Notably, MSAD mice demonstrated significant hippocampal dysregulation across multiple cytokines (Fig. 2H–J, Supporting Information Fig. S9): IL-6 levels were higher compared to both aged WT and mild AD mice (P < 0.05), IL-1β increased versus aged WT and versus mild AD (P < 0.01); while IL-10 showed decreased (vs mild AD, P < 0.01). As compared to aged WT mice, the levels of BDNF were significantly lower in the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex in MSAD mice, while no significant changes in GDNF or NGF were observed across groups (Fig. 2K, Supporting Information Fig. S10). Together, these results suggested that, compared with WT mice, MSAD mice exhibited a more pro-inflammatory environment and lower levels of BDNF in the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex.

Design and functional validation of engineered NE

To overcome the intrinsic limitations of native BDNF therapy, including serum instability, poor BBB penetrance, and rapid enzymatic degradation, we engineered an NE-hitchhiking liposomal system (NE-LNP@BDNF). LNP were synthesized via microfluidic mixing using an optimized lipid molar ratio (HSPC: DSPE-PEG2000: DOPE: cholesterol = 5:2:2:1), encapsulating recombinant human BDNF with 85.32 ± 3.10% efficiency (Fig. 3A, Supporting Information Fig. S11). The successful LNP encapsulation of BDNF was further confirmed by 1H NMR and XPS analyses (Supporting Information Figs. S12 and S13). LNP@BDNF exhibited a monodisperse size distribution (89.54 ± 2.12 nm; Fig. 3B) and a negative surface charge of −12.54 ± 2.15 mV (Supporting Information Fig. S14). Sustained BDNF release was confirmed in simulated physiological conditions (37 °C, pH 7.4), with 50.09 ± 0.10% of the BDNF having been released at 7 days, a value similar to that observed under inflammatory conditions (48.92 ± 1.77%, Supporting Information Fig. S15).

Bone marrow-derived NEs were isolated from 8-week-old C57BL/6 mice and purified by density-gradient centrifugation, yielding 6.50 × 106 cells/mouse. Flow cytometric analysis confirmed the NE identity and showed a purity of >98% (Fig. 3C and Supporting Information Fig. S16). Co-incubation with LNP@BDNF (2 h, 37 °C) resulted in efficient cellular uptake (9.06 ± 0.32 μg BDNF/106 cells) without compromising viability (97.58 ± 7.80% vs untreated NEs, P = 0.91) or morphology (Supporting Information Fig. S17). Immunofluorescence imaging and flow cytometric quantification confirmed intracellular localization of LNP@BDNF (Fig. 3D and E). The viability of NE-LNP@BDNF was monitored up to 72 h. While viability decreased after 24 h, >40% remained viable at 48 h, sufficient for in vivo delivery given their rapid homing (Supporting Information Fig. S18).

We validated the inflammatory responsiveness of NE-LNP@BDNF by assessing its core physiological functions. CD11b, a surface protein critical for NEs adhesion and migration40, was significantly upregulated in NE-LNP@BDNF compared to native NEs, with further enhancement observed upon stimulation with WKYMVm (a potent activator of murine NEs, Fig. 3F)41. Transwell migration assays demonstrated that WKYMVm-induced chemotaxis of NE-LNP@BDNF matched that of native NEs, exhibiting concentration-dependent migration dynamics (Fig. 3G). Notably, inflammatory activation also triggered a comparable superoxide-generating capacity between engineered and native NEs (Fig. 3H). These findings collectively confirm that NE-LNP@BDNF retains the inflammatory-responsive functionalities essential for targeted delivery.

We further characterized the stability and cargo release dynamics of the BDNF formulation across pathophysiological environments. DiR-labeled NEs carrying LNP that contain FITC-labeled BDNF were used to investigate the characteristics, and PMA was applied to simulate the inflammatory factors42. DiR/FITC dual-labeling revealed very little LNP@BDNF leakage from engineered NEs under physiological conditions over 8 h (Supporting Information Fig. S19). Conversely, PMA-induced inflammation triggered rapid payload release, with 80% of LNP@BDNF liberated after 8 h of stimulation (Fig. 3I).

To evaluate the bioactivity of delivered BDNF, SH-SY5Y cells were challenged with toxic Aβ42 aggregated to establish an in vitro AD model (Supporting Information Fig. S20). First, we assessed the effect of NE-LNP@BDNF on the viability of normal SH-SY5Y cells. As shown in Supporting Information Fig. S21, treatment with NE-LNP@BDNF significantly increased cell viability compared to the control group. More importantly, we evaluated the neuroprotective effect in a classic in vitro AD model. When SH-SY5Y cells were challenged with toxic Aβ42 aggregated, intervention with NE-LNP@BDNF again led to a significant increase in cell survival compared to the injured control (Fig. 3J). Collectively, these results demonstrate that the BDNF delivered by our system is functionally active, promoting both neuronal viability and resilience against MSAD-relevant pathology.

Inflammation-directed delivery of engineered NE

To evaluate BBB penetration capacity, we utilized an in vitro BBB model based on murine bEnd.3 cell monolayers (Fig. 4A). The presence and localization of tight junctions were visualized through immunofluorescence staining for the specific marker ZO-1 alongside phalloidin staining for F-actin (Supporting Information Fig. S22). Under physiological conditions, all BDNF formulations exhibited negligible permeation (3.12 ± 0.29% basolateral delivery for NE-LNP@BDNF; Fig. 4B). Remarkably, inflammatory stimulation with WKYMVm triggered a 17.4-fold increase in NE-LNP@BDNF transcytosis (54.30 ± 1.38% basolateral delivery, Fig. 4C). Concurrent apical chamber depletion and stable intracellular levels suggested NE-mediated paracellular transport rather than transcellular uptake. Notably, WKYMVm stimulation induced no significant enhancement in transcytosis for free BDNF (3.56 ± 0.14%) or LNP@BDNF (3.78 ± 0.02%) (Fig. 4C), confirming its specific effect on NE-mediated transport. This inflammation-responsive behavior aligns with the physiological role of NEs in breaching vascular barriers during immune responses.

To evaluate the brain targetability of NE-LNP@BDNF in vivo, we measured levels of Cy3-labeled BDNF formulations in the brain of MSAD mice after intravenous injection. BDNF and LNP@BDNF were widely distributed throughout the bodies of the mice, the fluorescent label was eliminated with a short half-life, and no obvious fluorescence enrichment was observed in the brain. Conversely, after administration of NE-LNP@BDNF, the peak of the fluorescence intensity derived from Cy3-BDNF was reached at 8 h, and the fluorescence enrichment was still significantly observed in the brain at 5 days (Fig. 4D). NE-LNP@BDNF showed high BBB permeability, rapid brain uptake, and optimal brain retention time. At 8 h after NE-LNP@BDNF administration, the fluorescence intensity of the brain in NE-LNP@BDNF was 25.8- and 22.4-fold higher than that of LNP@BDNF and BDNF, respectively, and low fluorescence intensity was observed in the heart, spleen, and kidney following administration of NE-LNP@BDNF (Supporting Information Fig. S23). Moreover, NE-LNP@BDNF was mainly distributed in the hippocampus and cortical regions of MSAD mice, and was concentrated near the site of Aβ deposition (Fig. 4E, Supporting Information Fig. S24), thus achieving significant elevation of BDNF levels in targeted brain areas (Supporting Information Fig. S25). These results showed that NE-LNP@BDNF can penetrate the BBB in MSAD mice, delivering therapeutic cargo to the brain, especially the hippocampus and cortex.

To address the multicellular complexity of the neurovascular unit, we applied a 3D brain organoid model, which can recapitulate the brain characteristics under the neuroinflammatory environment, to further explore the processes and neurotoxicity of NE-LNP@BDNF delivery (Fig. 4F). Using an isolation method for human peripheral blood NE (hNE), 2.25 × 106 hNE were isolated from each 1 mL from human peripheral blood, with a purity of 89.0% (Fig. 4G). The purified hNEs were incubated with LNP@BDNF, and the resulting hNE-LNP@BDNF cells were collected by centrifugation. Flow cytometric analysis of FITC-labeled hNE-LNP@BDNF suggested that 99.0% of the hNE had taken up LNP@BDNF (Fig. 4H). Human embryonic stem cell-derived 3D cerebral organoids were differentiated using a previously published method36. Immunofluorescence showed high positive expression of astrocytes, neural progenitor cells, immature neurons, and cortical neurons in 3D cerebral organoids cultured for 42 days (Fig. 4I and J). Neural progenitor cells generate immature neurons that migrate radially into the developing cerebral cortex, forming a typical layered structure, a key feature of the early human cerebral cortex36. The BDNF formulations tested were added to the 3D brain organoids, which were cultured for 8 h. Notably, hNE-LNP@BDNF was delivered to a depth of more than 200 μm in the model, while BDNF and LNP@BDNF remained in the periphery of the model, penetrating only to depths of approximately 20 to 50 μm (Fig. 4K). This enhanced penetration persisted in mature 91-day organoids (Supporting Information Fig. S26), further demonstrating NE’s ability to mediate deep parenchymal delivery.

Therapeutic efficacy of engineered NE

To determine the therapeutic efficacy of the formulation, MSAD mice were randomly divided into five groups (Saline, NE-LNP, BDNF, LNP@BDNF, and NE-LNP@BDNF). The experimental scheme is shown in Fig. 5A. The BDNF dosage across the different BDNF formulations was 0.25 mg/kg, consistent with previous studies16,43. Based on the long brain retention time of NE-LNP@BDNF (>5 days; Fig. 4D), the administration regimen was once per week. NE-LNP@BDNF administration exhibited no effects on murine body weight throughout the therapeutic regimen, confirming its favorable systemic safety profile (Supporting Information Fig. S27).

In the nesting test, Saline-treated MSAD mice displayed a significantly lower nesting score compared to aged WT mice after four weeks of treatment, but NE-LNP@BDNF treatment significantly improved the nesting score (Fig. 5B). Similarly, in the NOR test, NE-LNP@BDNF treatment also significantly improved the discrimination index and preference index for novel objects in MSAD mice relative to Saline treatment (Fig. 5C). In the MWM test, all six groups achieved comparable escape latencies during the five training days (Supporting Information Fig. S28). Nevertheless, on the probe test day, mice that underwent NE-LNP@BDNF treatment spent a greater proportion of time in the target quadrant and made more platform crossings than Saline-treated MSAD mice (Fig. 5D and E). Together, these results indicate that NE delivers BDNF effectively to the brain and that NE-LNP@BDNF treatment significantly improves cognitive performance in MSAD mice.

To investigate the effect of NE-LNP@BDNF on eliminating hippocampal Aβ deposition, we used PET/CT to assess Aβ deposits by calculating the SUVR of 18F-AV45 (Fig. 5F), an ideal tracer for brain Aβ deposits44. The results revealed that the SUVRs in the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex of untreated MSAD mice were significantly higher than those in aged WT mice (P < 0.05), while NE-LNP@BDNF treatment significantly improved the SUVR in the hippocampus of MSAD mice (P < 0.05, Supporting Information Fig. S29). These results suggest that the therapeutic effect of NE-LNP@BDNF in MSAD mice is associated with is associated with reduced hippocampal Aβ deposition.

To further elucidate the mechanistic basis of NE-LNP@BDNF efficacy, we performed RNA sequencing of hippocampal and prefrontal cortical tissues from MSAD mice treated with Saline or NE-LNP@BDNF. Transcriptomic profiling demonstrated substantial hippocampus gene expression remodeling, with 928 upregulated and 1924 downregulated upon treatment, whereas the prefrontal cortex exhibited comparatively fewer DEGs (487 up-regulated; 300 down-regulated) (Fig. 5G and Supporting Information Fig. S30). GO analyses demonstrated robust enrichment of neuronal projection, synaptic plasticity, and learning/memory-associated pathways among the hippocampus DEGs. Prefrontal cortical profiling revealed analogous pathway enrichment, suggesting convergent molecular reprogramming driving cognitive rescue across anatomically distinct regions (Supporting Information Fig. S31). Subsequent KEGG enrichment analysis identified significant involvement of phosphatidylinositol 3 hydroxykinase–protein kinase B (PI3K/Akt) signaling pathways across both regions, suggesting common neuromodulatory mechanisms underlying region-specific therapeutic effects (Fig. 5H).

To validate the importance of the signaling pathways identified in transcriptomic profiling, we performed immunofluorescence and Western blot analyses of brain tissues. NE-LNP@BDNF treatment significantly elevated hippocampus BDNF protein expression (Supporting Information Fig. S32), reduced the Aβ plaque burden (Supporting Information Fig. S33), and enhanced synaptic density as evidenced by increased Synapsin I immunoreactivity (Supporting Information Fig. S34), correlating with observed neurobehavioral improvements (Fig. 5B–E). Moreover, the results significantly revealed impaired PI3K/AKT pathway activation in MSAD mice (all P < 0.05), which was substantially restored by NE-LNP@BDNF treatment (all P < 0.05, Supporting Information Fig. S35). Notably, cAMP-response element binding protein (CREB) is a key transcriptional regulator critical for the clearance of Aβ45. Critically, NE-LNP@BDNF did not exacerbate neuroinflammatory responses in the MSAD hippocampus; instead, it attenuated pro-inflammatory cytokine levels, as demonstrated by a reduction in IL-6 (19.4% decrease vs Saline, P = 0.07, Supporting Information Fig. S36), further supporting the dual therapeutic efficacy of the formulation in modulating both neurodegenerative and neuroimmune pathways.

Neurotoxicity and systemic biosafety of engineered NE

To evaluate the potential neurotoxicity of hNE-LNP@BDNF, 3D brain organoids (42 days) received four administrations of hNE-LNP@BDNF or PBS at 4-day intervals, followed by transcriptomic profiling. RNA sequencing revealed minimal differential gene expression between groups, with only 152 upregulated and 131 downregulated genes in hNE-LNP@BDNF-treated organoids (Supporting Information Fig. S37). Importantly, no significant enrichment was observed in neurotoxicity-associated pathways, including apoptosis or oxidative stress response. Notably, we observed a reduction in amyloid precursor protein (APP) expression (P = 0.02), a hallmark of neurodegenerative pathology, suggesting attenuated amyloidogenic processing. Collectively, these results revealed no detectable neurotoxicity in this human-relevant model.

To comprehensively evaluate the biosafety profile of NE-LNP@BDNF, aged WT mice received weekly intravenous administrations over four consecutive weeks. Makers of hepatic and renal function, including alanine aminotransferase, aspartate aminotransferase, alkaline phosphatase, plasma urea, uric acid, and creatinine, revealed no statistically significant deviations between Saline- and NE-LNP@BDNF-treated cohorts (Supporting Information Fig. S38). Histopathological assessment via hematoxylin–eosin staining further confirmed the absence of drug-induced histological abnormalities in the major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney, and brain; Supporting Information Fig. S38), establishing the compatibility of NE-LNP@BDNF with repeated systemic administration.

Discussion

AD is most frequently diagnosed at moderate-to-severe stages, when patients present with clinically significant cognitive decline and progressive memory deficits46. Despite substantial advances in understanding early-stage AD pathophysiology over the past two decades, therapeutic development has predominantly targeted populations with mild AD47,48. This focus has left MSAD, a phase characterized by extensive synaptic loss and neuroinflammation, with alarmingly few effective interventions49,50. Our study introduces a novel engineered neutrophil-mediated delivery method for BDNF, addressing two critical challenges in MSAD treatment: the inability of therapeutics to penetrate the BBB and the lack of sustained neurotrophic support in disease-affected regions. By leveraging the innate inflammatory tropism of neutrophils and their BBB-penetrating capacity, these engineered neutrophils not only overcome traditional limitations of systemic drug delivery but also integrate dynamically with the pathological microenvironment of MSAD, offering a dual therapeutic strategy that combines immunomodulation with neuro-regeneration.

A key strategy lies in the exploitation of MSAD’s pro-inflammatory milieu as a biological guidance system. Inflammatory mediators such as IL-6 and IL-1β, which are elevated in MSAD brains, act as chemoattractants for NEs, directing them to cross the BBB and migrate toward Aβ-rich regions. This self-targeting mechanism eliminates the need for external navigation technologies (e.g., magnetic targeting or ligand conjugation), significantly reducing off-target risks and systemic toxicity51,52. Furthermore, inflammatory triggers within the brain microenvironment induce the controlled release of BDNF from these engineered NEs, ensuring localized, on-demand delivery. This approach contrasts sharply with conventional BDNF administration methods, such as intracranial injections or viral vector-based gene therapy, which may suffer from poor spatial control, short half-lives, or immunogenic risks53,54. It should be noted, however, that the inflammatory signatures used for NE recruitment in this study, primarily induced by agents such as WKYMVm and PMA, represent an acute and simplified model of neuroinflammation. Although these tools are valuable for proof-of-concept studies, they may not fully capture the chronic, low-grade inflammatory landscape observed in human MSAD. Future work should aim to validate neutrophil homing in more physiologically relevant chronic inflammation models.

The observed reduction in hippocampal Aβ plaques and restoration of cognitive functions in MSAD mice underscore the therapeutic potential of these engineered NEs. The roles of BDNF in enhancing synaptic plasticity and promoting neuronal survival are well-documented, but the clinical translation of BDNF-based treatments has been hampered by rapid degradation and poor BBB permeability55. The LNP@BDNF prepared using microfluidic technology is produced under relatively mild conditions, via a simple, rapid process that is also easily scalable for mass production. By synergizing the migratory precision of NEs with the protective capacity of LNP, we achieved prolonged stability and targeted delivery, enabling BDNF to exert its neuroprotective effects precisely in degenerating regions. Notably, these engineered NEs did not exacerbate neuroinflammation, a common pitfall of immune cell-based therapies, but instead contributed to attenuating inflammatory cascades by down-regulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines (such as IL-6). This dual action, neurotrophic support and immunomodulation, positions these engineered NEs as a promising candidate for treating MSAD, where chronic inflammation and neurodegeneration are intertwined.

To further evaluate targeted delivery in a human-relevant system, we employed 3D brain organoids. While these organoids do not fully recapitulate age-related AD pathologies, such as amyloid plaque deposition, they serve as a robust, well-controlled human 3D cellular model for studying drug delivery into complex neural tissue. Using this platform, we successfully demonstrated the drug-carrying and tissue-penetrating capacity of human-derived NEs, highlighting a promising translational pathway toward clinical application.

Several advantages support the translational potential of this platform. First, the use of autologous or donor-derived neutrophils minimizes concerns about immunogenicity, as these cells are naturally equipped to evade immune surveillance. Second, the modular design of the LNP cargo allows for future integration of additional therapeutic agents, enabling personalized combination therapies tailored to MSAD’s multifactorial pathology. Third, the transient lifespan of neutrophils ensures self-limiting therapeutic activity, reducing long-term toxicity risks, a critical consideration for aged populations with comorbidities. Nevertheless, the ultimate fate of drug-loaded neutrophils in vivo, including their death pathways and clearance kinetics, remains to be fully elucidated and will be a key aspect of future safety assessments.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. Our preclinical results are promising; however, scaling up neutrophil isolation and drug-loading protocols for clinical use will require further optimization to ensure cost–effectiveness and reproducibility. In addition, although brain organoids enable precise analysis of drug distribution, current models lack endothelial cells and functional vasculature, reflecting broader limitations in organoid vascularization. Finally, the heterogeneity of neuroinflammatory profiles among MSAD patients, as suggested, may necessitate biomarker-guided patient stratification to maximize therapeutic efficacy. Future studies should also explore the long-term safety of repeated neutrophil infusions and assess potential off-target effects in non-brain tissues.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study establishes engineered NEs as a versatile and clinically viable strategy for MSAD treatment. By harnessing the body’s innate immune cells as living drug carriers, we bridge the gap between systemic delivery and brain-specific targeting, offering a paradigm shift in neurodegenerative disease therapeutics. As aging populations worldwide face escalating rates of MSAD, our strategy provides a timely and scalable solution that aligns with the urgent need for non-invasive, efficient, personalized, and patient-centric therapies.
