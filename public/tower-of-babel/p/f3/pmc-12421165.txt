Community pharmacists’ knowledge, practices and barriers in diabetes mellitus management during Ramadan in Jordan: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Huda Y Almomani, Esra’ O Taybeh, Tasneem Basheer Ali
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo assess the knowledge and practices of community pharmacists regarding managing diabetes in fasting patients during Ramadan and the barriers the community pharmacists face when managing diabetic patients who fast in Ramadan.DesignA survey-based cross-sectional study.SettingA structured questionnaire was distributed online during April and May 2024.ParticipantsCommunity pharmacists who are registered with the Jordan Pharmacists Association and working in Jordan.Primary and secondary outcomesThe questionnaire consisted of four sections: sociodemographic characteristics, pharmacists’ knowledge of diabetes management during Ramadan, practices in managing diabetic patients during fasting, and barriers encountered in providing care. The knowledge level was classified according to the total score of 13 as poor (0–4), intermediate (5–8) or excellent (9–13). Practice was classified into three categories based on scores: low (0–12), moderate (13–24) and high (25–36). Continuous variables were expressed as mean±SD, while categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages. Logistic regression was employed to determine the significant factors influencing the knowledge and practice of pharmacists.ResultsA total of 473 pharmacists participated in the survey. While 9.5% demonstrated excellent knowledge, the majority (72.3%) had intermediate knowledge, and 18.2% showed poor understanding of diabetes management during Ramadan. Key gaps in knowledge were in risk stratification, dose adjustment and patient counselling. Assessment of diabetes management practices revealed that most pharmacists demonstrated high practice levels (58.1%), while 35.1% showed moderate, and only 6.8% had poor practice. Assessment of diabetes management practices revealed that most pharmacists demonstrated high practice levels (58.1%), while 35.1% showed intermediate, and only 6.8% had poor practice. The least frequently performed practices were stratifying patients by their fasting risk level and adjusting medication doses for fasting patients. Pharmacists reported barriers to providing effective care, such as time constraints, high workload, lack of educational resources and insufficient clinical knowledge.ConclusionsThe study reveals moderate knowledge and high practice levels among community pharmacists in Jordan regarding diabetes management during Ramadan. However, gaps in medication adjustments and patient risk stratification remain. Continuous professional development programmes are needed to enhance pharmacists’ knowledge and skills in these areas. Strengthening pharmacy resources, such as access to updated guidelines and patient education tools, can support better care practices. Future research should explore interprofessional collaboration, compare practice settings and regions, and employ qualitative and interventional study designs to deepen understanding and improve management of diabetes during Ramadan.

Introduction

The global prevalence of diabetes mellitus (DM) is growing rapidly, creating additional challenges for populations around the world. The worldwide prevalence is expected to rise from 529 million in 2021 to more than 1.31 billion by 2050, with a notable increase expected in African and Middle Eastern regions,1 where there are large Muslim populations.

Ramadan is the holy month for Muslims during which they abstain from food, drink and oral medications from dawn until sunset. The duration of fasting typically ranges from 10 to 20 hours, depending on the geographical location. Fasting during Ramadan is one of the five pillars of Islam, and all adult Muslims are obligated to fast. In 2021, Muslims made up 24.9% of the global population,2 and out of the 529 million individuals with diabetes worldwide,1 an estimated 131.7 million people may have chosen to fast during Ramadan. Although many patients with chronic diseases are religiously exempted from fasting, most individuals with diabetes choose to observe the fast.3

Certain groups of diabetic patients face a higher risk when fasting, including those with type 1 diabetes, those on insulin, those with gestational diabetes, those with stage 3 kidney disease, those with a history of ischaemic heart disease46 and patients with coronary heart disease.6 7 These groups are subject to experience disturbances in glycaemic control such as hypoglycaemia and hyperglycaemia.5 This increased risk may be attributed to prolonged periods without food or drink3 and alterations in meal and medication schedules.8 9 Moreover, religious practices during Ramadan, such as Taraweeh prayers, which involve repeated physical movements at night, may further impact glycaemic control.10 Patients with diabetes who fast during Ramadan are also at an elevated risk of developing metabolic emergencies like diabetic ketoacidosis and hyperosmolar hyperglycaemic syndrome, dehydration, renal impairment, hypotension and thrombosis.5

It is well-established that pre-Ramadan risk stratification and diabetes education are crucial for safe fasting.4 5 8 11 12 These measures help adjust medication doses and monitor glucose levels, thereby reducing complications. Comprehensive guidelines for the management of diabetes, developed by the American Diabetes Association (ADA) and the European Association for the Study of Diabetes (EASD)1315 have been adopted and tailored to address the unique needs of patients observing Ramadan.16 The International Diabetes Federation (IDF) and the Diabetes and Ramadan (DaR) International Alliance have also incorporated these principles to create Ramadan-specific recommendations,17 18 which include pre-Ramadan risk stratification and practical guidance on glucose monitoring, nutrition, physical activity and medication adjustments. These adapted guidelines and recommendations are intended to help healthcare professionals provide safe and individualised care for people with diabetes during the fasting period.131518

Globally, evidence has shown that pharmacists improve clinical outcomes in diabetes through patient education, medication review, adherence support and promotion of self-care behaviours.1924 Studies have demonstrated pharmacists’ effectiveness in improving glycaemic control, reducing HbA1c, preventing treatment side effects and enhancing quality of life.1924 In Jordan, most pharmacists, particularly in community and hospital pharmacies, primarily focus on dispensing and inventory management, with limited provision of structured clinical services such as patient education, medication reviews and individualised pharmaceutical care.2527 Nevertheless, pharmacists in Jordan play a growing role in diabetes management, moving beyond medication dispensing to active involvement in patient care, education, screening and therapy optimisation.222830 Integrating pharmacists into diabetes care teams in Jordan leads to better glycaemic control, improved patient knowledge and cost-effective health outcomes.222831 Despite some positive outcomes from pharmacist-led interventions in Jordanian settings, barriers such as lack of time for pharmacists, insufficient continuous education and limited patient knowledge about diabetes prevention and management hinder the full integration of pharmacists into diabetes care.28 32 33

Pharmacists play a vital role in the successful implementation of diabetes care guidelines and the overall management of diabetes during Ramadan by providing medication management and patient education.3436 As accessible healthcare professionals, pharmacists are in a unique position to provide free of charge personalised advice on medication adjustments and regular glucose monitoring, and offer guidance on diet, meal planning and physical activity.34 35 They can also help identify patients at higher risk of complications and consequently provide comprehensive care to diabetic patients during Ramadan.37

Jordan, a Middle Eastern country with a majority Muslim population, had an age-adjusted comparative prevalence of diabetes at 14.8% among Jordanians aged 20 to 79 years in 2021,38 making it one of the countries with the highest diabetes prevalence worldwide. The prevalence has significantly increased from 13% in 1994 to 23.7% in 2017, with further increases expected in the coming years.39 As a result, a growing number of people may be at risk of complications from fasting during Ramadan in Jordan. Furthermore, research from Jordan shows that DM is poorly controlled, and the level of adherence to diabetes self-care practices—such as diet, exercise, blood glucose monitoring and medication adherence—remains unsatisfactory among diabetic patients.40 41 This highlights the critical importance of effective diabetes management and education, particularly during Ramadan.

While pharmacists’ knowledge and practices regarding diabetes management during Ramadan have been investigated in some countries,34 36 37 42 such studies are still lacking in Jordan. Given that community pharmacies are highly accessible and many patients in Jordan seek medical advice from pharmacists before consulting a physician,26 43 it is crucial to assess the level of pharmacists’ understanding and practice of diabetes management during this period. This assessment can enhance patient care by addressing patients’ daily needs throughout the fasting period. Therefore, this study aimed to assess community pharmacists’ knowledge level and practices concerning diabetes management in Ramadan alongside the predictors affecting pharmacists’ knowledge and practice, and the barriers to providing optimal diabetes management in Jordan.

Methods

The study is reported in accordance with the consensus-based checklist for reporting of survey studies guidelines.44

Study design and setting

A survey-based, cross-sectional study of community pharmacists was conducted using an online questionnaire during April and May 2024 in Jordan. A convenience sampling approach was used to recruit participants. Registered pharmacists with the Jordan Pharmacists Association working in community pharmacies were eligible to participate in this study. Researchers disseminated the survey to community pharmacists using Google Forms via social media platforms and community pharmacists’ contact details at the Jordan Pharmacists Association. The introduction of the survey stated that respondents’ participation is voluntary and anonymous. Pharmacists who clicked the ‘agree’ button were considered to have consented to participate in the study.

While this approach enabled efficient access to pharmacists across different regions of Jordan, it did not employ probability-based sampling and may limit the representativeness of the sample in relation to the overall population of community pharmacists in Jordan.

Survey development and flow of questions

The survey was designed based on the IDF, the DaR International Alliance, the ADA and the EASD guidelines1318 to manage DM during Ramadan and on an extensive literature review on barriers to providing pharmacist-led diabetes management34 45 46 and community pharmacy services in Jordan.4750 The survey was developed by HYA and EOT, who are expert clinical pharmacists in diabetes therapeutics, to ensure the appropriateness and comprehensibility of the survey items. The final version of the survey consisted of four sections and a total of 28 items.

The first section gathered information about the pharmacists’ sociodemographic characteristics and their work in community pharmacies and consisted of 11 questions. The second section involved 15 multiple-choice and true/false questions about community pharmacists’ knowledge of diabetes management during Ramadan. The first part of this section involved two questions and attempted to explore the sources community pharmacists use to obtain information regarding the management of patients with diabetes during Ramadan and whether this information was up to date. The second part involved 13 questions that evaluated pharmacists’ ability to identify patients at high risk for fasting, assessed knowledge about the risk levels associated with different health conditions and the impact of fasting on diabetes management, investigated the pharmacists’ recommendations regarding dietary choices during Ramadan and adjusted doses of diabetes medications. In the second part, the correct answer for each item of knowledge was given a score of one, whereas a wrong answer or answering ‘I am not sure’ was marked as zero.51 The total score of correct answers amounted to 13. The knowledge level was classified according to the total score: 0–4 scored as ‘poor knowledge’, 5–8 scored as ‘intermediate knowledge’ and 9–13 scored as ‘excellent knowledge’. The categorisation of knowledge into poor, intermediate and excellent levels has been previously used in studies assessing healthcare professionals’ knowledge in clinical contexts.52 53

The third section evaluated the practical actions taken by community pharmacists in managing diabetes for patients observing Ramadan. It contains nine items that focus on specific activities related to patient care during the fasting period: advising, medical assessment, risk stratification, medication and lifestyle education, self-monitoring of blood glucose, when and how to break the fast, adjusting medication doses and checking for medication side effects. Each activity is rated on a 5-point scale ranging from ‘Never’ to ‘Always’, indicating how often the pharmacists engage in these practices. A score of 0 was given to never, 1 to rarely, 2 to sometimes, 3 to often and 4 to always. The total score of correct answers amounted to 36. Based on the total score, practice was categorised as low (0–12), moderate (13–24) and high (25–36). The categorisation of practice into low, moderate and high has been previously used in studies assessing healthcare professionals’ practices in clinical contexts.54 55

The fourth section identified the barriers that community pharmacists face when managing diabetes for patients who observe Ramadan. It contains a list of potential barriers that pharmacists can select, such as lack of knowledge, resources, focus, high workload, time constraints, patient rejection, inadequate privacy and communication issues.

The survey items were initially formulated in English and subsequently translated into Arabic. The final version of the survey was then distributed in both English and Arabic to ensure better understanding among participants. A bilingual expert proficient in both Arabic and English reviewed the translation. The survey is available in online supplemental file 1.

Survey validation and piloting

The face and content validity of the survey items, including the knowledge and practice scores, was evaluated by three external experts—an internal medicine specialist and two clinical pharmacists (one of whom works in a community pharmacy and the other in an endocrinology clinic)—in collaboration with the research team. This process aimed to ensure the relevance, clarity and comprehensiveness of the survey items, as well as to confirm that the scoring approach appropriately reflected the study objectives. Based on their feedback, revisions were made to improve wording, ensure domain coverage, eliminate redundancy and validate the scoring of the knowledge and practice items.

Before distribution, the survey was piloted with 10 community pharmacists who met the inclusion criteria but were not included in the final study. The pilot aimed to assess the clarity, formatting and logical flow of the survey items. Participants provided feedback, which was used to make necessary modifications to improve the survey. For evaluating aspects such as the clarity of instructions, item wording and ease of administration, a sample of 10 participants is generally considered sufficient in the literature.56 The internal consistency of the survey items yielded a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.703, which indicated an acceptable consistency level of the survey items.

Sample size calculation

In this study, the online Raosoft sample size calculator was used to determine the sample size, with a 95% CI and a 5% margin of error.57 According to the Jordan Pharmacists Association, the number of registered pharmacists in Jordan was 33 504 in 2024.58 Therefore, a sample size of 380 was considered representative of community pharmacists working in Jordan.

Statistical analysis

Data were analysed using the SPSS software, V.25 (SPSS, Chicago, IL, USA). In relation to missing data, the online platform required responses for all key items, resulting in no missing data for primary variables. Therefore, no imputation or additional handling was necessary, and all completed responses were included in the analysis. Continuous data were normally distributed.

Continuous variables were presented as mean±SD; meanwhile, categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. The outcome variable for knowledge was categorised into three levels: poor, intermediate and excellent. Practice was classified into three categories: low, moderate and high. Logistic regression analysis was conducted to identify predictors of better knowledge and higher practice in diabetes management during Ramadan. Sociodemographic characteristics served as the independent variables. Results were reported as ORs with 95% CIs, and a p value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of our research.

Ethical considerations

This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the declaration of Helsinki and good clinical practice guidelines. The study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee at Isra University (SREC/23/06/081). Participation in the study was voluntary, and informed consent was obtained electronically. The survey introduction provided participants with information on the study purpose, anonymity and their right to withdraw at any time. Pharmacists who clicked the ‘Agree’ button at the beginning of the survey were considered to have provided informed consent. No personally identifying information was collected. The online survey was administered via Google Forms, which was configured to prevent the collection of email addresses and limit access to authorised researchers only. Survey data were stored securely in a password-protected file accessible exclusively to the research team. Data gathered was treated confidentially in line with the general data protection regulations.

Results

Participants’ sociodemographic characteristics

The sociodemographic information of the 473 pharmacists who participated in this study is presented in table 1. Most of the participants were aged between 22 and 35 years (80.8%). Females (69.8%) were predominant over males. The largest share (80.8%) had a bachelor’s degree in pharmacy. More than one-third (36.3%) had 2 to 5 years’ experience in community pharmacies, while only 12.5% had worked for more than 10 years in community pharmacies. With regard to the participants’ medical history, only 2.7% were living with DM. Moreover, 46.9% had a family member or spouse diagnosed with DM. Among the participants, 27.7% were smokers of cigarettes, electronic cigarettes or hookah.

Two-thirds of the participants (66.6%) worked 6 to 9 hours each day in community pharmacies while fasting during Ramadan. The largest share (37.6%) worked alone during their shifts, whereas only 8.9% worked with more than two pharmacists per shift. More than half of the participants (58.6%) worked in community pharmacies located in the south of Jordan.

Sources and recency of information on managing diabetes mellitus during Ramadan

Figure 1 illustrates the pharmacists’ sources of knowledge on diabetes management during Ramadan. The results revealed that 34.9% of pharmacists used training workshops or courses, while around one-third (33.4%) relied on social media platforms. Guidelines from the ADA—the 2005 and 2010 versions—were referenced by 30.9% of pharmacists, and 29.6% followed local guidelines. Published scientific papers were a source for 25.6% of pharmacists, and 23.7% used the IDF guidelines, including both the 2016 and 2021 editions. When asked about the recency of their searches for reliable information on diabetes management during Ramadan, around half of pharmacists (237, 50.1%) had searched within the last 3 months, 117 (24.7%) within the last year, while 29 (12.5%) had never conducted such a search (online supplemental file 2).

Pharmacists’ knowledge about diabetes mellitus management during Ramadan

Of the 473 pharmacists surveyed regarding their knowledge of diabetes management during Ramadan, the largest share of pharmacists (72.3%) exhibited an intermediate level of knowledge, while 18.2% demonstrated a poor level of knowledge, and only 9.5% were found to have excellent knowledge. Pharmacists’ knowledge of diabetes management during Ramadan is presented in online supplemental file 3. When asked which well-controlled patients with type 2 diabetes are at the lowest risk for fasting during Ramadan, around one-third of pharmacists (33.8%) incorrectly identified people taking multiple daily mixed insulin, while a significant share of pharmacists (30.4%) were unsure about this information. Around half of pharmacists (49.9%) were certain that patients with diabetes and unstable heart failure are not at low risk and should not fast, while 37.8% mistakenly believed they were at low risk. Interestingly, when pharmacists were asked whether pregnant patients with diabetes should be considered at moderate risk of fasting, only 27.3% were able to pick the right answer. A fairly good share of pharmacists (47.6%) were aware that diabetic patients with HbA1c >9% (11.7 mmol/L) have the highest risk for fasting during Ramadan, while 27.7% thought the risk was highest for HbA1c levels between 7.5% and 9%. Additionally, most pharmacists (86.1%) correctly recognised that the duration of fasting can influence hypoglycaemic risk, and 85.4% agreed that patients who choose to fast should measure their blood glucose levels several times a day. Furthermore, 80.1% of pharmacists correctly stated that high-risk patients should be advised not to fast. Less than half of pharmacists (46.3%) knew that patients should break their fast if their blood glucose level drops below 70 mg/dL or rises above 300 mg/dL, or when experiencing hypoglycaemia symptoms. However, 74.4% correctly understood that pricking the skin to measure blood glucose does not invalidate the Ramadan fast.

Regarding dietary recommendations, less than half of pharmacists (47.8%) correctly advised that patients should consume complex carbohydrates at Suhoor and simple carbohydrates at Iftar. When discussing the benefits of Taraweeh prayers, 44.0% of pharmacists were certain that performing these prayers can help patients achieve HbA1c targets and lose weight.

Finally, pharmacists were asked about medication adjustments during Ramadan. For a patient whose pre-Ramadan metformin dose is 850 mg three times daily, only 30.2% correctly recommended taking the first dose before Suhoor and the second and third doses after Iftar, while most of the answers were either false or ‘I am not sure’. For a patient on glimepiride 2 mg two times per day with well-controlled blood glucose levels, 20.1% of pharmacists correctly suggested taking the first dose at Iftar and half the second dose before Suhoor, while the majority did not know the correct recommendation.

The overall mean knowledge score for the pharmacists was 6.2 (SD 2.1), which is considered an intermediate level of knowledge. The analysis of mean knowledge scores out of 1.0 across the questions revealed that the highest mean knowledge scores of 0.9 were recorded for questions on the influence of fasting duration on hypoglycaemic risk and the necessity for patients with diabetes who fast to regularly measure their blood glucose levels. Conversely, the lowest mean knowledge score of 0.1 was observed for the question regarding whether pricking the skin for measuring blood glucose levels invalidates the Ramadan fast.

Pharmacists’ practices towards diabetes mellitus management during Ramadan

The analysis revealed varying levels of practice among pharmacists: 6.8% (n=32) demonstrated poor practice, 35.1% (n=166) showed moderate practice, while the majority (58.1%, n=275) exhibited high practice levels. Table 2 outlines the practices of pharmacists in managing diabetes during Ramadan. A considerable proportion of pharmacists consistently advised patients to seek medical assessment by their physician within the 2 months preceding Ramadan, with more than one-third (37.2%) always doing so, while 33.4% often provided this advice. When it comes to stratifying patients by their risk level for fasting, 24.3% of pharmacists always performed this task, and 36.1% did so often. However, only 27.1% sometimes engaged in risk stratification.

Pharmacists also reported educating patients frequently on various aspects of diabetes management during Ramadan. A noticeable proportion (41.0%) always provided education on medication usage, with another 31.3% doing so often. Similarly, 40.0% of pharmacists always educated patients on nutrition and physical activity, while 32.6% often engaged in this practice. Education on self-monitoring of blood glucose was always practised by 44.0% of pharmacists, while 31.3% often covered this topic. A total of 38.5% of pharmacists always educated patients on when to break the fast, and 38.9% always guided on how to break it when necessary. Additionally, 31.5% and 29.2% of pharmacists often performed these activities, respectively.

Among the pharmacists, 33.4% reported always making necessary adjustments to medication doses, and 29.4% often do so. However, some pharmacists rarely (8.2%) or never (7.2%) engaged in this activity. According to the survey, 37.2% of pharmacists reported always inquiring about side effects, while 32.6% admitted to doing so often. Nonetheless, 20.7% asked sometimes, 4.2% rarely and 5.3% never addressed medication side effects.

The analysis of the pharmacists’ practices score showed that the most frequently performed activities during Ramadan included educating patients on self-monitoring of blood glucose (mean score: 4.1, SD: 1.0), antidiabetic medications (mean score: 4.0, SD: 1.0) and nutrition and physical activity (mean score: 4.0, SD: 1.1). In contrast, the least frequently performed practices were stratifying patients by their fasting risk level (mean score: 3.7, SD: 1.2) and adjusting medication doses for fasting patients (mean score: 3.7, SD: 1.2).

Predictors of pharmacists’ knowledge about diabetes mellitus management in Ramadan

Logistic regression analysis identified two significant predictors of pharmacists’ knowledge about diabetes management during Ramadan: experience in community pharmacies and the location of the pharmacy (online supplemental file 4). Pharmacists with 2–5 years of experience in community pharmacies had 1.7 times greater odds of having better knowledge about diabetes management during Ramadan compared with those with less than 2 years of experience (OR=1.70, 95% CI=1.10–2.60, p=0.016). Additionally, pharmacists working in the Middle region of Jordan were 1.59 times more likely to have greater knowledge compared with those working in the South (OR=1.59, 95% CI=1.06–2.36, p=0.025).

Predictors of pharmacists’ practices towards diabetes mellitus management in Ramadan

According to the logistic regression analysis of predictors of pharmacists’ practices towards diabetes management during Ramadan (online supplemental file 5), having a postgraduate degree in pharmaceutical-medical sciences was the only significant predictor, with pharmacists having significantly lower odds of good practice than pharmacists with bachelor’s degrees in pharmacy (OR=0.16, 95% CI=0.04–0.63, p=0.00).

Barriers to practising Ramadan-specific diabetes mellitus management

The study identified several barriers that pharmacists face in practising diabetes-specific management during Ramadan (figure 2). The most frequently reported barrier was time constraints, as noted by 205 pharmacists (43.3%). Lack of resources, such as educational materials to provide to patients, was another significant barrier, reported by 197 pharmacists (41.7%). Additionally, 195 pharmacists (41.2%) mentioned high workload as a hindrance. Inadequate knowledge about Ramadan-specific diabetes management was mentioned by 189 pharmacists (39.9%). Further, patients’ rejection of diabetes management advice was a barrier for 142 pharmacists (30.0%), while 152 pharmacists (32.1%) observed that patients’ focus was negatively affected by fasting.

Discussion

To our best knowledge, this is the first study in the Middle East region that evaluated pharmacists’ knowledge, attitudes and barriers toward DM management during Ramadan among community pharmacists.

The majority of community pharmacists (342, 72.3%) had an intermediate level of knowledge about DM management during Ramadan (mean score 6.2±2.1%), with only 18.2% having a poor level. Our results contrast with previous studies, which found that pharmacists in Sudan42 and healthcare professionals, including pharmacists, in Singapore12 possessed sufficient knowledge to actively engage in DM care, while Qatari pharmacists exhibited a moderate level of knowledge of appropriate patient care during Ramadan fasting.37

Although pharmacists, in the present study, demonstrated a moderate understanding of the identification of moderate and high-risk individuals and whether they need to break their fast, there remain significant gaps in the stratification of low-risk patients and in the understanding of the medication dose adjustments. One possible explanation is that the majority of pharmacists feel comfortable advising high-risk individuals not to fast while keeping those with low risk to seek medical advice,42 so they can easily distinguish high-risk individuals. Additionally, the limited exposure to DM management in Ramadan during pharmacy undergraduate programmes, as well as insufficient opportunities for continuing professional development after graduation, can have an effect on the pharmacists’ knowledge and capacity to advise patients during Ramadan.26 Moreover, the introduction of specific treatment strategies to manage DM during Ramadan may contribute to the difficulty some practising pharmacists experience in keeping best practices.

Logistic regression found that pharmacists with 2–5 years of experience in community pharmacies were more likely to have better knowledge about DM management during Ramadan compared with those with less than 2 years of experience. This finding aligns with previous research,59 and it suggests that after a couple of years in practice, pharmacists have had more opportunities to deepen their understanding and apply their theoretical knowledge in DM management in real settings.

Additionally, the findings demonstrated that pharmacists working in the Middle region of Jordan had greater odds of having better knowledge compared with those working in the South. This can be justified by their engagement with a larger number of diabetic patients in the Middle region compared with the South. The higher patient volume provides pharmacists with more frequent opportunities to apply their knowledge, interact with diverse patient cases and stay updated with treatment guidelines. Wang and his colleagues60 confirmed that the lack of diabetic patients visiting the pharmacy has negatively impacted understanding and promoting diabetes care among pharmacists in China. A similar finding was reported by Salem et al,61 where the lack of diabetic patients visiting the pharmacy was identified as one of the most important barriers the community pharmacists encountered towards diabetes management.

We observed that the most frequent role of pharmacists in DM management during Ramadan revolved around patient education, particularly on self-monitoring of blood glucose. However, the least frequent activities reported were related to direct clinical interventions, particularly in adjusting medication doses. Similar results were noted regarding the attitudes and practices of Sudanese pharmacists with advising patients to monitor their blood glucose levels during fasting and non-fasting hours and medication regimen adjustments during Ramadan.42 These results are expected since modifying medication regimens without sufficient knowledge and confidence can put patients’ health at risk.36 Further, pharmacists’ willingness to adjust medication regimens is influenced by patient social pressure, perceived behavioural capability and perceived patient benefit.62

In the present study, having a postgraduate degree in pharmaceutical-medical sciences was a predictor of pharmacists’ practices towards DM management in Ramadan. This can be attributed to the fact that pharmacists with postgraduate degrees tend to focus more on theoretical or academic knowledge, often at the expense of practical experience, resulting in less engagement in direct patient care.

The most commonly reported barriers to practising Ramadan-specific DM management in community pharmacies are the lack of time and resources. Having a heavy workload limits pharmacists’ ability to engage effectively with patients. This time constraint, which is also reported previously,50 59 60 hinders pharmacists from offering comprehensive counselling and health services in managing chronic conditions like DM. Additionally, the lack of diabetes-related educational materials in the pharmacy prevents providing up-to-date information and support to patients.59 60 63

A main strength of the study is that it included responses from community pharmacists across multiple geographic regions of Jordan (north, middle and south), enhancing the diversity of the sample and providing a more comprehensive understanding of pharmacists’ knowledge, practices and barriers nationwide. Another strength is the well-structured questionnaire that covered different and clinically important aspects of the pharmacists’ knowledge, practices and barriers related to DM management during Ramadan. Items of the questionnaire to measure pharmacists’ knowledge and practices were derived from the most recent international guidelines to categorise fasting diabetic patients based on risk and to manage diabetes during Ramadan.

The present study has some limitations. First, as a cross-sectional design, it cannot establish causal relationships between variables; it only reflects associations observed at a specific point in time. Second, the use of self-reported data may introduce inaccuracies due to recall or social desirability bias. However, we attempted to minimise recall bias by collecting responses during Ramadan and the month immediately following it, when pharmacists’ experiences were still recent. Third, the online nature of the survey and voluntary participation may have introduced selection bias, as pharmacists who were more engaged, knowledgeable or comfortable with technology may have been more likely to participate. This could affect the generalisability of the results. In addition, the lack of a known denominator (ie, how many pharmacists received the survey) prevented us from calculating a response rate, introducing the possibility of non-response bias. Another limitation is that no technical restrictions were implemented to prevent multiple participation in the web-based survey. While participation was anonymous and voluntary, and there was no apparent incentive to submit multiple responses, the possibility of duplicate entries cannot be entirely ruled out.

As a result, the external validity of our findings may be limited, particularly in generalising results to all community pharmacists or to pharmacists practising in different healthcare settings or countries. Future studies might consider using probability sampling methods to enhance representativeness and reduce selection bias. Further, longitudinal or experimental designs are recommended to establish causal relationships. To enhance data integrity in similar future web-based surveys, researchers should consider implementing technical measures such as blocking duplicate IP addresses or limiting one response per device/browser. These precautions can help reduce the risk of multiple submissions and improve the reliability of collected data.

Conclusion

We found that community pharmacists in Jordan had moderate knowledge and practices regarding diabetes management during Ramadan, with several practice gaps and barriers that hinder the optimal delivery of pharmaceutical care to DM patients during this period. Future directions should focus on continuous professional development programmes to foster pharmacists’ knowledge, particularly in identifying low-risk patients and adjusting medication doses for fasting diabetic patients. Additionally, enhancing resources available in community pharmacies, such as patient information materials and updated clinical guidelines, can support better care practices.

Future research should also include comparisons across various pharmacy practice settings—such as hospital pharmacists and clinical pharmacists—as well as different regions in Jordan or other countries, to explore differences in knowledge and practice across diverse contexts. Furthermore, future studies should include other healthcare professionals, such as physicians and nurses, to explore interprofessional collaboration in the provision of diabetes care during Ramadan. Employing qualitative research methods, such as interviews or focus groups, may provide deeper insights into the barriers faced by pharmacists. Clinical trials could help evaluate the effectiveness of interventions aimed at improving diabetes management during Ramadan. Future work should also evaluate the integration of Ramadan-specific diabetes management within undergraduate pharmacy curricula.
