CAR-T Therapy in HIV: Pioneering Advances and Navigating Challenges
Authors: Xiao Wang, Jianyang Liu, Feike Hao, Tatchapon Ajavavarakula, Xuanling Shi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractChimeric antigen receptor T (CAR-T) cell therapy, renowned for its successes in cancer treatment, is now entering the field of human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) therapy, presenting both opportunities and challenges. With the emergence of broadly neutralizing antibodies, multi-target CARs, and T cell receptor–like antibodies aimed at increasing specificity in targeting HIV reservoirs, CAR-T therapy is synergizing with other cutting-edge treatments, including gene-editing technologies, therapeutic vaccines, and latency-reversing agents, in pursuit of a potential functional cure. In this review, we delve into the role of CAR-T therapy in HIV treatment, highlighting its potential to overcome clinical obstacles. We discuss advancements in targeting strategies within CAR constructs and the intricate regulation of T cell proliferation and chemotaxis. Moreover, we explore the use of diverse immune cells, such as gamma-delta T cells and natural killer cells. We review advanced biotechnologies, manufacturing innovations, viral mechanisms, and immune microenvironments. We also discuss the current research landscape and potential future applications of CAR-T therapy against HIV, which remains a critical global health challenge.

1. The Battle Spanning Over Fouty Years

Over the past four decades, the human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) has posed significant challenges to the medical community due to its complexity and resilience.[1] Since its identification in 1981, HIV research and treatment have undergone significant transformations. Antiretroviral therapy (ART) has fundamentally altered the prognosis of what was once a fatal disease, turning it into a manageable chronic condition.[2,3] Despite these therapeutic advancements, however, a functional cure remains elusive, compelling researchers to explore innovative strategies. Among these innovations, chimeric antigen receptor T cell (CAR-T) therapy shines as a beacon of hope, offering a potential paradigm shift in the battle against HIV. In this review, we explore the landscape of CAR-T therapy in HIV treatment, highlighting its exciting possibilities and the challenges that need to be addressed to harness its full potential.

CAR-T therapy, with its remarkable efficacy in treating hematologic malignancies, has sparked interest in its use against HIV. This pioneering approach involves genetically modifying T cells to express chimeric antigen receptors (CARs) capable of targeting and eliminating HIV-infected cells. The core principle of CAR-T therapy lies in its cutting-edge ability to modulate the immune system, priming it to identify and eradicate HIV-infected cells. Thus, this approach has the potential to overcome the limitations of previous HIV therapies.

Despite its potential, however, the integration of CAR-T therapy into HIV treatment has encountered formidable obstacles. The development of HIV-specific CAR-T therapies has been hampered by the complex nature of the virus, particularly its ability to evade immune detection and target the immune system itself. The high mutation rate of HIV necessitates a rethinking of conventional CAR-T methodologies. Achieving precise reservoir targeting to ensure therapeutic efficacy while minimizing collateral damage to the patient’s immune system is a critical challenge. To address this challenge, more precise targeting technologies, such as logic gated CARs, and meticulous fine-tuning of T cell regulation within the CAR-T construct are needed. Such efforts require an in-depth understanding of immune cell interactions and the broader immune response.

Integrative treatment strategies, such as “shock and kill” and “block and lock,” combined with advancements in latency-reversing agents (LRAs), stem cell transplantation, and induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC) techniques, have significantly expanded the scope of HIV-specific CAR-T therapy research. Concurrently, the utilization of diverse immune cells, such as gamma-delta (γδ) T cells and natural killer (NK) cells, alongside the integration of CAR-T therapy with other cutting-edge treatments, like gene-editing technologies, therapeutic vaccines, and antibody drugs, offers a multifaceted approach to HIV treatment. However, although these strategies can enhance the efficacy of CAR-T therapy in targeting HIV, they introduce new complexities, especially given the rigorous and intricate manufacturing processes involved. Therefore, while these strategies hold promise for a more comprehensive and effective targeting of the virus, extensive pioneering research is required to harness their full potential.

2. New Hope for an HIV Functional Cure

HIV was first reported in 1981, and the number of HIV-related deaths has exceeded 40.4 million, to date according to the WHO 2023 report.[4] Over the past four decades, the search for a cure or a safe and effective vaccine has faced considerable challenges.[5,6] Despite its effectiveness in controlling HIV-1 replication, ART cannot eradicate HIV reservoirs and is therefore a lifelong treatment. The primary obstacle is the existence of latent HIV reservoirs within infected CD4+ T lymphocytes.[7,8]

A functional cure for HIV should achieve sustained suppression of the virus to undetectable or very low levels in the absence of ART. In contrast to a s sterilizing cure—that is, the complete elimination of the virus and its latent reservoirs—a functional cure appears to be more attainable and promising in the clinic.[9] In 2009, C-C Motif Chemokine Receptor 5 (CCR5) Δ32/Δ32 stem cell transplantation achieved long-term control of the virus in a patient in Berlin.[10] Encouraged by this case report, many strategies have been proposed for an HIV functional cure and have achieved optimistic development, including bone marrow transplantation, neutralizing antibodies, therapeutic vaccines, and cell and gene-editing therapies in the past decade.

2.1. Approaches to an HIV Functional Cure

Infected resting CD4+ T cells are established early in the HIV infection process and are subsequently undetectable by the host’s immune surveillance. Though subject to host immune selection pressure, latent reservoirs are very stable, with an estimated half-life of up to 44 months, and can be replenished with low levels of viral replication, steady-state proliferation, or antigenic stimulation.[11,12] One viable strategy proposed to eliminate latent HIV reservoirs is termed “shock and kill.”[13] This strategy is based on the use of LRAs to reactivate virus replication, triggering cytolysis or immune-mediated clearance to recognize and kill infected cells. HIV expression can be activated through multiple pathways, including enhanced Tat-dependent transcriptional elongation, epigenetic block removal, Nuclear Factor kappa B (NF-κB) activation through toll-like receptors (TLRs), cytokine stimulation through common γ-chain receptors and the JAK/STAT pathway. These LRAs, viz. vorinostat, panobinostat, and romidepsin, and the TLR9 agonist MGN1703 show potency in inducing latent HIV transcription and increasing virion production in vivo.[14]

However, LRAs have failed to reduce the size of reservoirs, mainly due to the existence of deep reservoirs and insufficient establishment of potent antiviral immune surveillance.[15,16] To tackle this problem, recent studies have suggested that the “shock and kill” approach may need to be combined with immune therapies that enhance the clearance of infected cells. CAR-T therapy has achieved remarkable success in treating B cell malignancies[17] and has the potential to be used in anti-HIV immune therapy. Several clinical studies have shown that anti-HIV CAR-T therapy effectively reduces viral reservoirs in HIV-1-infected individuals.[18,19]

Another novel approach, known as “block and lock,” aims to control latent HIV reservoirs by inhibiting and permanently silencing HIV expression.[20] Unlike HIV reactivation, this strategy involves inhibiting HIV-1 expression through various mechanisms, including the inhibition of Tat-dependent transcription, blockade of NF-κB activation, suppression of the JAK/STAT pathway, and interference with other cellular processes, such as RNA splicing.[20,21] HIV-1-suppressing agents include didehydro-cortistatin A, flavopiridol, rapamycin, spironolactone, uprosertib, filgotinib, and ruxolitinib and more.[22,23 and 24]

Gene-editing technology provides an alternative method for directly interfering with the expression of HIV. The “Berlin patient” and “London patient” have achieved HIV-free status.[25] In both cases, this success can be attributed to hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) procedures using CCR5Δ32/Δ32 donor cells.[10,26] CCR5 is the primary HIV-1 co-receptor; thus, CD4+ T cells that express less CCR5 or the mutant CCR5Δ32 are resistant to HIV-1 infection.[27] To address the utilization of secondary binding sites, such as CXCR4, studies have knocked down CCR5, CXCR4, and TRIM5 using CRISPR/Cas technology.[28,29,30,31 and 32] However, this approach faces considerable challenges in achieving precise and reliable in vivo delivery, ensuring safety and efficacy, and minimizing off-target effects (Fig. 1).

2.2. CAR-T Therapy: A New Hope

CAR-T therapy has shown great potential in the treatment of autoimmune, fibrosis, senescence, infectious, and other diseases.[33,34] The CAR-T treatment process generally consists of six sequential steps: (1) patient pheresis, (2) isolation and activation of T cells, (3) expansion of cells, (4) introduction of CAR structures targeting HIV antigens into T cells using lentiviral/retroviral/mRNA techniques and subsequent mass expansion of CAR-T cells to achieve the necessary dosage, (5) quality control and formulation, and (6) reinfusion of cells into the patient (Fig. 2A).

Ten CAR-T cell products have thus far been approved for use in the treatment of leukemia. The fundamental principle underlying CAR-T therapy involves harnessing the robust cytotoxic capabilities of T cells in conjunction with the targeted recognition by antibodies to enable the precise elimination of affected cells. CARs are composed of three major domains: the ectodomain, the transmembrane domain, and the endodomain.[35] The ectodomain, which consists of single-chain variable fragments (scFvs) of monoclonal antibodies, facilitates antigen binding and recognition. The transmembrane domain has a lipophilic alpha-helical structure and serves as a stabilizing element, enabling CARs to anchor to cell membranes. The endodomain consists of an intracellular immunoreceptor tyrosine-based activation motif, which plays a crucial role in activating T cells (Fig. 2B).

The first generation of CARs, developed in 1993, was composed of an scFv of an antibody linked with γ or ζ chains. Its success was limited because CAR-T cells exhibited premature aging due to the lack of costimulatory signals.[36] This limitation spurred the development of second-generation CARs that integrate a costimulatory domain, such as CD137 (also called 4-1BB) or CD28, leading to improved effector function and prolonged persistence.[37] Most approved CAR-T products are based on this second-generation CAR structure. Third-generation CARs are commonly equipped with two costimulatory domains, such as 4-1BB, CD28, OX-40 (also known as CD134), CD27, NKG2D, and TLR2—most commonly CD28 and 4-1BB.[38]

The activation of second-generation CARs leads to the concurrent expression of transgenes, including certain cytokines, resulting in the development of next-generation CARs.[39] One example of next-generation CARs that add such intracellular co-expressed cytokine domains to the CAR construct is T cell–redirected for universal cytokine-mediated killing (TRUCK).[40] The synthesis and release of these cytokines can facilitate tumor eradication through various mechanisms, including exocytosis mediated by granzymes and perforin and the activation of death ligand receptor systems, such as Fas ligand and tumor necrosis factor-related apoptosis inducing ligand (TRAIL). Another next-generation CAR based on second-generation CARs adds a truncated cytoplasmic Interleukin 2 (IL-2) receptor β chain domain to the CAR endodomain that binds to the transcription factor STAT3. The activation of the JAK/STAT signaling pathway promotes CAR-T cell proliferation and increased in vivo persistence and antitumor effects.[41] To increase the efficacy and reduce the toxicity of CAR therapy, several CARs are currently under development, such as dual CARs, logic gated CARs, SUPRA CARs, and variants thereof.[42,43 and 44]

3. Advances in Preclinical Research on HIV Therapy

Concerted efforts have been channeled into exploring avenues for a functional cure for HIV. This endeavor has been strengthened by the significant progress made in CAR-T clinical trials for various diseases, which has reigniting global interest in using CAR-T therapy to combat HIV. This interest is reflected in the multitude of CAR-T clinical trials dedicated to developing a functional cure for HIV, although the outcomes have yet to meet expectations. Three critical areas of innovation shaping the future of CAR-T therapy for HIV treatment are discussed in the following subsections.

3.1. Increased Precision Targeting of HIV Reservoirs

In HIV research, considerable emphasis is placed on increasing the precision of CAR targeting to eradicate HIV-infected cells while minimizing collateral damage. The utilization of broadly neutralizing antibodies (bnAbs) to precisely target a wide array of HIV strains is reshaping the landscape of CAR-T therapy. Such novel strategies, including the use of multi-CAR and T cell receptor (TCR)-like antibodies, represent promising approaches to combating HIV.

3.1.1. Transition from CD4 to bnAbs

The strategic shift from traditional CD4-based targeting to bnAbs represents a revolutionary strategic shift, aims to achieve the precise identification and elimination of HIV-infected cells, especially those harboring the virus in the most inaccessible sanctuaries. While CD4-based HIV-specific CAR-T cells have demonstrated long-term safety in clinical trials, they have failed to meet expectations for anti-HIV responses, despite multiple optimization efforts.[45,46] Integrating bnAbs into CAR-T cell therapy in a major histocompatibility complex (MHC)-I-independent recognition manner offers a safer and more effective approach that extends from in vitro successes to in vivo trials.[47,48] The mechanism involving the disruption of gp120 and gp41 envelope glycoprotein interactions by soluble CD4 molecules, resulting in irreversible gp120 release, is well established.[49,50] Furthermore, minimizing off-target cytotoxic effects caused by nonspecific T cell activation through the dual targeting of HIV Env glycoprotein is a promising strategy for effectively reducing HIV reservoirs by protecting against diverse HIV-1 strains.[51]

3.1.2. Broadening Novel Targets

Research has shifted from envelope proteins to more conserved HIV proteins to target stable regions within the virus’s structure, particularly internal proteins that are less prone to mutation. This approach can enhance the precision of CAR-T cell recognition and the elimination of “shocked” reservoirs, contributing to the development of more robust and adaptable strategies.

The idea of using prototypes of CAR-T therapy against HIV emerged in the 1980s,[52] focusing on manipulating T cells through TCR gene-editing to trigger a specific cellular response. Initial clinical applications focused on using cytolytic CD8+ T cells genetically engineered to carry the natural HIV CD4 ligand.[53,54] Subsequently, various strategies were developed to get or modify TCR sequences. The HIVACAT T cell immunogen (HTI) sequence, informed by functional T cell response data obtained from T cell specificities predominant in low viral load cases, has demonstrated significant immunogenicity.[55] Other approaches involve immunogen designs that incorporate long conserved regions across viral proteomes, such as Gag and Pol.[56,57] Gaiha et al. explored a networked residues approach to identify the topological relevance of residues and cytotoxic T lymphocyte (CTL) epitopes within the HIV proteome and create an immunogen design accordingly.[58] Furthermore, studying recombination in immune escape mechanisms has provided valuable insights into epitope discovery and design.[59] These diverse approaches offer promising avenues for refining HIV targeting.

The direct use of the TCR in cell therapies, such as TCR-T, for HIV has encountered obstacles due to the matching restriction of human leukocyte antigen (HLA) types and the downregulation of MHC-I in HIV-infected patients. However, the emergence of TCR-like antibodies and bispecific T cell engagers (BiTEs) represents a breakthrough in targeting strategies.[60] BiTEs targeting HIV, particularly the variable heavy chain homodimers (VHHs) of llama single-domain antibodies against targets such as the CD4 binding site have the potential to be used in therapeutic applications.[61,62] Another approach involves bi-binding one domain to HIV-1 envelope proteins and directing the other domain to cells expressing the epidermal growth factor receptor to capture and redirect the virus.[63] Moreover, TCR-like antibodies with increased affinity for peptide-major histocompatibility complexes (pMHC) can improve the detection of low-density epitopes on cell surfaces.[53] However, while these antibodies alone have shown efficacy in inhibiting HIV-1 replication in vitro, their performance in real-world studies has fallen short of expectations. Combining TCR modification with cutting-edge technologies such as BiTEs and TCR-like antibodies, especially when synergized with a robust CAR platform, may achieve more favorable clinical outcomes.

3.2. Fine-tuning T Cell Regulation

The second wave of innovation centers on fine-tuning T cell regulation. Efforts are being made to optimize CAR-T cell activation and persistence while maintaining safety to avoid undesirable off-target effects and unintended activation parallel signaling pathways, especially in gene editing outside of the basic CAR structure.

Strategies for developing CAR-T cell therapies better suited to treating HIV infection, particularly by addressing challenges such as immune evasion, are being investigated. One notable innovation involves the optimization of CAR structures, such as third-generation CARs incorporating signaling domains from 4-1BB, CD28, and CD3ζ. A study integrating the costimulatory properties of membrane-bound chimeric IL-15 with CARs demonstrated improved T cell persistence and achieved a memory stem cell phenotype independent of CAR signaling.[64] Multi-target CAR designs have shown enhanced effectiveness against rapid mutations of a broad spectrum of HIV strains.[51,65] Studies using multi-specific CAR-T cells derived from people infected with HIV have demonstrated the ability to target sanctuary-infected reservoirs in animal models.[19,66]

Safety considerations, particularly regarding CAR-T therapies using CD4 ligands, are of paramount importance. Strategies using a controlled expression, such as the using of switchable systems and logic gated CARs, are being investigated to maximize therapeutic benefits while minimizing potential off-target effects.[67,68 and 69] One notable example is the “tumor-sensing” strategy, which destroys targets that express two antigens but does not affect those expressing one antigen.[70] Enhancement strategies provide control over CAR-T cells. Convertible CARs allow multiplexing with several anti-HIV antibodies through ligand–receptor interactions, offering greater breadth and control over their killing ability, which makes them promising tools for attacking latent HIV reservoirs.[71]

Gene-editing techniques are being leveraged to increase the specificity and efficacy of CAR-T therapy. One approach, involving the use of HIV-specific CAR-T cells expressing a PD-1 dominant negative receptor alongside a broadly neutralizing antibody, has shown significant cytotoxic activity against HIV envelope–positive cells, greater proliferation capability of T cells, and potent lytic responses to HIV-infected cells and latent reservoirs.[72] Complementing this, the integration of PD-1-blocking scFv E27 into CAR-T cells has further enhanced cytotoxic activity and overall efficacy.[73] Gene-edited TCR-deficient CAR-T cells have shown effectiveness without severe graft-versus-host disease (GVHD) in mouse models. However, targeting follicular dendritic cells within HIV reservoirs remains challenging.[74]

3.3. A Diverse Arsenal of Immune Cells

The third area of innovation focuses on the utilization of diverse immune cell types introducing additional arsenals of weaponry in the battle against HIV. For instance, engineered γδ T cells and NK cells are attracting increasing interest due to their unique ability to target HIV. The former constitute a minor but important proportion of peripheral blood lymphocytes, and their immunological and clinical implications for HIV-positive individuals have been extensively studied.[75] Elevated counts of these cells in patients, especially the delta TCS1+ subset, suggest their active involvement in HIV-related pathological processes independently of exogenous antigen stimulation. Moreover, NK cells, a cornerstone of innate immunity, play a prominent role in targeting HIV-infected cells. Their interactions, particularly with HIV-infected macrophages, underscore the importance of understanding cell-specific dynamics in HIV clearance processes.[76] The impact of NK cell homeostasis and function on HIV pathogenesis and the use of NK cells derived from human embryonic stem cells (hESCs) should not be overlooked in potential therapeutic strategies,[77] as they have the potential to be integrated into clinical approaches to controlling HIV. Indeed, CAR-engineered NK cells have shown enhanced anti-HIV-1 activity.[78]

Allogeneic HSCT with CCR5– donor cells have also shown curative potential, but their broad use has been hampered by associated side effects.[79] However, the use of CAR-T cells derived from hematopoietic stem/progenitor cells (HSPCs) represents a promising new avenue. This approach showed long-term engraftment and immune surveillance, along with an immune memory–like response, in a macaque model of Simian-Human Immunodeficiency Virus (SHIV) infection.[80]

Overall, the use of these diverse cell types may lead to advancements in HIV research. The strategic selection of specific cell types or subtypes is critical for enhancing therapeutic efficacy and increasing resistance to viral immune escape mechanisms.

3.4. Combining CAR-T Therapy with Other Cutting-Edge Therapies

The use of LRAs has been fundamental to inducing HIV expression in latently infected cells, followed by the deployment of immune effectors to eradicate the reactivated virus. This approach underscores the potential synergy between LRAs and CAR-T therapy. However, the real-world application of such combinations is challenging, considering the diverse LRA classes and their various presenting states to the immune system in vivo.[81] Due to the complexity of the microenvironments of viral reservoirs, the understanding of how these agents can be strategically integrated with T cell signals remains limited.[14,82] The impact of LRAs on immune function, especially on CAR-modified T cells and NK cells, is being examined to identify potential limitations and inform future therapeutic strategies.

Developing LRAs that can effectively target various cell types, including those residing in anatomical sanctuaries such as the central nervous system and lymph nodes, remains a high priority.[83] Crucial insights into the challenges associated with the blood–brain barrier gained from clinical studies of CAR-modified T cells are being used to elucidate the potential of this approach to target latent reservoirs.[74,84] Furthermore, gene-editing technologies, notably transcription activator-like effector nucleases (TALENs) and CRISPR/Cas systems, as a complementary strategy to eliminate unresponsive HIV reservoirs, may be incorporated together with LRAs.[85]

Another revolutionary approach is the combination of CAR-T cells and dendritic cell (DC) vaccines. DCs not only play a crucial role in presenting antigens but also facilitate the infiltration of T cells into sanctuaries.[86]In vitro experiments on co-culturing DC vaccines with MSLN CAR-T cells have shown promising results in terms of cell proliferation, differentiation, and cytokine secretion.[87] Another groundbreaking strategy has been devised to prevent antigen loss in CAR-T cell therapy. The in vivo vaccine boosting of CAR-T cells has been found to engage the endogenous immune system, preventing antigen-negative target escape and leading to DC recruitment, increased antigen uptake, and endogenous T cell priming—a process termed antigen spreading.[88] This combination offers new possibilities for advancing immunotherapy for HIV and achieving more comprehensive and durable responses.

4. Clinical Trials of Anti-HIV CAR-T Cell Therapy

Clinical trials have explored CAR-T therapies for HIV for more than 20 years.[89] The earliest attempt involved modifying CD8+ T cells to express CD4 as an extracellular domain, along with a transmembrane domain and the signal-transducing intracellular domain CD3ζ.[54] However, due to their susceptibility to HIV virus, this approach failed to control HIV replication. Subsequently, several treatment strategies were devised to enhance the ability of CAR-T cells to eliminate HIV-infected cells. In the following subsections, we discuss anti-HIV CAR-T therapy clinical trials and the challenges associated with developing a functional cure.

4.1. Initial Clinical Trials of Anti-HIV CAR-T Cell Therapy

As discussed above, CD4-ζCAR-T cells, known as first-generation CAR-T cells, showed significant anti-HIV activity in several in vitro studies.[52,90,91] Based on these promising results, three clinical trials of CD4-based CAR-T therapy were conducted (Table 1).[54,92,93] These trials demonstrated the safety of obtaining T cells from HIV-infected patients, transduced with CD4-CAR then reinfused back into the same patient after expansion and reported no significant adverse effects. Mitsuyasu et al. engineered autologous CD4+ and CD8+ T cells with CD4-ζCAR and infused them into HIV-positive subjects in a single dose with or without IL-2. The results showed that CD4-ζCAR cells could survive up to one year after infusion and migrate to rectal tissue and isolated lamina propria lymphocytes.[92] It is worth noting that the subjects enrolled in that study had CD4+ cell counts of >50 cells/μL and viral loads of >1000 copies/mL, which differed to the criteria used in the clinical study by Deeks et al. (plasma HIV RNA of <50 copies/mL and CD4+ cell count of >200 cells/μL). Following the principle according to which immunotherapy is most effective in patients with a low disease burden, Deeks et al. recruited subjects with undetectable plasma viremia. This may explain why, among these three clinical studies, only Deeks et al.’s study showed a noticed decrease in the HIV burden from the baseline after infusion with CD4-ζCAR-T cells.[93] Walker et al. collected syngeneic lymphocytes from HIV-negative identical twins for transduction with CD4-ζCAR and subsequently transferred CD4-ζCAR-T cells to their HIV-infected counterparts. The results indicated an important role of antigen-specific CD4+ T cells in helping CD4-ζCAR-T cells sustain long-term survival in the circulation.[54] Most importantly, all three clinical trials demonstrated the safety of CD4-ζCAR-T cell infusion, with long-term persistence in circulation/tissue, and the ability to migrate to associated tissues. However, there was no significant sustained reduction in viral loads or eradication of HIV from blood or tissue reservoirs.

Moreover, several potential drawbacks were identified in first-generation CD4-based CAR-T cells, which might reduce their efficacy. First, competition for HIV binding between transduced CAR-T cells and native CD4+ cells, which naturally express high levels of CD4, might lead to suboptimal targeting of HIV or HIV-infected cells.[80] Second, the use of CD4 as a targeting motif in CAR-T cells might inadvertently increase the susceptibility of these modified cells to HIV infection.[46,94] Third, the typically stable, low-level activation of CAR-T cells due to tonic signaling through the CAR construct might unintentionally increase their susceptibility to HIV infection.[95] Finally, the potential off-target effects of CD4-based CAR-T cells, specifically their undesirable reactivity to cells expressing high levels of MHC-II, presented a considerable challenge.[52,90,96]

4.2. Clinical Trials of New Generations of Anti-HIV CAR-T Cell Therapy

Based on the successful clinical use of CD19 and B-cell maturation antigen (BCMA) CARs against hematologic malignancies, a new generation of CD4- or scFv-based anti-HIV CARs was developed by several groups.[46,47,48 and 49,97,98 and 99] As in the case of first-generation of anti-HIV CAR-T cell therapies, these studies suggested that almost all new-generation anti-HIV CAR-T cells exhibited potency in eliminating reactivated HIV-infected cells in vitro. Based on these in vitro results, several clinical trials are currently in progress (Table 2). Liu et al. constructed a third-generation CAR moiety with scFvs derived from VRC01 and found that VRC01-based CAR-T cells effectively eliminated CD4+ T lymphocytes infected with reactivated HIV in vitro.[48] They also improved the CAR design and conducted clinical trials. Recently published clinical data showed that CAR-T therapy based on VRC01 was safe and well tolerated. However, because of viral escape mutations, viremia rebound occurred in all subjects. “Nevertheless CAR-T cell infusion resulted in reduced cell-associated viral RNA, intact proviruses, and viral reservoirs.[48] Mao et al. developed a fourth-generation tri-functional CAR-T cells armed with an effective HIV-Env engagement CAR moiety, a 10E8 derived scFv-Fc for clearance of cell-free virus and CXCR5 to promote cellular migration into the germinal center to target viral reservoirs.[100] According to their recent clinical data, infusion of M10 CAR-T cells dramatically reduced patient plasma HIV load spiking induced by administration of chidamide. Furthermore, M10 CAR-T cells significantly shrank the latent viral reservoir size through detecting the cell-associated HIV RNA level. It was similar to the data of Liu et al., M10 CAR-T cells exerted selective pressure on the evn gene.

To prevent CAR-T cell infection by HIV, an ongoing clinical trial led by Tebas et al. is attempting to disrupt the CCR5 gene by integrating a CD4-based CAR into the CCR5 locus.[101] In this study, the subjects were infused with autologous T cells genetically modified to express a CD4 CAR and cause a zinc-finger nucleases (ZFN)-mediated disruption of the CCR5 gene (Table 2). It is expected that CCR5-modified CD4-based CAR-T cells will show greater persistence in vivo and longer-term reduction of viral loads than CAR-T cells without CCR5 disruption (NCT03617198). Another ongoing clinical trial, conducted by Deeks et al., utilizes two distinct anti-HIV CAR molecules targeting highly conserved HIV-1 gp120 Env glycoprotein epitopes.[66] An associated preclinical study demonstrated that this CAR duo could be recruited to HIV infection sites in the spleens of humanized mice to suppress HIV infection (NCT04648046). It is hoped that this migration will allow CAR-T cells to eliminate reactivated HIV-infected cells in lymphoid tissues.

Several factors may influence the function of new-generation bnAb-scFv anti-HIV CARs. These factors include the binding affinity of scFvs to the antigen, the localization and expression density of CARs on the cell surface, and the distance between CAR-T cells and target cells, which is determined by epitope localization in the antigen.

The progression to next-generation CAR-T therapies reflects the evolution of the field, with each iteration bringing us closer to an effective therapeutic strategy for HIV. Although the journey from bench to bedside has been marked by both successes and setbacks, the enduring commitment of the scientific community continues to foster innovation in CAR-T therapy research.

5. A Glance to the Future

The considerable complexity of HIV reservoirs, especially latent reservoirs, poses a formidable challenge to the use of CAR-T therapy to develop a functional cure for HIV. Central to these reservoirs are the various subsets of memory T cells, such as effector memory T (TEM) cells, central memory T (TCM) cells, stem cell–like memory T (TSCM) cells, and terminally differentiated T (TTD) cells, which are influenced by their respective maturation and differentiation states.[102] The susceptibility of Th17-polarized cells and γδ T cells to HIV-1, coupled with their capacity to harbor latent proviruses, further complicates reservoir targeting.[11] These cells are located deep within lymph nodes or scattered peripherally. The presence of the virus in the brain poses additional challenges due to the blood–brain barrier and the unique cell types, such as microglia.[22] LRAs can eradicate latently infected cells by either inducing or inhibiting HIV provirus expression. The synergy between CAR-T therapy and LRAs represents a cutting-edge method for effectively targeting these elusive HIV reservoirs.[103] However, this synergy requires a thorough understanding of mechanisms to carefully balance efficacy and safety. A multifaceted strategy incorporating CAR-T therapy, LRAs, gene editing, and immune modulation shows promise but necessitates further exploration and clinical validation to fully exploit its potential to combat HIV.

The debate over using CD8+ T cells alone or in combination with CD4+ cells in manufacturing processes reflects the critical need to achieve a balance between therapeutic efficacy, HIV susceptibility, and CMC (chemistry, manufacturing, and controls) processes.[104] Such manufacturing decisions extend to fresh infusions with higher efficacy, which require more precise timing and more complicated logistics than cryopreservation, which offers scheduling flexibility and allows remote manufacturing but necessitates the careful management of cryoprotectants and controlled thawing procedures. Questions also concern the choice between gene-modified universal CAR-T (U-CAR-T) and MHC-independent cells. Key innovations include genome-edited disruption of the T Cell Receptor Alpha Constant (TRAC) locus and GVHD risk reduction.[105,106] Alternative genetic approaches, such as the protein expression blocker (PEBL) system, employing a retention signal linked to an anti-CD3ε scFv, are also being explored to suppress TCR expression.[107]

In the pursuit of an HIV functional cure, CAR-T therapy represents a combination of advanced biotechnology and strategic innovation. This undertaking is marked by the exploration of diverse immune cells, including γδ T cells, NK cells, hESCs, and iPSCs, providing HIV-targeting mechanisms beyond traditional T cell functions.[108,109,110 and 111] A key aspect of such innovation is the integration of advanced gene-editing technologies, such as RNA interference (RNAi) and CRISPR/Cas9, into the CAR-T framework to enhance precision and T cell functionality. CRISPR/Cas9 can be used to excise HIV proviruses from the reservoir genome or to target essential host cell genes, such as CCR5.[97] However, integrating these gene-editing technologies is associated with challenges such as low transfection efficiency and greater safety concerns.

The development of next-generation CAR-T cells for HIV therapy represents an important step toward addressing such challenges. These advanced cells are expected to possess enhanced features, such as multiple new targeting mechanisms and logic gates. However, a critical aspect that remains underexplored is the influence of glycosylation on T cell epitope recognition. Emerging evidence suggests that the glycosylation of viral proteins and epitopes plays a significant role in T cell responses to HIV.[112] TCR-like antibodies, which are intended to increase specificity and efficacy against rapidly mutating HIV strains, may play a key role in this exploration.[113,114] Concurrently, systems such as synNotch, STAR, on/off-switch CARs, and convertible mechanisms like ON/OFF switch to leverage multiple recognition are being explored to mitigate potential off-target effects and increase precision and efficiency of this powerful T cell weapon.[115,116,117 and 118]

Certain strategies proven effective against solid tumor models and HIV mechanisms may be adapted to target latent HIV reservoirs. For example, strategies such as enhancing CAR-T cell trafficking to lymphoid tissues by overexpressing CXCR5 or secreting extracellular matrix (ECM)-modifying enzymes (e.g., heparinase) to facilitate their infiltration into dense tissue microenvironments have shown promising results[119,120] Recently, multiplexed effector guide arrays (MEGA) have been developed for editing T cell RNA to enhance T cells’ metabolic processes, endurance, and persistence in attacking target cells. This CRISPR/Cas13d-based RNA-targeting approach enables temporary and reversible RNA and gene expression modifications to reduce the risk of adverse outcomes, such as cells turning cancerous by allowing reversibility of the RNA modifications.[121]

The in vivo programming of CAR-T cells using sophisticated delivery systems represents a paradigm shift in CAR-T therapy, eclipsing current labor-intensive, time-consuming, and costly ex vivo methods. Research has shown that the infusion of nanoparticles loaded with CAR-DNA or CAR-mRNA can facilitate either stable or transient expression of specific CAR proteins in circulating T cells without inducing systemic toxicities, such as cytokine release syndrome (CRS) and immune effector cell–associated neurotoxicity syndrome (ICANS).[122] Studies have also explored novel viral vectors and nonviral methods, such as exosomes, virus-mimetic vesicles, and nano-needles.[123,124 and 125]

The use of automated systems such as the CliniMACS Prodigy (Miltenyi Biotec) and the Cocoon (Lonza), along with cutting-edge production methods, such as FasTCAR and TruUCAR (Gracell) platforms and other non-activation or in-vivo CARs, has substantially enhanced the scalability and safety of manufacturing processes related to CAR-T therapy for HIV.[126,127] However, challenges remain due to the use of viral vectors such as lentiviral vectors, which are associated with risks such as insertional mutagenesis and cancerous T cells, as highlighted by the US FDA.[128] Accordingly, cutting-edge gene-editing techniques using adeno-associated virus (AAV) vectors, mRNA, and transposons are being explored to develop more precise and safer gene-modifying delivery systems.[129,130,131,132 and 133] To promote such developments, there is an urgent need for more streamlined manufacturing processes to increase the feasibility and accessibility of CAR-T therapies while maintaining high safety and efficacy standards through stringent quality controls.[134,135]

Future clinical trials of CAR-T therapy for HIV must be broad and include various patient groups and cutting-edge CAR-T cell designs, such as sequential infusion and multi-CAR approaches. Moreover, such trials must address not only therapies’ short-term efficacy and safety but also their long-term impacts and adaptability to HIV mutation rates and immune evasion. Determining which patients are best suited for CAR-T therapy requires more precise patient stratification and a better understanding of functional cure pathways. There is also a pressing need for improved model validation methods to accurately translate laboratory discoveries into clinical trials.

The manufacture of CAR-T cells is evolving with the integration of artificial intelligence (AI) and automation, which can enable smart manufacturing at hospitals and bedside infusion systems. Such approaches aim to address the traditional challenges associated with CAR-T cell production, namely length, labor intensity, and cost, by streamlining the manufacturing processes, enhancing data analysis and process optimization, enabling real-time decision-making, and ensuring consistent manufacturing quality.[136,137]

The field of CAR-T therapy for HIV is evolving rapidly, showing considerable promise for future treatments. However, achieving an effective and safe therapy requires continuous research and innovation. Overcoming the difficulties in targeting HIV reservoirs, leveraging novel immune cells and gene-editing techniques, and conducting comprehensive clinical trials can bring us closer to achieving a functional cure.
