Artificial Intelligence Empowers Physical Therapy for Older Adults: A Perspective
Authors: Haoqin Huang, Cai Zhang, Jie Hao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Physical therapy plays a central role in maintaining mobility and independence in older adults; however, increasing clinical complexity challenges the delivery of individualized care. Introduction

The geriatric population, commonly defined as individuals aged 60 years and older, presents growing challenges to global healthcare systems. Aging is associated with marked interindividual variability, high prevalence of multimorbidity, and complex interactions among physical, cognitive and psychosocial factors. Worldwide population aging has accelerated at an unprecedented pace, healthcare and rehabilitation system face increasing demand for chronic disease management, rehabilitation services, and long‐term care [1]. Older adults frequently experience multiple chronic conditions that require long‐term management, often accompanied by functional decline, increased dependency, and adverse mental health outcomes [2]. Consequently, healthcare systems must adapt to address the expanding need for geriatric‐specific services, chronic disease management, and long‐term care [3].

Physical therapy plays a central role in geriatric healthcare by focusing on the assessment, prevention, and management of age‐related impairments. The primary goals of geriatric physical therapy include optimizing mobility, maintaining functional independence, enhancing quality of life, supporting autonomy, and reducing the risk of injury and hospital readmission [4]. However, increasing clinical workload, patient complexity, workforce shortage and resource constraints pose significant challenges to delivering high‐quality, individualized care in geriatric rehabilitation settings.

In this context, artificial intelligence (AI) has emerged as a promising supportive tool to enhance assessment, intervention, and monitoring in geriatric physical therapy. AI refers to computer systems capable of mimicking human cognitive functions, including learning, pattern recognition, problem‐solving, and prediction [5]. In healthcare, AI enables the analysis of large‐scale, multidimensional clinical data to identify patterns, detect abnormalities, and support personalized care planning [6]. Applications such as machine learning algorithms, deep learning models, and AI‐powered conversational agents can assist physical therapists in clinical decision support, risk prediction, medical imaging interpretation, and rehabilitation monitoring. Machine learning refers to algorithms that learn patterns from data to generate predictions; deep learning is a subset of machine learning that uses multilayer neural networks to model complex, high‐dimensional data; and rule‐based (knowledge‐based) systems generate outputs by applying predefined expert rules rather than learning from data [7] By facilitating data‐driven and proactive care, AI has the potential to offer meaningful support in high‐demand and complex geriatric rehabilitation environments.

This article is a narrative perspective rather than a systematic or scoping review. Relevant literature was identified through thematic selection, focusing on the domains most central to geriatric physical therapy practice: fall prevention, osteoarthritis, frailty and sarcopenia, mobility monitoring, telerehabilitation, and AI‐enabled clinical decision support. The unifying perspective advanced here is that AI should be viewed as a supportive framework spanning the geriatric rehabilitation care continuum. Across assessment, risk identification, intervention planning, treatment monitoring, and long‐term management, AI‐enabled tools may help physical therapists integrate multidimensional clinical information and deliver more proactive, personalized care. This care‐continuum perspective provides the organizing framework for the present discussion.

AI Applications Across Major Geriatric Physical Therapy Domains

Falls represents one of the most significant health concerns in older populations. Falls are the second leading cause of unintentional injury‐related deaths globally, with approximately 37.3 million severe falls annually requiring medical attention [8]. Older adults account for a substantial proportion of fatal fall events. Age‐related declines in strength, balance, sensory function, and cognition, combined with environments that are often poorly adapted to aging, contribute to elevated fall risk.

AI techniques have been increasingly applied to identify fall‐related risk factors and predict fall risk among older adults [9]. These technologies support physical therapy practice by enabling continuous monitoring and early intervention strategies. AI‐based systems can classify daily movements, detect sedentary behavior, identify abnormal gait patterns, and provide personalized alerts and feedback through wearable sensors and machine learning algorithms [10]. Such systems contribute to proactive fall prevention and improved safety in both clinical and community settings.

Beyond fall risk, several geriatric‐specific clinical domains are central to physical therapy assessment and intervention and can be meaningfully supported by AI technologies. One such domain is osteoarthritis, a highly prevalent musculoskeletal condition in older adults characterized by progressive breakdown of articular cartilage, accompanied by subchondral bone changes, joint space narrowing, osteophyte formation, and varying degrees of inflammation. Osteoarthritis is frequently associated with persistent pain, reduced joint mobility, and impaired functional performance, all of which substantially affect participation in daily activities [11]. AI‐based approaches can support osteoarthritis management by assisting with imaging interpretation [12], longitudinal symptom tracking, and development of personalized exercise progression models [13] that adapt to pain severity and functional capacity, thereby facilitating individualized and responsive rehabilitation strategies [14, 15, 16].

Another key domain in geriatric physical therapy is frailty and sarcopenia, both of which reflect age‐related declines in physiological reserve and musculoskeletal integrity. Frailty is a multidimensional syndrome associated with increased vulnerability to stressors and elevated risks of disability, hospitalization, and mortality [17]. Sarcopenia is defined by progressive loss of skeletal muscle mass, strength, and physical function [18]. AI‐enabled systems can facilitate early identification and monitoring of frailty and sarcopenia by integrating data from wearable sensors, performance‐based measures, and activity monitoring to quantify gait speed, muscle strength, and movement patterns [19, 20, 21]. These data‐driven insights support targeted intervention planning and timely adjustment of rehabilitation programs.

Aging is commonly associated with reductions in habitual physical activity and mobility, which can accelerate functional decline and limit participation in rehabilitation. AI‐based platforms can objectively monitor activity levels, assess movement quality, and track adherence to exercise programs, providing real‐time feedback to patients and clinicians [22]. Furthermore, cognitive function and mental health significantly influence motor performance, balance control, and engagement in rehabilitation. AI technologies that integrate movement, behavioral, and interaction data, and that provide motivational or conversational support, may help address cognitive and psychosocial barriers [23], thereby promoting sustained participation and more holistic geriatric rehabilitation care.

AI Across the Geriatric Care Continuum

Across the geriatric care continuum, from inpatient and outpatient rehabilitation to long‐term care facilities, artificial intelligence plays an increasingly important role in supporting assessment, monitoring, and care coordination. Older adults often require ongoing surveillance to ensure safety and timely medical intervention, which places substantial demands on caregivers and healthcare providers. In this context, AI‐based monitoring systems function as supplementary observational tools by continuously tracking mobility patterns, activity levels, behavioral changes, and selected physiological indicators, while detecting abnormal signals that may indicate increased health or safety risks.

By reducing reliance on constant in‐person supervision, these systems enable healthcare providers to allocate clinical resources more efficiently and prioritize individuals who require immediate intervention. Many contemporary AI platforms enable caregivers to remotely monitor health status and functional activity through mobile applications, while older adults can access emergency assistance using voice‐activated technologies such as smart home devices [24, 25]. In rehabilitation settings, AI supports continuous assessment of functional recovery and therapy progression [26], whereas in long‐term care facilities it enhances safety monitoring and early detection of functional decline [27].

Beyond monitoring, AI demonstrates strong capacity to process complex, multidimensional data, enabling a more comprehensive understanding of geriatric syndromes through integrated analysis of mobility, behavior, and health indicators [28, 29]. Long‐term gait monitoring and movement analysis supported by AI can improve diagnostic accuracy, support continuous functional assessment, and inform personalized therapeutic interventions [30]. By identifying subtle abnormalities and generating timely alerts, AI‐supported systems enhance clinical decision‐making, promote individualized rehabilitation planning, reduce caregiver burden, and ultimately contribute to improved patient safety and quality of life.

The capacity of AI to integrate and analyze multidimensional data may be particularly valuable for older adults with cognitive impairment, including dementia. Beyond physical limitations, cognitive decline can adversely affect mobility, balance, safety awareness, exercise adherence, and overall functional independence, creating additional challenges for rehabilitation. Although AI cannot alter the underlying progression of dementia, it may support care through continuous monitoring of gait, physical activity, behavioral patterns, and fall risk, enabling earlier identification of functional decline and more timely intervention. Systems such as Affectiva can analyze emotional and cognitive states to detect signs of distress, fatigue, or reduced attention, potentially prompting more timely support from caregivers and clinicians. AI applications have also been developed to address the cognitive and communication challenges associated with dementia: for example, Egocentric Live 4D Perception has been proposed to assist with memory‐related tasks such as locating misplaced objects and navigating daily activities, while Project Relate aims to facilitate communication for individuals with speech impairments through AI‐assisted speech recognition. AI‐enabled technologies may also provide personalized reminders, cueing strategies, and conversational support to encourage participation in rehabilitation and daily activities [31].

Furthermore, AI designed to encourage communication and social engagement may help reduce isolation and improve overall well‐being. Together, these capabilities suggest that AI may contribute to more comprehensive and individualized rehabilitation management for older adults living with cognitive impairment [31].

AI‐Enabled Tools and Technologies

Rapid technological advancement has substantially expanded the range of AI‐enabled tools available to support geriatric rehabilitation. These technologies span multiple levels of care delivery, including remote service provision, immersive training environments, behavioral support systems, and data‐driven clinical decision support. Collectively, they enhance access to care, promote engagement, and enable more personalized rehabilitation strategies for older adults.

One major category of AI‐enabled rehabilitation technology is telerehabilitation and remote monitoring. Telerehabilitation involves the delivery of rehabilitation services through video conferencing, mobile applications, and virtual platforms, allowing individuals to maintain or regain function without requiring in‐person clinic visits. This approach improves access to rehabilitation services for populations limited by geographic distance, financial constraints, or resource availability. Although telerehabilitation expanded rapidly during the COVID‐19 pandemic and is often viewed as a complementary mode of service delivery, accumulating evidence suggests that its effectiveness is comparable to traditional in‐person rehabilitation for many conditions and outcomes [32].

Virtual reality (VR) represents another important AI‐enabled rehabilitation modality. VR systems create immersive, computer‐generated three‐dimensional environments using head‐mounted displays and controllers to support task‐specific training. These environments have been shown to improve static balance, dynamic balance, and gait performance in older adults [33]. Importantly, VR allows therapists to manipulate task difficulty and environmental complexity in real time, enabling individualized progression while maintaining patient safety. By enhancing motivation and providing precise control over training parameters, VR serves as a promising adjunct to conventional balance and gait rehabilitation in geriatric populations [34].

Beyond physical training technologies, AI‐driven conversational agents and virtual companions have emerged as tools to support behavioral engagement, education, and emotional well‐being [35, 36]. These systems can encourage greater participation, longer training duration, and improved adherence to home exercise programs. In addition, AI companions have been associated with benefits related to sleep quality, medication adherence, and overall well‐being [22]. While such tools cannot replace the clinical expertise of physical therapists, they can augment home‐based rehabilitation programs and support sustained participation, particularly for older adults with limited social support.

Finally, predictive analytics and personalized care systems represent a critical application of AI in geriatric physical therapy. By analyzing large datasets derived from clinical records, wearable sensors, and standardized assessments, AI systems can generate individualized predictions related to risk profiles, recovery trajectories, and functional outcomes [36]. These analytics support physical therapists in tailoring interventions, optimizing treatment timing and intensity, and facilitating shared decision‐making, thereby promoting more precise and patient‐centered rehabilitation care.

Ethical Considerations and Challenges

Despite the potential benefits of artificial intelligence in geriatric rehabilitation, several ethical and practical challenges limit its widespread implementation. Key ethical concerns include data privacy and security, as AI systems often rely on continuous collection and analysis of sensitive personal and health‐related data. Robust data governance and secure data management frameworks are essential to protect patient confidentiality and maintain trust.

Algorithmic bias and fairness also remain critical issues. AI models trained on non‐representative datasets may produce biased predictions, potentially disadvantaging subgroups of older adults with complex comorbidities, cognitive impairment, or diverse socioeconomic backgrounds. In addition, limited transparency and interpretability of AI‐generated outputs may hinder clinician trust and complicate clinical decision‐making in rehabilitation contexts that require nuanced judgment.

From an implementation perspective, accessibility and usability pose significant barriers. Many older adults face challenges related to cost, limited digital literacy, sensory impairments, and reluctance to adopt unfamiliar technologies. Furthermore, effective clinical integration requires that AI tools complement, rather than replace, professional expertise, with clear accountability and adequate clinician training. Addressing these ethical and practical challenges is essential to ensure the responsible, equitable, and effective use of AI in geriatric rehabilitation.

A further consideration concerns the strength of the current evidence base supporting AI applications in geriatric rehabilitation. Although many studies have demonstrated promising technical performance, such as accurate risk prediction, movement classification, and remote monitoring, evidence demonstrating meaningful improvements in functional outcomes, participation, or quality of life remains comparatively limited. In addition, many AI applications have been evaluated primarily in feasibility or pilot studies involving relatively small and selective samples, which may limit generalizability to broader geriatric populations and routine clinical practice. Real‐world adoption further depends on integration into existing clinical workflows, interoperability with health information systems, cost, reimbursement, and clinician acceptance, dimensions that remain underexplored in current implementation and health‐systems studies [27, 37].

Summary

Population aging has substantially increased the demand for both the quality and quantity of healthcare and rehabilitation services, driven by high rates of multimorbidity and age‐related declines in physical function. Physical therapy plays a critical role in promoting mobility, independence, and injury prevention among older adults, thereby reducing caregiver burden. In this context, AI has emerged as a valuable supportive tool in complex and high‐demand clinical environments by enhancing assessment, monitoring, and individualized intervention through the analysis of large volumes of clinical data (Table 1).

AI applications in geriatric rehabilitation span multiple domains, including prediction of fall risk and unsafe conditions associated with diverse health conditions across care settings. AI‐enabled tools such as telerehabilitation, virtual reality, conversational agents, and predictive analytics offer opportunities to improve access, safety, engagement, and personalization of care. However, despite promising early findings, evidence from large‐scale clinical trials evaluating functional outcomes and long‐term effectiveness remains limited. Future research should prioritize human‐centered AI design, interdisciplinary collaboration, and integration of AI tools into clinical workflows to ensure ethical, accessible, and effective use of AI in supporting geriatric rehabilitation outcomes.

AI applications in geriatric rehabilitation demonstrate varying levels of evidence across clinical domains. Relatively stronger evidence currently supports the use of AI for activity and mobility monitoring, fall‐risk detection, movement analysis, and clinical decision support, whereas applications involving conversational agents, virtual companions, and behavioral support remain at earlier stages of development. Although many studies have demonstrated promising technical feasibility, evidence supporting long‐term improvements in functional outcomes, participation, and quality of life is still emerging. Future research should prioritize large‐scale, longitudinal studies that evaluate clinically meaningful outcomes (e.g., functional mobility, falls, and quality of life), specify target populations and adequate follow‐up duration, include diverse geriatric populations such as individuals with frailty, cognitive impairment, and multimorbidity, and examine the scalability and integration of AI technologies within routine rehabilitation practice. Such efforts will be essential to establish the effectiveness, sustainability, and real‐world impact of AI‐supported geriatric rehabilitation.
