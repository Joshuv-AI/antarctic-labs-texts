Abnormal amniotic fluid volume is associated with neonatal respiratory outcomes in the late preterm period
Authors: Lauryn C. Gabby, Jenny B. Koenig, Dana Canfield, Kelsey Pinson, Leah Lamale‐Smith, Jerasimos Ballas, Cynthia Gyamfi‐Bannerman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe clearance of fluid from the fetal lungs is important for a neonate to successfully transition to breathing room air. We hypothesized that abnormal amounts of amniotic fluid (AF; either oligohydramnios [OLIGO] or polyhydramnios [POLY]) would increase the risk of neonatal respiratory morbidity.MethodsThis study is a secondary analysis of a multicenter randomized controlled trial. Patients were included if they were of late preterm gestation (34w0d‐36w5d) and had documented AF volume. Two exposures were separately investigated—OLIGO (maximum vertical pocket [MVP] <2 cm or AF index [AFI] <5 cm) and POLY (MVP > 8 cm or AFI > 24 cm). The primary outcome was a composite of neonatal respiratory support, including continuous pulmonary airway pressure or high flow nasal cannula for ≥2 h, or oxygen support (fraction of inspired oxygen ≥30%) for ≥4 h within the first 72 h of life. Secondary outcomes were major respiratory morbidity, respiratory distress syndrome (RDS), transient tachypnea of the newborn (TTN), and neonatal intensive care unit admission. Statistical analysis was performed with Student's t‐test, Wilcoxon rank sum, and chi‐square analyses, followed by logistic regression modeling to adjust for baseline differences between groups.ResultsOur study included 2792 subjects (normal fluid: 2590, OLIGO: 172, and POLY: 29). The OLIGO subjects were less likely to require neonatal respiratory support (6.4% vs. 12.5%, p = 0.02) or experience major respiratory morbidity (4.7% vs. 10.0%, p = 0.02), compared to normal AF controls. After controlling for baseline characteristics, OLIGO was still associated with less use of neonatal respiratory support (adjusted odds ratio [aOR], 0.52; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.28–0.97).The primary outcome did not differ between the POLY group and the control group (20.7% vs. 12.5%, p = 0.18). However, after controlling for baseline characteristics, POLY was significantly associated with TTN (aOR, 2.80; 95% CI, 1.03–7.62) and use of mechanical ventilation (aOR, 4.22; 95% CI, 1.19–14.95).ConclusionAF status in the late preterm period is associated with neonatal respiratory outcomes—OLIGO is associated with a lower requirement for neonatal respiratory support, while POLY is associated with TTN and the need for mechanical ventilation.

INTRODUCTION

Delivery during the late preterm period (34 weeks to 36 weeks 6 days) is associated with increased neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) admission and respiratory morbidity [1, 2, 3]. Infants delivered in the late preterm period are also at elevated risk of requiring respiratory support with increased respiratory distress syndrome (RDS) (pooled risk ratio 17.3; 95% CI, 9.8–30.6), transient tachypnea of the newborn (TTN) (pooled risk ratio 7.5; 95% CI, 5.0–11.2), and apnea (pooled risk ratio 15.7; 95% CI, 11.8–20.9) relative to their term counterparts [4]. The late preterm period marks an important time point for fetal lung development, as the transition from the saccular to the alveolar phase occurs at 36 weeks’ gestation [5, 6], and fetal lung development is dependent on maintaining appropriate fetal lung volumes [6]. Despite this, there is a paucity of data regarding how amniotic fluid (AF) quantity in the late preterm period relates to neonatal respiratory morbidity.

Oligohydramnios (OLIGO) has been shown to be associated with fetal distress in labor, obstetric interventions, cesarean delivery, admission to the NICU, meconium aspiration syndrome, stillbirth, and neonatal demise [7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13]. OLIGO can develop at all gestational ages: fetal anomalies like bilateral renal agenesis cause OLIGO early in gestation, while OLIGO can also be used as a marker for emerging placental insufficiency later in gestation [14, 15]. Regardless of mechanism, OLIGO in the mid‐trimester is known to have profound effects on fetal lung development and neonatal respiratory outcomes [16]. Less is known about the effects of OLIGO in the fetal lung in late preterm gestation. We hypothesize that OLIGO may increase the risk of neonatal respiratory morbidity in the late preterm period due to the impact on fetal lung development during the saccular and alveolar lung development phases.

Conversely, polyhydramnios (POLY) is usually idiopathic, particularly if the AF index (AFI) is <30 cm (∼50%–60% of cases) [17]. However, POLY can also be associated with congenital defects and gestational diabetes [18]. POLY is independently associated with large for gestational age infants [19], respiratory morbidity in term infants [20], and perinatal mortality [21]. Clearance of fetal lung fluid is thought to be an important aspect of the neonatal transition to breathing room air [22], and thus it is plausible that excess fetal AF could lead to challenges with clearing fetal lung fluid. Changes in clearance of fetal lung fluid may partially explain the increased respiratory morbidity and incidence of RDS in neonates born by elective cesarean section relative to vaginal delivery [23, 24], although other mechanisms have also been proposed. TTN, specifically, is associated with insufficient clearance of fluid from the lungs at the time of delivery [22, 25]. Further, this may be especially relevant in the late preterm period as TTN is relatively more common in this population relative to term infants [26], and the expression of epithelial sodium channels that contribute to the clearance of fluid does not peak until term [27, 28]. Given this pathophysiology, we hypothesized that POLY increases the risk of neonatal respiratory morbidity due to increased difficulty with clearing excess AF from the fetal lungs.

There is a paucity of data regarding the effect of abnormal AF amount on respiratory outcomes in late preterm infants. The objective of this study was to assess the association between abnormal quantities of AF with neonatal respiratory outcomes in late preterm infants.

METHODS

This study is a secondary analysis of the Antenatal Late Preterm Steroids (ALPS) trial [29]. The ALPS trial was a multicenter, randomized controlled trial of non‐anomalous singleton pregnancies at risk of delivery during the late preterm period, conducted between October 2010 and February 2015. Participants were eligible if they were at risk for spontaneous or medically indicated delivery between 34 weeks 0 days and 36 weeks 5 days of gestation. Subjects were excluded from the original study if imminent delivery was suspected within <24 h, they did not have reliable gestational dating by ultrasound, they previously received steroids for lung maturity during the pregnancy, or they had pregestational diabetes. Other exclusion criteria were previously described [29]. In the parent study, subjects were randomized to receive intramuscular injections of either 12 mg of betamethasone or matching placebo, with a second injection of the same at 24 h if undelivered. Research staff, investigators, and study participants were blinded to treatment assignment. Approval and oversight for the original trial were provided by the institutional review board at each center. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants for the original trial.

For our study, we planned a sequential analysis of OLIGO versus normal AF groups, followed by POLY versus normal AF groups. We included subjects with documented AF volume assessment within 4 weeks of enrollment (subjects were excluded if there was no response to queries regarding OLIGO or POLY, such as if there was no documented ultrasound within 4 weeks). Normal AF volume was defined as an AFI ≥5 cm and ≤24 cm or a maximum vertical pocket (MVP) ≥2 cm and ≤8 cm; subjects with a normal AFI or MVP were included in our control group. OLIGO was defined as MVP <2 cm or AFI <5 cm, while POLY was defined as MVP >8 cm or AFI >24 cm. The choice of definition was specific to the definition used at each respective center. OLIGO subjects were included if they met the above criteria for documented fluid volume or the primary indication for delivery was OLIGO. Any subject with POLY documented at enrollment in the study was included in the POLY group. Infants with major congenital anomalies identified at birth were excluded.

The primary outcome for this study is neonatal respiratory support, which was defined as a composite outcome including at least one of the following: continuous pulmonary airway pressure (CPAP) or high flow nasal cannula (HFNC) for ≥ 2 h, or oxygen support (fraction of inspired oxygen ≥ 30%) for ≥ 4 h within the first 72 h of life. Secondary outcomes include major respiratory morbidity (defined as CPAP or HFNC for ≥ 12 h, oxygen support with FiO2 ≥ 30% for ≥ 24 h, extracorporeal membrane oxygenation, mechanical ventilation, neonatal demise, and stillbirth), RDS, TTN, and NICU admission. These outcomes were compared initially between the OLIGO and normal AF groups, followed by the POLY and normal AF groups.

Primary and secondary outcomes were compared using Student's t‐test or Wilcoxon rank sum for continuous variables, and chi‐square or Fisher's exact tests for categorical variables, as appropriate. We fit logistic regression models with Firth's correction, where appropriate, to adjust for baseline differences between groups. Baseline characteristics that were either clinically relevant or statistically different were included in the logistic regression model. Gestational age at delivery and mode of delivery were not included in the regression because they are in the causal pathway, and dependent outcomes would not be appropriate to include in the regression. Sensitivity analyses were performed to assess the impact of gestational age on respiratory outcomes. Statistical output was generated using SAS software, Version 9.4.

Our sample size was fixed by the number participating in the parent study (n = 2831). Incorporating our exclusion criteria, we determined the detectable effect size assuming a power of 80% and alpha of 0.05. In subjects exposed to OLIGO, this study could detect a difference in the odds of our primary outcome of 4.25 or greater or 0.24 or less. In subjects exposed to POLY, we could detect a difference in the odds of our primary outcome of 3.15 or greater or 0.31 or less.

RESULTS

Overall, 2762 subjects were included in the OLIGO analysis and 2619 subjects were included in the POLY analysis (Figures 1 and 2). Of these, 2590 subjects were included in the normal AF group, while 172 (6.2%) and 29 (1.1%) subjects were included in the OLIGO and POLY groups, respectively. There were five patients excluded from our secondary analysis because of unknown OLIGO status, meaning that a delivery indication with the presence or absence of OLIGO was not recorded.

Oligohydramnios

OLIGO subjects had similar baseline characteristics as the normal fluid group (Table 1). Infants in the OLIGO group were born with lower birthweights, on average, compared to the control group (2440 ± 471 g vs. 2657 ± 477 g, p < 0.001), although the gestational age at delivery (36.2 ± 1.0 weeks vs. 36.1 ± 1.1 weeks, p = 0.74) and incidence of NICU admission (45.4% vs. 42.4%, p = 0.45) did not differ (Table 2). Late preterm delivery was similar between OLIGO and normal fluid groups (86.1% vs. 83.2%, p = 0.34).

The neonates born to the OLIGO group were less likely to require neonatal respiratory support (6.4% vs. 12.5%, p = 0.02) or experience major respiratory morbidity (4.7% vs. 10.0%, p = 0.02; Table 3) compared with the normal fluid group. After adjusting for factors that were statistically different or clinically relevant at baseline (including fetal sex, preeclampsia, and treatment group—betamethasone vs. placebo), OLIGO remained associated with significantly less need for neonatal respiratory support (aOR, 0.52; 95% CI, 0.28–0.97, p = 0.04) (Table 4). As in the parent trial, betamethasone exposure was independently associated with less need for respiratory support.

Sensitivity analyses were performed to further evaluate the effect of gestational age of delivery on respiratory outcomes, and the findings persisted in those born preterm. There were no differences observed at term, but the numbers were too small to draw conclusions. After adjusting for confounders in the preterm cohort, the need for neonatal respiratory support remained lower in the OLIGO group compared to normal fluid (aOR, 0.49; 95% CI, 0.25–0.95).

Polyhydramnios

Subjects in the POLY group were more likely to be obese (57.1% vs. 29.9%, p = 0.02) and have gestational diabetes (27.6% vs. 10.7%, p = 0.004) compared to controls (Table 1). Infants from the POLY group had higher birthweights compared to normal AF controls (3022 ± 601 g vs. 2657 ± 477 g, p < 0.001) (Table 2) despite being born at a similar gestational age (35.1 ± 1.1 weeks vs. 36.1 ± 1.1 weeks, p = 0.20). The proportion of infants in the POLY group born preterm at <37 weeks was similar to the normal AF group (75.9% vs. 83.3%, p = 0.29). The POLY group had a higher rate of cesarean delivery compared to the control group (51.7% vs. 30.6%, p = 0.01).

In unadjusted analyses, neonatal respiratory support did not differ between POLY and normal subjects (20.7% vs. 12.5%, p = 0.18) (Table 3). Similarly, major respiratory morbidity (13.8% vs. 10.0%, p = 0.53) and RDS (10.3% vs. 6.0%, p = 0.42) did not differ between POLY and normal subjects. TTN was not significantly increased in POLY subjects (17.2% vs. 8.3%, p = 0.08, OR 2.31; 95% CI, 0.87–6.13) in univariable analysis. However, after adjusting for treatment group, maternal age, body mass index, nulliparity, gestational diabetes, and preeclampsia, TTN was significantly increased in neonates in the POLY group relative to the normal AF group (aOR, 2.80; 95% CI, 1.03–7.62) (Table 5). Mechanical ventilation was also more commonly required in POLY subjects compared to control in our logistic regression model, although the overall incidence was low (aOR, 4.22; 95% CI, 1.19–14.95). There was still no difference observed in the primary outcome (composite of neonatal respiratory support) between POLY and normal subjects after adjustment for the factors outlined above (aOR, 2.17; 95% CI, 0.86–5.49).

Further sensitivity analyses were performed to explore the relationship between gestational age at delivery, preterm or term delivery, and respiratory outcomes. No differences in respiratory outcomes were noted in those with POLY born at term compared to those with normal fluid born at term. However, of those born preterm, POLY remained significantly associated with an increased risk for TTN.

DISCUSSION

In this study, subjects with OLIGO in the late preterm period were less likely to require neonatal respiratory support or to have major neonatal respiratory morbidity when compared to subjects with normal amounts of AF. Conversely, POLY was associated with increased TTN and requirement for mechanical ventilation in the neonate when controlling for baseline maternal characteristics.

The findings in the POLY group were overall in line with our hypothesis—if TTN results from inadequate clearance of fetal lung fluid, it follows that excess AF in the POLY group would be associated with increased TTN (once controlled for differences in baseline characteristics). The neonates in the POLY group also had an increased requirement for mechanical ventilation despite being delivered at a similar gestational age to those in the normal AF group. These effects were observed after using logistic regression modeling to control for exposure to gestational diabetes, providing support for the independent association of idiopathic POLY and worsened respiratory outcomes. We did not adjust for mode of delivery, as it is part of the causal pathway. Thus, the higher rate of cesarean delivery could potentially contribute to the outcome difference seen in the rate of TTN. Our sensitivity analyses further show that the associated increase in TTN is more specific to infants with POLY who delivered preterm. These findings add an important perspective regarding the effects of excess AF in the late preterm period, a time point at which there has been relatively limited study of POLY. However, interpretation remains limited due to small numbers.

Surprisingly, the subjects in the OLIGO group were less likely to require respiratory support or have serious respiratory morbidity, which is in contrast to prior studies showing an association of OLIGO with meconium aspiration syndrome, NICU admission, and other neonatal morbidity [7, 8]. Sensitivity analyses suggest that OLIGO is most protective for neonates who delivered preterm. Our results suggest that OLIGO in the late preterm period does not result in significant negative effects on fetal lung development, and instead may be protective by contributing relatively less fluid to clear from the lungs at the time of delivery. Additionally, OLIGO is thought to be one sign of placental insufficiency and fetal stress [15]. Endogenous fetal corticosteroid production from the fetal stress response may accelerate fetal lung maturity and reduce perinatal pulmonary morbidity [30].

Interestingly, a secondary analysis has been performed from the same parent trial [29] examining outcomes for small for gestational age (SGA) infants versus those who were appropriate for gestational age [31]. Despite SGA and OLIGO often co‐occurring, Bartal et al. found no difference in their primary outcome (a composite of respiratory morbidity) between groups. They did observe an increase in NICU admission and other issues of prematurity in the SGA group. This further supports the specific association we observed between OLIGO and respiratory outcomes, independent of birthweight.

This study examines a population at elevated risk of requiring neonatal respiratory support due to late preterm gestation and identifies AF volume as a specific factor that contributes to early neonatal respiratory outcomes. This information can be considered in patient counseling prior to a late preterm delivery, and can be utilized in planning for the resources to be made available in the delivery room and for other early neonatal support.

A key limitation of this study is that it was a secondary analysis of a prior randomized controlled trial, and was therefore not originally designed or powered to assess our outcomes—for example, while the need for respiratory support was nearly twice as common in the POLY group, our finding did not reach statistical significance due to the small sample size. However, statistically significant differences were found in many of the respiratory outcomes described above. Additionally, given the nature of how the AF status was reported in the parent trial, the AFI at time of delivery is unknown. For example, based on ACOG guidelines, OLIGO was an indication for delivery whereas POLY was not; therefore, the measurement of AF in the OLIGO group may have been assessed closer to the time of delivery relative to the POLY group but the time latency between AF measurement and delivery is unknown. This does have the potential to bias the results.

This study has several notable strengths. It utilized data from a randomized controlled trial with rigorous data collection, consistent neonatal resuscitation protocols, and a large study population spanning multiple centers. Importantly, there was no difference in the initial study's exposure (late preterm betamethasone administration) between the OLIGO, POLY, and normal fluid groups in our analysis. Further, we had access to granular data, which separated individual respiratory disorders of the neonate (i.e., RDS vs. TTN), allowing us to consider possible differential pathophysiology of these disorders relative to AF volume.

Another strength of the study was the exclusion of congenital anomalies and the ability to control for maternal medical conditions using logistic regression modeling, which allowed us to focus on the effects of AF quantity on respiratory outcomes. Of note, it is impossible to fully isolate the effects of fluid status alone, as there is a complex interplay between placental function, fetal metabolism/processing of fluid, and other factors that lead to changes in AF quantity, which cannot be controlled for with this study design.

This study raises several additional research questions, including if these described associations hold true at earlier gestational ages, as the fetal lungs are still developing. The length of the exposure to abnormal fluid volume may have an association with respiratory outcomes and could provide clues to the underlying mechanism of our reported findings; therefore, this information should be collected and assessed in the future. Additionally, future studies with larger sample sizes could help elucidate differences between the groups in a more targeted way (e.g., a study focused on late preterm POLY with a larger sample size may observe a statistical difference in neonatal respiratory support, while the data presented here only show a signal).

Finally, future studies should be pursued to elucidate how alterations in AF quantity affect the mechanical, hormonal, and other physiologic methods for clearance of fetal lung fluid at delivery, as understanding of the basic physiology could help guide the development of novel therapeutic approaches.

CONCLUSION

In summary, there is an association between AF volume and neonatal respiratory outcomes in the late preterm period. OLIGO was associated with a lower requirement for neonatal respiratory support and morbidity, while POLY resulted in increased TTN and requirement for mechanical ventilation. These findings will aid in perinatal counseling and in appropriate preparation for postnatal management of late preterm newborns exposed to abnormal AF volume.
