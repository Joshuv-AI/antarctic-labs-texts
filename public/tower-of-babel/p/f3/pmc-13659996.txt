How place, trust, and care location influence rural and non-metropolitan patients’ preferences for decentralized long-acting injectable HIV therapy: A mixed-methods study
Authors: Emmanuel Nazaire Essam Nkodo, Renae Furl, Kate Kough, Elizabeth Lyden, Jennifer O’Neill, Maureen Kubat, Daniel Cramer, Titilola Labisi, Jennifer M. Davis, Nada Fadul
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLong-acting injectable antiretroviral therapy (LAI-ART) offers an alternative to daily oral ART and may reduce pill fatigue, stigma, and daily treatment burden. However, its implementation in rural and non-metropolitan settings is challenged by travel burden, limited local healthcare infrastructure, and privacy concerns. This study evaluated interest in, and preferences for cabotegravir/rilpivirine (CAB + RPV LA) among people with HIV (PWH) living outside a metropolitan area and identified factors influencing uptake.MethodsFrom March 2024 through December 2024 we conducted a cross-sectional mixed-methods study, using an explanatory sequential mixed-methods design. We surveyed 102 PWH receiving care at a Midwestern HIV specialty clinic, followed by qualitative interviews with approximately 10% of respondents. Surveys assessed sociodemographic characteristics, knowledge of CAB + RPV LA, perceived appropriateness, facilitators, barriers, and location preferences. Quantitative analyses used chi-square, Wilcoxon, and Kruskal–Wallis tests. Interviews were thematically analyzed through iterative deductive and inductive coding.ResultsParticipants had a median age of 51; 83.3% were male, 72.5% non-Hispanic White, and 44% self-identified as rural. Awareness of CAB + RPV LA was high (72.5%), and 63.7% expressed interest in switching. Interest was associated with younger age, shorter duration since HIV diagnosis, higher perceived appropriateness, and benefits such as reduced pill burden. Barriers included injection fear, concerns about prolonged side effects, and increased clinic visits. Most participants (63.7%) preferred local injection sites, influenced strongly by travel time, convenience, and feeling safe in their community. Qualitative interviews emphasized pill fatigue, trust in providers, and privacy considerations as major drivers of acceptability.ConclusionsHigh interest in CAB + RPV LA among non-metropolitan PWH underscores the need for flexible, decentralized delivery models that reduce logistical barriers while maintaining trusted provider relationships.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12913-026-15104-x.

Background

Advances in HIV treatment have led to the development of a new treatment modality. Long-acting injectable antiretroviral therapy (LAI-ART) represents a recent innovation that allows eligible people with HIV (PWH) to receive intramuscular (IM) ART injections, a significant departure from the daily pill regimen [1, 2]. Cabotegravir-rilpivirine (CAB + RPV LA) is the first and only complete long-acting regimen FDA-approved for the treatment of HIV-1 infection that can be administered either monthly or every two months for PWH who are virologically suppressed on their current regimen [1, 3–5]. These formulations have demonstrated success in long-term HIV management with high levels of patient satisfaction, including the potential to address pill fatigue, reduce internalized HIV stigma, and remove the unwanted daily reminder of HIV infection [1–7]. Despite the clinical success of LAI-ART in sustaining viral suppression and eliminating onward transmission, structural and societal barriers may hinder equitable uptake. These barriers include lack of access to care, inability to meet subsistence needs, homelessness and unstable housing, HIV-related stigma, language barriers, medical mistrust, and structural racism [8–15].

According to a Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) report, HIV rates in suburban and rural areas are elevated in the South and Midwest regions of the United States compared to the Northeast and West [16, 17]. Specifically in Nebraska, 2023 HIV prevalence data showed that 23% of PWH reside in rural areas; the University of Nebraska Medical Center (UNMC) Specialty Care Center (SCC), the state’s principal HIV specialty referral clinic, reports that 16% of Nebraska PWH and 30% of Iowa PWH served at the SCC meet their respective states’ rural definitions [18].

HIV specialty care in Nebraska is organized around a centralized hub model, in which the UNMC Specialty Care Center (SCC) in Omaha serves as the primary referral destination for HIV-positive individuals across the state and parts of western Iowa. This arrangement reflects the broader national pattern in which HIV clinical expertise, specialized pharmacy services including dispensing through the AIDS Drug Assistance Program (ADAP), multidisciplinary case management, and access to clinical trials are concentrated in a small number of academic medical centers rather than distributed across community health centers or primary care practices. As a result, many PWH in Nebraska must travel substantial distances to access HIV specialty care (from 20 to 240 min one way, with a median of 60 min). Local healthcare facilities including primary care clinics, community health centers, and pharmacies are geographically closer to patients’ homes but are not currently authorized or equipped to initiate or administer CAB + RPV LA. For the purposes of this study, questions about receiving injections at a “local” facility were therefore prospective and hypothetical in nature, probing where participants would prefer to receive injections if services were decentralized, rather than where they currently access HIV care. This distinction is central to interpreting the preference findings reported below.

Providing HIV care to PWH living in non-metropolitan areas thus presents a unique set of challenges, including increased travel burden to meet the monthly or bimonthly injection schedule of CAB + RPV LA, transportation barriers, infrastructure and staffing limitations at local facilities, stigma and privacy concerns, and cost and insurance issues [19–22]. These factors highlight the impact of social and structural determinants of health on HIV care access and highlight why decentralizing LAI-ART delivery is both a logistical and an equity imperative for non-metropolitan PWH.

While several studies have examined the barriers and facilitators for implementing LAI-ART in US HIV clinics, there has been limited research on factors associated with patients’ interest in and preference for specific locations to receive LAI-ART, particularly among non-metropolitan and rural dwellers [19, 20, 23–26]. To ensure equitable access to CAB + RPV LA for patients highly impacted by these multiple barriers, we aimed to investigate interest in, and location preferences for CAB + RPV LA among PWH living remotely from the clinic and identify the factors associated with their preferences.

Methods

Study design and participants

From March 2024 through December 2024, we conducted a cross-sectional study, using an explanatory sequential mixed-methods design. In the first phase, quantitative survey were administered, and findings informed purposive sampling and semi-structured interviews to contextualize and expand upon survey results.

CAB + RPV LA became available at the UNMC SCC prior to FDA approval in January 2021, through clinical trials hosted in clinic since 2014. At the time of this study initiation in March 2024, the SCC counted approximately 1265 patients. Of those, 73 PWH had received at least one dose of CAB + RPV LA and were therefore excluded from enrollment, as the study focused on pre-implementation preferences among LAI-naïve individuals. The target population came down to 319 eligible PWH we identified from the SCC HIV patient registry, an internal database with the electronic medical record (EMR). Of these, we can’t accurately report how many had previously been offered CAB + RPV LA by their provider. However, they formed the sampling frame from which study participants were recruited as they were never administered CAB + RPV LA.

Participants were eligible if they were at least 19 years old (Nebraska’s definition of legal age of adulthood) and able to complete the survey in English or Spanish. Additionally, patients had to reside outside the Omaha metropolitan area, which is defined by residing outside the following cities: Omaha, Bellevue, Papillion, La Vista, Ralston, Valley, Waterloo, Gretna, Elkhorn, and Council Bluffs, located in Nebraska and Southwest Iowa.

In the U.S., federal agencies use distinct but overlapping systems to classify geography. The U.S. Census Bureau defines metropolitan statistical areas (MSAs) as counties containing a core urban area of at least 50,000 residents, plus adjacent counties with strong social and economic integration with that core. Non-metropolitan residence therefore encompasses a wide spectrum from small cities and towns (often labeled “micropolitan”) to genuinely remote countryside [27]. The US Department of Agriculture (USDA)’s Rural-Urban Continuum Codes (RUCC) further subdivide non-metropolitan counties into nine levels, with codes 4–9 broadly corresponding to progressively smaller and more isolated communities [28]. Our eligibility criterion was residence outside the Omaha metropolitan area, which deliberately captured this full spectrum of non-metropolitan experience not only residents of farms or countryside.

All enrolled participants received their primary HIV specialty care exclusively at the UNMC SCC at the time of study enrollment; no participants were concurrently receiving HIV specialty care from a local community provider, primary care clinic, or pharmacy. This reflects the centralized structure of HIV specialty care in Nebraska, where the SCC functions as the state’s principal referral hub and local facilities are not currently authorized or equipped to provide HIV specialty services or administer CAB + RPV LA. Therefore, PWH in non-metropolitan areas are routinely referred to, and retain long-term care relationships with the SCC regardless of the distance involved. Survey items presenting local facilities as potential injection sites, including primary care clinics, pharmacies, community health centers, and local health departments were therefore hypothetical and prospective in nature, probing where participants would prefer to receive injections under a decentralized care scenario, rather than reflecting existing patterns of local HIV service utilization. Currently the SCC counts 1467 PWH, with 186 patients who had received at least one dose of CAB + RPV LA and 26 patients who have moved or discontinued. Exclusion criteria included current use of CAB + RPV LA or incarceration at the time of outreach, and patients who are otherwise unable to give informed consent for participation. Eligible PWH were purposefully sampled and those who consented were surveyed either in-person or online via an electronic link deployed through the Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) platform [29].

Procedure

First, we administered a cross-sectional survey (Additional File 1; see supplementary materials for the full survey instrument) to People with HIV (PWH) on oral antiretroviral therapy (ART) and naïve to Long-acting injectable antiretroviral therapy (LAI-ART), who receive HIV care at the University of Nebraska Medical Center (UNMC) Specialty Care Center (SCC). The survey was a self-administered 47-item questionnaire developed specifically for this study, with some of the questions incorporated, adapted from several previously validated and published measures such as The Intervention Appropriateness Measure (IAM), Healthy Opportunities Screening Tool, Hunger Vital Sign™ [30–34]. To further ensure clarity, relevance, and face validity, the questionnaire and interview guide were reviewed by members of the study team with expertise in HIV care and implementation research prior to deployment. We collected sociodemographic (age, gender, race/ethnicity, zip code, and insurance) and clinical data (HIV transmission category, diagnosis date, viral suppression, ART duration, comorbidities using ICD-10 codes, and engagement in care). Viral suppression was defined as HIV RNA levels < 50 copies/mL, measured in the last 12 months. Engagement in care was measured using the Ryan White HIV/AIDS program definition, which requires at least one HIV medical visit in each 6 months during the measurement year, with a minimum of 60 days between medical visits.

We assessed patients’ (1) knowledge and attitudes toward CAB + RPV LA, (2) perceived benefits and barriers, (3) interest in CAB + RPV LA, (4) access to local care, access to technology (access to one chart, previous use of Telehealth, internet coverage), and transportation, and (5) preferred administration site between local (geographically close to where they live) and UNMC SCC. Within the knowledge and attitudes domain, awareness of CAB + RPV LA was assessed through a single item asking participants whether they had heard of CAB + RPV LA prior to completing the survey. This item was designed to capture any form of prior awareness including through clinical discussions with providers, pharmaceutical advertisements, peer conversations, or media exposure and was not structured to distinguish between participants who had been formally offered CAB + RPV LA by a provider and those with more passive, non-clinical awareness. As a result, it is possible that a subset of LAI-aware but LAI-naïve participants had previously been offered CAB + RPV LA and consciously declined, while others had encountered information about it through non-clinical channels without having faced an explicit treatment decision. This distinction could not be ascertained from the survey data and is acknowledged as a potential source of unmeasured selection bias, as participants who had previously declined a provider offer may hold systematically different attitudes toward CAB + RPV LA than those with passive awareness. Additionally, we measured patients’ perceived Appropriateness of LAI-ART using the validated Intervention Appropriateness Measure (IAM) [30]. IAM was selected as the primary implementation outcome given its theoretical primacy in the implementation process. Per Proctor et al. (2011), appropriateness functions as an antecedent to implementation, such that provider and site-level judgments of fit are likely to subsume perceptions of feasibility and acceptability [35]. Accordingly, IAM was prioritized over the full Weiner et al. (2017) trilogy to maintain parsimony while retaining the construct most proximal to adoption decisions. Survey completion required approximately 60 min.

Second, a purposive subsample of survey respondents (n = 10, approximately 10% of the total sample) was invited to participate in individual semi-structured interviews between June and October 2024. Participants were selected to ensure variation across key dimensions, including self-reported residential status (rural vs. urban), level of interest in CAB + RPV LA (interested, unsure, or not interested), race/ethnicity, gender, and one-way travel time to the SCC. The interview guide (Additional File 2) was developed by the study team around the theoretical domains informing the survey instrument and was refined iteratively following the first two interviews to improve question clarity and probe depth. Interviews were conducted by E.N.E.N. and R.F., both trained in qualitative data collection, in a one-on-one format by telephone according to participant preference. Both interviewers were members of the clinic-affiliated research team but held no direct clinical care role with study participants, and participants were informed of this distinction prior to consent. Given the interviewers’ institutional affiliation with the SCC, participants may have felt some social desirability pressure to present favorable views of their care team or hesitancy to disclose dissatisfaction with clinic services; this potential influence is acknowledged as a limitation of the qualitative findings, particularly those related to trust and provider relationships. All interviews were audio-recorded with participants’ written informed consent and transcribed verbatim; transcripts were subsequently verified for accuracy against the original audio recordings by K.K., a team member with training in qualitative data analysis. Interviews lasted approximately 30 min.

Thematic saturation, defined as the point at which no new codes or meaningful themes emerged from successive interviews, was assessed iteratively throughout data collection and was considered reached after the ninth interview; the tenth interview was conducted to confirm saturation.

Interview transcripts were imported into Dedoose 10 for analysis using a hybrid deductive-inductive thematic approach structured across two coding rounds. The coding process was approached through open coding. In the first round, K.K. applied codes deductively, with a majority of codes predetermined based on thematic domains identified from the quantitative survey findings, consistent with the explanatory sequential design of the study. In the second round, K.K. revisited all transcripts to further interrogate and refine the deductive codes and to identify and apply emergent inductive codes arising from the first-round analysis. K.K. and E.N.E.N. independently reviewed a subset of coded transcripts to assess consistency, and discrepancies were resolved through consensus discussion. Themes and subthemes were developed through synthesis of codes across both rounds. Representative quotes were selected for each theme, lightly edited for readability, and de-identified prior to inclusion in the manuscript.

Participants received a $20 gift card for each activity completed.

Data analysis

We summarized quantitative data using medians with interquartile ranges, counts, and percentages. Associations between categorical variables and interest or location preferences were tested using chi‑square tests. It should be noted that the study sample (n = 102) was not powered for inferential statistical testing of subgroup differences. A minimum sample of n = 241 would be required to achieve 80% power to detect a modest effect size (Cohen’s w = 0.2) using chi-square tests. All reported chi-square statistics and p-values should therefore be interpreted descriptively and with caution, as indicators of the direction and magnitude of observed associations rather than as confirmatory hypothesis tests. Statistically non-significant findings should not be interpreted as evidence of equivalence between groups. In the absence of that distinction, a stratified analysis by awareness status of CAB + RPV LA across participants alone would risk conflating meaningfully different experiential groups and could produce misleading inferences; this analytic limitation is acknowledged in the Discussion. Wilcoxon rank‑sum tests compared continuous variables across two groups, and Kruskal–Wallis tests compared continuous variables across the three interest categories (yes/no/unsure); pairwise comparisons used the Dwass–Steel–Critchlow–Fligner adjustment.

The qualitative component used a descriptive thematic analysis approach (Braun and Clarke), nested within an explanatory sequential mixed-methods design, to contextualize and expand upon quantitative survey findings [36]. Interview transcripts were checked for accuracy, then coded iteratively using a combined deductive (survey‑informed) and inductive approach, with multiple passes to refine themes and ensure reliability among coders. Quantitative and qualitative findings were triangulated by comparing thematic patterns from interviews against statistically significant associations identified in the survey data, allowing convergent or divergent findings to inform interpretation; member checking with participants was not performed and is noted as a limitation. We used the SRQR reporting guidelines and checklist to draft and edit this manuscript, included in supplement A [37].

Results

Survey results

Among 102 participants, the median age was 51 years (range 21–73), 83.3% were male, and 72.5% were non‑Hispanic White (Table 1). Nearly two‑thirds (64.7%) had a high school education or less. The median time since HIV diagnosis was 14.5 years, and the median time on ART was 8 years. Three‑quarters (75.5%) were engaged in care and 94.1% were virally suppressed. Most participants had private-individual insurance (33.3%), followed by Medicare (25.5%) and Private-employer (21.6%). Mental health conditions (39.2%) and substance use disorders (19.6%) were the most frequent comorbidities. Approximately 44.1% identified their residence as rural. The median travel time to the UNMC SCC was 60 min (range 20–240).

Knowledge, interest, and location preference

Overall, 63.7% of participants expressed interest in CAB + RPV LA; 13.7% were not interested and 22.5% were unsure (Table 2). If offered a choice of injection location, 63.7% preferred to receive injections locally and 36.3% preferred the UNMC SCC. If starting CAB + RPV LA, 67.6% indicated they would receive injections locally, with primary care clinics (60.9%) and pharmacies (13.8%) most commonly preferred local sites.

Factors associated with interest in CAB + RPV LA

Interest in CAB + RPV LA varied by age group (p = 0.037), with participants 50 years or younger (55.4%, p = 0.037) and participants with shorter duration since HIV diagnosis (12.3 years, p = 0.025) more likely to express interest. Higher perceived appropriateness (IAM) scores (16) was associated with interest (p < 0.0001). Facilitators reported by participants interested in CAB + RPV LA included not having to remember daily pills (87.7%; p < 0.0001), reducing daily reminders of HIV (73.8%; p = 0.002), and confidence in treatment efficacy between injections (75.4%; p = 0.001). Barriers cited more frequently by those unsure or not interested included fear of injections (28.6%; p = 0.039), concerns about side effects and their persistence (p = 0.049 and p < 0.001, respectively), and the time commitment and number of required clinic visits (p < 0.001 and p = 0.002, respectively). Concern about travel costs for injection visits did not differ significantly by interest, although a higher proportion of unsure participants (65.2%) reported concern. Full results of bivariate analyses examining sociodemographic, clinical, and structural factors associated with interest in CAB + RPV LA, including variables not reaching statistical significance, are presented in Additional File 3.

Preference for injection location

Sixty-five participants (63.7%) preferred to receive CAB + RPV LA in their local area, and 37 (36.3%) preferred the SCC (Table 3). Although not statistically significant, those residing in rural areas were more likely to prefer receiving CAB + RPV LA at the SCC (54.1% vs. 38.5%). Participants preferring local administration had slightly longer travel time to the SCC (65 vs. 60 min, p = 0.005), shorter travel times to their primary care provider (10 vs. 40 min, p = 0.005), and were more likely to report willingness to travel less than 60 min for CAB + RPV LA (81.5% vs. 62.2%, p = 0.036). A full comparison of travel-related factors by location preference, including variables not reaching statistical significance, is presented in Additional File 4. Perceptions of safety also differed significantly by location preference: nearly all participants preferring local injections reported feeling physically and emotionally safe where they lived, compared to a somewhat lower proportion among those preferring the SCC (98.5% vs. 89.2%, p = 0.037). A complete breakdown of safety perception items by location preference is presented in Additional File 5.

Qualitative results

Qualitative interviews provided context for participants’ interest in CAB + RPV LA and preferences regarding injection location (see Table 4 for summary). Consistent with the explanatory sequential design, qualitative themes were interpreted in direct relation to quantitative survey findings, for example, examining whether participants’ stated reasons for location preference corroborated the travel-time and safety-perception associations identified in Table 3, providing a form of methodological triangulation between data sources. Interest in CAB + RPV LA was primarily shaped by relational trust with providers, perceived treatment stability, autonomy in decision-making, and pragmatic considerations related to travel, privacy, and daily life burden. Even participants expressing hesitancy toward CAB + RPV LA described strong therapeutic alliances and a sense of agency in treatment decisions.

Interest in LAI-ART: pill burden, stability, and risk tolerance

Interest in CAB + RPV LA was primarily motivated by the burden and fatigue associated with daily oral pill-taking, a theme raised by participants regardless of their overall enthusiasm for switching. Many described oral ART as a continual cognitive and emotional reminder of HIV, whereas CAB + RPV LA was viewed as offering greater normalcy and reduced daily intrusion. Stability emerged as a nuanced consideration: for some, CAB + RPV LA represented enhanced treatment stability, while for others, the novelty of the regimen raised concerns about potentially disrupting well‑controlled HIV. Risk tolerance also varied across participants. Individuals with prior personal or familial experience with injectable therapies tended to express greater openness to CAB + RPV LA, whereas those satisfied with their existing oral regimens often preferred a more cautious, “wait‑and‑see” approach.

Barriers and facilitators to LAI-ART uptake

Barriers to CAB + RPV LA were discussed frequently and centered on psychosocial and clinical concerns, including fear of injections, anticipated pain or prolonged side effects, mistrust of new treatments, and perceived loss of control associated with moving away from a daily pill routine. These concerns were more prominent among participants who were unsure or not interested in CAB + RPV LA.

In contrast to the barriers described above, the facilitators most frequently raised by participants centered on provider communication and patient self-knowledge. Participants emphasized the importance of receiving clear, individualized information from trusted providers, having dedicated opportunities to ask questions and voice concerns, and feeling empowered to assess whether CAB + RPV LA aligned with their own health needs and lived experience. These facilitators were elicited through open-ended interview questions and emerged unprompted from participant narratives, reflecting genuine priorities rather than responses to structured prompts about predetermined categories. Many participants described confidence in their ability to “know their body” and assess whether a treatment aligned with their needs, underscoring the role of autonomy and shared decision-making in supporting engagement with new treatment options. Structural facilitators such as flexible scheduling, reminders, local staff training, and privacy safeguards were mentioned less frequently but were still viewed as important supports, particularly for long-term maintenance of CAB + RPV LA.

Safety, efficacy, and trust

Safety and efficacy concerns were common among participants. Although most expressed baseline trust in their providers and in HIV treatment, nearly all sought reassurance that CAB + RPV LA would match the effectiveness of oral ART, particularly regarding viral suppression and long‑term outcomes. Concerns about reversibility and unknown long‑term effects were more pronounced among hesitant participants and were somewhat more frequently raised by men.

Trust emerged as a central theme influencing both interest and hesitation. Participants highlighted confidence in clinical expertise, evidence‑based guidance, and strong provider relationships as facilitating open dialogue and informed decision-making. Notably, trust did not imply passive acceptance; participants valued providers who offered recommendations while actively supporting patient choice.

Location preference: trust, privacy, and quality of care over proximity

Preferences for injection location were not determined solely by geographic proximity. Many participants, particularly those preferring the UNMC SCC, emphasized confidence in their care team, continuity of care, and perceived quality of specialized HIV services, even when travel distances were substantial. For these participants, established relationships and predictability outweighed the burden of travel.

Privacy concerns also influenced location preferences. Participants from smaller or rural communities expressed concerns about being recognized at local clinics or encountering acquaintances working in healthcare settings, making the SCC feel safer and more anonymous despite longer travel. Notably, few participants indicated absolute unwillingness to travel; rather, willingness to travel was often conditional on trust, perceived quality, and privacy protections.

Discussion

In this mixed-methods study of PWH living in rural and non-metropolitan areas in the Midwest, we found high acceptability and interest in CAB + RPV LA, and a strong preference for local administration if available. Nearly two-thirds of participants expressed interest in CAB + RPV LA, and a similar proportion preferred to receive injections locally rather than at a centralized specialty HIV clinic. Importantly, preferences for CAB + RPV LA delivery were shaped less by sociodemographic characteristics and more by travel burden, perceived safety, and trust in care relationships. Qualitative findings provided critical context, demonstrating that pill fatigue, provider trust, and confidence in care settings underpin both enthusiasm and hesitancy toward CAB + RPV LA.

Similar to studies exploring patients’ interest and acceptability of LAI ART, participants of our study reported a substantial interest and preference for that treatment option, with many individuals finding the new injectable appealing, particularly regarding those experiencing pill fatigue or confidentiality concerns [26, 38–40]. Additionally, qualitative interviews revealed that participants of our study reported some facilitators to switch to CAB + RPV LA, including not having to remember daily pills, reducing daily reminders of HIV, and confidence in treatment efficacy between injections, which aligns with previous studies’ findings showing that patients expressed that LAI-ART offers convenience and freedom, reduced pill burden, and privacy and reduced stigma [23–25].

Looking at specific populations, interest in CAB + RPV LA was higher among participants under 50 years of age, which is directionally consistent with prior studies reporting elevated interest among PWH 50 years and below, including youth and adolescents [39, 41–43]. However, direct comparison with studies focused on youth and adolescents is limited by meaningful differences in age categorization (our binary classification of participants does not correspond to the narrower adolescent or young adult definitions typically under 25 or under 30 years in some studies. Furthermore, the other populations identified in prior studies as having particularly high LAI interest, including people experiencing homelessness, those with substance use or mental health challenges, and women, face distinct adherence challenges with oral ART that may not be fully captured by age alone as a predictor variable. Future studies enrolling non-metropolitan PWH should incorporate finer age stratification, particularly a category for participants under 30 or 35 years, to enable more meaningful comparison with the existing literature on age-related differences in LAI-ART interest [1, 20, 24]. Among participants aged 50 and older, lower interest in CAB + RPV LA may stem from strong satisfaction with oral ART, which has historically been the predominant treatment modality for this age cohort. Many of these individuals are virally suppressed and report feeling stable on their current regimen, reducing their motivation to consider switching, even when an alternative may offer meaningful benefits. A similar pattern may apply to participants with longer ART experience; in our sample, the median duration on ART was 8 years (range 0.5–12), and those with extended treatment histories may have developed strong adherence routines and therapeutic familiarity that reduce openness to regimen change. It is important to note, however, that age and ART duration, while often correlated, are not interchangeable: a younger participant may have a long ART history if diagnosed early, while an older participant newly diagnosed may have limited ART experience. Disaggregated analysis of interest by ART duration specifically, rather than age alone, was not possible within the scope of the current study given sample size constraints, and this represents a direction for future research. Nonetheless, the median ART duration reported in Table 1 provides context for interpreting interest patterns and should be considered alongside age when drawing inferences about treatment experience and openness to LAI-ART [20, 26]. Moreover, some patients faced skepticism or mistrust of new injectable modalities, which has been documented in a previous study, reporting that patients had limited knowledge about injectable treatment and were distrusting of the new modality [20]. Concerns about medication harm are high, patients may view new or unfamiliar therapies with heightened caution, reducing their willingness to adopt them, as previous research showed that some people living with HIV hold negative beliefs about HIV treatment, such as thinking that ART does more harm than good and these beliefs were linked with lower ART use [44].

The potential influence of prior LAI awareness on interest and preference findings warrants explicit acknowledgment. Among the 74 participants (72.5%) who reported having heard of CAB + RPV LA before completing the survey, it is possible that some had previously been offered CAB + RPV LA by their provider and made a conscious decision not to switch, while others had encountered the therapy through advertisements, peer conversations, or media without facing an explicit treatment decision. These represent meaningfully different experiential positions that our awareness item was not designed to distinguish. A formal subgroup comparison of interest and preference by awareness status was therefore not conducted, as doing so without this critical distinction could produce misleading inferences.

Nearly two-thirds of participants in our study (63.7%) preferred receiving CAB + RPV LA injections in their local area rather than at the SCC. This preference appeared to be strongly shaped by travel-related factors. Participants favoring local administration reported substantially shorter travel times to their primary care provider and were more likely to indicate willingness to travel ≤ 60 min for injections. Qualitative data reinforced this interpretation, with participants describing how travel burden encompasses missed work, scheduling disruptions, and transportation reliability in addition to travel time. Interestingly, participants residing in rural areas showed a trend toward preferring administration at the SCC, though this difference was not statistically significant. Interviews clarified this pattern, revealing that long-standing relationships, perceived expertise, and continuity of care often outweighed geographic convenience. For many participants, particularly those with extensive HIV care histories through established care at the SCC, the SCC represented a trusted environment where high-quality, specialized care was assured. These findings caution against assuming that decentralization alone will meet patient preferences and highlight the importance of preserving trusted care relationships during implementation. Additionally, these findings underscore the central role of geographic accessibility in shaping patient preferences for CAB + RPV LA delivery, aligning with prior studies that have identified transportation burden as a persistent barrier to HIV care engagement and retention, and further suggesting making LAI-ART available in locations closer to where PWH live [26]. Participants further expressed preferences to receive CAB + RPV LA at their primary care providers or pharmacies as alternative administration sites. While these options present with alternative delivery models to address access inequities, especially in rural/remote areas where HIV specialists might be scarce, it remains unclear whether, within the United States, CAB + RPV LA should be administered through a patient’s PCP, through an HIV-specific care provider, or in alternative settings [24]. It is also important to note that all participants in this study received their primary HIV specialty care exclusively at the UNMC SCC; none were concurrently receiving HIV services from a local provider. Location preference findings in Table 3 therefore cannot be attributed to a simple preference to continue receiving care at an already-familiar local site. Rather, the strong preference for local administration reflects a genuine prospective desire for decentralized delivery, driven by travel burden, convenience, and perceived safety in the local community, among patients whose entire HIV care experience has been centered at a specialty clinic located a median of 60 min from their homes.

While CAB + RPV LA can offer privacy by eliminating daily pills, the need for frequent clinic visits can increase the risk of inadvertent HIV status disclosure, especially in smaller communities or if injections are administered at local pharmacies. Broader community stigma also contributes to these fears [19, 20, 23, 24, 26]. Additionally, infrastructure and staffing challenges could be implicated, as clinics in rural areas are implicitly acknowledged to be less prepared with needed resources for LAI-ART implementation compared to urban sites, illustrated by the need for additional staff to administer injections and for coordinating injection appointments and follow-up, which could be a greater challenge in rural, under-resourced clinics [19, 26].

Perceptions of safety also emerged as an important differentiator. Participants who preferred local injections were significantly more likely to report feeling physically and emotionally safe in their living environment. Qualitative findings suggest that willingness to receive CAB + RPV LA locally may depend on broader contextual factors, including stigma, privacy, and community support. These results align with trauma-informed and stigma-sensitive frameworks, emphasizing that expanding access to LAI-ART must be accompanied by attention to social context and patient comfort within local care settings [20, 26]. Trust emerged as a unifying theme across both quantitative and qualitative findings. Trust in clinical expertise and relational trust with providers enabled participants to engage in shared decision-making, even when expressing hesitancy. For some, trust supported careful consideration of CAB + RPV LA; for others, it empowered enthusiastic uptake. Importantly, trust appeared to mitigate fear of new treatments and sustain engagement among participants who were uncertain or risk-averse.

Implications for LAI-ART implementation

Taken together, these findings have important implications for LAI-ART implementation in non-metropolitan and mixed urban-rural settings. First, implementation strategies should prioritize flexible delivery models that integrate LAI-ART into existing primary care and pharmacy settings while maintaining strong connections to specialty HIV expertise. Second, provider education and communication are central to uptake; participants consistently emphasized the need for clear, individualized counseling and reassurance regarding safety and efficacy. Third, implementation efforts must account for patient heterogeneity, recognizing that pill fatigue, risk tolerance, and trust vary and that no single delivery model will meet all patient needs. Hybrid models that allow patients to choose between local and centralized administration, supported by shared protocols and ongoing specialty consultation, may be particularly effective. Such approaches could reduce travel burden while preserving continuity and trust, thereby supporting equitable and patient-centered expansion of LAI-ART.

Limitations

This study has important limitations that warrant consideration.

First, the sample was drawn mostly from a single HIV care center in Nebraska, which may restrict the generalizability of findings to other geographic regions or care settings. Second, the cross-sectional design captures attitudes and preferences at one point in time and does not allow for assessment of temporal changes or causal relationships. Third, several methodological constraints affect the qualitative component of this study. Thematic saturation from interviews conducted with a subsample of participants should be interpreted cautiously, given the small, purposive sample considered for the qualitative phase. Member checking with participants was not performed, limiting our ability to confirm that interpreted themes accurately reflected participants’ intended meaning. Additionally, interviewers’ affiliation with the clinical research team may have introduced social desirability bias in participants’ reports of trust and satisfaction with their care team, as discussed in the Methods. Fourth, awareness of CAB + RPV LA was assessed through a single item that captured any form of prior exposure, including provider discussions, advertisements, peer conversations, or media, without distinguishing between participants who had been formally offered CAB + RPV LA and declined and those with more passive, non-clinical awareness. Participants who had consciously chosen not to switch to CAB + RPV LA following a provider offer may hold systematically more negative or entrenched attitudes than those who were simply aware of the therapy through incidental exposure, potentially biasing interest and preference findings among the aware-but-naïve subgroup. Future studies should include a dedicated item asking whether a provider has previously offered LAI to enable this critical distinction. Fifth, the study was not designed or powered for inferential statistical testing of subgroup differences. All statistical comparisons reported in this manuscript should therefore be interpreted with caution as descriptive and exploratory rather than confirmatory, and statistically non-significant findings should not be taken as evidence of equivalence. This limitation is inherent to pre-implementation mixed-methods studies conducted within single clinic settings and underscores the need for larger, multi-site studies to confirm or refute the patterns observed here. Sixth, reliance on self-reported data introduces potential recall and social desirability biases, particularly for sensitive topics such as stigma and disclosure, as well as definitional heterogeneity around residential classification. Finally, individuals currently using CAB + RPV LA and those incarcerated were excluded, which may omit viewpoints from populations with distinct access barriers.

Conclusion

In this mixed-methods study, interest in Long-acting Cabotegravir/Rilpivirine was high among people with HIV living in non-metropolitan areas, with most participants preferring local administration when available. Preferences for CAB + RPV LA delivery were shaped primarily by travel burden, perceived safety, and trust in care relationships rather than by sociodemographic characteristics or rural residence alone. Qualitative findings highlighted that pill fatigue, confidence in provider expertise, and reassurance regarding safety and efficacy are central to patients’ willingness to consider CAB + RPV LA, while established relationships with specialty care teams continue to influence preferences for centralized delivery. These findings underscore the need for flexible, patient-centered implementation strategies that expand access to LAI-ART without disrupting trusted care relationships. Future implementation research should evaluate real-world uptake, persistence, and equity impacts of decentralized LAI-ART delivery models, particularly in rural and non-metropolitan settings, to ensure that expanded access translates into sustained engagement and improved outcomes.
