Fulminant truncal necrotizing fasciitis of perianal origin with mediastinal extension: a case report
Authors: Haoyan Li, Chaoyun Chen, Ziwen Wang, Huacong Zhu, Lisi Jian, Lu Zhou, Shiwei Chen, Yang Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNecrotizing fasciitis (NF) is a rare but life-threatening soft-tissue infection characterized by rapidly progressive necrosis of the fascia and subcutaneous tissue. Early manifestations are often nonspecific, and delayed diagnosis is associated with substantial mortality. Early recognition and immediate intervention are critical to improving outcomes.Case presentationA 34-year-old man presented with progressive left lower abdominal pain and persistent perianal discomfort 5 days after radical drainage of a perianal abscess combined with internal hemorrhoid ligation. Despite initial treatment with antibiotics, glycemic control, and fluid resuscitation, his condition continued to deteriorate, and he was subsequently transferred to our hospital for definitive treatment. Emergency debridement demonstrated extensive spread of infection from the ischiorectal fossa through the obturator and abdominal fascial planes to the contralateral gluteal region and mediastinal/para-mediastinal fascial planes. He was successfully extubated on postoperative day 3 and subsequently transitioned to high-flow nasal oxygen. Necrotizing fasciitis (NF) is a rare but life-threatening soft-tissue infection characterized by rapidly progressive necrosis of the fascia and subcutaneous tissue. NF carries substantial mortality and often presents with nonspecific early manifestations [1, 2]. Reported incidence ranges from 3 to 50 cases per million population, with an overall mortality rate of approximately 20.6% [2, 3]. NF can occur in any part of the body, but the most common sites are the perineum (36%), lower limbs (15.2%), postoperative wounds (14.7%), and the abdomen, mouth, and neck [4]. NF can spread rapidly and, if untreated, may lead to systemic infection, sepsis, multiple organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS), and even death [5]. Early diagnosis and urgent surgical source control are critical determinants of survival. If admission is delayed beyond 24 h, the risk of death in patients with NF increases approximately ninefold [6–8]. Current management follows established principles, including early diagnosis, prompt surgical debridement, and appropriate antibiotic therapy [1]. In the present case, fulminant NF developed after radical drainage of a perianal abscess combined with internal hemorrhoid ligation. The infection spread from the 6 o’clock anal crypt through the ischiorectal fossa and obturator fascia to the left abdominal wall, crossed to the contralateral gluteal region, and extended superiorly into the thoracodorsal and mediastinal/para-mediastinal fascial planes on CT. Intraoperative evaluation confirmed no additional gas accumulation. With subsequent perioperative management, the patient recovered and was discharged from the hospital. This case report has been reported in line with the SCARE 2020 criteria [9]. Because the infection originated in the perianal region but extended far beyond the perineum to involve the abdominal wall, gluteal region, and mediastinal fascial planes, we describe it throughout the manuscript as fulminant truncal necrotizing fasciitis of perianal origin.

Case presentation

Patient information

A 34-year-old man was transferred to our hospital for management of rapidly progressive NF that developed after radical drainage of a perianal abscess combined with internal hemorrhoid ligation. He had no known history of diabetes mellitus or other major comorbidities. No known drug allergies were reported, and the medication history before transfer was incomplete. Although he had no known history of diabetes mellitus before transfer, subsequent evaluation showed markedly uncontrolled hyperglycemia, including diabetic ketosis and an HbA1c of 11.2%, suggesting previously unrecognized diabetes.

Clinical findings

Chief complaint

Perianal swelling and pain accompanied by left lower abdominal discomfort.

Symptoms on admission: On arrival, the patient was alert but appeared fatigued. He reported severe left lower abdominal pain, chest tightness, dyspnea, and epigastric discomfort, without fever or chills. He also reported mild dizziness and headache. He denied cough, sputum production, or lower-extremity edema. He was passing flatus but had not opened his bowels for 3 days; no hematochezia was reported. Appetite and sleep were generally normal.

Physical examination

Physical examination at admission revealed a temperature of 36.8 ℃, pulse rate of 110 beats/min, respiratory rate of 21 breaths/min, and blood pressure of 140/80 mmHg. Abdominal examination revealed distension, marked tenderness, local warmth, and fluctuance over the left lower abdominal wall, without rebound tenderness (Fig. 1A). Perineal examination showed fresh granulation tissue at the previous operative site, tenderness at the 6 o’clock position, and a small amount of purulent blood-stained discharge. Tenderness, erythema, warmth, and fluctuance extended from the left groin to the left lower abdominal wall (Fig. 1B).Fig. 1Clinical presentation of the patient on admission

Diagnostic assessment

Laboratory findings

Based on the admission laboratory results, the calculated scores were as follows: LRINEC 10, SIARI 7, and LARINF 5 [10–12]. These results supported a high clinical suspicion of NF and suggested severe systemic inflammation.

Imaging findings

Emergency CT of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis demonstrated extensive soft-tissue swelling involving the anterior abdominal wall, left abdominal wall, lower abdomen, perineum, and right pelvic wall. Multiple gas-containing collections were present. Soft-tissue gas was also identified in both gluteal regions, the left thoracodorsal fascial plane, and the mediastinal/para-mediastinal region. Fascial planes were poorly defined, with streaky and bubbly soft-tissue gas consistent with necrotizing infection (Fig. 2). Microbiological findings

Intraoperative pus and postoperative wound exudate were sent for bacterial and fungal cultures with susceptibility testing. Cultures yielded Escherichia coli, Enterococcus raffinosus, and Candida albicans, confirming polymicrobial type I infection. Blood cultures remained negative.

Diagnostic reasoning

Differential diagnoses included severe postoperative soft-tissue infection, abdominal wall abscess, and cellulitis. However, disproportionate pain, rapid clinical progression, marked inflammatory response, and extensive fascial gas on CT strongly favored NF.

Diagnostic justification

The diagnosis of NF was based on the combination of rapidly progressive severe pain, marked systemic inflammatory response, extensive fascial gas on CT, and intraoperative demonstration of widespread necrosis along multiple fascial planes with foul-smelling purulent exudate and devitalized fascia. Although the infection originated in the perianal region and overlapped clinically with Fournier’s gangrene/perineal necrotizing soft-tissue infection, the disease had clearly extended beyond the perineum to involve the abdominal wall, gluteal region, and mediastinal/para-mediastinal region. We therefore considered the most appropriate description to be fulminant truncal NF of perianal origin.

Therapeutic intervention

Emergency surgical treatment

Based on the clinical presentation, laboratory abnormalities, CT findings, and supportive risk scores, a prompt diagnosis of NF was established. Emergency surgery was performed under general anesthesia 4 h 46 min after admission. With the patient in the left lateral lithotomy position, routine disinfection and draping were performed.

Debridement was initiated through a curvilinear incision over the left abdominal wall parallel to the umbilical level. Exploration revealed extensive necrotic fascia beneath malodorous purulent exudate. Radical debridement was extended along the left abdominal wall to the subcostal margin, and all visibly necrotic fascia and devitalized tissue were excised. The involved fascial planes were opened widely, a communicating subcutaneous tract was identified and excised, and additional necrotic fascia in the left posterior and lower abdominal wall was removed. Multiple drains were left in situ to maintain dependent drainage of the communicating fascial planes. According to the immediate postoperative transfer records, these included four abdominal (abdominal wall) drainage tubes and one perianal drain.

Further exploration toward the suprapubic region demonstrated extension of necrosis toward the right groin and pelvic compartment. The incision was therefore extended, and all devitalized tissue along this pathway was removed. Necrosis was also found along the outer aspect of the right sphincter complex toward the 6 o’clock anal crypt, where local debridement and rubber drainage were performed. An extended incision was then made at the 6 o’clock position, with counter drainage established to the inguinal region. A curved incision was also made in the right ischiorectal fossa external to the sphincter, where necrotic tissue was removed and counter drainage was connected to the left lower abdominal wall.

All cavities were repeatedly irrigated with copious normal saline. After debridement of both ischiorectal fossae, oozing from the wound base was observed, and the wound edges were trimmed to facilitate adequate drainage. Hemostasis was secured, and no active bleeding remained at the end of the procedure. The wounds were dressed with sterile gauze, and the resected specimens were sent for histopathological examination. Estimated blood loss was 50 mL, and intraoperative urine output was 300 mL. Although multiple skin incisions were required, these were not intended as limited isolated drainage openings but were strategically placed and progressively extended according to the anatomical distribution of fascial necrosis to achieve complete debridement and dependent drainage. Because the patient developed rapid shallow breathing and hemodynamic instability postoperatively, he was transferred to the intensive care unit for further management. Postoperative management

Postoperatively, the patient was transferred to the ICU and managed with endotracheal intubation, invasive mechanical ventilation, broad-spectrum antimicrobial therapy, hemodynamic support, insulin therapy, nutritional supplementation, and stress-ulcer prophylaxis. On ICU admission, ventilatory support was initiated in SIMV mode with PS/PC 12 cmH2O, PEEP 3 cmH2O, and FiO2 50%. Initial arterial blood gas analysis showed a PaO2 of 90.2 mmHg and a PaO2/FiO2 ratio of 180 mmHg.

On the morning of postoperative day 1, the patient developed worsening hypoxemia, with peripheral oxygen saturation falling to 80%. Repeat arterial blood gas analysis showed a PaO2 of 44.4 mmHg and a PaO2/FiO2 ratio of 98.6 mmHg. Bedside assessment suggested volume overload with increased pulmonary B-lines, and bronchoscopy excluded major airway obstruction. Ventilatory support was intensified, with FiO2 increased and PEEP gradually raised to 14 cmH2O. Fluid restriction and diuretic therapy were also initiated. Oxygenation subsequently improved, and later arterial blood gas analyses showed progressive recovery. The available operative record documented an intraoperative urine output of 300 mL and an estimated blood loss of 50 mL. Formal cumulative intake/output data for the intraoperative period and the first 24 postoperative hours were not fully retrievable from the available chart. However, bedside ultrasound at the time of respiratory deterioration showed volume overload with increased pulmonary B-lines, and oxygenation improved after fluid restriction and diuretic therapy.

The patient remained on invasive mechanical ventilation through postoperative day 3. After improvement in oxygenation and successful spontaneous breathing trial assessment, he was extubated on postoperative day 3 and transitioned to high-flow nasal oxygen at 50 L/min with FiO2 50%. By the following day, oxygenation had improved further, with a PaO2/FiO2 ratio of 309 mmHg. After stabilization, he was transferred back to the anorectal department. Antimicrobial therapy was subsequently de-escalated according to culture and susceptibility results. Because repeated deep wound cultures yielded Candida albicans and susceptibility testing showed fluconazole sensitivity, fluconazole was incorporated into the inpatient anti-infective regimen in addition to antibacterial therapy. The exact initiation timing and total duration of fluconazole could not be fully reconstructed from the available chart and are therefore not reported more specifically. Daily wound care, nutritional support, thromboprophylaxis, and glycemic management were continued. During the first postoperative week, the thoracic component was followed clinically in the ICU with serial assessment of respiratory symptoms, oxygenation, arterial blood gas analyses, bedside pulmonary ultrasound, bronchoscopy, inflammatory markers, and overall hemodynamic status. As oxygenation and systemic parameters improved after source control and supportive management, no additional thoracic intervention was undertaken during that period.

Follow-up and outcomes

The patient improved progressively after surgery and was discharged after 31 days of hospitalization. Inflammatory markers decreased markedly during the hospital stay, and blood glucose was maintained within an acceptable range under insulin therapy. The wound exudate gradually decreased, granulation tissue developed well, and no further progressive necrosis was observed. By the time of discharge, the wound area had decreased by more than 80%, with no evidence of active infection. At 46 days after surgery, the abdominal and anal wounds had healed well. Follow-up CT performed 79 days after surgery showed significant resolution of the inflammatory process and no newly developed gas shadow, indicating no evidence of active infection.

Changes in key laboratory parameters during hospitalization

Inflammatory markers peaked on the day of admission, with a C-reactive protein level > 200 mg/L, a white blood cell count of 43.3 × 109/L, and a procalcitonin level of 1.69 ng/mL. These markers declined rapidly over the following 10 days and had decreased to 13.5 mg/L, 9.7 × 10^9/L, and 0.16 ng/mL, respectively, by the time of discharge. The hemoglobin level reached a nadir of 80 g/L and stabilized at 86 g/L before discharge. Electrolytes and serum creatinine remained within the normal range. Postoperative wound healing

On the first postoperative day, the wound drainage volume was approximately 300 mL, and a small amount of necrotic fascia was observed at the wound base. With daily dressing changes and ethacridine lactate wet dressing, the amount of exudate gradually decreased. By postoperative day 7, the drainage volume had fallen to less than 50 mL per 24 h, and the granulation tissue appeared bright red and healthy, with no malodor or progressive necrosis. From postoperative day 14 onward, the four abdominal drainage tubes documented in the postoperative records were removed sequentially, whereas the perianal drainage tube was retained temporarily for continued dependent drainage. The wound edges showed obvious epithelialization. At discharge on postoperative day 31, the wound area had decreased by more than 80%, the wound bed was covered with healthy pink granulation tissue, and there were no signs of active infection. Follow-up dressing changes were continued in the outpatient setting. Postoperative CT findings

Follow-up abdominal CT performed 79 days after surgery showed residual soft-tissue defects and mild thickening of the left abdominal wall, bilateral pelvic sidewall regions, and left hip, with no newly developed gas shadow (Fig. 5A, B). Subcutaneous edema in the abdomen and both thighs had markedly resolved. Discussion

This case illustrates fulminant NF originating from a perianal abscess with rapid superior and contralateral extension to the abdominal wall, gluteal region, and para-mediastinal tissues. The diagnosis was supported by rapidly progressive severe pain, systemic inflammatory response, extensive fascial gas on CT imaging, and intraoperative confirmation of widespread devitalized fascia with foul-smelling purulent exudate. Although the disease initially overlapped clinically with Fournier’s gangrene/perineal necrotizing soft-tissue infection because of its perianal origin, the extensive superior and contralateral spread beyond the perineum supports describing this case as fulminant truncal necrotizing fasciitis of perianal origin [1–5].

A notable feature of this case was the anatomical pattern of spread. CT imaging and intraoperative exploration revealed that the infection originated at the 6 o’clock anal crypt and ischiorectal fossa, then tracked along the obturator fascia to the abdominal wall, crossed the pelvic and perineal compartments and extended superiorly along the thoracodorsal and para-mediastinal fascial planes [13]. These findings demonstrate why superficial skin changes underestimated the true extent of disease [14, 15]. CT was essential for diagnosis, delineating the full extent of infection, and guiding operative planning [13–15]. Radiologically, the thoracic component was characterized by gas in the left thoracodorsal plane and mediastinal/para-mediastinal region, accompanied by small bilateral pleural effusions and lower-lobe inflammatory or atelectatic changes on the admission CT. Clinically, the patient reported chest tightness and dyspnea on admission, but the thoracic extension was managed within the overall multidisciplinary treatment strategy, while primary source control was achieved through abdominal and perineal debridement.

The surgical approach involved multiple communicating incisions, progressively extended along involved fascial planes to achieve thorough debridement, and established dependent drainage. This compartment-based strategy ensured complete removal of devitalized fascia while preserving uninvolved skin. Close postoperative reassessment, including serial arterial blood gas measurements, bedside pulmonary ultrasound, and bronchoscopy, facilitated early detection and management of hypoxemic respiratory failure [3, 6, 7, 11]. In this patient, the respiratory deterioration was accompanied by bedside findings suggestive of volume overload and improved after ventilatory optimization, fluid restriction, and diuretic therapy; therefore, we intentionally used the term “postoperative hypoxemic respiratory failure” rather than ARDS. Together with broad-spectrum antimicrobial therapy, glucose control, nutritional support, and intensive care monitoring, these measures contributed to the favorable outcome after a single debridement. Microbiologically, this case represented a type I polymicrobial infection. In addition to Escherichia coli and Enterococcus raffinosus, repeated wound cultures yielded Candida albicans, whereas blood cultures remained negative. In the context of extensive deep soft-tissue necrosis, uncontrolled diabetes, and severe systemic infection, we considered the Candida isolation clinically relevant rather than simple surface colonization. Therefore, fluconazole was incorporated into the anti-infective regimen in addition to antibacterial therapy. Nevertheless, candidemia was not demonstrated, and the precise contribution of Candida albicans to disease severity cannot be determined from a single case.

This case also raises considerations regarding concomitant anorectal procedures. The patient underwent internal hemorrhoid ligation at the time of perianal abscess drainage. Intraoperatively, the necrotic process was traced toward the 6 o’clock anal crypt. However, direct involvement of the specific mucosal sites of internal hemorrhoid ligation was not clearly documented in the operative record. Therefore, a direct causal relationship between the ligation sites themselves and the necrotizing process cannot be confirmed. Although causality cannot be established from a single case, the presence of previously unrecognized and markedly uncontrolled diabetes (HbA1c 11.2%) suggests that additional elective procedures may increase tissue trauma and complicate postoperative management. In acute perianal infection, priority should remain adequate drainage and source control, especially in patients with poorly controlled diabetes [1, 9, 16].

Published reports of perineal necrotizing infection with thoracic or mediastinal extension remain exceedingly rare. Lopez-Fernandez et al. described mediastinal Escherichia coli abscess during Fournier’s gangrene, illustrating that spread beyond the perineum into the mediastinal region can occur [17]. Zhang et al. reported a retrospective case series of Fournier’s gangrene in the perineal and perianal region and emphasized early aggressive debridement, broad-spectrum antimicrobial therapy, and structured wound management as key components of successful treatment [18]. Against this background, the present case is notable for thoracodorsal and mediastinal/para-mediastinal extension controlled after single early radical debridement with close ICU monitoring and multidisciplinary support. Nevertheless, these results should not be generalized; repeat debridement remains standard when ongoing necrosis is suspected. The main lesson emphasizes early recognition, imaging-based delineation of infection extent, aggressive initial source control, and close postoperative reassessment as key components in managing rapidly progressive NF.
