Understanding the impact of valproate on male fertility: insights from preclinical and clinical meta-analysis
Authors: Muhammad Arif Asghar, Shixin Tang, Bing Wan, Hang Han, Li Ping Wong, Xiao Zhang, Qinjian Zhao
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundValproic acid (VPA) is a widely used antiepileptic drug (AED) often prescribed as a first-line treatment for many idiopathic and symptomatic generalized epilepsies. Several studies have highlighted the side effects of VPA on male fertility and reproductive factors in males, although the specific underlying etiology of these abnormalities is not clear. The present systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to assess the preclinical and clinical evidence concerning the impact of VPA on male fertility and reproductive factors.MethodsThe scientific literature was reviewed for eligibility using PubMed, Web of Science, and PsycINFO, encompassing preclinical and clinical studies. Factors related to male fertility and reproduction, such as differences in sperm count, sperm motility, and the percentage of abnormal sperm, were compared between the experimental groups treated with VPA (in both preclinical and clinical) and the control groups using the Standardized Mean Difference (SMD) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Additionally, differences in follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) and luteinizing hormone (LH) were explicitly assessed in clinical studies.ResultsMale fertility data were extracted from 7 preclinical studies (112 animals) and 5 clinical studies (274 male individuals). The results of animal studies found that the sperm count (SMD = -2.28, 95% CI: -3.39 to -1.18, P = 0.335) and sperm motility (SMD = -2.32, 95% CI: -3.34 to -1.30, P = 0.368) were decreased in the treated groups compared to the control groups. The percentage of abnormal sperm (SMD = 3.27, 95% CI: 1.98 to 4.56, P = 0.019) was significantly increased, while a non-significant reduction was revealed in the weight of the testis (SMD = -2.73, 95% CI: -4.23 to -1.23, P = 0.673) in treated groups. The outcomes of clinical studies indicated a non-significant decrease in sperm count (SMD = -0.78, 95% CI: -1.58 to 0.03, P = 0.286) among patients with epilepsy treated with VPA compared to control subjects. However, a significant reduction in sperm motility (SMD = -1.62, 95% CI: -2.81 to -0.43, P = 0.033 was observed. The percentage of abnormal sperm showed a non-significant increase (SMD = 0.93, 95% CI: -0.97 to 2.84, P = 0.616) after being treated with VPA. Furthermore, there was a non-significant reduction in the levels of FSH (SMD = -1.32, 95% CI: -2.93 to 0.29, P = 0.198) and LH (SMD = -0.96, 95% CI: -1.95 to 0.04, P = 0.211) observed in clinical participants.ConclusionThis meta-analysis of both preclinical and clinical studies revealed that VPA causes a significant reduction in male fertility and reproductive factors among male patients with epilepsy. Clinical neurologists should be more cautious when prescribing VPA, especially to young male adult patients with epilepsy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40360-024-00791-1.

Introduction

Epilepsy is a severe chronic neurological disorder, yet our understanding of its etiology and optimal treatment remains incomplete. Antiepileptic drugs (AEDs) are the first-line treatment for managing epilepsy, with over 70% of patients achieving seizure control with these medications [1]. Valproic acid (VPA) is widely used and highly effective AED in treating various types of seizures, often administered as the initial monotherapy in one-fifth of patients with epilepsy [2]. High utilization of VPA among patients with epilepsy during their reproductive and child bearing ages has also been documented in previous studies [3–6]. However, there is evidence indicating that VPA is associated with various side effects, including gastrointestinal and hepatic disorders, tremors, weight gain, blood disorders, and sedation [7]. It has been reported that VPA treatment in women with epilepsy increases the risk of menstrual disorders, anovulatory cycle, hyperandrogenism, and polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) due to its interaction with both central and peripheral hormonal systems [8]. However, the effect of VPA on the male reproductive system remains an ongoing controversy.

Various factors including psychosocial, medical, economic, and cultural influences can affect sexual function by impacting hormonal balance. Several survey-based studies emphasized the importance of male sexual health and its strong relationship with quality of life (QoL) [9, 10]. However, male sexual health has been neglected in clinical research, with few registered studies addressing this issue. Several studies have reported that VPA may interact with reproductive factors, leading to male sexual dysfunction [2, 11, 12]. Both preclinical and clinical studies have shown that prolonged use of VPA in epilepsy is associated with reduced sperm count and motility, as well as increase in abnormal sperm [11, 13–15]. Several pathways have been proposed to explain VPA’s impact on male fertility, including alterations in serum sex hormone concentrations, oxidative stress, direct effects on the gonads, and changes in mitochondrial function [14, 16]. Specifically, VPA has been shown to induce the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), leading to oxidative damage in sperm cells, which can impair motility, morphology, and overall sperm function [17]. Furthermore, VPA may alter levels of key reproductive hormones, including testosterone, further contributing to its detrimental effects on male reproductive health [18]. However, the exact mechanism underlying VPA’s effects on the male reproductive system is still poorly understood. Additionally, it is also evident that male fertility is significantly reduced in patients with epilepsy compared to those without epilepsy, regardless of VPA usage. Furthermore, the literature regarding the effect of VPA on male fertility appears inconsistent due to differences in study designs, drug combinations, follow-up duration, demographics, and limited sample sizes. Several recent reviews have explored the relationship between VPA and male fertility [2, 19, 20]. However, the overall effect of VPA on male fertility remains quantified in both preclinical and clinical research, and further evidence is needed to establish the association between male fertility and VPA usage in patients with epilepsy. Therefore, we conducted a systemic review along with a meta-analysis of both preclinical and clinical studies to gain a clearer understanding of VPA’s effect on male fertility. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first comprehensive systemic review and meta-analysis exclusively focuses on determining the potential side effects of VPA on male fertility in both preclinical and clinical experimental models.

Methods

The systematic review and meta-analysis of preclinical studies were conducted using the Collaborative Approach to Meta-analysis and Review of Animal Data in Experimental Studies (CAMARADES) bias risk checklist [21]. However, the Newcastle-Ottawa Quality Assessment Scale and PRISMA guidelines were used to assess clinical studies [22, 23]. Rigorous inclusion and exclusion criteria were applied to ensure the comprehensive capture of all relevant studies reporting the effects of VPA on male fertility in rodents or humans. Conference abstracts were excluded due to their limited information. The ethical committee approval was deemed unnecessary for this review study.

Search strategy

A systematic and comprehensive search was carried out across scientific databases, including PubMed, Medline, Web of Science, PsycINFO, the Cochrane Library, and the Chinese Biomedical Database up to January 2024, utilizing both quick and advanced search tools. The studies included in our analysis were published between from January 1, 2000, to March 31, 2024. The search terms included valproate OR valproic acid OR VPA AND male fertility OR reproduction OR reproductive function. Detailed search strategies for both preclinical and clinical studies are provided in the Table S1. Additionally, the reference lists from each identified article were manually screened for relevance, and the search was restricted to publications in English language.

Study selection

Two authors conducted the search, screening, and evaluation of studies according to a standardized method [24]. They evaluated the retrieved articles and examined the references of retrieved studies to find additional relevant studies. Only full-text articles were included, while non-original studies, those with insufficient data, or review articles were excluded. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion until a consensus was reached. Detailed criteria for study selection of preclinical and clinical studies are described below.

Preclinical criteria

Preclinical studies were considered eligible if they fulfilled the following criteria: (1) study involved mice or rats as subjects; (2) VPA was administered experimentally; (3) male fertility parameters were measured. Studies were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: (1) absence of control or matched group; (2) VPA was not administered as monotherapy; (3) incomplete data outcomes related to male fertility; (4) full-text articles were not available in English. The complete screening and selection process of preclinical studies are presented in Fig. 1. The study selection criteria were carefully established to ensure the inclusion of high-quality, relevant research. However, we acknowledge the exclusion of non-English language studies, which could introduce language bias. This decision was made to maintain consistency in the analysis and due to resource limitations, as translating and interpreting studies in multiple languages was beyond the scope of this work.

Clinical criteria

Clinical studies were considered eligible if they met the following criteria: (1) randomized controlled study design was employed; (2) VPA was administered in at least one interventional arm; (3) male fertility parameters were measured as primary or secondary outcomes; (4) study involved human’s participants. Studies were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: (1) absence of control or matched group participants; (2) VPA was not administered as monotherapy; (3) incomplete data on outcomes related to male fertility; (4) full-text articles were not available in English. The complete screening and selection of clinical studies are presented in Fig. 2.

Data extraction and quality assessment

Data extraction from preclinical and clinical studies was conducted based on CAMARADES and PRISMA guidelines, including information on published articles, study design characteristics, and data outcomes related to male fertility. Equivalency measures were applied for male fertility-related data for each meta-analysis, with final assessment data selected when assessments were performed at multiple time points. The potential risk of study bias was assessed using the CAMARADES bias risk checklist and Newcastle-Ottawa Quality Assessment Scale (NOS) for preclinical and clinical studies, respectively [21, 25]. Articles scoring over six stars on these scales were considered to be of high-quality publications. Quality assessments and the risk of bias evaluations were conducted independently by two reviewers, and any discrepancies were resolved through discussion.

Statistical analysis

Data was analyzed using the version 22 of the statistical software program Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Statistical program R was used to conduct meta-analysis. The standardized mean difference (SMD), also known as Hedges’ g, was calculated with corresponding confidence intervals to measure the levels of male fertility factors between the treated and control groups [26]. In our meta-analysis, we assessed heterogeneity using the I² statistic, which quantifies the proportion of variation across studies that is due to heterogeneity rather than chance. For analyses with low heterogeneity (I² < 50%), a fixed-effects model was employed, assuming that the true effect size was consistent across studies. Conversely, for analyses with high heterogeneity (I² ≥ 50%), a random-effects model was used, which accounts for variations in effect sizes across different studies. This approach was chosen to ensure that the results were robust and reflective of the variability in the underlying data [27]. The z-test was conducted to determine the overall significance of SMD or Hedges’ g. Egger’s regression test was also used to examine publication bias, with a P-value of 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Preclinical meta-analysis

Selection and characteristics of included preclinical studies

Initially, a total of 2962 research studies were searched, and after removing 832 duplicates, 2130 studies related to male fertility in patients with epilepsy were found in the literature search engines. Upon inspection of the titles and abstracts of these studies, 2068 were excluded, leaving 62 studies eligible for further assessment according to the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Finally, seven preclinical studies [28–34] met the inclusion criteria for meta-analysis (Fig. 1). These studies, published between 2000 and 2021, involved a total of 112 male rodent subjects, were analyzed in the preclinical studies’ meta-analysis, i.e., 56 rodents each in the treatment and control groups. Rats were used as experimental subjects in six studies, while mice were used in only one study [32]. The baseline characteristics of the seven selected publications are presented in Table 1. All seven studies assessed male fertility through quantitative analysis of sperm count, five studies measured sperm motility, and six studies examined the percentage of abnormal sperm and weight of the testes. VPA was administered in all studies, and the duration of experiments ranged from 7 to 70 days.

Meta-analysis

We assessed the preclinical publications for reported male fertility factors i.e., sperm count, sperm motility, normal percentage of sperm, and weight of testes in male rodents administered with VPA with the random-effect model due to moderate heterogeneity. The meta-analysis found a non-significant decrease in sperm count (Overall SMD = -2.28, 95% CI: -3.39 to -1.18, P = 0.335) and sperm motility (Overall SMD = -2.32, 95% CI: -3.34 to -1.30, P = 0.368) in the treated groups compared to the control groups as presented in Figs. 3 and 4, respectively.

Publication bias and heterogeneity

Analysis of funnel plots for each variable reported in preclinical studies (Figs. S2–S5) did not show significant publication bias, but several SMD values fall outside the area of the funnel plots. However, based on the Egger’s test results presented in Table 5, there was no significant evidence for publication bias detected for most of the male fertility factors (P > 0.05) except the weight of the testes (P = 0.004).

Clinical meta-analysis

Selection and characteristics of included clinical studies

A total of 2087 research studies were found in scientific search engines, and after the removal of 664 duplicate studies, 1423 studies related to male fertility in patients with epilepsy were identified. Upon examining the titles and abstracts of the searched studies, 1392 were excluded, leaving 31 clinical studies eligible for further assessment. Finally, five clinical studies [11, 13, 35–37] met our eligibility criteria for meta-analysis (Fig. 2). The baseline characteristics of the five included clinical studies are provided in Table 3. All five studies, conducted between 2003 and 2021, were prospective, with 274 male individuals analyzed in the meta-analysis of clinical studies, i.e., 99 adult subjects in treated and 192 in control groups. No significant difference was found between the ages of the treated and control groups (P > 0.05). However, the VPA dosages in all five studies were found to be within the range of recommended therapeutic concentrations for treating epilepsy. The duration of VPA treatments ranged from 16 months to 9 years. One clinical study included in the analysis utilized a crossover randomized control trial (RCT) design [13], while the other four research studies employed a parallel design. One study divided their treated group into low and high-dose VPA-receiving patients [35]. All five studies assessed male fertility factors using quantitative analysis of sperm count, sperm motility, and abnormal sperm, while four studies measured the FSH and LH levels.

Meta-analysis

We assessed the clinical research studies for reported male fertility factors (sperm count, sperm mobility, normal percentage of sperm, and FSH and LH levels) in male patients with epilepsy administered with VPA and a control group using the random effect model. The statistical meta-analysis results found that the sperm count (Overall SMD = -0.78, 95% CI: -1.58 to 0.03, P = 0.286) was decreased non-significantly in the VPA-treated patients with epilepsy compared to the control individuals, as presented in Fig. 6. However, sperm motility (Overall SMD = -1.62, 95% CI: -2.81 to -0.43, P = 0.033) was significantly reduced in the treated groups compared to the control groups (Fig. 7). The percentage of abnormal sperm (Overall SMD = 0.93, 95% CI: -0.97 to 2.84, P = 0.616) increased non-significantly in treated groups (Fig. 8).

Publication bias and heterogeneity

Visual analysis of funnel plots for each variable associated with male fertility (Figs. S8–S12) demonstrated symmetry in most of the variables, reflecting the absence of publication bias except for the percentage of abnormal sperms. Moderate heterogeneity was observed among most male fertility factors across all five clinical studies. Discussion

Despite the presence of diverse literature examining reproductive and male fertility disorders associated with AEDs in patients with epilepsy, there remains a need for clarification of mechanisms underlying this reproductive dysfunction. Our literature search indicates that this systematic review and meta-analysis represent the first comprehensive effort to report the potential impacts of VPA on male fertility in both preclinical and clinical studies. The current systematic review and meta-analysis of seven preclinical studies (112 male animals) revealed a significant side effect of VPA administration in reducing male fertility factors in rodents. The outcomes of this review revealed that VPA reduced sperm count, sperm motility, and testis weight in male rodents, alongside an increasing the percentage of abnormal sperm. The forest plots also indicated that the factors related to male fertility were reduced after the administration of VPA, with observed pooled SMDs of -2.28, -2.32, 3.27, and − 2.73 for sperm count, sperm motility, percentage of abnormal sperm, and weight of testis, respectively. These results indicate a substantial effect of VPA administration on male fertility factors compared to non-VPA controls. However, the differences between treated and control groups were not statistically significant for most male fertility factors (P < 0.05), except for the percentage of abnormal sperms.

Similarly, the meta-analysis of five clinical studies (comprising 274 male participants) also revealed that VPA-treated male patients with epilepsy exhibited abnormal male fertility factors, including sperm count, sperm motility, percentage of abnormal sperm, and FSH and LH levels, compared to male control groups. The forest plots for the meta-analysis of clinical studies similarly demonstrated a decrease in male fertility factors following VPA treatment. The observed pooled SMDs of -0.78, -1.62, 0.93, -1.32, and − 1.95 for sperm count, sperm motility, percentage of abnormal sperm, and levels of FSH and LH, respectively, reflect a substantial effect of VPA treatment on male fertility factors compared to male subjects not treated with VPA. The findings of meta-analysis on clinical studies align with the conclusions drawn from recent narrative reviews [2, 19], suggesting that VPA may have the potential to produce reversible male infertility. However, unlike these reviews, the present study employed meta-analysis to quantitatively measure the magnitude of VPA effects on male fertility factors and the extent to which the previously reported preclinical and clinical effects of VPA in different studies. The meta-analysis approach is distinguished by its ability to synthesize multiple factors related to male fertility from previously published relevant studies into a single summary.

The actual mechanism behind male infertility caused by VPA treatment remains to be completely identified. Previous research has suggested that long-term VPA administration induces oxidative stress, leading to cellular damage through the degradation of cellular proteins, DNA, and lipids [38]. Furthermore, the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) can also cause alterations in signal transduction cascades during the process of gene translation [39]. Both sperm cells and the testis contain substantial amounts of polyunsaturated fatty acids, making them susceptible to oxidative damage initiated by ROS, consequently leading to oxidative damage [40, 41]. It is also been reported that the administration of VPA causes an augmentation in lipid carbonyl level and lipid peroxidation (LPO), while significantly reduce glutathione (GSH) content in rat testis, causing oxidative damage to cellular macromolecules [31, 42]. Our meta-analysis showed that sperm count and motility decreased following VPA administration in both preclinical and clinical studies, which may lead to reproductive and sexual dysfunction in male patients with epilepsy. A decrease in sperm count and sperm motility following VPA administration is linked to an escalation in oxidative damage to cellular proteins and fats. However, it has been reported that VPA is associated with reduced levels of carnitine [43]. Carnitine facilitates the transport of long-chain fatty acids across the inner mitochondrial membrane for beta-oxidation. L-carnitine, derived from carnitine, is taken up by the spermatozoa and contributes to their maturation and motility [44]. Low carnitine levels result in lower fatty acid concentrations within mitochondria, reducing energy production and sperm motility [45]. Previous studies have shown a significant correlation between carnitine levels and sperm motility, abnormal sperm count, and testicular volume in a VPA duration-dependent and dose-dependent pattern [46, 47]. This offers yet another possible mechanism by which VPA administration adversely affects male fertility via the reduction of sperm count and sperm motility.

Some recent literature has indicated that VPA, at therapeutic doses, acts as a histone deacetylase inhibitor, causing the hyperacetylation of histone proteins [48, 49]. This alters the transition of histones to protamine, an essential phase in spermatogenesis, given that protamine is an integral nuclear protein. Moreover, VPA treatment has been shown to induce histopathological changes such as degenerative alterations in germinal cells, thinning of the germinal cell layer, vacuolization in Sertoli cells, partial cessation of maturation, impaired spermatogenesis, and increased Leydig cell population in testis tissue [15]. Additionally, it is assumed that VPA administration could inhibit specific testicular enzymes, reducing Ki-67 and phosphorylated protein expression at 41-, 51-, and 83-kDa [30]. Following VPA administration, decreased expression of Ki-67 and testicular phosphorylated proteins may have affected acrosome formation during spermiogenesis, leading to premature acrosome reaction and abnormal sperm head development. In our analysis, VPA administration caused a significant increase in the percentage of abnormal sperm count in both preclinical and clinical studies. This finding aligns the conclusions drawn from previous studies [13, 14, 50]. However, it is important to note that publication bias was detected (Egger’s test, P = 0.019) in the percentage of abnormal sperm count in clinical studies. On the other hand, all 5 eligible articles related to clinical studies, showed an increased percentage of abnormal sperm count in the male patients with epilepsy treated with VPA compared with the control groups. The abnormal sperm percentage of 15.35 ± 3.3% reported for healthy controls in the research study by Hamed et al. [35], seems to be lower than controls in other included studies, suggesting a possible publication bias regarding abnormal sperm counts. The reason for the lower abnormal sperm counts of the controls in the study by Hamed et al. might be due to the adoption of more lenient criteria for the sperm abnormality compared to other studies.

Regarding VPA’s pharmacological action, studies have reported its ability to enhance GABA synthesis while simultaneously reducing its metabolic conversion. These dual pharmacological properties of VPA enhance the inhibitory effects of GABA [51]. The GABA concentration increases after administration of VPA, which may induce negative feedback on FSH and LH, resulting in a decline in their levels among male patients with epilepsy. Conversely, GABA neurons have the capacity to directly regulate noradrenaline levels, thereby modulating FSH and LH serum levels. This alteration in the balance of the hypothalamic-pituitary-testicular (HPT) axis reduces the serum concentration of these two reproductive hormones [52]. Gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) regulates the HPT axis by modulating the secretions of FSH and LH in a pulsatile manner [53]. LH activates adenylate cyclase upon binding to Leydig cell receptors to promote testosterone synthesis, while FSH binds to receptors on Sertoli cells and produces androgen-binding protein. This protein binds in the seminiferous tubules with testosterone, facilitating the production of spermatozoa [54]. Testosterone, the principal androgen in males, extends its importance beyond supporting the seminal vesicles and prostate gland. It also intricately regulates various aspects of libido modulation and sexual physiology. Reduction in LH levels may reduce testosterone production by Leydig cells. Consequently, any reduction in FSH, LH, and testosterone serum concentrations would affect spermatogenesis and sperm maturation, potentially induce testicular atrophy. It could also impact various aspects of male sexuality, such as libido and erectile function. In addition, VPA could also produce potential impacts on women during child bearing ages, as it also affects female reproductive hormones, i.e. FSH and LH [55]. VPA has also been reported to act as an antagonist to progesterone and androgen receptors, which are implicated in the disruptions of reproductive endocrine [56]. Our study results indicate a significant reduction in LH and FSH levels following VPA treatment, which potentially may be linked to reproductive and sexual dysfunction among male patients with epilepsy.

In addition, our findings indicate that VPA significantly impacts male fertility by altering sperm parameters and inducing oxidative stress. It is also important to note that beyond male fertility, VPA has been associated with an increased risk of autism in the offspring of mothers who take the drug during pregnancy [57, 58]. This serious side effect underscores the importance of careful consideration of VPA’s risks and benefits, particularly in women of childbearing age.

However, the lack of significant differences in most of the male fertility factors within the random effect-model could be attributed to the heterogeneity among studies and the limited number of eligible selected articles, thereby reducing the statistical power of our analysis. These types of heterogeneity and asymmetry in funnel plots are well-reported problems, specifically during the meta-analysis of preclinical studies [59]. Several studies concluded that the impairments in semen parameters and reproductive hormone levels associated with VPA treatment are reversible, even after long- term treatment spanning several years [30, 34, 60]. Hence, it is essential to closely monitor male epilepsy patients on VPA for reproductive and sexual complications. If such issues are linked to VPA, switching to a different AED should be considered.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. Firstly, substantial heterogeneity was found among both preclinical and clinical analyses, determined by variations in subject characteristics, VPA dosing, treatment duration, and outcomes. While random-effects models were used to address this, the limited number of small-sized studies restricted our ability to fully explore these differences [61]. Secondly, the assessment of sexual and endocrine factors was not comprehensive. Key reproductive parameters like testosterone, prolactin, and estradiol were not consistently evaluated across all studies, limiting the scope of our conclusions. Finally, the clinical meta-analysis included only five studies, highlighting the need for more clinical research to better understand VPA’s impact on male fertility. The exclusion of non-English studies may have introduced language bias, and the variability in the quality and generalizability of the included studies further limits the findings. Future research should focus on addressing these limitations with more diverse and rigorous studies.

Conclusion

The meta-analysis of clinical studies revealed significant abnormalities in male fertility factors among male patients with epilepsy treated with VPA, while preclinical studies also observed a notable increase in male infertility factors following VPA administration in animals. Study found sperm counts, motility, and the percentage of abnormal sperm changed following administering VPA in both preclinical and clinical trials. Clinical studies showed significant reductions in FSH and LH levels following VPA treatment. To further elucidate the mechanisms by which VPA impacts male fertility, future research should focus on detailed mechanistic studies that explore the pathways of oxidative stress, hormonal regulation, and their interactions in male reproductive health. Longitudinal studies in diverse human populations are also needed to determine the long-term effects of VPA on fertility. Additionally, clinical trials investigating potential therapeutic interventions, such as antioxidants or hormone therapy, could provide valuable insights into mitigating VPA’s adverse effects. Neurologists in clinical practice should be more cautious when recommending VPA to patients with epilepsy who are in their reproductive ages. Early-stage monitoring is also crucial to assess male fertility or reproductive factors in male patients with epilepsy undergoing VPA treatment. If deemed necessary, switching to another antiepileptic drug should be considered.
