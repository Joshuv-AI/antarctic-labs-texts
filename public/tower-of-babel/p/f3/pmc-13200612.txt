Association of Hospital Surgical Volume With Outcomes in Resected Stage I Non—Small Cell Lung Cancer
Authors: Steven Tohmasi, Daniel B Eaton, Brendan T Heiden, Nikki E Rossetti, Theodore S Thomas, Martin W Schoen, Su-Hsin Chang, Ana A Baumann, Yan Yan, Mayank R Patel, Whitney S Brandt, Bryan F Meyers, Varun Puri
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroduction:Prior research examining the volume-outcome relationship for lung cancer resection has focused primarily on non-Veteran populations treated across diverse healthcare systems. This study aimed to evaluate the relationship between hospital surgical volume and postoperative outcomes for early-stage non–small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) in Veteran and non-Veteran populations in the United States.Methods:We conducted a retrospective cohort study of patients diagnosed with stage I NSCLC who underwent surgical resection in the Veterans Health Administration (VHA) from 2007–2019 and National Cancer Database (NCDB) from 2010–2019. Hospitals were categorized based on their annual volume of lung cancer resections. Multivariable regression models were used to evaluate the association between hospital surgical volume and postoperative outcomes, adjusting for relevant patient-, tumor-, and treatment-related covariates.Results:In total, 11,623 patients from the VHA and 125,213 patients from the NCDB were included. Patients who underwent surgical resection at high-volume VHA hospitals had similar risk-adjusted odds of prolonged hospitalization (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] 0.876, 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.741–1.034), 30-d major complications (aOR 1.146, CI 0.969–1.357), 90-d mortality (aOR 0.899, CI 0.666–1.215), cumulative incidence of recurrence (adjusted hazard ratio [aHR] 1.039, CI 0.923–1.169), and overall survival (aHR for death 1.060, CI 0.990–1.136), compared with those treated at low-volume VHA hospitals. In contrast, among patients within the NCDB, surgical treatment at high-volume Commission on Cancer (CoC)-accredited hospitals was associated with lower risk-adjusted odds of prolonged hospitalization (aOR 0.643, CI 0.588–0.703) and 90-d mortality (aOR 0.755, CI 0.655–0.857), as well as improved overall survival (aHR for death 0.893, CI 0.860–0.929), compared with treatment at low-volume CoC-accredited hospitals.Conclusions:The volume-outcome relationship seen in the surgical treatment of early-stage NSCLC at CoC-accredited hospitals was not observed within the VHA system. The VHA's integrated healthcare model and academic partnerships may help mitigate volume-related outcome differences following surgery for early-stage lung cancer.Keywords: Lung cancer, National cancer database, Outcomes, Regionalization, Surgery, Veteran, Veterans Health Administration, Volume

Introduction

Several studies have demonstrated variations in outcomes between high- and low-volume hospitals after major oncologic operations, including non–small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) resection.1–11 To optimize patient outcomes and resource utilization, national, single-payer healthcare systems, such as those in Canada and France, have consolidated lung cancer surgery to designated high-volume hospitals, a concept referred to as regionalization.1,8,12–14 Subsequent studies have inconsistently demonstrated long-term improvements in outcomes following the regionalization of lung cancer surgery.1,8,12–14 This raises important questions about whether similar regionalization strategies could benefit lung cancer patients navigating the diverse and often fragmented healthcare environment within the United States (US).15

The association between hospital surgical volume and outcomes following lung cancer resection within the Veterans Health Administration (VHA), the nation's largest integrated healthcare system, remains largely unexamined.16,17 Most prior research exploring the volume-outcome relationship has focused on patients treated at non-VHA hospitals, creating a critical gap in understanding whether regionalizing lung cancer surgery to high-volume centers would yield consistent benefits across the diverse healthcare models nationwide.2,4,10,11 Furthermore, a limitation of prior studies is the inclusion of heterogeneous US health systems with wide variability in patient demographics, hospital infrastructure, and surgeon experience. These differences in healthcare delivery may influence access to high-volume centers and, in turn, contribute to disparities in outcomes. Consequently, there remains a need to examine whether the volume-outcome relationship broadly observed in lung cancer surgery applies across integrated healthcare models, where barriers to care access are theoretically minimized, such as the VHA.

To address these gaps, this study aimed to examine the relationship between hospital surgical volume and postoperative outcomes for early-stage NSCLC within two national oncology databases, each reflecting distinct healthcare models: (1) the VHA, an integrated healthcare system and (2) the National Cancer Database (NCDB) representing the general US healthcare system.

Methods

Study design and patient population

This retrospective cohort study included patients diagnosed with stage I NSCLC who underwent lung resection in the VHA (between January 01, 2007 and December 31, 2019) and NCDB (between January 01, 2010 and December 31, 2019). Analyses were conducted independently within each dataset to evaluate volume-outcome relationships within the NCDB and VHA populations, rather than to draw conclusions between them. Our research protocol was approved by the Veterans Affairs St. Louis Health Care System Research and Development Committee and Institutional Review Board, who waived the requirement for informed consent given the deidentified and retrospective nature of this analysis.

VHA data were obtained from the VHA Informatics and Computing Infrastructure and Corporate Data Warehouse platforms, which integrate national clinical and administrative datasets including the Veterans Affairs Surgical Quality Improvement Program (VASQIP).18 Patients diagnosed with NSCLC were identified using International Classification of Diseases (ICD) for Oncology, third edition, codes. Procedures were identified using ICD-9/10 and Current Procedural Terminology codes. Inclusion criteria consisted of age ≥18 y, diagnosis with clinical stage I NSCLC (tumors ≤5 cm without lymph node involvement based on the American Joint Committee on Cancer's 7th edition staging system), and receipt of curative-intent pulmonary resection. We excluded patients with a missing date of diagnosis, those who received neoadjuvant therapy, and those with recurrent cancers.

Similarly, we extracted data from the NCDB 2020 Participant User File (released in 2022). The NCDB, supported by the American Cancer Society and the American College of Surgeons Commission on Cancer (CoC), collects information on approximately 70% of all cancer diagnoses annually from over 1400 CoC-accredited hospitals nationwide.19 Cases submitted from VHA hospitals are not included in the NCDB Participant User Files.20 As a result, there is no overlap or duplication of patients between the VHA and NCDB cohorts analyzed in this study. Similar inclusion and exclusion criteria were applied to the NCDB cohort, with the exception that cancer stage was determined using the NCDB analytical stage variable, which assigns clinical stage when pathological stage is unavailable, and patients treated at >1 facility were excluded, as the NCDB does not specify which hospital performed the resection. Additionally, we could not exclude patients undergoing surgery for recurrent cancer, as recurrence is not reported in the NCDB.

Volume thresholds

To categorize hospitals by surgical volume, we calculated the annual number of lung cancer resections performed at each unique VHA and CoC-accredited hospital. In the primary analysis, hospitals were categorized into three volume groups (low, medium, and high) using tertiles based on the average number of resections performed across all sites for each year of the study period. The annual thresholds for each cohort were defined as follows: low-volume (VHA: 1–14 operations; CoC-accredited: 1–10 operations), medium-volume (VHA: 15–23 operations; CoC-accredited: 11–25 operations), and high-volume (VHA: ≥24 operations; CoC-accredited: ≥26 operations). Different thresholds were chosen for each system as VHA hospitals typically have fewer annual patients and therefore lower surgical volumes. Each hospital was assigned to an annual surgical volume group for that specific year, allowing for dynamic categorization over the study period.

We also conducted a sensitivity analysis using a consistent annual surgical volume threshold across datasets. In this analysis, hospitals performing ≥40 lung cancer resections per year were classified as high-volume, while those performing <40 were considered low-volume. This threshold aligns with the minimum volume standards recommended by the Leapfrog Group, a national organization focused on healthcare quality and patient safety.21,22

Covariates

Patient-, tumor-, and treatment-related data were collected including age, sex, race, distance lived from treatment facility (calculated using straight-line distance from the center of the patienťs residential ZIP code to the hospital address), comorbidities, tumor size, histologic type, surgical approach (i.e., minimally invasive surgery [MIS], thoracotomy), lung resection type (i.e., lobectomy, pneumonectomy, segmentectomy, wedge resection), number of lymph nodes sampled, and resection margins. Comorbidities were evaluated using the Charlson–Deyo Comorbidity Index (CCI), a composite measure derived from ICD-9/10 codes.23,24 Timely surgery was defined differently in each dataset due to inherent differences in data structure. In the NCDB, time to surgery was measured from the clinically coded date of diagnosis to the date of surgery, using an 8-wk cutoff consistent with prior literature.25 In contrast, the VHA dataset allowed for a more precise definition of diagnosis based on radiographic findings, and therefore time to surgery was calculated from the most recent preoperative computed tomography scan, providing a more objective measure of treatment timeliness.26

Outcomes

The study's primary outcome was overall survival (OS), defined as the time between surgery and all-cause mortality. For the VHA cohort, the VHA Death Ascertainment File was used to identify the date of death and OS data were censored at the end of the study's follow-up period (January 15, 2025). For the NCDB cohort, OS was calculated from the date of surgery to the reported date of death or last contact. We also assessed multiple secondary outcomes, including prolonged hospitalization (hospital stay lasting ≥14 d), 30-d readmission, 30-d major complications (available only in the VHA dataset), 90-day mortality, and cumulative incidence of recurrence (CIR; available only in the VHA dataset). Major complications were defined as occurrences of respiratory failure, empyema, pneumonia, stroke, myocardial infarction, or renal failure within 30 d following surgery and were detected through query of the VASQIP database and ICD-9/10 codes.23,24,27 CIR was assessed using the Corporate Data Warehouse Oncology database and was further supplemented by identifying additional Current Procedural Terminology and ICD-9/10 diagnoses codes suggestive of recurrence, as previously described in the VHA literature.26

Statistical analysis

Multivariable logistic regression was used to examine the association between hospital surgical volume and postoperative outcomes. OS was evaluated using multivariable Cox proportional hazards model. CIR was assessed with a multivariable competing risk model (Fine–Gray subdistribution hazard model), with cancer recurrence as the outcome and mortality as a competing event. If neither event occurred by the end of the study's follow-up period, the patient was censored. Regression results are presented as adjusted odds ratios (aORs) or hazard ratios (aHRs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The statistical models assessing the association between hospital surgical volume and postoperative outcomes in VHA patients adjusted for age, sex, race, smoking status, body mass index, comorbidities, American Society of Anesthesiologists physical status class, number of medications prescribed in the year prior to surgery, distance lived from treatment facility, annual hospital surgical volume, area deprivation index score, tumor size, tumor grade, tumor location, histology type, year of surgery, timely surgery, surgical approach, lung resection type, number of lymph nodes sampled intraoperatively, surgical resection margins, and pathological upstaging. Similarly, the statistical models including patients from the NCDB adjusted for age, sex, race, comorbidities, distance lived from treatment facility, annual hospital surgical volume, tumor size, histology type, year of surgery, insurance type, timely surgery, surgical approach, lung resection type, number of lymph nodes sampled intraoperatively, and surgical resection margins.

Summary statistics are presented as means with standard deviations (SDs) or median values with interquartile ranges (IQRs) for continuous data and as frequencies with corresponding proportions for categorical data. Continuous variables were compared using the Studenťs t-test or Mann–Whitney U test, as appropriate. Categorical variables were analyzed using Pearson's chi-squared test. Missing data were minimal and categorized as unknown. Statistical tests were two-sided, and significance was defined as a P value <0.05. The Bonferroni correction method was applied to adjust the significance level when analyzing multiple groups. Analyses were performed using SAS version 9.3 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC).

Results

Patient characteristics

In total, 11,623 and 125,213 patients from the VHA and NCDB met inclusion criteria, respectively. The mean ages were 67.9 (SD: 7.6) and 68.4 y (SD: 9.1) in the VHA and NCDB cohorts, respectively. Most patients in both cohorts were White (VHA: n = 9,526, 82.0%; NCDB: n = 109,287, 87.3%), diagnosed with adenocarcinoma (VHA: n = 6,418, 55.2%; NCDB: n = 85,319, 68.1%), had tumors ≤3 cm (VHA: n = 8,052, 69.3%; NCDB: n = 63,282, 50.5%), and underwent a lobectomy (VHA: n = 8,139, 70.0%; NCDB: n = 88,490, 70.7%) via thoracotomy (VHA: n = 6,155, 53.0%; NCDB: n = 65,229, 52.1%). NCDB patients were frequently females (n = 69,513, 55.5%) without comorbidities (CCI score 0: n = 64,149, 51.2%). Conversely, the majority of VHA patients were males (n = 11,157, 96.0%) with multiple comorbidities (CCI score ≥2: n = 7,918, 68.1%). Additional characteristics from the VHA and NCDB cohorts are displayed in Tables 1 and 2, respectively. The median follow-up length was 11.0 (IQR: 8.2–14.2) and 5.3 (IQR: 3.2–7.8) years in the VHA and NCDB cohorts, respectively.

Relationship between VHA hospital volume and postoperative outcomes

Of the 11,623 VHA patients, 2615 (22.5%), 3404 (29.3%), and 5604 (48.2%) underwent surgery at low-, medium-, and high-volume VHA hospitals, respectively. Compared to patients treated at low-volume VHA hospitals, patients who underwent surgery at high-volume VHA hospitals were significantly more likely to have no comorbidities (CCI score 0: 12.2% versus 9.2%, P < 0.001), travel >50 miles for surgical care (44.8% versus 34.2%, P < 0.001), receive timely surgery (73.3% versus 68.0%, P < 0.001), have MIS (56.3% versus 29.5%, P < 0.001), undergo sublobar resection (29.1% versus 27.4%, P < 0.001), have ≥10 lymph nodes sampled (40.6% versus 28.9%, P < 0.001), and have pathological upstaging (13.5% versus 11.3%, P = 0.009). Median hospital length of stay was 7 d (IQR: 5–11), 7 d (IQR: 4–10), and 6 d (IQR: 4–9) at low-, medium- and high-volume VHA hospitals, respectively (P < 0.001). In multivariable analyses, receipt of surgery at high-volume VHA hospitals was associated with similar risk-adjusted odds of prolonged hospitalization (aOR 0.876, CI 0.741–1.034), 30-d major complications (aOR 1.146, CI 0.969–1.357), 30-d readmission (aOR 0.867, CI 0.698–1.076), 90-d mortality (aOR 0.899, CI 0.666–1.215), OS (aHR for death 1.060, CI 0.990–1.136; Fig. 1), and CIR (aHR 1.039, CI 0.923–1.169; Fig. 2), compared to treatment at low-volume VHA hospitals (Table 3).

Discussion

This study found that receipt of pulmonary resection for early-stage NSCLC at high-volume CoC-accredited hospitals was associated with lower risk of perioperative and long-term mortality than treatment at low-volume CoC-accredited hospitals. However, this trend was not observed among patients treated within the VHA, where hospital surgical volume was not associated with postoperative morbidity or mortality. Notably, patients treated at both high-volume VHA and CoC-accredited hospitals were more likely to receive timely surgery, MIS, parenchyma-preserving sublobar resection, and comprehensive lymph node sampling than those treated at low-volume centers. These findings suggest that high-volume hospitals may more consistently follow guideline-concordant surgical quality metrics for NSCLC, underscoring the need to invest in national initiatives to promote adherence to these standards to ensure all patients benefit, regardless of where they receive care.28

Several factors may explain the differences in outcomes between high- and low-volume CoC-accredited hospitals. Studies on hospital surgical volume support the idea that “repetition builds expertise” for lung cancer surgery, as high-volume centers with more experienced surgical care teams tend to achieve better outcomes.2–4,6,7 Moreover, high-volume hospitals are more likely to perform MIS techniques, which promote quicker postoperative recovery and fewer major complications.29 High-volume hospitals are also often larger teaching institutions with more resources, including greater house staff and subspecialty presence, which may aid in the early recognition and management of postoperative complications, potentially reducing the risk of failure to rescue.3 Transparent reporting of surgical outcomes data, as seen with initiatives such as the VASQIP, may enable self-assessment and quality improvement efforts among US hospitals to enhance the quality of care across entire health systems.30 Additionally, adopting evidence-based recovery pathways after lung cancer surgery may also help reduce disparities in outcomes between low- and high-volume hospitals.31

The absence of a volume-outcome relationship within the VHA cohort may be due in part to its integrated healthcare model, which theoretically provides more equitable healthcare access than other health systems within the US. The VHA provides comprehensive care to the approximately 8000 Veterans diagnosed with lung cancer annually.16,17,32 It has previously been shown that Veterans with NSCLC have comparable, if not better, outcomes to the general population, with the integrated healthcare model contributing to reduced racial and socioeconomic disparities in outcomes.16,27 Veterans benefit from services such as free transportation to perioperative appointments, referrals for health-related social needs, and comprehensive coverage for outpatient visits, medications, and procedures, often with minimal to no out-of-pocket expenses.33,34 Additionally, the VHA serves as a training site for approximately 95% of US medical schools, with nearly 70% of VHA physicians holding academic faculty appointments.35,36 Unlike many smaller US hospitals, the VHA's academic partnerships may contribute to the presence of experienced thoracic oncology and surgical care teams even at low-volume VHA hospitals. For instance, a patient at a low-volume VHA hospital may still receive care from a surgeon who is a faculty member at a nearby academic medical center. Collectively, these system-level factors may help reduce variation in the quality of surgical and oncology care across VHA hospitals.

Although we observed no significant differences in short- and long-term postoperative outcomes among VHA hospitals of varying surgical volume, our findings reveal important variation in operative quality indicators. Specifically, lower volume VHA centers demonstrated lower rates of intraoperative nodal sampling and pathologic upstaging. We acknowledge that these factors could influence the completeness of staging and potentially impact long-term oncologic outcomes. While outcomes may appear volume-independent in this setting, the observed disparities in operative quality metrics suggest that surgical volume alone may not fully capture differences in lung cancer care across VHA sites. These differences may reflect variations in surgical technique or compliance with lymph node sampling guidelines and warrant further investigation.

Notably, we also found that surgical patients at high-volume hospitals were more likely to travel farther distances for care, highlighting the potential logistical and financial challenges affecting lung cancer patients when seeking care at experienced centers. Farther travel distances may limit access to postoperative care due to disrupted care coordination and fragmentation, especially when multiple treatment teams are involved.15 In our study, non-White patients and those without private insurance more often received care at low-volume CoC-accredited hospitals aligning with prior research demonstrating that racial minorities and Medicaid patients are disproportionally treated for lung cancer at lower volume hospitals and experience higher mortality rates.37,38 Future studies should investigate the impact of travel distance on patient experiences and outcomes to inform policies that balance the potential benefits of regionalization with the need for equitable access to high-quality lung cancer care.

This study has several strengths. First, we utilized two nationally representative oncology databases, enabling a robust analysis of Veteran and non-Veteran populations receiving lung cancer care within two different healthcare models. Second, both the VHA and NCDB adhere to comparable coding standards, minimizing potential sources of bias. Third, the analysis incorporated rigorous statistical methodologies to adjust for relevant demographic-, tumor-, and operation-related differences between groups. Fourth, we defined hospital surgical volume categories using the real-world distribution of case volumes and the Leapfrog Group's discretionary volume threshold (≥40 lung resections annually), which is met by only 15–27% of US hospitals.22,39

This study has some limitations. One constraint was the inability to fully mitigate fundamental differences between the two databases, particularly concerning the NCDB's lack of granular clinical details including smoking status and postoperative complications. Consequently, our conclusions could be affected if additional variables were available for analysis. Additionally, it is well established that lung cancer patients benefit from a multidisciplinary care approach and that outcomes are improved when resections are performed by board-certified thoracic surgeons.40,41 However, due to limitations in the available datasets, we were unable to account for surgeon experience and institutional support in this analysis. Last, direct comparisons between the two cohorts are limited by differences in patient populations and staging definitions. The VHA cohort included patients with clinical stage I disease, whereas the NCDB cohort used the analytical stage variable, which prioritizes pathologic stage but defaults to clinical stage when pathologic data are unavailable. This approach was necessary due to high missingness in the clinical stage variable in the NCDB, as previously reported in the literature.42,43 Given these differences and the use of distinct covariates in the respective statistical models, our analyses were designed to evaluate volume-outcome relationships for stage I NSCLC within each dataset, rather than across them.

Conclusions

Hospital surgical volume was associated with differences in postoperative outcomes for stage I NSCLC patients treated at CoC-accredited hospitals, with high-volume centers generally demonstrating better outcomes after risk adjustment. In contrast, this volume-outcome relationship was not observed among patients treated within the VHA system. These findings may reflect features of the VHA's integrated healthcare model, which could help attenuate volume-related disparities observed in other healthcare settings within the US. Additionally, the VHA's academic affiliations and system-wide infrastructure may contribute to maintaining high-quality lung cancer care, even at lower volume centers, though further research is needed to explore these potential mechanisms.
