Exploring the health promotion needs of refugees in a regional town in NSW, Australia: protocol for a two-phase participatory co-designed qualitative research study
Authors: Masum Billah, Ratika Kumar, Gail Moloney, Michele Greenwood, Gillian Gould
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractIntroductionRefugees experience significant health needs and well-being inequities. Smoking tobacco, nutrition disorders, alcohol use and physical inactivity are potential contributors to developing non-communicable diseases and mental health conditions. This study aims to explore refugees’ health promotion needs in a regional town in New South Wales (NSW), Australia, suggest appropriate health promotion methods, and co-design a health promotion intervention. Privileging refugees’ voices and experiences is central to co-designing appropriate health promotion interventions.MethodsWe will employ a two-phase participatory qualitative co-design method. As there is a lack of knowledge about refugees’ health needs, a participatory research design has the potential to explore the topics holistically. The social–ecological model and the behaviour change wheel model will guide this study. During Phase 1, a semistructured interview guide will be used for in-depth interviews and focus group discussions with refugees. A deductive reflexive thematic analysis will be applied to analyse data using NVivo. In Phase 2, two workshops will be conducted with refugees and health professionals. A reflexive thematic analysis will be performed to identify the top health promotion strategies.Ethics and disseminationEthical approval was obtained from the Human Research Ethics Committee (HREC) of the North Coast NSW Local Health District (HREA370 2023/ETH00444). The Human Research Ethics Committee approved a minimisation of duplication at a regional university in Australia (SCU HREC 2024/106). Study findings will be disseminated through embedding chapters in the PhD thesis, publishing high-quality papers and presenting at conferences, lay reports, newsletters and media.Keywords: Health Education, Refugees, Smoking Reduction, Physical Fitness, NUTRITION & DIETETICS, Health Equity

Introduction

Refugees are defined as people located outside of their country of origin because of fear, persecution, conflict, violence or other circumstances that have seriously disturbed public order in their country of origin.1 As a result, they require ‘international protection’.1 Globally, 37.6 million refugees were forcibly displaced due to violence, conflict and human rights violations at the end of 2023.2 In 2023, approximately 6.4 million refugees originated from the Syrian Arab Republic and Afghanistan, 6.1 million from Venezuela and 6.0 million from Ukraine, respectively.2 The Islamic Republic of Iran hosted around 3.8 million refugees, Türkiye hosted 3.3 million and Colombia hosted 2.9 million.2 Since World War II, refugees and humanitarian entrants have been welcomed in Australia, and the number is projected to pass one million in late 2025.3

Refugees experience significant health needs and inequities in well-being. Migrants, including refugees, account for 31.5% of the Australian population and experience chronic diseases disproportionately.4 5 These culturally and linguistically diverse (CALD) populations remain significantly under-represented in health research and health interventions, including clinical trials.5 Accessing health services is challenging for refugee communities in high-income countries.6 7 A review of 64 studies of CALD populations in Australia showed that insufficient health literacy, multimorbidity, unhealthy food, language and communication factors, inadequate interpretation services and insufficient cultural competency of service providers at the community and organisational levels were cited as reasons for not accessing health services.8 Likewise, several structural disadvantages and vulnerabilities at the system and policy level (ie, shortage and turnover of staff, travel time) make accessing health service utilisation for these disadvantaged communities challenging.8

Non-communicable disease burden affects quality of life and life expectancy.9 Public health experts and policymakers emphasise early prevention, detection and treatment of modifiable health risk behaviours (ie, diet, tobacco and alcohol use) to lessen chronic health burdens, including mortality.10 The WHO emphasises monitoring and surveillance of risky health behaviours to prevent new cases in low-resource settings.11

Health promotion is a comprehensive approach to promoting health by engaging multiple stakeholders and using various strategies.12 13 Health promotion responds to direct or indirect impacts on health, such as inequalities, lifestyle and consumption changes, and cultural beliefs.12 13 Mass media can be used to convey smoking, nutrition, alcohol and physical activity (SNAP) related health messages to populations. For instance, antitobacco campaigns create awareness about tobacco control or smoking cessation using television, radio, electronic billboards, the Internet and social media.14 15

Given the multifactorial nature of refugee health needs, this study is expected to inform the development of a complex intervention. Complex interventions are characterised by multiple interacting components, diverse stakeholders and varied outcomes.16,18 A systems thinking perspective highlights the interconnections between individuals, communities and health services, while complexity science emphasises the dynamic nature of these relationships.19,21 Applying these approaches ensures that any intervention arising from this formative research is not only evidence-based but also acceptable, feasible and transferable across refugee contexts.18 22

This research project will contribute evidence to support the co-development of health promotion interventions for Burmese and Iraqi/Syrian refugees living in a regional town in New South Wales (NSW), Australia. The local refugee health clinic has been a multidisciplinary primary healthcare clinic for newly arrived humanitarian entrants since 2006.23 This clinic streamlines the early detection of health conditions of humanitarian entrants and provides effective healthcare in a culturally appropriate and timely manner.23 It also facilitates appropriate referrals of refugees to health services, including general practices.23 This regional town has been a settlement for refugees since 2001.23 As of May 2024, approximately 3000 new arrivals have settled, with Burmese and Iraqi/Syrian refugees being the largest community in the region.24 For instance, 354 refugees, the highest in any year, were resettled in this regional town in 2019.24 Approximately 46% of refugees were from Iraqi/Syrian communities, and around 42% were from Burmese.24

Refugees experience difficulty finding general practitioners (GPs) interested in taking refugee patients and referral services. Refugees also struggle to find bilingual interpreters when needed. Health education and information on SNAP behaviours are also not tailored to refugees due to insufficient health literacy, language and communication factors.24

The refugee health clinic provides general medical care and health assessments, initial medications, supplies free of charge, immunisation catch-up, influenza vax clinics, health education and information, advocacy and referrals to medical specialists, STARTTS (NSW Service for the Treatment and Rehabilitation of Torture and Trauma Survivors).24 This multidisciplinary clinic ensures comprehensive health screening, referrals to other specialists, referrals to a permanent GP in the community and timely follow-up for refugees settled in Coffs Harbour.23 Immediate outcomes involve improved knowledge and access to the Australian refugee healthcare system.23 Long-term impacts include improved long-term health outcomes, reduced adverse health outcomes and better settlement experience.23 This study aims to explore refugees’ unaddressed health promotion needs in a regional town in NSW and suggest appropriate health promotion methods influencing health outcomes. This study will co-design a health promotion blueprint for refugees. Privileging refugees’ voices and experiences is central to co-designing appropriate health promotion interventions.

Theoretical framework

Complex interventions are referred to as interventions that comprise several interacting components.16 There are also other contributing factors (ie, number and variability of outcomes, degree of flexibility or tailoring of the intervention permitted) that evaluators should consider.16 The Medical Research Council identified several steps to develop a complex intervention.16 First, identify the existing evidence about similar interventions and the methods that have been applied to evaluate them.16 If there is no recent evidence, a high-quality systematic review of the relevant literature should be conducted and updated as the evaluation proceeds.16 Second, theory about the likely change process needs to be developed by focusing on the existing evidence and theory, supplemented by new primary research if needed.16 The nature and direction of change are often complicated issues for a complex intervention at the outset.16 Researchers must determine whether they are developing the intervention or evaluating one already developed.16 Third, the modelling process and outcomes: modelling a complex intervention can provide significant guidelines about the design of both the intervention and the evaluation before a full-scale evaluation is in place.16 Several substantial studies may be required to refine the design before progressing to a full-scale evaluation.16

The nature of complex intervention research is intricate. The scope of complex intervention should consider developing, identifying and evaluating the system interventions, leading to system change.25 26 The social–ecological model and the behaviour change wheel (BCW) model will guide this study.

The social–ecological model

The social–ecological model is a recognised system-level approach in health promotion research.27 This model encompasses the complex, reciprocal and interdependent societal factors influencing our health.28 All factors are argued to be reciprocally connected. Health promotion researchers explore cause-and-effect relationships in a multilayered social environment. According to the Centre for Disease Control and Prevention,29 four factors are grounded in the social–ecological theory influencing health (figure 1).

The social–ecological model: a framework for prevention.29

First, individual health characteristics and other attributes (ie, age, gender, education and health history) affect a person’s health behaviour. Second, relationships define a person’s intimate circle, where parents, friends, partners and family members are the influencers who contribute to their experiences. Third, community includes social relationships developed in settings (ie, schools, neighbourhoods and workplaces) affecting the community’s health. Fourth, societal mechanisms involve sociocultural norms and educational, economic and social policies that positively or negatively impact health outcomes. These factors create, neutralise or reduce socioeconomic inequalities and inequities among groups.29,31

To identify the health promotion needs regarding SNAP behaviours, the findings of this study will be interpreted using four layers of the social–ecological model. For instance, tobacco addiction that started at age 15 may be stopped by taking the following steps.

At the individual level, knowledge and awareness can be imparted by providing guidelines on tobacco smoking cessation. Repeated attempts to stop smoking may help people overcome smoking behaviour. At the stage of the interpersonal relationship, friendships with peers who do not smoke tobacco and engaging in volunteer or humanitarian work may help stop smoking among refugees. At the community level, inviting a doctor or public health expert to conduct group discussion sessions or workshops on smoking cessation may benefit refugee communities. At the societal level, warning messages about smoking cessation on televisions and electronic billboards and imposing additional charges to buy tobacco products may contribute to stopping tobacco smoking.

The behaviour change wheel model

The BCW is credited as the most comprehensive and applied methodology to promote behaviour change interventions.32 33 The developers of the BCW consulted 19 domain-specific intervention frameworks and synthesised their insights. The BCW comprises three concentric circles in the behaviour system—the COM-B model is the central focus.

This model explains the interactional mechanisms that contribute to designing and promoting behaviour change interventions at the macro level. A specific intervention can change one or more components in the system. The causal connections can decrease or increase the effect of particular interventions, leading to changes elsewhere. Capability, opportunity and motivation are the three constituents of interaction that produce behaviour and influence each other consecutively.34

The single and double-headed arrows demonstrate potential connections among the constituents (figure 2). Capability and opportunity can influence motivation to generate behaviour, which can counter-influence capability, motivation and opportunity.

The COM-B model.34

From a macro-level perspective, community and state-level policies and interventions may affect these substantial changes. The study findings will be aligned with these constructs of the intervention functions and policy categories of the BCW. The retrieved narratives will assist the research team and participants in coproducing health promotion interventions for refugees in regional Australia.

Methods

Study design

This study employs a participatory qualitative research design. Where there is sparse knowledge on an issue, a participatory research design has the potential to explore the topics holistically.35 36 Refugees have various unique health needs that are potentially unknown and unexplored. So, the participatory research will be employed to uncover their complex health needs. The engagement of refugees to count their voices regarding their health in research is rare.37 A standardised participatory research approach can contribute to designing health promotion interventions for refugees.37 38 This participatory research emerged as a new paradigm in recent years,37 which will be used to explore their multilayered complex health needs and associated challenges, leading to the design of health promotion interventions for the refugee population.

The study will comprise two phases. Phase 1 includes exploratory face-to-face focus group discussions and in-depth interviews with 20 Burmese and Iraqi/Syrian refugees. Each focus group and in-depth interview will take around 60 min. It will be conducted in the meeting rooms at a local refugee health clinic and a regional university campus.

Phase 2 comprises two co-design workshops. In workshop 1, approximately 10 refugees will be introduced to the themes gathered during Phase 1 and invited to contribute to a discussion to identify and develop suitable health promotion methods. In workshop 2, 10 health promotion officers, public health researchers and health professionals will be invited to contribute to a discussion to develop ideas for health promotion materials. Each workshop takes approximately 60 min. MB will lead and facilitate each focus group discussion and workshop and conduct the in-depth interviews. All the sessions will be audio-recorded and transcribed using online software. The planned study duration is 18 months, starting from June 2024 and ending in December 2025.

Research team

GG leads this PhD project as a coordinating principal investigator and supervises MB. MG guides the project as co-principal investigator and co-supervises MB. A/P GM and RK guide the project as research investigators and co-supervise MB. The other members include MB (PhD candidate and co-investigator), Dr Patricia Collie (co-investigator), Mr Robin Auld (research associate), Ms Teisha Schirmer (research associate) and Ms Layla Zum Akter (research associate).

Participants and sampling

Burmese, Iraqi and Syrian refugees will be recruited for the focus group discussions, in-depth interviews and workshop 1. Refugees of all genders aged 18 and over will be interviewed. Refugees must reside in a specified regional town in NSW or the surrounding areas. Refugees who have current health behaviours such as people who smoke, people who have never smoked, alcohol users, non-alcohol users and engagement with healthcare will be purposively sampled for this study. A balance of age and genders will also be considered for purposive sampling.

Refugees who can give informed consent will be recruited only. Health professionals living in the specified regional town in NSW or the surrounding areas will be invited to participate in workshop 2. This study will follow purposive sampling to recruit refugees from Burmese (n=10) and Iraqi/Syrian (n=10) communities. For conducting each focus group discussion, refugees will be grouped according to their spoken languages and gender, if needed, after recruitment.

Recruitment

Information sessions will be conducted at a regional TAFE campus to recruit enrolled students. Recruitment materials will be posted onsite at Settlement Service International and North Coast Settlement Service (NCSS), dedicated to refugees and migrants. Recruitment materials will be posted on NCSS’s Facebook pages.

A local neighbourhood centre and a university student association will post recruitment materials onsite and on relevant Facebook pages. MB will use suitable social media pages and groups, such as a local community notice board’s Facebook page. Investigators in the region will use personal connections and networks to recruit participants through word-of-mouth. Flyers will be put in international grocery stores, cafes and restaurants in the regional town. Recruited participants will be encouraged to provide referrals and disseminate study information to potential candidates.

Refugee participants will be invited to workshop 1. During Phase 1, their names and preferred contact details will be recorded. Participants will receive the participant information sheet and consent form (PICF) for the workshop if they wish to participate. Health professionals within the research investigators’ networks will be invited to workshop 2 via email. They will receive the PICF as an attachment. MB will confirm their participation based on their interest. They may return the signed PICF electronically or in person before workshop 2 begins.

Data collection

During Phase 1, a semistructured interview guide will be used for data collection (online supplemental material 1). It was developed by consulting the available literature,839,41 and the study’s specific objectives. During phases 1 and 2, the Teams software, logged into a laptop, will transcribe in real-time and audio record sessions. Bilingual interpreters will provide real-time translation support where needed. A digital voice recorder will serve as a backup. MB will download audio recording sessions and transcripts from the online system each time after independently completing in-depth interviews, focus group discussions and workshops. MB will play each audio-recorded session and compare it with the respective transcripts. During phase 2, transcription of workshops will be conducted using Otter.ai software. All participants will be reimbursed for their time ($A50 as a shopping voucher—not redeemable for alcohol or tobacco).

Data management and access procedures

The digital recordings will be immediately saved onto a university password-protected laptop and the cloud-based server. MB will go back to the participants to check the completed transcript. At least 25% of all the transcripts will go through this member-checking process. Once the transcript is deemed accurate, all recordings will be destroyed. All data will be deidentified and stored on university servers, accessible only to designated investigators. Scanned lists, consent forms and withdrawal forms will also be stored there. For interviews, numbers will be used to identify participants and remove data in the event of a withdrawal. Any documents, such as reports or publications, will not contain individual identifying information. Participant information will solely be used for the research study and shared with their consent, except as mandated by law. All the data will be retained on a university server for 5 years after the publication of the research results, after which all electronic data will be securely destroyed.

Data analysis

Phase 1

A deductive reflexive thematic analysis method will be used to analyse data.42 MB will conduct qualitative data analysis using NVivo software (V.14). The research team will support data analysis. MB and RK from the research team will read and reread the transcripts closely and identify codes for analysis. Both coders will complete 25% of the coding for the project and compare their results for quality assurance. MB will then code the remaining transcripts. MB will check the transcripts to ensure all information is captured. After reading and rereading the transcripts, codes will be generated. After following a check and recheck process, only appropriate codes will be sorted out for further steps, provided that the compatibility and rigour of the coding process are maintained. The identified codes will be used to develop subthemes leading to themes.

Phase 2

A reflexive thematic analysis will be performed to identify the top health promotion strategies discussed in workshops 1 and 2.43 The APEASE criteria will be applied (Affordability, Practicability, Effectiveness and cost-effectiveness, Acceptability, Side effects and safety, Equity)33 34 to evaluate the health promotion strategies. A qualitative assessment technique will be applied to assess health promotion strategies in both workshops. MB will use this technique to determine participants’ views on each health promotion strategy. MB will record the final appropriate health promotion strategies based on the consensus of participants in the discussions.

The immediate outcomes will help to identify the health interventions for quitting smoking (ie, written materials, app-based messages), reducing alcohol use (ie, group discussion, mass media), increasing physical exercise (ie, workshops, education sessions) and encouraging healthy food (ie, cooking programmes, healthy canteen). Based on the results of this assessment, recommendations will be made in a report to the Local Health District (LHD). Health promotion is a critical issue for refugees worldwide, and our health promotion findings will be generalisable for refugees in Australia and other countries.

Outcomes and significance

Phase 1

People from refugee backgrounds are at the centre of NSW Health.44 NSW Health responds flexibly to meet new and emerging healthcare needs for refugees by collaborating with other government agencies and non-government organisations.44 Our study findings will help the participants, healthcare professionals, policymakers and health promotion officers. We expect the findings to inform the health promotion needs of Burmese and Iraqi/Syrian refugees in regional Australia, along with appropriate health promotion strategies.

Phase 2

Refugee health is a priority in NSW Health.44 NSW Health tends to address and reduce health inequities experienced by refugee communities.44 Along with considering current health conditions, NSW Health also employs holistic approaches to healthcare, acknowledging people’s life experiences and family conditions, cultural diversity and spiritual beliefs.44 For instance, strengthening efforts to prevent and reduce tobacco use among people from CALD communities who have come from countries with high rates of tobacco use is a national priority in the Australian National Tobacco Strategy 2023–2030.45

In the project report, presentations and publications, we will provide policy and practice suggestions on health promotion methods and strategies for preventing tobacco use and other SNAP behaviours for refugee communities. The LHD may directly implement some recommendations, while others may require feasibility testing through further implementation research. The results will inform future funding applications for designing and implementing local and national interventions.

Ethics and dissemination

Ethical considerations

Consent

A PICF will be provided to the participants about the privacy and confidentiality of the data. Some refugees cannot read written language, while others have a minimum literacy level.24 Interpreters will read aloud and translate the PICF to obtain participants’ consent. According to the National Statement (NS), oral expression is allowed depending on the participant’s personal and social circumstances (NS 2.2.5). Bilingual interpreters are registered at the Hunter New England Healthcare Interpretation Service. These interpreters are accredited by the National Accreditation Authority for Translators and Interpreters, followed by a professional code of ethics. Participants’ understanding will be monitored through body language and verbal expression. An online and a digital audio voice recorder will record participants’ verbal consent. Participants will be asked to sign the written consent form.

Each participant is free to withdraw from the study at any time without consequence. Participants will be provided with a withdrawal form with their name in a sealed, postage-paid envelope, which participants can post for withdrawal. Participants will have the researchers’ contact details for further communication. Participants will be removed from the analysis if participants withdraw. Focus group and workshop data will be removed by cross-checking the deidentified background information. Participants will be advised that their information will be used for academic purposes to support the potential development of policy and practice interventions. No coercion will be used. Participants remain free to ask questions and clarify concerns face-to-face or via phone and email. Participants may get advice from their family, health provider or community member.

This research complies with the NS on Ethical Conduct in Human Research 2023. Questions are worded sensitively. Any question that makes anyone uncomfortable or causes distress. In that case, participants are free to let the researcher know their preferences, such as to skip that question, take a break or withdraw entirely from the research.

If participants become distressed and would like support, they will be asked if they would like to contact Lifeline for 24/7 telephone support. Potential participants have an unequal relationship with the researchers and healthcare providers. It may influence the participant’s decision, so that the following measures will be considered.

First, participants will be reassured that they will not experience different medical and settlement issues depending on whether they agree or not to be involved. Second, participants will receive a copy of the PICF beforehand. Third, we will organise individual interviews as an alternative option for the focus group discussions. Fourth, all data will be confidential. Finally, vulnerable communities experience a risk of being over-researched. Our initial consultation with the bilingual interpreters suggests that refugees in this regional town have minimal prior research engagement.

Ethical approval was obtained from the Human Research Ethics Committee (HREC) of the North Coast NSW LHD (HREA370 2023/ETH00444). The Human Research Ethics Committee approved a minimisation of duplication at a regional university in Australia (SCU HREC 2024/106).

Patient and public involvement

We discussed with the bilingual interpreters from Burmese (Myanmar) and Kurdish-Kurmanji (Iraq/Syria) language groups. We also shared our research thoughts and ideas with the professional GPs and nurses at the refugee health clinic in a regional town in NSW, Australia. We received some suggestions from the healthcare professionals and bilingual interpreters before designing the research plans.

Dissemination plan

This research will be part of a PhD thesis for the Faculty of Health at SCU, Australia, and will be provided to the Mid North Coast Local Health District. Newsletters or lay reports will be prepared to communicate the potential research outcomes to the participating communities, as suggested and required by the local context. Study findings may be presented at national and international conferences. This research will generate high-quality publications for peer-reviewed journals. The authors may post research findings on social media once published. They may share extracts of the published results through print media and attend interviews through electronic media.
