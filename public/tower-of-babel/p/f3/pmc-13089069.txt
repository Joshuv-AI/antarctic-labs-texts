Meniscal engraftment of iPSC-derived cartilage particles as hyaline and fibrous cartilage in a rabbit injury model
Authors: Kenjiro Okimura, Takehiko Matsushita, Yuta Nakanishi, Tetsuya Yamamoto, Kyohei Nishida, Kanto Nagai, Noriyuki Kanzaki, Yuichi Hoshino, Keiichiro Uehara, Takashi Aoi, Ryosuke Kuroda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionMeniscal tears are associated with the development of knee osteoarthritis. Introduction

Meniscal tears are common orthopedic injuries of the knee joint and strongly associated with the development of knee osteoarthritis (OA) [[1], [2], [3]]. The complete or partial removal of an injured meniscus (meniscectomy) is considered an effective therapeutic approach. However, meniscectomy markedly promotes the progression of knee OA [4,5]. Therefore, preserving the meniscus is critical, particularly in younger patients with longer life expectancies [6,7]. The meniscus has unique anatomical and histological characteristics; the outer peripheral regions comprise fibrous cartilage with relatively abundant vascularity, whereas the inner regions comprise avascular hyaline cartilage-like tissue [8,9]. Therefore, the self-healing potential of the inner meniscus is low, often necessitating resection after injury [[10], [11], [12]]. As there is no widely established approach for promoting meniscal repair and regeneration, the treatment of meniscal injuries remains challenging.

Human induced pluripotent stem cells (hiPSCs) are reprogrammed somatic cells with the same functions as embryonic stem cells [13]. Thus, they are a promising cell source for regenerative therapies, owing to their unlimited proliferative and differentiation potential [13,14]. HiPSC-derived cartilage tissue particles (hiPSC-Cart) can form reparative tissues when implanted into animal models of articular cartilage damage, including mice, rats, and pigs [15]. Given the compositional similarities between the inner meniscus and articular cartilage, hiPSC-Carts may also be applicable for the repair of meniscal injuries. However, no studies have investigated meniscal repair using hiPSCs to date. Therefore, we aimed to investigate the therapeutic potential of hiPSC-Cart transplantation for meniscal repair using a rabbit meniscal defect model. To our knowledge, this study is the first in vivo study that demonstrated human iPSC-derived cartilage successfully engrafted with the surrounding meniscus in an animal model. This engraftment has potential applications in cartilaginous tissue repair or regeneration.

Methods

Maintenance of hiPSCs

A dermal fibroblast-derived hiPSC line 409B2 [16] was transferred to feeder- and serum-free culture conditions [17] and maintained as previously described [18].

Chondrogenic differentiation of hiPSCs

Matrigel-coated dishes were used to differentiate hiPSCs into hyaline cartilage. Matrigel-coated dishes were prepared through incubation with Matrigel (1:10 (0 dilution) in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) F12 overnight at 4 °C. The culture substrate was changed from laminin 511-E8 to Matrigel when Matrigel-coated dishes were used (day −7). Chondrogenic differentiation was induced as previously described [15]. After 7 days (day 0), the medium was changed from iPSC medium to chondrogenic medium (DMEM containing 1% insulin-transferrin-selenium, 1% fetal bovine serum, 1 × 10−4 M nonessential amino acids, 1 mM sodium pyruvate, 50 units of penicillin and 50 mg/mL streptomycin, 50 μg/mL ascorbic acid, 10 ng/mL BMP2, 10 ng/mL transforming growth factor β1, and 10 ng/mL GDF5) to differentiate hiPSCs into chondrocytes. After another 14 days (day 14), the cells were physically detached from the bottom of the dish to form cartilage particles, which were transferred to a suspension culture in 3.5-cm petri dishes. The particles were harvested for histological and mRNA expression analyses after another 28 days of culture (day 42).

RNA isolation and real-time quantitative reverse-transcription polymerase chain reaction

During the adherent cell culture period up to day 14, total RNA was extracted from whole-cell lysates, including on-column DNase I digestion, using the RNeasy Mini Kit according to the manufacturer's instructions. RNA samples were collected on days 0, 3, 7, 10, and 14. The cells were transferred to a suspension culture in Petri dishes after day 14, and the suspended cartilage particles were collected and homogenized using a multibead shocker. Total RNA was isolated using the RNeasy Mini Kit with the RNase-Free DNase Set in accordance with the manufacturer's protocol. RNA samples were collected on days 21 and 28 for further analysis.

Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized with a high-capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit using 200 ng of total RNA as a template. Real-time polymerase chain reaction (PCR) was conducted using a StepOne System with SYBR Green reagent. The primers used were NANOG, T, SOX9, and COL2A1. The amplified products were used to generate standard curves for quantitative real-time PCR. The mRNA expression levels were normalized to those of beta-ACTIN as a reference gene. The RNA was generated thrice at different times following the same protocol, and the results were averaged.

Animal experiments

This study was approved by the Animal Experiment Committee of the Graduate School of Medicine at Kobe University. Twelve mature Japanese white rabbits (body weight: 3.0–3.3 kg) were used. Immunosuppression was performed as described in 2.5 for hiPSC-Cart transplantation into meniscal tears in rabbits.

Immunosuppression

Immunosuppression was performed as described previously [19]. Tacrolimus was intramuscularly administered at 1.6 mg/kg daily from two days before the transplant until 10 days after the transplant. It was then administered every other day until four weeks after surgery. Intramuscular injections were administered alternately to the right and left hind limbs.

Operative procedure

The surgery was performed under general anesthesia with intravenous injection of sodium pentobarbital solution (30 mg/kg). Both knees were arthrotomized using a medial parapatellar approach. After lateral dislocation of the patella and full flexion of the knee, a reproducible full-thickness cylindrical defect (diameter = 1.5 mm) was created in the avascular inner two-thirds of the anterior portion of the medial meniscus using a biopsy punch (Kai Medical, Tokyo, Japan).

This study comprised three groups: the hiPSC-cartilage transplant (hiPSC-Cart; nine rabbit knees), rabbit autogenous cartilage transplant (Rab-Cart; three rabbit knees), and control groups. In the hiPSC-Cart group, hiPSC-Cart (day 42) was transplanted into the right knee; the left knee remained untreated and served as a control. In the Rab-Cart group, articular cartilage was harvested from the rabbit femoral trochlear groove using a biopsy punch and transplanted into the meniscal defect. The knee joint was closed in layers using 4-0 nylon sutures after confirming that the cartilage particles were securely embedded within the defect area. After surgery, the rabbits were returned to their cages and allowed to bear their full weight. At 4 weeks post-surgery, the rabbits were euthanized by intravenous injection of a lethal dose of pentobarbital, and the menisci were harvested for evaluation.

Histology

Particles from the suspension culture (day 42) were collected, fixed overnight in 4% paraformaldehyde at 4 °C, and embedded in paraffin. Rabbit knees were harvested four weeks after transplantation, and the tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde at 4 °C overnight. Each specimen was subsequently cut into 6-μm-thick slices. For hematoxylin and eosin staining, these sections were stained with hematoxylin for 4 min, followed by eosin staining for 1.5 min. For safranin O and fast green double staining, sections were first stained with hematoxylin for 4 min, followed by fast green staining for 5 min and safranin O staining for 1 min. For toluidine blue staining, the sections were stained with 1% toluidine blue at room temperature for 1 min.

Immunohistochemistry

The deparaffinized sections were digested with proteinase for 10 min and treated with 3% hydrogen peroxide to block endogenous peroxidase activity. After antigen retrieval, the sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against mouse type I collagen (1:100) and type II collagen (1:100). For negative control, PBS without primary antibody was applied. Sections were then incubated at room temperature (25–28 °C) for 30 min with peroxidase-labeled anti-mouse immunoglobulin (Histofine Simple Stain MAX Po; Nichirei, Japan). The signals were visualized as brown reaction products using 3,3-diaminobenzidine as the peroxidase substrate, followed by counterstaining with methyl green or hematoxylin. Non-immune mouse IgG (1:50) was used as a negative control instead of primary antibodies. As positive control for type II collagen, femoral head obtained from a 28-week-old Japanese white rabbit was used.

In addition to type I and II collagen, the sections were examined using an anti-human mitochondrial antibody (1:100) to confirm the presence of hiPSC-derived cells for in vivo analysis. The following antibodies were used: Type I collagen (Abcam, Cambridge, UK), type II collagen (Abcam), and anti-human mitochondrial antibody (Abcam).

In situ hybridization

In situ hybridization was performed using an ISH reagent kit (Genostaff, Tokyo, Japan) according to the manufacturer's instructions to confirm the presence of human cells. This kit uses oligoprobes to detect human ribosomal RNA. Tissue sections were deparaffinized and rehydrated using an ethanol series and phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). The sections were placed in 0.2 N HCl for 10 min at 37 °C and washed with PBS, treated with ProteinaseK and washed with PBS, and subsequently placed in a Coplin jar containing 1xG-Wash (Genostaff). Hybridization was performed with probes at concentrations of 250 ng mL−1 in G-Hybo-L (Genostaff) for 18 h at 60 °C. After hybridization, the sections were washed with 1xG-Wash for 10 min at 60 °C and with 50% formamide in 1xG-Wash for 10 min at 60 °C. Next, the sections were washed twice with 1xG-Wash for 10 min at 60 °C, twice with 0.1xG-Wash for 10 min at 60 °C, and twice with TBST (0.1% Tween 20 in tris-buffered saline (TBS)) at room temperature. After treatment with 1xG-Block (Genostaff) for 15 min at room temperature, the sections were incubated with an anti-DIG AP conjugate (Roche Diagnostics, Basel, Switzerland) diluted to 1:2000 with x50G-Block (Genostaff) in TBST for 1 h at room temperature. The sections were washed twice with TBST and incubated in 100 mM NaCl, 50 mM MgCl2, 0.1% Tween 20, and 100 mM Tris-HCl at pH 9.5. Coloring reactions were performed overnight with an NBT/BCIP solution (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), and the samples were washed with PBS. The sections were counterstained with Kernechtrot stain solution (Muto Pure Chemicals, Tokyo, Japan) and mounted using G-Mount (Genostaff).

Statistical analysis

To examine statistical difference in the time-course gene expression changes, Friedman test with post-hoc Dunn test comparison was used. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA) setting statistical significance as P < 0.05.

Results

Chondrogenic differentiation of hiPSCs

To create hiPSC-Cart, chondrogenic differentiation of hiPSCs was induced using a previously reported protocol that consists of 14-day culture on the Matrigel-coated dishes, followed by a suspension culture for 28 days [17] (Fig. 1a). First, changes in gene expression from the pluripotent toward the mesoderm state were examined by real-time PCR during the 14-day culture (Fig. 1b). The expression of the pluripotency marker NANOG peaked on day 0, decreased to less than one-hundredth of its initial level on day 3, and further decreased between days 7 and 14, indicating a transition from the undifferentiated state (day 0 vs day 14, P < 0.05). The expression of brachyury, an early mesoderm marker, increased by approximately 100-fold on day 7 compared to that on day 0 and decreased after day 10, indicating progression to the next differentiation stage. The expression of MESP1, another mesoderm marker, showed a transient 15-fold increase on day 7 compared to that on day 0 and decreased on day 14 (day 7 vs day 14, P < 0.05). Next, chondrogenic differentiation during the 14-day Matrigel-coated culture and the subsequent 14-day suspension culture was examined using two chondrogenic markers, SOX9 and COL2A1. The expression of SOX9, a master gene for chondrogenic differentiation, progressively increased by approximately 30-fold on day 28 compared to that on day 0 (day 0 vs day 28, P < 0.05). The expression of COL2A1, a chondrocyte-specific marker, increased by approximately 10-fold on day 14. This subsequently increased by approximately 25-fold on day 28 following the transition to the suspension culture (day 0 vs day 28, P < 0.05). These results indicate the differentiation of hPSCs into chondrocytes.

Maturation of hyaline-cartilaginous particles in the suspension culture

The iPSCs formed cartilaginous particles after 28-day suspension culture. Histological analysis was conducted to assess the differentiation status of the particles (Fig. 2). The particles were composed of cells surrounded by a safranin O-positive extracellular matrix (ECM), indicating hyaline cartilage specification. Moreover, immunohistochemical analysis revealed that the ECM was strongly positive for type II collagen, a cartilage marker, and the staining pattern was confirmed by positive and negative control. Additionally, the particles were surrounded by a membranous structure expressing type I collagen. These findings suggest that the particles successfully differentiated into hyaline cartilage.

Macroscopic evaluation of the meniscal samples after transplantation of hiPSC-Cart in the rabbit meniscal defect model

hiPSC-Cart was transplanted into the meniscal defect to evaluate the therapeutic potential of its transplantation for meniscal repair in rabbits. The meniscal defect was created in the avascular inner two-thirds of the anterior portion of the medial meniscus, wherein hiPSC-Car was subsequently transplanted (Fig. 3A-a, b, c). The rabbits received immunosuppressants to avoid potential immune rejection. No complications associated with immunosuppression, such as weight loss or infection, were observed. Macroscopic observations at four weeks post-transplantation revealed that the meniscus defect site was only covered by thin membranous tissue in the control group. In contrast, the defect was filled with a dense tissue and appeared to be repaired in both the Rab-Cart and hiPSC-Cart groups. However, the transplanted area remained distinguishable from the native meniscus (Fig. 3B).

Histological analysis of meniscal defects after transplantation of hiPSC-Cart in the rabbit meniscal defect model

Histological analyses were performed to clarify the characteristics of the tissue within the meniscal defect. In the control group, the defect was partly filled with fibrous tissue that was negative for safranin O and toluidine blue. COL2 was detected in the inner region of the meniscus but not in the defect area. COL1 was weakly detected throughout the meniscus, including the fibrous tissue within the defect (Fig. 4). In the Rab-Cart group, the defect was repaired with the tissue containing cartilage components that were positive for safranin O, toluidine blue, and COL2 (Fig. 4). However, the cartilage component area was relatively small in the repaired tissue and the tissue surrounding the cartilage component was strongly positive for COL1, suggesting that a part of the implanted cartilage degenerated or disappeared after implantation. In the hiPSC-Cart Group, two distinct patterns were observed at the transplantation site: Hyalin-rich and fibrous-rich repairs. In the hyalin-rich repair, the defect was repaired with tissues containing abundant hyaline-cartilaginous components positive for safranin O, toluidine blue, and COL2 (Fig. 4, left panels). In contrast, the defect was filled with tissue that was negative for safranin O and positive for toluidine blue in the fibrous-rich repair, indicating fibrous cartilage formation (Fig. 4, right panels). Of the nine menisci, six and three repairs were classified as the types hyalin-rich and fibrous-rich, respectively.

Confirmation of the engraftment of hiPSC-derived tissue at the transplantation sites

Immunostaining was performed using an anti-human mitochondrial antibody to identify hiPSC-derived tissue (Fig. 5a). In the Rab-Cart group, no positive cells were detected at higher magnification, despite the nonspecific staining in the outer region of the meniscus. In the hiPSC-Cart group, nonspecific staining was similarly observed in the outer region, but no positive cells were identified. In contrast, positive cells were observed within the engrafted site in both hyalin-rich and fibrous-rich repairs, confirming the presence of human iPSC-derived cells.

In situ hybridization for human ribosomal RNA was performed to further validate the presence of human-derived cells. No positive cells were observed in any field of the views in the Rab-Cart group (Fig. 5b, left panel). In contrast, positive cells were detected in the hiPSC-Cart groups. These were scattered around the transplant site in the hyalin-rich repair (Fig. 5b, middle panel). Similarly, positive cells were observed within the transplant site in the fibrous-rich repair (Fig. 5b, right panel). At higher magnification, a few positive cells were observed not only around the cartilage tissue but also within the repaired tissue. Although the expression patterns of human mitochondria and human rRNA were not same, the difference is likely due to difference in location: mitochondria vs cytoplasm. Another reason is technical limitation that IHC for mitochondria was associated with some background staining. However, these findings indicate that cells from hiPSC-Cart contributed to both hyalin-rich and fibrous-rich meniscal repairs.

Discussion

Previous studies have reported the successful generation of hyaline cartilage from iPSCs [15,20]. Transplantation of iPSC-derived cartilage into articular cartilage defects mediated cartilage repair through direct formation of cartilage in animal studies [15]. The transplantation of hiPSC-derived particles into articular cartilage defects in immune-deficient rats and immune-suppressed minipigs revealed integration of the transplanted particles into the native cartilage [20]. Furthermore, the transplantation of hiPSC-Cart into the nucleus pulposus of nude rats prevented degeneration of the intervertebral disc and maintained its mechanical properties [21]. To date, no study has reported the transplantation of iPSC-derived cartilage into a meniscus consisting of hyaline cartilage and fibrocartilage. The present study is the first to successfully engraft hiPSC-Cart into the surrounding meniscus in a rabbit meniscal defect model. Together with findings of previous studies, these observations suggest the possible application of hiPSC-Cart for cartilaginous tissue repair or regeneration.

Two distinct types of tissues were formed in the recipient meniscal defect sites following the transplantation of iPSC-derived hyaline cartilage-like particles in the present study. The meniscus defect was filled with hyaline and fibrous cartilage in the hyalin-rich and fibrous-rich repairs, respectively. In the Hyaline-Rich group, a Fibrous-Rich region was also visible in the inner margin. The possible reason why both phenotypes coexisted in the meniscus could be the influence of the native meniscus structure, since the meniscus is largely composed of fibrous meniscus. Another reason could be phenotypic change of the engrafted iPSCs toward fibrous cartilage since chondrocytes easily differentiate into fibroblasts and lose the hyaline chondrocyte phenotype. To the best of our knowledge, no study has investigated the in vitro induction of differentiation into fibrous cartilage from hiPSCs or the in vivo transplantation of hyaline cartilage, followed by its transformation into fibrocartilage. However, whether the presence of the two repair types is determined by host factors or by intrinsic differences within the transplanted tissue itself remains unclear. Thus, future studies are required to clarify these points and establish the technology to selectively induce the two types of repair.

Previous studies on meniscal repair examined the potential application of mesenchymal cells via scaffold-based transplantation or the administration of cell suspensions, rather than the transplantation of mature meniscal tissue. The cell sources considered include chondrocytes [22], bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) [23], adipose tissue-derived MSCs [24], and synovial-derived MSCs [[23], [24], [25]]. Kon et al. [22] reported that scaffolds seeded with chondrocytes tended to promote greater cartilage formation than those without cells in a sheep total meniscectomy model. However, the difference was not statistically significant, and the effect was limited. Yamasaki et al. [23] transplanted decellularized meniscal allografts with bone marrow-derived MSCs into a rat meniscus resection model. The cell-seeded grafts promoted ECM production, and cells remained up to 8 weeks after the implantation. These findings suggest that cells in the constructs contributed to the repair. Although the study showed a new approach for meniscal regeneration, allografts are not available in many countries. In addition, the approach did not address the fundamental problems associated with allografts. Nakagawa et al. [24] examined the effects of synovial MSCs on meniscus healing in minipigs. They administered synovial MSC suspension on the tear site to facilitate meniscal repair. Meniscal healing in the synovial MSC-treated group was significantly better than that in the control group. In another study [25], injection of atelocollagen sponge with non-cultured adipose-derived cells promoted regeneration of the meniscus in a rat partial meniscectomy model; the injected cells remained for up to 12 weeks after injection. Although these studies have demonstrated a promotion of meniscal repair, cell suspensions are generally unsuitable for large meniscal defects. In addition, the maturation time required to obtain sufficient biomechanical strength is a concern in potential clinical applications. Contrary to cell transplantation, transplantation of mature chondrogenic tissue has the potential for tissue regeneration in cases of extensive damage or defect. Previous studies have demonstrated that iPSC-Cart could adhere to and grow with the native tissue, suggesting a possible application for extensive meniscal defects. Therefore, we aimed to repair the meniscus by replacing mature chondrogenic tissue. Although we successfully repaired the meniscus, the main mechanism underlying this effect remains unclear. Nonetheless, this technique has potential applications in the repair and regeneration of meniscal injuries histologically characterized by hyaline cartilage-like tissue and limited blood supply.

This study had several limitations. The experiment was conducted using an atypical injury model in rabbits, and the observation period was only four weeks. Therefore, more long-term follow-up studies are required to evaluate the sustained effects of hiPSC-Cart transplantation. Second, the mechanical strength of the repaired tissue and the chondroprotective effect were not assessed in this study. These data are essential for the clinical application of this approach. Third, although we aimed to create a defect in the white-white zone of the meniscus, the narrow width of the rabbit meniscus (approximately 3 mm) resulted in the creation of defects in the red-white zone. Thus, future studies should incorporate larger animals such as pigs, clinically relevant injury models, and long-term follow-up observations to better evaluate the effects of hiPSC-Cart on meniscal repair.

Conclusion

In the present study, transplanted hiPSC-derived hyaline cartilaginous particles engrafted and integrated with the recipient meniscus, forming either hyaline- or fibrous cartilage-like tissues in a rabbit injury model. To our knowledge, this is the first report showing that hiPSC-derived cartilage contributes to meniscal tissue repair. These findings demonstrate the potential of iPSC-derived cartilage particles as a feasible therapeutic option for meniscal repair and regeneration.
