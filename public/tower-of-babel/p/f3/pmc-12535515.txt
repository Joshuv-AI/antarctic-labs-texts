Diagnosis and Triage Performance of Contemporary Large Language Models on Short Clinical Vignettes
Authors: Lei Xu, Wenzhe Zhao, Xin Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractGeneral-purpose large language models (LLMs) are increasingly proposed for diagnostic and triage decision support, yet their reliability relative to humans remains unclear. We evaluated eight contemporary LLMs (ChatGPT-4, ChatGPT-o1, DeepSeek-V3, DeepSeek-R1, Gemini-2.0, Copilot, Grok-2, Llama-3.1) on 48 single-turn clinical vignettes spanning four triage levels (Emergent, 1-day, 1-week, Self-care). Models were tested without prompts and with structured prompts comprising exemplar cases. Primary outcomes were diagnostic and triage accuracy. Secondary measures included confusion matrices, over-triage, safety of advice, and the Capability Comparison Score (CCS). Structured prompting improved performance across models: mean diagnostic accuracy increased from 89.84% to 91.67%, and mean triage accuracy increased from 76.82% to 86.20%. The best diagnostic accuracy was 93.75% (ChatGPT-o1 and DeepSeek-R1; Grok-2 matched this when prompted). Prompting shifted models toward safety: safety of advice rose from 89.06% to 94.53%, accompanied by higher over-triage (from 53.15% to 65.62%). CCS values were numerically lower than accuracy but preserved rankings and conclusions (diagnosis CCS: from 49.54 to 50.46; triage CCS: from 47.66 to 52.34). Error analyses showed predominant over-triage, with rarer but clinically important under-triage. On concise, text-only vignettes, the diagnostic accuracy of advanced LLMs was high, in some cases nearing benchmarks set by physicians in prior studies, whereas triage remained a more significant challenge. Structured prompting provided a practical, training-free lever to enhance robustness. Future work should evaluate uncertainty-aware prompting and real-world, multi-turn/multi-modality cases to strengthen clinical reliability.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10916-025-02284-y.Keywords: Large language models, Short clinical vignettes, Diagnosis and triage

Introduction

The rapid evolution of large language models (LLMs), such as ChatGPT-4, DeepSeek, and Gemini, has significantly transformed natural language processing (NLP) by demonstrating unprecedented capabilities in text generation, complex reasoning, and domain-specific knowledge integration. These models, trained on massive datasets and leveraging advanced neural network architectures such as Transformers, excel in contextual understanding, conversation management, and generating remarkably human-like responses [1–4]. Their impressive scalability, ranging from millions to trillions of parameters, allows them to effectively process highly specialized medical texts, including clinical notes, electronic health records (EHRs), and extensive medical research literature, thereby making them particularly promising for healthcare-related applications [5, 6].

Moreover, emerging models such as DeepSeek and Gemini demonstrate rapid advancement, reflecting significant improvements in clinical reasoning and accuracy, owing to novel training methods, specialized fine-tuning, and strategic prompt engineering. These advances further diversify the competitive landscape of medical artificial intelligence (AI).

In medical contexts, these LLMs exhibit substantial potential across diverse tasks, such as disease diagnosis, clinical triage, therapeutic decision support, and patient management [7]. However, the extent to which general-purpose LLMs can reliably generate accurate and clinically valid diagnostic information remains an open research question. Prior studies have produced mixed findings. Levine et al. demonstrated that the diagnostic triage performance of ChatGPT-3, though superior to laypersons conducting online searches, falls significantly short of expert physicians [8]. Conversely, recent evaluations of ChatGPT-4 indicated substantial improvements, suggesting enhanced diagnostic capabilities approaching human expert performance in specific clinical tasks [9, 10]. Moreover, emerging models such as DeepSeek and Gemini demonstrate rapid advancement, reflecting significant improvements in clinical reasoning and accuracy, owing to novel training methods, specialized fine-tuning, and strategic prompt engineering. These advances further diversify the competitive landscape of medical AI [11, 12].

Despite rapid progress, numerous critical challenges persist. The lack of standardized evaluation frameworks and benchmark datasets for medical tasks severely complicates direct comparison of LLM performance, hindering objective assessments of their relative strengths and weaknesses [13]. Furthermore, ethical considerations, including data privacy, algorithmic transparency, and biases inherent in medical training data, pose additional hurdles, raising legitimate concerns regarding model reliability, fairness, and interpretability in clinical practice [14, 15]. Misdiagnoses driven by LLM-generated outputs could result in serious patient harm if blindly trusted or improperly interpreted by healthcare providers or patients [16]. Therefore, systematic investigations incorporating ethical analyses, bias assessments, and interpretability metrics are crucial before widespread clinical adoption.

This study aims to address these knowledge gaps by systematically comparing the predictive accuracy of several prominent commercial LLMs. Performance will be evaluated against baseline groups composed of lay individuals and primary care physicians. Specifically, the study focuses on clinical diagnosis and triage tasks, which are common yet complex responsibilities requiring careful interpretation of patient-reported symptoms. Through rigorous assessment, we aim to quantify both the strengths and limitations of current LLMs. Ultimately, this research provides empirical evidence to help inform the responsible development and future evaluation of advanced language models for potential integration into clinical workflows and medical decision-making.

Methods

Dataset

The synthetic clinical case and corresponding diagnose and triage labels were obtained via previous studies [8, 17]. We utilized the vignette dataset developed by Levine et al. to evaluate model performance. This dataset included validated physician and layperson benchmarks, which allowed for the direct positioning of LLM results relative to human baselines. It also provided balanced four-level triage labels and concise single-turn narratives, which corresponded to the design of our diagnostic and triage tasks under both non-prompted and prompted conditions. In addition, the vignettes were written in simple, natural language that closely reflected how patients typically describe their symptoms, making them particularly suitable for assessing general-purpose LLMs trained primarily on everyday text.

The dataset included 48 synthetic clinical case, each comprising a primary symptom accompanied by relevant supporting details. The vignettes and reference standards were originally developed and validated by Levine et al., with procedures reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board of Harvard Medical School for the prior study [8, 17]. This study involved secondary analysis of a previously published vignette dataset. All vignettes were concise (fewer than 50 words), written at or below a sixth-grade reading comprehension level, and designed to represent four distinct triage urgency categories: emergent, within 1 day, within 1 week, and self-care, with 12 cases allocated to each category. Clinical scenarios selected included both common conditions (e.g., viral illness) and severe conditions (e.g., heart attack), excluding exceedingly rare or obscure presentations.

Diagnosis and Triage by LLMs

In this study, diagnostic assessments were performed using LLMs under two distinct scenarios: without prompts (non-prompted) and with prompts (prompted). For the diagnosis without prompts scenarios, the LLMs were first explicitly instructed to assume the role of a senior physician, intended to simulate a clinical diagnostic environment [18]. Subsequently, detailed clinical vignettes along with relevant supplemental patient information were provided to the LLMs, which were then asked to determine the most likely diagnosis and assign an appropriate clinical triage category. An illustrative example for Case 1 was presented below, demonstrating the instruction format and content clarity provided to the LLM [8, 17]:

“You are a senior doctor. Given clinical vignette and additional details, give me the diagnosis. Only give me the most probable diagnosis results. Further, classify into one of the 4 triage categories: Emergent, 1-day, 1-week, and Self-care.

The vignette detailed a 48-year-old female who developed confusion, sleepiness, and disorientation lasting for several hours after taking an increased dose of her regular medication for migraines, Tylenol.”

For the diagnosis with prompts scenarios, the assessment began by clearly identifying the target clinical case intended for diagnosis. We refer to this setup as “answer-revealing teaching” to distinguish it from standard few-shot prompting. Whereas standard few-shot prompting typically provides a small number of fixed examples to guide the model’s output format and reasoning style, our approach dynamically provides the model with the vast majority of the dataset (47 of 48 cases) along with their gold-standard answers. This method was intended not just to demonstrate the task, but to equip the LLM with a rich, in-context knowledge base directly relevant to the cases under evaluation. Following this preparatory step, the clinical vignette and additional details for the target case were presented to the LLMs for independent diagnostic evaluation. The goal of this structured prompting approach was to systematically assess whether exposure to relevant case examples could enhance diagnostic accuracy and clinical judgment of the models, thus simulating the process of learning and decision-making refinement analogous to real-world clinical practice. The diagnosis examples of the prompted and non-prompted were provided in Appendix.

All LLMs were tested between March 1 st and March 15th, 2025.

Evaluation

We assessed diagnostic and triage performance using multiple metrics. In addition to accuracy, we quantified triage behavior with two safety-oriented measures. Over-triage was defined as the proportion of vignettes for which the system recommended a more urgent triage level than the gold standard, among all incorrect triage recommendations. Safety of advice was defined as the proportion of vignettes for which the system’s recommendation was at least as urgent as the gold standard. Both metrics were computed based on the urgency order Emergent > 1-day > 1-week > Self-care. We calculated the over-triage and safety-of-advice metrics using the symptomcheckR package in R software [19].

Further, we included the capability comparison score (CCS), a metric created by Kopka et al. to facilitate a more equitable comparison by accounting for case difficulty [20]. The methodology begins by calculating the item difficulty (ID) for each clinical vignette, defined as the proportion of all systems that correctly solved it. A high ID therefore signifies an easier case, whereas a low ID indicates a more challenging one. The CCS algorithm leverages this ID to differentially weight outcomes: it confers a greater reward for correctly diagnosing difficult vignettes (weighted as 1-ID) and applies a larger penalty for diagnostic errors on simple ones (weighted as ID). This process yields a final, normalized score on a scale of 0 to 100, providing a more insightful measure of a model’s true capabilities than raw accuracy alone. During CCS computation, the results of Prompted and Non-Prompted Diagnosis were pooled, after which ID was estimated and performance evaluated.

Diagnosis and Triage by LLMs with Prompts

Structured prompting notably enhanced both diagnostic and triage performance across all evaluated LLMs, as summarized in Table 1. For diagnosis, the overall mean accuracy increased from 89.84% (non-prompted) to 91.67% (prompted). The best-performing models, ChatGPT-o1, DeepSeek-R1, and Grok-2, all achieved 93.75%, confirming that prompting consistently boosted diagnostic accuracy compared with the non-prompted condition. Several models benefited markedly from prompting: ChatGPT-4 improved its diagnostic accuracy from 89.58% to 91.67%, while Grok-2 rose from 89.58% to match the top-performing systems at 93.75%. In contrast, Llama-3.1 consistently underperformed, showing only modest improvement from 83.33% to 87.50% despite prompting. For triage, prompting produced even greater improvements. The mean triage accuracy increased from 76.82% (non-prompted) to 86.20% (prompted), representing nearly a 10-percentage-point gain on average. ChatGPT-o1 achieved the highest prompted triage accuracy at 93.75%, while DeepSeek-V3 showed a striking enhancement from 68.75% to 81.25%. ChatGPT-4 also benefited, improving from 72.92% to 79.17%, whereas Llama-3.1, though still the weakest performer, rose from 68.75% to 85.42% with prompting, narrowing the gap relative to stronger models. Overall, most systems exceeded 85% triage accuracy under the prompted condition, substantially reducing the performance disparity between diagnostic and triage tasks. The detailed performance of triage accuracy by LLMs with prompts was shown in Table 2.

We further examined error patterns in triage classification. The prompted ChatGPT-o1 misclassified two cases by overestimating urgency, Malaria and Pneumonia, which were labeled as “emergent” despite their correct triage being less urgent. Conversely, in the acne case, the same model underestimated urgency, predicting “Self-care” instead of the correct “1-week” triage level. Interestingly, among all prompted LLMs, only ChatGPT-4 and Copilot correctly triaged the acne case. CCS analysis again produced lower absolute values but more nuanced differentiation. The mean CCS for prompted diagnosis was 50.46, marginally higher than in the non-prompted condition. ChatGPT-o1, Grok-2, and DeepSeek-R1 all scored 51.50, the highest among the evaluated models, whereas Copilot and Llama-3.1 remained below 50. These findings indicated that while prompting improved average accuracy, it also helped stabilize performance across difficult cases. Prompting also yielded substantial gains in CCS for triage. The mean prompted CCS was 52.34, nearly five points higher than in the non-prompted setting. ChatGPT-o1 again achieved the top score (56.12), followed by DeepSeek-R1, Gemini-2.0, Grok-2, and Copilot (52.99). This demonstrated that structured prompting not only improved raw triage accuracy but also enhanced robustness on more challenging vignettes, as captured by the CCS framework.

Prompting shifted recommendations toward a more precautionary stance. Safety of advice approached a ceiling across models (from 91.67% to 97.92%), accompanied by a systematic rise in over-triage (from 50.00% to 90.00%). ChatGPT-4 showed the largest shift (over triage: 90.00%, safety of advice: 97.92%), while DeepSeek-R1 (over triage: 83.33%, safety of advice: 97.92%) and DeepSeek-V3 (over triage: 77.78%, safety of advice: 95.83%) followed a similar but slightly milder results. Discussion

The integration of LLMs into clinical practice received considerable attention. A primary area of interest was their ability to support diagnosis and triage decisions. In this study, we assessed the diagnostic and triage performance of several LLMs. Evaluations were conducted both with and without structured prompting.

Beyond raw accuracy, CCS provided an additional perspective by adjusting for vignette difficulty. Across both diagnosis and triage, CCS values were consistently lower than accuracies, reflecting the stricter weighting scheme that penalized errors on easier cases and rewarded correct predictions on more difficult ones. As a result, average CCS values clustered around 50, even when mean accuracies exceeded 90% for diagnosis and 85% for triage. It was noteworthy that the CCS metric was disproportionately influenced by items at the extremes of difficulty. A small subset of vignettes was answered incorrectly by all models, which gave these items very high weights and substantially reduced overall scores across the board. Conversely, several vignettes were solved correctly by all models, yielding near-zero weights and thus contributing little to model differentiation or score elevation. These patterns help explain why CCS values remained modest despite high accuracies, and they illustrate both the strengths and the limitations of CCS as a difficulty-adjusted measure of capability. Although the absolute values of CCS were much lower than those of accuracy, the relative rankings and overall conclusions regarding model performance were fully consistent across both metrics.

Prompting systematically shifted models toward a high-sensitivity/low-specificity, safety-first operating point. Safety of advice nearly reached a ceiling (94.53%) while over-triage rose substantially (from 53.15% to 65.62%). Notably, ChatGPT-o1 maintained top-tier safety (97.92%) with only moderate over-triage (66.67%), indicating a more favorable safety-resource trade-off. Conversely, without prompts the systems were more restrained. Over-triage fell to 53.15% but safety also declined to 89.06%. Clinically, avoiding under-triage typically takes priority over limiting over-triage. Thus, prompting can be viewed as a “safety-first” mode, though sustained high over-triage may drive unnecessary visits and resource strain. For personal users, model triage recommendations should be treated as symptom-specific reference information rather than a definitive diagnosis. Users should interpret the advice in light of their exact symptoms and circumstances.

When contextualizing our results with those reported by Levine et al., clear parallels emerged [8]. In their evaluation of the same vignette set, lay individuals relying on internet searches achieved a diagnostic accuracy of 54% (Top-3), whereas primary care physicians reached 96%. For triage, laypersons correctly classified 74% of cases, compared with 91% among physicians. In our study, the best-performing LLMs, such as ChatGPT-o1 and DeepSeek-R1, achieved diagnostic accuracy up to 93.75%. For context, this figure approaches the 96% accuracy benchmark reported for primary care physicians on the same vignette set, while substantially surpassing the performance of laypersons. However, triage accuracy for these models remained lower than that of physicians, with systematic tendencies toward over-triage. This contrast highlights both the rapid progress of general-purpose LLMs in text-based diagnostic reasoning and the persisting challenges in achieving safe and reliable triage on such tasks.

Prior studies reported heterogeneous findings regarding the diagnostic and triage performance of LLMs. Fraser et al. showed that LLMs performed below physicians and, importantly, exhibited safety-relevant triage errors [21]. A likely explanation was that their evaluation relied on older versions of GPT and provided relatively limited prompting, which may have constrained performance. By contrast, Ito et al. reported that GPT-4 achieved diagnostic and triage accuracy comparable to board-certified physicians, and concluded that these findings could inform health systems considering conversational AI to improve diagnostic and triage efficiency [22]. One notable methodological difference was vignette length: in Ito’s study, sample vignettes contained approximately 111 words, whereas in our dataset the average vignette length was only 37 words. This suggested that providing richer clinical detail might have facilitated higher diagnostic accuracy, which could partly explain why GPT-4 in their study matched physician-level performance and exceeded results from other settings. This observation was further supported by the RepVig framework proposed by Kopka et al., which demonstrated that representative vignettes could substantially shift triage accuracy, and ultimately underscored the sensitivity of triage results to vignette design [23].

Our findings showed that, in the absence of prompts, models such as ChatGPT-o1 and DeepSeek-R1 achieved diagnostic accuracies of 93.75%, a result that can be contextualized by the performance levels of experienced physicians in prior work. However, their triage accuracies were notably lower, with ChatGPT-o1 reaching 89.58% and DeepSeek-V3 only 68.75%. This difference highlighted a significant challenge for clinical implementation, as precise triage classification was critical in emergency medicine. Previous studies also reported variability in triage outcomes using LLMs underscoring the need for further development before widespread clinical adoption [24, 25]. For instance, a study evaluating ChatGPT’s performance in disaster patient triage using the START protocol found an overall accuracy of only 63.9%, with concerns about suboptimal repeatability and reproducibility [26].

Introducing structured prompts markedly improved both diagnostic and triage accuracy for all models tested. Our method of “answer-revealing teaching”, where 47 solved cases were provided as context, proved to be a highly effective strategy for boosting performance. For example, ChatGPT-4’s diagnostic accuracy increased from 89.58% to 91.67%, and its triage accuracy improved from 72.92% to 79.17% when prompts were provided. These results align with existing literature, demonstrating that structured prompting can significantly enhance the clinical reasoning capabilities of LLMs [27, 28]. This significant improvement demonstrates the models’ powerful in-context learning capabilities. By exposing the models to gold-standard examples, we effectively enabled them to learn the diagnostic patterns and triage criteria specific to the dataset more efficiently. These results align with existing literature, demonstrating that rich, structured prompting can significantly enhance the clinical reasoning capabilities of LLMs.

For diagnostic predictions, all evaluated large language models (LLMs) consistently produced incorrect diagnoses for cases numbered 1, 18, and 42. Specifically, for Case 18, the correct diagnosis was Hemolytic Uremic Syndrome (HUS), yet the models incorrectly diagnosed it as either bacterial gastroenteritis or an E. coli infection. This misclassification likely stemmed from limited and ambiguous clinical information provided in the case description. The symptoms described, “Nothing unusual in diet though did have a hamburger at a cookout 3 days before pain started. Has fever. Diarrhea may have blood in it.“, are indeed characteristic of bacterial gastroenteritis or an E. coli infection, making the differential diagnosis challenging. Similarly, for Case 42, the correct diagnosis was a “Bee sting without anaphylaxis,” yet the models erroneously classified it as a “Localized bee sting reaction.” This confusion may also be attributed to the subtlety and ambiguity in symptom presentation. These findings underline the importance of precise, comprehensive clinical descriptions to facilitate accurate diagnostic predictions by LLMs and suggest that improving the quality and clarity of input information could enhance model performance in complex or ambiguous clinical scenarios.

​An analysis of triage errors across all models revealed distinct patterns between non-prompted and prompted LLMs. Among the 8 non-prompted LLMs, five exhibited a higher frequency of over-triage errors-assigning higher urgency levels than appropriate-compared to under-triage errors. Similarly, in the prompted LLMs, five models demonstrated more over-triage errors, while the remaining three models had equal instances of both error types. Notably, when under-triage errors occurred, most models misclassified cases as requiring “self-care”. These findings align with existing literature indicating that LLMs often tend to overestimate patient severity during triage assessments. The study of Haim et al. evaluated ChatGPT-4’s performance in emergency triage and found that the model assigned lower median Emergency Severity Index scores compared to human evaluators, suggesting a propensity to overestimate patient severity [29].

This study still had several limitations. Firstly, although all the clinical cases utilized were verified with accurate diagnoses, they were synthetic rather than actual clinical cases. Additionally, the number of cases analyzed was relatively small, and the variety of conditions included was limited. Consequently, this may restrict the comprehensive assessment of the strengths and weaknesses of different LLMs in diagnostic accuracy. Future research will address these limitations by incorporating a larger, more diverse set of real clinical cases, enabling a more robust evaluation of various models’ clinical decision-making capabilities. While the vignettes of Levine et al. provided clear advantages for comparability and task alignment, they also shared the well-documented limitations of synthetic vignette datasets, including restricted clinical context, single-turn design, and potential exposure in model training corpora. Recent work has proposed improved or more realistic alternatives. Accordingly, our findings should be interpreted as an upper-bound estimate of single-turn, text-only reasoning. Therefore, future studies will also explore multiple prompting strategies to identify the most effective approach, providing valuable guidance for both individual users and clinicians when utilizing large language models in clinical practice.

Despite some remaining limitations identified in our study, the overall performance of LLMs in clinical diagnosis and triage tasks remains highly promising. LLMs have demonstrated considerable strengths, including their exceptional capability to comprehend and generate human-like text, enabling sophisticated reasoning and detailed interpretation of complex medical cases. Their inherent scalability and flexibility allow for adaptation across diverse clinical scenarios, providing valuable decision support in personalized medicine and patient management. While current models exhibit specific challenges-such as uncertainty in triage classification and difficulties in managing ambiguous or limited clinical data-ongoing advancements in training methods, prompt engineering, and increased availability of high-quality medical data offer promising pathways to address these issues. Overall, the encouraging results achieved thus far underscore the substantial potential of LLMs as valuable tools in healthcare, warranting continued investment in research and validation to determine the feasibility and safety of future clinical integration.

Conclusion

In conclusion, GPT-o1 and DeepSeek-R1 achieved the highest diagnostic accuracy among the evaluated LLMs. On this specific vignette-based task, their performance approached benchmarks set by experienced primary care physicians in a previous study and clearly exceeded that of lay individuals. Triage performance improved with structured prompting but still fell below physician standards. GPT-o1 showed the best overall balance across diagnostic and triage tasks, especially under structured prompts. These findings highlight the potential of general-purpose AI to quickly achieve high performance on specific clinical tasks, though considerable research on safety and reliability is required before any consideration of clinical integration. Ongoing evaluation and refinement are essential to ensure reliable decision support in safety-critical settings. Future work should evaluate uncertainty-aware prompting and real-world, multi-turn/multi-modality cases to strengthen clinical reliability.
