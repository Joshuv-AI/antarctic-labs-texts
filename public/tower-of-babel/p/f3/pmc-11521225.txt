Is Virtual Reality Effective in Decreasing Pain during Clinical Procedures among Children: A Systematic Review and Meta-analysis
Authors: Saundarya Priyadarshini, Sandeep Agarwala, Harsh Priya, Vishesh Jain, Prabudh Goel, Anjan Kumar Dhua, Devendra Kumar Yadav, Sachit Anand, Nishant Patel
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:Virtual reality (VR) refers to a computer-generated simulation or environment that can be experienced and interacted with by a person through special electronic equipment, such as a headset with a screen and motion-tracking sensors. VR has been used as a distraction technique to alleviate pain and discomfort during medical procedures or as an alternative to traditional pain medication. This article explores VR’s effectiveness in reducing pain in pediatric patients undergoing painful clinical procedures.Methodology:This systematic review and meta-analysis was performed as per the preferred reporting item for systematic review and meta-analysis guidelines. A systematic literature search was conducted for all articles published till December 2022 in four different databases: PubMed, Scopus, Embase, and LILACS, which yielded 6886 studies. Studies showing the effect of VR on pediatric patients undergoing clinical procedures were included in the study. Risk of bias (ROB) 2.0 was used to assess the ROB. Outcome data were pooled using a random-effects model and reported as standardized mean difference (SMD) with a 95% confidence interval (CI). The meta-analysis was performed on RevMan5.4. Subgroup analysis was done for the different clinical procedures.Results:A total of 2477 participants from 34 studies were included in the meta-analysis. Children in whom VR was used had a significantly lower pain score as compared to the control group with an SMD of 0.87 (CI 95% −1.20, −0.53; (P < 0.00001). Subgroup analysis based on the type of intervention did not reveal any significant difference (P = 0.19). Heterogeneity for overall pain using a random effect model with I2 was significant (P < 0.00001).Conclusion:This systematic review suggests that the use of VR distraction in children undergoing painful procedures is associated with significantly lower pain scores. Hence, VR can be a promising intervention for reducing pain experienced by pediatric patients during medical procedures.

INTRODUCTION

The International Association for the Study of Pain describes pain as an unpleasant sensory and emotional experience linked to actual or potential tissue damage.[1] Consequently, pain encompasses various sensory, cognitive, behavioral, and psychological elements, making it a multifaceted experience.

The primary challenges in pediatric care revolve around pain, particularly during procedures involving needles such as vaccinations, blood draws, or medication administration.[12] These difficulties can hinder health-care delivery and lead to parental dissatisfaction.[3]

Virtual reality (VR) refers to a computer-generated simulation or environment that can be experienced and interacted with by a person using special electronic equipment, such as a headset with a screen and motion-tracking sensors.[4] VR has been used as a distraction technique to alleviate pain and discomfort during medical procedures or as an alternative to traditional pain medication. Immersing patients in a virtual environment can divert their attention away from painful stimuli, reducing pain perception. VR has emerged as a promising nonpharmacological intervention for managing pain in pediatric patients.[56]

A study by Shahrbanian et al.[7] found that VR distraction was well-received by pediatric patients and did not cause any adverse effects. Virtual reality has been successfully utilized in pediatric populations to alleviate discomfort related to procedures such as burn wound treatments and routine care.[8] Moreover, VR is a feasible and safe intervention for pediatric patients. A systematic review by Wiederhold et al.[9] reported that VR distraction was effective in reducing pain in pediatric patients undergoing medical procedures such as chemotherapy and venipuncture.[1011] By conducting a meta-analysis, the authors aim to provide a quantitative synthesis of the existing evidence regarding the efficacy of VR in reducing pain during clinical procedures in children. This study aims to perform a systematic review and meta-analysis to establish the efficacy of VR in relieving pain in children undergoing clinical procedures.

METHODS

Study design

The study was conducted by the Preferred Reporting Item for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) 2020 checklist. This SRMA was conducted and reported according to the Cochrane Handbook[12] and PRISMA statement.[13] The a priori protocol is available on the PROSPERO database as CRD42023401958. According to PICO, the research question formed was “Is Virtual Reality an effective method for decreasing the pain of pediatric patients during a clinical procedure?” To answer the research question studies were selected based on the eligibility criteria.

Search strategy

A systematic review was performed on PubMed, Scopus, Embase, and LILACS for articles in the English language between January 03, 2023 and January 15, 2023. The publication was searched both online and offline.

MeSH terms were used for VR, whereas synonyms were found for pediatric patients. Boolean operators “AND” “OR” and “NOT” were used with these terms for searching the articles. The following search term was used adding a filter for age to 18 years. A manual search was also conducted. The full text was retrieved for all articles after screening and was independently reviewed by all authors.

Eligibility criteria

We included studies that involved patients below 19 years of age and who underwent any clinical procedure utilizing VR. We considered articles published in the English language up until December 2022. The study excluded review articles, case series, case reports, and letters to the editor. Quality appraisal and risk of bias

Quality appraisal and calibration of risk of bias (ROB) were performed by two independent reviewers following the ROB 2.0 scale for randomized control trials (RCTs). The table is presented as traffic light plots and overall ROB.

Data extraction

Authors independently gathered study features and numerical data from included studies using: (i) study characteristics (design, clinical setting, country, and allocation ratio), (ii) patient characteristics (number, gender, and age), (iii) procedure type, (iv) pain score, (v) Intervention: VR type, (vi) No of groups, (vii) type of control or distraction used, and (viii) pain scale used. Data were extracted by two reviewers and entered in an Excel sheet©. In case of any disagreement, it was resolved by the third reviewer who was an expert in the field.

A score of pain in the form of continuous data was assessed using different scales on a scale of 10. Few studies noted pain score on a scale of 100 which was converted to a score of 10 for standard measurement.

The mean pain score was extracted from articles, calculated from the median, and interquartile range or asked by the author.

Data synthesis and analysis

Data were run into Rev Man 5.4 for quantitative analysis. The data were analyzed to determine and calculate the pooled estimate of the SMD of the pain assessment scores using the random effect model. The heterogeneity was assessed using the I2 statistic. To take into account the current heterogeneity and provide a range of potential effects for a future clinical environment, 95% prediction ranges were constructed for meta-analyses of included studies. The prediction interval was calculated from comprehensive meta-analysis©.[14]

Data from studies using more than one non-VR comparison group were combined and included as controls in the current analysis. Few studies noted pain scores on a scale of 100, which was converted to a score of 10 for standard measurement.

Subgroup analysis was also done based on different types of clinical procedures. Sensitivity analysis was done for studies that used a control group other than standard treatment care and studies that reported pain as the median. Publication bias was calculated using MedCalc 20.104©.

RESULTS

Study selection

The database was searched, resulting in the retrieval of 6886 articles from various databases. Out of these, 3641 articles were eliminated due to duplicates. Three thousand two hundred and forty-five articles were screened, and 3205 articles were excluded after the title and abstract screening. The remaining 40 articles were selected for full-text screening. Of these, three were excluded as one could not be retrieved and two were protocols, therefore, 37 were included in the final qualitative analysis and 34 in the quantitative analysis. Study characteristics

The age range of the participants was between 3 and 19 years. All studies included in the review were RCTs.

A total of 37 articles were utilized in the present systematic review focused on clinical procedures in children. VR was employed as a distraction technique in various scenarios, including venipuncture (n = 27), dental procedures (n = 6) such as extraction, restoration, and fluoride application) and dressing for burn (n = 4). The studies included in the analysis were conducted between the years 2002 and 2023. Studies that were excluded from the meta-analysis were Gershon et al.[43] and Chang et al.[25] as data were not available and qualitative, and Caruso et al.[47] mentioned the confidence interval (CI), but specific data were missing.

Three studies were crossover RCT[151932] only data from Phase I for the crossover study were included in the study. The included RCTs compared VR to standard treatment or usual care. Studies that compared other distractions along with standard care of treatment or control were combined for mean as a comparison group. Data for pain were collected as continuous data as mean and standard deviation (SD). Mohanasundari et al.,[18] Kasper et al.[51] Eijlers et al.,[40] Sander et al.,[37] and Walther-Larsen et al.[46] presented data as median and interquartile range; hence, mean ± SD was calculated from the given data. A study by Jeffs et al.[34] did not mention the SD, hence, it was calculated from the Rev Man calculator.

Risk of bias

The ROB 2.0 was utilized to assess the ROB on their methodological quality and varied between low, moderate, and high risk based on study quality. More than 50% of studies had a low ROB. The majority of studies mention randomization but four studies showed a high ROB. The study quality may have been affected by several specific findings. Studies showing a high ROB were carefully evaluated during the data synthesis and interpretation stages. We acknowledged the limitations introduced by studies with a high ROB, and these limitations were transparently communicated in the ROB traffic light plots. These specific findings were considered in the overall evaluation of study quality. For example, in the burn care study by Das et al.,[32] some patients were allowed to participate more than once, even though the initial inclusion criteria only allowed for patients who experienced burns for the first time. In the study on venous access by Piskorz and Czub,[21] children were included in the VR condition if they enjoyed playing the VR game, whereas data for the control group were collected afterward. Gerceker et al.[31] excluded unsuccessful phlebotomy attempts from their analyses, specifically when there was no blood flow into the tube within 5 s during the first attempt [Figure 2 and Supplemental Figure 1].

Meta-analysis

The choice of a random-effects model was based on the anticipation of heterogeneity among the included studies. Given the diverse clinical procedures, patient populations, and VR interventions, a random-effects model was deemed more appropriate than a fixed-effects model. The implications of this choice were acknowledged, highlighting that the observed effects might vary across different populations and settings.

A meta-analysis was performed by pooling data from 34 studies with a total of 2477 participants, out of which 1148 were patients who underwent VR and 1329 belonged to the control group. The weighted average effect size of VR on pediatric pain during medical procedures was found to be [SMD = −0.87; 95% CI, −1.2, −0.53; P < 0.00001, Figure 3]. The analysis favored VR in reducing pain compared to the control group, however, there was considerable heterogeneity among the study effects (I2 = 93%).

Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analysis criteria were clearly defined, including the exclusion of studies with additional distractions or reporting pain as median. Studies using more than standard care or other distraction techniques in the control group were excluded from the study. Studies using median were removed from the analysis, and crossover studies were also excluded from the study. The analysis included 21 studies with 1530 participants. The VR group comprised 715 participants in the VR group and 815 in the control group. The pooled SMD was estimated as SMD - 1.07 CI 95% (−1.53, −0.60); P < 0.0001 with I2 94% [P < 0.00001, Supplemental Figure 3].

Subgroup analysis

Subgroup analysis was done for different clinical procedures [Supplemental Figure 4].

The rationale behind subgroup analysis was to explore potential variations in the effectiveness of VR distraction across different clinical procedures. Subgroups were defined based on the nature of the medical procedures (venipuncture, dental, and burn), aiming to provide insights into the specific contexts where VR might be more or less effective.

Thousand seven hundred and one participants were included in venipuncture (762 used VR and 939 in control) and the net pooled effect of VR in pain assessment was SMD – 0.93 CI 95% (−1.34, −0.52) and statistically significant (P < 0.00001).

The dental procedure involved 657 participants, of which 328 were in the VR group and 329 were in the control group. It was observed that the reduction in pain score for dental procedures was significant (P = 0.05) SMD – 0.91 CI 95% (−1.81, −0.01), which was compared to the control group.

Including a total of 119 studies from four studies for burn, the VR group included 58 participants and 61 in the control group. The pooled estimate for burn was observed to be-0.33, CI 95% (−0.70, −0.04) and was not significant (P = 0.08).

The test for subgroup differences suggests that there is no statistically significant subgroup effect (P = 0.08). The lack of a statistically significant subgroup effect suggested that, while the treatment effect favored VR across most procedures, the magnitude of the effect varied. The treatment effect favors VR over standard treatment or control for most clinical procedures, although the treatment effect was greater in the venipuncture group.

Prediction interval

The dispersion of the studies was used to calculate a prediction interval. Assuming that the true effects follow a normal distribution, we can estimate that, in 95% of all comparable populations, the change in the pain score on using VR in children undergoing procedures may range from −2.81 to 1.07 [Figure 3].

Publication bias

Publication bias was assessed for studies including funnel plots. The plot showed publication bias. Eggers test was found to be significant (P = 0.0228), indicating a publication bias. Twelve out of 34 studies lay outside the contour of the funnel plot [Figure 5].

DISCUSSION

The objective of this systematic review was to consolidate the existing literature on the effectiveness of VR interventions in alleviating the pain experienced by pediatric patients during medical procedures. The meta-analysis revealed that VR distraction had a pain-reducing effect in pediatric patients undergoing venipuncture, dental treatment, and burn dressing. The commonly employed technique in the studies was nonimmersive VR, which utilizes a computer screen or a mobile device to create a virtual environment. Users view the virtual world through a screen without experiencing full immersion, such as 360° videos or virtual tours.

Significant reductions in pain were observed across all studies, except for Armstrong et al.,[17] Eijlers et al.,[40] and Walther-Larsen et al.[46] The study by Walther-Larsen et al. showed elevated pain in the control group but was statistically insignificant. The patients showed better acceptability with the VR technique. Although few studies did not report a significant difference, no adverse effect was reported with VR.

In one study conducted by Andrea et al.,[20] topical anesthetics EMLAR© or Fluori-MethaneR© were applied at the site of injection as a standard procedure while using other distraction techniques, resulting in a significant pain reduction with VR intervention. The pooled estimate from our analysis yielded a SMD of −0.87 at a 95% CI of (−1.20, −0.53).

To explore the presence of heterogeneity, a subgroup analysis based on the clinical procedure was employed. Burn dressing procedures demonstrated low heterogeneity (0%) and were not significant, indicating positive rehabilitation outcomes without adverse effects across all evaluated studies.[17323351] However, dental procedures exhibited heterogeneity, potentially due to variations in the specific procedures performed and the VR techniques employed. The dental procedures included local anesthesia administration, rubber dam placement, restoration, and fluoride application. Further, a sensitivity analysis was conducted excluding studies using distraction group, median, and crossover study design. The prediction interval showed that the effect of VR on pain in pediatric patients will vary from −2.81 to 1.07. On analyzing for publication bias, it was found to be significant according to Egger’s test and funnel plot analysis. Possible contributing factors to this bias include small sample sizes, lack of blinding, and investigator bias in outcome reporting. Another review conducted by Wiederhold et al.[4] encompassed 13 studies but did not perform a meta-analysis.

Although information regarding VR interventions was often limited in the included studies, it was evident that the interventions varied in terms of technical specifications, interactivity levels, and displayed media.

A study by İnangil et al.[29] used tablets to view cartons as a medium of distraction, another study by Ran et al.[39] used books, DVD movies, a television set, an iPad, and bubbles to distract children. Jeffs et al.[34] used “Cloudy with a Chance of Meatballs” produced by Columbia Pictures and Sony Animations, on a small, arm-mounted, movable television at the bedside. They listened through high-quality, external sound-cancelling Bose Quiet Comfort 3 headphones.

Immersion, a critical aspect, is influenced by the user’s interaction with the virtual environment, including translation, rotation, point of view, and field of view. Non-VR content, such as regular (cartoon) videos or 360° videos, provides less immersion as the user’s movements and progress are limited to the filmmaker’s perspective. Increased immersion has been hypothesized to correlate with greater pain reduction due to reduced attention available for pain perception.[1516] However, objectively analyzing this phenomenon proves challenging. Patients were required to keep their heads still during certain medical procedures, such as dental treatment, potentially limiting immersion.

The review indicated that VR distraction decreased pain in pediatric patients undergoing venipuncture, reservoir puncture, and other needle procedures, as supported by the included trials. The quality of the studies included in this systematic review and meta-analysis varied. While many studies employed randomization and provided clear inclusion and exclusion criteria, concealed treatment allocation was not always ensured, and intention-to-treat analyses were frequently omitted. No adverse effects were observed among the study participants. The heterogeneity observed in this review can be attributed to demographic variations among the study populations, different geographical locations, variations in the age groups included, differences in clinical procedures and their durations, diverse VR modes, and the utilization of different pain scales.

Considering the cost-effectiveness of VR interventions is crucial. While VR technology incurs upfront acceptable costs for equipment and software development, potential cost savings can be achieved through resource reduction, workflow optimization, and improved patient comfort. Future research should strive to standardize the types of medical procedures analyzed and the pain assessment measures employed.

Evidence limitations

The studies included in this review had publication bias, and small sample sizes, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Pain, being a subjective factor, is mostly reliant on patients’ subjective responses and can vary depending on the patient’s age and gender. Blinding proved challenging in these trials as the participants were aware of the intervention, potentially leading to the Hawthorne effect and bias from the investigators. This review includes papers on clinical procedures’ applicability for a wider range of VR in pediatric surgical procedures yet to be tested.

Limitations of the current review

Our review revealed various procedures that can influence patients’ pain perception. All the studies employed different pain scales, introducing variation in the findings.

Recommendation

VR can be a useful tool to decrease pain perception during procedures in children.

CONCLUSION

Overall, the results of this systematic review suggest that VR distraction is a promising intervention for reducing pain experienced by pediatric patients during medical procedures. Health-care professionals should consider incorporating VR into their practice to improve the patient experience and alleviate the distress associated with needle procedures.
