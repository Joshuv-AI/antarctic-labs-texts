Cross–cell-line conservation-resolved interactions reveal conservation-dependent relationships among CTCF loops
Authors: Maryam Mirabolghasemi, Mohammad Hossein Karimi-Jafari, Ali Mohammad Banaei-Moghaddam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe three-dimensional organization of the genome is shaped by CTCF-mediated chromatin loops, which vary widely in strength, conservation, and cell-type specificity. A longstanding model proposes that highly conserved loops form autonomously and establish a structural scaffold that supports the subsequent formation of less conserved, cell-type-specific interactions. However, genome-wide evidence for an ordered dependency among loops of different conservation levels has remained limited.Here, we tested this hierarchical model using high-resolution CTCF ChIA-PET data from eight human cell lines. We developed a two-stage predictive framework in which high-confidence loops were first identified using sequence features, chromatin context, and cross-cell-line conservation, and then augmented with neighboring-interaction features that quantify the influence of pre-existing loops at nearby CTCF anchors. Incorporation of neighboring-interaction information resulted in only modest overall improvements in predictive performance.To directly assess hierarchical dependencies, we stratified loops into eight conservation classes based on their recurrence across cell lines and systematically evaluated how neighboring interactions from each class contributed to loop prediction in others. This conservation-resolved analysis revealed a structured pattern in predictive relationships: loops within a given conservation class were most strongly predicted by neighboring loops from adjacent conservation classes. In contrast, the most highly conserved loops showed little improvement from local loop-context information.Together, these results demonstrate that the predictive value of neighboring chromatin interactions depends strongly on loop conservation and that chromatin interaction neighborhoods contain structured conservation-dependent information. More broadly, our neighboring-interaction framework provides an interpretable approach for identifying structured associations within chromatin interaction networks and for generating hypotheses regarding the organization of three-dimensional genome architecture.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12864-026-13169-w.Keywords: Chromatin interactions, CTCF-mediated loops, ChIA-PET, Cross-cell-line conservation, 3D Genome architecture

Background

The spatial organization of the genome provides a critical regulatory layer that shapes enhancer–promoter communication and stabilizes cellular identity [1, 2]. Rather than functioning as a linear polymer, chromatin folds into a hierarchical three-dimensional architecture composed of chromosome territories, A/B compartments, topologically associating domains (TADs), sub-TADs, and focal chromatin loops [3, 4]. Disruption of this architecture has been widely linked to developmental defects and cancer-associated transcriptional dysregulation, underscoring the importance of understanding how these structural features are established and coordinated [5, 6].

Within this hierarchy, TADs and sub-TADs represent fundamental organizational units that constrain regulatory interactions while enabling long-range communication within defined genomic neighborhoods [7, 8]. Notably, TAD boundaries are highly conserved across cell types, whereas the internal organization of domains—including sub-TAD structures and chromatin loops—exhibits substantial cell-type-specific variability [9, 10]. The spatial organization of the genome can further be viewed as a layered boundary system, in which large, stable TADs are demarcated by canonical boundaries, while smaller, more dynamic sub-TADs are often delineated by TAD-like boundaries. Unlike strong, evolutionarily conserved boundaries that establish the primary chromosomal framework, TAD-like boundaries frequently display weaker insulation and greater cell-type specificity. These boundary classes also differ in their molecular features: canonical boundaries are typically enriched for dense arrays of CTCF binding sites, whereas TAD-like boundaries are more often associated with active chromatin states and regulatory elements. This distinction has important implications for interpreting CTCF-mediated loops, as canonical boundaries tend to anchor highly persistent structural interactions, while TAD-like boundaries may support more flexible, cell-type-specific looping patterns [2, 11, 12]. This coexistence of boundary conservation and internal plasticity suggests that chromatin architecture is not assembled uniformly, but instead emerges through distinct and potentially ordered organizational principles [11, 13].

A major determinant of both TAD and sub-TAD boundary formation is the CCCTC-binding factor (CTCF). CTCF binding sites anchor chromatin loops, contribute to domain insulation, and regulate enhancer–promoter interactions through cohesin-mediated loop extrusion [2, 14]. A key molecular determinant of CTCF-mediated loop formation is the relative orientation of CTCF binding motifs at the two anchor loci, as formalised by the loop extrusion model [15, 16]. In this model, cohesin complexes extrude chromatin until they encounter CTCF sites that act as barriers [16, 17]. The relative orientation between the two sites influences but does not strictly determine loop formation [18]. Three orientation classes are defined based on the strand direction of the CTCF motif (forward + or reverse -) at the left and right anchors: convergent (+ on left, - on right), divergent (- on left, + on right), and tandem (both + or both -). Convergent orientation is the most frequently observed configuration among loops identified by Hi-C and ChIA-PET [19], consistent with the extrusion model’s prediction that head-to-head sites efficiently stall cohesin.

While boundary-associated CTCF sites tend to be constitutively occupied and conserved, CTCF binding within TAD interiors is more dynamic, providing a molecular basis for the observed flexibility of sub-TAD structures. Perturbation of CTCF binding, cohesin activity, or loop extrusion dynamics leads to widespread reorganization of chromatin architecture and altered transcriptional programs [20, 21]. Genome-wide chromosome conformation assays such as Hi-C and ChIA-PET have revealed extensive heterogeneity in CTCF-mediated loop strength, presence, and conservation across cell types [22–26].

Together, these observations suggest that the conservation of TAD and sub-TAD boundaries may reflect an underlying hierarchy in CTCF-mediated loop formation. In this view, a subset of highly conserved CTCF loops—often corresponding to TAD boundaries—forms early and establishes a stable structural scaffold. This scaffold may subsequently facilitate or constrain the formation of less conserved, cell-type-specific CTCF loops within domains [27–31]. Such a model implies that chromatin loop formation is a dynamic and interdependent process, in which the presence of existing loops influences the probability of additional loop formation nearby. However, direct genome-wide evidence supporting this ordered and directional dependency between CTCF loops of different conservation levels remains limited [32, 33].

Here, we test this scaffold-based model of CTCF loop formation using high-resolution CTCF ChIA-PET data from eight human cell lines. We leverage the conservation properties of TAD- and sub-TAD-associated loops to stratify CTCF-mediated interactions and introduce data-driven neighboring-interaction features that quantify how pre-existing loops influence the formation of additional loops at nearby CTCF anchors. Using a two-stage predictive framework (Fig. 1), we first identify high-confidence loops based on sequence features, chromatin context, and cross-cell conservation, and then assess how incorporating neighboring-interaction information alters loop predictability.

Overview of the two-stage modeling framework for predicting CTCF-mediated chromatin loops. The proposed framework captures how the formation of chromatin loops is influenced by neighboring interaction features. Stage 1: CTCF-mediated loops are identified within a given cell line (cell line X) using CTCF binding site (CBS) motifs, CTCF ChIP-seq, and ChIA-PET data. Sequence-based, chromatin-derived, and cross-cell conservation features are extracted and used to train a gradient boosting classifier to predict high-confidence loops. Stage 2: Loops predicted with high confidence (probability > 0.95) and low confidence (probability < 0.05) are used to derive neighboring interaction features, including the degree of upstream and downstream high- and low-confidence edges associated with nearby CBSs. These features are then incorporated into a second gradient boosting model to refine loop prediction by modeling spatial dependencies among adjacent chromatin loops

By systematically evaluating interactions across conservation classes in a leave-one-cell-line-out setting, we uncover a strikingly ordered dependency structure: loops of a given conservation level are most strongly influenced by neighboring loops that are more highly conserved, whereas the most conserved loops show minimal dependence on local context. Together, these results provide genome-wide quantitative support for a dynamic, cascade-like model of CTCF-mediated loop formation, in which conserved TAD-associated loops establish a structural scaffold that guides the formation of progressively less conserved, cell-type-specific interactions. More broadly, this conservation-informed framework offers a generalizable strategy for predicting CTCF-mediated loops and inferring the order and directionality of chromatin architecture establishment.

Results

Conservation-stratified properties of CTCF-mediated loops across eight human cell lines

To systematically investigate how CTCF-mediated loops vary across cellular contexts, we analyzed ChIA-PET interaction maps from eight human cell lines (H1, HCT116, SK-N-SH, MCF-7, HepG2, IMR-90, K562, and GM12878). After filtering for canonical CTCF motifs, CTCF ChIP-seq peaks, and replicate-supported interactions, we quantified loop and anchor counts per cell line (Fig. 2A). H1, SK-N-SH, and MCF-7 displayed the largest numbers of CTCF loops, consistent with their more dynamic chromatin states, whereas differentiated lines such as IMR-90 exhibited fewer unique interactions, reflecting their more stable nuclear architecture. To assess whether variation in loop abundance could be explained by differences in CTCF occupancy, we examined the relationship between the number of CTCF ChIP-seq peaks and the number of detected ChIA-PET loops across cell lines. The number of loops showed a moderate positive correlation with the number of CTCF peaks (Pearson r = 0.571, p = 0.139), indicating that while increased CTCF occupancy is associated with a greater number of loops, the number of CTCF binding sites alone does not fully account for the observed variation in loop abundance across cell types.

Loops were assigned to eight conservation groups based on their presence across cell lines. Loop anchors were generally more conserved than loops themselves (Fig. 2A), indicating that anchor availability alone does not determine loop formation and suggesting contributions from interval features or neighboring topological context. To further characterize the genomic context of anchors with different conservation levels, we overlapped GM12878 CTCF anchors with Roadmap chromatin states (Supplementary Table S1 and Supplementary Figure S1). Conserved and cell-type-specific anchors exhibited remarkably similar distributions across all 20 chromatin states. This observation suggests that anchor conservation is not simply a consequence of broad chromatin environment or epigenetic state. Instead, the formation and persistence of CTCF-mediated loops are likely influenced by more specific factors, such as motif orientation, CTCF binding strength, and local topological context. Approximately 2,913 loops were shared across all eight cell lines; these universally conserved loops likely demarcate core structural domains associated with housekeeping and essential regulatory programs. The extent of pairwise loop sharing between cell lines is summarized in Supplementary Table S2. In contrast, loops with limited conservation corresponded to lineage-specific or oncogenic processes [34–37].

Loop-level features showed systematic dependence on conservation. PET counts, CTCF peak intensity, and motif strength all increased monotonically with conservation level (Fig. 2B), suggesting that persistent loops are anchored by stronger and more consistently bound CTCF sites. Loop length did not vary substantially across conservation groups, indicating that conservation is not simply a function of genomic distance. Conservation also strongly correlated with anchor orientation: convergent CTCF motifs predominated in highly conserved loops, whereas divergent and tandem orientations were enriched among less conserved groups (Fig. 2C). Together, these results establish a quantitative, multi-feature profile of loop conservation that forms the basis for downstream predictive and cross-influence analyses. Statistical significance of differences across conservation groups was assessed using linear mixed-effects models (see Methods; Supplementary Figures S2–S3 and Supplementary Tables S3-S4).

Contributions of cross-cell conservation and neighboring-interaction features to loop prediction

We next evaluated how cross-cell conservation features and neighboring-interaction features improve loop prediction. Candidate CBS pairs were classified as interacting or non-interacting. Interacting pairs were defined as CBS pairs forming ChIA-PET loops, whereas non-interacting pairs were CBS pairs that lacked a loop in the test cell line but formed a loop in at least one other cell line, resulting in distinct interacting-to-non-interacting class ratios across the eight cell lines analyzed (Fig. 3A). Because conservation features require that a CBS pair appear at least once in the training set, cell-type–unique loops were excluded from this evaluation.

Adding cross-cell conservation features to the baseline model (sequence- and chromatin-derived features) substantially increased predictive performance, with AUC values improving from 0.77 to 0.85 to 0.84–0.91 (Fig. 3C). Similar improvements were observed for the precision–recall area under the curve (PRAUC), the results of which are shown in Supplementary Figure S4. These gains reflect the ability of cross-cell conservation features to capture both loop presence and PET-count–based interaction strength across multiple cell types, thereby enabling the model to leverage shared chromatin architectural patterns.

To test whether local topological context further improves prediction, we developed a two-stage framework incorporating Neighboring-Interaction features. High-confidence interacting and non-interacting regions were first identified using predicted probabilities (> 0.95 or < 0.05). The resulting high-confidence loops were enriched in conserved groups (Fig. 3B). From these, we derived upstream and downstream interaction-degree metrics for each CBS, which constitute the Neighboring-Interaction features, weighted by distance and computed for both training and test cell lines. The design and rationale of these features are detailed in the Methods section.

Incorporating these Neighboring-Interaction features produced a modest additional AUC gain (≈ 0.01–0.02; Fig. 3C). To assess the statistical significance of these gains, we performed pairwise Wilcoxon signed-rank tests (paired by cell line) on the AUCs of the three models across the eight cell lines. The addition of cross-cell conservation features (conservation model vs. baseline model) yielded a large, statistically significant improvement (p = 0.0078, effect size |r| = 0.891). Adding neighboring-interaction features on top of conservation (two-stage model vs. conservation model) produced a moderate effect (|r| = 0.545) that did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.1484). This limited improvement likely reflects the dominance of conserved loops among the high-confidence set, reducing discrimination between interacting and non-interacting classes and constraining the added value of neighboring context.

This small gain aligns with the fact that conserved loops—already easy to predict—dominate the high-confidence set, whereas variable loops that might benefit most from contextual information remain intrinsically difficult to classify from static features [9, 31].

Feature importance analysis further supported the contribution of conservation-based predictors (Fig. 4). Following the addition of cross-cell conservation features, measures of interaction conservation and PET-count variability (e.g., ave_pet and std_pet) ranked among the most influential predictors, alongside CTCF and RAD21 occupancy at loop anchors. The strong contribution of conservation-related features may be partially influenced by the construction of the training dataset, in which interacting and non-interacting pairs are unevenly distributed across conservation groups. After neighboring-interaction features were incorporated in the second-stage model, several local topological context variables appeared among the top-ranked predictors. However, their importance scores were generally lower than those of conservation- and occupancy-based features, consistent with the comparatively modest improvement in overall predictive performance. Data-driven neighboring-interaction features reveal conservation-level cross-influence

To determine whether the limited improvement observed in Contributions of Cross-Cell Conservation and Neighboring-Interaction Features to Loop Prediction reflects a biological signal rather than statistical saturation, we quantified cross-influence among conservation groups using data-driven neighboring-interaction features derived directly from observed loops. Loops were stratified into eight conservation groups (Fig. 5A). For each group, we sampled an equal number of interacting and non-interacting pairs (approximately 10,000 each) to remove size bias. Groups 6, 7, and 8 contained fewer than 10,000 non-interacting pairs and were therefore excluded from the analysis. We then treated loops from one conservation tier as the known “initiating set” and evaluated their ability to predict loops from all other tiers using ΔF1 scores under a leave-one-cell-line-out design, comparing performance with and without neighboring interaction features derived from the initiating set. To avoid potential bias from cross-cell conservation features, we excluded them from the analysis.

For each tested conservation group, we computed four corresponding neighboring-interaction feature sets—one from each remaining conservation tier. Each bar in Fig. 5B reflects the mean influence across eight held-out cell lines.

Two notable patterns emerged. First, conservation groups exerted the strongest influence on their immediate neighbors; loops generally showed the greatest predictive effect on groups located one conservation tier above or below them. This pattern suggests that loops with similar conservation levels share regulatory or architectural contexts that mutually reinforce formation. Second, among the introduced neighborhood features, the number of loops contained within a candidate loop (inner_loop) had the strongest predictive effect. However, its influence progressively decreased as the initiating loops became more conserved (Supplementary Figures S5–S9). One possible explanation is that highly conserved loops and their neighboring conserved loops represent more stable chromatin structures. As a result, their formation and maintenance may depend less on the surrounding loop architecture and more on intrinsic chromatin features, such as CTCF and RAD21 binding. This interpretation is consistent with the observations presented in the Conservation-Stratified Properties of CTCF-Mediated Loops Across Eight Human Cell Lines section, which showed that chromatin features become increasingly associated with loop conservation.

Together, these analyses support a model in which loop formation is neither independent nor simultaneous. Instead, conservation-stratified groups exhibit directional, near-neighbor dependencies consistent with a stepwise, cascade-like organization of CTCF-mediated folding.

Discussion

The primary objective of this study was to determine whether information from neighboring chromatin interactions can improve the prediction of CTCF-mediated loops. To address this question, we developed a two-stage framework in which candidate loops were first predicted using sequence features, chromatin context, and cross-cell conservation and were subsequently augmented with features describing their local interaction environment. Incorporating neighboring-loop information consistently improved prediction performance, demonstrating that chromatin interaction neighborhoods contain predictive information beyond that captured by intrinsic loop features. However, the overall performance gain was modest, suggesting that the contribution of neighboring interactions varies across different classes of chromatin loops.

Conservation-stratified analyses revealed a clear pattern: neighboring interactions were most informative for less-conserved loops and progressively less informative as the conservation level of the neighboring loops increased. Among the neighborhood-derived features, the number of loops contained within a candidate loop (inner_loop) provided the strongest predictive signal, although its contribution declined with increasing conservation of the neighboring loops. These findings indicate that predictive information derived from local chromatin neighborhoods is distributed unevenly across conservation groups.

The relationships among conservation groups were also non-uniform. Neighboring loops generally exerted their strongest influence on loops within the adjacent conservation categories, indicating that loops with similar conservation levels share common regulatory or architectural contexts. In addition, features derived from more highly conserved loops improved the prediction of nearby less-conserved loops. Together, these observations suggest that chromatin interaction neighborhoods are structured rather than random and that predictive information is organized according to loop conservation.

The reduced contribution of neighborhood features derived from highly conserved loops may reflect differences in the factors underlying loop formation and maintenance. In our conservation-stratified analysis of loop properties, highly conserved loops showed stronger associations with architectural proteins such as CTCF and RAD21. This observation suggests that conserved loops may represent relatively stable chromatin structures whose occurrence is determined primarily by intrinsic architectural features. Consistent with this interpretation, the predictive influence of inner_loop and other neighborhood features progressively declined as the conservation level of the neighboring loops increased, suggesting that highly conserved loops contribute less neighborhood-derived predictive information than less-conserved loops.

Our findings are consistent with previous studies showing that chromatin interactions differ in their stability and regulatory dependence. Perturbation experiments have demonstrated that CTCF and cohesin contribute differently to chromatin organization, while architectural proteins play distinct roles in shaping genome structure during lineage commitment [38–41]. Together, these observations support the view that conserved and less-conserved loops occupy distinct organizational contexts, with conserved loops representing stable architectural features and less-conserved loops reflecting more context-dependent interactions.

Importantly, the predictive relationships identified here should not be interpreted as direct evidence of mechanistic dependencies among chromatin loops. Although neighboring-loop features improve prediction performance, these associations may arise from shared regulatory determinants, common chromatin environments, or other aspects of genome organization. Nevertheless, the conservation-dependent patterns observed in this study are compatible with models in which different classes of chromatin loops occupy distinct organizational roles within the genome. In particular, the observation that features derived from more highly conserved loops improve the prediction of nearby less-conserved loops is consistent with the possibility that stable chromatin interactions provide an architectural context for more variable interactions. However, because the conservation groups analyzed here were derived from diverse cell types rather than from a defined temporal process, the present data cannot distinguish whether these relationships reflect temporal patterns of loop establishment or other forms of chromatin organization. Future analyses using temporal and perturbation-based datasets, including differentiation systems, lineage-specification models, and single-cell chromatin interaction measurements, will be necessary to determine whether the conservation-dependent associations identified here correspond to a cascade-like process of chromatin loop formation and reorganization.

A major strength of the proposed framework is its interpretability. Rather than treating chromatin loop prediction as a black-box classification task, the conservation-stratified approach quantifies how neighboring interactions contribute to prediction across biologically meaningful categories of loops. More broadly, the framework provides a practical method for identifying structured associations within chromatin interaction networks and may be applicable to comparative analyses of development, disease, and experimental perturbation datasets.

In summary, neighboring chromatin interactions improve the prediction of CTCF-mediated loops, although their contribution depends strongly on loop conservation. Neighborhood-derived features provide the greatest predictive benefit when derived from less-conserved loops, whereas highly conserved loops appear to contribute comparatively little neighborhood-based information. These conservation-dependent patterns reveal structured relationships among chromatin interactions and suggest that different classes of loops may occupy distinct organizational contexts within the genome. While additional temporal and perturbation-based data will be required to evaluate whether these relationships reflect a cascade-like process of loop establishment, our framework provides a simple and interpretable approach for identifying structured associations within chromatin interaction networks and generating hypotheses regarding the organization of three-dimensional genome architecture.

Materials and methods

ChIA-PET data collection and processing

To investigate CTCF-mediated loops, we collected ChIA-PET datasets for eight human cell lines: H1 (GEO: GSE242609), HCT116 (GEO: GSE176973), HepG2 (GEO: GSE177153), MCF-7 (GEO: GSE176598), SK-N-SH (GEO: GSE177127), IMR-90 (GEO: GSE176936), K562 (GEO: GSE177471), and GM12878 (4DN: 4DNES7IB5LY9) [42–44]. Raw sequencing data were processed using the ChIA-PIPE pipeline [45]. which performs linker filtering, read alignment, peak calling, and loop identification. Chromatin loops supported by more than three paired-end tags (PETs) were retained for downstream analyses.

To specifically identify CTCF-mediated loops, additional filters were applied. First, loop anchors were intersected with CTCF ChIP-Seq peaks to select regions likely bound by CTCF. Second, loop anchors were required to overlap CTCF binding site (CBS) motifs, with each motif extended by 1 kb upstream and downstream to account for the limited length of the core motif relative to experimentally defined loop anchor regions and to capture local sequence context relevant to CTCF-mediated looping. This approach aligns with previous sequence-based chromatin interaction prediction studies, including DeepMILO [46], and DeepCTCFLoop [47], have incorporated kilobase-scale flanking regions. This ensured that all loop anchors corresponded to CBS motifs, making loops comparable across cell lines and allowing the interactions of CBS motifs to be tracked consistently across cell lines. Overlap analysis was performed using BEDTools [48].

To enable motif-level analyses, chromatin interactions were represented at the level of individual CBS motifs rather than entire loop anchor regions. Consequently, anchors overlapping multiple CBS motifs were allowed to contribute multiple motif-level interactions. When a ChIA-PET anchor contained CBS motifs with different orientations, each possible motif pair was treated as a distinct interaction during feature extraction and downstream analyses. Accordingly, motif orientation and motif strength were assigned based on the specific interacting CBS motif pair rather than the anchor region as a whole.

To further enhance reliability, only loops detected in both replicates of each ChIA-PET dataset were retained, and the minimum PET count across replicates was used as the final PET count for these loops. Summary data quality metrics for each ChIA-PET dataset are provided in Supplementary Table S8.

Classification of CBS sequences into interacting and non-interacting classes

The CBS sequences identified in the training model were categorized into two classes: interacting and non-interacting. Interacting CBSs comprised filtered loops derived from ChIA-PET experiments, representing regions where CBS motifs overlapped with CTCF ChIP-Seq peaks and exhibited physical interactions between two DNA segments within a given cell line. In contrast, non-interacting CBSs were defined as motifs that formed loops in at least one other cell line but did not interact in the cell line of interest. This classification was performed separately for each training cell line considered as the cell of interest. The interacting and non-interacting CBSs from all training cells were then combined to create a comprehensive dataset for model training, containing positive samples (interacting CBSs) and negative samples (non-interacting CBSs). This dataset enabled the predictive model to learn patterns and features associated with CBS-mediated interactions across multiple cell lines. A potential limitation of this design is that defining negatives as loops present elsewhere makes conservation features inherently more discriminative, because the ratio of negative to positive in the more conserved groups is lower (i.e., skewed toward positive examples). The sizes of the training and test datasets are provided in Supplementary Table S9.

Feature engineering for model training

The features used for model training were divided into four categories: sequence-based features, chromatin features, cross-cell conservation features, and neighboring-interaction features.

Sequence-based features

Sequence-based features included CBS motif strength, motif orientation, and loop length.1 for divergent, 2 for tandem, and 3 for convergent arrangements [49, 50]. The position weight matrix (PWM) for the CBS motif (MA0139.1) was obtained from the JASPAR database [51], and FIMO [52] was used to compute motif strength and orientation scores. Loop length was defined as the genomic distance between the two interacting CBS motifs [53].

Chromatin features

Chromatin features were derived from ChIP-seq data for 11 histone modifications, as well as CTCF and Cohesin binding profiles [43]. The mean peak intensity of all overlapping peaks was taken as the anchor’s chromatin signal. For loop interval regions, we ran SICER to obtain enrichment scores. All values were converted to RPKM: RPKM=(signal_mean*109) / (total_mapped_reads * region_length). The ENCODE accession codes corresponding to these chromatin features for each cell line are provided in Supplementary S7 Table. The histone marks analyzed included H3K4me1, H3K4me2, H3K4me3, H3K9me3, H3K36me3, H3K79me2, H3K27ac, H3K9ac, H3K27me3, H2AFZ, and H4K20me1. ChIP-seq peak data from the ENCODE Project [43] were used to extract features for loop anchor regions, while SICER [54] was used to compute enrichment signals across loop interval regions. Chromatin signal intensity was quantified using RPKM (Reads Per Kilobase per Million) values.

Cross-cell conservation features

Cross-cell conservation features were designed to assess the interaction status of CBS motifs across multiple cell lines and to predict their likelihood of interaction in a given target cell line. To do this analysis each cell line’s interactions were represented as a graph, where CBS motifs corresponded to vertices and loops between them to edges. Graph-based metrics, such as vertex degree and edge weight (PET count), were extracted from this representation. To prevent data leakage, features for a target cell line were computed while excluding data from that same cell line. Additional cross-cell conservation features included the frequency of each loop (edge) across the training cell lines and the activity frequency of CBS motifs (proportion of vertices with degree > 0).

Neighboring-interaction features

Neighboring-interaction features were developed to assess how pre-existing chromatin loops formed by nearby CBS motifs influence the likelihood of a given CBS motif participating in a new CTCF-mediated interaction. This design captures potential hierarchical dependencies in chromatin folding, consistent with the organization of topologically associating domains (TADs) [1, 2], which are characterized by enriched intra-domain interactions and conserved structural patterns.

Neighboring-interaction features were computed in two steps

First, pre-existing loop activity was quantified by measuring the interaction degree of upstream and downstream loops associated with each CBS motif in both the target and training cell lines. In the training data, interaction degrees were aggregated across all training cell lines. Each CBS motif was thus characterized by four features: (i) upstream loop degree in the target cell line, (ii) downstream loop degree in the target cell line, (iii) upstream loop degree across training cell lines, and (iv) downstream loop degree across training cell lines. This distinction is inspired by the Directionality Index (DI) used in Hi-C to identify TAD boundaries. A CBS with high downstream degree resembles a locus with positive DI (preferential downstream interactions), while high upstream degree mimics negative DI [11]. Thus, these metrics capture the local topological polarity of each CBS. In addition, we examined the relationship between pre-existing loops and candidate motif pairs by quantifying three counts: how many pre-existing loops fall entirely within the candidate loop, how many lie completely outside it, and how many of the existing loops cross over the candidate loop.

Second, neighboring information was integrated by aggregating features from the five nearest upstream and five nearest downstream CBS motifs relative to each tested loop anchor. This number was chosen based on empirical observation: the mean distance from a loop anchor to its fifth nearest CBS is approximately 450 kb, and over 90% of all loops in our dataset have a total length less than 450 kb. Each neighboring CBS contributed a five-dimensional feature vector comprising the four interaction-degree features described above and a distance feature representing its genomic distance from the target anchor. Consequently, the neighboring-interaction feature vector for a single tested anchor consisted of 55 elements (11 CBS motifs × 5 features per motif).

Pre-existing loops in the test cell line (red arcs) and loops aggregated across all training cell lines (black arcs) are mapped around adjacent CBS motifs (–5 to + 5) centered on the target CBS (0). For each CBS, upstream and downstream loop degrees are computed separately in the held-out test cell line and from the aggregated training loops (union of all non-test cell lines), yielding four features: upstream and downstream loop degrees in the test cell line and in the training set. This process is repeated such that each cell line serves once as the test condition, generating neighboring interaction feature vectors used for loop prediction

Statistical analysis of conservation-associated features

To evaluate differences in loop- and anchor-level characteristics across conservation groups while accounting for variation between cell lines, we fitted linear mixed-effects models with conservation group as a fixed effect and cell line as a random intercept. Pairwise comparisons between conservation groups were assessed using model-derived coefficients and p-values. Heatmaps of model coefficients for loop- and anchor-level features are presented in Supplementary Figures S2–S3. Complete statistical results, including estimated coefficients, confidence intervals, and exact p-values for all comparisons, are provided in Supplementary Tables S3-S4.

Two-stage training framework for CTCF-mediated loop prediction

To predict CTCF-mediated chromatin loops, interacting and non-interacting classes were defined, and the model was trained in two sequential steps.

In the first step, CTCF-mediated loops were predicted using three classes of features: sequence-based features, chromatin-derived features, and cross-cell conservation features. Model training was performed using a Gradient Boosting Classifier [55, 56]. To address class imbalance, within each cross-cell conservation group, if the ratio of non-interacting to interacting pairs exceeded 2:1, the majority class was downsampled to achieve a 1:1 ratio .In the second step, high-confidence loops were identified as those with predicted probabilities greater than 95% (interacting class) or lower than 5% (non-interacting class). These high-confidence predictions were then used to derive neighboring-interaction features based on the local loop context. Using this expanded feature set—comprising sequence-based, chromatin-derived, cross-cell conservation, and neighboring-interaction features—CTCF-mediated loops were subsequently re-predicted in a second training round.

Conservation-stratified modeling of neighboring interactions features

To analyze the influence of certain loop formations on the establishment of other loops, we incorporated neighboring-interaction features in a modified framework. Loops were first categorized into eight conservation groups, corresponding to the eight analyzed cell lines. For each iteration, one conservation group was designated as the initiating group, from which neighboring-interaction features were derived. These features were then used to predict loop formation in the remaining conservation groups by classifying CBS motifs into interacting and non-interacting categories. The predictive performance was evaluated using the F1 score, based on models trained with four types of features: sequence-based, chromatin-derived, cross-cell conservation, and neighboring-interaction features, utilizing a Gradient Boosting Classifier [55, 56].
