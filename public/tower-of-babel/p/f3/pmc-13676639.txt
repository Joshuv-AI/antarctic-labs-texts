Streptococcal toxic shock syndrome caused by ST525 Streptococcus dysgalactiae subsp. equisimilis with genomic characterization of virulence and antimicrobial resistance
Authors: Shuma Tsuji, Shinnosuke Fukushima, Kazuyoshi Gotoh, Koji Iio, Maki Tanioka, Hideharu Hagiya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Streptococcus dysgalactiae subsp. equisimilis (SDSE) is an emerging cause of severe invasive infections. We describe the clinical course of an immunocompromised patient with advanced breast cancer who developed streptococcal toxic shock syndrome, necrotizing fasciitis, and disseminated intramuscular abscesses caused by a multidrug-resistant Lancefield group G SDSE isolate, alongside a comprehensive genomic characterization of its virulence and antimicrobial resistance profiles. Genomic analysis identified the isolate as emm subtype stG840.0 and sequence type 525 (ST525), harboring genes encoding several virulence-associated factors, including an emm-like gene encoding an M-like surface protein, streptokinase, streptolysin O, streptolysin S, and SpeG. Through comprehensive genomic analysis of the highly virulent SDSE isolate, we provided insights into its underlying genetic background. Further accumulation of genomic data is expected to fully elucidate the mechanisms of virulence and antimicrobial resistance in SDSE.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s10096-026-05590-6.

Streptococcus dysgalactiae subsp. equisimilis (SDSE), mainly expressing Lancefield group C or G antigens, shares numerous virulence factors with Streptococcus pyogenes and thus is capable of causing a spectrum of severe invasive diseases, including streptococcal toxic shock syndrome (STSS) and necrotizing fasciitis [1, 2]. Recent epidemiological studies have shown that invasive SDSE infections are increasing particularly among older adults [3]. In a nationwide study in Japan, SDSE accounted for 49.5% of adult STSS cases, representing the largest proportion among the three major β-hemolytic streptococcal species [4]. Although invasive SDSE infections have been generally well documented, the clinical and genomic profiles of severe disease driven by multidrug-resistant stG840/ST525 strains remain poorly characterized. Here, we describe the comprehensive features of a multidrug-resistant Lancefield group G SDSE isolate detected in an immunocompromised patient presenting with STSS, necrotizing fasciitis, and multiple systemic abscesses. This case potentially contributes valuable insights to the clinical and genomic surveillance of emerging SDSE lineages in Japan.

The patient was a 50-year-old Japanese woman with Stage IV breast cancer, characterized by multiple lymph node and bone metastases. She had been receiving first-line endocrine therapy consisting of the aromatase inhibitor letrozole in combination with the cyclin-dependent kinase (CDK) 4/6 inhibitor abemaciclib. She presented with a 4-day history of fever and progressive erythema extending from the anterior chest and back to the left upper arm (Fig. 1). She was initially diagnosed with cellulitis and treated with oral levofloxacin; however, her condition deteriorated and she was subsequently admitted with hypotension and acute kidney injury. On the day after hospitalization, blood cultures yielded S. dysgalactiae, which was determined by matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS) (BIOtyper, Bruker Daltonics, Bremen, Germany). Antimicrobial susceptibility testing was performed using the broth microdilution method (Dry Plate “Eiken”, Eiken Chemical, Tokyo, Japan), and the isolate was found to be highly susceptible to penicillin G (MIC ≤ 0.06 µg/mL), while showing resistance to macrolides (erythromycin [MIC > 2 µg/mL], clarithromycin [MIC > 16 µg/mL], and azithromycin [MIC > 4 µg/mL]), clindamycin (MIC > 2 µg/mL), and minocycline (MIC > 4 µg/mL) based on the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) M100 35th edition. Given the presentation of septic shock, diffuse erythema, and renal failure, she met the diagnostic criteria for STSS. She was empirically started on piperacillin/tazobactam, followed by a combination with ampicillin and clindamycin once susceptibility results were available. Clindamycin was used with the aim of suppressing toxin production despite its in vitro resistance, while β-lactam therapy was continued as definitive antimicrobial treatment.

Contrast-enhanced computed tomography revealed subcutaneous and fascial fat stranding in the left upper arm without evidence of abscess formation, and exploratory incision confirmed the diagnosis of necrotizing fasciitis. Although follow-up blood cultures obtained on admission day 2 were negative, systemic inflammation persisted. Repeated computed tomography performed one week later demonstrated multiple systemic abscesses involving the neck, shoulders, abdomen, and thighs (Fig. 2). The patient required multiple drainage procedures and received a total of 57 days of antimicrobial therapy, including ampicillin, clindamycin, vancomycin, penicillin G, and levofloxacin, after which her general condition gradually improved and she was finally discharged.

The isolate was classified as Lancefield group G using the Strept LA NX kit (Denka Seiken Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). Genome sequencing was performed using a DNBSEQ-G400RS platform (MGI Tech, Shenzhen, China), and short-read sequencing via DNBSEQ was outsourced to a commercial vendor (Genome-Lead Co., Kagawa, Japan). Based on genomic analysis, the isolate was assigned to sequence type 525 (ST525) by multilocus sequence typing (MLST) [5]. In addition, average nucleotide identity (ANI) analysis using JSpeciesWS [6] showed 98.54% identity to S. dysgalactiae subsp. equisimilis ATCC 12,394. The emm typing was performed using the CDC emm sequence database (https://www2.cdc.gov/vaccines/biotech/strepblast.asp). The query sequence showed 100% identity over the 180-bp subtype-determining region to stG840.0.

Furthermore, virulence-associated genes were investigated according to a previous report [7], using BLASTx against a custom database constructed from sequences deposited in the NCBI database. Targeted sequences included genes encoding virulence-associated factors reported in SDSE, alongside group A Streptococcus (GAS)-associated capsule synthesis and superantigen genes. Following genome annotation using DFAST v1.3.0 [8], genes encoding an M-like surface protein, streptokinase (ska), streptolysin O (slo), streptolysin S (sagA), and SpeG (speG) were detected in the present SDSE isolate. While, none of the GAS-associated capsule synthesis genes or superantigen genes were identified in this isolate. Screening for acquired antimicrobial resistance genes was performed using ResFinder (https://cge.food.dtu.dk/services/ResFinder/). ResFinder identified erm(B), associated with macrolide-lincosamide-streptogramin B resistance, and tet(M), associated with tetracycline resistance, with sequence identities of 100.0% and 98.91%, respectively. The Whole Genome Shotgun project for strain OUH-GGS1 was deposited at DDBJ/ENA/GenBank under the accession number JBYVEC000000000, and the version described in this paper is JBYVEC010000000. The draft genome assembly was 2,111,284 bp in length, with a GC content of 39.9%, 328 contigs, an N50 of 63,742 bp, an L50 of 11, and an average coverage of 140×. Recent nationwide surveillance in Japan demonstrated that invasive SDSE infections predominantly affected older adults (a median patient age of 83 years), resulting in an overall 28-day mortality rate of 12.2% [9]. That study also reported a marked increase in emm type stG840, although no significant differences in patient outcomes were observed across distinct emm types. The present case indicates that the severe invasive SDSE infection can occur not only in older populations but also possibly in immunocompromised patients with advanced malignancy. Previous genomic surveillance in Kyoto–Shiga region of Japan reported that ST525 increased after 2016 and that emm type stG840 was found only among ST525 isolates [10]. In that study, all ST525 isolates were non-susceptible to erythromycin, minocycline, and clindamycin and carried both erm(B) and tet(M), which were located on Tn916-like integrative and conjugative elements. These findings suggest that the stG840/ST525 lineage has recently expanded in Japan as an antimicrobial-resistant clone, although a direct association between this lineage and disease severity has not been established.

SDSE isolates are known to harbor genes encoding several virulence-associated factors, including emm or emm-like genes encoding M-like surface proteins. These proteins are homologous to the M protein of S. pyogenes and are considered to contribute to immune evasion and invasive potential, as well as speGdys, a speG orthologue, and other streptococcal pyrogenic superantigen genes, such as speA and speC [11], potentially underlying their capacity to cause invasive diseases. Notably, genomic analysis has revealed a closer phylogenetic relationship between SDSE and GAS than with other streptococci [3, 12]. This genetic homology extends to numerous virulence factors, such as streptolysin O and streptokinase, accounting for their overlapping clinical spectra [13, 14]. However, SDSE differs from GAS in that it generally lacks several major GAS virulence determinants, including speB, superantigens other than SpeG-like genes, and the hasABC operon responsible for capsule synthesis. The fulminant course observed in this case was likely driven by a synergistic interaction between host factors—most notably the immunocompromised state secondary to advanced malignancy—and a repertoire of bacterial virulence factors, including M-like protein, streptokinase, streptolysin O, streptolysin S, and SpeG, rather than the presence of any single toxin gene.

Whole-genome sequencing enabled us to characterize the present isolate as emm subtype stG840.0/ST525 and to identify both virulence-associated genes and acquired antimicrobial resistance genes, including erm(B) and tet(M). These genomic findings were consistent with previous reports describing the recent expansion of antimicrobial-resistant stG840/ST525 SDSE in Japan [10]. However, because this report describes only a single clinical isolate, detailed comparative genomic analyses, including phylogenetic reconstruction, mapping of mobile genetic elements, genetic context analysis of antimicrobial resistance determinants, recombination analysis of virulence-associated genes, and assessment of genotype–phenotype associations, were beyond the scope of this report.

In conclusion, we described an invasive infection caused by emm subtype stG840.0/ST525 SDSE, presenting as STSS complicated by necrotizing fasciitis and disseminated systemic abscesses. Further studies incorporating additional SDSE isolates are needed to clarify whether the genomic features observed in this isolate are associated with invasive disease severity, antimicrobial resistance, clonal expansion, tissue tropism, or the evolution of resistance determinants.
