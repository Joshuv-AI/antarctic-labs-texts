Cholesterol-depleted macrophage membrane-coated nano-rapamycin for targeted atherosclerosis therapy
Authors: Ruru Zhang, Yan Liu, Zeyuan Cao, Zhe Yang, Nan Wen, Dongliang Hu, Kuan Lu, Keyang Xu, Xiao Xiao, Ning Wang, Mengdan Xu, Mengyao Wu, Yadan Shi, Xiuqi Hu, Ling Wen, Jianfeng Zeng, Wu Cai, Shenghong Ju
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Atherosclerosis is the main pathological basis of cardiovascular disease and urgently requires more effective and targeted therapies. Here, we present a cholesterol-modulated macrophage membrane–mimetic nanoplatform for rapamycin delivery, in which β-cyclodextrin is employed to selectively deplete cholesterol from donor cell membranes. Cholesterol depletion significantly improves nanoparticle uptake by inflammatory macrophages, potentially through enhanced membrane fluidity and preserved key receptor–ligand interactions. In vitro, the cholesterol-depleted nanomedicines promote foam cell cholesterol efflux and suppress pro-inflammatory cytokine secretion, with therapeutic efficacy increasing as membrane cholesterol content decreases. In vivo, the resulting “slimming” membrane–coated nanoparticles exhibit enhanced immune evasion, prolonged systemic circulation, and improved plaque targeting, while maintaining excellent biosafety. In atherosclerotic mice, treatment with these nanoparticles reduces plaque area and lipid accumulation while increasing collagen content in a membrane cholesterol–dependent manner, indicating therapeutic effects and enhanced plaque stability. Notably, these benefits are achieved without altering systemic lipid levels, suggesting a primarily lesion-localized mechanism of action. Collectively, this study demonstrates that the “slimming” membrane–mimetic nanoplatform offers a promising approach for precise, inflammation-targeted therapy of atherosclerosis and may be extended to other chronic inflammatory vascular disorders.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12951-026-04758-8.

Introduction

Cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) remain the leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide [1–5]. Atherosclerosis, the principal pathological underpinning of most CVDs, is defined by lipid accumulation, chronic inflammation, and fibrous tissue expansion in the arterial wall [6]. Plaque rupture can rapidly initiate platelet activation and coagulation cascades, culminating in acute thrombosis, vascular occlusion, and severe ischemic events such as myocardial infarction or stroke [7, 8]. Consequently, the development of effective strategies to stabilize or regress atherosclerotic lesions represents a critical clinical objective.

Atherosclerosis is a multifactorial disorder driven by the interplay between cholesterol accumulation and chronic inflammation. The process begins with endothelial dysfunction: elevated low-density lipoprotein (LDL), hypertension, hyperglycemia, or mechanical stress compromise the endothelial barrier, allowing LDL to penetrate into the intima and undergo oxidative modification to form oxidized LDL (ox-LDL). ox-LDL activates endothelial cells to express adhesion molecules (e.g., VCAM-1, ICAM-1), facilitating monocyte adhesion, transmigration, and differentiation into macrophages. These macrophages engulf ox-LDL via scavenger receptors and gradually transform into foam cells, leading to intracellular cholesterol overload and metabolic stress [9, 10]. Concurrently, ox-LDL stimulates activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome and NF-κB pathway, resulting in the release of inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β and TNF-α. This cholesterol-driven inflammatory cascade amplifies local inflammation and promotes necrotic core formation and fibrous cap thinning, markedly increasing plaque vulnerability [11, 12]. Thus, simultaneous regulation of cholesterol metabolism and inflammation has emerged as a key therapeutic strategy for atherosclerosis.

Current clinical management of atherosclerosis mainly relies on lipid-lowering agents (e.g., statins, ezetimibe, PCSK9 inhibitors) and anti-inflammatory drugs (e.g., rapamycin and colchicine) to mitigate disease progression [13]. However, these drugs are typically readily metabolized or cleared before reaching the lesion site, resulting in limited local efficacy and potential off-target toxicity [14]. Among them, rapamycin is particularly notable for its dual lipid-lowering and anti-inflammatory properties. By inhibiting mTORC1 signaling, rapamycin suppresses macrophage-driven inflammation and foam-cell formation while restraining aberrant vascular smooth-muscle proliferation, thereby exerting potent anti-atherosclerotic effects. Nevertheless, its pronounced hydrophobicity, poor in vivo bioavailability, and dose-dependent immunosuppression severely limit clinical translation [15, 16]. Interventional approaches such as angioplasty or stent implantation can temporarily restore blood flow but fail to durably stabilize plaques or reverse inflammation, while also introducing risks of restenosis and vascular injury [17]. Therefore, achieving precise, targeted, and long-term modulation of atherosclerotic plaques remains a major challenge in current cardiovascular therapy.

Nanomedicine delivery systems have emerged as promising platforms for atherosclerosis therapy, owning to their tunable physicochemical properties and capacity for controlled, targeted drug delivery. Various nanocarriers, including polymeric nanoparticles, liposomes, metal-organic frameworks, micelles, and inorganic nanoparticles, have been explored for atherosclerosis treatment [18]. However, synthetic nanoparticles are prone to opsonization and rapid clearance by the mononuclear phagocyte system, which restricts their plaque accumulation and therapeutic window. To overcome these barriers, biomimetic cell membrane coating technology has been developed. By cloaking nanoparticles with natural membranes derived from red blood cells, platelets, or macrophages, these systems inherit the parent cells’ surface adhesion molecules, immune-regulatory receptors, and “self-recognition” markers, thus enabling immune evasion and lesion targeting [19, 20]. For instance, red blood cell membranes prolong circulation, platelet membranes facilitate adhesion to injured vasculature, and macrophage membranes promote homing to inflamed plaques [21, 22].

Despite these advantages, traditional biomimetic systems are often hampered by suboptimal targeting efficiency, limited homologous uptake, and membrane instability [23]. Membrane cholesterol content plays a critical role in governing membrane fluidity, receptor conformation, and immune recognition [24, 25]. Excessive cholesterol promotes lipid raft formation, complement deposition, and serum protein adsorption, accelerating clearance in vivo [26]. Emerging evidence indicates that lowering membrane cholesterol extends circulation and enhances targeting of membrane-cloaked nanomaterials. For example, cholesterol-depleted, PD-1–overexpressing T-cell membranes have enabled efficient co-delivery of photothermal agents and STING agonists to improve tumor control. In addition, shear-dependent signaling has been shown to be modulated by membrane cholesterol, and cholesterol reduction increases the shear adaptability and delivery performance of live macrophage carriers. Moreover, partial cholesterol removal increases membrane permeability and favors membrane-associated functions. Collectively, these observations identify membrane-cholesterol modulation as a practical lever to improve biomimetic delivery [27–29].

β-cyclodextrin (β-CD), widely used as biocompatible solubilizers in the clinic and in drug-delivery systems, can extract cholesterol from cell membranes in a concentration-dependent manner via host–guest complexation [30]. Building upon these insights, we developed a cholesterol-tunable macrophage membrane–camouflaged nanoplatform. As shown in Scheme 1, the system comprises a biodegradable PLGA core loaded with rapamycin and cloaked with cholesterol-depleted macrophage membranes, which preserve the native “self-recognition” and inflammation-homing functionalities of the source cells. First, β-CD was employed to selectively extract cholesterol in a controlled manner, generating a series of macrophage membrane-coated nanoparticles with graded cholesterol levels. These nanostructures were then systematically characterized for their hydrodynamic size, drug-loading capacity, and membrane protein integrity. Subsequently, in vitro assays were conducted to evaluate hemocompatibility, targeting efficiency, and cholesterol-handling ability, followed by in vivo studies to quantitatively assess circulation lifetime, plaque targeting, and therapeutic efficacy. These investigations firmly establish membrane cholesterol modulation as a powerful and effective strategy to enhance the targeting and therapeutic performance of biomimetic nanomedicines for atherosclerosis treatment.

Methods

Materials

Rapamycin was purchased from TargetMol (Shanghai, China). Dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) was obtained from China National Pharmaceutical Group Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. Poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA), 1,1’-Dioctadecyl-3,3,3’,3’-Tetramethylindodicarbocyanine Perchlorate (DiD), hydroxypropyl β-cyclodextrin (HP-β-CD), Oil Red O, DAPI, SDS-PAGE buffer, BCA protein assay kit, horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG, and goat anti-mouse IgG were all sourced from Beyotime Biotechnology (Shanghai, China). RIPA lysis buffer was supplied by Yeasen Biotechnology (Shanghai, China). Lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid disodium salt (EDTA-2 K) were obtained from Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). Oxidized low-density lipoprotein (ox-LDL) was purchased from Yiyuan Biotechnology (Guangzhou, China). Fetal bovine serum (FBS) was obtained from SenBeiJia Biological Technology Co., Ltd. ELISA kits for TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-10 were purchased from Xinboshen Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shenzhen, China). Antibodies against CD47 and integrin β1 were sourced from Abways (Shanghai, China), and the anti-integrin α4 polyclonal antibody was obtained from Biodragon (Beijing, China). Anti-VCAM-1 antibody was purchased from Abcam (Cambridge, UK).

Animals model

Male ApoE-/- mice (3–8 weeks old) and C57BL/6 mice (3–8 weeks old) were purchased from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. All animals were housed in a specific pathogen-free (SPF) facility under standardized conditions, including a 12-hour light/dark cycle, controlled temperature (22 °C), and relative humidity (40–70%). To establish the atherosclerosis model, ApoE-/- mice were fed a high-fat diet containing 15% fat and 1.25% cholesterol for a duration of 10 weeks.

Ethics statement

All animal experiments were conducted in compliance with the institutional guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals and were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Soochow University (Approval No. 202209A0828).

Cell line

The murine macrophage cell line RAW264.7 was obtained from Wuhan Pronex Life Science Co., Ltd. Cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. All cultures were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂.

Preparation of nano-rapamycin

Nano-Rapamycin were prepared using a nanoprecipitation method. Briefly, 10 mg of PLGA was dissolved in 1 mL of DMSO and sonicated until fully solubilized, forming the organic solvent phase. Subsequently, 1 mg of rapamycin was added to this solution and sonicated again to ensure complete dissolution, resulting in a homogeneous polymer–drug mixture. Meanwhile, 6 mL of ultrapure water was prepared as the antisolvent phase. The organic phase was added dropwise into the aqueous phase under continuous stirring. The resulting suspension was centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 3 min, and the supernatant was collected and subjected to dialysis against ultrapure water for 24 h to remove residual DMSO and unencapsulated drug, yielding the final Nano-rapamycin. DiD-labeled nanoparticles were prepared using the same procedure, with DiD addition as the encapsulated agent.

Extraction of cholesterol-depleted macrophage membrane

Macrophage membranes with different cholesterol levels were extracted from RAW264.7 cells following treatment with varying concentrations of β-CD. Briefly, cells were incubated with β-CD at final concentrations of 0, 5, or 20 mM for 30 min at 37 °C. After treatment, cells were harvested by centrifugation, and the resulting pellets were lysed in pre-cooled hypotonic Tris buffer (pH 7.4) at 4 °C for 1 h. The lysates were then subjected to ultrasonic disruption on ice for 10 min (pulse cycle: 2 s on / 3 s off, 20% amplitude). Following lysis, cellular debris was removed by centrifugation at 10,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was subsequently ultracentrifuged at 100,000 × g for 1 h at 4 °C to isolate the membrane fraction. The pellet containing cell membranes was resuspended in PBS supplemented with EDTA-free protease inhibitor and gently pipetted to obtain a uniform membrane suspension.

Preparation of macrophage membrane-coated nano-rapamycin

To prepare the macrophage membrane–coated nanoparticles, the previously isolated cell membranes were first sonicated on ice to ensure uniform dispersion in PBS. Subsequently, preformed Nano-Rapamycin were added to the membrane suspension, and the mixture was sonicated on ice for 2 min (100 Hz, 100 W) to promote initial membrane–nanoparticle interaction. The resulting mixture was then extruded through a 200 nm polycarbonate membrane using a mini-extruder, performing 10 extrusion cycles to achieve complete membrane coating of the nanoparticles. This process facilitates the formation of a core–shell structure, with the macrophage membrane forming a biomimetic shell around the nanoparticle core.

Characterizations

The morphological features of the nanoparticles were examined using transmission electron microscopy (TEM, Talos F200S G2) operated at an acceleration voltage of 200 kV. The hydrodynamic diameter and zeta potential of the nanoparticles were determined using dynamic light scattering (DLS) on a Zetasizer Nano ZS90 (Malvern Instruments, UK). The absorbance spectra were recorded using a UV–visible spectrophotometer (PerkinElmer, USA).

Drug loading efficiency and release profile

A standard calibration curve for rapamycin was constructed by measuring the absorbance of serially diluted rapamycin solutions at 280 nm using a UV–visible spectrophotometer. To determine drug loading and encapsulation efficiency, lyophilized nano-rapamycin were dissolved in DMSO, and the rapamycin content was quantified based on the calibration curve. The in vitro release profile of rapamycin from nanoparticles was evaluated using a dialysis method. Briefly, nanoparticle suspensions were sealed in dialysis bags and immersed in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4) at 37 °C under gentle agitation. At predetermined time points, aliquots of the release medium were withdrawn and replaced with fresh PBS. The rapamycin concentration in the samples was measured using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) to determine cumulative release over time.

Hemolysis assay

Fresh mouse blood was collected and washed three times with physiological saline to remove plasma components. The isolated erythrocytes were then resuspended and incubated with various concentrations of nanoparticle. Ultrapure water served as the positive control, and physiological saline as the negative control. After incubation at 37 °C for 1 h, the samples were centrifuged at 1500 rpm for 3 min to pellet the red blood cells. The absorbance of the supernatant was measured at 545 nm using a microplate reader to quantify hemoglobin release. The hemolysis rate was calculated using the following equation: Hemolysis rate (%) = (treatment group − negative control) / (positive control − negative control) × 100%.

CCK-8 assay

To evaluate the cytotoxicity of the nanoparticle formulations, RAW 264.7 macrophages (0.8 × 104 cells/well) and HUVECs (0.5 × 104 cells/well) were seeded into 96-well plates and incubated overnight at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. The next day, cells were treated with PBS (control) or macrophage membrane-coated nano-rapamycin (each at an equivalent rapamycin concentration of 10 µg/mL). After 24 h of incubation, cell viability was assessed using a Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

In vitro cell uptake assay by endothelial cells and macrophages

To assess nanoparticle uptake by endothelial cells, HUVECs were seeded into confocal dishes at a density of 1 × 105 cells per dish and cultured overnight for adherence. The next day, cells were activated with LPS (100 ng/mL) for 24 h to simulate an inflamed endothelium. After activation, the cells were incubated with DiD labeled macrophage membrane-coated nano-rapamycin for 6 h. Following treatment, cells were gently washed with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, and stained with DAPI for nuclear visualization. Cellular uptake of the fluorescently labeled nanoparticles was observed using confocal laser scanning microscopy. For the antibody-blocking assay, HUVECs were first stimulated to establish an inflamed endothelial model and then incubated with an anti-VCAM-1 antibody (300 µg/mL) for 1 h. The cells were subsequently incubated with DiD-labelled nanoparticles, followed by washing, fixation and DAPI staining. Cellular uptake was then visualized by confocal microscopy and quantified accordingly. For macrophage uptake analysis, RAW264.7 cells were similarly seeded in confocal dishes and allowed to adhere overnight. Cells were then activated with LPS (100 ng/mL) and subsequently incubated with DiD labeled macrophage membrane-coated nano-rapamycin for 1, 2, or 4 h, respectively. After each incubation time point, the cells were washed, fixed, and stained with DAPI. Nanoparticle internalization was visualized using confocal microscopy, and the degree of uptake was qualitatively compared across groups and time points.

In vitro assessment of cholesterol efflux and anti-inflammatory

RAW264.7 macrophages were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 104 cells per well and cultured for 12 h to allow cell adherence. To induce foam cell formation, cells were then stimulated with oxidized low-density lipoprotein (oxLDL, 50 ng/mL) and LPS (100 ng/mL) for 24 h. After foam cell induction, cells were treated with macrophage membrane-coated nano-rapamycin at a final rapamycin concentration of 10 µg/mL for an additional 24 h. To evaluate cholesterol efflux, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with Oil Red O (ORO) for 30 min to visualize intracellular lipid accumulation. The morphology and lipid droplet content of foam cells were examined under a fluorescence inverted microscope. For quantitative analysis, cells were incubated with isopropanol for 1 h to extract the ORO dye, and absorbance was measured at 550 nm using a microplate reader. Additionally, intracellular total cholesterol levels were measured using a commercial cholesterol assay kit, following the manufacturer’s instructions. For anti-inflammatory assessment, culture supernatants were collected following treatment, and levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-6) and the anti-inflammatory cytokine (IL-10) were quantified using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), according to the kit protocols.

In vivo circulation and targeting evaluation

To evaluate systemic circulation time, male C57BL/6 mice were intravenously injected with various DiD-labeled nanoparticle formulations via the tail vein. At predetermined time points (0, 0.25, 0.5, 1, 6, 12, and 24 h post-injection), 50 µL of blood was collected from the retro-orbital venous plexus into anticoagulant-containing tubes. Fluorescence intensity of the collected samples was measured immediately using an in vivo imaging system (IVIS) to assess nanoparticle retention in circulation. For in vivo targeting assessment, atherosclerotic ApoE⁻/⁻ mice were randomly divided into four groups and intravenously administered PBS, NCNR, MCNR, or LCNR, respectively. After 24 h, the mice were euthanized following PBS perfusion to remove residual blood. The entire aorta (from the aortic root to the iliac bifurcation) was carefully dissected and subjected to ex vivo fluorescence imaging using the IVIS system. Fluorescence intensity was quantitatively analyzed to evaluate nanoparticle accumulation in atherosclerotic plaques.

Treatment of atherosclerotic mice with cholesterol-depleted macrophage membrane-coated nano-rapamycin

Atherosclerotic ApoE⁻/⁻ mice were randomly assigned to four treatment groups (n = 3 per group): PBS, NCNR, MCNR, and LCNR. An additional group of C57BL/6 mice receiving saline served as the normal control. Each formulation was administered via intravenous tail vein injection every three days over a period of 30 days, during which all ApoE⁻/⁻ mice continued on a high-fat diet to sustain atherosclerotic progression. At the end of the treatment period, mice were euthanized, and the entire aorta (from the aortic root to the iliac bifurcation) was excised. For macroscopic plaque analysis, the aortas were subjected to ORO staining to visualize and quantify lipid-rich lesions. For histological evaluation, the harvested aortas were thoroughly washed with PBS, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, and sectioned. Cross-sections of the aortic root were stained with hematoxylin and eosin to assess plaque morphology, ORO to detect lipid accumulation, and Masson’s trichrome to evaluate collagen content and plaque stability.

In vivo biosafety evaluation

At the end of the treatment period, major organs including the heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney were collected from all mice for histopathological analysis via H&E staining to assess potential tissue damage or inflammatory responses. In addition, whole blood samples were collected in EDTA-containing anticoagulant tubes for routine hematological analysis, including red blood cells (RBC), white blood cells (WBC), platelets (PLT), lymphocytes (LYMPH), and hemoglobin (HGB) levels. Serum samples were also isolated to evaluate hepatic and renal function, including measurements of alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), and creatinine (CREA).

Statistical analysis

All quantitative data were obtained from three or more independent replicates, and results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (s.d.). Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism. For comparisons between two groups, an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test was used. For comparisons among multiple groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post-hoc multiple comparison test was applied. For in vivo therapeutic efficacy studies, n refers to the number of biologically independent animals. Technical measurements from multiple tissue sections, microscopic fields, or repeated measurements from the same animal were averaged to obtain a single animal-level value before statistical analysis and were not treated as independent biological replicates. Statistical significance was indicated as follows: *p < 0.05; **p < 0.01; ***p < 0.001; ****p < 0.0001; ns: no significance.

Results and discussion

Preparation and characterization of cholesterol-depleted macrophage membranes

To regulate the cholesterol content in macrophage membranes, β-CD was employed as a cholesterol-scavenging agent based on its host–guest inclusion complex with cholesterol molecules (Fig. 1a). Macrophages were treated with increasing concentrations of β-CD (0, 1, 5, 10, and 20 mM), enabling the concentration-dependent extraction of cholesterol from the cellular membrane. As shown in Fig. 1b, cell viability gradually decreased as the β-CD concentration increased. The average viability of macrophages after treatment with 1, 5, 10, and 20 mM β-CD was 87.6%, 90.3%, 63.3%, and 44.9%, respectively, indicating that high concentrations of β-CD, particularly above 5 mM, induced mild cytotoxicity. To assess the efficiency of cholesterol depletion, we quantified membrane cholesterol using a commercial assay kit, normalizing the measurements to total membrane protein content. Compared with untreated membranes, exposure to 5, 10, and 20 mM β-CD resulted in 58.1%, 35.9%, and 20.3% of the initial cholesterol levels, respectively (Fig. 1c), confirming a concentration-dependent cholesterol reduction by β-CD. Based on these findings, we selected three representative membrane types for subsequent use, corresponding to distinct levels of membrane cholesterol: untreated membranes (designated as Nchol-MM), membranes treated with 5 mM β-CD (Mchol-MM), and membranes treated with 20 mM β-CD (Lchol-MM).

Following cholesterol adjustment, the membranes were subjected to ultrasonication in an ice bath followed by gradient centrifugation to ensure efficient isolation and purification. The resulting membrane fractions were subsequently examined using transmission electron microscopy (TEM). As shown in Fig. 1d, the membranes predominantly appeared as irregular sheet-like or vesicular structures with well-defined boundaries, indicating good structural preservation after isolation. The presence of curved membrane fragments further reflected the inherent flexibility of lipid bilayers, supporting the successful extraction of biological membranes.

To further assess the compositional integrity of the extracted membranes, both total protein content and characteristic membrane proteins were analyzed. As shown in Fig. 1e and Fig. S1, total membrane protein levels remained largely unaffected by β-CD treatment, suggesting minimal protein loss during the cholesterol depletion process. Notably, two key functional membrane proteins were evaluated: integrin α4β1, an adhesion molecule that binds specifically to VCAM-1 on inflamed endothelial cells, and CD47, an anti-phagocytic “don’t-eat-me” signal that interacts with SIRP-α on immune cells to mediate immune evasion. Western blot analysis confirmed that both integrin α4β1 and CD47 were retained in the membrane fractions following β-CD treatment (Fig. 1f; Fig. S2). These results indicate that although β-CD effectively removes membrane cholesterol, it does not disrupt essential membrane-associated proteins critical for targeting and immune-evasive functionalities. Collectively, the data confirm that macrophage membranes with tunable lipid content can be successfully obtained without compromising their biological integrity, laying a solid foundation for their subsequent application in nanoparticle surface functionalization.

Fabrication and characterization of slimming membrane–coated nano-rapamycin

As illustrated in Fig. 1g, nano-rapamycin (NR) was first synthesized using a nanoprecipitation method by encapsulating rapamycin into PLGA-based nanospheres. The resulting core nanoparticles were subsequently coated with macrophage membranes of varying cholesterol content via a liposome extrusion technique to yield three distinct formulations: NCNR (Nchol-MM coated NR), MCNR (Mchol-MM coated NR), and LCNR (Lchol-MM coated NR), respectively. Before selecting the final formulation, membrane mass ratios and extrusion passes were compared based on DLS size-distribution profiles (Fig. S3). Among the tested conditions, a membrane mass ratio of 1:1 and 10 extrusion passes produced a relatively narrow and uniform size distribution; therefore, this condition was used for the final preparation. TEM images (Fig. S4) showed that the nanoparticles before and after membrane coating exhibited well-defined spherical or near-spherical morphologies with good dispersity. The substantial increase in particle size following membrane coating supports successful surface functionalization. HAADF-STEM elemental mapping further revealed the presence of C, O, N, and P signals in the coated nanoparticles, with P serving as supportive indicators of membrane-associated proteins and phospholipids (Fig. S5).

To further validate the composition of the nanocarriers, UV–vis absorption spectra were recorded (Fig. 1h). PLGA exhibited a characteristic absorption peak at approximately 207 nm, while pure rapamycin showed a distinct peak at 280 nm. The NR nanoparticles displayed both peaks, confirming rapamycin loading into the PLGA core. Following membrane coating, the spectra of NCNR, MCNR, and LCNR retained both absorption features, indicating that the membrane-coating process did not disrupt the structural integrity or composition of the NR core.

Dynamic light scattering (DLS) analyses were subsequently conducted to assess the hydrodynamic size of the nanoparticles (Fig. 1i). The measured hydrodynamic diameters of uncoated NR and membrane-coated variants (NCNR, MCNR, and LCNR) were 90.9 nm, 107.7 nm, 118.3 nm, and 104.2 nm, respectively. These results further confirmed that membrane coating increased particle size in all cases, consistent with TEM observations. In contrast, the isolated membrane vesicles showed relatively larger hydrodynamic diameters (Fig. S6a), which may be attributed to the formation of swollen vesicles or membrane aggregates in aqueous solution. After coating onto the nanoparticle cores, the membranes reorganized under the templating effect of the solid cores into a more compact and uniform core-shell structure, leading to a smaller hydrodynamic diameter than that of the free membrane vesicles. Zeta potential measurements (Fig. 1j) showed values of -23.6 mV for NR, -12.2 mV for NCNR, -18.7 mV for MCNR, and − 24.9 mV for LCNR. The relatively negative potential of uncoated NR is mainly attributed to the presence of carboxyl and other anionic groups on the PLGA surface. As shown in Fig. S6b, the isolated macrophage membranes were also negatively charged, but their zeta potentials were less negative than that of NR. Therefore, after membrane coating, the outer membrane layer partially masked the highly negative charge of the PLGA core, resulting in a less negative zeta potential for the membrane-coated nanoparticles [31, 32]. In addition, with decreasing membrane cholesterol content after β-CD treatment, the zeta potential of the membranes became progressively more negative (Fig. S6b). Consistently, the corresponding membrane-coated nanoparticles also exhibited increasingly negative zeta potentials, with LCNR showing the most negative value. These results suggest that cholesterol depletion affects the interfacial charge presentation of the membrane, thereby influencing the overall surface potential of the coated nanoparticles [33–36]. Colloidal stability was evaluated by monitoring the hydrodynamic sizes of all formulations over seven days. As shown in Fig. S7, NR, NCNR, MCNR, and LCNR maintained consistent particle sizes with negligible variation, indicating excellent storage stability in aqueous conditions.

Prior to conducting drug release studies, the drug loading content and encapsulation efficiency of the NR formulation were determined to be approximately 2.1% and 20.3%, respectively (Table S1). In vitro release profiles were then assessed in PBS (pH 7.4, 0.01 M) at 37 °C (Fig. S8). All tested formulations, including NR, NCNR, MCNR, and LCNR, exhibited sustained rapamycin release behavior. Within the first 24 h, cumulative release percentages reached 23.0%, 19.7%, 21.3%, and 21.9%, respectively, while by 72 h, the values increased to 38.4%, 33.3%, 34.8%, and 35.9%, respectively. These results demonstrate that membrane coating moderately slowed rapamycin release compared to the uncoated formulation, suggesting a diffusion-barrier effect imparted by the macrophage membrane shell. This outer coating likely hinders direct drug efflux, thereby contributing to a prolonged release profile. Notably, despite differences in membrane cholesterol content, the NCNR, MCNR, and LCNR groups exhibited highly comparable release kinetics. This suggests that moderate lipid depletion did not significantly alter membrane permeability or interfere with the sustained-release behavior, findings consistent with prior reports on cell membrane–camouflaged nanocarriers [37].

Macrophage targeting of slimming membrane–coated nano-rapamycin

Prior to evaluating cellular targeting performance, the biosafety profile of the membrane-coated nanoparticles was first assessed. NCNR, MCNR, and LCNR were co-incubated with human endothelial cells and macrophages for 24 h, and cell viability was subsequently measured using a standard CCK-8 assay. As shown in Fig. S9, none of the formulations exhibited significant cytotoxicity toward either cell type, confirming their good biocompatibility at the tested concentrations.

Given the inevitable interaction between intravenously administered nanomedicines and blood components, the hemocompatibility of the formulations was further evaluated using a hemolysis assay. As shown in Fig. 2a, incubation with anticoagulated whole blood followed by centrifugation yielded clear supernatants in all nanomedicine-treated groups, similar to the negative control, while the positive control group showed visible red coloration indicative of hemolysis. Quantitative analysis of supernatant absorbance at 545 nm (Fig. 2b) revealed no significant differences between the treatment groups and the negative control. The calculated hemolysis rates for NCNR, MCNR, and LCNR were 1.2%, 1.0%, and 1.5%, respectively (Table S2), all well below the national safety threshold of 5%, indicating excellent red blood cell compatibility [38]. These results confirm that the slimming membrane-coated nanoparticles possess favorable biosafety profiles suitable for systemic administration.

Atherosclerotic plaques consist primarily of dysfunctional endothelial cells and infiltrating macrophages. A key focus of this study was to evaluate whether the membrane-coated nanomedicines could selectively accumulate at plaque sites following systemic administration. To enable fluorescence tracking, the nanoparticles were labeled with the near-infrared dye DiD. To simulate inflamed endothelium in vitro, endothelial cells were pre-treated with lipopolysaccharide (LPS). As shown in Fig. 2c–d, there was no significant difference in the uptake of NCNR, MCNR, and LCNR by non-activated endothelial cells (p > 0.05), suggesting low basal cellular interaction and minimal off-target uptake. However, upon LPS activation, cellular uptake increased significantly in a cholesterol-dependent manner, with LCNR > MCNR > NCNR (p < 0.001), indicating that reduced membrane cholesterol enhances binding or internalization by inflamed endothelial cells. Furthermore, antibody-blocking experiments using Anti-VCAM-1 (Fig. S10) showed that nanoparticle uptake by LPS-activated endothelial cells was markedly reduced after VCAM-1 blockade, further confirming that the enhanced cellular association was mediated, at least in part, by the specific interaction between the membrane-coated nanoparticles and VCAM-1 overexpressed on inflamed endothelial cells.

To further evaluate uptake by immune cells, macrophages were also activated using LPS to induce a pro-inflammatory phenotype. Following co-incubation with DiD-labeled nanomedicines for 1 to 4 h, cellular uptake was assessed. As shown in Fig. 2e–f, uptake by non-activated macrophages remained low across all formulations over time (p > 0.05), indicating that the membrane coatings confer resistance to nonspecific phagocytosis, which may contribute to prolonged circulation. In contrast, LPS-stimulated macrophages exhibited a significant time-dependent increase in uptake. After 4 h of incubation, internalization was significantly enhanced for all formulations, with LCNR showing the highest uptake, followed by MCNR and NCNR (4 h uptake: LCNR > MCNR > NCNR, p < 0.01). Compared to 1-hour values, a 3–5 fold increase was observed at 4 h.

These results underscore a crucial cholesterol-dependent mechanism underlying the uptake of biomimetic nanomedicines by inflammatory macrophages. Upon LPS stimulation, macrophages exhibited a marked increase in nanoparticle internalization, with uptake levels correlating inversely with membrane cholesterol content. This observation suggests that reducing the cholesterol density of the membrane shell facilitates recognition and phagocytosis by activated immune cells. In the pathological microenvironment of atherosclerosis, inflammatory macrophages are highly enriched within plaques and contribute significantly to plaque development and instability. The ability of “slimming” membrane-coated nanoparticles, particularly LCNR, to exploit this inflammatory niche by promoting preferential uptake represents a strategic advantage for plaque-targeted therapy. By harnessing the innate phagocytic behavior of activated macrophages, LCNR can be efficiently trafficked into lesion sites, delivering rapamycin directly to areas of active inflammation. This targeted delivery mechanism holds promise for enhancing therapeutic efficacy while minimizing off-target effects, thereby advancing the clinical potential of biomimetic nanoplatforms for atherosclerosis treatment.

Cholesterol efflux and anti-inflammatory activity of slimming membrane–coated nano-rapamycin

After confirming that the membrane-coated nanomedicines preferentially accumulate at inflamed atherosclerotic plaques, we next investigated their therapeutic functionalities, specifically their ability to promote cholesterol efflux and suppress inflammation in an oxidized low-density lipoprotein (ox-LDL)-induced foam cell model (Fig. 3a). Lipid accumulation was first assessed using Oil Red O staining. As shown in Fig. 3b, all treatment groups (NCNR, MCNR, and LCNR) exhibited substantial reductions in lipid droplet accumulation, with the extent of clearance inversely correlated with the cholesterol content of the membrane coating. Notably, the LCNR-treated group demonstrated the fewest Oil Red O–positive droplets, indicating the greatest reduction in intracellular neutral lipid storage. This trend was confirmed by absorbance quantification (Fig. 3c), showing a progressive decrease in intracellular lipid burden across the NCNR, MCNR, and LCNR groups. This finding may indirectly indicate enhanced cholesterol efflux, as increased cholesterol export reduces the intracellular free cholesterol available for esterification and subsequent storage in lipid droplets. To further validate these findings, total intracellular cholesterol levels were quantified (Fig. 3d). Foam cells treated with NCNR, MCNR, and LCNR showed cholesterol contents of 184.8, 160.0, and 142.1 mol/g, respectively, indicating that decreasing the membrane cholesterol content of the nanoparticles significantly enhances their cholesterol-clearing capacity.

The anti-inflammatory properties of the formulations were subsequently examined by measuring the secretion of key inflammatory cytokines. As shown in Fig. 3e–g, secretion levels of the pro-inflammatory cytokines TNF-α and IL-6 decreased in a cholesterol-dependent manner. Specifically, TNF-α secretion declined from 1.56 pg/mL in the NCNR group to 1.06 pg/mL in the LCNR group, while IL-6 levels decreased from 2.72 to 1.79 pg/mL, corresponding to reductions of 32.1% and 34.2%, respectively. In contrast, the secretion of the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 remained statistically unchanged across all treatment groups (0.09–0.11 pg/mL, p > 0.05), suggesting that the anti-inflammatory effects of the nanoparticles were primarily mediated through suppression of pro-inflammatory signaling pathways, rather than direct stimulation of anti-inflammatory cytokine expression.

These findings, together with prior observations that inflammatory macrophages exhibit enhanced uptake of low-cholesterol membrane–coated nanomedicines, highlight a synergistic relationship between cellular internalization and therapeutic efficacy. Given that foam cells arise from inflammatory macrophages through the phagocytosis of ox-LDL, the superior cholesterol efflux observed in the LCNR group may result from both improved nanoparticle–cell interactions and the intracellular actions of rapamycin. One plausible explanation is that cholesterol-depleted membrane coatings confer increased structural flexibility and membrane affinity, which facilitates receptor-mediated binding and endocytosis by foam cells. Once internalized, rapamycin can modulate intracellular pathways that regulate lipid homeostasis. Specifically, rapamycin has been reported to upregulate ATP-binding cassette transporter A1 (ABCA1), a key protein in the reverse cholesterol transport pathway responsible for exporting intracellular cholesterol to extracellular acceptors [39]. To further verify whether the observed reduction in lipid accumulation and intracellular cholesterol was associated with enhanced cholesterol efflux, we evaluated ABCA1 expression. As shown in Fig. S11, Western blot analysis and corresponding quantitative results demonstrate a significant upregulation of ABCA1 in foam cells following rapamycin treatment. These findings provide direct experimental evidence supporting the involvement of ABCA1-mediated cholesterol efflux in our system. This mechanistic pathway may account for the progressive cholesterol reduction in intracellular cholesterol observed across formulations with decreasing membrane cholesterol levels.

In addition to enhancing cholesterol efflux, the membrane-coated nanoparticles also reduced the secretion of inflammatory cytokines, as evidenced by ELISA results. This suggests a mechanistic link between lipid metabolism and inflammatory regulation. During atherosclerosis progression, excessive intracellular cholesterol in foam cells can induce lipid toxicity and activate inflammatory responses, leading to increased expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-6. By lowering intracellular cholesterol levels, the slimming membrane–coated nanoparticles may alleviate lipid stress and suppress inflammatory signaling. For instance, cholesterol depletion may impair activation of the nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) pathway, a key transcriptional regulator of inflammation [40]. Attenuation of NF-κB signaling could therefore underlie the observed reduction in TNF-α and IL-6 levels, further supporting the enhanced anti-inflammatory efficacy of the LCNR formulation.

In vivo therapeutic efficacy of slimming membrane–coated nano-rapamycin

The systemic circulation time of nanomedicines plays a pivotal role in determining their therapeutic efficacy. Prolonged blood circulation enhances drug bioavailability by increasing the likelihood of interactions with target tissues or cells. To assess whether membrane cholesterol content influences in vivo pharmacokinetics, male C57BL/6 mice were randomly divided into four groups: PBS, NCNR, MCNR, and LCNR. DiD-labeled nanoparticles or PBS were intravenously injected via the tail vein, and blood samples were collected at designated time points to quantify circulating fluorescence. As shown in Fig. S12a–b, all nanoparticle-treated groups exhibited a rapid decline in fluorescence intensity within the first hour post-injection, indicative of an initial distribution phase. From 1 to 24 h, the rate of decline slowed, and a cholesterol-dependent trend in fluorescence retention emerged, with LCNR showing the highest residual fluorescence, followed by MCNR and NCNR. These differences in circulation time may be attributed to variations in the immune evasion capabilities of the nanoparticle coatings. Prior studies have shown that CD47, a membrane-bound “don’t-eat-me” signal, can bind to SIRPα receptors on mononuclear phagocytes, thereby inhibiting nanoparticle clearance by the liver and spleen. Although western blot analysis revealed a reduced CD47 level in LCNR after more extensive cholesterol depletion (Fig. 1f), the immune-evasion capacity of biomimetic nanoparticles is not determined solely by the total abundance of CD47, but also by its membrane accessibility and functional presentation on the nanoparticle surface. It is postulated that reducing membrane cholesterol enhances membrane fluidity, promoting the lateral mobility and clustering of CD47 molecules, which may increase their binding affinity for SIRPα and amplify immune evasion signaling [41]. Therefore, despite a lower overall CD47 level, LCNR may still exhibit prolonged systemic circulation. In contrast, cholesterol-rich membranes, such as those on NCNR, may restrict the lateral mobility of CD47 within the lipid bilayer, thereby attenuating its immune-inhibitory signaling and resulting in a shorter systemic circulation time compared to cholesterol-depleted counterparts.

To further evaluate the plaque-targeting capacity of the nanoparticles, an atherosclerosis model was established in ApoE−/− mice maintained on a high-fat diet for 10 weeks. Nanoparticles with equivalent fluorescence intensities were intravenously injected, and the entire aorta, from the aortic root to the iliac bifurcation, was harvested 24 h post-injection for ex vivo fluorescence imaging. As shown in Fig. S12c, minimal fluorescence was detected in the PBS group, consistent with background autofluorescence. In contrast, the NCNR, MCNR, and LCNR groups displayed progressively stronger fluorescence signals, particularly localized to the aortic arch and abdominal aorta that commonly associated with plaque formation. Quantitative analysis (Fig. S12d) revealed an inverse correlation between membrane cholesterol content and fluorescence intensity, indicating that cholesterol-depleted membranes significantly enhance the plaque-targeting efficiency of the biomimetic nanoparticles. These enhanced targeting effects likely result from multiple complementary mechanisms. As previously demonstrated, the macrophage-derived membranes used for nanoparticle coating retained critical functional proteins such as integrin α4β1, even after cholesterol depletion (Fig. 1f). Notably, western blot analysis showed a higher integrin α4β1 level after β-CD treatment. This integrin specifically binds to VCAM-1, a cell adhesion molecule that is highly expressed on inflamed endothelial cells within atherosclerotic plaques, thereby facilitating selective adhesion and uptake at lesion sites. Additionally, membrane cholesterol depletion has been demonstrated to enhance immune evasion, which suppresses phagocytic clearance by the mononuclear phagocyte system (Fig. S12a-b). This prolonged systemic circulation further increases the likelihood of nanoparticle accumulation at target vascular regions.

Encouraged by the favorable pharmacokinetic and targeting performance of the “slimming” membrane–coated nanoparticles, we next conducted a systematic evaluation of their therapeutic efficacy in vivo using an atherosclerotic mouse model. As illustrated in Fig. 4a, ApoE−/− mice with established atherosclerosis lesions were randomly assigned to four treatment groups: PBS, NCNR, MCNR, and LCNR. Mice continued to receive a high-fat diet throughout the four-week treatment period. At study completion, the entire aorta from the aortic root to the iliac bifurcation was harvested and subjected to Oil Red O (ORO) staining to assess lipid-rich plaque burden. As shown in Fig. 4b, compared to normal controls, PBS-treated atherosclerosis mice exhibited extensive ORO-positive staining along the aortic wall, indicative of significant lipid deposition. In contrast, treatment with NCNR, MCNR, or LCNR resulted in a marked reduction of ORO-positive areas. Notably, this reduction followed a cholesterol content–dependent trend, with the LCNR group exhibiting the smallest lesion areas. Quantitative analysis (Fig. S13) revealed that the average aortic plaque areas were 29.25% in PBS-treated mice, compared to 20.72%, 16.31%, and 11.45% in the NCNR, MCNR, and LCNR groups, respectively, highlighting the superior therapeutic efficacy of cholesterol-depleted membrane coatings in attenuating atherosclerosis progression. Similar patterns were observed in aortic root cryosections stained with ORO (Fig. 4c–d), further validating the macroscopic findings.

To gain deeper insight into plaque composition and vascular remodeling, histological examination of aortic root cross-sections was conducted using hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and Masson’s trichrome staining (Fig. 4e). The results consistently demonstrated that both plaque burden and collagen content declined in a membrane cholesterol–dependent manner, with the LCNR-treated group exhibiting the most significant improvement. Quantitative analysis (Fig. 4f–g) revealed that H&E-stained plaque areas accounted for 28.06%, 13.23%, 8.30%, and 5.87%, while Masson-positive collagen deposition areas were 11.13%, 20.90%, 30.09%, and 39.60% in the PBS, NCNR, MCNR, and LCNR groups, respectively. These findings indicate that beyond reducing lipid accumulation, the “slimming” membrane–coated nanomedicines also modulate plaque morphology and extracellular matrix remodeling, potentially contributing to improved plaque stability. Collectively, these results demonstrate that reducing membrane cholesterol content enhances the in vivo anti-atherosclerotic performance of macrophage membrane–coated nano-Rapamycin. The observed cholesterol-dependent therapeutic gradient, manifested across plaque size reduction, lipid clearance, and collagen remodeling, underscores the critical role of membrane lipid composition in the rational design of biomimetic nanotherapeutics for atherosclerosis treatment.

In vivo biosafety profile of slimming membrane–coated nano-rapamycin

To evaluate the biosafety of the “slimming” macrophage membrane–mimetic nanoplatform, a comprehensive in vivo assessment was performed. As shown in Fig. S14, no significant differences in body weight were observed among mice treated with PBS, NCNR, MCNR, or LCNR throughout the treatment period, suggesting minimal systemic toxicity. Upon completion of the four-week regimen, major organs including the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys were harvested for histological examination. H&E staining (Fig. S15) revealed no apparent pathological alterations or inflammatory lesions in any group, further supporting the biocompatibility of the nanomedicines.

In addition to histopathological analysis, standard hematological parameters (RBC, WBC, PLT, LYMPH, and HGB) were evaluated (Fig. S16). No statistically significant deviations were found between treated groups and PBS controls, and all measured values remained within physiological ranges. Similarly, hepatic (ALT, AST, ALP) and renal (BUN, CREA) function markers (Fig. S17) exhibited no signs of organ toxicity across all treatment groups, indicating favorable systemic tolerance.

To examine potential effects on lipid metabolism, serum lipid profiles were also measured (Fig. S18). Compared to healthy controls, all atherosclerosis model mice, including those in the PBS and treatment groups, displayed elevated levels of total cholesterol (TC) and low-density lipoprotein (LDL), while triglyceride (TG) and high-density lipoprotein (HDL) levels remained largely unchanged. Importantly, treatment with NCNR, MCNR, or LCNR did not significantly alter these systemic lipid parameters. These findings suggest that the therapeutic effects of the “slimming” nanomedicines are primarily localized within atherosclerotic plaques, rather than exerting systemic lipid-lowering effects. This localized mechanism of action is particularly advantageous for minimizing off-target effects and maintaining systemic homeostasis. However, given that persistently elevated blood lipid levels are a major risk factor for both the formation and recurrence of atherosclerotic plaques, the limited impact of these nanoparticles on systemic lipid metabolism also presents a challenge for long-term disease control. To address this, future strategies could consider the combination of “slimming” membrane–coated nanomedicines with conventional lipid-lowering agents, such as statins, ezetimibe, or PCSK9 inhibitors. Such a combinatorial approach may not only accelerate plaque regression through localized delivery of therapeutic agents but also reduce circulating lipid levels, thereby preventing the formation of new plaques and minimizing the risk of recurrence. This synergistic strategy has the potential to provide a more comprehensive and sustained therapeutic benefit in the clinical management of atherosclerosis.

However, the current study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the animal experiments were conducted exclusively in male mice, which precludes the evaluation of potential sex-dependent differences and may limit the generalizability of the findings. Second, the in vivo therapeutic efficacy experiments were performed with a limited number of biologically independent animals. Although consistent therapeutic trends were observed across multiple related endpoints, including plaque burden, lipid deposition, inflammatory responses, and histological observations, the use of three biologically independent animals per group limited the ability to robustly assess the normality and homogeneity of variance assumptions required for parametric one-way ANOVA. Future studies with larger animal cohorts are therefore warranted to further strengthen the statistical power and validate the in vivo therapeutic efficacy. Third, the Nano-Rapamycin formulation exhibits relatively low encapsulation efficiency and drug loading. Although these parameters were sufficient to achieve the desired therapeutic effects in this proof-of-concept study, further optimization is required for translational applications. Potential strategies include tuning the molecular weight of PLGA, optimizing the polymer-to-drug ratio, and refining formulation conditions to enhance drug loading and encapsulation efficiency.

Conclusions

In this study, we developed a cholesterol-modulated macrophage membrane–mimetic nanoplatform, termed the “slimming” nano-Rapamycin system, by using β-cyclodextrin to selectively deplete cholesterol from donor cell membranes. Cholesterol depletion significantly increased nanoparticle uptake by inflammatory macrophages in vitro, potentially through improved cellular interactions associated with enhanced membrane fluidity and preservation of key receptor–ligand interactions. Beyond targeting, the membrane-coated nanoparticles also promoted cholesterol efflux and suppressed inflammatory cytokine production in foam cells. In atherosclerotic mouse models, treatment with cholesterol-depleted nanoparticles significantly reduced plaque burden and lipid accumulation while increasing collagen content in a membrane cholesterol–dependent manner. These therapeutic effects were achieved while maintaining excellent biosafety, with no evident hepatic, renal, hematological, or histological toxicity. Importantly, although the platform effectively regulates cholesterol metabolism within plaques, it does not significantly alter systemic lipid levels. This highlights its potential for localized therapy and suggests that future strategies could combine such nanomedicines with systemic lipid-lowering agents to further improve outcomes and prevent recurrence. In summary, our findings demonstrate that modulating membrane cholesterol content is a powerful strategy to optimize the biological performance of biomimetic nanomedicines. By leveraging the pathological features of atherosclerosis and enhancing nanoparticle–cell interactions, the “slimming” membrane–coated nano-Rapamycin system represents a promising platform for targeted plaque therapy and potentially other inflammation-related cardiovascular diseases.
