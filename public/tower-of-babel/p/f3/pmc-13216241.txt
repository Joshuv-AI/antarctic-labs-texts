Advancing airborne eDNA sampling methods for monitoring diverse terrestrial vertebrate communities
Authors: Kasun H Bodawatta, Agnete le Maire Madsen, Luke E Holman, Jacob Agerbo Rasmussen, Matthew S Johnson, Tobias Guldberg Frøslev, Christina Lynggaard, Kristine Bohmann
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEffective biodiversity survey methods are crucial to monitor ecosystems threatened by climatic fluctuations and anthropogenic pressures. Here we advance methods for collecting a novel source of biodiversity data - airborne environmental DNA (eDNA), and investigate whether it yields habitat- and season-specific signatures of terrestrial vertebrate communities. Using custom-made, portable, and low-budget samplers, we sampled airborne eDNA in three protected nature areas across Denmark spanning different nature types and seasons. We show that coarse grade air filters, larger filter area, increased airflow rate, and dry storage of filters at -20 °C yield detections of higher numbers of vertebrate taxa with more consistent detections of communities across samples. Further, we find that detected vertebrate communities are characteristic of the sampled nature types and seasons. Collectively, these refinements enable effective monitoring of terrestrial vertebrate biodiversity using portable and low-budget air samplers.Subject terms: Biodiversity, Ecosystem ecology

Introduction

Global terrestrial ecosystems encompass an immense diversity of habitats nurturing biodiversity and providing critical and irreplaceable ecosystem services and functions1. Thus, in the ongoing biodiversity crisis there is a need for efficient, non-invasive, and scalable tools to survey and study biodiversity and inform terrestrial conservation efforts2–5. To this end, filtration of airborne particles has been explored as a source of environmental DNA (eDNA) from land-living organisms2,6–13. A recent surge of studies shows that vertebrates can be detected by collecting DNA-containing bioaerosols, but also that the low quantity of eDNA in such samples can result in stochastic species detections8. This can inflate the likelihood of false positive and false negative detections and increase the number of samples that need to be collected and analysed, which in turn increases the cost and effort of the method8. However, we hypothesise that advancing airborne eDNA sampling and storage will reduce the detection stochasticity and increase the efficiency of the method. In turn, this will allow the investigation of whether airborne eDNA can provide spatio-temporal characterisations of terrestrial vertebrate communities.

Here, we use a custom-made, portable, and affordable airborne eDNA sampler to assess the effects of air filter type, filter storage method, air flow rate, and filter area on detected vertebrate communities. Through advancing the sampling methods, we demonstrate the ability of airborne eDNA to yield seasonal and habitat-specific biodiversity profiles of terrestrial vertebrate communities in three protected nature areas in Denmark across two seasons (Fig. 1). As such, we refine airborne eDNA sampling to provide robust vertebrate detections and create the foundation for its implementation in ecological research, and conservation and biomonitoring practices.

Results

Coarser grade filters yield more diverse vertebrate communities

In Experiment 1, we explored the effect of air filter type for collection of airborne eDNA. We collected air samples at Kalvebod Fælled Wetland Bird Reserve, Denmark, using air samplers fitted with microfibre air filters with different filter grades, i.e., varying particle capture efficiency profiles: class ISO ePM1 65%, class ISO ePM 2.5 70%, and class ISO ePM10 65% (Luftladen, Germany, Fig. 1F, Supplementary Data 1). To investigate whether microfiber filters within the same filter grade, but constructed in different ways, yielded different results we included air filters in the grade ISO ePM1 65% produced by two different companies: Luftladen (Germany) and Filterbyen (Sweden), hereafter ‘LL’ and ‘FB’. The class ISO ePM1 65% and ISO ePM 2.5 70% are considered higher grade filters with higher trapping efficiency of bioaerosols, while ISO ePM10 65% is a lower/coarse grade filter with lower trapping efficiency. Eighteen field replicates were collected for each of the four filter types, however one sample belonging to the filter class ISO ePM 2.5 70% was not included in the analyses due to a fan malfunction. Following sample collection, we characterised vertebrate communities through DNA metabarcoding of the mitochondrial 12S gene with a primer set (BirtT) designed to target birds14 and the mitochondrial 16S gene with a primer set (16Smam) designed to target mammals15.

There were 39 bird taxa identified in the BirT dataset of which seven were domesticated bird species (Supplementary Data 2). The most prevalent domesticated bird species was domestic chicken (Gallus gallus), which was found in 70 of the 71 analysed samples with an average of 58.6% (SD ± 18.2) of sequences per sample. The 16Smam dataset yielded 21 mammal taxa of which seven were identified as human or domesticated animals (Supplementary Data 2). Pig (Sus scrofa - 72 samples), dog (Canis lupus - 56 samples), and cattle (Bos taurus - 53 samples) were the most prevalent domesticated taxa. Sequences belonging to humans and domesticated animals accounted for most of the sequences assigned to OTUs. Among these, pig sequences accounted for an average of 62.7% (SD ± 30.9%) of sequences across all analysed samples. Detections of domesticated animals were removed prior to further analyses (Supplementary Data 2 and Supplementary Table 2). Some negative controls contained sequences from domesticated animals, but after removal of these taxa, none of the negative controls retained sequences.

After removal of sequences assigned to domesticated birds, 70 of the 71 analysed samples contained wild bird detections in the bird dataset (Supplementary Table 3). The class ISO ePM1 65% from the LL company was the only one in which one of the analysed samples did not yield wild bird detections (Supplementary Table 3). In the mammal dataset, 31 out of the 71 analysed samples yielded wild mammal sequences (Supplementary Table 3). Of these, seven samples from class ISO ePM1 65% from LL, three samples from class ISO ePM1 65% from FB, seven samples from class ISO ePM 2.5 70%, and 14 samples from class ISO ePM10 65% contained wild mammal sequences (Supplementary Table 3).

Using linear mixed effect models (lmms), we evaluated the effect of filter type on wild bird and mammal taxonomic richness detected in all analysed samples, while setting the sample site and sample date as random effects. The filter type significantly impacted the number of detected wild bird ((lmm): Fdf = 35.723, p < 0.0001) and wild mammal taxa (lmm: Fdf = 7.5813, p = 0.0002) (Fig. 2A). Pairwise differences revealed that the ISO ePM10 65% filter captured a significantly higher number of bird taxa (average taxa per sample ± SD: 8.50 ± 2.45) and mammal taxa (1.39 ± 1.14) than the other three filter types (Fig. 2A, Supplementary Table 4). The 71 analysed samples from the four filter types yielded a total of 46 wild vertebrate taxa (Supplementary Data 3). Of these, ten bird taxa and two mammal taxa were detected by all filter types (Fig. 2B). The ISO ePM10 65% filter type captured the most unique bird and mammal taxa (Fig. 2B).

The two employed filter types from the same filter grade (ISO ePM1 65%) but from different companies and constructed in different ways (LL and FB, Fig. 1F), yielded varying numbers of taxa (Fig. 2A, B). Specifically, FB filters yielded significantly more bird detections than the LL filters (2.82 taxa per sample ±1.19(LL) and 5.28 ± 1.74(FB)). However, the FB filters did not yield a significantly different number of mammal taxa (0.41 ± 0.51(LL) and 0.33 ± 0.77(FB)) (Fig. 2A–D).

Community-level analyses were carried out using permutational multivariate analysis of variance tests (PERMANOVA) on community composition measured with Raup–Crick dissimilarity. Only samples with detections of wild animals were included in the analyses. Neither the detected bird community composition (PERMANOVA: Fdf = 0.48373, R2 = 0.0225, p = 0.8500; Fig. 2C) nor the mammalian community composition (PERMANOVA: Fdf = 0.80083, R2 = 0.0817, p = 0.6936; Fig. 2C) differed significantly between the filter types. However, multivariate dispersion analyses showed that the filter type influenced the heterogeneity of detected bird communities (lmm: Fdf = 6.3713, p = 0.0008), but not the mammal communities (lmm: Fdf = 0.95373, p = 0.4287) (Fig. 2D). Pairwise analyses showed that ISO ePM10 65% filters captured significantly more homogeneous bird communities than the ISO ePM1 65% filters (LL) (Fig. 2D, Supplementary Table 5).

To evaluate whether there was a difference in the amount of DNA captured by different filter types and whether it was correlated with the number of detected wild taxa, we quantified bird eDNA through qPCR with the BirtT primer set. We chose the BirtT primer set because it only amplifies bird sequences14 and thereby not human and some of the most prevalent domesticated animals, such as pig and cow. Using multiple lmms we investigated the association of bird DNA concentration from the qPCR analyses with the filter type, detected bird species richness, and the proportion of chicken sequences from the BirT metabarcoding analyses. In all these models we used the sample site and sample date as random effects. We found that filter type had a significant effect on the concentration of total bird DNA (wild and domesticated) (lmm: Fdf = 30.613, p < 0.0001; Supplementary Data 4, Supplementary Fig. 1A). Specifically, the ISO ePM10 65% filter yielded a higher concentration of bird DNA than the two other filter types (Supplementary Fig. 1A). There was a significant positive association between the quantity of bird DNA within a sample and the number of detected wild bird taxa (lmm: Fdf = 14.881, p = 0.0003; Supplementary Fig. 1B), indicating that filters that capture a higher amount of bird DNA yield detection of more diverse bird communities. However, we found no significant association between the amount of bird DNA and the arcsine-transformed proportion of chicken sequences detected using metabarcoding (lmm: Fdf = 0.14981, p = 0.7001). Further, we found no association between the number of wild bird taxa and the proportion of chicken sequences detected in a sample (lmm: Fdf = 0.65221, p = 0.4227). This suggests that the proportion of domesticated chicken sequences found within a sample did not impact the recovery of wild bird taxa.

Overall, the coarsest grade filter type, the ISO ePM10 65%, yielded the highest quantity of bird DNA and the highest number of wild bird and mammal taxa. Specifically, it yielded 25 bird taxa (with detections in all the 18 analysed samples), 12 mammal taxa (with detections in 14 of the 18 analysed samples), and an average of 9.89 (±2.83) wild bird and mammal taxa per sample (range: 4–14 taxa) (Fig. 2A).

Dry storage yields more diverse vertebrate communities

In Experiment 2, we investigated the effect of the air filter storage method on vertebrate detections. We deployed 20 air samplers on the island of Æbelø in the northern Fynen Ramsar Wetland in Denmark (Fig. 1) and preserved two halves of each of the 20 collected air filters using two storage methods (Fig. 1, Supplementary Data 5 and 6). Specifically, one half was dry-stored at −20 °C with silica beads, while the other half was stored in 12 ml Longmire’s buffer. From the latter, the filter and the remaining Longmire’s buffer were extracted and sequenced separately. This resulted in 60 DNA extract samples.

The BirT dataset (the bird dataset) yielded 42 bird taxa (Supplementary Data 6). Of these taxa, three were domesticated bird species, of which chicken was the most common. Specifically, chicken was detected in 48 sample DNA extracts representing an average of 38.3% (SD ± 30.1%) of sequences per sample. The 16Smam dataset (the mammal dataset) yielded 24 taxa, of which two were birds. The remaining taxa included one amphibian and 21 mammals including eight domesticated mammals (Supplementary Data 6). The most prevalent taxon was pig, which was detected in all samples and accounted for on average of 71.9% (SD ± 18.7%) of sequences per sample. Domesticated animals (Supplementary Table 2) were removed prior to further analyses. None of the negative controls contained wild vertebrate taxa (Supplementary Data 6). In the bird dataset, all 20 dry-stored air filter samples, 19 of the 20 Longmire’s buffer stored filter samples, and eight Longmire’s buffer samples yielded detections of wild birds (Supplementary Table 6). In the mammal dataset, 18 of the 20 dry-stored samples, all Longmire’s buffer-stored filter halves, and 11 Longmire’s buffer samples yielded wild mammal detections (Supplementary Table 6).

We evaluated the impact of sample storage method on bird and mammal taxonomic richness (across all samples) using lmms, where each sampler was included as a random effect. The number of detected wild vertebrate taxa differed between storage methods. There was a significant effect of the storage method on the detected number of bird taxa (Fig. 3A, lmm: Fdf = 99.862, p < 0.0001). Specifically, dry-stored filter halves yielded a significantly higher average number of wild bird taxa (10.75 ± 3.81) than Longmire’s buffer-stored filter halves (4.45 ± 2.68) and buffer samples (0.7 ± 1.03) (Fig. 3A, Supplementary Table 7). Eight bird taxa were detected in all three sample types and dry-stored samples had the highest number of unique bird taxa (9) (Fig. 3B). Dry-stored and Longmire’s buffer-stored filter halves shared 19 bird taxa (Fig. 3B). There were no unique taxa detected in the buffer samples, indicating that extraction of DNA from the buffer does not improve results (Fig. 3B). In the mammal dataset, there was also a significant effect of storage method (lmm: Fdf = 11.782, p = 0.0001) with dry-stored filters yielding a significantly higher average number of taxa (1.8 ± 1) than buffer samples (Fig. 3A, Supplementary Table 7). The dry-stored filter halves yielded the highest number of unique mammal taxa (7). Only three mammal taxa were shared between the three sample types, and two taxa were shared between dry-stored, and Longmire’s buffer-stored filter halves. There were no unique mammal taxa in buffer samples (Fig. 3B).

The storage methods did not influence the community composition measured with Raup–Crick dissimilarity in the BirT dataset (PERMANOVA: Fdf = 0.40163, R2 = 0.0179, p = 0.7768), but there was a significant influence of storage method in the mammal dataset (Fdf = 14.633, R2 = 0.3896, p < 0.0001; Fig. 3C). Pairwise comparisons indicated that mammal communities in buffer samples significantly differed from samples from dry-stored and Longmire’s buffer-stored filter halves (Supplementary Table 8).

Active filtration with large filters yields more vertebrate taxa

In Experiment 3, we explored the importance of the filter area and the power of the vacuum source. To do so, we compared six airborne eDNA sampler setups consisting of a combination of filters with two filter area sizes (large and small) and three different airflows (12 V/1.56 W, 5 V/0.8 W and no air flow; Fig. 1 and Supplementary Table 1). Sampling was carried out in ten sites at the Tofte Forest in Lille Vildmose in Denmark and resulted in a total of 180 filter samples (Fig. 1, Supplementary Data 7 and 8).

The BirT dataset yielded 45 bird taxa. Of these two were domesticated birds (Supplementary Data 8). Chicken was the most prevalent domesticated bird, and it was detected in 73 samples (Supplementary Table 2), accounting for an average of 19.7% (SD ± 27.6%) of sequences per sample. The mammal dataset yielded 31 taxa. Of these three taxa were birds. The rest of the taxa included 27 mammals and one amphibian. Of the 27 mammalian taxa, 12 were domesticated mammals (Supplementary Data 8). Pig and dog were the most detected domesticated mammal taxa. They were detected in 131 and 138 samples, respectively, with an average of 55.1% (SD ± 38.4%) and 32.7% (SD ± 38.4%) of sequences per sample, respectively. Detections of domesticated animal were removed prior to further analyses (Supplementary Table 2). None of the negative controls yielded wild vertebrate sequences.

After removing domesticated birds in the bird dataset, all 30 samples from the 12 V fans with large filters, 29 samples from the 12 V fans with small filters, 11 samples from the 5 V fans with small filters, and 29 samples from the 5 V fans with large filters yielded wild bird sequences (Supplementary Table 9). Two of the 30 passive samples with large filters yielded wild bird sequences, while there were no detections with the passive samplers with small filters (Supplementary Table 9). In the mammal dataset, 22 samples from the 12 V fans with large filters, 13 samples from the 12 V fans with small filters, 18 samples from the 5 V fans with large filters, and 12 samples from the 5 V fans with small filters yielded wild mammal and amphibian sequences (Supplementary Table 9). Only three passive samplers with large filters contained wild mammal taxa and there were no wild taxa detected with the passive samplers with small filters (Supplementary Table 9).

Using lmms, with sampling site and date set as random effects, we evaluated the impact of air sampler setup on bird and mammalian taxonomic richness. The air sampler setup had a significant effect on the number of detected wild bird taxa (Fig. 4A, lmm: Fdf = 40.434, p < 0.0001) with 12 V samplers with large filters yielding a higher average number of bird taxa (6.2 ± 2.87) than other setups (Supplementary Table 10). Similarly, in the mammal dataset, the sampler setup significantly influenced the number of detected taxa (Fig. 4A, lmm: Fdf = 11.054, p < 0.0001) with the four active filtration setups yielding a higher number of taxa than passive samplers.

Community analyses were only conducted with samples with wild animal detections. Bird community composition (measured with Raup–Crick dissimilarity) was not influenced by the sampler setup (PERMANOVA: Fdf = 0.76884, R2 = 0.0265, p = 0.6653), but there was a significant effect of the sampling site (PERMANOVA: Fdf = 1.4839, R2 = 0.1153, p = 0.0225). Similarly, mammal communities did not differ significantly between sampler setups (PERMANOVA: Fdf = 1.5634, R2 = 0.0759, p = 0.0521), but the sampling site had significant effect on the detected communities (PERMANOVA: Fdf = 2.3849, R2 = 0.2607, p < 0.0001). For both mammals and birds, the sampling sites explained a large proportion of the variation in the community composition (BirT: 11.53% and 16Smam: 26.07%), indicating localised detection of taxa.

Overall, we found that the setup with 12 V fan and a large filter yielded the highest average number of total detected wild vertebrate taxa (birds and mammals combined) per sample with an average of 7.33 taxa (SD ± 3.19) (Fig. 4A). This setup also yielded more consistent detections of taxa across multiple samples (Fig. 4B). The second-best sampler setup was the 5 V fan equipped with a large filter which had an average of 5.2 taxa/sample (SD ± 3.03) followed by the 12 V fan with small filters which had an average of 4.6 taxa/sample (SD ± 2.9) (Fig. 4A). The results also show that active sampling is important for collection of vertebrate airborne eDNA. Specifically, the setup with small filters and no active filtration did not yield wild vertebrate taxa, and the setup with large filters and no active air filtration yielded five wild vertebrate taxa in four of the 30 analysed samples (Fig. 4A). Samples from these two passive setups thereby essentially functioned as negative controls, supporting the authenticity of results from active filtering.

Overall, the progressive advancement of airborne eDNA sampling and storage across the three experiments yielded more wild vertebrate taxa for a given sample number (Fig. 5). Airborne eDNA allows seasonal detection of local vertebrate communities across habitats

We collected 311 air samples in three protected nature areas across Denmark in the three experiments: 71 samples from the Kalvebod Fælled Wetland Bird Reserve (Experiment 1), 60 samples from the island Æbelø (Experiment 2), and 180 samples from Tofte Forest of which 120 samples were collected with active filtration and 60 with no airflow (passive) (Experiment 3) (Fig. 1). After removing detections of domesticated animals (Supplementary Table 2) in the 251 analysed samples collected with active air filtration, 228 samples (90.8%) had detections of wild bird taxa, 144 samples (57.4%) had detections of wild mammal taxa, and 31 samples (12.4%) had detections of an amphibian taxon (Supplementary Data 3). Overall, we detected 90 wild vertebrate taxa of which 72 could be identified to species level, while the rest were identified at genus level (Figs. 6 and 7). Of these, we identified 52 bird species belonging to 11 orders, 19 mammal species belonging to 6 orders, and one amphibian species (Fig. 7, Supplementary Data 3). The detected species varied in body sizes, ecological and behavioural traits and levels of sociality.

Air sampling provided habitat-specific profiles of the vertebrate communities in the three protected nature areas (Figs. 6 and 7; Supplementary Data 3). In the Natura 2000 wetland bird reserve with open vegetation in Kalvebod Fælled in Amager Nature Park (Fig. 1A), the detected bird community comprised water and wading birds, such as Eurasian teal (Anas crecca), common snipe (Gallinago gallinago), bearded reedling (Panurus biarmicus), and Northern lapwing (Vanellus vanellus), all of which are species typically associated with Danish wetland habitats (Fig. 6). In Æbelø, an island with a forested interior surrounded by a Ramsar wetland, we detected a mix of water and forest birds. In addition to the white-tailed eagle (Haliaeetus albicilla), we detected the long-eared owl (Asio otus), European fallow deer (Dama dama), and the introduced mouflon (Ovis spp.), which are known to inhabit the island. In the old-growth mixed-savanna forest at Tofte Forest, we detected forest specialist bird species, such as tawny owl (Strix aluco), great spotted woodpecker (Dendrocopos major), and Eurasian bullfinch (Pyrrhula pyrrhula). Furthermore, we detected European bison (Bison bonasus), which has been introduced to the area.

Airborne eDNA uncovered fine-scale spatial patterns of bird biodiversity in Æbelø through a distance-decay effect16, with more similar bird communities detected in air samplers placed closer to each other (Fig. 3D). This potentially reflects the variety of microhabitats covered by our sampling transect, which included swamp forest and dry and hilly beach forests (Fig. 1A). However, this trend was only significant in dry-stored filters (Mantel r = 0.2032, p = 0.0187) and not in filters stored in Longmire’s buffer (Mantel r = 0.109, p = 0.1097). The ability of the dry-stored samples to reveal the fine-scale spatial patterns could be due to the dry-stored filters yielding more diverse communities and robust detections (Fig. 3). However, we did not detect such a pattern for mammals in dry-stored filters (Fig. 3D).

Airborne eDNA provided season-specific profiles of the vertebrate communities in the three sampled nature areas (Figs. 6 and 7; Supplementary Data 3). Collection of airborne eDNA in Kalvebod Fælled was carried out at the end of autumn/onset of winter. Here, we did not detect long-distance northern migrating bird groups, such as warblers and swallows, which had likely migrated south prior to sampling. Rather, we detected species which could be expected to occur in the area during this season (Supplementary Data 3). Similarly, collections in Æbelø and Tofte Forest during summer yielded taxa, which could be expected to present at this time. This included many summer visitors, such as warbler and swallows, and summer-active taxa, such as bats and toads.

Discussion

Against the backdrop of global terrestrial biodiversity loss, it is critical to have efficient and cost-effective tools to survey land-living animals. To this end, the analysis of airborne eDNA has been demonstrated as a promising survey tool2,8,12, but the method needs to be developed before widespread application. Here, we addressed key challenges related to low sampling yield of airborne vertebrate eDNA by optimising collection and storage of airborne eDNA samples. Specifically, we found that a higher voltage fan (12 V) equipped with a large (6 cm × 6 cm) ISO ePM10 65% class microfibre filter outperformed lower voltage fans (5 V) and higher-grade filter classes, and that for storage of filters, drying and freezing outperformed storage in Longmire’s buffer (Fig. 5). These advances yielded increases in the robustness and effectiveness of airborne eDNA sampling and demonstrated the potential of airborne eDNA to provide seasonal and habitat-specific profiles of vertebrate communities in a temperate region (Fig. 6).

We found that the air filters’ operational range of particle sizes impacted the diversity and homogeneity of the detected vertebrate communities (Figs. 2 and 5). Specifically, the coarsest filter, the lower grade ISO ePM10 65% filter, which has an operational range of particle sizes between 0.3 and 10 μm, outperformed less coarse, higher-grade filters, i.e. filters with narrower operational ranges and designed to be better at trapping smaller particle sizes (Fig. 2). The size range of aquatic fish eDNA is most abundant in the 1–10 μm particle fraction of filtered water17 and thereby the similar operational range of particle sizes of the ISO ePM10 65% filter may explain why it performed better. The performance of the lower-grade filter might also be explained by its high volumetric airflow rates (both inflow and outflow: Fig. 1 and Supplementary Table 1). Lower-grade filters are designed to favour capture of coarse particles, thereby providing less resistance to airflow (i.e., lower pressure drop). This in turn results in a higher fan speed, which results in a high and consistent airflow rate. In contrast, less coarse, higher-grade filters favouring smaller particle sizes have higher resistance to air flow, leading to reduced fan speed and airflow rates. Specifically, the coarsest filter with the lowest grade in the study, the ISO ePM10 65% filter had an inflow volumetric airflow of 3.49 m3 h−1 and an outflow volumetric airflow of 12.27 m3 h−1, while the higher-grade filters from the same company, ISO ePM1 65% and ISO ePM2.5 70%, had inflow airflow rates of around 0.1 and 0.65 m3 h−1 and outflow airflow rates of around 10.79 and 10.83 m3 h−1, respectively. Thus, the ISO ePM10 65% filtered more air compared to the less coarse filters, which can explain the increased detection of vertebrates with this filter.

From the least coarse filter class, the ISO ePM1 65%, we tested two filters, one with the same construction and from the same company as the two other tested filter classes (LL) and one from a different company (Filterbyen: FB) and with a different construction (Fig. 1F). The LL filters were constructed with progressive depth filtration with multi-layered filters where fibre diameter decreases at each layer18, while the FB filter was constructed with NanoWave® technology, which contains a special inner wave structure that increases the filter’s surface area19. This FB filter yielded an outflow volumetric airflow rate of 12.33 m3 h−1, which is comparable to the outflow airflow of the coarsest filter type, the ISO ePM10 65% filter from the company LL (12.27 m3 h−1). Despite this, the FB ISO ePM1 65% filter yielded significantly fewer vertebrate taxa than the ISO ePM10 65% filter (Fig. 2A). This could be explained by that the inflow airflow rate of the LL ISO ePM10 65% filter (3.78 m3 h−1) was higher than the FB ISO ePM1 65% filter (1.64 m3 h−1), which could suggest that inflow airflow rate is more crucial than the outflow airstream for capture of airborne vertebrate eDNA. In addition, when comparing the two filters belonging to the same filter grade (ISO ePM1 65%), but from different companies and constructed with different methods, the filter from the FB company yielded significantly more bird taxa, however not mammals (Fig. 2A). This filter had higher volumetric airflow rates (inflow = 1.64 m3 h−1, outflow = 12.33 m3 h−1) than the filter from the company LL (inflow = 0.1 m3 h−1, outflow = 10.79 m3 h−1) (Fig. 2). This further suggests that the filter construction that yield higher airflow rates can yield increased vertebrate detections.

Finally, the filter construction and structure could impact the lysis process during DNA extraction. For example, thicker filters with multiple layers may release filter-bound particles less effectively, compared to thin filters with fewer layers during the digestion. Thus, the lysis efficiency of different filter types should be tested using a more controlled experimental setting. Overall, our finding that air filter construction affects detections is in line with aquatic eDNA studies, where filter types constructed in different ways have also been shown to affect detections e.g.20,21. As such, other air filter constructions could be assessed for airborne eDNA collection, such as positively charged air filters, which may provide electrostatic attraction of negatively charged ‘free’ airborne eDNA22. However, in aquatic environments, passive collection with positively charged nylon filters were outperformed by non-charged cellulose ester membranes, indicating that ‘free’ eDNA might be less important for taxa detections, at least in aquatic environments21. However, as airborne eDNA might be in a different state than aquatic eDNA, the performance of charged filters should be assessed. Further, it is worth bearing in mind that eDNA sampling in water and air may differ. When collecting aquatic eDNA, filters that capture smaller particle sizes lead to increased clogging17,23, and increased volumes of filtered water can increase the likelihood of clogging, thereby reducing sampling efficiency. In contrast, paradoxically, the particle removal efficiency of aerosol particle fibre filters often increases with time due to the increase in surface area associated with the buildup of the filter cake22. However, the mechanism of an aerosol particle filter is fundamentally different from the membrane filters used to collect aqueous eDNA. The latter have a characteristic pore size and collect all particles larger than that size. In contrast, the microstructure of aerosol particle filters is largely open and is built of a network of fibres. Air streamlines move around the fibres, but the momentum of the relatively larger particles causes them to collide with fibres leading to sticking. Thus, particle filters cannot be characterised by a cutoff size in the same way as for membrane filters, and their performance depends on fibre diameter and density.

We found that storing air filter samples dry and frozen yielded better characterisation of vertebrate communities than storage in Longmire’s storage buffer (Figs. 3 and 5). Even though we did not detect many taxa in the leftover buffer extracts (Fig. 3A), the large volume of Longmire’s buffer may have diluted the collected airborne eDNA, and vertebrate detections might have been improved by extracting the entire volume of Longmire’s buffer along with the filter. However, this would increase laboratory processing time and cost. The filters that were stored dry and frozen were frozen immediately upon collection. As this might not be feasible in remote and challenging field conditions24 it should be investigated how storing filters at ambient temperature for a period of time prior to freezing affects detections. In contrast to our findings, it has been found that storage of aquatic eDNA filters in Longmire’s buffer leads to better preservation of DNA25 or that both dry-stored and Longmire’s buffer-stored aquatic eDNA filters lead to comparable community detections26. However, it is not straightforward to compare airborne eDNA results to the findings of aquatic eDNA studies as the structure of water and air filters differ. Specifically, water filters are generally thin membranes, while air filters are bulky with multiple layers of filter material. In addition, the mechanism of filtration of aerosol particles (impaction and diffusion) is fundamentally different from the mechanism of aqueous filters, which act as a sieve to catch all particles larger than a specific critical size. This differential binding could cause a difference in the effect of Longmire’s buffer on eDNA preservation in water and air filters. Moreover, airborne and aquatic eDNA might contain different proportions of DNA states (e.g., intracellular, free-floating, or particle-bound DNA27), from vastly different types of taxa, making a direct comparison difficult.

Air samplers with large filters and high voltage fans (12 V) with high power (1.56 W) yielded the most vertebrate detections, while the second-best setup containing large air filters and lower voltage fans (5 V) with low power (0.8 W) captured comparable mammal communities (Fig. 4). The airflow rate of the best performing sampler setup in our study filtered ca. 83.76 m3 (based on inflow) of air in 24 h and thereby this sampler filters a higher volume than the commercially available spore trap sampler, which filters 14.4 m3 of air in 24 h, and has been found to perform better at characterising vertebrate communities than other available commercial air samplers12. This suggests that our low-cost portable air samplers perform comparably to commercially available samplers, which can be expensive, difficult to manoeuvre, and need stationary power sources. The volumes of filtered air aside, sampler setups with larger filters yielded more diverse vertebrate communities (Fig. 4), showing that larger filter areas can optimise airborne eDNA sampling. Overall, our results support the use of active air filtration using fans equipped with a low-grade air filter (i.e. a coarse filter with wide operational range and favouring larger particle sizes) with a large filter area for the most efficient airborne eDNA collection.

We observed negligible vertebrate detections in samples collected with no airflow (passive) (Fig. 4A). This contradicted findings from passive sampling of aquatic eDNA, in which passive filtering provided robust detection of aquatic vertebrates21. As such, wind and water may not carry eDNA over filters in a similar way, and active filtration of airborne eDNA is crucial, at least for the relatively short sampling durations and the air sampler employed in the present study (24 h). However, passive sampling may yield better results if the samplers are placed for longer time periods or using a different material. For example, a material which is not designed to purify moving air, i.e. not an aerosol particle filter as used here.

We found the detected vertebrate communities to be characteristic of the three temperate nature area sampled in the three experiments (Figs. 1 and 6), demonstrating the potential of using airborne eDNA for capturing spatial trends. This is further supported by vertebrate detections made with airborne eDNA collected using commercial spore and pollen samplers in the Netherlands12. We further provide preliminary insights into the use of airborne eDNA to detect even finer scale spatial resolution of bird communities (Fig. 3D) through sampling in Æbelø, where we placed our samplers covering multiple habitats along the coastline, including swamp forest, and dry and hilly beach forests (Fig. 1). The detected changes in bird communities along this gradient may represent fine-scale community changes in these microhabitats. Moreover, in Tofte Forest, we found that sampling sites explained a high proportion of the variation in both bird and mammal communities, further showing the potential spatial resolution of airborne eDNA from vertebrates. Thereby, our results indicate that airborne eDNA generally reflects vertebrate communities at a fine local scale, potentially as a result of eDNA dilution, degradation under the influence of e.g. UV, time, or temperature28, settlement on surfaces29, or a combination. Regarding temporal patterns, we found vertebrate detections to reflect the two seasons that samples were collected. Specifically, in samples collected in late autumn, there was a lack of summer migrants and summer active taxa, while these taxa were present in samples collected in summer (Figs. 6 and 7). This shows that airborne eDNA captures seasonal fluctuations in vertebrate community composition. Together, these results indicate that airborne eDNA can capture seasonal and localised vertebrate community patterns2. However, more comprehensive examinations are needed to investigate the spatial and temporal resolution of airborne eDNA from vertebrate communities across climate zones and environments, including investigations into the state, transportation, and persistence of vertebrate airborne eDNA.

The use of airborne eDNA for vertebrate surveys is still in development, and the method shares the same suite of limitations and sources of error as all other eDNA methods, such as analytical errors, primer biases, incomplete reference databases, cost-efficiency of sampling, laboratory costs, and co-amplification of non-target taxa such as domesticated animals. Here, we focused on advancing knowledge on fundamental steps in the airborne eDNA workflow, namely sample collection and storage methods, to pave the way for affordable and portable airborne eDNA samplers to be used for non-invasive and robust large-scale vertebrate surveys capable of detecting spatio-temporal trends. We thereby facilitate the application of airborne eDNA for informing conservation efforts and answering questions in biogeography and community ecology. With further developments, airborne eDNA may ultimately mirror the success of aquatic eDNA in surveying freshwater and marine environments, and in the future the parallel use of airborne and aquatic eDNA sampling can be envisioned to provide comprehensive inventories of biodiversity across both terrestrial and aquatic realms.

Methods

Sampling sites

We carried out air sampling in three protected nature areas across Denmark during summer and autumn spanning three habitat types (Fig. 1A). To assess the effect of filter type and airborne eDNA capture efficiency (Experiment 1), air sampling was carried out in the Natura 2000 wetland bird reserve with open vegetation in Kalvebod Fælled, which is part of Amager Nature Park located close to Copenhagen (55.58809° N, 12.5381233° E: Supplementary Data 1). Average daily weather conditions based on Danish weather archives (https://www.dmi.dk/vejrarkiv, accessed June 2025) were stable across the sampling period from 1 to 4 November 2022; temperature ± SD: 11.37 ± 0.75 °C, wind speed: 6.01 ± 0.68 ms−1, precipitation: 0.04 ± 0.04 mm, humidity: 84.66 ± 4.61%, and air pressure: 1011.4 ± 4.3 hPa (Supplementary Data 9). To assess the effect of storage (Experiment 2), sampling was carried out on the island Æbelø (55.6389333° N, 10.16815° E: Supplementary Data 5) on 30–31 August 2023 (Fig. 1). Æbelø is a restoration site, and the island is part of northern Fynen’s global Ramsar convention on wetlands of international importance. Æbelø is a 2.09 km² island characterised by varied uncultivated nature with mixed forest, wetland, and open habitats. Average daily weather in the sampling area was stable across the two sampling days, however with some rain on 31 August; temperature: 14.89 ± 0.89 °C, wind speed: 2.4 ± 0.57 ms−1, precipitation: 0.23 ± 0.72 mm, humidity: 83.96 ± 0.91%, and air pressure: 1005.7 ± 0.23 hPa (Supplementary Data 9). Finally, to examine the effect of airflow rate (Experiment 3), air sampling was conducted on 6–9 September 2023 in Tofte Forest (56.8339617° N, 10.24971° E: Supplementary Data 7) in the Natura 2000 Lille Vildmose nature area, which is Denmark’s largest land conservation area and has reintroduced large mammals; European bison (Bison bonasus) and moose (Alces alces). Tofte Forest spans 40 km² and is characterised by a mixed forest consisting of alder, birch, beach, and oak. Average daily weather conditions did not vary across the sampling days; temperature: 18.25 ± 1.31 °C, wind speed: 2.93 ± 0.55 ms−1, precipitation: 0 mm, humidity: 86.28 ± 2.86%, and air pressure: 1021.9 ± 4.03 hPa (Supplementary Data 9).

Air back trajectories

At each sampling area for each sampling day, we calculated air back trajectories using the HYSPLIT model (Global Data Assimilation System – GDAS - wind fields and parameters includes: KMSL = 0, khmax = 48, nstr = 8, mhrs = 192) from the United States National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration30,31 (Supplementary Figs. 2–4). During sampling in Kalvebod Fælled (Experiment 1), air trajectories showed that air was arriving from the West and Southwest having crossed the Jutland peninsula and before that, the Northern coast of Europe and the British Isles. Air had been in the boundary layer with some subsidence from higher altitudes (Supplementary Fig. 2). On Æbelø (Experiment 2), air was arriving from a range of directions from West and Northwest to South. Air had been in the boundary layer with some subsidence from higher altitudes (Supplementary Fig. 3). Finally, in Tofte Forest (Experiment 3), air was arriving from all directions in the beginning of the sampling period, while in the later part of the period air was arriving from a range of directions from Northwest through West and South to Southeast. In contrast to the other two sample periods, these filters were exposed to air arriving from the continent (e.g. Poland) and from the Scandinavian peninsula (e.g. Norway) to a much larger extent. Air had been in the boundary layer with some subsidence from higher altitudes (Supplementary Fig. 4).

Airborne eDNA samplers and air filters

Airborne eDNA was collected using custom-made airborne eDNA samplers with 5 V (0.8 W; Sunon Inc., Taiwan: EE40100S2-1000U-999) or 12 V (1.56 W; Sunon Inc., Taiwan - EE60251S1-1000U-999) auxiliary fans powered by power banks or AA batteries, respectively8 (Fig. 1B). Four types of filter housings were used for securing the air filters: two are used in previous studies6,8 (original housings) and two new cone-shaped filter housings. The cone-shaped filter housing either had a base dimension of 6 × 6 cm that can be attached to a 12 V fan, and front dimension of 4 × 4 cm (to fit a small filter), or vice versa (Fig. 1B, Experiment 3). Original filter housings (6 × 6 cm) were used in Experiment 1 and 2 (Fig. 1B), while original and cone-shaped housings were used in Experiment 3. Each fan was attached to an analogue timer to measure running time for each air collection. Fans and housings were placed in plastic boxes as described in Lynggaard et al.8 with a smaller sized box for the 5 V than for the 12 V samplers (Fig. 1B). In Experiment 1 and 2 one sampler was fitted in each plastic box, while in Experiment 3 two samplers with the same power source (i.e., AA batteries, power bank, or no power source) were fitted in each plastic box. Samplers were mounted to the vegetation ~1 m above ground. In all three experiments, samplers were placed along transects (Fig. 1A). Filters were changed using sterile plastic tweezers, while wearing sterile nitrile gloves and a facemask. Between each filter change we cleaned gloves and the housing of the fan with ~5% household bleach followed by 99% ethanol.

Three different grades of microfibre air filters were used; class ISO ePM1 65% which have an optimal operational particle size range of 0.3–1 μm at a efficiency of 65%, class ISO ePM 2.5 70% which have a 70% efficiency of removal of 0.3–2.5 μm particles, and class ISO ePM10 65% which have a 65% efficiency of removing particle sizes of 0.3–10 μm32. The particle removal efficiency (e.g., ePM1 65%) is determined by the product of the air flow and the size ranges of removed particles, commonly called the Clean Air Delivery Rate (CADR) in the ventilation industry. While the particle removal efficiency increases with increasing filter grade (e.g., PM10 to PM2.5 to PM1 filters), the resistance to air flow of the filter also increases. The filters were acquired from the company Luftladen, referred to as LL (https://www.luftladen.com/, Germany). Catalogue numbers were FM-F8-06-1-TaFi (thickness = 4.5 mm), FM-F7-06-1-TaFi (thickness = 4.5 mm), and FM-M6-06-1-TaFi (thickness = 6 mm), respectively. Further, a class ISO ePM1 65% filter was included from the company Filterbyen referred to as FB (https://www.filterbyen.dk/, Sweden). This filter has an optimal operational particle size range of 0.3 to 1 μm at an efficiency of 65% (catalogue number: 15273 (thickness = 3 mm)) (Fig. 1). All filters were bought as mats and were cut into two sizes, referred to as small and large filters. The small filters were cut into ~6 × 6 cm pieces and they had a 4 × 4 cm surface area when fitted on the fan. The large filters were cut into 8 × 8 cm pieces and they had a 6 × 6 cm surface area when fitted on the fan. The cutting took place in a pre-PCR lab dedicated to airborne eDNA. Following cutting, the filter pieces were wrapped in foil and autoclaved at 120 °C for 15 min and then treated with UV for 15 min in a laminar airflow before they were packed individually in sterile zip lock bags.

Volumetric airflow rates

Volumetric airflow rates (m3h−1) of air samplers were measured using a Testo 405i Smart Probe thermal anemometer (order number: 05601405) with an accuracy of ± 0.1 m/s + 5% of mechanical ventilation (0–2 ms−1). The volumetric airflow rates of both inflow and outflow were measured for each of the four tested filter types in Experiment 1 (Fig. 1) and for each of the four air sampler set ups with active filtration in Experiment 3 (Fig. 1). First, the filter to be measured (e.g., type and size) was fitted on the appropriate fan (12 V or 5 V), after which the fan was placed in a 12 cm long plastic tube with a cross-section area of 6 × 6 cm. To measure the outflow airflow rate, we attached the fan to the tube with back side of the fan facing the tube interior. We then placed the sensors of the anemometer at the other end of the tube. We used the cross-section area of the tube to automatically calibrate the airflow measurements using the “volume flow outlet – return air” function. To measure the inflow airflow rate, we attached the fan to the tube with front side of the fan with filter facing inside the tube. Then we placed the sensors of the anemometer at the other end of the tube and measured the airflow rate using the “volume flow outlet – supply air”. We measured the air flow rate each second for 3 minutes to calculate the average volumetric airflow rate (Supplementary Table 1). The two measurements (inflow and outflow) of airflow rates yielded contrasting results due to differences in how air enter and released by the air samplers. The fan’s outflow forms a focused jet with enough momentum to counteract viscous losses, while the inflow consists of non-directional entrainment of the surrounding air. Consequently, outflow measurements are consistently higher than inflow measurements when measured at a single point with the thermal anemometer. These airflow measurements are intended to capture relative trends and differences between sampler setups, rather than to provide an exact measure of the total volume of air sampled.

Experiment 1: The effect of air filter type (Kalvebod Fælled)

To examine the effect of filter grade, 24 12 V samplers were deployed on three consecutive days for ~24-h sampling events. Samplers were oriented in the same direction (mainly south-facing) with approx. 2 m distance between samplers (Fig. 1, Supplementary Data 1). Four filter types described above were used. For each sampling event, the four classes of filters were randomly assigned to a sampler (n = 6 for each filter type per sampling event), yielding 18 field replicates per filter type and a total of 72 samples. However, one sample belonging to the filter class ISO ePM 2.5 70% was not included in further analyses due to a fan malfunction. For each day we included one filter from each filter type as negative filter controls. Air filters were stored in 25 ml sterile Eppendorf falcon tubes with Whatman® silica bead bags sterilised by 15 min of UV exposure on each side. Filters were transported to the laboratory in a cooling bag with a freezer pack and stored at −20 °C until the DNA extractions.

Experiment 2: The effect of filter storage (Æbelø)

To contrast storage of air filters in Longmire’s buffer versus direct freezing in the field, we deployed 20 12 V samplers coupled with large ISO ePM10 65% filters (based on Experiment 1 results) (Figs. 1 and 2). One approx. 24-hour sampling event was carried out. Upon collection of air filters, we cut each filter diagonally using a sterile scalpel. One half was dry stored at −20 °C in a 25 ml sterile Eppendorf falcon tube with silica beads (see Experiment 1). The other half was stored in 12 ml Longmire’s buffer33 (100 mM Tris (pH 8.0), l00mM EDTA (pH 8.0), 10 mM NaCl, 0.5% SDS (Sodium Dodecyl Sulfate), 0.2% sodium azide). The 20 filter halves stored in Longmire’s buffer were kept at room temperature for two weeks to mimic field conditions without access to a freezer after which they were stored at −20 °C prior to DNA extraction. From the Longmire’s buffer stored samples, the filter and the buffer were extracted separately to detect if the buffer leftovers contained vertebrate DNA. We saved an unused filter and unused vial with Longmire’s buffer as filter and buffer negative controls.

Experiment 3: The effect of filter size and airflow rate (Tofte Forest)

To assess the effect of filter size and airflow rate, we used four combinations of auxiliary fans coupled with different filter sizes (i.e. 5 V and 12 V coupled with small and large air filters), and two combinations of passive filters (i.e. small and large filters mounted just on the filter housing). This yielded six combinations hereafter referred to air sampler setups (Fig. 1B). We used ISO ePM10 65% class filters based on the results of Experiment 1. Air sampling was carried out at ten sites (Fig. 1A, Supplementary Table 6). At each site, all six samplers were mounted on the same tree facing the same direction. Each sampling event spanned ~24 h and was repeated for three consecutive days (Supplementary Table 6). This yielded 30 samples for each of the six sampler setups and a total of 180 air samples. For each sampling event, we saved an unused filter as a filter negative control. Air filters were stored as in Experiment 1.

DNA analysis

DNA was extracted in a pre-PCR lab dedicated to airborne eDNA8. DNA was extracted from the 291 air filter samples, 16 negative filter controls (unopened filters brought to the field), and one negative Longmire's buffer control (evaluating contamination in the sampling equipment). Extractions were carried out using the Qiagen DNeasy Blood & Tissue kit (Hilden, Germany). Prior to DNA extractions, we used a sterile scalpel to remove the edges of the filters that had been in contact with the fan housing during sampling. Each filter was then cut into approx. 16 small pieces and tranffered to a DNA LoBind 15 ml Eppendorf tube with two Qiagen 7 mm sterile stainless beads. During the initial digestion step, for the large filters (Experiment 1 and 3) we used 2700 μl of ATL buffer and 300 μl of Proteinase K (19.3 mg/ml), while for small filters (Experiment 3) and dry stored filter halves (Experiment 2) we used 1350 μl of ATL and 150 μl of Proteinase K. For filter halves stored in Longmire’s buffer (Experiment 2) we added 720 μl of ATL and 150 μl of Proteinase K to the filter and 1000 μl were taken from the leftover buffer, and was mixed with 720 μl of ATL and 80 μl of Proteinase K. All samples were then shaken in a Tissuelyser II (Qiagen) for 1 min at 20 Hz after which samples were incubated at 56 °C overnight on a shaker with a rotor head. For further extraction steps we used 2000 μl digest of the samples from large filters, 1500 μl from the small filters and dry stored filter halves, 3000 μl from the Longmire’s buffer-stored filter halves, and the total volume (1800 μl) from the buffer itself. We added AL buffer and ice-cold 99% ethanol at 1:1 ratio to the digest. This mixture was run through spin columns using a Qiagen vacuum manifold (Hilden, Germany). Subsequent steps were the same as in Lynggaard et al.8. Moreover, we included an extraction negative control for each 21 samples, a room negative control for each 42 samples consisting of 200 μl water in a 2 ml Eppendorf tube left open in the lab outside the flow hood, and a vacuum manifold negative control for each 21 samples consisting of a spin column introduced to the extraction process from the vacuum manifold step onwards.

Prior to metabarcoding PCR amplification, we conducted quantitative PCRs (qPCR) on a subset of air filter sample extracts (142 DNA extracts from air samples, spanning the three experiments) to screen for PCR inhibition and determine the optimal PCR cycle number34. The qPCRs were carried out using two metabarcoding primer sets; the BirT primer set, which targets an approx. 260 bp mitochondrial 12S marker (excl. primers) for birds14 and the 16Smam primer set which targets an approx. 90 bp mitochondrial 16S marker (excl. primers) for mammals15. Both primer sets carried unique 5′ 7-nucleotide tags with at least three mismatches between different tags. Each tag also contained one to two complexity bases at the 5’ end to increase the complexity on flow cells during sequencing. The qPCRs were carried out with 2 or 3 µl of sample extracts and 2 µl of negative controls. As positive controls, we used DNA from rufous-naped bellbird (Aleadryas rufinucha) and bowhead whale (Balaena mysticetus). The qPCRs were conducted in 20 µl reactions and included 1 unit AmpliTaq GoldTM DNA polymerase, 1x AmpliTaq GoldTM PCR buffer, 2.5 mM MgCl2, and 0.2 mM dNTPs (Applied Biosystems, Waltham, MA, USA), 0.5 mg/mL Bovine Serum Albumin (BSA: Experiment 1 and 2) or Recombinant Albumin (RA: Experiment 3) (New England BioLabs), 0.6 µM forward and 0.6 µM reverse primer, 1 µl of SYBR green ROX (1:4:2000 SYBR green (Invitrogen), ROX reference dye (Invitrogen), high grade DMSO), and 3 µM human blocker for reactions with 16Smam primers35. For BirT primers the cycling parameters were initial denaturation at 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 50 cycles of 95 °C for 30 s, 60 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 1 min followed by a melting curve. For 16Smam primers the cycling parameters were initial denaturation at 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 50 cycles of 95 °C for 12 s, 59 °C for 30 s, and 70 °C for 25 s followed by a melting curve.

Reaction mixtures and cycling conditions for the metabarcoding PCRs were identical to the qPCR, with omission of the SYBR Green ROX and replacing the melting curve with a final extension of 72 °C for 7 min. Informed by the qPCR screening we used tagged PCR cycle numbers spanning from 33 to 41 for the BirT primers and 35–39 for the 16Smam primers and 2 µl or 3 µl of sample extracts (Supplementary Table 11).

We conducted six PCR replicates for each DNA extract, including for negative and positive controls. Positive controls were DNA from rufous-naped bellbird or mottled berryhunter (Rhagologus leucostigma) for BirT primers and bowhead whale or common vampire bat (Desmodus rotundus) blood meal36 for 16Smam primers. PCR replicates from each sample were amplified with primers carrying different tag combinations. PCR products were visualised on 2% agarose gels. Subsequently, PCR replicates were pooled according to the brightness of the bands. Specifically, 4 µl of PCR products were added from PCR replicates with bright bands, 6 µl from medium strength bands and 8 µl from PCR replicates with weak or no bands. From positive control samples we added 2 µl of PCR products. All negative controls (extraction, filter, buffer, and room controls) showed no visual evidence of amplification but were pooled (8 µl of PCR products) and sequenced alongside experimental samples. The six PCR replicates from each DNA extract were placed in different pools. The amplicon pools were purified using Magbio HighPrepTM PCR magnetic beads (amplicon pool to bead ratio for BirT was 1:1, while 1:1.6 for 16Smam). Purified pools were then quantified using dsDNA high-sensitivity qubit assays (Thermo Fisher). Equimolar amounts of the purified amplicon pools were then used to build dual-indexed Illumina libraries using the PCR-free TagSteady protocol37. Amplicon libraries purified using Magbio HighPrepTM PCR magnetic beads and were quantified using NEBNext® Library Quant Kit for Illumina® (New England BioLabs). Purified libraries were pooled and sequenced on an Illumina MiSeq instrument, with eight paired-end 150 bp (V3) sequencing runs at the GeoGenetics Sequencing Core, University of Copenhagen, Denmark.

DNA concentration measurements

To assess the quantity of airborne bird eDNA collected with different filter types in Experiment 1, we conducted a new set of qPCRs on all 71 air filter samples (excluding one sample of the ISO ePM 2.5 70%). The qPCRs were carried out in triplicate for each sample. We chose BirT primers as they are bird-specific, thereby avoiding problems associated with amplification of domesticated mammals and humans14. The qPCR set-up followed the MIQE guidelines38. Sample extracts were run against a standard curve consisting of DNA extracts from a rufous-naped bellbird with starting concentrations of 10 ng/µl, 1 ng/µl, 0.1 ng/µl, 0.001 ng/µl, 0.0001 ng/µl, and 0.00001 ng/µl. The qPCRs were conducted in three batches with all the qPCR replicates for each sample included in the same batch. The threshold cycle (Ct) values and the log-transformed starting concentration of the dilution series was used to determine the PCR efficiency and to calculate the sample DNA concentration for each run independently.

Bioinformatics and sequence processing

Illumina adaptors and low-quality stretches from the raw sequences were removed and the paired reads were merged using AdapterRemoval v.2.3.239 with the following parameters: minlength = 100, qualitybase = 33, minquality = 28. Subsequently, using the begum pipeline40 metabarcoding primers were trimmed allowing for two mismatches, and sequences were demultiplexed using Begum.py_sort command. We then retained only sequences found in minimum three of the six PCR replicates with minimum 5 reads, and minimum length of 250 bp for the BirT and 90 bp for the 16Smam using the Begum.py_filter command. Sequences were then trimmed using convertToUSearch.py41 with lmin and lmax set to 260 and 300 bp for BirT, and 90 and 110 bp for 16Smam. Filtered sequences were then clustered to operational taxonomic units (OTUs) at 97% similarity using sumaclust v1.0.3142. An OTU table was then created using tabulateSumaclust.py. We used the LULU algorithm43 to identify potential erroneous OTUs and merge them with their “real” biological OTUs. LULU was conducted for BirT primers using minimum_ratio = 1, minimum_match = 97 and minimum_relative_cooccurence = 0.95 and for 16Smam primers using minimum_ratio = 1, minimum_match = 99 and minimum_relative_cooccurence = 0.95.

Taxonomy was assigned to OTU sequence using the NCBI online nucleotide BLAST tool against the Nucleotide Collections (nt/nr) between June to November 2023. Any OTU with 100% identity and 100% query coverage to a single species was considered a species-level match. Secondly, if an OTU sequence had taxonomic matches to multiple species with 100% identity and query cover, we used the lowest common ancestor to capture all hits (i.e., genus or family level). Thirdly, if an OTU sequence had a perfect match to a species, but that species is rare or not found in Denmark, we assigned those OTUs to local species and these taxa are indicated with “Potential” (Supplementary Data 3, Fig. 7). For example, in Experiment 2, an OTU was assigned to pine bunting (Emberiza leucocephalos), but as it is not found in Denmark, this OTU was assigned to E. schoeniclus, as the 12S sequences for locally occurring species in the genus (E. schoeniclus) was not found in Genbank. Further, one of the most common OTUs in Experiment 1 was assigned to Branta canadensis (Canada goose) with 100% identity and query cover, but by far the most common Branta species in the study area is barnacle goose (B. leucopsis), which did not have a reference sequence for the targeted 12S region. We therefore assigned this OTU to B. leucopsis. Fourthly, we merged all OTUs (along with the sequences) identified to the same species. Finally, we removed any OTU identified as human or domesticated animals (Supplementary Data 2, 6, and 8), and we removed OTUs identified as birds from the 16Smam dataset, as these birds were already detected with BirT primers. Negative controls yielded no wild taxa, and we did not detect positive control taxa in samples. The only detected taxon that did not belong to Danish fauna was Loxodonta africana (African bush elephant), which was only found in one sample from Experiment 1 (Supplementary Table 2) and not in any of the negative or positive controls. We consider this detection a laboratory contaminant. The resulting taxa table was then used for statistical analyses.

Statistics and reproducibility

All statistical analyses were conducted in R v.4.3.144. To analyse the effect of independent variables on dependent variables (e.g., number of taxa detected, BirT DNA concentrations) we utilised linear models (lm) and linear mixed effect models (lmms) in the lme4 package v.1.1-35.145. In the experiments involving multiple sampling days (Experiment 1 and 3), we used the sample day as a random effect, while in Experiment 2, we used the sampler itself and in Experiment 3 we included the sampling site as a random effect in our models. Type III ANNOVAs were used to calculate the significance of these models. We used the emmeans package v.1.10.546 to investigate the pairwise differences between categories of independent variables. Principal Coordinate Analysis (PCoA) with the Raup-Crick dissimilarity (based on presence/absence data and the probability of communities sharing species) was used to visualise the community compositional differences with the “pcoa” function in the ape package v.5.847. Raup-Crick dissimilarity was calculated using the function “raupcrick” in the vegan package48 with the null parameter set to “r1” and nsimul parameter set to 999. Bird and mammal community differences (based on Raup-Crick dissimilarity) were statistically examined using permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) using adonis2 tests in the vegan package48. We conducted PERMANOVAs with 10,000 permutations and used sampling day as a random effect (strata parameter) in Experiments 1 and 3. Pairwise differences between communities were investigated using the “pairwise.adonis” function49. Heterogeneity of detected communities was evaluated through calculating the beta dispersal with the “betadisper” function in vegan v.2.6-848. We set the “bias.adjust” parameter to TRUE and used the group’s centroid. In Experiment 2, we conducted Mantel tests with the vegan package v.2.6-848 to investigate the association between detected vertebrate communities and distance between air samplers. We calculated the physical distance between air samplers using the GPS coordinates in the sp package v.2.1-450.
