Characterization of a local case of severe fever with thrombocytopenia syndrome in Shenzhen, China: Clinical and immunological insights
Authors: Xiaomin Zhang, Jing Wu, Yalan Huang, Chunli Wu, Yue Li, Renli Zhang, Xiaohe Li, Bo Peng, Fan Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•First local SFTS case in Shenzhen with DBV gene rearrangement.•Serum samples from patient and contacts tested for DBV via RT-PCR and ELISA.•SZ-202,101 strain genotype BCB, no recombination detected.•Comprehensive study on immunological and pathological features of SFTS.•Current DBV sequencing research has significant limitations.

Background

Severe fever with thrombocytopenia syndrome (SFTS), a zoonotic disease caused by Dabie bandavirus (DBV) infection, is characterized by fever, leukopenia, thrombocytopenia, and gastrointestinal symptoms. It is predominantly transmitted through the bite of ticks infected with the pathogen. Additionally, there is potential for transmission via direct contact with infected body fluids (Jiang et al., 2015). A subset of patients may exhibit bleeding tendencies and neurological manifestations, including limb tremors and alterations in consciousness. Prompt initiation of symptomatic treatment and supportive care at the early stages tends to yield a favorable prognosis for the majority of patients. Conversely, severe manifestations of SFTS can escalate to disseminated intravascular coagulation, multi-organ dysfunction, and, in extreme cases, fatal outcomes. Currently, no efficacious vaccines or antiviral therapies have been developed to combat DBV infection (Zhan et al., 2017; Casel et al., 2021; T Yoshikawa et al., 2015). Epidemiologically, as of 2021, China has reported a cumulative total of 18,902 cases, with a mortality rate of 5.11 % (Chen and Chen, 2022). DBV cases have been sporadically reported in 27 provinces (Yue and Lu, 2024).

DBV, also referred to as severe fever with thrombocytopenia syndrome bunyavirus (SFTSV), was initially classified as a novel Bunyavirus. It was first isolated and identified by Chinese scientists in 2011 (Yu et al., 2011; Xue et al., 2011). In 2021, the International Committee on Taxonomy of Viruses (ICTV) reassigned DBV to the genus Bandavirus within the family Phenuiviridae, under the order Bunyavirales (Yu et al., 2011; Zhang et al., 2011; Xu et al., 2011). It is an enveloped, single-stranded, negative-sense, segmented RNA virus. The virions are spherical, measuring 80 to 120 nm in diameter. The DBV genome is segmented into L, M, and S segments. The L segment, comprising 6368 nucleotides, encodes an RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RdRp) consisting of 2084 amino acids, which is essential for viral genome replication and transcription. The M segment, consisting of 3378 nucleotides, encodes a glycoprotein precursor consisting of 1073 amino acids that is proteolytically processed into two distinct glycoproteins, Gn and Gc. These Gn and Gc glycoproteins recognize and bind to specific receptors on the surface of host cells, facilitating viral entry into various cell types (Hofmann et al., 2013; van Kooyk, 2008). The S segment is an ambisense single-stranded RNA molecule containing 1746 nucleotides, with two open reading frames (ORFs) positioned 54 nucleotides apart in opposite orientations, encoding proteins involved in viral replication and host interaction. The ORF near the 5′ end is transcribed from the negative-sense strand, encoding the non-structural protein (NS), while the nucleocapsid protein (NP) is encoded by the complementary RNA at the 3′ end of the S segment genome. NS markedly suppresses the production of type I interferons by disrupting cellular signaling pathways, thereby dampening the host's antiviral immune response (Santiago et al., 2014; Kitagawa et al., 2018; Jin et al., 2016). The NP is pivotal in safeguarding the viral genome, facilitating viral replication, and facilitating the formation of inclusion bodies. Upon binding of the viral genome to NP, ribonucleoprotein (RNP) complexes are formed that include the S, M, and L segments to which multiple RdRp enzymes attach, engaging in the replication and transcription of the viral genome (Ferron et al., 2017; Poranen et al., 2001). A high mutation rate, characteristic of RNA virus replication and transcription, results from the absence of proofreading mechanisms; this phenomenon is also observed in DBV (Domingo et al., 2012; Li et al., 2019). Research into these biologically significant mutations has been limited, with a focus on the RdRp within the L segment and the Gn and Gc proteins within the M segment. The elevated mutation rate of RNA viruses increases the genetic variability that complicates predictions of their evolutionary trajectory. Moreover, mutations in key viral proteins may impact viral replication efficiency, host range, and transmissibility, presenting challenges for vaccine and drug development (Domingo et al., 2012; Liu et al., 2023).

Currently, there are two main DBV genotyping methods. One is to classify DBV strains into Chinese and Japanese lineages: C1-C6 and J1-J4, according to the origin of the virus in China and Japan (Takahashi et al., 2014; T Yoshikawa et al., 2015; Lv et al., 2017; Shi et al., 2017). However, with the increasing number of cases worldwide, the diversity and distribution of viral sequences have gradually expanded. The C and J genotypes may appear simultaneously in the same area. Therefore, this genotyping method, based solely on geographical distribution, is no longer suitable for DBV genotyping. Another commonly used method is to construct a phylogenetic tree based on the inter-genotype and intra-genotype differences of DBV nucleotide sequences using the maximum likelihood method. Sequences with high similarity cluster together. DBV can be roughly divided into six genotypes (A-F) according to the genetic distance between different viral branches (Yun et al., 2017; Fu et al., 2016; Yun et al., 2020; Li et al., 2021; Rattanakomol et al., 2023). At present, the A-F genotyping approach has become the preferred method for DBV genotyping due to its wide applicability (Fu et al., 2016; Huang et al., 2014). However, due to the lack of uniform genotyping criteria, different studies have reported conflicting genotype assignments for the same strain. It is difficult to compare and integrate data from different studies, which greatly hinders the progress of DBV molecular evolution research. Therefore, it is urgent to establish a unified DBV genotyping method.

On August 5, 2021, the Shenzhen Center for Disease Control and Prevention (Shenzhen CDC) received a report from the Shenzhen Third People's Hospital of a patient suspected of having severe fever with thrombocytopenia syndrome (SFTS). Shenzhen CDC immediately organized a professional team to carry out an epidemiological investigation and blood sample collection. Following sample collection, the laboratory quickly carried out nucleic acid testing, antibody screening, and gene sequencing. The results provided a solid scientific basis for the prevention and control of the SFTS epidemic and public health decision-making.

Materials and methods

Diagnostic criteria

Diagnoses were made in accordance with the diagnostic and therapeutic protocols outlined in the 2010 edition of the "Guidelines for the Prevention and Treatment of Fever with Thrombocytopenia Syndrome," published by the Ministry of Health of China on September 29, 2010.

Epidemiological investigation protocol

A thorough investigation was conducted, encompassing patients' basic information; the onset and diagnostic treatment process; epidemiological history; assessment of close contacts; investigation of shared exposures and community case searches; as well as surveillance of ticks and dogs.

Specimen collection

Blood specimens were collected from the patient and his close contacts for viral serological and etiological testing. Ticks collected around his factory, and blood samples from the dog he had contact with, were also collected for viral testing.

Viral RNA extraction and nucleic acid detection

Viral RNA from serum was extracted using Qiagen's QIAamp Viral RNA Mini Kit (batch number: 52,906), followed by nucleic acid detection with the DBV nucleic acid detection kit (real-time fluorescent PCR assay) from Jiangsu Shuoshi Biotechnology Co., LTD. (batch number: 20,210,501). The procedures were strictly adhered to the manufacturer's instructions.

Serological testing

The detection of DBV IgM and IgG was performed using the DBV IgM/IgG detection kit produced by Zhongshan Bioengineering Co., Ltd., strictly following the instructions provided with the kit (G202505016).

Sequence alignment and evolutionary analysis

Viral RNA extracted from serum samples that were confirmed positive by real-time fluorescent RT-PCR was sequenced. Subsequently, sequences were aligned using the MAFFT software. DNAMAN 5.2.2 software was employed to identify the reading frame and translate the amino acid sequences corresponding to the L, M, and S segments. These sequences were then compared with the DBV strains HB2012–197 (GenBank accession numbers: MT114242 for L segment, MT114256 for M segment, MT114286 for S segment) and HB29 (GenBank accession numbers: NC018136 for L segment, NC018138 for M segment, NC018137 for S segment) using BLAST to compare nucleotide and amino acid sequences and identify variations.

Genetic and phylogenetic analysis

Genetic and phylogenetic analyses were conducted by retrieving published full-length DBV sequences from China, South Korea, and Japan. Then, multiple sequence alignments were carried out using MAFFT software, with phylogenetic analyses based on the complete L, M, and S segments of DBV. For the S segment sequences NC018137 and AB985541, which were reverse complements, we performed reverse complement conversion using Primer Premier 5.0 before sequence alignment. Phylogenetic trees were constructed using the Maximum Likelihood (ML) method in MEGA software. Evolutionary distances were calculated based on the Kimura 2-parameter model. The robustness of the ML trees was assessed through 1000 bootstrap replicates.

On August 6, 2021, the Shenzhen CDC confirmed Dabie bandavirus (DBV) infection in his blood sample through RT-qPCR. Sequence determination and amino acid sequence comparison

The DBV strain isolated from the positive blood sample of the case was designated SZ-202,101. The L, M, and S sequences of obtained in this study were uploaded to GenBank, with the assigned accession numbers PQ374004 (6356 bp), PQ374005 (3365 bp), and PQ374006 (1734 bp), respectively. BLAST analysis revealed that the L, M, and S segments exhibited the highest similarity to those of the HB2012–197 strain from Hubei Province, with similarity percentages of 99.50 %, 99.49 %, and 99.60 %, respectively. When compared to the HB29 strain, the similarities of the L, M, and S segments of strain SZ-202,101 were 95.69 %, 95.16 %, and 95.44 %, respectively. The L segment contained 14 amino acid variation sites, which were distributed across the Linker, Core lobe, vRBL, Fingers, Fingemode, Thumb ring, and CBD domains (Fig. 1A). The M segment-encoded glycoproteins exhibited sixteen mutations located in the Gn protein across the A, B, β, C, and IM domains and nine in the Gc protein situated in the I, III, and TM domains, along with one mutation site in the Linker domain (Fig. 1B). Evolutionary analysis

We aligned the DBV sequences retrieved from GenBank and employed Mega11.0 software to construct the phylogenetic trees. Separate phylogenetic analyses were conducted for the L, M, and S segments. Utilizing the DBV genotyping methodology proposed by Seok et al. (Yun et al., 2017), we performed genetic evolutionary analysis and constructed phylogenetic trees using the Maximum Likelihood (ML) approach. The findings revealed a high degree of homology for the L, M, and S segments between the DBV strain SZ-202,101 and the HB2012–197 strain. Specifically, the L and S segments were positioned within the same clade, both affiliating with the B genotype (Fig. 2 and Fig. 4), whereas the M segment was associated with the C genotype (Fig. 3). Discussion

Since 2012, the Shenzhen Center for Disease Control and Prevention has actively monitored DBV (DBV) cases. This study identified the local SFTS case in Shenzhen, which had no confirmed contact history. Furthermore, sequence analysis revealed that the genotype of the DBV strain SZ-202,101 is BCB. This marks the first occurrence of gene assortment phenomena in DBV in G Since 2012, the Shenzhen Center for Disease Control and Prevention has been actively monitoring DBV cases. This study identified a local case of SFTS without a confirmed contact history in Shenzhen. This case is the local SFTS case caused by the BCB genotype in Shenzhen. It is the first time that DBV gene matching has occurred in Guangdong Province, rather than the first discovery of this genotype in China.

The patient presented with a high fever of unknown origin and mild respiratory symptoms on July 30, 2021. Clinical laboratory tests revealed a significant decrease in the patient's white blood cell and platelet counts. Five days after the onset of symptoms, the patient showed significant abnormalities in liver function tests and myocardial enzyme levels. These findings are consistent with the clinical features of DBV infection documented in previous research (Ma and Zhang, 2020). Six days after the onset of the illness, the laboratory conducted nucleic acid testing on the patient’s blood sample using real-time fluorescent quantitative PCR. This test returned a positive result for DBV, confirming the patient as the case of fever with thrombocytopenia syndrome in Shenzhen in 2021. Nucleic acid testing was performed on several serum samples from the SFTS patient on days 12 and 20 after the onset, but no disease was detected in any of the samples. This finding is consistent with the results of studies conducted by Zhan and Jiang.They found that the nucleic acid detection rate is higher in the first week after onset compared to the second week in blood samples from SFTS patients (Jiang and Pang, 2018; Hu et al., 2018). We observed that DBV-IgM remained positive for 12 days after the onset of the disease, while DBV-IgG was not detected at any time during hospitalization. Jiang's research also identified IgM as the earliest detectable antibody, present within 2 days after infection (Jiang and Pang, 2018)

In this study, we successfully determined the nearly complete sequence of the DBV gene (SZ-202,101) using first-generation sequencing technology. Wang et al. identified three conserved functional domains in the RdRP of DBV: the PA-C-like domain (amino acid positions 296–763), the Polymerase core (positions 763–1346), and the PB2-N-like domain (positions 1346–1571) (Wang et al., 2020). Compared to the HB29 strain, the SZ2021–01 strain exhibits mutations in the RdRP that are primarily concentrated in the PA-C-like domain, potentially affecting the template entry process during viral RNA synthesis. Viruses can hijack the 5′ cap structures from host cell RNA and attach them to viral mRNA, a process known as "cap-snatching," which allows the host's ribosomal translation machinery to recognize and process the viral mRNA (Wu et al., 2017; Du et al., 2023). The V1717Z mutation in the SZ-202,101 strain occurs within the CBD domain, raising questions about its potential impact on the virus's ability to capture and transcribe capped RNA, which requires further scientific investigation.

In this study, we identified 16 mutation sites across the A, B, C, and IM domains in the Gn of SZ-202,101 strain compared to the HB29 strain. The Gc exhibited eight mutation sites located in Domain I, Domain III, and TM domains. Studies have shown that the Gn protein primarily mediates the binding of the virus to host receptors, aiding in the internalization of viral particles into endosomes (Lozach et al., 2011; Sun et al., 2014). Acidification of the endosomal environment causes conformational changes in the Gc protein. This change promotes the fusion of the endosomal membrane with the viral envelope, allowing the viral genome to enter the cytoplasm (Hofmann et al., 2013). Further research is needed to assess whether the mutation sites in the Gn and Gc proteins of the SZ-202,101 strain affect viral attachment and entry into target cells.

The nucleocapsid protein (NP) of DBV engages in interactions with adjacent molecules via hydrophobic bonds and hydrogen bonding facilitated by its N-terminal arm, a process that entails the linkage of one NP monomer to the N-terminal arm of another (Ferron et al., 2011; Lokupathirage et al., 2021). Deleting the N-terminal region of NP or mutating key sites (M1-D34) prevents the formation of ring oligomers, which hinders NP assembly (Zhou et al., 2013, Walter et al., 2011). Proper assembly of NP oligomers is crucial for the formation of viral ribonucleoproteins (RNP) (Ferron et al., 2011; Lokupathirage et al., 2021). Non-structural proteins (NSs) suppress the phosphorylation of interferon (IFN) regulatory factor 3 (IRF3) and interferon β (IFN-β), as well as their nuclear translocation. This action helps the virus evade the host's antiviral immune response and enhances its replication capabilities (Moriyama et al., 2018). In the SZ-202,101 strain, there are six mutation sites in the S protein, contrasting with the HB29 strain—one of which is situated in the N-lobe (K52R) of NP, with the remaining five located in the C-terminal region of NSs. The effects of these mutations on the virus's ability to evade the immune response and replicate efficiently need further investigation.

Through whole-genome sequencing and phylogenetic analysis of the isolated DBV virus, it was found that the virus could exhibit different genotypes and phenotypic characteristics under different climatic and environmental conditions (KANEKO et al., 2022). Genotyping is of great significance in virology research and disease prevention and control. Its essence is to classify viruses into different genetic types based on the regular differences among gene sequences, in order to describe the variation characteristics of virus genes. Mutations and recombination events of viruses may affect their virulence and pathogenicity, and are also closely related to human susceptibility to DBV, the severity of the disease, reliable and practical DBVand clinical outcomes. Therefore, further research on the genetic diversity of fever with thrombocytopenia syndrome virus (SFTSV) is of great value for clarifying the mechanism of clinical disease severity, predicting disease prognosis through genotyping, and guiding targeted treatment. We constructed a phylogenetic tree and performed an evolutionary typing analysis on the sequence from this case along with other published sequences. Seok et al. retrieved 196 complete DBV sequences from various countries and categorized these strains into types A through F (Yun et al., 2017). Their study found that the predominant DBV types in China were F (63 sequences), A (30), D (26), and B (17). In contrast, Japan (42 sequences) and South Korea (11) predominantly exhibited type B (Yun et al., 2017). Using the genotyping method from Seok’s study as a reference, we constructed a phylogenetic tree that included 28 reported DBV sequences along with SZ-202,101 from our study. We classified these sequences into six genotypes, labeled A through F. Our results indicated that the L and S segments of SZ-202,101 were classified as Type B, whereas the M segment was categorized as Type C. Thus, the overall genotype of SZ-202,101 was identified as BCB. This case represents the local occurrence of SFTS caused by the BCB genotype in Shenzhen, though it is not the first detection of this genotype in China.

The sequences of SZ-202,101 are most closely related to those of HB2012–197 strain from Hubei Province, with similarity rates of 99.50 %, 99.49 %, and 99.60 %, respectively. Consequently, HB2012–197 was classified as the BCB type, differing from Li’s study. Li’s study categorized HB2012–197 as the FEF type (Li et al., 2021). We hypothesize that the type B identified in our study corresponds to type F, while our type C aligns with their type E. The above research indicates that these genotyping methods rely on phylogenetic tree analysis. However, there are no specific standards for establishing evolutionary tree models, methods, and bootstrap, and there is a lack of standardized genotyping schemes. When multiple studies are involved, fragments from the same virus strain can be classified into different genotypes, resulting in unclear relationships among existing typing systems and making it difficult to standardize the results, which poses a huge challenge to disease research. Therefore, establishing a unified, reliable and practical DBV genotyping method is also a top priority in the research.

Gene reassortment is the exchange of entire RNA molecules between genetically related multi-segmented viruses, typically occurring when viruses co-infect the same host cell (Lin et al., 2023; Meng et al., 2015; Gerrard et al., 2004). DBV has three distinct RNA segments, and the reassortment of these segments is a key mechanism for the emergence of new viral genotypes. Our study found that reassortment occurred in the M segment of the virus, marking the first discovery of DBV reassortment in Shenzhen (Yang and Zhang, 2019). In this study, the DBV case with reassorted genes progressed from illness onset to recovery in 20 days. None of the case's family members, colleagues, or healthcare workers were infected, and the virus did not spread widely. This suggests that the gene segment reassortment in this case may not increase transmissibility or virulence.

Between 2010 and 2011, a serosurvey of Severe Fever with Thrombocytopenia Syndrome Virus (SFTSV) was conducted among hospitalized patients in Shenzhen who were clinically suspected of having Epidemic Hemorrhagic Fever (EHF) but tested negative for EHF IgM antibodies (a total of 91 serum samples), as well as among wild rodents (64 individuals) captured from Wutong Mountain (Cai et al., 2015). The results revealed serological evidence of both acute and past SFTSV infections among the hospitalized population. Meanwhile, indicators of past SFTSV infection were also detected in local brown rats (Rattus norvegicus). This investigation demonstrated that as early as this period, SFTSV had already established occult transmission in the Shenzhen region, with the virus circulating not only among humans but also among local rodent hosts.

The case worked and resided in close proximity to Yuanshan Scenic Area, surrounded by mountains on three sides. The case frequently collected herbs in the mountains without protective measures and had unprotected direct contact with free-roaming dogs within the factory premises. The case reported observing ticks parasitizing the dogs and crushing detached ticks. Both the working and living environments were confined to the same factory compound, which exhibited poor sanitary conditions and a high rodent density. Based on the case's exposure history and environmental characteristics, it is hypothesized that the infection was likely acquired through a tick bite either within the factory area or during activities in Yuanshan Scenic Area.

Although Shenzhen is not a recognized traditional natural focus of SFTS, and no previous detection of SFTSV has been reported in local ticks—supported by the negative results from the factory dogs and their parasitic ticks in this investigation—the lack of systematic collection of free-living ticks in Yuanshan Scenic Area represents a sampling limitation. Therefore, the possibility of infection via tick bite cannot be ruled out.

In conclusion, after a comprehensive investigation, this outbreak is confirmed as Shenzhen's local case of DBV in 2021 and the city's first documented instance of DBV gene reassortment. It is crucial to strengthen surveillance and detection efforts in areas that may trigger DBV outbreaks, including ticks, wildlife, and human cases. The main limitation of this study is the inability to identify the source of the DBV infection. The patient reported no recent foreign travel and no contact with known cases, leading to the preliminary conclusion that the infection was locally acquired. Additionally, several attempts to isolate the virus from positive serum samples failed, which hindered the acquisition of purified DBV strains. This setback hindered further assessment of pathogen transmission risks and investigation into the pathogenesis of the disease.

Therefore, we suspect that the negative DBV-IgG antibody result is due to the lack of DBV-IgG production at the time of his discharge on the 19th day of illness. We recommend that the detection of DBV infection should incorporate both RT-qPCR and DBV-IgM detection methods. This combined approach enhances detection sensitivity and allows for more accurate evaluation of the dynamic fluctuations in viral load and immune responses in SFTS patients.
