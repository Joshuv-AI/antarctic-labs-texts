Upadacitinib enhances platelet procoagulant responses in a subset of healthy individuals and patients with SLE: a pilot study
Authors: Veronica Tsegai, Halimat Lawal, Cini John, Ibukun Akinrinade, Stella Duncan, Antoine Dufour, Claire E H Barber, Glen Hazlewood, May Y Choi, Ejaife O Agbani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundJanus kinase inhibitors (JAKis) are emerging therapies for SLE, but concerns regarding thromboembolic risk persist. Clinical trials in SLE have not consistently demonstrated an increased risk of thrombotic events and the effects of JAK inhibition on platelet function remain incompletely understood.ObjectivesTo investigate whether the JAK1 inhibitor upadacitinib modulates platelet procoagulant membrane dynamics (PMD) in vitro and to explore the associated cytokine signatures.MethodsWhole blood from patients with SLE (n=10) and healthy controls (n=10) was preincubated with upadacitinib (0, 60 and 300 ng/mL). Platelet-rich plasma was analysed using confocal microscopy and Volocity software (Quorum Technology) to quantify PMD markers. We used Alexa Fluor 488 CD62p to detect degranulation, Alexa Fluor 568 Annexin-V to detect phosphatidylserine exposure and Alexa Fluor 647 PAC-1 to detect integrin αIIbβ3 activation. Participants were stratified as JAKi-activated or JAKi-null depending on whether PMD markers were amplified on JAK inhibition or remained unaffected. We then used Luminex xMAP technology to quantify 96 human plasma cytokines, chemokines and growth factors.ResultsPlatelet responses were heterogeneous. Approximately 30% of both groups exhibited increased platelet activation and procoagulant responses (‘JAKi-activated’), whereas the remainder remained unaffected. Platelets of patients with SLE demonstrated elevated baseline activation, characterised as preactivation or autoactivation. JAKi-activated phenotypes were associated with increased plasma levels of interleukin (IL)-4 and tumour necrosis factor-beta (TNF-β) after JAK inhibition. In vitro, we showed that recombinant IL-4 and TNF-β directly activated platelets or primed platelet responses to procoagulant agonists, supporting a potential indirect mechanism for the enhanced procoagulation observed in our JAKi-activated subpopulations.ConclusionsUpadacitinib was associated with enhanced platelet activation and procoagulant responses in vitro in a subset of individuals with SLE and healthy controls. These findings are preliminary and hypothesis-generating. Further studies are required to determine clinical relevance and validate stratification approaches.Keywords: Lupus Erythematosus, Systemic; Antibodies, Antiphospholipid; Autoimmune Diseases

WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC

Janus kinase inhibitors (JAKis) are promising therapies for SLE, but concerns remain about thromboembolic risk, particularly given the prothrombotic state and heightened platelet activation already present in SLE. However, clinical trials have not consistently shown an increased risk of thrombosis, and the direct effects of JAK inhibition on platelet procoagulant function are poorly understood.

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

This pilot study demonstrates that the JAK1 inhibitor upadacitinib enhances platelet activation and procoagulant responses in vitro in a distinct subset (∼30%) of both healthy individuals and patients with SLE, identifying a heterogeneous ‘JAKi-activated’ phenotype. It also links this response to cytokine changes, particularly interleukin-4 and tumour necrosis factor-beta, which can directly or indirectly prime platelet activation.

HOW THIS STUDY MIGHT AFFECT RESEARCH, PRACTICE OR POLICY

These findings suggest that thrombotic risk with JAKi may be driven by susceptible subpopulations rather than uniform drug effects, highlighting the need for stratification approaches such as platelet procoagulant membrane dynamics profiling. While not yet practice-changing, this work supports further studies to validate biomarkers for risk prediction and to inform safer, more personalised use of JAK inhibitors in SLE.

Introduction

The repertoire of effective therapies for SLE, a complex autoimmune disease with few effective treatments, is limited; however, Janus kinase inhibitors (JAKis) have shown promising results in other autoimmune conditions and are undergoing highly anticipated phase III clinical trials for SLE. A primary concern of JAK inhibitors demonstrated thus far in other conditions has been increased susceptibility to thromboembolic events, which may be amplified in SLE. Upadacitinib inhibits JAK1 kinase activity, which is essential for inflammatory signalling pathways,1 2 thereby implicating numerous regulatory mediators and highlighting its potential for treating moderate-to-severe SLE, for which it is currently being explored in clinical trials.3 Multiple cytokines can act as inflammatory signals and bind to transmembrane receptors, activating JAKs, which are recruited to the intracellular domain of the receptor on activation. The activation process requires ATP to phosphorylate JAKs, but upadacitinib competes with ATP, thereby blocking ATP’s ability to transfer phosphate groups and downstream protein phosphorylation. This inhibition prevents the phosphorylation and activation of signal transducer and activator of transcription (STAT) proteins, which normally dimerise and translocate to the cell nucleus to activate genes that produce inflammation-modulating proteins and regulatory cytokines. Upadacitinib modulates the chronically overactive inflammatory cascade and associated symptoms observed in autoimmune and highly inflammatory conditions such as SLE, rheumatoid arthritis (RA), psoriatic arthritis, atopic dermatitis, ulcerative colitis and in patients with giant-cell arteritis.14,7

However, while this selective JAK inhibitor is potentially a promising therapy for SLE, both JAK inhibitors and SLE can independently pose as risk factors for thromboembolic events.8 9 Patients with SLE are already at a 2–50-fold increase for myocardial infarction and stroke,10 11 even after accounting for traditional cardiovascular disease (CVD) factors. Indeed, CVD is a major cause of death among patients with SLE.11 Platelet activation is thought to be an important part of SLE pathogenesis, including the development of CVD and a prothrombotic state.12 Antiphospholipid antibodies (APLA), which are present in 30–40% of patients with SLE, play a role in platelet activation.13 These antibodies have been identified as significant risk factors for thrombotic events in several large multiethnic SLE cohorts (adjusted OR 1.57–4.71),14,20 and concerns regarding thromboembolic risk have emerged from both mechanistic and clinical studies. In myeloproliferative neoplasms, constitutive activation of JAK2 is a well-established driver of thrombosis, highlighting the central role of JAK-STAT signalling in platelet biology and haemostasis. However, clinical trials of JAK inhibitors in autoimmune diseases, including baricitinib and deucravacitinib, have not consistently demonstrated substantial increases in arterial or venous thrombotic events.21 Similarly, the phase 2 and extension trial of upadacitinib in SLE have not reported a marked increase in thrombotic outcomes.3 22 This apparent discrepancy between mechanistic plausibility and clinical observations underscores the need to investigate whether specific patient subpopulations may exhibit differential platelet responses to JAK inhibition. Platelets predominantly express JAK2, which plays a key role in thrombopoiesis and platelet activation signalling.23 24 JAK1 is expressed in platelets, where it plays a role in signalling, although it is often less dominant than JAK2 in platelet regulation.25 JAK1 expression in platelets is less well characterised and may be upregulated under inflammatory conditions,26 27 suggesting that JAK1 inhibition may influence platelet function indirectly via immune-mediated pathways.

Given that platelets are implicated in thrombogenicity, platelet-based studies are warranted to assess the risk of thromboembolism associated with JAK inhibitor treatment in patients with SLE. We hypothesised that JAK1 inhibition by upadacitinib may indirectly enhance platelet activation in a subset of individuals by modulating cytokine networks, leading to increased levels of platelet-priming or platelet-activating mediators such as interleukin (IL)-4 and tumour necrosis factor-beta (TNF-β). Accordingly, we evaluated the prothrombotic tendencies of patients with SLE by studying blood samples after in vitro exposure to the JAK inhibitor upadacitinib.

Methods

Patients with SLE and healthy controls

This study was a prospective cohort study of individuals who fulfilled the 2012 Systemic Lupus International Collaborating Clinics classification criteria for SLE28 and is approved by the University of Calgary Conjoint Health Research Board (REB23-0378). Eligibility criteria for patients with SLE included treatment with standard-dose hydroxychloroquine and moderate-severe SLE according to the SLE Disease Activity Index 2000 (SLEDAI-2K) score.29 Healthy controls included individuals who did not take any medications, had no chronic medical conditions and were not pregnant or nursing.

Participants’ binary classification

Participants were classified as ‘JAKi-activated’ if exposure to upadacitinib induced a statistically significant increase in at least two procoagulant membrane dynamics (PMD) markers (P-selectin expression, annexin V binding or PAC-1 binding) relative to untreated controls. Samples were classified as ‘JAKi-null’ if exposure to upadacitinib decreased or did not significantly change at least two PMD markers. This threshold was applied uniformly across samples; however, we acknowledge that this classification has not yet been validated across independent datasets.

Materials

Alexa Fluor 405 CD42b/GP1b alpha antibody (Cat#: NB500-511AF405) was from Bio-Techne. Alexa Fluor 488 anti-human CD62P (P-selectin) antibody (Cat#: 304916) and Alexa Fluor 647 anti-human CD41/CD61 (PAC-1) antibody (Cat#:362806) were from BioLegend (UK). Annexin-V, Alexa Fluor 568 conjugate (Cat#: A13202) and paraformaldehyde fixative solution (Cat#: J61984.AP) were from Thermo Fisher Scientific. Glass bottom 35 mm dishes (P35G-1.5-14-C) were obtained from MatTek Corporation (USA). Bovine serum albumin (BSA) (Cat#: A7906-50G) was from Sigma-Aldrich. Fibrillar collagen (Cat#: 385) was from Chrono-Log Corporation (USA). Upadacitinib (ABT-494; Cat#: S8162) was from Selleckchem, Houston, Texas, USA.

Upadacitinib pharmacokinetics and study concentrations

Upadacitinib exhibits dose-dependent pharmacokinetics and biphasic elimination with a terminal half-life of 9–14 hour following administration of the extended-release formulation.5 30 Upadacitinib administered as 36 mg immediate release or 30 mg extended release once-daily formulation yielded plasma concentrations of 277± (44.5 SD) ng/mL or 63.7± (21.1:SD) ng/mL.30 These doses are reported in clinical trials and are comparable to recommended RA treatment doses.5 6 30 This study exposed plasma platelets from healthy participants and patients with SLE to 60 and 300 ng/mL upadacitinib based on clinically observed plasma levels.5

Blood sample treatment

JAK inhibitors have not yet been approved for the clinical treatment of SLE, but trials are ongoing. For this reason, we collected whole blood via venipuncture from both patients with SLE and healthy controls. Plasma platelets obtained as described below, from eligible patients with SLE and healthy controls, were from whole blood that was either untreated or preincubated with either IL-4, TNFβ and/or upadacitinib (0, 60, 300 ng/mL) for 30 min before experimentation.

Platelet-rich and platelet-poor plasma preparation

Blood collected into blue-capped sodium citrate tubes (0.109 M / 3.2%) was centrifuged at 180 g for 17 min to prepare platelet-rich plasma (PRP), the supernatant. Additionally, PRP was centrifuged at 600 g for 10 min to obtain the platelet-poor plasma supernatant.

Confocal microscopy

Confocal images were acquired using a Nikon A1R laser scanning confocal microscope. The system is equipped with 405 nm, 488 nm, 561 nm and 640 nm solid-state lasers and 450/50, 525/50, 595/50 and 700/75 filter sets, respectively. The microscope has a fully motorised inverted frame, a high-precision stage for multifocus and multipoint imaging, a piezo drive and a Nikon Perfect Focus System for drift-free imaging. Images were captured at Nyquist using Nikon NIS-Elements imaging software and an oil-immersion Plan Apo Lambda objective lens (60×; numerical aperture: 1.4; working distance: 0.13 mm). The acquisition employed fast/sensitive four-colour and transmitted-light imaging. The acquisition setting was kept constant at high-speed/high-definition resonant scanning (up to 1024×1024 pixels). In some experiments (4D), z-stack images (xyz, 3D) were acquired over time (t).

Platelet procoagulant membrane dynamics sample preparation and imaging

We monitored platelet activation and degranulation using a fluorescent antibody that detects CD62P (P-selectin) expression on the platelet membrane. We quantified annexin V binding to the platelet membrane as an indicator of phosphatidylserine (PS) externalisation, and we used the PAC-1 antibody to detect integrin αIIbβ3 (Glycoprotein IIb/IIIa) activation. This critical platelet protein mediates platelet aggregation by binding fibrinogen. Glass-bottom MatTek dishes were precoated with serum albumin (BSA) or fibrillar collagen. Aliquots of plasma platelets recalcified to 1 mmol/L with CaCl2 were preincubated (15 mins) with fluorescent probes (as indicated in figures and legends) and added onto the precoated MatTek dishes. After 45 min of incubation, adherent plasma platelets were fixed with 1% paraformaldehyde for 15 min. High-resolution fluorescent images of platelets adhered to BSA-coated or agonist (collagen)-coated surfaces were obtained at 25°C. Image resolution was improved by the restoration complement of Volocity imaging Software Suite and analysed using the same software (Quorum Technologies, Canada).

Cytokines measured in plasma likely reflected contributions from multiple cellular sources, including leucocytes, platelets and other circulating immune cells. Data analysis and statistics

Data from each experiment were presented as box-and-whisker plots, with the minimum and maximum values shown. The median and the IQR were represented by the horizontal line through the box and the box height, respectively. Data were analysed using GraphPad Prism V.11 (San Diego, California, USA). Given the small sample size, non-parametric tests were used where appropriate. For cytokine analysis, mean fold change relative to baseline was used to generate a heat map comparing cytokine levels in JAKi-null and JAKi-activated samples across the SLE and healthy control groups. Cytokine analyses were exploratory, and no correction for multiple comparisons (eg, false discovery rate) was applied.

Platelet procoagulant membrane dynamic analysis

In 70% of healthy controls tested, upadacitinib doses up to 300 ng/mL, used to replicate physiological steady-state concentrations, did not cause significant platelet degranulation, procoagulation or integrin activation compared with untreated platelets (figure 1Ai, Bi). However, in 30% of healthy controls, upadacitinib at 60 and 300 ng/mL induced platelet degranulation, PS exposure and αIIbβ3 integrin activation (up to sevenfold. figure 1Aii, Bii, iii). Notably, platelets derived from patients with SLE were already somewhat activated in vivo (auto/preactivated) before exposure to upadacitinib (figure 2Ai), with up to a 10-fold increase in membrane P-selectin expression compared with untreated healthy control platelets. Similarly, annexin-V and PAC-1 binding intensities were higher in SLE platelets than in healthy control platelets at baseline (figure 2Bi vs 1Bi; see online supplemental video S1). Despite this preactivated or autoactivated status of platelets in SLE, upadacitinib (60 and 300 ng/mL) induced an additional increase in platelet P-selectin expression, annexin-V binding and PAC-1 in 30% of patients with SLE (figure 2Aii, Bii, iii). We used this information to categorise patients with SLE and healthy controls as either JAKi-null (figure 1Ai, 2Ai; 70%) or JAKi-activated (figure 1Bii–iii, 2Bii-iii; 30%), based on whether preincubation with upadacitinib resulted in minimal changes or increased expression of markers of platelet activation, procoagulation and/or aggregation. In figures 1 and 2, we used one to two representative healthy controls and patient samples to demonstrate the phenotype of each category. In sum, the platelet PMD response to JAK inhibition is heterogeneous; platelets from a subset (30%) of healthy controls generated a procoagulant and aggregatory phenotype with upadacitinib treatment, and platelets from a subset (30%) of patients with SLE showed an amplified procoagulant response beyond baseline preactivation.

Plasma multiplex immunoassays

We used a multiplex immunoassay to determine whether differential overexpression of specific plasma chemokines and cytokines correlated with our categorisation of healthy controls and patients with SLE into JAKi-null or JAKi-activated phenotypes. The heatmaps in figure 3 are derived by normalising individual participant post-treatment plasma cytokine levels to untreated levels to ensure only the effect of the drug, and not the disease, is described and illustrated. A comparison of the cytokine levels in the plasma of healthy controls showed an increase in FGF-2, or Fibroblast Growth Factor 2,31 RANTES (CCL5),32 ENA-7833 and ILs (IL-1β, IL-16, IL-23), and Granzyme B in JAKi-activated healthy controls but not in JAKi-null healthy controls after exposure to upadacitinib (figure 3A). By similar analysis, plasma levels of sCD40L, EGF, ILs (IL-4, IL-12p70, IL-17E/IL-25), MCP-3,33 TNFβ, April and IFNs (IFN-γ and IFN-ω) were significantly increased in JAKi-activated but not in JAKi-null patients with SLE. (figure 3B). Concurrently, levels of several cytokines, including IL-3, IL-4, IL-5 and IL-7, were suppressed in both the ‘JAKi-activated’ and ‘JAKi-null’ healthy controls (figure 3).31,33 Our literature search revealed that some of these cytokines have been found within platelet granules31,33 (figure 3, Table insert).

Interleukin-4 and tumour necrosis factor-beta prime and/or activate platelets

To study the effect of plasma cytokines such as IL-4 and TNF-β, elevated by upadacitinib treatment, on platelet PMD, platelets derived from our healthy controls were preincubated with recombinant IL-4 and assessed for markers of PMD. At 50 pg/mL, IL-4 did not alter platelet PMD but instead increased platelet sensitivity to glycoprotein VI activation by collagen (figure 4). This may suggest that IL-4 at 50 pg/mL may prime platelets and increase their susceptibility to activation by agonists. Furthermore, IL-4 at 100 and 300 pg/mL directly activated platelets in a concentration-dependent manner (figure 4A). In experiments with recombinant TNFβ, we observed increased P-selectin expression in platelets of healthy controls preincubated with TNF-β at concentrations of 1, 3, 10, 30, 100 and 300 pg/mL. While TNFβ caused platelet degranulation at 1–10 pg/mL, higher concentrations (30–300 pg/mL) were required to induce procoagulation (figure 4C,D). Platelet activation at 1–10 pg/mL TNFβ involved lamellipodial spreading, and ballooned phenotypes were observed after preincubation with 300 pg/mL TNFβ. At this high TNFβ concentration, procoagulant fibrin mesh was visible in our medium (figure 4C).

Discussion

Studies have evaluated the pharmacokinetics, safety and tolerability of upadacitinib (ABT-494) in healthy volunteers and subjects with several autoimmune diseases.30 Upadacitinib exhibits dose-dependent pharmacokinetics and biphasic elimination with a terminal half-life of 9–14 hour following administration of the extended-release formulation.5 30 Upadacitinib administered as a 36 mg immediate release or 30 mg extended release once-daily formulation yielded plasma concentrations of 277± (44.5 SD) ng/mL or 63.7± (21.1:SD) ng/mL.30 These doses are reported in clinical trials and are comparable to recommended RA treatment doses.5 6 30 The concentrations of upadacitinib used in this study are within clinically relevant plasma levels and exceed the reported IC50 for JAK1 inhibition.2 However, we did not directly assess JAK1 inhibition or downstream STAT phosphorylation in platelets. Therefore, we cannot exclude the possibility of off-target kinase effects. Given that platelets predominantly express JAK2 rather than JAK1, the observed procoagulant effects are more likely mediated indirectly through cytokine modulation rather than direct inhibition of platelet JAK signalling. There is currently no clinical dose recommended for SLE. Here, we exposed plasma platelets to 0, 60 and 300 ng/mL upadacitinib in vitro and demonstrated the sensitivity of the platelet PMD test to the prothrombotic tendencies of upadacitinib. The JAK1 and JAK2 enzymes are key components of the JAK-STAT pathway, which is known to regulate normal platelet homeostasis and immune cell function.26 34 Triggering the JAK-STAT pathway can induce platelet degranulation, integrin activation, aggregation and thrombus formation.23 While JAK-STAT pathway inhibitors are potentially a promising therapy for SLE, both JAK inhibitors and SLE independently pose as risk factors for thromboembolic events.8 9 In this study, we evaluated the prothrombotic responses of blood samples from patients with SLE and healthy participants to the JAKi upadacitinib and identified two distinct phenotypes.

The stratification of our study population is based on platelet PMD, which demonstrates either an insignificant response to upadacitinib or a substantial activation, leading us to categorise each participant as either ‘JAKi-null’ or ‘JAKi-activated’ phenotype, respectively. The results yielded an approximately 70/30 split in both healthy controls and patients with SLE. This dichotomy reveals inter-individual variability in the response to JAK-STAT inhibition, which could influence thrombotic risk during JAKi therapy. In 70% of healthy controls, upadacitinib concentrations up to 300 ng/mL produced no significant changes in platelet PMD, suggesting a mostly minimal direct effect on platelets at those concentrations (figure 1Bi). However, in 30% of healthy controls, upadacitinib at 60 and 300 ng/mL induced platelet degranulation, PS exposure and significant integrin αIIbβ3 activation (up to sevenfold or more; figure 1Bii, iii). This indicates a subpopulation of healthy controls with heightened platelet sensitivity and/or a JAK-inhibition milieu that stimulates platelet activation pathways, leading to procoagulant membrane remodelling. In healthy controls, the JAKi-activated subgroup showed elevated plasma FGF-2, CCL5, ENA-78, IL-1β, IL-16, IL-23 and Granzyme B (figure 3). However, the localisation of most of these chemokines and cytokines within platelet granules supports a model whereby enhanced platelet activation or preactivation, and the associated granule release, may account for or contribute to circulating cytokine levels.

Large randomised controlled trials, including ORAL (Oral Rheumatoid Arthritis Trial) Surveillance, have identified increased cardiovascular risk associated with certain JAK inhibitors.9 35 The findings from our pilot study should be interpreted as mechanistic and hypothesis-generating rather than practice-changing. Although the PMD assay identified a subset of participants with enhanced platelet responses to upadacitinib, this pilot study was not designed to determine whether PMD responder status predicts clinical thrombotic events. Accordingly, these data do not support immediate approvals for patient stratification or thrombotic risk monitoring in routine clinical practice. This distinction is important in the broader evidence base.

The Food and Drug Administration boxed warning for tofacitinib, baricitinib and upadacitinib should be interpreted in the context of the ORAL Surveillance trial,9 35 which was conducted in patients with RA aged 50 years or older with at least one cardiovascular risk factor, and in which smoking history and prior cardiovascular or thrombotic events were important risk-enrichment features. Notably, the warning for baricitinib and upadacitinib was based on class extrapolation rather than on an analogous dedicated safety trial in SLE. In our pilot study, the SLE cohort was younger overall (mean age 41.8 years), 20% were people who smoke and 10% had a personal history of blood clots, but these characteristics were not analysed by PMD responder subgroup. Therefore, we cannot conclude that the approximately 30% JAKi-Activated subgroup identified here corresponds to the clinical high-risk phenotype described in ORAL Surveillance. Rather, our findings should be viewed as a preliminary, mechanistic signal that may complement, but does not yet explain, the population-level safety observations from RA and SLE trials. Published SLE trials of upadacitinib, baricitinib and deucravacitinib have not shown a clear, substantial excess of arterial or venous thrombotic events,22 although those studies were not designed to resolve rare-event risk within biologically susceptible subgroups.22 Nevertheless, the PMD high-responder or ‘JAKi-Activated’ phenotype identified here requires prospective validation in larger, clinically phenotyped cohorts with adjudicated thrombotic outcomes before any regulatory or clinical application can be considered.

Platelet preactivation or autoactivation is a condition in which a proportion of circulating platelets shows variable degrees of biochemical and morphological features of activation in the absence of overt stimuli.36,41 Preactivated platelets express P-selectin, eternalise PS and/or show partial conformational changes in surface glycoproteins such as integrin αIIbβ3, and may or may not form aggregates. In this study, increased P-selectin expression on platelet membranes in patients with SLE indicated degranulation of α-granules, a hallmark of platelet activation,42 43 and is consistent with well-studied prothrombotic states and heightened platelet reactivity reported in systemic autoimmune conditions.44 45 Notably, upadacitinib significantly increased platelet activation and procoagulation in 30% of patients with SLE, despite their preactivated or autoactivated baseline status. This suggests that JAK-STAT signalling may contribute to platelet activation in the majority of patients with SLE; paradoxically, however, JAK inhibition may amplify procoagulation in a proportion of patients with SLE.27 Furthermore, elevated plasma levels of sCD40L, EGF, IFN-γ, IL-4, IL-5, MCP-1, TNF-β and APRIL in JAKi-activated patients with SLE delineate a prothrombotic cytokine signature associated with the JAKi-activated phenotype. The elevated plasma concentrations of IL-4 and TNF-β, two cytokines which have not been shown to be stored in platelets, imply some level of crosstalk between circulating immune mediators and platelet function in whole blood exposed to upadacitinib, raising prothrombotic risks for some patients with SLE.

The SLEDAI-2K is a widely used clinical tool for assessing disease activity in SLE, based on 24 clinical and laboratory findings over the past 10 days. This tool may help doctors assess disease severity, guide treatment and track disease activity in patients, with higher scores indicating higher disease activity. In this pilot study, SLEDAI-2K did not capture prothrombotic tendencies after JAKi treatment, nor did SLEDAI-2K indicate a correlation with APLA positivity or the extent of disease-driven platelet preactivation. Surprisingly, neither the number nor the titre of APLA showed any correlation with our PMD response-based stratification in this small-sample pilot study (table 1B). We hypothesise that PMD analysis may capture a prothrombotic mechanism not explained by conventional clinical risk indices, but we acknowledge that the lack of association between SLEDAI-2K scores and PMD stratification may be a reflection of the limited statistical power and restricted variability in disease activity within this cohort rather than true independence.

Our results imply a bidirectional interaction in which some cytokines may directly activate and/or prime platelets and amplify PMD, as demonstrated by IL-4 and TNF-β. In turn, platelet degranulation associated with activation and preactivation contributes to elevated plasma cytokine levels. Possibly, IL-4 at 50 pg/mL may lower the agonist/receptor threshold for degranulation and procoagulant membrane remodelling, although the exact mechanism of this priming requires further investigation in future studies. Thus, platelets exposed to IL-4 are susceptible to heightened activation by plasma procoagulant agonists, such as fibrinogen and fibronectin, which are preponderant or elevated in patients with SLE.46 We also found that TNF-β can directly activate platelets; low concentrations induced platelet degranulation, and higher concentrations augmented procoagulation in the presence of agonists. While the plasma levels of IL-4 and TNF-β cytokines were not elevated in healthy controls, a related trend was observed; the JAKi-null healthy controls showed suppression of both IL-4 and TNF-β, while levels were unchanged in the JAKi-activated healthy controls subgroup after upadacitinib treatment (figure 3). Comparably, in patients with SLE, the JAKi-null subgroup showed unchanged levels of IL-4 and TNF-β. In contrast, plasma levels were elevated in the JAKi-activated subgroup, suggesting that, in both healthy controls and SLE, failure to suppress IL-4 and TNF-β significantly can lead to elevated plasma levels of these cytokines. This suggests that both IL-4 and TNF-β are not only proinflammatory in patients with SLE but may also contribute to the increased susceptibility to thromboembolic events previously reported.10 11 Upadacitinib is a selective JAK1 inhibitor, whereas JAK2 plays a more direct role in thrombopoiesis and platelet signalling. Therefore, the procoagulant effects observed in this study are unlikely to result from direct inhibition of platelet JAK2 signalling. Instead, our data support an indirect mechanism whereby JAK1 inhibition alters cytokine networks, leading to increased levels of platelet-activating mediators such as IL-4 and TNF-β. Overall, the increased platelet PMD with upadacitinib exposure suggests a heterogeneous prothrombotic effect in a subset of patients with SLE and questions the notion that JAK inhibitors merely suppress cytokine signalling without functional consequences in platelets. Our data assume the importance of stratification and the need to monitor thrombotic risk when JAKi are used in patients with SLE. We also reveal that active platelet PMD and the relative overexpression of IL-4 and TNF-β during JAK inhibition may be indicative of increased risk of procoagulation and intravascular thrombosis. Our findings suggest that a subset of individuals may exhibit enhanced platelet procoagulant responses to JAK inhibition. If validated in larger prospective studies with clinical endpoints, PMD profiling could potentially contribute to patient stratification and risk assessment prior to JAKi therapy. This study focused exclusively on upadacitinib, a selective JAK1 inhibitor. Whether similar platelet responses occur with other JAKi, such as baricitinib or tofacitinib, remains unknown and warrants investigation in future comparative studies. Furthermore, we did not directly measure JAK expression or phosphorylation status in platelets. Future studies incorporating phospho-specific antibodies (eg, p-JAK1 Tyr1034/1035) will be important to determine whether platelet responses correlate with direct JAK signalling activity or reflect off-target mechanisms.

Conclusion

Upadacitinib was associated with enhanced platelet activation and procoagulant responses in vitro in a subset of individuals with SLE and healthy controls. These findings are preliminary and hypothesis-generating, and require validation in larger, clinically integrated studies to determine their relevance to thrombotic risk in vivo.

Study limitations

This study has several important limitations. First, the sample size (n=10 per group) is small, and no formal power calculation was performed; thus, findings should be considered hypothesis-generating. Second, the age mismatch between healthy controls and patients with SLE represents a potential confounder, as platelet function and cytokine profiles are age dependent.47 48 Third, all patients with SLE were receiving hydroxychloroquine, which has known antiplatelet effects and may influence PMD measurements. Additionally, the use of biological therapies (eg, belimumab, anifrolumab) introduces heterogeneity in immune signalling that was not controlled for. Fourth, the in vitro spiking model does not recapitulate in vivo pharmacokinetics, including high plasma protein binding (∼97%), tissue distribution or endothelial interactions. Fifth, the classification of participants into JAKi-null versus JAKi-activated phenotypes, while quantitatively defined, requires validation for reproducibility. Sixth, the small number of JAKi-activated patients means that individual outliers (eg, triple-positive APLA patient L-129) may disproportionately influence results, as sensitivity analyses could not be performed. Finally, the multiplex cytokine analysis involved multiple comparisons without correction, as this was an exploratory analysis; however, this increased the risk of false-positive findings. Importantly, PMD changes observed in vitro cannot be directly extrapolated to clinical thrombotic risk.
