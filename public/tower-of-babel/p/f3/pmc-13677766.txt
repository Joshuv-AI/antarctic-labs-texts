B3GALT4-dependent gangliosides are required for normal late spermatogenesis and male fertility
Authors: Shino NAGATA, Saki NISHIOKA, Masahito IKAWA, Manabu OZAWA
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGangliosides play important roles in membrane organization and cellular signaling. Although their involvement in spermatogenesis has been suggested, the specific functions of individual ganglioside species during each stage of germ cell development remain poorly understood. In this study, we generated beta-1,3-galactosyltransferase 4(B3galt4) knockout (KO) mice to investigate the role of B3GALT4-dependent ganglioside in male germ cell development. Immunostaining analyses demonstrated that B3galt4 deficiency caused selective alterations in ganglioside distribution. Although testis size did not differ significantly between KO and control mice, KO males were completely infertile. Histological examination revealed that spermatogenesis in KO mice proceeded to the elongating spermatid stage; however, abnormalities were observed during the late stages of spermatogenesis, including a reduced number of elongating/elongated spermatids and multinucleated giant cells in subsets of seminiferous tubules. Consistent with these observations, the number of sperm recovered from the cauda epididymis was markedly decreased, accompanied by morphological abnormalities and impaired motility. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that B3galt4 deficiency compromises late spermatogenesis and sperm function, resulting in male infertility. Our results further suggest that B3GALT4-dependent gangliosides have stage-dependent roles during late spermatogenesis and sperm development.Keywords: B3galt4, Ganglioside, Male infertility, Spermatogenesis, Sperm function

Spermatogenesis is a highly regulated process in which spermatogonia differentiate into spermatozoa. During meiosis, spermatocytes give rise to haploid round spermatids, which then undergo dynamic morphological remodeling, including nuclear condensation, flagellum formation, and cytoplasmic removal, to become elongating and elongated spermatids and eventually spermatozoa. Following their release into the lumen of the seminiferous tubules, spermatozoa migrate to the epididymis, where they undergo further maturation to acquire motility and fertilizing capacity. Defects in these processes are known to cause reduced sperm counts, abnormal sperm morphology, and impaired motility, resulting in male infertility [1, 2].

Extensive membrane remodeling and structural reorganization occur during the late stages of spermatogenesis [3]. In particular, spermatozoa possess highly specialized membrane structures, and precise regulation of lipid composition is essential for their formation and functional maintenance [4].

Gangliosides are glycosphingolipids that contain one or more sialic acid residues in their glycan moieties and are localized mainly in the outer leaflet of the plasma membrane, where they contribute to the formation of lipid rafts. A wide variety of ganglioside species have been identified, and these molecules are known to participate in signal transduction and in the regulation of membrane protein localization [5]. In addition, gangliosides exhibit tissue- and cell type-specific expression patterns and perform distinct physiological functions depending on the biological context [6,7,8,9]. Although gangliosides are abundant primarily in the central nervous system, particularly the brain, their expression has also been detected in the testis, suggesting potential roles in spermatogenesis. Indeed, mice lacking the ganglioside synthase beta-1,4-N-acetyl-galactosaminyltransferase 1 (B4galnt1) exhibit spermatogenic arrest at the round spermatid stage and consequent male infertility [10]. These findings indicate that gangliosides are required for the progression of late spermatogenesis. However, previous studies have primarily demonstrated the overall requirement for gangliosides, whereas the specific roles of individual ganglioside species at distinct stages of spermatogenesis remain unclear.

Moreover, gangliosides are unlikely to function independently [11, 12]; rather, multiple ganglioside species may act cooperatively within lipid rafts, which serve as membrane microdomains. It is therefore conceivable that multiple ganglioside species act cooperatively or compensate for one another. Nevertheless, the extent of their functional specialization and redundancy has not been fully characterized. In particular, how individual ganglioside species contribute to different biological processes, such as the progression of spermatogenesis and the development of sperm function, remains unclear.

To address these questions, targeted disruption of specific enzymes involved in ganglioside biosynthesis provides a useful strategy for selectively eliminating defined subsets of gangliosides. In the present study, we focused on the ganglioside synthase gene beta-1,3-galactosyltransferase 4 (B3galt4) and generated knockout (KO) mice. In this model, downstream gangliosides are expected to be lost, whereas intermediate gangliosides, including GA2, GM2, GD2, and GT2, are expected to be preserved. Using these KO mice, we investigated the roles of B3galt4-dependent gangliosides in late spermatogenesis, sperm morphology, sperm motility, and male fertility.

Materials and Methods

Animals

All mouse experiments were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of the University of Tokyo (approval no. A2024IMS005) and Osaka University (approval no. R03-01-1) and were conducted in accordance with institutional guidelines and the ARRIVE guidelines (https://arriveguidelines.org). B3galt4-deficient mice were originally generated at Osaka University on a BDF1 genetic background. Frozen fertilized embryos obtained by crossing homozygous deficient females with heterozygous deficient males were transferred at our institution to establish the mouse colony. Thereafter, Het and KO mice were maintained by intercrossing. Mice were housed under specific pathogen-free conditions at the Institute of Medical Science, The University of Tokyo, in a temperature-controlled environment under a 12-h light/dark cycle with ad libitum access to food and water. Some experiments were conducted at Osaka University. Unless otherwise indicated, all experiments were performed using 8-week-old mice.

Generation of B3galt4 KO mice using the CRISPR/Cas9 system

B3galt4 KO mice were generated using the CRISPR/Cas9 genome editing system. gRNAs were designed using CRISPRdirect software (https://crispr.dbcls.jp/) to minimize potential off-target effects (https://crispr.dbcls.jp/) [13] (Table 1). Mouse production was performed at Osaka University as previously described [14,15] using mice on a BDF1 genetic background. The established B3galt4 mutant line was subsequently maintained by crossing B3galt4 homozygous mutant females with B3galt4 heterozygous mutant males.

Genotyping and PCR analysis

Genotyping was performed by PCR using genomic DNA extracted from mouse toe biopsy samples by alkaline lysis. Wild-type and knockout alleles were amplified using allele-specific primer sets (Table 2). PCR products were separated by agarose gel electrophoresis. Bands corresponding to the wild-type and knockout alleles were detected at 468 bp and 710 bp, respectively, whereas both bands were detected in heterozygous mice.

Quantitative PCR analysis

Total RNA was extracted from the testes of Het and KO mice using NucleoSpin RNA Plus (740984.250; Takara Bio, Shiga, Japan) and reverse-transcribed into cDNA using SuperScript VILO Master Mix (11755-500; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Quantitative PCR was performed using THUNDERBIRD SYBR qPCR Mix (QPS-201; TOYOBO, Osaka, Japan) and the primers listed in Table 3. Relative B3galt4 expression levels were normalized to Gapdh and calculated using the ΔΔCt method.

Mating test

Mating tests were conducted at Osaka University. Each male mouse was housed with wild-type female mice for 3 months, and fertility was assessed by counting copulatory plugs, deliveries, and offspring.

Sperm count and sperm analysis

To evaluate sperm count, the cauda epididymis was collected and incised in liquid paraffin (26137-85; Nacalai Tesque, Kyoto, Japan) to allow spermatozoa to swim out into KSOM medium (ARK Resource, Kumamoto, Japan). After incubation at 37°C for 10 min, 10 μl of sperm suspension was collected from the upper layer of the medium and used for sperm counting with an automated cell counter (Luna FL Automated Fluorescence Cell Counter, Logos Biosystems, Gunpo-si, Republic of Korea).

For morphological and motility observation, 10 μl of sperm suspension was placed on a glass slide, and sperm morphology and movement were observed using a bright-field microscope (BX53; Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). For morphological observation, the samples were cooled at 4°C for 15 min after being placed onto the slide to reduce sperm motility. At least 100 spermatozoa were evaluated per animal for morphology assessment. Animals in which fewer than 100 spermatozoa could be evaluated after examination of 9 microscopic fields were excluded from the morphology analysis.

Sperm motility analysis was performed at Osaka University using the CEROS II sperm analysis system (software version 1.5; Hamilton Thorne Biosciences, Beverly, MA, USA) as described previously [15]. Briefly, spermatozoa suspended in TYH medium [16], prepared according to a previously described formulation [17], were analyzed after 10 and 120 min of incubation. The following motility parameters were evaluated: curvilinear velocity (VCL), straight-line velocity (VSL), and average path velocity (VAP).

Histological and immunofluorescence analyses

Testes and epididymides were fixed in Bouin solution (HT10132-1L; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) over night at 4°C, followed by dehydration in 70% [v/v] ethanol over night. The dehydrated tissues were then embedded in paraffin and sectioned at a thickness of 4 μm. For hematoxylin and periodic acid–Schiff (H-PAS) staining, sections were deparaffinized using Lemosol A (128-04417; FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical, Osaka, Japan), and rehydrated with ethanol (14713-53; Nacalai Tesque). Sections were subjected to periodic acid–Schiff staining using 1% periodic acid solution (40913; Muto Pure Chemicals, Tokyo, Japan) and Cold Schiff reagent (40932; Muto Pure Chemicals), followed by counterstaining with New Hematoxylin Type M (30141; Muto Pure Chemicals). After dehydration through an ethanol series and clearing in Lemosol A, sections were mounted with Mount-Quick mounting medium (DM-01; Daido Sangyo, Tokyo, Japan) and examined under a microscope (BX-43; Evident, Tokyo, Japan). For immunofluorescence staining, tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (09154-85; Nacalai Tesque), embedded in OCT compound (45833; Sakura Finetek, Tokyo, Japan), frozen, and sectioned at a thickness of 7 μm. After washing with PBS for 10 min and twice in Tris-buffered saline containing 0.025% [v/v] Triton X-100 (TBS-Triton X, TBS, 12748-31; Nacalai Tesque, Triton X-100, 35501-15; Nacalai Tesque) for 5 min each to remove OCT compound, sections were blocked for 1 h at room temperature in TBS containing 1% [w/v] bovine serum albumin (BSA) (A7030-100G; Sigma-Aldrich) and 10% [v/v] normal donkey serum (017-000-121; Jackson ImmunoResearch, West Grove, PA, USA). Sections were then incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies diluted in TBS containing 1% BSA. Immunoreactivity was visualized using Alexa Fluor 488-, 555-, or 647-conjugated species-specific secondary antibodies. The sections were mounted with ProLong Glass Antifade Mountant with NucBlue Stain (P36983; Thermo Fisher Scientific). Fluorescence images were acquired using an epifluorescence microscope (BZ-X710; Keyence, Osaka, Japan). Antibodies and reagents used in this study are listed in Table 4.

Seminiferous tubule staging

Seminiferous tubule staging was performed using H-PAS-stained sections and classified into stages I–XII according to acrosome morphology and germ cell arrangement [18, 19]. Tubules containing multinucleated giant cells, reduced numbers of elongated spermatids, abnormally rounded elongating spermatids, or mislocalized spermatids were classified as abnormal seminiferous tubules.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) of three or more biological replications. Comparisons of body weight, testis weight, and testis weight/body weight ratio were performed using Welch’s t-test. Comparisons of sperm count and sperm morphology data were performed using the Brunner–Munzel test. A P-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10 (version 10.5) and R (version 4.2.2).

Results

Generation and validation of B3galt4 KO mice

To generate B3galt4 KO mice, an approximately 1.2-kb genomic region encompassing the single cording exon of B3galt4 was deleted using the CRISPR/Cas9 system (Fig. 1A). Sequence analysis confirmed that the targeted deletion was introduced as designed (Fig. 1B). Genomic PCR analysis further verified the deletion (Fig. 1C). In addition, quantitative PCR analysis demonstrated a marked reduction in B3galt4 expression in the testes of KO mice (Fig. 1D). Together, these results confirmed the successful generation of B3galt4 KO mice.

Generation and validation of B3galt4 KO mice. (A) Schematic diagram of CRISPR/Cas9-mediated deletion of the B3galt4 locus. B3galt4 consists of a single coding exon, and an approximately 1.2-kb region encompassing this exon was deleted. (B) Sequencing analysis results of the deleted region. The deletion was confirmed as designed. (C) Genotyping PCR analysis. The wild-type and knockout alleles were detected as 468-bp and 710-bp bands, respectively. (D) Relative B3galt4 mRNA expression levels measured by qPCR. B3galt4 mRNA expression was markedly reduced in KO testes.

Altered ganglioside distribution in B3galt4 KO mice

To evaluate the effect of B3galt4 deficiency on ganglioside distribution, the localization of gangliosides located upstream and downstream of the B3GALT4-dependent biosynthetic step was analyzed by immunostaining. The upstream ganglioside GD2 was detected in both Het and KO testes, although its signal appeared to be increased in KO testes (Fig. 2A). In contrast, the downstream ganglioside GD1b was undetectable in KO testes (Fig. 2B). These findings indicate that B3galt4 deficiency alters the distribution of B3GALT4-dependent gangliosides in the testis, consistent with disruption of downstream ganglioside biosynthesis.

Changes in ganglioside localization due to B3galt4 deficiency. (A) Immunofluorescence staining for the upstream ganglioside GD2 in testes from Het and KO mice. GD2 signals were detected in both Het and KO testes and appeared to be increased in KO testes. (B) Immunofluorescence staining for the downstream ganglioside GD1b in testes from Het and KO mice. GD1b signals were detected in Het testes but were undetectable in KO testes. Yellow arrowheads indicate GD2-positive germ cells (A) and GD1b-positive germ cells (B). SOX9 and TRA98 were used as markers for Sertoli cells and germ cells, respectively. Scale bar: 50 μm.

B3galt4 KO mice exhibit male infertility

To assess male fertility, mating tests were performed using KO males. KO males exhibited complete infertility (Fig. 3A). However, no significant differences were observed in gross testis size or in the testis-to-body weight ratio (Figs. 3B, 3C). Sperm collected from the cauda epididymis by the swim-up method were counted, and a significant decrease in the number of recovered sperm was observed in KO mice (Fig. 3D). Furthermore, morphological analysis revealed various sperm abnormalities, including defective hook formation, bent-neck sperm, and separation of the head and tail resulting in head-only and tail-only structures (Fig. 3E, Supplementary Fig. 1). The proportion of morphologically normal sperm was significantly decreased in KO mice (Fig. 3F). Sperm morphological abnormalities, including bent-neck sperm and separation of the head and tail resulting in head-only and tail-only structures, were frequently observed in KO mice. Computer aided sperm analysis (CASA) further demonstrated that sperm derived from KO mice exhibited markedly impaired motility (Fig. 3G).

Spermatogenesis proceeds to late stages despite abnormalities in seminiferous tubules and sperm morphology

To evaluate spermatogenesis, testicular sections were analyzed by H-PAS staining. Elongating spermatids were detected in KO mice, indicating that spermatogenesis proceeded to late stages despite B3galt4 deficiency (Fig. 4A). Consistent with the reduced number of sperm recovered by the swim-up assay, fewer spermatozoa were observed in the cauda epididymis of KO mice. Histological abnormalities were observed within seminiferous tubules of KO mice, including reduced numbers of elongating/elongated spermatids and disruption of normal tubule architecture (Fig. 4B). Abnormal tubules exhibited multinucleated giant cells, mislocalization of elongating spermatids, and reduced numbers of elongating spermatids. In addition, although infrequent, spermatids with abnormally rounded heads were also observed (Figs. 4C–4F). Mislocalized elongating spermatids were identified based on their presence within inner germ cell layers, such as among round spermatids or between spermatocytes and round spermatids, rather than their normal position adjacent to the tubular lumen (Fig. 4F). Staging analysis of seminiferous tubules showed a higher proportion of abnormal tubules in KO mice compared with Het mice (Fig. 4G). When all stages were combined, approximately 7% of seminiferous tubules were classified as abnormal in the Het group, whereas this proportion increased to approximately 24% in the KO group. Analysis according to seminiferous epithelial stages revealed that abnormal tubules were increased in KO mice at all stages examined, with the difference being particularly evident stages II–III.

Discussion

In the present study, we demonstrated that B3galt4 KO mice exhibited complete male infertility despite the progression of spermatogenesis to the elongating spermatid stage. Although no overt changes in testis weight were observed in KO mice, the number of sperm recovered from the cauda epididymis was markedly reduced, accompanied by abnormal sperm morphology and impaired motility. These findings suggest that B3GLT4-dependent gangliosides are required for normal late spermatogenesis, sperm morphology, sperm motility, and male fertility.

Histological analysis and seminiferous tubule staging revealed an increased proportion of abnormal seminiferous tubules in KO testes. These abnormal tubules were characterized by the presence of multinucleated giant cells, mislocalization of elongating spermatids, and a reduction in the number of elongating/elongated spermatids. These observations indicate that, although spermatogenesis proceeds in the absence of B3galt4, the structural integrity and cellular organization of the seminiferous epithelium are impaired. Notably, the proportion of abnormal seminiferous tubules was particularly increased during stages II–III of the seminiferous epithelial cycle. This stage-dependent increase suggests that B3GALT4-dependent gangliosides may contribute to the maintenance of seminiferous epithelial organization during specific stages of spermatogenesis. In addition, the appearance of multinucleated giant cells suggests possible defects in germ cell survival, cytokinesis, or syncytial organization [3]. Given that gangliosides are major constituents of lipid rafts, altered membrane microdomain organization caused by altered ganglioside composition may affect membrane protein localization, cell adhesion, or germ cell–Sertoli cell interactions [20]. In Sertoli cells, flotillin-1-containing lipid rafts have been identified as major raft domains and are suggested to participate in Sertoli cell functions during spermatogenesis [21, 22]. In addition, N-cadherin, a critical adhesion molecule involved in Sertoli–germ cell junctions, has been shown to associate with lipid rafts in other cellular systems, where raft association contributes to the stabilization of cadherin-mediated cell–cell adhesion [23]. Therefore, these raft-associated proteins may represent potential candidates through which altered ganglioside-dependent membrane organization could influences spermatogenesis in B3galt4-deficient testes.

Nevertheless, although the proportion of abnormal seminiferous tubules was increased in KO testes, the majority of seminiferous tubules were not classified as abnormal. The infertile phenotype therefore appears more likely to result from defects in sperm maturation and function than from the seminiferous tubule abnormalities themselves. The reduced number of recovered sperm, abnormal sperm morphology, and impaired motility observed in KO mice may reflect defects in sperm membrane organization and membrane-associated signaling. Ganglioside deficiency may disrupt lipid raft function, thereby affecting signal transduction and membrane protein distribution required for normal sperm function. Consistent with this possibility, previous studies have shown that fucosylated gangliosides are involved in spermatogenesis and sperm function, suggesting that specific glycan structures contribute to male fertility [24]. Gangliosides such as GM1 have been identified in the sperm membrane, and alterations in their localization or abundance have been associated with impaired fertility [25].

Previous studies of B4galnt1 KO mice demonstrated that spermatogenesis arrests at the round spermatid stage. In contrast, spermatogenic differentiation in B3galt4 KO mice progressed to the elongating spermatid stage. This difference suggests that gangliosides generated at distinct steps of the biosynthetic pathway may contribute to different stages of spermatogenesis. The present findings further suggest that upstream or intermediate gangliosides, including GA2, GM2, GD2, and GT2, may allow spermatogenesis to proceed to late stages, whereas downstream gangliosides synthesized through B3GALT4 may be required for normal spermatid organization, sperm morphology, and sperm motility. These observations raise the possibility that different ganglioside species have stage-dependent and functionally distinct roles during spermatogenesis and sperm development.

Furthermore, St3gal2 and St3gal3 encode sialyltransferases that act downstream of B3GALT4 in the ganglioside biosynthetic pathway. St3gal2 single-deficient mice reportedly show no obvious abnormalities, whereas St3gal3 single-deficient mice exhibit mild growth retardation and reduced breeding performance. In addition, breeding after sexual maturity has not been observed in St3gal2/St3gal3 double-deficient mice [26]. These findings suggest that the severity of the phenotype and the affected physiological functions may differ depending on the disrupted step of the ganglioside biosynthetic pathway. However, because St3gal2/St3gal3 double-deficient mice also exhibit severe neurological and systemic developmental abnormalities, it remains unclear whether the impaired breeding phenotype is directly attributable to defects in gametogenesis or sperm function [26, 27].

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the specific ganglioside species and changes in ganglioside organization responsible for the observed sperm defects remain unidentified. Although our analysis of GD2 and GD1b expression in testes demonstrated altered ganglioside metabolism associated with B3galt4 deficiency, the detailed composition and subcellular distribution of gangliosides in sperm cells remain to be determined. Future studies examining sperm glycosphingolipid composition and ganglioside localization will be required to clarify whether altered ganglioside organization contributes to the observed defects. In addition, the mechanisms underlying the abnormal localization of elongated spermatids remain unclear. Further studies will be required to determine whether these abnormalities arise from impaired Sertoli-germ cell adhesion, disruption of membrane polarity, or defects in intercellular bridge maintenance. Whether altered ganglioside composition affects the localization or function of raft-associated proteins involved in these processes remains to be determined.

In conclusion, the present study demonstrates that B3GALT4-dependent gangliosides are not essential for the completion of spermatogenesis itself, but are required for the maintenance of seminiferous epithelial organization, normal sperm morphology, sperm motility, and male fertility.
