Antibacterial effects of Zataria multiflora and Eucalyptus camaldulensis extracts versus 0.2% chlorhexidine on Streptococcus mutans count: an in vitro study
Authors: Mina Rahmandost, Azadeh Babaei, Zahra Momeni
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChlorhexidine (CHX) is the most widely used synthetic antibacterial mouthwash to decrease the count of certain microorganisms. However, considering its side effects, this study aimed to compare the antibacterial effects of Zataria multiflora (Z. multiflora) and Eucalyptus camaldulensis (E. camaldulensis) extracts with 0.2% CHX on Streptococcus mutans (S. mutans) count.MethodsIn this in vitro experimental study, Z. multiflora and E. camaldulensis extracts were first obtained. The diameter of the growth inhibition zones of S. mutans in the presence of 10,000 and 50,000 ppm concentrations of the extracts and 0.2% CHX was then recorded in an agar well diffusion test; 0.2% CHX mouthwash served as the positive control, and distilled water served as the negative control. The minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) and minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC) of the extracts, and 0.2% CHX were also determined against S. mutans. Moreover, the S. mutans count was measured before and after exposure to the extracts, and 0.2% CHX was compared. Data were analyzed by SPSS 26.0.ResultsThe agar well diffusion test revealed that the diameter of the growth inhibition zones caused by Z. multiflora and E. camaldulensis extracts, and 0.2% CHX was 10 mm, 10 mm, and 13 mm, respectively for 10,000 ppm concentration, and 18 mm, 22 mm, and 23 mm for 50,000 ppm concentration, respectively. The MIC of 0.2% CHX, E. camaldulensis extract, and Z. multiflora extract was 2500, 2500, and 5000 ppm, respectively, and their MBC was 20,000, 10,000 and, 10,000 ppm, respectively. E. camaldulensis extract caused a greater reduction in S. mutans colony count than Z. multiflora and 0.2% CHX.ConclusionsThe results revealed significant antibacterial activity of Z. multiflora and E. camaldulensis extracts against S. mutans. It appears that E. camaldulensis may be suitable for incorporation in the formulation of mouthwashes given that its optimal biocompatibility and lack of cytotoxicity are confirmed in future studies.Keywords: Streptococcus mutans, Herbal extract, Zataria multiflora, Eucalyptus camaldulensis, Chlorhexidine

Introduction

Considering the existing evidence, dental caries remains a public health dilemma due to its increasing prevalence worldwide, particularly in developing countries like Iran. It can also negatively impact overall health [1]. Oral health is a multi-dimensional topic encompassing physical, psychological, emotional, and social domains that are all integral to overall health and well-being. Oral health is a subjective and dynamic concept, enabling essential functions such as eating, speaking, smiling, and socializing without pain, discomfort, or embarrassment [1]. Thus, caries control is highly prioritized to promote oral and general health [2–5]. In addition, due to the excessive costs of dental treatments, dental caries is known as a socioeconomic problem [2, 6].

Dental caries is a biofilm or dental plaque-induced multifactorial, non-communicable, and dynamic disease characterized by dental hard tissue demineralization [7]. This preventable disease is caused by bacterial colonization on the tooth surface. Acids produced by the metabolism of carbohydrates by the bacteria cause tooth surface demineralization, hard tissue degradation, and subsequent cavitation [2].

Considering the high prevalence of dental caries, its related complications, and treatment costs, caries prevention is more logical and cost-effective than treatment [8]. Decreasing the amount and frequency of carbohydrate intake, and adherence to oral hygiene (toothbrushing, dental flossing, mouthwash, etc.) are among the main caries preventive measures [9].

There are four main parameters involved in caries development, namely oral microbiota, time, presence of carbohydrates, and dental substrate. Of the different etiological factors responsible for the development of dental caries, the activity of oral microbiota is the principal factor, and Streptococcus mutans (S. mutans) is the main microorganism responsible for caries development [2].

Mouthwashes contain effective cariostatic agents and play a prominent role in oral health promotion. There are different types of mouthwashes available in the market such as chlorhexidine (CHX), and fluoride- and xylitol-containing mouthwashes [10]. CHX is the most widely used chemical antibacterial mouthwash for the reduction of the count of certain microorganisms [11] The mechanism of action of CHX is exerted through its positive charge, which is attracted by the negatively charged cell wall of S. mutans, causing its degradation [12]. However, clinical evidence shows that it has several side effects such as tooth discoloration, alteration of the sense of taste, calculus formation, oral mucosal desquamation, and occasional mucosal erosion [13]. Thus, considering the side effects of CHX, researchers have attempted to assess the antibacterial effects of different plant extracts for their commercialization as an alternative to synthetic mouthwashes.

Thyme, with the scientific name Zataria multiflora (Z. multiflora), is a member of the Lamiaceae family and is amongst the oldest medicinal herbs and spices. It is rich in polyphenols and flavonoids. Its main constituents include thymol and carvacrol; it also contains limonene, cineole, and alpha-terpineol, which may vary in amount and type depending on the age of the plant, season of harvesting, and geographical and climate conditions. This medicinal plant has antifungal, antioxidant, and anti-inflammatory properties, and protects the immune system; thymol and carvacrol are mainly responsible for its effects [14].

Eucalyptus, with the scientific name Eucalyptus camaldulensis (E. camaldulensis), is among the most important genera in the botanical family Myrtaceae and is a widely cultivated plant species worldwide. It is most famous for its antibiotic effect, which has been noticed for a long time. Phenolic compounds present in the eucalyptus leaf extract include chlorogenic acid, gentisic acid, gallic acid, caffeic acid, and catechol. Chromatographic separation shows that phenolic compounds are the most abundant in the eucalyptus leaf extract and are responsible for the antimicrobial activity of the plant extract. The eucalyptus essence has numerous applications in the pharmaceutical industry. It also has antioxidant, cytotoxic, antimicrobial, larvicidal, pesticidal, and anti-dermatophytic effects [15–17].

Considering all the above, this study aimed to compare the antibacterial effects of Z. multiflora and E. camaldulensis extracts with 0.2% CHX on S. mutans count.

Materials and methods

This in vitro study was conducted at the Microbiology and Biotechnology Laboratory of Tehran University of Medical Sciences. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Alborz University of Medical Sciences, Karaj, Iran (IR.ABZUMS.REC.1400.220).

The methodology of the present study was adopted from a study by Swadas et al. [18]. The study was performed in 4 steps, which were (I) obtaining the extracts, (II) assessing the antibacterial activity of the extracts and 0.2% CHX by the agar well diffusion test and measuring the diameter of the growth inhibition zones, (III) determining the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) and minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC) of the extracts and CHX, and (IV) measuring the number of viable bacteria in the culture medium in presence of extracts and 0.2% CHX (viable cell counting).

Step I: obtaining the extracts

Aerial parts of Z. multiflora and E. camaldulensis were obtained from Karaj botanical market and identified by Khadijeh Mahmoudi, Herbalist of the Herbarium of the Faculty of Pharmacy, Alborz University of Medical Sciences, Karaj, Iran, and deposited under the codes Z01-013 (Z. multiflora) and E01-009 (E. camaldulensis).

Next, 80% hydroalcoholic solvent was added to 300 g of aerial parts of each plant for extraction. The mixture was stored in a dark and cool place (23 °C) for 72 h, and then the solution was filtered, and the extract was obtained by the maceration method. This process was repeated three times every 24 h, and the obtained solution was transferred to a rotary evaporator (Senco, China) to remove the hydroalcoholic solvent at 50 °C (Fig. 1). The dried extract was weighed and kept at −20 °C for further experiments [19].

E. camaldulensis and Z. multiflora herbs (1 & 2) and their pure extracts (3 & 4)

Also, the yield of extracts was calculated by dividing the amount of the obtained extract (in grams) by the amount of powdered plant (in grams) multiplied by 100.

Step II: agar well diffusion test

S. mutans (ATCC 25175) was purchased from the Iranian Biological Resource Center, 0.2% CHX (as the positive control) was purchased from Irsha company (Iran), and distilled water (as the negative control) was purchased from a drugstore.

The agar well diffusion test is a qualitative method for the assessment of antibacterial activity. Accordingly, the diameter of the growth inhibition zones of S. mutans in the Mueller Hinton agar plate was measured in the presence of each extract and CHX.

For this purpose, S. mutans () was surface-cultured on Mueller Hinton agar solid medium by a cotton swab. Next, a sterile cylinder was used to create wells with 4 mm diameter and adequate distance from each other in the plates. Subsequently, 50 µL of each extract and CHX at concentrations of 10,000 ppm and 50,000 ppm were pipetted into the wells using a sterile pipette tip. Next, the plates were incubated in an anaerobic jar at 37 °C for 48 h. After incubation, the diameter of the growth inhibition zones was measured by a digital calliper (Mitutoyo, Tokyo, Japan), and reported in millimetres (mm).

Step III: Determination of MIC and MBC [20–25]

The MIC and MBC of the extracts against S. mutans were determined and compared with the MIC and MBC of 0.2% CHX.

Determination of MIC: Sterile 48-well plates were used for this purpose. A total of 100 µL of Mueller Hinton broth culture medium (Merck, Germanny) was added to each well. The first well served as the negative control and only contained the culture medium. The second well served as the positive control and contained the culture medium and bacteria. The third well contained the extracts (Z. multiflora or E, camaldulensis (or CHX mouthwash at 10,000 ppm concentration (determined by the agar well diffusion test), and the next wells contained dilutions by 1:2 (5,000 ppm in the fourth well, 2,500 ppm in the fifth well, and so on until the 16th well (ranged from (10,000–1.2 ppm). After serial dilutions of the extracts, equal amounts of S. mutans () were added to all wells except for the first well. The total volume of solution in each well was 200 µL and the final concentration of S. mutans in each well was 5 × 105 CFU/mL. All 48-well plates were incubated in an anaerobic jar at 37 °C for 48 h. Clear wells indicated no bacterial growth while turbid wells indicated bacterial proliferation. Accordingly, the minimum concentration of the extracts that caused bacterial growth inhibition (clear wells) was recorded as their MIC (Fig. 2). The similar experiment was repeated in triplicates to confirm the reproducibility of the results.

Sterile 48-well plates in three groups were used for the determination of MIC of Z. multiflora and E. camaldulensis extracts, and 0.2% CHX against S. mutans. Clear wells indicated no bacterial growth while turbid wells indicated bacterial proliferation

Determination of MBC: After the determination of MIC, the MBC of the extracts was determined. For this purpose, the subculturing on Muller-Hinton agar plates was performed for each sample, starting from their MIC value to higher concentrations of extracts and CHX, to determine their MBC value. In such a way that, the plates were incubated in an anaerobic jar at 37 °C for 48 h, and the minimum concentration causing no proliferation of S. mutans was recorded as the MBC of the extracts and 0.2% CHX (Fig. 3). The experiment was performed in triplicates and the results were the same.

Eight-well plates for determination of MBC of E. camaldulensis and Z. multiflora extracts, and 0.2% CHX for S. mutans

Step IV: viable cell counting

For viable cell counting, first, the required number of repetitions was calculated. For this purpose, the minimum sample size was calculated to be 6 in each group (a total of 12) according to a previous study [18] and using the following sample size formula for the comparison of two independent means in G*Power software:

where and are the variances and δ is the significance level of the difference between the two means being compared. Also, α and 1-β are the type I error and statistical power, respectively. To increase accuracy, 7 repetitions were considered for each group.

Viable cell counting was then performed as a quantitative method to confirm the antibacterial activity of the extracts. In this method, the bacteria are counted, and the antibacterial activity is reported as the percentage and log reduction of the bacterial count. The extracts and 0.2% CHX in equal concentrations of 10,000 ppm were added to the Mueller Hinton broth, and then of S. mutans were added to it in equal volume. Next, the culture media containing the extracts or 0.2% CHX along with the bacteria were placed in a shaker incubator (FTSH301M; Finetech, South Korea) operating at 100 rpm at 37 °C. After 30 min, 3 h, and 6 h of incubations, 0.1 cc of the solutions was spread-plated on tryptic soy agar solid medium (Merck, Germany) by an L-shaped rod. The plates were then incubated in an anaerobic jar at 37 °C for 48 h. Finally, the colony count was reported as the number of viable microorganisms per milliliter (CFU/mL) in percentage and log scale. The number of S. mutans colonies was counted before and after exposure to the extracts and 0.2% CHX.

Statistical analysis

Data regarding the colony count (7 repetitions for each extract and 0.2% CHX) were analyzed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The results were reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for the diameter of the growth inhibition zones (n = 7), number of colonies (n = 7), and percentage of reduction of S. mutans count at 30 min and 3 h after exposure to E. camaldulensis, Z. multiflora, and 0.2% CHX in 10,000 ppm concentration (n = 7). Normal distribution of data was confirmed by the Shapiro-Wilk test (P > 0.05) and homogeneity of the variances was confirmed by the Levene’s test (P > 0.05). Thus, the mean of colony count at each time point (30 min and 3 h) was compared by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) across the groups. Post hoc Tukey multiple comparisons test was subsequently applied for pairwise comparisons of Z. multiflora, E. camaldulensis, and 0.2% CHX regarding the percentage of mean reduction of S. mutans colonies count at 30 min and 3 h after exposure. The significance level was set at 0.05.

Results

In this study, a total of 57.07 g of Z. multiflora and 94.34 g of E. camaldulensis were obtained from 300 g of powder, yielding 19.02% and 31.45% efficiency, respectively.

Agar well diffusion test

The mean diameter of the growth inhibition zones (mean ± SD, mm) caused by Z. multiflora and E. camaldulensis extracts, and 0.2% CHX was)10 ± 1 mm), (10 ± 1 mm), and (13 ± 1.5 mm), respectively for 10,000 ppm concentration. However, higher growth inhibition occurred for 50,000 ppm concentration with values of (18 ± 2 mm), (22 ± 2.2 mm), and (23 ± 2 mm), respectively. The mean diameter of the growth inhibition zone caused by 0.2% CHX was significantly larger than that caused by the extracts (P < 0.05) for both concentrations. However, the difference between the mean diameter of the growth inhibition zones of 0.2% CHX and E. camaldulensis extract at 50,000 ppm concentration was not significant (P = 0.623) (Fig. 4). Distilled water (as the negative control) caused no growth inhibition zone.

Mean diameter of the growth inhibition zones of S. mutans caused by 10,000 and 50,000 ppm concentrations of the respective Z. multiflora and E. camaldulensis extracts, and 0.2% CHX in agar well diffusion test

MIC and MBC

As shown in Fig. 5, the MIC of E. camaldulensis, Z. multiflora, and 0.2% CHX was 2,500 ppm, 5,000 ppm, and 2,500 ppm, respectively. The MBC values of E. camaldulensis, Z. multiflora, and 0.2% CHX were 10,000 ppm, 10,000 ppm, and 20,000 ppm, respectively. The MIC of E. camaldulensis was equal to the MIC of 0.2% CHX, indicating the high sensitivity of S. mutans to this extract. The MBC of 0.2% CHX was higher than the MBC of the two extracts.

Comparison of the MIC and MBC values of E. camaldulensis and Z. multiflora extracts, and 0.2% CHX against S. mutans

Viable cell counting

As mentioned earlier, the number of S. mutans colonies (mean ± SD %) was counted before and after exposure to the extracts and 0.2% CHX. The results showed that the mean bacterial count reduction in 7 repetitions was higher (62.85 ± 5%) by E. camaldulensis than that of Z. multiflora (50 ± 2%) and 0.2% CHX (50 ± 3%) after 30 min of treatment. The mean reduction further increased to 99.7%, 94.28 ± 1%, and 96.7 ± 2% after 3 h, respectively (Fig. 6). After 6 h, the percentage of reduction increased to > 99.9% for E. camaldulensis; this value was > 99% for Z. multiflora and 0.2% CHX. Figure 6 depicts the percentage of mean reduction of S. mutans count across the three groups. Accordingly, the results showed that the mean reduction in S. mutans count by E. camaldulensis was greater than that by Z. multiflora and 0.2% CHX (P < 0.05).

Percentage of reduction of S. mutans count at 30 minutes and 3 hours after exposure to E. camaldulensis, Z. multiflora, at 10,000 ppm concentration (a, b) and their mean comparisons, 0.2% CHX (c)

One-way ANOVA indicated a significant difference among Z. multiflora, E. camaldulensis, and 0.2% CHX in the percentage of mean reduction of S. mutans count at both 30 min (P = 0.005) and 3 h (P < 0.001). Pairwise comparisons were performed by the Tukey multiple comparisons test at each time point (Table 1). As shown, the efficacy of E. camaldulensis for the mean reduction of S. mutans count was significantly higher than that of 0.2% CHX at both 30 min (P = 0.010) and 3 h (P = 0.007). Also, E. camaldulensis had a significantly greater efficacy than Z. multiflora for the mean reduction of S. mutans colony count at both 30 min (P = 0.010) and 3 h (P < 0.001). At 30 min, the efficacy of Z. multiflora and 0.2% CHX was not significantly different in the mean reduction of colony count (P = 0.999). However, at 3 h, 0.2% CHX had significantly higher efficacy than Z. multiflora in the mean reduction of S. mutans count (P = 0.033).

Discussion

Dental caries is a major health dilemma and is believed to be the most prevalent and costly disease worldwide. It is a preventable disease with adverse effects on general health [2]. Dental caries has been linked to several systemic diseases, such as cardiovascular, renal, and immune system diseases, head and neck cancer, and children’s growth and development, among others. Such data suggest oral health is fundamental to overall health [26]. Thus, knowledge about effective caries preventive measures is imperative.

Carious lesions contain a high percentage of certain bacteria such as S. mutans and Lactobacillus [2]. S. mutans is the major microorganism responsible for the development of caries. Due to the high financial burden imposed by dental caries, and its adverse health consequences, it is pivotal to find efficient antibacterial agents to reduce the load of cariogenic microorganisms. CHX is the most potent chemotherapeutic agent and the gold standard for reducing S. mutans count [27, 28]. However, due to its side effects, this study compared the antibacterial effects of Z. multiflora and E. camaldulensis extracts with 0.2% CHX on S. mutans.

The study showed a larger growth inhibition zone by 0.2% CHX in 10,000 ppm concentration. Nonetheless, the difference between 0.2% CHX and E. camaldulensis extract was not noticeable in the 50,000 ppm concentration. The MIC of E. camaldulensis and 0.2% CHX was the same, and lower than that of Z. multiflora. The MBC of both E. camaldulensis and Z. multiflora extracts was lower than that of 0.2% CHX. Finally, the reduction in S. mutans colony count caused by E. camaldulensis was greater than that caused by Z. multiflora and 0.2% CHX.

In line with the present results, Hugar et al. [29], (2022) demonstrated that herbal mouth rinse had superior antimicrobial efficacy against salivary S. mutans than CHX in children in the mixed dentition period. Also, Khoramian et al. [28], (2020) assessed the effect of a mouthwash containing Teucrium polium extract on salivary S. mutans count in a group of dental students. The results revealed a significant reduction in the salivary count of S. mutans after using the mouthwash. Moreover, Dashtizadeh and Mahmoodi [30] (2017) evaluated the antibacterial activity of vulgaris Thymus, E. camaldulensis, Matricaria chamomilla, and vulgare Foeniculum extracts on Escherichia coli (responsible for urinary tract infections). They calculated the MIC and MBC of the extracts by the disc diffusion technique and, showed that among the tested plant extracts, the aqueous and alcoholic extracts of Thymus vulgaris followed by E. camaldulensis had the highest antibacterial activity against Escherichia coli. However, Swadas et al. [18], (2016) evaluated the antibacterial activity of different concentrations of grape seed extract versus CHX against S. mutans. The results indicated that higher concentrations of grape seed extract caused a significantly greater reduction in bacterial count, such that in low concentrations, no significant reduction occurred in bacterial count. In general, the sensitivity of S. mutans to CHX was higher than its sensitivity to different concentrations of grape seed extract.

In line with our study, several studies have shown the antimicrobial activity of Z. multiflora. In this regard, Fazelinasab et al. [31], (2019) evaluated the antimicrobial properties of 29 herbal extracts against Escherichia coli and Staphylococcus aureus. They found that Staphylococcus aureus had the highest sensitivity to Z. multiflora in the agar well diffusion test, which may indicate the relatively optimal antimicrobial activity of Z. multiflora related to thymol in composition. Also, Motevasel et al. [32], (2013) obtained Z. multiflora extract from dried leaves by the maceration technique and, showed that the extracts inhibited the growth of Staphylococcus epidermidis, Staphylococcus saprophyticus, and methicillin-sensitive Staphylococcus aureus.

Our findings about the antimicrobial activity of E. camaldulensis extract are consistent with the results of an experimental study by Ghasemian et al. in 2019 that revealed the effects of E. camaldulensis against most infectious diseases such as bacterial, fungal, parasitic, and viral agents. This study showed that the active ingredients of these plants are primarily alkaloids, flavonoids, pigments, phenolics, terpenes, starches, steroids, and essential oils [33]. Also, Mosad et al. (2018) reported that ethyl acetate extract E. camaldulensis leaves showed potent antimicrobial and antischistosomal activity. This activity may be attributed to the phenolic compounds identified through structure elucidation [16].

Limitations

This study had some limitations. For instance, S. mutans occurs in the form of biofilm in the oral cavity, while it was evaluated in a planktonic form in the present in vitro study. Moreover, despite the attempts to simulate the clinical setting as much as possible, this study had an in vitro design and complete simulation of the clinical setting was not possible. Thus, generalization of the results to the clinical setting should be done with caution. Also, the phytochemical characterization and identification of the possible bioactive compounds responsible for the antibacterial effects were not carried out in the present study, which was another limitation.

Recommendations

Further studies are required on the mechanism of antibacterial activity of E. camaldulensis and Z. multiflora extracts against S. mutans. Moreover, the possible side effects and cytotoxicity of the extracts should be evaluated in future studies before their widespread use in oral health products. Also, the phytochemical characterization and identification of the possible bioactive compounds is recommended.

Conclusion

The results revealed significant antibacterial activity of Z. multiflora and E. camaldulensis extracts against S. mutans. The antibacterial effect of E. camaldulensis on S. mutans count was greater than that of Z. multiflora and 0.2% CHX. E. camaldulensis may be suitable for incorporation in the formulation of mouthwashes given that its optimal biocompatibility and lack of cytotoxicity will be confirmed in future studies.
