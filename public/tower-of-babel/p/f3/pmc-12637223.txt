Asprosin as a regulator of hepatic lipogenesis and its association with hepatic steatosis
Authors: Joshua Ayork Acevedo-Carabantes, Sophia Eugenia Martínez-Vázquez, Lilia G Noriega, Martha Guevara-Cruz, Laura A Velázquez-Villegas, Mónica Sánchez-Tapia, Luis F Uscanga Domínguez, Nimbe Torres, Armando R Tovar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAsprosin is a recently discovered adipokine involved in the regulation of hepatic gluconeogenesis and appetite, but its role in hepatic lipid metabolism, a key aspect of metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), is unknown. This study aimed to investigate whether asprosin could regulate hepatic lipid metabolism in vitro and in an animal model and to determine the potential association between circulating levels of asprosin and the severity of hepatic steatosis in humans. The effects of asprosin on the metabolic phenotype of mice with diet-induced obesity (DIO), as well as on de novo lipogenesis, β-oxidation, and mitochondrial function, were analyzed in both cultured primary hepatocytes and mice by assessing mRNA and protein levels of metabolic pathway components, along with mitochondrial function. Furthermore, serum asprosin levels were measured in 314 subjects who also underwent transient elastography. The results showed that in vitro, asprosin reduced the expression of lipogenic genes and increased those of fatty acid oxidation and mitochondrial activity in hepatocytes. In the DIO model, asprosin-treated mice showed reduced lipid accumulation, improved metabolic parameters and increased abundance of proteins of lipid catabolism. Furthermore, asprosin levels were positively correlated with the controlled attenuation parameter (CAP) and liver stiffness (KPa) in FibroScan measurements. Higher asprosin levels were observed in subjects with severe steatosis (S2 and S3) compared to those without (S0). This adipokine appears to play a protective role in early hepatic steatosis by modulating lipid metabolism and enhancing mitochondrial function.Supplementary key words: asprosin, hepatic steatosis, lipid metabolism, biomarker

Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), previously known with the term non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) (1), is a term used to describe a spectrum of disorders that can range from isolated liver steatosis in at least 5% of hepatocytes to ballooning degeneration with inflammation (metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis, MASH), fibrosis and cirrhosis in the presence of at least one cardiovascular risk factor and in absence of excessive alcohol consumption (2, 3, 4).

The disease is closely associated with components of the metabolic syndrome, including obesity, insulin resistance, and type 2 diabetes, which are risk factors for its development (5, 6, 7). In addition, MASLD is an independent risk factor for major cardiovascular events and for the development of other metabolic disorders (8, 9, 10, 11, 12). It is also part of a continuous process leading to cirrhosis, liver failure and ultimately hepatocellular carcinoma, which are major causes of morbidity and mortality worldwide (13, 14, 15, 16).

One of the hallmarks of its pathophysiology is alterations in de novo lipogenesis, which accounts for a quarter of the lipid content stored in the diseased hepatocyte. The remaining free fatty acids are supplied by white adipose tissue or diet (17). On the other hand, abnormal ß-oxidation in the liver leads to endoplasmic reticulum stress and is associated with mitochondrial dysfunction, accumulation of reactive oxygen species and increased lipoperoxidation products. This stress induces the production of proinflammatory cytokines and contributes to the progression to MASH (4, 18).

Adipose tissue dysfunction is a major contributor to the development of metabolic diseases and MASLD. Increased lipolysis and the subsequent release of free fatty acids, which are taken up by the liver, promote steatosis, lipotoxicity, hepatic insulin resistance, and inflammation. The production of adipokines is also altered and affects metabolism and insulin sensitivity in distant organs (19). Asprosin is a recently discovered hormone that has been associated with several metabolic disorders. Encoded by the Fbn1 gene, it is produced by mesenchymal tissues, particularly white adipose tissue (20, 21). The liver is one of its target organs, where asprosin binds to G protein-coupled receptors, increasing cyclic AMP levels and protein kinase A activity, thereby stimulating hepatic glucose release (20). OLFR734 has been identified as one of these G protein-coupled receptors (22).

Pathologically elevated plasma levels of asprosin are found in animal models of obesity and insulin resistance. Interventions that reduce these abnormal increases, such as immunological neutralization or genetic knockdown, have resulted in improved glucose levels and body weight in these animals (20, 21). Furthermore, Olfr734 knockout mice (Olfr734−/−) show reduced metabolic disturbances in response to a high-fat diet. Interestingly, the Olfr734−/− mice do not completely eliminate the asprosin-stimulated effect on hepatic glucose production, suggesting that the effect of this hormone may persist through other hepatic receptors (22).

Elevated plasma asprosin levels have been found in patients with MASLD and NAFLD compared to healthy individuals, and a correlation has been observed between levels of this hormone and markers of MASH such as platelet count, fasting blood glucose, triglycerides, albumin, and Homeostatic Model Assessment for Insulin Resistance (HOMA-IR) among others (23, 24, 25). Nevertheless, asprosin has not been directly associated with hepatic lipid content or steatosis severity in subjects. Stimuli that activate cAMP production in the protein kinase A pathway, such as catecholamines or glucagon, are known to be regulators of fatty acid metabolism (26, 27, 28, 29), which might suggest that asprosin could have an effect on fatty acid biosynthesis or catabolism, but this has not yet been studied.

Thus, the aim of this study was to evaluate the effect of asprosin on the regulation of hepatic lipid metabolism using primary hepatocytes, and to assess these effects in the context of a mouse model of diet-induced obesity at different stages of obesity development, and to determine the association of circulating asprosin levels with the prevalence and severity of hepatic steatosis in humans.

Materials and Methods

Experimental animals

For the study, 7–8 weeks old male C57BL/6 mice weighing 22–26 g were provided by the Experimental Research and Animal Care Unit of the Instituto Nacional de Ciencias Médicas y Nutrición Salvador Zubirán (INCMNSZ) and housed in cages with 2–3 animals/cage with a 12-h light-dark cycle at 22°C. Two different cohorts of mice were followed, one for 9 weeks and the other for 13 weeks, and the animals were randomly assigned to one of the 2 groups studied for administration of the diets (6–8 per group): 1) AIN-93 diet (control diet) or 2) high-fat, high-sucrose diet (HFHSD). The diets were fed in dry form, and the composition of each diet is described in Supplemental Table S1. Animals had ad libitum access to food and water. Body weight was recorded daily, and food consumption was measured twice weekly. Following 8 or 12 weeks of feeding period, mice were injected subcutaneously daily with 30 μg Human recombinant asprosin (rAsprosin) (Aviscera Biosciences) or vehicle on the last week of dietary treatment, resulting in a total of 7 injections per mouse. Body composition (Quantitative Magnetic Resonance, EchoMR1 system) and energy expenditure (Oxymax Laboratory Monitoring System, Columbus Instruments) were measured before and at the end of the injection period; for the intraperitoneal glucose tolerance test (IPGTT) mice were injected a bolus of 20% dextrose solution for a total dose of 3 g/kg of lean mass following a 6-h fast, blood glucose levels were measured at baseline and at 15, 30, 60, 90 and 120 min using a Freestyle Opgtium Neo (Abott) glucometer. Animals were euthanized after an 8-h fast. Whole blood was collected centrifuged at 1200 g for 10 min, and serum was used to measure the biochemical parameters. The rest of the sample was frozen at −80°C. Livers were also collected, a fraction was used for histological analysis, and the rest of the tissue was snap frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C for measurement of triglyceride content and quantitation of protein abundance. All procedures were previously approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Ethics Committee (approval number CICUAL-FNU-1975-19-21-1).

Human study

The present study in human subjects was approved by the Ethics Committee in Human Research of the Instituto Nacional de Ciencias Médicas y Nutrición Salvador Zubirán (CEI: 3794). The study protocol adhered to the ethical principles according to the World Medical Association’s Declaration of Helsinki. Participants with normal body mass index (BMI), or with overweight or obesity, were selected according to the following criteria: > 18 years of age, male or female, without a known diagnosis of metabolic disease (diabetes, dyslipidemia or hypertension) or hepatitis virus infection, absence of excessive alcohol consumption or drugs associated to liver steatosis. All selected subjects signed the consent form to participate in the present study. A detailed medical history was taken to corroborate the absence of a known metabolic illness and assess alcohol and drug intake by self-report. Subjects were weighed and anthropometric measurements were taken; body composition was assessed by electrical bioimpedance analysis (Seca Instrument, model mBCA 514). Transient elastography (Fibroscan Echosens model 502) was performed to measure liver stiffness, and controlled attenuation parameter (CAP) values were used to determine the degrees of fibrosis and steatosis as defined by the EASL-EASD-EASO Clinical Practice Guideline on the management of steatotic liver disease associated with metabolic dysfunction for each of the subjects (2). Blood was withdrawn, serum was obtained by centrifugation, and samples were kept at −70°C.

Cell culture and in vitro steatosis model

Male C57BL/6 mice, aged 10–12 weeks, were provided by the Department of Experimental Research and Animal Care of the Instituto Nacional de Ciencias Médicas y Nutrición Salvador Zubirán (INCMNSZ). Primary hepatocytes were harvested by the in situ double perfusion of the liver method according to the protocol of Berry and Friend (30). Briefly, cannulation and exsanguination of the liver in vivo was followed by continuous perfusion with collagenase type IV (Gibco). The liver was then excised and placed in Hank's balanced salt solution (HBSS) (Sigma Aldrich) to disaggregate the tissue. The resulting cell suspension was filtered through a 70 μM mesh. The filtered cells were then washed three times with HBSS and resuspended in M199 medium (Sigma Aldrich) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco), 0.1% bovine serum albumin (BSA (Sigma Aldrich), 1 nM insulin (Sigma Aldrich), and 100 nM dexamethasone. The percentage of liver cells was determined by trypan blue (Golden Bell) staining, and a value of 85% or higher was considered adequate. Cells were seeded at a density of 40,000 liver cells/cm2 of plate surface. Cells were treated with rAsprosin for 24 h with or without prior induction of steatosis.

For the in vitro steatosis model, hepatocytes were exposed for 24 h to a palmitic acid: BSA conjugate at a final fatty acid concentration of 200 μM and 2.2% BSA in culture media (6:1 M ratio), 100 nM insulin (Sigma Aldrich), and 100 nM dexamethasone. The conjugate was prepared according to Pike et al. (31).

Cell viability assay

12 mM 3-(4,5-dimethyl-2-thiazolyl)-2,5-diphenyl-2H-tetrazolium bromide (MTT)/PBS and 10% SDS/0.01 M HCl solutions were freshly prepared. Cells were seeded into 96-well plates, and after each treatment, the cell culture medium was removed and replaced with fresh medium after two washes with sterile PBS. 10 μl of MTT solution was added to each experimental or negative control (medium only) wells. After incubation for 4 h in a 37°C humidified chamber, 100 μl of HCl-SDS solution was added, and each well was thoroughly mixed by pipetting and incubated for another 4 h. The absorbance at 570 nm was read using a Bio-Rad xMark microplate spectrophotometer.

Biochemical parameters and asprosin measurement

Fasting blood glucose, triglycerides, total cholesterol and AST were measured in mice serum, while glucose, triglycerides, total cholesterol, HDL-Cholesterol, LDL-Cholesterol, AST and ALT were measured in human serum using a Cobas c 111 analyzer (Roche). Asprosin measurement was determined from mice and human sera by quantitative ELISA using a commercial kit (SK00229-08, Aviscera Biosciences).

Histological analysis

Liver samples were fixed in 10% phosphate-buffered formalin and embedded in melted paraffin blocks. Paraffin blocks were cut to a thickness of 4 μm and slides were stained with 95% hematoxylin-eosin. The histological sections were observed under a Leica microscope (Leica DM750Wetzlar) and the steatosis percentage was calculated measuring pixel area of the lipidic vesicles from each group (N = 5) using the Image View 3.7.13593 software.

Real-time quantitative PCR

After treatments, the culture medium was removed and the cells were lysed using Trizol reagent (Invitrogen). The total RNA pellet was stored in a mixture of 70% ethanol and nuclease-free water at −20°C until further analysis. RNA was resuspended in nuclease-free water and quantified spectrophotometrically using a Nanodrop 2000; purity was assessed by measuring the A260/A280 and A260/A230 ratios.

Total RNA was reverse transcribed using the iScript cDNA synthesis kit (Bio-Rad) according to the reaction protocol. Real-time quantitative PCR was performed on a LightCycler 480 system (Roche) using 36B4 as a housekeeping gene. Primer sequences and references are listed in Supplemental Table S2. PCR amplification was performed using LightCycler 480 SYBR Green master mix (Roche). Relative quantification was calculated using the Pfaffl method, taking into account the efficiency of each gene reaction. Results were normalized and presented as the mean of at least six different determinations for each gene.

Transcription factor activity assay for SREBP-1

Protein extracts were prepared from cell nuclei using the CellLytic NuCLEAR Extraction Kit (Sigma Aldrich), 15 μl of nuclear extracts were used for each reaction and the DNA binding activity of the transcription factor SREBP-1 was determined colorimetrically using the SREBP-1 Transcription Factor Assay Kit (Abcam). Results are presented as the mean of at least six different determinations for each group.

Western blot analysis

For total protein extraction, cells and tissues were homogenized using lysis buffer (0.5% sodium deoxycholate, 0.1% SDS, 0.006% sodium azide) plus cOmplete Mini (Roche) and PhosSTOP (Roche). The antibodies used for the proteins of interest were: acetyl-CoA carboxylase (ACC) H-76 (Santa Cruz 30212), p-ACC S-79 (Millipore 07 303), Peroxisomal acyl-coenzyme A oxidase 1 (ACOX1) H-140 (Santa Cruz sc-98499), AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) H-300 (Santa Cruz 25792), pAMPK T-172 (Santa Cruz 33524), Carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1 (CPT1) 8F6AE9 (Abcam 128568), OXPHOS Antibody Cocktail (Abcam 110413), fatty acid synthase (FAS) H-300 (Santa Cruz 20140), sterol regulatory element binding protein 1 (SREBP1) C-20 (Santa Cruz 366), toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) 25 (Santa Cruz 293072), Actin C-11 (Santa Cruz 1615), anti-goat (Abcam 6885), anti-mouse (Abcam 6789), and anti-rabbit (Abcam 6721).

Seahorse metabolic assessment

Oxygen consumption rate (OCR) was measured to assess effects on mitochondrial respiration using the Seahorse XFe96 Extracellular Flux Analyzer (Agilent Technologies). Briefly, 4,000 cells were seeded on collagen type I (Gibco) coated XF96 plates. After 18 h incubation with rAsprosin, cells were washed with XF basal medium supplemented with 10 mM glucose, 2 mM glutamine and 1 mM pyruvate (pH 7.4) and then incubated in this medium for 1 h at 37°C in a non-CO2 incubator.

Mitochondrial respiration and glycolysis were determined using the XF Mito Stress and Glycolysis Stress Test Kits (Agilent); fatty acid oxidation analysis was performed using the XF Palmitate Oxidation Stress Test Kit (Agilent); metabolic capacity and dependence analysis were performed using the XF Mito Fuel Flex Test Kit (Agilent). All results were normalized to the number of cells; in the final step of each assay, cells were stained with Hoechst 2 μM (Invitrogen); a BioTek Cytation 1 instrument was used for cell counting. Normalized results were analyzed using Wave software (Seahorse Biosciences).

Fluorescence staining

To assess mitochondrial membrane potential, the cultures were removed from the medium, washed once with 1x PBS, and then a pre-warmed solution of 100 nM MitoTracker Red CMXRos probe plus 2 μM Hoechst was added and incubated for 30 min, washed three times with 1X PBS and finally fixed for 20 min with 10% calcium formaldehyde fixative (1.0 g CaCl2, 25.0 ml 16% paraformaldehyde (4.0% final) and 75.0 ml distilled water). Staining and fixation were performed in a dark environment. Fluorescence intensity was measured and normalized by cell counting using a BioTek Cytation 1 instrument.

To assess neutral lipid accumulation, cells were stained with BODIPY 493/503 dye (Invitrogen). Cells were removed from the medium, washed once using 1x PBS, a solution containing 20 μg/ml BODIPY plus Hoechst 2 μM diluted in 1x PBS was added and stained for 20 min at 37°C in a 5% CO2 incubator. After 20 min, all the BODIPY solution was removed and immediately washed three times with 1X PBS. Cells were then fixed for 20 min using 10% formaldehyde calcium fixative (1.0 g CaCl2, 25.0 ml 16% paraformaldehyde (4.0% final) and 75.0 ml distilled water). Staining and fixation were performed in a dark environment. Fluorescence intensity was measured and normalized by cell count using a BioTek Cytation 1.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed with GraphPad Prism 9. P-values < 0.05 were considered significant. Normality was checked using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test before performing parametric tests; in case of non-normality, non-parametric tests were used. Pearson's r and simple linear regressions were calculated to explore correlations between variables. Differences between groups were estimated using ordinary one-way ANOVA and corrected for multiple comparisons using the Bonferroni test. Statistical significance of the difference between two groups was calculated using Student's t tests.

Results

Asprosin reduces hepatic fatty acid synthesis capacity while promoting fatty acid oxidation and mitochondrial activity in hepatocytes

To investigate whether asprosin is involved in the regulation of lipid metabolism in the liver, we first examined the effect of different concentrations of asprosin on primary hepatocytes. As observed in (Fig. 1A, B), asprosin up to a concentration of 30 nM had no deleterious effects on cultured hepatocytes as measured by MTT cell viability assay. To evaluate the effect of asprosin on hepatic lipogenesis, insulin, a known stimulus for lipid biosynthesis and accumulation, was used as a positive control. Indeed, insulin significantly increased Acc and Fasn mRNA expression by 6.99- and 2.42-fold, respectively (Fig. 1C). We also observed this effect on protein abundance of ACC and FASN, where insulin increased abundance by 29% and 7.2-fold, respectively. Interestingly, asprosin significantly decreased the abundance of these proteins, especially at the 30 nM concentration, by 92% and 68%, respectively. Furthermore, we found that the protein abundance of CPT1 and ACOX1, enzymes involved in the fatty acid oxidation process, increased concomitantly with asprosin concentration, and both increased significantly at 30 nM asprosin (Fig. 1D).

To further investigate changes in oxidative activity, we measured mitochondrial oxygen consumption rate (OCR) by extracellular flux analysis. Stimulation with asprosin at 20 and 30 nM, but not at 10 nM, produced significantly higher maximal respiration and reserve capacity (Fig. 1E). Increased mitochondrial activity in hepatocytes with the higher asprosin concentration was also demonstrated by Mitotracker staining, indicating a 28% higher membrane potential (Fig. 1F). Next, we examined the changes in mitochondrial fuel utilization induced by the addition of 30 nM asprosin. As shown in (Fig. 1G), asprosin favored a shift to the mitochondrial pathway of glucose oxidation as a compensatory response to the addition of mitochondrial fuel pathway inhibitors to meet basal energy demands in hepatocytes. There was no difference in fuel dependence for the mitochondrial glutamine, fatty acid or glucose pathways.

Asprosin induces changes in fatty acid metabolism and reduces lipid accumulation in an in vitro model of hepatic steatosis

Next, we asked whether the effects of asprosin on fatty acid-metabolizing enzymes would be observed under steatosis conditions. For this purpose, we incubated mouse hepatocytes with 200 mM palmitic acid and 100 nM insulin as previously described (31). The accumulation of lipid droplets in this model was verified by staining with BODIPY (Fig. 2A); after 24 h of incubation, an increase of approximately 72% in accumulated lipids was observed.

Interestingly, the repressor effect of asprosin on the relative mRNA expression of lipogenic genes, including Acc, Fasn and Mlxipl (ChREBP), was preserved in the steatotic hepatocyte model (Fig. 2B). Since SREBP1 is a major promoter of fatty acid synthesis, we assessed whether the lower gene expression was associated with changes in the activity of this transcription factor. Indeed, palmitic acid induced a 1.82-fold increase in SREBP 1 activity, whereas asprosin at 10 and 30 nM reduced it by 62% and 72%, respectively (Fig. 2C). Western blot analysis revealed that this change was not due to a reduction in the total protein content of SREBP1, but to a decrease in the active nuclear form of the transcription factor, resulting in a significant reduction of one of its target genes, ACC. Notably, not only did ACC abundance decrease, but there was also an increase in the inactivating phosphorylation of this enzyme, which correlated with an increase in the abundance of phosphorylated AMPK, favoring a reduction in fatty acid oxidation (Fig. 2D).

We next evaluated the effect of asprosin on mitochondrial function in primary hepatocytes with induced steatosis. Exposure to palmitic acid strongly suppressed oxidative activity in mitochondria with marked impairment in all key parameters. Notably, the addition of asprosin at concentrations of 10 nM and 30 nM partially rescued mitochondrial dysfunction with significant improvements in basal respiration, ATP production, maximal respiration and reserve respiratory capacity. This was particularly evident at the highest asprosin concentration (Fig. 2E).

To investigate whether asprosin specifically modified lipid catabolism in steatotic hepatocytes, we performed a fatty acid oxidation assay. The results showed that asprosin increased the ability of hepatocytes to oxidize exogenous palmitic acid and endogenous fatty acids under conditions of high metabolic demand. Increased uncoupling was also observed with exogenously added fatty acids, which was reversed by asprosin treatment (Fig. 2F). BODIPY staining demonstrated a decrease in accumulated lipids in steatotic hepatocytes treated with 30 nM asprosin (Fig. 2G). Altogether, these results demonstrate that asprosin reduces hepatic lipogenesis and increases fatty acid oxidation in both normal or steatotic primary hepatocytes. To evaluate whether these effects are conserved in vivo, the effect of asprosin was studied in mice fed a high-fat diet that favors the development of hepatic steatosis.

Asprosin only ameliorates the effects on body weight and body composition in the early stages of diet-induced obesity

To assess the impact of asprosin treatment on diet-induced obesity (DIO), two separate mice cohorts were fed either a control AIN-93 diet (64% carbohydrate, 19% protein, 17% fat) or a high-fat, high-sucrose (HFHS) diet (40.6% carbohydrate, 14.8% protein, 44.6% fat), one cohort was fed for 9 weeks and the other for 13 weeks total. After completing 8 or 12 weeks of feeding period, the mice received daily subcutaneous injections of 30 μg of human recombinant asprosin or a vehicle for one week, a total of 7 injections per mice (Fig. 3A).

A: Mice were subjected to 8 or 12 weeks of HFHS diet before treatment with asprosin for 7 days. B and F: Changes in body weight. C and G: Body composition analysis. D, E, H, and I: Indirect calorimetry following asprosin treatment. ∗P < 0.05, ∗∗P < 0.01, ∗∗∗P < 0.001, ∗∗∗∗P < 0.0001 by analysis of variance coupled with Bonferroni multiple comparison test.

After 8 weeks of dietary treatment, there was a 16% difference in body weight gain between mice in the DIO group compared to mice in the control group. After one week of subcutaneous injection with asprosin or vehicle, mice under a control diet had similar body weight gain between vehicle (6.5%) and asprosin administration (5.8%). In contrast, mice in the DIO-vehicle group had an increase of 7.1% body weight gain, while mice in the DIO-asprosin group only presented a 1.8% increase (Fig. 3B). These differences were independent of energy consumption between the vehicle and asprosin groups. In terms of body composition, DIO mice showed a significant increase in fat mass percentage (up by 13%) compared to controls (Fig. 3C). Lean mass decreased by 10.6% in the DIO group compared to the control group (Fig. 3C). Interestingly, mice under asprosin treatment did not presented changes in body composition after one week intervention while mice in the vehicle groups significantly increased fat mass and decreased lean mass in both dietary interventions (Fig. 3C). Despite changes in body weight gain and body composition there were no significant differences in VO2 consumption after 8 weeks of dietary treatment, independently on the administration of vehicle or asprosin (Fig. 3D). As expected, there was a significant reduction in the Respiratory Exchange Ratio (RER) in DIO mice compared to control mice, nonetheless between 11 and 15 h of the light-dark cycle there was significant decrease in RER in asprosin-treated DIO mice (Fig. 3E), indicating a higher percentage of fatty acid oxidation during this period.

After 12 weeks of dietary treatment, the DIO mice showed an even greater increase in body weight (58.9%) (Fig. 3F) and fat mass percentage (up by 12.1%), and a decrease in lean mice (9.1%) (Fig. 3G) compared to control mice. However, asprosin administration did not exert significant changes in body weight and body composition compared to the vehicle groups. In addition, mice in the DIO groups presented a significant decrease in VO2 compared to the control groups (Fig. 3H), while the difference observed in RER at 8 weeks in asprosin-treated mice was no longer observed after 12 weeks of dietary intervention (Fig. 3I).

Time-dependent effects of asprosin on glucose metabolism, lipid accumulation, and inflammatory markers in DIO mice

On the other hand, after 8 weeks of dietary interventions, the intraperitoneal glucose tolerance test (IPGTT) for the DIO-Asprosin group showed a non-significant increase in the area under the curve (AUC) compared to controls, while the DIO-Vehicle group had a significant increase (Fig. 4A). In contrast, after 12 weeks of dietary treatment both DIO-Vehicle and DIO-Asprosin groups showed significantly elevated AUCs on the IPGTT compared to controls (Fig. 4A), indicating that the effect of asprosin on glucose metabolism control is lost at this stage of DIO exposition. In addition, after 8 weeks of dietary intervention fasting glucose levels in the DIO-Asprosin mice were not significantly higher than controls, in contrast to the DIO-Vehicle group (Fig. 4B). However, after 12 weeks-dietary treatment fasting glucose was significantly elevated in both DIO groups, regardless of asprosin administration (Fig. 4B).

Serum cholesterol levels increased in the DIO-Vehicle group but not in the DIO-Asprosin group after 8 weeks of intervention compared to controls (Fig. 4C), while serum triglycerides showed no significant differences (Fig. 4D). After 12 weeks of intervention, there was a significant increase in serum cholesterol in both DIO groups compared to control groups (Fig. 4C), while a significant increase in serum triglycerides was only observed between DIO-Vehicle and Control-Vehicle groups (Fig. 4D).

Asprosin prevented a significant increase in serum aspartate transaminase (AST) levels when administered after 8 weeks of DIO intervention (Fig. 4E), but this effect was lost after 12-weeks of DIO exposition since AST was significantly increased in both DIO-Vehicle and DIO-Asprosin groups compared to controls (Fig. 4E). When analyzing serum asprosin levels we observed that at 8-weeks of intervention, Asprosin-administrated groups (Control-Asprosin and DIO-Asprosin) significantly increased circulating levels of this adipokine compared to vehicle groups (Fig. 4F). After 12-weeks of dietary interventions, asprosin levels in control mice were similar to those observed at 8-weeks. However, mice in the DIO groups showed a significant 1.65-fold increase in asprosin serum levels, with no differences between the administration of asprosin or vehicle (Fig. 4F).

Interestingly, Asprosin-treated mice showed lower liver triglyceride accumulation (Fig. 4G) and a reduced steatosis percentage, as visualized by Hematoxylin & Eosin staining (Fig. 4H, I) in the control groups (8 and 12 weeks) fed with AIN diet, and after 8 weeks of DIO intervention. No differences in liver triglyceride accumulation and steatosis percentage were observed between asprosin and vehicle groups at 12 weeks in mice fed a high-fat diet (Fig. 4G–I).

Western blot analysis performed at 8-week intervention showed a decrease in liver ACC protein abundance in both control and DIO groups with asprosin administration (Fig. 5A). After 12 weeks, ACC protein abundance was significantly increased in the DIO-Vehicle group, while this effect was prevented in the DIO-Asprosin group showing no differences compared to the mice fed a control diet (Fig. 5A, B). FAS protein abundance significantly increased in the DIO-Vehicle and DIO-Asprosin groups compared to the Control-Vehicle groups after 8 weeks of intervention. Surprisingly, the control groups with asprosin also had a significant increase in the abundance of this protein (Fig. 5A, C). At 12-week intervention, we observed a significant increase in FAS abundance between the control and the DIO groups, an effect that was not modified upon asprosin administration (Fig. 5A, C). Animals fed the control diet and injected with asprosin exhibited increased abundance of CPT1 (Fig. 5A,D) and mitochondrial complexes of the oxidative phosphorylation chain (OXPHOS), specifically in complexes II and V (Fig. 5A, E) in both 8 and 12-week feeding cohorts. No significant difference in these proteins was found between the DIO groups. Due to the changes observed in the in vitro experiments, we were interested in evaluating the abundance of total and phosphorylated AMPK in our in vivo models. We observed that after 8-week intervention, total and phosphorylated AMPK abundance (Fig. 5A, F) was significantly decreased in the DIO-Vehicle mice compared to the Control-Vehicle group. As observed for other parameters, asprosin administration in DIO mice prevented total AMPK reduction after 8 weeks of consumption of a HFHS diet (Fig. 5A, F), whereas total and phosphorylated AMPK abundance were also significantly decreased in both DIO groups after 12 weeks of intervention (Fig. 5A, G). In contrast to the in vitro assays, the effects in the in vivo models were mainly observed in total AMPK abundance, where the consumption of an energy overload in the DIO groups had an evident impact in AMPK turnover.

The effect of the DIO model also impacted the abundance of some proteins associated with the immune response. Liver TLR4 significantly increased in the DIO-vehicle groups after 8 and 12 weeks of DIO intervention (Fig. 5A, H). The effect of asprosin administration in preventing TLR4 upregulation was only significant after 12 weeks of DIO exposition, since the increase in this receptor was higher after longer term energy overload (Fig. 5A, B). On the other hand, the transcription factor NFκB, a key regulator of the inflammatory response, was significantly increased in the liver of DIO mice after 12 weeks of intervention without difference between the DIO-Vehicle and the DIO-Asprosin groups (Fig. 5A, I), further highlighting the differential effects of asprosin at different stages of obesity development.

Thus, after 8 weeks of DIO, asprosin was able to improve glucose tolerance and fasting glucose, to prevent cholesterol accumulation and protect the liver from triglyceride accumulation in DIO mice, which is consistent with the mechanism observed in vitro in hepatocytes. However, these protective effects of asprosin were not maintained, as most of the differences disappeared after 12 weeks of consumption of an HFHS diet. Interestingly, these results were associated with circulating asprosin levels found upon these interventions, suggesting that upon longer DIO exposure, the secretion of this cytokine by the adipose tissue is dysregulated and the capacity of asprosin to regulate hepatic fat accumulation is lost, generating some resistance to the action of this cytokine in the liver. Therefore, an exploratory study was conducted in humans to study whether an association was observed between circulating asprosin levels and the degree of hepatic steatosis in subjects with different BMI.

Serum asprosin levels associated with prevalence and severity of hepatic steatosis in humans

A total of 314 subjects were enrolled between March 2022 and March 2023, 132 males and 182 females, with a BMI of 26.96 ± 4.62 kg/m2 participated in this study. Serum asprosin concentration was 25.16 ± 14.28 nM (minimum value of 5 nM and maximum of 75.40 nM), showing a large variability (Fig. 6A).

Interestingly, these results indicate that in subjects without fatty liver or with low levels of steatosis, the concentration of asprosin was not elevated, suggesting a relationship between the homeostasis of this adipokine and the onset of fat accumulation in the liver. However, when a higher level of fat accumulation in the liver was reached, even though circulating concentrations of asprosin increased, they did not have the capacity to decrease fat accumulation, an effect similar to that observed in mice after consuming for a longer period of time HFHS diet. Therefore, as an indirect consequence, asprosin levels could potentially serve as a biomarker of liver steatosis progression, which would have to be validated in further studies.

Discussion

Asprosin, a hormone predominantly secreted by adipose tissue, has emerged as a significant player in the complex network of metabolic regulation (32, 33) with particular influence on hepatic metabolism (20, 22). Recent advances in the study of this adipokine place it as a potential target for the treatment of MASLD and metabolic diseases (23, 25). However, the evidence regarding its mechanism of action is scarce and could largely depend on the model studied.

The present work integrates a new mechanism by which asprosin affects hepatic lipid metabolism, where the concentration of this hormone is crucial to determine its beneficial or counterproductive potential for metabolic diseases, particularly MASLD. Deregulation of de novo lipogenesis (DNL) is a critical factor in the pathophysiology of MASLD. Our data from cell culture and in vivo mouse model showed that under normal conditions, asprosin downregulates fatty acid synthesis by reducing the expression and levels of acetyl-CoA carboxylase (ACC) and fatty acid synthase (FAS). In addition, asprosin also increased mitochondrial oxidative activity in mouse-derived primary hepatocyte cultures. The beneficial effect of asprosin extended under conditions of acute fatty acid overload, where it was able to attenuate the pathological increase in lipogenic enzymes and enhance mitochondrial capacity, leading to a reduction in the accumulation of lipid vacuoles, suggesting its potential role in the prevention or attenuation of MASLD. However, experimental data obtained in animals fed a HFHS diet showed contrasting results that differ from the known mechanisms of action for this adipokine.

Two different times of exposure to DIO were considered in the in vivo model to explore the relationship between asprosin function or lack thereof associated with the temporal occurrence of metabolic alterations (34). On one hand, Asprosin treatment in early stages of DIO exposition improved the metabolic consequences of HFHS feeding preventing body weight and fat mass gain, glucose intolerance and hepatic lipid accumulation, functioning as a regulator of hepatic fatty acid metabolism. However, after 12-weeks of energy overload the ability of asprosin to decrease liver lipogenesis was ineffective and this effect was associated with a significant boost in asprosin serum levels. These results suggest that asprosin exerts different responses under physiological or pathological conditions that are highly dependent on the circulating levels of this adipokine.

Asprosin is known to activate the cyclic AMP (cAMP) pathway, which has several beneficial effects in MASLD. These effects include suppression of DNL, associated with an improvement of β-oxidation, in addition to activation of protein kinase A (PKA) and AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK), increased SIRT1-mediated autophagy, and inhibition of pro-fibrogenic pathways in hepatic stellate cells (29). Although we observed a significant increase in AMPK phosphorylation after asprosin stimulation in primary hepatocyte cultures, asprosin administration failed to upregulate the AMPK pathway in the liver in our in vivo model. These results were associated with a significant fluctuation in total AMPK abundance between the different experimental groups. We observed that DIO mice had a significant decrease in total AMPK levels, an effect that was prevented by asprosin after 8 weeks but not after 12 weeks of dietary interventions. This downregulation of total AMPK highly decreased the possibility of observing an effect on the phosphorylation of this protein, especially after 12 weeks of HFHS diet consumption. A previous study reported that asprosin concentration is increased in the serum of mice fed a high-fat diet for 12 weeks. This increase in asprosin levels was associated with a decrease in AMPK activity and the development of steatotic liver disease, while asprosin inhibition reverted this phenotype (35). Likewise, we observed in our study that mice following 12 weeks of DIO had a significant reduction in total and phosphorylated AMPK in the liver, and these same mice presented significantly higher serum asprosin concentrations, indicating an overload of this adipokine in the circulation is associated with a counterproductive effect in the development of MASLD.

Moreover, in apparently healthy adults with different levels of BMI, we found that elevated serum asprosin levels correlated with the presence and severity of hepatic steatosis. These results reveal that asprosin is a potential biomarker of liver steatosis progression, suggesting that significantly higher levels of this hormone are indicative of worsening liver fat accumulation. These findings warrant further investigation to clarify the complexity and potential dual role of asprosin.

The functionality of asprosin may also be altered in other metabolic disorders. For example, Groener et al. observed impaired asprosin responses to hypoglycemia in subjects with greater liver stiffness (36). These subjects had reduced fluctuations in asprosin levels between the hypoglycemic and basal phases and a reduced hormonal response to hypoglycemia, suggesting a potential dysfunction in asprosin signaling in more severe metabolic states. Altered and even detrimental effects of asprosin function in the context of metabolic disease models have been previously reported (35) and also observed in the present study on lipid metabolism, and further studies using diverse models of metabolic disease are necessary to elucidate its role. Changes in the Olfr734 receptor have been shown to occur during the development of metabolic diseases and its loss of function to exacerbate the development of MASLD in mice (37). Additionally, the possibility that asprosin binds to another receptor or may alter a downstream pathway leading to a metabolic alteration in this context cannot be ruled out. How asprosin function may be altered is still a work in progress, and we can only speculate.

Furthermore, during metabolic diseases, elevated asprosin levels may not only result from the disease itself but may also contribute to its progression. Research indicates that high asprosin concentrations can lead to increased insulin resistance, inflammation, and endoplasmic reticulum stress (22, 38, 39, 40). Interestingly, it has been shown that interventions in rodents with asprosin as short as 5–7 days have demonstrated to induce changes in parameters such as body weight (21) and blood glucose (22). More studies are required to understand the mechanisms by which asprosin acts in both normal and disease conditions. However, elevated asprosin levels could serve as a biochemical marker for dysfunctional tissue activity, particularly in white adipose tissue and the liver, during the development of metabolic diseases. Future research should evaluate its potential use in clinical practice and determine which populations would benefit most from its measurement.

Understanding the association between asprosin and MASLD may provide new insights into the metabolic pathways involved in hepatic steatosis. Although elevated asprosin levels may reflect increased adiposity and potential metabolic dysregulation, further research is needed to determine how asprosin plays a direct regulatory role in hepatic lipid accumulation and how this action may be lost when there is an increase in the development of obesity. Elucidating these mechanisms could still contribute to the development of targeted interventions for MASLD and related metabolic disorders.
