Beyond the Crisis: The Role of Positive Psychological Capital in the Association with Suicidal Ideation Among College Students—An Empirical Study Based on a Multiple Chain Mediation Model
Authors: Hongmin Chen, Zixuan Qin, Tian’ai Qi, Lei Su, Shitong Zheng, Lei Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCrisis life events are important risk factors for suicidal ideation among college students. This study aimed to examine the mechanisms underlying the association between crisis life events and suicidal ideation, with a particular focus on the multiple chain mediating effects of social support, positive psychological capital, and depression.MethodsA cross-sectional convenience sampling design was used. Data were collected from 1296 college students across nine universities in China via an online questionnaire (mean age = 19.46 ± 3.05 years). Participants completed the self-developed Questionnaire on Crisis Life Events for College Students, the Self-Rating Idea of Suicide Scale (SIOSS), the Social Support Rating Scale (SSRS), the Self-Rating Depression Scale (SDS), and the Positive Psychological Capital Questionnaire (PPQ). A manifest variable path analysis model was constructed in Mplus 8.3, and the bias-corrected bootstrap method with 5000 resamples was used to estimate 95% confidence intervals for the indirect effects.Results(1) Crisis life events were significantly and positively associated with suicidal ideation among college students (β = 0.178, p < 0.001). (2) Social support, positive psychological capital, and depression showed multiple chain mediating roles in the association between crisis life events and suicidal ideation, with a significant total indirect effect (β = 0.196, p < 0.001). (3) Before social support was included in the model, the mediating roles of resilience, hope, and optimism among the dimensions of positive psychological capital were significant, with resilience showing the largest effect size (single mediation: β = 0.068, p < 0.001; chain mediation: β = 0.029, p < 0.001); after social support was included, the mediating effect of self-efficacy became significant (β = 0.012, p < 0.001), and the mediating effect of optimism was substantially strengthened, with its significance level increasing from p < 0.05 to p < 0.001.ConclusionCrisis life events positively predicted suicidal ideation among college students. Depression, social support, and positive psychological capital played parallel mediating roles in the association between crisis life events and suicidal ideation among college students, and jointly formed a chain mediation pathway. Among the dimensions of positive psychological capital, resilience, hope, and optimism mediated the association between crisis life events and suicidal ideation among college students, with resilience showing the most prominent role. In addition, after social support was included in the model, the mediating role of self-efficacy became significant, and the significance of the mediating effect of optimism was further enhanced. These findings suggest that universities should integrate the construction of social support systems with psychological capital training when developing prevention strategies for suicidal ideation among college students.

Introduction

Suicide is the second leading cause of death among young people worldwide and the second leading cause of death among college students globally.1,2 Surveys have shown that approximately 6% of college students worldwide have attempted suicide, and 18% have experienced suicidal ideation.3 In recent years, suicidal ideation and suicidal behaviors have shown an increasing trend among youth aged 18–25 years.4,5 Suicidal behavior is not a single, static event, but rather a dynamic developmental process. As a precursor within this dynamic process,6 suicidal ideation is considered one of the most sensitive indicators for assessing suicide risk.7,8 Suicidal ideation refers to the presence of thoughts or ideas about death, suicide, and serious self-harm, including plans, steps, and anticipated outcomes related to suicidal behavior.9,10 To mitigate suicide-related risks among college students, it is necessary to further investigate the correlates of suicidal ideation in this population, which has important implications for promoting college students’ mental health.

Crisis Life Events as Correlates of Suicidal Ideation

Crisis life events refer to major life events that can elicit intense negative affective responses.11–15 Among college students, crisis life events commonly include interpersonal conflicts, academic stress, health and adjustment difficulties, loss events, such as the loss of an intimate relationship, a significant other, or property, as well as experiences of punishment or disciplinary action.16 Negative life events may be associated with an increased risk of suicidal ideation by inducing psychological stress and disrupting individuals’ psychological equilibrium.17 Multiple empirical studies have shown that negative life events are one of the critical predictors of suicidal ideation among college students. For example, a survey of 9808 Chinese college students found that among students who reported having had suicidal ideation recently, 46% had experienced negative life events in the past year, with the most commonly reported events involving academic and work-related stress, romantic or marital conflicts, and financial difficulties.18 The Stress-Diathesis Model posits that psychological disorders or abnormal behaviors arise from the interaction between external stressors and individual diathetic vulnerabilities.19 In this model, stressors primarily refer to external factors such as life events and environmental stimuli, whereas diathesis encompasses internal factors such as genetic predispositions, cognitive styles, and childhood experiences.20 Based on this framework, the present study proposed Hypothesis 1 (H1): Crisis life events positively predict suicidal ideation among college students.

The Chain Mediating Role of Social Support and Depression

Depression is a major type of mood disorder, typically characterized by intense feelings of sadness and hopelessness. Normal depressive feelings are generally considered an individual’ s response to recent stressors.21 The occurrence of negative life events often induces negative emotional states such as depression, and the accumulation of these negative emotions may reduce individuals’ life satisfaction and contribute to the development of suicidal ideation.22 According to the Stress-Diathesis Model, individuals who experience more negative life events and adopt maladaptive coping strategies, such as emotion-focused coping, are at a higher risk of developing depression.23 Previous studies have confirmed that negative life events are significantly and positively associated with depressive symptoms among college students.24–26 In addition, depression has been shown to significantly predict suicidal ideation.27

Social support refers to the extent to which individuals perceive acceptance, care, and concern from significant others, and is regarded as an important resource for enhancing well-being.28–31 According to social support theory, social support is considered a key external resource that can buffer the psychological impact of negative life events. A large body of empirical research indicates that social support from family, friends, and school can mitigate the negative emotional impact of depression triggered by negative life events by enhancing an individual’s sense of belonging, thereby reducing the incidence of suicidal ideation.32–35 Based on this, the present study proposed Hypothesis 2 (H2): Social support and depression play a chain mediating role between crisis life events and suicidal ideation.

The Chain Mediating Role of Positive Psychological Capital and Depression

Positive psychological capital (PsyCap) refers to the positive psychological resources that individuals develop and display during the course of their growth and development.36 It helps promote individuals’ awareness of their own developmental potential and enhances their well-being. According to the Conservation of Resources Theory, when individuals lack sufficient resources to cope with environmental demands or stress triggered by negative events, they are more likely to experience depressive emotions. Positive psychological capital is therefore regarded as a core internal resource that may help individuals resist stress and reduce negative emotions.37 Empirical studies have shown that positive psychological capital can effectively buffer the effect of negative life events on depression.38–40 When facing negative life events, individuals with lower levels of positive psychological capital are more likely to report higher levels of suicidal ideation, whereas those with higher levels of positive psychological capital are better able to adjust their psychological state, thereby reducing suicidal ideation.41,42 Based on this, the present study proposed Hypothesis 3 (H3): Positive psychological capital and depression play a chain mediating role between crisis life events and suicidal ideation.

The Multiple Chain Mediating Roles of Social Support, Positive Psychological Capital, and Depression

Within the framework of the Stress-Diathesis Model, social support, as an external protective resource, and positive psychological capital, as an internal protective resource, jointly constitute a dual protective system that helps individuals cope with crises. These two resources may not only separately buffer the negative effects of crisis life events as external stressors, but also work synergistically to further reduce the risk of depression through a resource-gain pathway, thereby decreasing suicidal ideation. Conservation of Resources Theory also provides a specific explanation for this synergistic mechanism: psychological resources such as self-esteem, optimism, and self-efficacy originate from shared environmental and developmental conditions.43 Individuals can maintain and enhance their internal psychological capital by obtaining external resources such as social support, thereby buffering the adverse effects of negative life events on mental health. One study found that the association between social support and depression was not a direct linear relationship, but was fully mediated by psychological capital.44 Similarly, a study of college students found that positive psychological capital fully mediated the relationship between social support and life satisfaction. Social support not only provides practical assistance and informational guidance but also enhances individuals’ positive psychological capital.45 Based on this, the present study hypothesized that social support and positive psychological capital may be significantly associated with lower levels of depression and suicidal ideation. Therefore, the present study proposed Hypothesis 4 (H4): Social support, positive psychological capital, and depression play multiple chain mediating roles between crisis life events and suicidal ideation.

The Mediating Roles of the Dimensions of Positive Psychological Capital

Positive psychological capital (PsyCap) comprises four dimensions: self-efficacy, optimism, resilience, and hope.36 An empirical study of adolescents found that each dimension of positive psychological capital plays an important role in reducing depression levels and improving mental health. For adolescents, hope has been found to be significantly associated with mild depressive symptoms rather than severe depressive symptoms.40 Multiple studies have shown that resilience fully mediates the relationship between social support and suicidal attitudes among college students.46,47 Higher levels of resilience are associated with better mental health among college students. Compared with individuals with low resilience, those with high resilience are less sensitive to subsequent negative events and are able to maintain a more stable mental state.48,49 Other studies have found that self-efficacy partially mediates the relationship between social anxiety and depression among college students, suggesting that enhancing self-efficacy may help prevent depressive emotions.50 Optimism has also been regarded as an effective predictor of mental health among college students and is significantly involved in the relationship between life events and psychological well-being.51 Moreover, a more important finding is that, compared with any single dimension, the integrated PsyCap-HERO structure of positive psychological capital dimensions can more effectively predict reductions in depressive symptoms.39 Based on this, the present study proposed Hypothesis 5 (H5): The four dimensions of positive psychological capital, namely self-efficacy, optimism, resilience, and hope, play different roles in the chain mediation pathways between crisis life events and suicidal ideation.

In summary, current academic research on the factors influencing suicidal ideation among college students tends to examine only the role of a single external factor (such as negative life events or social support) or internal factor (such as depression or psychological capital) in isolation, either individually or in terms of their interaction. Such studies lack a systematic and holistic approach. In fact, suicidal ideation among college students is often the result of a complex interplay of internal and external factors. When studying internal and external risk factors, it is important to consider relevant internal and external protective factors as well. First, drawing on the Stress-Diathesis Model, this study integrates external stressors (crisis life events) and external protective factors (social support), as well as internal risk factors (depression) and internal resources (positive psychological capital), into a single framework to examine the synergistic effects of internal and external protective factors in reducing suicidal ideation among college students. Second, this study further elucidates the critical roles played by the four dimensions of psychological capital—optimism, hope, resilience, and self-efficacy—with the aim of providing a theoretical foundation for future intervention studies. Methods

Study Design

This study employed convenience sampling and collected data through an online survey. All variables were measured at the same time point, with the aim of testing a multiple chain mediation model of the association between crisis life events and suicidal ideation among college students based on the Stress-Diathesis Model (see Figure 1).

Participants

The sample size was calculated according to the requirements for Structural Equation Modeling (SEM). In general, at least 10–20 samples are required for each estimated parameter.52 The multiple chain mediation model in the present study involved a total of 64 estimated parameters. Therefore, based on this criterion, 640–1280 participants were required.

The participants in this study were college students recruited from nine universities in China, including universities directly administered by the Ministry of Education, provincial key universities, and provincial general universities. All participants agreed to take part in the study by signing an electronic informed consent form, which clearly stated that all data would be collected and stored anonymously and that no personally identifiable information, such as names or student identification numbers, would be recorded. In accordance with established ethical principles, the researchers informed participants that their data would be used only for academic research purposes and would be kept strictly confidential. The researchers emphasize that participation in this study is entirely voluntary. Participants may withdraw at any time during the survey if they feel uncomfortable, as well as withdraw their data at any time without providing any reason, and will not experience any adverse consequences as a result. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) being a full-time undergraduate or graduate student; (2) being able to independently read and understand the questionnaire content; (3) voluntarily agreeing to participate and signing the electronic informed consent form. Data were collected from January to February 2023. A total of 1500 questionnaires were distributed, and 1296 valid questionnaires were retained after data screening, yielding an effective response rate of 86.4%. Questionnaires were deemed invalid according to the following criteria: (1) The entire questionnaire was completed in less than 5 minutes (obviously too short); (2) The same option was selected for 10 or more consecutive items; (3) The number of missed items exceeds 10% of the total number of items; (4) The score on the SIOSS’s concealment factor is ≥ 4 (considered invalid according to the scale instructions). The final sample was composed as follows: males accounted for 31.9% of the sample (n = 413), and females accounted for 68.1% (n = 883). Regarding grade distribution, freshmen accounted for 42.3% (n = 548), sophomores for 19.4% (n = 251), juniors for 17.9% (n = 232), seniors for 17.9% (n = 232), and graduate students for 2.5% (n = 33). Participants ranged in age from 17 to 26 years (M = 19.46, SD = 3.05). In terms of family residence, students from urban areas accounted for 72.3% (n = 937), whereas those from rural areas accounted for 27.7% (n = 359). Student leaders accounted for 48.6% of the total sample (n = 630). Regarding self-rated family economic status, 5.9% of students (n = 76) reported their family economic status as “very good,” 25.2% (n = 327) as “good,” 54.4% (n = 705) as “average,” 12.3% (n = 159) as “poor,” and 2.2% (n = 29) as “very poor.”

Measures

Questionnaire on Crisis Life Events for College Students

This study employed a self-developed Questionnaire on Crisis Life Events for College Students. The questionnaire was developed based on General Adaptation Syndrome (GAS),53 Through a literature review, with reference to the Adolescent Life Events Scale developed by Xianchen Liu et al,54 combined with open-ended questionnaire surveys to collect common crisis life events among college students, an initial pool of questionnaire items was formulated. After two psychology professors and two doctoral students examined the items for content validity, 32 items with satisfactory content representativeness were retained. Following exploratory factor analysis (EFA) and confirmatory factor analysis (CFA), and after removing inappropriate items, the questionnaire was finalized to include 5 dimensions and a total of 26 items, respectively labeled as: life adaptation crisis (3 items; eg, “being unaccustomed to seasonal climate changes”), personal development crisis (4 items; eg, “being dissatisfied with one’s major”), family crisis (5 items; eg, “being scolded or beaten by parents”), interpersonal crisis (6 items; eg, “frequent conflicts with others”), and academic crisis (8 items; eg, “failing a course examination”). For each item, participants were first asked to indicate whether the event had occurred since their enrollment. If the event was rated as “not occurring,” it was scored as 0. If the event was rated as “occurring,” participants were then asked to evaluate the perceived severity of the crisis using a 5-point Likert scale (ranging from 1 = “no crisis” to 5 = “extreme crisis”). Accordingly, the score for each item ranged from 0 to 5, and the total score ranged from 0 to 130, with higher scores indicating greater perceived severity of crisis life events. In the present study, the Cronbach’s α coefficient for the questionnaire was 0.92. The Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) yielded the following fit indices: NFI = 0.86, CFI = 0.87, IFI = 0.87, and RMSEA = 0.08, indicating acceptable construct validity of the questionnaire.

Self-Rating Idea of Suicide Scale

Suicidal ideation was assessed using the Self-rating Idea of Suicide Scale (SIOSS) developed by Xia et al55 This scale consists of 26 dichotomous items, answered with “Yes” or “No,” and comprises four factors: hopelessness (12 items; eg, “I am convinced that life is cruel to me”), optimism (5 items; eg, “My daily life is filled with things that interest me”), sleep disturbance (4 items; eg, “I sleep restlessly and am easily awakened”), and concealment factor (5 items; eg, “Sometimes I also tell lies”). The total score for suicidal ideation was obtained by summing the scores of the hopelessness factor, the reverse-scored optimism factor, and the sleep disturbance factor, with total scores ranging from 0 to 26. Higher scores indicated greater levels of suicidal ideation. In the main mediation analyses of the present study, suicidal ideation was treated as a continuous variable. Meanwhile, according to the scale manual, a total score of 12 or higher indicates the presence of suicidal ideation, serving as the clinical cutoff for identifying high-risk participants. A score of 4 or higher on the response bias factor was considered indicative of invalid responses. In the present study, the Cronbach’s α coefficient of the SIOSS was 0.85.

Social Support Rating Scale

Social support was measured using the Social Support Rating Scale (SSRS) developed by Xiao.56 The scale consists of 10 items covering three dimensions: objective support, subjective support, and utilization of support. Higher total scores indicate higher levels of perceived social support. In the present study, the Cronbach’s α coefficient of the SSRS was 0.70.

Self-Rating Depression Scale

Depression was assessed using the Self-Rating Depression Scale (SDS) developed by Zung.57 The scale consists of 20 items requiring participants to rate the frequency of depressive symptoms experienced during the past week or at the present moment. A 4-point Likert scale was used, ranging from 1 (“rarely or none of the time”) to 4 (“most or all of the time”), with 10 items being reverse-scored. Higher total scores indicate more severe depressive symptoms. In the present study, the Cronbach’s α coefficient of the SDS was 0.88.

Positive Psychological Capital Questionnaire

Positive psychological capital was measured using the Positive Psychological Capital Questionnaire (PPQ) developed by Zhang.58 The questionnaire consists of 26 items covering four dimensions: self-efficacy, optimism, resilience, and hope. The participants’ overall level of positive psychological capital was assessed by summing the scores on the four subscales. A 7‑point Likert scale was used, ranging from 1 (“strongly disagree”) to 7 (“strongly agree”), with 5 items being reverse-scored. Higher scores indicate higher levels of positive psychological capital. In the present study, the Cronbach’s α coefficient for the total scale was 0.94. The Cronbach’s α coefficients for the subscales were 0.90 for self-efficacy, 0.89 for optimism, 0.77 for resilience, and 0.88 for hope.

Procedure

This study was approved by the Psychology Ethics Committee of Capital Normal University (approval number: CNU-202212001). In addition, permission to use all measurement instruments was obtained from the original authors via email. Prior to data collection, the researchers explained the purpose and significance of the study to the participants in detail. Given the sensitive nature of the content assessing depression and suicidal ideation, the titles of these two questionnaires were not presented in the survey to avoid potential psychological discomfort among participants. After data collection, the researchers provided all participants with comprehensive psychological assistance referral information, including contact details for the university counseling center and off-campus mental health professionals. Participants received corresponding compensation upon completion of the questionnaires. For participants identified as high-risk based on a total score of 12 or higher on the Self-Rating Idea of Suicide Scale (SIOSS), the research team proactively contacted them within 24 hours following the established protocol to conduct a safety assessment. Prior informed consent was obtained from participants regarding the possibility of breaching confidentiality when necessary to ensure their safety. If an assessment confirms that there is a real risk of self-harm or harm to others, the principle of confidentiality will be waived, and the relevant information will be immediately reported to the school’s counseling center. The counseling center will then notify the participant’s legal guardian in accordance with the law, while simultaneously initiating the school’s crisis intervention and referral procedures to ensure the participant’s life safety. In addition, for the 17-year-old minor participant in this study, following review by the Psychology Ethics Committee of Capital Normal University, the study obtained additional informed consent from the participant’s legal guardian. The informed consent form clearly outlined the purpose of the study, anonymity, the right to voluntarily withdraw, and how to seek assistance. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and relevant Chinese laws and regulations.

Data Analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 28.0 and Mplus 8.3. Independent-samples t‑tests were used to examine differences in the main variables across gender, student leadership status (yes vs no), and family residence (urban vs rural). One‑way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was employed to test differences in the variables across grade levels and family economic status. Pearson correlation analysis was performed to examine the relationships among the main variables (all core variables examined in this study were continuous, and suicidal ideation was analyzed as a continuous variable rather than as a dichotomous cutoff). Multicollinearity was assessed by examining the variance inflation factor (VIF) for all predictor variables. Mplus 8.3 was used to construct the multiple chain mediation model and to test the mediation effects, with all reported effect sizes being standardized coefficients. Maximum likelihood estimation with robust standard errors (MLR) was used to address potential non-normality. All variables were treated as observed composite scores (rather than latent variables). Thus, the path model in this study was a saturated model (all possible paths were estimated), and the conventional fit indices showed perfect values. Accordingly, the robustness of the indirect effects was primarily evaluated based on Bootstrap confidence intervals. To address the issue of non-normality in the variables, this study employed an MLR estimation method robust to non-normality and used bias-corrected bootstrap confidence intervals (5000 resamples) for all indirect effects. Prior to the formal analysis, outliers were removed from the data, and invalid questionnaires, such as those with duplicate responses or missing answers, were excluded to ensure the objectivity and completeness of the dataset. Missing data were minimal in this study (less than 0.5% of all item responses). After excluding invalid questionnaires, the remaining missing data were handled using the Full Information Maximum Likelihood (FIML) method in Mplus, which estimates parameters using all available data without the need for individual data point deletion.

Results

Common Method Bias

To minimize the potential common method bias inherent in self‑report questionnaires, this study adopted the procedural control methods recommended by Zhou and Long.59 Specifically, participants were assured of anonymity and confidentiality during data collection, and several reverse‑scored items were included to reduce response bias. Statistically, we first conducted Harman’s single‑factor test.60 The unrotated exploratory factor analysis extracted 20 factors with eigenvalues greater than 1, and the first factor accounted for 20.54% of the total variance, which was well below the 40% critical threshold, preliminarily suggesting no severe common method bias. Subsequently, the controlling for the effects of an unmeasured latent methods factor (ULMC) approach was further employed to validate this finding.61 A baseline model was constructed using five trait factors: crisis life events, suicidal ideation, social support, positive psychological capital, and depression. After incorporating an unrelated common method factor, the model fit did not improve, and the changes in all fit indices remained below conventional standards (ΔCFI and ΔTLI increased by no more than 0.1, while ΔRMSEA and ΔSRMR decreased by no more than 0.05). Based on the above examination results, it can be concluded that there are no serious common method biases in this study.

Descriptive Statistics and Correlation Analyses

We conducted difference tests on the demographic variables. The results showed that male students reported significantly higher levels of crisis life events than female students (t (569.54) = 4.26, p < 0.001, d = 0.30), whereas female students reported significantly higher levels of social support than male students (t (708.41) = −3.90, p < 0.001, d = −0.25). Significant differences across grades were found for crisis life events (F (4, 1291) = 10.71, p < 0.001, η2 = 0.032), social support (F (4, 1291) = 4.09, p < 0.01, η2 = 0.013), positive psychological capital (F (4, 1291) = 3.33, p < 0.01, η2 = 0.010), and depression (F (4, 1291) = 5.57, p < 0.001, η2 = 0.017). Relative to freshmen, students in their sophomore (p < 0.05), junior (p < 0.001), and senior (p < 0.001) years reported significantly greater exposure to crisis life events. Social support was significantly lower among juniors (p < 0.05) and seniors (p < 0.01) relative to freshmen. Juniors also scored significantly lower than freshmen on positive psychological capital (p < 0.05). Additionally, sophomores (p < 0.01) and juniors (p < 0.01) reported higher levels of depression compared to freshmen. Student leaders reported significantly lower levels of suicidal ideation (t (1294) = −2.96, p < 0.01, d = −0.16) and depression (t (1294) = −2.92, p < 0.01, d = −0.16) than non-leaders, but higher levels of social support (t (1294) = 4.83, p < 0.001, d = 0.27) and positive psychological capital (t (1294) = 5.53, p < 0.001, d = 0.31). Urban students reported significantly lower levels of depression than rural students (t (1294) = −3.05, p < 0.01, d = −0.19) and higher levels of positive psychological capital (t (720.33) = 2.23, p < 0.05, d = 0.13). Significant differences across family socioeconomic status were observed for crisis life events (F (4, 1291) = 7.51, p < 0.001, η2 = 0.023), suicidal ideation (F (4, 1291) = 8.61, p < 0.001, η2 = 0.026), social support (F (4, 1291) = 10.72, p < 0.001, η2 = 0.032), positive psychological capital (F (4, 1291) = 12.85, p < 0.001, η2 = 0.038), and depression (F (4, 1291) = 9.18, p < 0.001, η2 = 0.028). Students from families with “very good” or “good” family socioeconomic status reported lower levels of crisis life events, suicidal ideation, and depression, and higher levels of social support and positive psychological capital, compared to students from families with “average”, “poor”, or “very poor” family socioeconomic status. Based on these findings, gender, grades, student leadership, family residence, and family socioeconomic status were included as control variables in subsequent analyses.

The Relationship Between Crisis Life Events and Suicidal Ideation: A Multiple Chain Mediation Analysis

As shown in Table 1, all variables were significantly correlated with one another, and no multicollinearity issue was detected, providing preliminary support for the hypothesized mediation pathways. To further test the theoretically hypothesized model (see Figure 1), a multiple-chain mediation analysis was conducted. After controlling for gender, grade, family residence, student leadership status, and family socioeconomic status, a mediation model was constructed and tested with crisis life events as the antecedent variable, suicidal ideation as the outcome variable, and social support, positive psychological capital, and depression as the mediators, to examine both the direct association between crisis life events and suicidal ideation and the indirect associations through the aforementioned mediating variables.

The chain mediation model in this study was estimated using manifest variable path analysis. Because all possible direct paths were estimated, the model was a just‑identified saturated model (df = 0). According to the fit criteria proposed by Hu and Bentler,62 the conventional fit indices (CFI, TLI, RMSEA, SRMR) all indicated perfect fit for the saturated model (CFI = 1.000, TLI = 1.000, RMSEA = 0.000, SRMR = 0.000). Although the saturated model does not allow for the evaluation of model parsimony, its mathematical property of zero degrees of freedom ensures that the sample covariance matrix is exactly reproduced, thereby satisfying the fundamental requirements for model acceptability in psychometrics.

To further verify the robustness of the model structure, this study used the bias-corrected percentile bootstrap method to test for mediating effects and calculated 95% confidence intervals. Generate 5000 Bootstrap resampled samples based on the original data, and perform model fitting on each resampled sample. The results showed that the indirect effects of all mediation pathways were statistically significant (see Table 2 for effect sizes of each specific pathway), indicating that the estimated indirect effects were stable and reliable. The Multiple Chain Mediation Model Linking Crisis Life Events to Suicidal Ideation.

As shown in Table 2, crisis life events had a significant direct effect on suicidal ideation among college students, with a direct effect of β = 0.178 (95% CI [0.129, 0.228]), accounting for 47.47% of the total effect, supporting Hypothesis 1 (H1). The total indirect effect between them was β = 0.196 (95% CI [0.162, 0.231]), accounting for 52.27% of the total effect. This finding indicates that 52.27% of the total association between crisis life events and suicidal ideation was transmitted through the seven indirect pathways constituted by social support, positive psychological capital, and depression. Among these, the indirect effect through the single mediation pathway of positive psychological capital (Path 2) was the largest (β = 0.046, accounting for 12.27% of the total effect), followed by the indirect effect through the single mediation pathway of depression (Path 3, β = 0.045, accounting for 12.00% of the total effect) and the indirect effect through the chain mediation pathway of social support and positive psychological capital (Path 4, β = 0.041, accounting for 10.93% of the total effect), both of which were also relatively substantial. Furthermore, the chain mediation pathway of social support and depression (Path 5, β = 0.008, accounting for 2.13% of the total effect) supported Hypothesis 2 (H2); the chain mediation pathway of positive psychological capital and depression (Path 6, β = 0.017, accounting for 4.53% of the total effect) supported Hypothesis 3 (H3); and the chain mediation pathway of social support, positive psychological capital, and depression (Path 7, β = 0.015, accounting for 4.00% of the total effect) supported Hypothesis 4 (H4).

Discussion

Characteristics of Crisis Life Events in the College Student Population

The present study found significant gender differences in crisis life events, with male students reporting higher levels of crisis life events than female students. This may be related to different social role expectations and the greater competitive pressure faced by young men. According to social role theory, society typically places higher career expectations on men, requiring them to demonstrate greater competitiveness and the ability to withstand stress.63,64 In addition, upper-grade students reported significantly higher levels of exposure to crisis life events than freshmen. This phenomenon may be attributed to factors such as increased academic pressure, more complex interpersonal relationships, and growing uncertainty regarding future employment as students advance in grade level. Furthermore, compared with students from families with better socioeconomic status (“very good” or “good”), those from families with below-average economic status (“average”, “poor”, or “very poor”) reported higher levels of crisis life events, depression, and suicidal ideation. Economic pressure not only directly restricts students’ quality of life, but may also be accompanied by a range of psychological problems, such as increased depression and negative psychological reactions, including suicidal ideation. In contrast, students from families with better economic status (“very good” or “good”) have more resources and options to cope with adversity, and therefore are at a relatively lower risk of developing suicidal ideation.65,66

Exploring the Link Between Crisis Life Events and Suicidal Ideation in the College Population

The analysis results indicate that the direct effect of life crisis events on suicidal ideation accounts for 47.47% of the total effect, highlighting that crisis life events are one of the factors closely associated with suicidal ideation, which supports the conclusions of previous empirical studies.17,18,67,68

The Stress-Diathesis Model provides a fundamental explanatory framework for understanding this pathway. When crisis life events, as external stressors, exceed an individual’s psychological adaptive threshold, they may directly activate the cognitive priming process of suicidal ideation.20 This direct pathway suggests that the stressor itself constitutes a risk channel independent of individual diathesis. As exogenous stressors, crisis life events may be associated with suicide risk through the following two pathways: on the one hand, they are associated with the onset of psychological crisis as acute stressors; on the other hand, they are associated with the decline of individual psychological homeostasis as chronic stressors. The Integrated Motivational-Volitional (IMV) model provides a further process-oriented theoretical explanation for this pathway: the role of crisis life events corresponds precisely to the core mechanism of the pre-motivational phase in the IMV model, in which major negative life events trigger psychological stress responses, which in turn are associated with the decline of psychological stability and the formation of suicidal ideation.69,70

Thus, the pre-motivational phase may play an important role in the developmental process of suicidal behavior, providing a theoretical reference for the early identification of college students at high risk for suicide. Based on the above findings, suicide prevention efforts in universities could focus on the following two aspects: first, proactively monitoring the occurrence of students’ crisis life events and assessing their levels of exposure and severity; second, establishing a tiered intervention system to identify high-risk individuals early on and intervene promptly after an event occurs, thereby reducing the risk of suicidal behavior in this population.

Multiple Chain Mediating Roles of Social Support, Positive Psychological Capital, and Depression

The present study found that social support, positive psychological capital, and depression played chain mediating roles in the relationship between crisis life events and suicidal ideation. From the perspective of the Stress-Diathesis Model, individuals’ psychological and behavioral outcomes are the result of the interaction between external stressors and intrinsic diathesis.19 Within this framework, crisis life events constitute external stressors, while low levels of social support and insufficient positive psychological capital are regarded as important diathetic factors. The chain mediation results of the present study are consistent with this theoretical expectation.

First, depression played a mediating role in the relationship between crisis life events and suicidal ideation (Path 3, accounting for 12% of the total effect). According to the Stress-Diathesis Model, when external stressors exceed an individual’s psychological adaptive threshold, individuals with higher diathesis are more likely to experience negative emotional reactions such as depression.19 The motivational phase of the Integrated Motivational-Volitional (IMV) model further explains this process: experiences of defeat or humiliation may be theoretically associated with individuals’ sense of “entrapment,” which in turn is associated with suicidal ideation. Depression is the core emotional manifestation of this sense of entrapment. When individuals encounter crisis life events that exceed their psychological adaptive threshold, their cognitive appraisal system may encode such events as uncontrollable stressors, thereby forming a maladaptive “defeat–entrapment” cycle.41,69–72 Within this theoretical framework, depressive affect may be associated with risk in two respects: on the one hand, as the emotional manifestation of entrapment; on the other hand, as being associated with negative cognitive biases and impaired problem-solving ability. These factors jointly contribute to the association with suicidal ideation. This pathway suggests that, within the Stress-Diathesis Model, depression serves as a core emotional pathway through which external stressors trigger psychological crises in individuals.

Second, positive psychological capital (PsyCap) not only played a single mediating role in the relationship between crisis life events and suicidal ideation (Path 2, accounting for 12.27% of the total effect), but also played a chain mediating role with depression (Path 6, accounting for 4.53% of the total effect). Within the framework of the Stress-Diathesis Model, positive psychological capital can be regarded as a protective factor in the individual diathesis system. Conservation of Resources Theory further points out that stressors from the external environment or negative life events are associated with adverse emotional reactions such as depression, while positive psychological capital, as an individual internal psychological resource, may be associated with individuals’ ability to cope with the resource depletion effects brought about by stressors,37 and with their ability to cope with life challenges and pressures, thereby being associated with a lower risk of mental health problems.36

Third, the present study further revealed the roles of social support (external resources) and positive psychological capital (internal resources) in the relationships among crisis life events, depression, and suicidal ideation. The results showed that social support played a single mediating role in the relationship between crisis life events and suicidal ideation (Path 1, accounting for 6.4% of the total effect), and played chain mediating roles with depression (Path 5, accounting for 2.13% of the total effect) and with positive psychological capital (Path 4, accounting for 10.93% of the total effect). Social support and positive psychological capital serve as important external and internal protective factors, respectively, in buffering the relationships among external stressors, individuals’ internal negative emotional reactions, and suicidal ideation. Conservation of Resources Theory provides a possible explanation for this association from the perspective of resource gain: obtaining external resources such as social support may be associated with individuals’ maintenance and accumulation of internal psychological capital, and the enhancement of internal psychological capital is in turn negatively associated with the adverse impact of negative events on mental health.43 This also suggests that enhancing social support and positive psychological capital may be associated with reducing the risk of suicidal ideation among college students (eg, Path 3, Path 4), and may serve as promising avenues for future intervention research. It should be noted that while some indirect effects (such as Path 5) reached statistical significance due to the large sample size, their effect sizes are extremely small, indicating limited practical significance.

Mediating Roles of the Dimensions of Positive Psychological Capital

The present study further found that, in the context of crisis life events, resilience, hope, and optimism played single mediating roles in the relationship between crisis life events and suicidal ideation among college students, and all played chain mediating roles with depression. Among these, the role of resilience was the most prominent. Resilience theory posits that adverse triggers or high-risk events do not necessarily lead to negative emotions or psychological problems. Rather, the key lies in whether individuals possess protective factors to cope with adversity and high-risk events. As an important psychological resource, resilience enables individuals to effectively adapt and respond even when facing pressure, setbacks, or adversity.73,74 In addition, the mediating effects of optimism and hope can also be explained within the framework of the Broaden-and-Build Theory of Positive Emotions, providing a new explanatory dimension for the field of stress coping.75 Specifically, hope can broaden individuals’ cognitive and action resources in stressful situations, expand their behavioral repertoire, and provide sustained motivation and a clear sense of direction for coping with challenges. Similarly, an optimistic disposition can enhance individuals’ confidence and positive expectations about the future, alleviate excessive worry and fear about negative outcomes, broaden their attentional scope, and increase cognitive flexibility, thereby enabling individuals to face life challenges in a more positive and adaptive manner.

Importantly, the indirect effects of self-efficacy did not reach statistical significance, whether as a single mediating variable or in a chain mediation pathway with depression. This finding is consistent with previous research, which found that the mediating role of self-efficacy between interpersonal sensitivity and depressive symptoms was not significant among Chinese college students.76 According to self-efficacy theory, successful or failed experiences in individual behavior have the strongest influence on self-efficacy, while emotional and physiological states also participate in its formation process.77 In the context of multiple crisis events such as academic pressure and interpersonal conflicts, college students’ evaluations of their own abilities may change, leading to dynamic fluctuations in their self-efficacy.78 However, after social support was included in the model, the mediating effect of self-efficacy became significant, and the significance of optimism was also notably enhanced. Conservation of Resources Theory posits that psychological capital such as self-efficacy and optimism does not emerge spontaneously, but is gradually shaped through interactive processes within a shared environmental and developmental framework.43 When individuals are in a supportive social environment, they are more likely to receive higher levels of objective social support (such as financial or material resources) and subjective social support (such as emotional encouragement and comfort), which also leads to the development of self-efficacy and optimism. Based on the findings of the present study, when college students encounter negative life events, resilience serves as a powerful resource for coping with stress. A well-functioning social support system can facilitate the enhancement of college students’ self-efficacy and optimism, thereby strengthening their ability to cope with stress. These findings may provide empirical support for practical interventions aimed at helping college students cope with crisis life events.

Limitations and Future Research

Although this study yielded some meaningful findings, several limitations remain to be further addressed in future research. First, this study mainly relied on self‑report data. Retrospective reports of crisis life events are susceptible to memory bias, and the measurement of suicidal ideation was not combined with clinical assessment, which may have missed individuals at potential risk. Future research could enhance study validity by integrating multiple data sources (eg, behavioral records, physiological indicators) and adopting longitudinal study designs.

Second, this study adopted a cross-sectional design, which does not allow for the determination of causal relationships among the variables. Although this study hypothesized a sequential pathway from crisis life events to suicidal ideation based on the Stress-Diathesis Model, the cross-sectional design cannot rule out reverse or reciprocal causality. For example, students with pre-existing suicidal ideation or higher levels of depressive symptoms may exhibit negative cognitive biases,79 tending to interpret neutral or ambiguous events as more threatening, thereby overreporting the severity of crisis life events while underestimating their perceived levels of social support and positive psychological capital. Furthermore, low levels of positive psychological capital may render individuals more vulnerable to crisis life events (eg, academic failure or interpersonal conflicts due to low self-efficacy). In addition, this study did not collect baseline data on the participants’ mental health, and a reciprocal reinforcing cycle may also exist between depression and suicidal ideation.80 Future longitudinal studies could conduct pre- and post-tests on college students’ mental health status and adopt cross-lagged panel models to examine bidirectional causal pathways, thereby further clarifying the interaction mechanisms between crisis life events and suicidal ideation.

Third, although the present study found that social support significantly enhanced the mediating effect of self-efficacy, it did not further explore which dimensions of social support and to what extent contributed to this effect. Future research is still needed to conduct in-depth examinations of these dimensions to clarify the main influencing factors.

Fourth, cultural background and cross-cultural applicability. The sample of this study was drawn from nine Chinese universities, with a predominantly a collectivist cultural background. The finding that self-efficacy showed a significant mediating effect only after social support was included may be related to the fact that, in collectivist culture, self-efficacy is more dependent on external feedback and relational support (eg, family affirmation, peer recognition), and is therefore more susceptible to the influence of social support rather than purely intrinsic self-evaluation. By contrast, in individualistic cultures, self-efficacy may have a stronger direct association with suicidal ideation and be less dependent on social support.81,82 Therefore, caution is needed when generalizing the conclusions of this study to other cultural contexts. Future research should conduct cross-cultural comparisons to test the applicability of the present model across different cultural settings.

Conclusion

Based on cross-sectional convenience sampling data from nine Chinese universities, this study drew the following conclusions:

(1) Crisis life events positively predicted suicidal ideation among college students.

(2) Depression, social support, and positive psychological capital not only played parallel mediating roles but also jointly played chain mediating roles in the relationship between crisis life events and suicidal ideation among college students.

(3) Among the dimensions of positive psychological capital, resilience, hope, and optimism played mediating roles in the relationship between crisis life events and suicidal ideation among college students, with resilience showing the most prominent role. In addition, after social support was included in the model, the mediating effect of self-efficacy became significant, and the significance of the mediating effect of optimism was further enhanced.
