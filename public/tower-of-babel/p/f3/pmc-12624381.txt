The First Isolation of Multiple Antifungal‐Drug‐Resistant Trichophyton Rubrum in China and the Novel Resistance Mechanism
Authors: Ge Song, Wenting Xie, Xue Kong, Hailin Zheng, Clement K M Tsui, Xiaodong She, Weida Liu, Xiaofang Li, Guanzhao Liang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesThis study reports the first isolation of a multidrug‐resistant Trichophyton rubrum strain in China, characterizing its drug resistance profile and mechanisms.MethodsThe isolate was identified by internal transcribed spacer (ITS) sequencing and phylogenetic analysis. In vitro antifungal susceptibility testing (AFST) was performed according to the M38‐A3 CLSI guideline to determine minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) against eight antifungals (terbinafine, itraconazole, fluconazole, amorolfine, griseofulvin, voriconazole, luliconazole and amphotericin B). Whole genome sequencing (WGS), transcriptome sequencing and qRT‐PCR were performed to explore the resistance mechanism.ResultsThe multidrug‐resistant T. rubrum strain L‐6424 was isolated from a Chinese patient with generalised tinea corporis/cruris, tinea unguium and tinea manuum. It exhibited elevated MICs to terbinafine (2 mg/L), itraconazole (0.5 mg/L), and amorolfine (0.5 mg/L). The phylogenetic tree based on genome‐wide single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) showed L‐6424 is not a novel genotype of T. rubrum , with high genetic similarity (99.94%) with the reference strain (CBS 139224). There were three amino acid substitutions in the squalene epoxidase (SQLE), including the previously reported F397L and H440Y, as well as a newly discovered V105M, and one amino acid substitution in the CYP51A (R239C) was identified. Also, significant differences at the transcriptome level between the drug‐resistant and sensitive strains were observed, and it was screened and found that CYP51A, TruMDR5 and TERG_08139 may be related to azole resistance.ConclusionsDrug‐resistant T. rubrum has emerged in China, indicating the possibly increasing severity of antifungal resistance. The complex mechanism of multidrug‐resistant dermophytes poses challenges to clinical treatment, needing more attention.Keywords: antifungal resistance, CYP51A, dermatophytosis, itraconazole, MDR5, SQLE, terbinafine, Trichophyton rubrum

Introduction

Dermatophytes are the most common superficial fungi affecting 20%–25% of the global population, and Trichophyton rubrum is the major pathogen accounting for about 70% of all dermatophytes in the world [1, 2]. The issue of terbinafine‐resistant or even multi‐drug‐resistant dermatophyte infections has been intensifying and raising significant concern [3], particularly with the widespread dissemination of T. indotineae originating from the Indian subcontinent [4, 5], however, the cases of drug‐resistant T. rubrum infections are also emerging [6], and it has not received corresponding attention.

T. rubrum is an anthropophilic dermatophyte that can be transmitted from human to human. It can cause infections in different age groups and in different parts of the body, breaking down keratin in skin, nails and hair, and even causing deep dermal invasion in immunocompromised individuals [7, 8]. Until now, a few cases of drug‐resistant T. rubrum infections have been reported in the United States [9], Japan [7], Greece [10], Italy [11], Switzerland [3], France [12], etc. Moreover, these studies indicated that the infections are often intractable and relapses frequently after stopping antifungal therapy. The mechanisms of antifungal resistance are complex, not only involving squalene epoxidase (SQLE) mutations associated with terbinafine resistance, but also higher expression of CYP51A/B, ATP‐binding cassette (ABC) transporter family or major facilitator superfamily (MFS) linked to azole resistance, posing greater challenges to in‐depth mechanism research [13, 14].

Due to the increased prevalence and widespread transmission of T. rubrum in the global population, along with the emergence of multi‐drug‐resistant strains, this fungus may become a potential burden in public health. In this investigation, we reported the first isolation of itraconazole‐ and terbinafine‐resistant T. rubrum from a patient with extensive lesions in China. Our study aimed to present the clinical, microbiological, and genetic findings of this critical emerging pathogen using conventional and whole genome sequencing (WGS) approaches. Also, we characterized the genetic relationships and the mechanisms that confer drug resistance using the genome data.

Materials and Methods

Clinical Specimens and Fungal Isolates

The clinical strain L‐6424 was isolated from a Chinese patient with tinea corporis, tinea cruris, tinea unguium and tinea manuum, who was admitted and treated by Dr. Guanzhao Liang. The scales on the margins of the buttock and fingernail lesions were scraped for direct microscopic examination with fluorescence staining and cultured on Sabouraud Dextrose Agar (SDA) containing chloramphenicol and cycloheximide to inhibit mould at 28°C for 7 days. Lactophenol Cotton Blue (LPCB) staining was performed and examined microscopically at 40×. This strain (designated L‐6424) has been preserved in the CAMS Collection Center of Pathogen Microorganisms‐D (CAMS‐CCPM‐D), Nanjing, China.

Identification

Genomic DNA was extracted from the colonies growing on the SDA at 28°C for 7 days using the Nucleic Acid Extraction Kit (DAAN GENE Co. Ltd., China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Amplification and sequencing of the rDNA internal transcribed spacer (ITS) region were performed using ITS1 (5′‐TCCGT AGG TGA ACC TGCGG‐ 3′) and ITS4 (5′‐TCCTC CGC TTA TTG ATATGC‐ 3′) primers [15]. The sequences were aligned using Codon Code Aligner software (Codon Code Corp., Centerville, MA, USA), and identified by comparing them to the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) data using BLAST.

Antifungal Susceptibility Testing

In vitro susceptibility testing of eight antifungal drugs was carried out with reference to the CLSI M38‐A3 broth microdilution method [16] with some modifications. Briefly, stock solutions of the drugs were dissolved and prepared with dimethyl sulfoxide and then further diluted in RPMI medium with a twofold dilution scheme. The prepared drug solution was added to 96‐well plates from high to low concentrations in an inoculum volume of 100 uL per well. Conidial inoculum suspensions were obtained from spore cultures grown on a spore‐producing medium for 10 days, then filtrated, counted, and diluted to an inoculum concentration of 2 ~ 6 × 103 CFU/mL using RPMI. The working suspension of conidia was added to the above 96‐well plate, and the inoculum size remained at 100 μL per well. The final test concentrations of antifungals were as follows: 0.25 ~ 64 mg/L for fluconazole, 0.063 ~ 32 mg/L for griseofulvin, 0.002 ~ 16 mg/L for terbinafine, 0.016 ~ 8 mg/L for itraconazole, 0.008 ~ 4 mg/L for voriconazole and amorolfine, 0.0005 ~ 0.25 mg/L for luliconazole, 0.032 ~ 16 mg/L for amphotericin B. The minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) were the lowest drug concentration that prevents approximately 100% growth inhibition for amphotericin B and 80% growth inhibition for other antifungals after 96 h of incubation at 35°C. T.rubrum ATCC MYA‐4438, T. mentagrophytes ATCC MYA‐4439, and Candida krusei ATCC6258 were used as quality controls. Clinical breakpoints and epidemiologic cutoff values for T. rubrum are currently unavailable, with reference to the tentative ECOFFs for T. rubrum given by EUCAST, the wild‐type (WT) T. rubrum had MIC values of ≤ 0.125 mg/L, 0.03 mg/L, 0.25 mg/L and 0.125 mg/L for amorolfine, terbinafine, itraconazole and voriconazole, respectively [17].

DNA And RNA Extraction for Genome And Transcriptome Sequencing

Total DNA and RNA were extracted from fungal strains cultured on SDA at 28°C for 7 days. Genomic DNA was isolated from each isolate using the E.Z.N.A Fungus DNA Kit (Omega Bio‐tek, USA) in accordance with the manufacturer's protocol, and its concentration was quantified using the TBS‐380 fluorometer (Turner BioSystems Inc., USA). Total RNA was extracted from the fungal colonies using TRIzol Reagent (Invitrogen, USA) following the manufacturer's instructions and genomic DNA contamination was removed by treating the RNA samples with DNase I (TaKara Bio Inc., Japan). RNA quality was assessed using the 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, USA), and RNA concentration was measured with the ND‐2000 spectrophotometer (NanoDrop Technologies, USA).

Library Construction and High‐Throughput Sequencing

Paired‐end libraries with an approximate insert size of ~350 bp were prepared according to the preparation protocol of MGIEasy Fast FS Library Prep Set V2.0 (Item No.: 940–001197‐00; MGI Tech Co. Ltd., China). Briefly, fragmentation of the genomic DNA and end‐repairing were combined into one step, and then the desired fragment sizes were purified using gel electrophoresis, followed by selective enrichment and amplification via PCR. RNA‐seq transcriptome libraries were prepared using the MGIEasy RNA Library Prep Kit V3.0 (Item No.: 1000006383; MGI Tech Co. Ltd., China) with 1 μg of total RNA as input. Both kinds of libraries were quantified using the Qubit 4 Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., USA), and sequenced on the DNBSEQ‐T7 platform (MGI Tech Co. Ltd., China) (150 bp paired‐end reads; sequencing service provided by Shanghai BIOZERON Co. Ltd., China).

Genome Assembly and Annotation

Clean data was generated by Trimmomatic with default parameters [18]. Genome assembly was performed with SPAdes with ‘—isolate’ mode [19]. Gene models were predicted by AUGUSTUS (http://augustus.gobics.de/) and then subjected to functional annotation by performing BLASTp against the non‐redundant (NR) database from NCBI, SwissProt (http://uniprot.org), KEGG (http://www.genome.jp/kegg/), and COG (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/COG) with e‐value < 1e‐10.

Single Nucleotide Polymorphism (SNP)‐Based Phylogenetic Analysis

To determine the genetic relationship, a total of 75 strains were used to construct a phylogenetic tree using high‐resolution SNP analysis, including 25 strains with clear isolation source information from the NCBI GenBank database, 49 strains from clinical patients in 5 hospitals in China and this isolated drug‐resistant strain (Table S1). Specifically, the raw sequencing data were retrieved from NCBI for each strain and aligned to the T. rubrum CBS 139224 (GCA_001651445.1) [20] reference genome using BWA‐MEM [21] and GATK4 [22] pipeline for SNP calling. SNPs located within repetitive regions were filtered out to ensure the reliability of the data. Phylogenetic analysis was performed using the maximum likelihood method based on the SNP matrix derived from the isolates and the reference strain. The phylogenetic tree was constructed with PhyML, employing the general time‐reversible (GTR) model with GAMMA distribution to account for among‐site rate variation, and statistical support was assessed using 1000 bootstrap replicates [23].

Comparative Genomics Analysis

We performed the comparative analysis to investigate the structural variations between the sample and the reference genome CBS 139224 at the genomic level, including genomic synteny, SNPs, InDels (Insertion and Deletion), SV (Structural Variation), copy number variation (CNV) analysis and genome visualisation. (1) Genomic alignment between the two genomes was performed using the average nucleotide identity (ANI) analysis with fastANI, The strains with the ANI value > 95% were considered as the same species and ANI value > 99% was considered as the same strains [18, 24]. (2) Genomic synteny was found by the genomic alignment results among samples using MUMmer and LASTZ. (3) For the CNV analysis on candidate genes and previously reported genes (CYP51A/B, MDR and MFS) associated with azole resistance, firstly sequencing depth was assessed using the SAMtools depth module [25]. Then the CNV for each gene was calculated as the average sequencing depth across the corresponding genomic segment within each gene.

Transcriptome Library Preparation and Sequencing

To explore the expression profile of the isolates at the mRNA level, the transcriptome sequencing was performed. RNA‐seq transcriptome libraries were prepared using the TruSeq RNA Sample Preparation Kit (Illumina Inc., USA) with 1 μg of total RNA as input. Messenger RNA (mRNA) was isolated via polyA selection using oligo (dT) beads and subsequently fragmented with fragmentation buffer. cDNA synthesis, end repair, A‐base addition, and ligation of Illumina‐indexed adapters were performed according to the manufacturer's protocol. The cDNA libraries were size‐selected for target fragments of 200–300 bp using 2% Low Range Ultra Agarose, followed by PCR amplification (15 cycles) with Phusion DNA Polymerase (New England Biolabs, USA). Library quantification was performed using the TBS‐380 Fluorometer (Turner BioSystems, USA), and paired‐end sequencing (150 bp × 2) was conducted on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (sequencing service provided by Shanghai BIOZERON Co. Ltd., China).

Differential Expression Analysis and Functional Enrichment

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the drug‐resistant and sensitive strains were identified by calculating gene expression levels using the fragments per kilobase of exon per million mapped reads (FPKM) method. Differential expression analysis was performed using the edgeR package (Empirical analysis of Digital Gene Expression in R, http://www.bioconductor.org/packages/release/bioc/html/edgeR.html/), which implements a negative binomial model to assess gene expression differences. DEGs were selected based on the following criteria: log2 fold change > 2 and false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05. To further investigate the biological roles of the DEGs, Gene Ontology (GO) functional enrichment and KEGG pathway analysis were performed using Goatools (https://github.com/tanghaibao/Goatools) and KOBAS (http://kobas.cbi.pku.edu.cn/home.do), respectively. DEGs were considered significantly enriched in specific GO terms or metabolic pathways if the Bonferroni‐corrected p‐value was less than 0.05.

Expression of Candidate Gene

First‐strand cDNA was synthesised using a reverse transcription (RT) mix kit with gDNA clean for qPCR Ver.2 (Accurate Biology Co. Ltd., China). The qRT‐PCR analysis was performed using SYBR Green Pro Taq HS qPCR kit (Accurate Biology Co. Ltd., China) on a Step One real‐time PCR system (Bio‐Rad Laboratories Inc., USA) under standard conditions, according to the manufacturer's recommendations. The qRT‐PCR was carried out in a 50 μL reaction and conditions included an initial denaturation step for 3 min at 95°C followed by 40 cycles of 15 s at 95°C, 20 s at 62°C, and 20 s at 72°C. Primers were shown in Table S2.

Sanger Sequencing

Sanger sequencing was performed to validate the mutations detected by Illumina sequencing, and the specific primers can be found in Table S2. The PCR products were sequenced using an Applied Biosystems 3730xl DNA Analyser (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The forward and reverse sequences were spliced using the DNAMAN software suite (https://www.lynnon.com/dnaman.html), and the resulting DNA sequences were subjected to a BLASTn analysis (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi) search in the GenBank database to determine their taxonomy.

Statistics

Figures were generated using GraphPad Prism (v8.0.0; GraphPad Software Inc., USA), and statistical analyses, including ANOVA, were conducted using SPSS (v26.0; IBM Corp., USA). Differences were considered statistically significant when the p‐value was less than 0.05.

Results

The Case Report of Refractory Tinea Corporis/Cruris

A 51‐year‐old man presented with a 20‐year history of large erythema and scales with itching affecting the chest, back, lower abdomen and buttocks, along with thickened, discoloured nails and keratinized hands (Figure 1A). This patient with no history of underlying disease or use of immunosuppressive agents or other medications, and no history of travel abroad or contact with imported goods, was admitted to the Hospital for Skin Diseases, Chinese Academy of Medical Science. He had been irregularly treated with oral terbinafine or itraconazole capsules, and multiple topical antifungals (such as terbinafine cream, naftifine hydrochloride & ketoconazole cream, ketoconazole cream, clotrimazole cream and econazole nitrate cream, etc.) or steroid‐antifungal preparations (triamcinolone acetonide & econazole nitrate cream, hydrocortisone butyrate & bifonazole cream and clobetasol propionate & miconazole cream, etc.) for several years in the local hospital, but the lesions continued to exacerbate. In our hospital, mycology revealed positive direct microscopy from the buttocks and nails, with a positive culture from the buttocks (the fingernail culture failure). The patient was diagnosed with tinea corporis, tinea cruris, tinea unguium and tinea manuum, and subsequently treated with oral terbinafine (250 mg/d) for 2 months, as well as standard doses (200 mg/d) and pulse doses (400 mg/d) of itraconazole for a total of 4 months; the lesions still persisted. This patient has received multiple standardised antifungal treatments, but the lesions have not been fully cured, and the fungal microscopic examination is still positive, indicating the possibility of antifungal‐resistant dermatophyte infections. Until now, the patient is still receiving treatment and ongoing follow‐up.

Multiple fingernails exhibited thickening and discoloration, and the hands showed signs of hyperkeratosis. (B) Hyphae were observed under fluorescence microscopy. T. rubrum was Identified as the Pathogenic Fungus

Skin scrap was taken from the lesions on the patient's buttocks. Fluffy to cottony white colonies on the obverse and wine‐red colonies on the reverse with powdery margins were observed in the SDA; fluorescent microscopic examination revealed septate hyphae; and LPCB staining revealed profusely branched hyphae with abundant microconidia (Figure 1B). Sequencing of the ITS and BLAST search revealed 100% similarity to T. rubrum .

Multiple Antifungal‐Drug‐Resistant T. rubrum was Determined by Antifungal Susceptibility Testing

We selected eight commonly used antifungals (including terbinafine, itraconazole, fluconazole, amorolfine, griseofulvin, voriconazole, luliconazole and amphotericin B) for in vitro susceptibility testing, and the susceptibility of the five T. rubrum isolates by broth microdilution method of CLSI was shown in detail in Table 1. Notably, the L‐6424 isolate exhibited reduced susceptibility against multiple antifungal agents, including terbinafine, fluconazole, itraconazole, and amorolfine, with MIC values of 2, 8, 0.5 and 0.5 mg/L, respectively. The other 4 isolates exhibited decreased sensitivity to fluconazole, but remained susceptible to the remaining seven antifungals. The MICs of the quality control strains were also within the scope of results provided in the CLSI M38M51S document, which was attached as Table S3.

Genetic Characterisitics of L‐6424 Isolates

To investigate the genomic features of the L‐6424 strain, we sequenced the whole genomes using Illumina sequencing techniques and obtained 3,242.4 Mb raw data. After removing lower‐quality sequences, the clean data after filtering was 3180.6 Mb, and clean data Q20 and Q30 values were above 99% and 98%, respectively.

Besides, to further understand the differences between the two strains, we compared the genome assembly of L‐6424 to the T. rubrum reference CBS 139224 and found that L‐6424 had a slightly smaller genome size (22.2 Mb) with more scaffolds (78), higher GC content (48.46%). Besides, we annotated 7164 genes with a shorter average gene length (1599 bp) in total in L‐6424 compared with 7051 genes in CBS 139224 (Table 2). At the nucleotide level, both revealed a 99.94% identity; meanwhile, the L‐6424 and CBS 118892 strains have 6303 orthologous genes, and 416 and 380 special genes, respectively. A total of 5204 SNPs were identified for the L‐6424 compared to CBS 139224 strains, including 335 nonsynonymous SNPs, 163 synonymous SNPs, and 5 stop‐codon gained SNPs. There were 2036 indels identified between the two strains, including 314 insertions and 1722 deletions. We performed CNV analysis on genes previously implicated in azole resistance (CYP51A/B, MDR and MFS family), and our results indicated that these genes do not exhibit CNV in the L‐6424 strain. Although the genetic similarity between them is high, there are still some structural variations, including scaffold inversions, transpositions, and combined transposition‐inversion events (Tran+Inver) (Figure S1).

SNP‐Based Phylogenetic Analysis to Construct a Phylogenetic Tree

To understand the evolutionary relationships between L‐6424 and other strains, we analysed the SNP differences among 75 isolates (Table S1) to construct a phylogenetic tree, including one drug‐resistant strain L‐6424 and 74 sensitive strains T. rubrum (Figure 2). All 75 T. rubrum strains were collected from ten countries, and the most common infection site was the foot (21/75), followed by nail (18/75) and thighs (14/75). The number of SNPs varies from 2, 489 to 21,146 among these T. rubrum strains. Based on the phylogenetic tree, it was observed that L‐6424 clustered with others, suggesting that it is a common type within the species rather than a newly emerging variant of T. rubrum .

The genome of T. rubrum (GCA_001651445.1) was used as the reference and the metadata of the isolates, including collection data, geographic location, and isolation source, were visually represented using distinct colours.

Comparison of Transcriptome Profiles Between Drug‐Resistant and Sensitive Strains

To explore the differences between drug‐resistant strain L‐6424 and sensitive strains, we conducted transcriptome sequencing of L‐6424 and four other clinically isolated sensitive strains (CBS 139224, Tru 6225, Tru 6256 and Tru 6320), and performed a comparative analysis of the two groups. The upregulated genes for L‐6424 versus sensitive strains were mainly in the oxidation–reduction process, small molecule metabolic process, oxidoreductase activity, isomerase activity and small molecule biosynthetic process of the GO pathways, while the downregulated genes were mainly in the nucleolus, ncRNA processing, rRNA metabolic process and ncRNA metabolic process (Figure 3A). In KEGG enrichment analysis, compared with sensitive strains, drug‐resistant strains showed that the pathways of the upregulated genes were mainly enriched in glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, carbon fixation in photosynthetic organisms, methane metabolism and steroid biosynthesis, while downregulated genes were enriched in peroxisome, ribosome biogenesis in eukaryotes and fatty acid metabolism (Figure 3A). These differences indicated that drug‐resistant strain L‐6424 enhanced its survival ability through a series of metabolic adaptation mechanisms, while sensitive strains lacked this regulatory ability, making them more susceptible to inhibition under drug pressure.

The Validation of Candidate Genes Expression

To further seek the mechanisms of resistance to azole antifungal drugs, we selected genes with significant differential expression based on the transcriptome data (TERG_504, TERG_506 and TERG_08139) and genes previously implicated in azole resistance mechanisms (CYP51A/B, TruMDR and MFS family) for qRT‐PCR validation. We found that there were three genes significantly overexpressed in the drug‐resistant strain L‐6424 compared to sensitive strains, namely TERG_08139, TruMDR5, CYP51A (Figure 4), which may play a key role in the development of azole resistance.

Sequencing of Drug Resistance‐Related Genes

We performed in‐depth sequencing analysis on genes with azole resistance (TERG_08139, TruMDR5, CYP51A) and the SQLE gene (associated with terbinafine resistance) in L‐6424 strain. In the SQLE gene, we detected three amino acid substitutions (Figure 5A). Two of these (Phe397Leu, mutation 1191C>A; His440Tyr, mutation 1380 C>T) are known resistance mutations, while the third (Val105Met, mutation 313G>A) has not been previously reported. In CYP51A gene, we identified a single amino acid substitution (Figure 5B), whereas no mutations were found in TERG_08139 or TruMDR5.

Discussion

Antifungal resistance in dermatophytes has been attracting increasing concerns, and the cases of antifungal drug‐resistant T. rubrum infection are emerging in China and worldwide [3, 10, 26]. In the past decades, T. rubrum has always been the predominant species causing dermatophytoses, followed by the T. mentagrophytes/interdigitale complex. Since 2017, there have been numerous antifungal‐drug‐resistant T. indotineae isolates, causing chronic, generalised and refractory skin infections, and it has already become a major public health issue [27, 28]. Until now, only scattered cases of terbinafine‐resistant infections of T. rubrum [9], Microsporum canis [29], T. tonsurans [30] have been reported. However, decreased terbinafine susceptibility strains have been identified in United States patients with onychomycosis these years, indicating an emergency of drug‐resistant T. rubrum infection tendency [31]. In China, domestic T. indotineae has been isolated [26], while drug‐resistant T. rubrum has not been reported yet, despite this species accounting for 70%, as the most common pathogenic dermatophytes. Here, we reported the first case of terbinafine‐and itraconazole‐resistant T. rubrum infection in China, unveiling a previously unrecognised threat of drug resistance.

The rising drug resistance in T. rubrum poses a significant challenge to clinical management. As in this patient, the clinical presentation is characterized by a prolonged illness, generalized lesions and severe resistance, resulting in a lowered quality of life and family financial burden. The underlying mechanisms of drug resistance are multifaceted and warrant focused attention. According to literature, misuse or overuse of antifungal agents can lead to increased drug exposure, resulting in drug resistance in dermatophytes, which has been widely discussed in T. indotineae [32]. In addition, the in vitro antifungal susceptibility testing confirmed that this strain is resistant to both first‐line terbinafine, itraconazole and amorolfine, which is the main factor causing difficulties in treatment. T. rubrum commonly causes multi‐site infections, most frequently onychomycosis, followed by tinea pedis [6], In contrast, the currently circulating drug‐resistant strains of the T. mentagrophytes/interdigitale complex, specifically T. indotineae, predominantly cause widespread tinea corporis and tinea cruris but rarely infect the nails [33]. Based on reported cases, T. rubrum tends to cause a mild inflammatory response, often presenting as erythema with scales, and is less likely to manifest as vesicles, pustules, or tinea imbricata, compared to T. indotineae [7]. Therefore, the possibility of drug resistance in T. rubrum should be considered clinically in the refractory tinea unguium and tinea corporis/cruris with mild inflammatory reaction. If necessary, further clarification of the genetic variation characteristics of suspected drug‐resistant strains is required.

The variations of the drug‐resistant isolate L‐6424 at the genomic and transcriptome levels were studied. Genome‐wide comparison of the L‐6424 demonstrated a remarkable genetic similarity of approximately 99.94% to the reference strain CBS 139224. However, there were some structural variations, such as SNPs, indels, and rearrangements, that could potentially contribute to phenotypic divergence, such as variations in pathogenicity, metabolic pathways, or drug resistance mechanisms. We also conducted CNV analysis to explore its potential role in resistance mechanisms. Yamada et al. previously found that the CYP51B gene showed an average sequencing depth elevation in drug‐resistant T. indotineae [34, 35], however, no CNV on genes related to azole resistance, such as CYP51A/B, MDR and MFS family, was found in the resistant T. rubrum strains. Based on the high‐quality whole‐genome SNPs phylogenetic analysis, it revealed that the L‐6424 strain clustered together with other susceptible strains of T. rubrum , indicating it is not a novel genotype, and its specific geographic origin could not be determined. Furthermore, we observed significant differences between the drug‐resistant and sensitive strains at the transcriptome level and identified differentially expressed genes. Through GO and KEGG enrichment analysis, it was found that upregulated pathways in the drug‐resistant strain L‐6424 suggested an enhanced ability to generate energy, adapt to oxidative stress, and synthesise essential compounds. Conversely, downregulated pathways indicated a shift away from energy‐intensive processes such as ribosome biogenesis, nucleolus activity and fatty acid metabolism. These expression differences suggest that drug‐resistant strains may regulate their metabolic and biosynthetic pathways to minimise the negative impact of drugs and enhance their survival under drug pressure. These adaptive mechanisms could provide important insights for further understanding and combating drug resistance.

The resistance mechanism of T. rubrum is complex, mainly due to resistance to the first‐line drug terbinafine. More alarmingly, isolates resistant to itraconazole have also been identified. Terbinafine‐resistant T. rubrum exhibit gene mutations in SQLE, which encodes a key enzyme in the ergosterol biosynthetic pathway [36]. The mutations I121M, V237I, L393F, L393S, F397L, F397I, F397V, Q408K, F415S, F415V, F415I, H440Y, F484Y and A398A399G400 deletion were detected in terbinafine‐resistant strains of T. rubrum , while F397L has the highest prevalence at 27% among resistant T. rubrum strains [3]. Actually, azole resistance is less commonly identified in T. rubrum , and only a few studies have been performed to explore the mechanism of azole resistance. Azole resistance in fungi, such as T. rubrum , T. indotineae and Aspergillus, is attributed to the mutation or overexpression of genes encoding for sterol 14‐α demethylase, cytochrome P450 CYP51 superfamily, as well as the overexpression of genes encoding efflux pumps including the ABC transporters and MFS transporters [7]. In our study, we first sequenced the hot spot mutations in SQLE genes and three amino acid substitutions were identified, including previously reported F397L and H440Y, as well as a newly discovered V105M, which had not been reported in previous studies. The F397L mutation has been confirmed to be associated with high MICs (mean MIC = 3.071 μg/mL) and H440Y had lower MICs of < 1 μg/mL [31]. The correlation between the V105M mutation and terbinafine resistance needs further investigation. Besides, in the azole resistance mechanisms, we discovered three genes (namely CYP51A, TruMDR5 and TERG_08139), showing overexpression in the strain L‐6424, and CYP51A had a substituted amino acid in position 239.

In previous studies, it has been confirmed that the mutations and overexpression of CYP51A or CYP51B are associated with azole resistance in T. rubrum and T. indotineae, but the R239C mutation has not been reported. In T. rubrum , four ABC transporters, TruMDR1, TruMDR2, TruMDR3 and TruMDR5, and two MFS transporters, TruMFS1 and TruMFS2, have been identified as efflux pumps capable of actively exporting at least one of the tested azole compounds [14]. Notably, TruMDR2 specifically contributes to itraconazole resistance, while TruMDR3 exhibits broad substrate specificity toward azoles and plays a pivotal role in voriconazole resistance [13]. TruMDR2 is closely related to TruMDR5, which also showed the ability to efflux ITC when expressed in S. cerevisiae . Yamada et al. found the overexpression of TruMDR2 but not TruMDR5, while the high expression of TruMDR5 was found in our isolated strains, indicating a potential resistant mechanism [13]. The new gene TERG_08139 is annotated by the DOG (Domain‐based Ontology of Gene) database as NAD‐dependent epimerase dehydratase, which may play an important role in metabolic pathways or correspond to new virulence factors, and it has homologous sequences in T. verrucosum , T. benhamiae and Nannizzia gypsea. The correlation between TERG_08139 and drug resistance was identified on the mRNA expression level by transcriptome sequencing and qRT‐PCR in this study, while the specific molecular interactions need to be further investigated. Resistance to multiple antifungal agents in T. rubrum is exceedingly rare; however, our recent findings have documented a clinical isolate exhibiting resistance to terbinafine, itraconazole and amorolfine. Also, it illuminates the drug‐resistant mechanism, especially azoles, which should be attributed to multifactorial interaction.

It is important to acknowledge that antifungal resistance in dermatophytes is no longer an exceptional phenomenon. The rising number of drug‐resistant cases, particularly in the most common anthropophilic dermatophyte, T. rubrum , represents a growing global health threat. Now, drug‐resistant T. rubrum has emerged in China, and the appearance of multidrug‐resistant strains indicates the looming severity of antifungal resistance. Moreover, the resistance mechanism of multidrug‐resistant strains is more complex, which will pose a significant challenge to clinical treatment and drug selection. Consequently, priority attention should be paid to epidemiological investigation and monitoring of dermatophyte resistance, and international collaboration is needed.
