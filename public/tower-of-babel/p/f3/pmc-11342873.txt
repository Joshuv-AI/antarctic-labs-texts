A case report of BIA-ALCL: Diagnostic, treatment, and mammary reconstruction

Introduction

The breast implant-associated anaplastic large cell lymphoma (BIA-ALCL) is an unusual T-cell non-Hodgkin lymphoma, exclusively related to breast implants [1]. It was first described in 1997 by Keech and Creech [2]. In 2011, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) issued a health alert indicating a higher ALCL risk in breast implant patients [3]. In 2016, the World Health Organization (WHO) nomenclature officially classified it as a new ALK-negative lymphoma [4,5]. According to the American Society of Plastic Surgeons (ASPS) 2024 report, there have been 434 suspicious or confirmed cases of BIA-ALCL in the United States and 1566 cases around the world [6]. The classic presentation of this lymphoma includes persistent late periprosthetic seroma followed by breast swelling, breast pain, asymmetry, cutaneous symptoms (swelling and papules), and regional lymphadenopathy [7]. Diagnosis is made through cytologic analysis of periprosthetic fluid samples obtained by ultrasound-guided fine-needle aspiration or core needle biopsy near the implant. Ultrasound or magnetic resonance imaging can be useful to characterize the lesions and fluid effusion [8].

In the pathology, the main immunophenotypic characteristics of this lymphoma are the intense expression of CD30 and the lack of expression of kinase of anaplastic lymphoma (ALK-negative) [9]. The treatment includes total capsulectomy and breast implant removal, with 93 % of patients free of lymphoma after 3 years. In cases where there is involvement beyond the capsules, en bloc capsulectomy is required. For advanced cases, adjuvant treatments such as immunotherapy and chemotherapy are added [10].

We present a rare case of a woman without any cancer family background, who developed BIA-ALCL after 12-years of implantation of microtextured breast implants from Allergan. The patient presented with peri-implant effusion and incipient formation of a visible nodule on the left breast, despite of the low incidence of this type of lymphoma, an early-stage diagnosis was successfully made. The treatment involved breast implant removal with total capsulectomy, without involvement of adjacent tissues. Subsequently, the patient underwent a satisfactory reconstruction with autologous tissue. The importance of this case lies in the low prevalence and rare reporting of BIA-ALCL, especially in microtextured implants. It contributes to the evidence of the relationship between textured implants and BIA-ALCL development, emphasizing the need for long-term surveillance. This case report has been reported in line with the SCARE Criteria [11].

Presentation of case

A 41-year-old female patient originally from Venezuela and residing in the United States, with a history of breast augmentation in 2010 using Allergan microtextured implants placed in a subpectoral position.

In January 2022, the patient noticed breast asymmetry and the appearance of a soft, mobile lump in the left breast associated with breast pain (Fig. 1). In March 2022, imaging studies, including ultrasound and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), revealed peri-implant effusion in both breasts without evidence of intra- or extracapsular implant rupture. Ultrasound-guided aspiration of the fluid was performed for cytological study, which showed positive immunostaining, consistent with malignant lymphoma. The atypical lymphoid cells were positive for CD30 and negative for calretinin, corresponding to breast implant-associated anaplastic large cell lymphoma (BIA-ALCL).

Lump on the left breast. A. Breast asymmetry. B. The mass is indicated by the arrow.

A PET-CT scan showed no evidence of extension to lymph nodes or organs other than the breast. In June 2022, breast implant removal and capsulectomy were performed. Pathology of the left capsular tissue showed morphology consistent with BIA-ALCL without extracapsular extension, classified as an early-stage IA (TNM). The right side showed no morphological or immunophenotypic involvement.

The patient had a history of gastroesophageal reflux disease managed with proton pump inhibitors and had undergone liposuction and functional rhinoplasty. Her father had a history of hypertension.

Two years later, the patient underwent autologous tissue reconstruction without reported symptoms or new breast lesions. Physical examination revealed normal vital signs and no pathological findings (Fig. 2). Pathology of the breast tissue showed no macroscopic lesions suggestive of malignancy (resume in Fig. 3). Pre-surgical laboratory tests were within normal limits (Table 1).

Discussion

The BIA-ALCL has a variable incidence, between 1:300/400 and 1:30,000 cases. This variability is likely related to the rarity of the disease, passive reporting, and prolonged exposure times before diagnosis [12]. In Colombia, 28,606 breast augmentation surgeries are performed annually according to the ISAPS 2023 report, making it the third most important destination for the execution of this procedure in South America. Despite this, there are no national reports that assess the precise incidence of this pathology [13]. Between 2011 and 2019 eighteen cases of BIA-ALCL were identified in Colombia, all in patients with macrotextured implants. More than a third of these cases were related to Allergan brand implants; three cases used Mentor brand implants, and two used Silimed Polyurethane; the remaining cases are distributed among other brands [14]. In our case, the patient, of foreign origin and Colombian nationality, reported the breast implant removal of microtextured Allergan brand prostheses. Although these implants are less frequently associated with BIA-ALCL, their link to the disease has been documented [15].

Although BIA-ALCL has been primarily associated with textured implants, there is still uncertainty about the possibility of it occurring in smooth implants. To address this, efforts have been made to quantify the risk according to the degree of texturization, classifying surfaces into four groups: smooth (group 1), microtextured (group 2), macro textured (group 3), and polyurethane-coated implants (group 4) [15]. The hypothesis suggests that the incidence of BIA-ALCL increases with the degree of surface texturization.

The etiopathogenesis of BIA-ALCL is not fully elucidated and is likely caused by several combined factors, such as the accumulation of bacteria on textured implants that provide a rough environment conducive to their growth. This theory suggests that chronic inflammation caused by biofilms could trigger a dysregulation in T lymphocytes in the implant capsule. Other possible causes include fragmentation of particles from the implant covering, contamination with heavy metals during manufacturing, and the influence of the genetic predisposition of the patient [16].

The most common clinical presentation of BIA-ALCL is the accumulation of fluid and/or late seromas, which complicates its diagnosis since it is often confused with implant rupture, yet without an increase in breast volume. Initially, ultrasound is the ideal imaging technique to detect the presence of fluid, masses, or suspicious lymphadenopathy, with a sensitivity and specificity of 84 % and 75 % respectively; on the other hand, magnetic resonance imaging is used if the ultrasound results are inconclusive, with a sensitivity of 82 % and a specificity of 33% [17]. In the reported case, the patient shows swelling in the lower quadrant of the left breast, and the imaging diagnosis was first made with MRI, followed by ultrasound to confirm the finding of peri-implant fluid in the breast, remembering that due to the irregular distribution of the fluid, precise measurement is difficult and requires expertise in MRI and ultrasound techniques for proper management.

The precise diagnosis of BIA-ALCL is made through an immunohistochemical study of CD30-positive cells, by taking periprosthetic fluid with fine needle aspiration. Sometimes other markers such as CD2, CD3, CD4, CD5, CD7, CD8, CD45, and anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK) may be required. Furthermore, the extent of the lesions, pre-surgical staging, and follow-up can be assessed using positron emission tomography (PET-CT) to rule out the involvement of the chest wall, regional lymphadenopathy, or distant organ metastasis [4]. In contrast to this case, the diagnosis was made through positivity in the cytological study for CD30 and was confirmed with the pathological report of lymphoid involvement of the left periprosthetic capsule; the PET-CT report ruled out distant involvement, and it was classified as an early-stage IA (TNM). Finally, breast implant removal with total capsulectomy was performed and did not require adjuvant management with chemotherapy or radiotherapy, treatment useful in advanced stages. The strength of this case lies in the early diagnosis which allow an effective treatment.

After the breast implant removal due to BIA-ALCL, reconstruction procedures can be performed, which include reconstruction with smooth implants, immediate mastopexy, autologous tissue, and fat grafting [18]. In this case, the patient chose to undergo mastopexy with autologous tissue two years after her breast implant removal, expressing satisfaction with the results and without any mention of tumor recurrence. Thanks to early identification, she received effective treatment.

One of the limitations of this case was genotyping. Clonal T-cell receptor (TCR) gene rearrangements have been found in all cases of BIA-ALCL to date, similarly, mutations in other genes such as STAT3 have been found related to this type of cancer [19]. The analysis of these genes could be a prognostic factor that aids in the prevention and decision-making regarding breast implants.

Conclusion

In Colombia, with a high incidence of breast augmentation surgeries, this case provides crucial data about the disease, which is rarely reported locally. The precise diagnosis allowed successful treatment through breast implant removal and capsulectomy without the need for chemotherapy or radiotherapy, and reconstruction with autologous tissue showed satisfactory long-term results. The inclusion of molecular studies and genotyping, such as the analysis of TCR and STAT3 mutations, underscores the importance of advancing the understanding and management of BIA-ALCL.
