A Systematic Review on the Outcomes of Implementing Public‐Private Partnerships in Healthcare Systems: The Experience of the Middle East
Authors: Reyhane Izadi, Zahra Zare, Mohsen Khosravi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsPublic‐Private Partnerships (PPPs) have been proposed with an aim to improve performance of healthcare systems. This paper aimed to investigate the outcomes of implementing PPPs in healthcare systems within the Middle East.MethodsIn 2024, a systematic review was conducted on the studies within the literature. The quality of the included studies was assessed using the AACODS (Accuracy, Coverage, Objectivity, Date, Significance) checklist. Subsequently, a thematic analysis was conducted on the gathered data adhering to the guidelines published by Thomas and Harden. The search was conducted across multiple scholarly databases, such as PubMed, Scopus, ProQuest, and the Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews.Results12 studies were found to meet the inclusion criteria. The quality assessment of these studies indicated a high standard and an acceptable level of bias. A thematic analysis of the data from these studies revealed four main themes: ‘positive client‐based outcomes’, ‘positive service delivery outcomes’, ‘positive economic outcomes’, and ‘challenging outcomes’.ConclusionPPPs have been presented to improve outcomes and efficiency but face organizational, financial, legal, and ethical challenges. Their success requires specific conditions, ongoing oversight, and adaptation to unlock their transformative potential in regional healthcare.

Introduction

Today, the healthcare systems within the Middle East are confronted with a multitude of structural and economic hurdles. A significant number of healthcare professionals, particularly specialists, are departing from nations such as Egypt, Gaza, Yemen, and Syria primarily due to inadequate remuneration, substandard working conditions, and prevailing conflicts or instability [1]. Notably, certain Middle Eastern nations like Bahrain, Qatar, and the UAE exhibit elevated healthcare expenditures; however, commensurate improvements in health outcomes are not realized, suggestive of inefficient resource utilization. Conversely, countries like Iraq, Jordan, and Saudi Arabia demonstrate substantial resource allocation, yet suffer from inefficiencies [2]. Moreover, numerous countries in the region grapple with exorbitant out‐of‐pocket healthcare expenses, posing a risk of impoverishment for patients [3].

A PPP entails a contractual arrangement between one or more public and private entities, typically of a prolonged duration, delineating shared obligations towards advancing mutual interests. PPPs are designed to enhance the efficacy and efficiency of public services while bolstering financial, operational, and infrastructural sustainability [4, 5].

Evidence indicates that Public‐Private Partnerships (PPPs) leverage strengths from both public and private sectors to expand healthcare access, create economies of scale, and develop sustainable financing models [6]. By addressing limitations in public sector healthcare provision such as human resource shortages and inefficiencies, PPPs establish sustainable financial systems, enhance capacity, control costs, and improve healthcare coverage, quality, and infrastructure [7, 8].

PPPs have garnered considerable interest in the Middle Eastern region, notably in Saudi Arabia, as a strategy to augment healthcare services and contend with escalating demands [9]. Another factor contributing to the growing interest in PPPs in regions such as the Middle East is the widely reported view that private‐sector involvement in the delivery of public services plays a significant role in improving population well‐being, particularly in Low‐ and Middle‐Income Countries (LMICs) [10, 11].

Upon conducting this manuscript, to the best of the authors' knowledge, there has been no systematic review investigating the outcomes of implementing PPPs in the healthcare systems of the Middle East. This phenomenon underscores the novelty and significance of this paper.

Multiple systematic reviews have investigated the outcomes of implementing PPPs in healthcare systems globally within the literature. A systematic review assessed quality improvement initiatives within PPPs in low‐ and middle‐income countries, revealing their common use in maternal and child health, HIV/AIDS, and infectious diseases. Despite varied approaches and outcomes, positive results in clinical effectiveness, care processes, and cost management were reported [12]. Another review analyzed over 1,400 publications on PPPs, highlighting their widespread adoption but limited empirical investigation; The study reported that Key outcomes of PPPs encompass enhanced infrastructure development, heightened efficiency in service delivery, and improved risk‐sharing between the public and private sectors. PPPs offer superior value for money, expedited project completion, and greater innovation compared to conventional public procurement methods. Nonetheless, the efficacy of PPPs relies significantly on meticulous contract design, judicious risk allocation, and robust governance frameworks to uphold accountability and transparency [13].

The investigation of the outcomes of PPPs in healthcare presents significant implications for various stakeholders, including healthcare policymakers and administrators who aim to develop strategies for restructuring their respective healthcare organizations. In this context, these stakeholders can obtain essential data to inform their planning processes and the formulation of strategic initiatives within their organizations.

Methods

In 2024, the systematic review followed the guidelines stipulated by the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses (PRISMA) [14]. The study aimed to present the outcomes of implementing public‐private partnerships in healthcare systems within the Middle East, as reported by the existing studies within the literature. The following sections present each step of the methodology.

Data Sources and Search Method

Initially, a comprehensive search was undertaken within the Cochrane Library to ascertain the distinctiveness of this investigation. Given the advancements observed in measurement methodologies throughout the preceding two decades, the timeframe for the search was delimited to the years 2000 through 2024. Subsequently, two authors independently conducted the exploration across a range of databases, comprising PubMed, Cochrane, Scopus, and ProQuest. The search strategy entailed the utilization of the “AND” operator for connecting disparate concepts and the “OR” operator for encompassing synonyms, with a particular emphasis on the “Title, Abstract, and Keyword” fields. Keywords were meticulously selected from the “Mesh Terms” section of PubMed and pertinent scholarly resources. The delineation of the search strategy is provided in Table 1.

Screening, Inclusion/Exclusion Standards, and Data Extraction

Two authors conducted a rigorous screening of studies based on predefined eligibility criteria, which encompassed three sequential stages: 1) evaluation of titles, 2) assessment of abstracts, and 3) examination of full‐text articles. Any disparities encountered during this process were thoroughly deliberated upon by the two authors, with recourse to a third author for resolution, if deemed necessary. Furthermore, meticulous scrutiny was applied to articles with high citation rates to forestall any potential bias in their inclusion.

All studies available in English with full‐text accessibility were considered for potential inclusion, spanning a diverse array of study designs, including but not limited to clinical trials, cross‐sectional studies, case‐control studies, observational studies, feasibility studies, and case studies. Additionally, all studies pertinent to our research query, were subjected to thorough evaluation and inclusion, without imposition of any restrictive criteria.

Studies not published in English, those lacking keywords across all three sections of title, abstract, and full text, as well as studies not directly relevant to the research question, were systematically excluded, along with review articles, editorials, conference papers, and brief reports. Subsequently, adherence to the 2020 PRISMA guidelines facilitated a methodical step‐by‐step screening process. Furthermore, studies identified from the data sources were compiled into an X9 EndNote library, wherein duplicate entries were meticulously identified and eliminated.

Data collection was carried out by one author and independently validated by another to enhance data validity and reduce potential bias. The extracted information included the year of study, country, study type, context, and a summary of the findings.

Quality Assessment

Two authors evaluated the quality of all integrated studies utilizing the AACODS (Accuracy, Coverage, Objectivity, Date, Significance) checklist. The AACODS checklist comprised six inquiries [15]. A standardized scoring system was applied during this evaluation, assigning a score of ‘Yes’ as 2, ‘Can't Tell’ as 1, and ‘No’ as 0. Scores varied from 0 to 12, with higher scores indicating enhanced quality. Subsequently, the studies were classified into four groups based on their scores: very low quality (0–3), low quality (4–6), medium quality (7–9), and high quality (10–12). Only studies classified as medium or high quality were considered appropriate for inclusion in research efforts. Any disparities between the two evaluators were resolved through deliberation and consultation with a third‐party arbitrator. These procedures were reiterated twice to ensure consistency.

Data Extraction and Analysis

Data extraction was performed utilizing MAXQDA version 10. The data extraction form comprised several components, including the publication year, country of study, study type, study context, and a succinct summary of the findings. Subsequently, thematic analysis was undertaken in accordance with the methodological framework delineated by Thomas and Harden [16]. Initially, all extracted data underwent iterative reviews and comparisons with the original source materials to facilitate comprehensive familiarization. Following this, preliminary codes were discerned, guided by the research inquiry and the desired research outcomes.

In the subsequent stage, an interpretive analysis of the initial codes was conducted, leading to the organization of these codes into subthemes and overarching themes. The subsequent phase involved a meticulous review of these emergent themes, allowing for their amalgamation, refinement, segregation, or exclusion as warranted by the data. Finally, the process culminated in the definitive delineation and labeling of themes and their associated subthemes, with particular emphasis on their relevance to the content under scrutiny. Thematic analysis was concurrently conducted by two authors under the supervision of a third author to bolster the reliability of the analysis and mitigate potential biases inherent in the process.

Results

As presented in Figure 1, the search within the databases resulted in the identification of 80 references. Following a thorough screening process of titles, and abstracts, 12 studies were determined to meet the criteria for inclusion in the study. The data corresponding to all of the studies were successfully retrieved, resulting in the inclusion of all 12 references in our study (Figure 1). Selected studies were conducted in countries such as the United Arab Emirates (UAE), Jordan, Qatar, Iran, Saudi Arabia, Oman, Iraq, Egypt, Yemen, and Turkey. The studies encompassed qualitative, quantitative, and mixed‐methods approaches, reflecting a multifaceted investigation into different aspects of healthcare systems. The studies explored the outcomes of PPP in various contexts of healthcare systems, including healthcare facilities such as hospitals and primary healthcare centers. Additionally, some studies focused on specific components of the healthcare infrastructure, such as Biolab and public laboratories. Further details regarding the characteristics of the included studies are presented in Appendix 1 (Bibliography).

The thematic analysis conducted on the data acquired from the conclusive studies obtained through the systematic review revealed four principal themes. The themes encompassed ‘positive client‐based outcomes’, ‘positive service delivery outcomes’, ‘positive economic outcomes’, and ‘challenging outcomes’ (Table 2).

The theme of “positive client‐based outcomes” included three sub‐themes of social justice promotion, increased responsiveness, and increased satisfaction. The papers corresponding to this theme reported that the implementation of PPP has led to a significant emphasis on social justice promotion [12, 17, 19]. Moreover, there was a notable increase in responsiveness, indicating a heightened level of attentiveness and adaptability to the needs of the community [17, 20]. Furthermore, the initiation of PPP led to a marked improvement in satisfaction levels among both staff and clients, suggesting a positive impact on the overall quality of services rendered [20, 21, 22].

The next theme was “positive service delivery outcomes” and included five sub‐themes of efficient information dissemination, efficient governance, enhanced quality, increase in performance indicators and standardized protocols. The corresponding papers reported that the implementation of PPP in healthcare has resulted in several notable outcomes, contributing to more efficient and effective healthcare service delivery. One significant outcome was the introduction of innovative information technology platforms, which has facilitated efficient information dissemination. These platforms allow for the swift transmission of results directly to key stakeholders, supporting public health decision‐making with timely and valid data [21, 22, 23]. Moreover, the initiation of PPP has been associated with efficient governance of healthcare facilities, particularly during times of crisis. The approach has optimized resource allocation in outsourced hospital services [17, 20, 22, 24, 25, 26, 27]. Furthermore, the quality of healthcare services has been assured and enhanced through the implementation of various performance indicators [17, 19, 21, 23, 25, 27, 28]. The indicators include an increase in testing capacity, bed occupancy rates, average length of stay, bed efficiency, hospitalizations, admissions, clinic referrals, emergency referrals, and surgical operations [17, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22, 25, 26]. Additionally, the implementation of standardized protocols has been instrumental in streamlining procedures, removing unnecessary clinical and administrative burdens while maintaining essential protocols [26].

The third theme was “positive economic outcomes” and had four sub‐themes, which include infrastructure development, enhanced equipment management, improve in financial constraints, and increase in resources. The papers reported that the implementation of PPP has yielded multifaceted outcomes, significantly impacting infrastructure development, equipment management, financial constraints, and resource availability. One notable outcome was the substantial progress observed in infrastructure development. Through PPP initiatives, there has been a marked improvement in the development and maintenance of essential infrastructure, ranging from transportation networks to public utilities [27]. Furthermore, the management of equipment and resources has been enhanced, ensuring optimal utilization and maintenance of vital assets [22]. Moreover, PPP has proven effective in addressing financial constraints by leveraging private sector investments and expertise to supplement public funding. This collaborative approach has enabled projects to be completed within budget constraints while maintaining high‐quality standards [25, 26, 27, 28]. Additionally, the initiation of PPP has led to a notable increase in resources, particularly in the workforce. By engaging the private sector, PPP policies have facilitated the identification of alternative resources and revenue sources, mitigated resource shortages and ensured sustained project viability [20, 24, 25, 26, 27].

The last theme was “challenging outcomes” and included three sub‐themes: organizational challenges, financial challenges, and legal and ethical challenges. The papers reported that the implementation of PPP has also encountered notable obstacles, particularly in organizational, financial, legal, and ethical realms. Organizational challenges have been emerged, including a lack of coordination in outsourced sectors and a dearth of expert contractors participating in tenders. Additionally, there's been a deficiency in infrastructure, manifested in the use of non‐specialized personnel by the private sector and inadequate monitoring and evaluation mechanisms. Such deficiencies have contributed to gaps in service provision and hindered the continuity of care [17, 18, 19, 20, 27, 28]. Financial challenges have also been apparent, with delays in payments posing significant issues for project sustainability. Private sector uncertainty regarding return on capital investment has further complicated financial viability [17, 20, 27, 28]. Furthermore, legal and ethical challenges have surfaced, characterized by restrictive laws and regulations impeding partnership effectiveness and limiting public access to justice [19, 20, 28].

Discussion

As outlined in the previous section, the study identified four main themes and fifteen sub‐themes. Among these, the theme of “positive service delivery outcomes” was the most frequently cited, appearing in 100% of the included studies. This theme was explored across multiple countries, including Jordan, Qatar, Iran, the UAE, Turkey, Iraq, Egypt, and Yemen. The second most frequently cited theme was “positive economic outcomes,” referenced in 67% of the studies, primarily conducted in Turkey, Iran, Iraq, Egypt, Yemen, and the UAE. This section of the study focuses on a discussion of these two prominent themes, contextualizing the findings within broader settings.

Our study's findings on the positive service delivery outcomes of implementing PPPs in Middle Eastern healthcare systems align with evidence from other regions. Research from Europe, and Africa consistently present that PPP‐operated hospitals and clinics provide higher‐quality care compared to solely public facilities, demonstrated by faster diagnosis and treatment, improved clinical outcomes, and greater patient satisfaction [4, 29, 30]. Similarly, our results on enhanced performance indicators correspond with global literature, which highlights increases in patient volumes and service utilization, reduced waiting times for critical procedures, lower infection and mortality rates, shorter hospital stays, and improved financial efficiency [29, 30]. Furthermore, PPPs have been presented to promote systematic data collection and continuous evaluation, contributing to sustained performance improvements [4].

Literature from other regions corroborated our findings on the positive economic impact of implementing PPPs in Middle Eastern healthcare systems. For example, hospital PPP models in Portugal demonstrated better adherence to timelines and budgets than traditional procurement, resulting in improved infrastructure delivery and reduced cost overruns [30, 31]. Similarly, WHO reports from the European Region highlight PPPs' potential to optimize health infrastructure by addressing capacity and obsolescence issues [32]. Furthermore, PPPs have been shown to ease fiscal pressures on governments by mobilizing private investment and sharing risks, thereby enhancing the financial performance of health facilities [30, 33]. Although the literature regarding the implementation of PPPs in Europe has reported mixed results on cost efficiency compared to public management, many document lower direct medical costs and reduced out‐of‐pocket expenses in PPP‐managed hospitals, including gains in delivery and tuberculosis treatment costs. Nonetheless, in certain cases, PPP hospitals have not surpassed traditional hospitals in profitability or cost control [30].

In contrast with the findings on the positive outcomes of the implementation of PPPs in healthcare, as our study findings stated corroborated by other findings within the literature, empirical evidence does not consistently confirm the expected cost savings and efficiency gains of PPPs in hospitals, particularly regarding task integration and leveraging private sector expertise. PPPs are deemed effective only when specific conditions are met, including a supportive political and social environment, accurate benefit assessment, consideration of public concerns, and evaluation of private contributions [4]. Implementation challenges such as political and financial instability, delayed reimbursements, poor stakeholder coordination, and insufficient continuous quality monitoring have adversely affected PPP outcomes [34]. Additionally, concerns exist that PPPs may exacerbate inequalities and limit healthcare access for disadvantaged populations, potentially reducing care quality by linking it to patients' ability to pay—contradicting the principle of free healthcare [33]. Reported issues in countries like Malawi, Pakistan, and Vietnam include irregular reimbursements, drug shortages, declining service quality, ineffective referrals between public and private facilities, weak integration of national health programs, and limited governmental regulatory capacity [35].

To address the challenges of implementing PPPs in healthcare, the literature proposes several solutions. Governments should develop long‐term, context‐specific plans rather than adopting one‐size‐fits‐all approaches [7]. Clear, flexible, and robust contracts with appropriate risk‐sharing and effective governance are essential, as rigid agreements can impede adaptability and collaboration [33]. Inclusive engagement of all stakeholders—including public and private sectors, insurers, and communities—in design, management, and evaluation fosters shared goals and trust [34, 36]. Additionally, establishing strong monitoring and evaluation systems enables ongoing assessment and improvement, ensuring service quality and responsiveness [34, 37].

Limitations and Implications

The study had limitations that should be acknowledged. During the database search process, it was not possible to include local databases from the countries within the study sample. Conducting searches within local databases in the respective local languages could potentially yield more accurate and comprehensive results related to the topic in the region. This represents an important consideration for future researchers, who may enhance the depth and precision of their investigations by incorporating data from local databases in each country's native language. However, this objective could not be achieved in the present study due to the authors' limited familiarity with most of the languages spoken in the region, as well as constraints related to accessibility, time, and available resources. In addition, studies published in the authors' native language were not included, as this may have introduced heterogeneity by combining data from internationally published research indexed in major academic databases with studies produced within the authors' home country, where peer‐review standards were considered to be comparatively less rigorous. Another limitation of this study was the use of the AACODS checklist for assessing the quality of the included studies instead of other widely recognized critical appraisal tools. The AACODS checklist was selected because of its simplicity, applicability across diverse study designs, and ease of interpretation, which may facilitate its use by future researchers. Nevertheless, the use of this tool may limit the comparability of the quality assessment with studies that have employed more established appraisal instruments. Another limitation of this study was that diverse types and models of PPPs were examined collectively as a single entity. Different PPP types and models may produce varying outcomes; however, analyzing and presenting outcomes according to each specific PPP model was beyond the scope of this study due to time and resource constraints, as well as the limited availability of relevant data in the existing literature. Future research should focus on investigating and comparing the outcomes associated with different PPP types and models.

The study had several implications to address. The study identified healthcare service delivery as the primary outcome domain of PPPs in Middle Eastern healthcare systems, a finding consistently reported across all included studies. This has important implications for beneficiaries, particularly policymakers and administrators seeking to improve organizational service delivery performance. Additionally, the study highlighted several challenges following PPP implementation, notably concerning cost savings and efficiency gains, as well as other context‐specific issues that stakeholders and future researchers should carefully consider. Furthermore, the study revealed that PPPs may produce adverse effects for vulnerable populations by exacerbating inequalities and restricting healthcare access, an important consideration for regions with lower socioeconomic status.

Conclusion

The integration of PPPs into Middle Eastern healthcare systems has yielded diverse outcomes, ranging from advancements in social justice and service delivery to economic resilience. While PPPs has improved client‐focused outcomes and service efficiency through innovative technologies and governance models, they have also faced persistent organizational, financial, legal and ethical challenges. In this regard, PPPs are deemed effective only when specific conditions are met. Further research is warranted, as the existing findings are inconsistent and their generalizability remains limited.
