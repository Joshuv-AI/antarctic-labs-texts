Medial patellofemoral ligament reconstruction in skeletally immature patients: distal versus proximal femoral physis fixation
Authors: Yi Hua, Hua Wang, Xu Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo compare clinical outcomes and complications of medial patellofemoral ligament (MPFL) reconstruction using femoral fixation proximal versus distal to the physis in skeletally immature patients with patellar instability.MethodsThis retrospective study included 23 skeletally immature patients (24 knees) undergoing isolated MPFL reconstruction from January 2021 to August 2024. Patients were divided into proximal (n = 12 knees, mean age 14.1 ± 0.9 years ) and distal (n = 12 knees, mean age 13.8 ± 1.6 years ) physis fixation groups. Preoperative imaging assessed patellar height, trochlear dysplasia, and the tibial tuberosity–trochlear groove (TT-TG) distance. Clinical outcomes (Tegner, Kujala, Lysholm and International Knee Documentation Committee [IKDC]) and complications (re-dislocation, patellar fracture, infection, stiffness, quadriceps weakness and growth disturbance) were compared.ResultsThe mean follow-up durations were 24.4 ± 4.3 months (range, 17–33) in the proximal group and 22.8 ± 4.7 months (range, 17–30) in the distal group (P = 0.374). Both groups showed significant improvements in all functional outcomes (P < 0.001) with no intergroup differences (P > 0.05). Subjective satisfaction was similar (8.5 vs. 8.3, P = 0.726). Return to sport occurred in 91.7% of both groups. Complication rates were numerically higher in the proximal group (41.7%; mainly knee pain (n = 3, 25%) and quadriceps weakness (n = 2, 16.7%) than in the distal group (24.9%; infection (n = 1, 8.3%), pain (n = 1, 8.3%), weakness (n = 1, 8.3%)(P = 0.667). No redislocations, patellar fractures, stiffness, or growth disturbances occurred.ConclusionsBoth proximal and distal femoral physis fixation for MPFL reconstruction yield similar clinical outcomes and complication rates in skeletally immature patients. However, due to the limited sample size of this study, the current conclusion should be interpreted with caution.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13018-026-07125-3.

Introduction

Patellar dislocation is common in children and adolescents, with an annual incidence of approximately 50 per 100,000 [1–4]. The medial patellofemoral ligament (MPFL) is the primary restraint against lateral patellar displacement and is injured in up to 90% of first-time dislocations [5–7]. Nonoperative treatment carries a high redislocation risk, particularly in skeletally immature patients [8–11]. MPFL reconstruction is effective, but femoral graft fixation remains debated to avoid physeal injury and ensure isometry [12–15]. Fixation is typically proximal or distal to the epiphyseal line [16]. Growth-related migration of the fixation point may cause failure, and it is unclear which position yields superior outcomes. Biomechanical study have indicated that distal physeal fixation may be associated with better graft isometry and clinical outcomes, as proximal physeal fixation may lead to excessive graft tightening during knee flexion, resulting in reduced range of motion or pain [16, 17]. However, no clinical studies have directly compared these two fixation techniques.

This study compared clinical outcomes and complications between proximal and distal femoral physis fixation in skeletally immature patients. It was hypothesized that distal fixation would provide comparable outcomes with similar or lower complication rates.

Methods

After institutional review board approval, we retrospectively reviewed skeletally immature patients undergoing isolated MPFL reconstruction from January 2021 to August 2024.

Inclusion criteria: (1) first-time acute or recurrent patellar dislocation patients; (2) complete clinical and preoperative imaging (lateral radiographs, CT); (3) isolated MPFL reconstruction with proximal or distal femoral fixation.

Exclusion criteria: (1) prior knee surgery; (2) multi-ligament injury; (3) skeletal maturity; (4) habitual or fixed patellar dislocation with severe lateral contracture.

For patients with first-time patellar dislocation, skeletal immaturity itself is a risk factor. After systematic evaluation and thorough discussion with the patient and their guardians, those who choose isolated MPFL reconstruction are included in this study [18, 19].

Surgical technique

Patients were positioned supine, and spinal anesthesia was utilized in all cases. Diagnostic arthroscopy was routinely conducted to evaluate and address any intra-articular pathology. Lateral release was performed if tight lateral structures present under anesthesia. The semitendinosus tendon was then harvested and prepared.

Patellar Fixation identical in both groups: 3-cm incision was made over the medial margin of the patella. Two 4.5mm suture anchors were inserted into the midpoint and proximal one-third of the medial edge of the patella. A bony trough was made between the two points of the medial border of the patella using rongeurs. The midpoint of the graft was then fixed on the patella through the suture anchors.

Femoral approach: 3-cm incision at medial epicondyle. The choice of femoral fixation location was based on individual surgeon preference.

Proximal group: The femoral anatomical insertion point of the MPFL was determined under fluoroscopy using the method described by Schöttle et al. [20]. A guide pin was inserted obliquely and proximally posterior to the adductor tubercle. Fluoroscopy confirmed that the insertion point was approximately 7 mm proximal to the epiphyseal line. A 5 mm diameter drill was used to create the femoral tunnel along the guide pin, with the distal end of the drill reaching the lateral femoral cortex (Fig. 1A).

Distal group: A guide pin was inserted obliquely anteriorly and distally at the Schöttle point. Fluoroscopy confirmed that the positioning point was approximately 7 mm distal to the physis. A 5 mm diameter drill was used to create the femoral tunnel approximately 3 cm in length along the guide pin (Fig. 1B).

Graft passed and tensioned at 60° flexion with neutral patellar tracking. After confirming normal patellar tracking, a 6 mm diameter bioabsorbable interference screw was used.

Rehabilitation protocol

Both patient groups followed an identical postoperative rehabilitation protocol. The operated knee was immobilized in a hinged knee brace for the first 6 weeks postoperatively. On the first postoperative day, patients were permitted to perform weight bearing as tolerated while wearing the brace. Quadriceps strengthening exercises were initiated postoperatively and continued throughout rehabilitation, including isometric quadriceps sets and straight-leg raises. Range of motion (ROM) exercises commenced at 2 weeks postoperative. Patients achieved 90° of knee flexion by 4 weeks postoperatively, with full ROM restored between 8 and 12 weeks postoperatively. Noncontact sports activities were permitted beginning at 4 months postoperatively, and unrestricted return to sports (jogging, swimming or cycling) was allowed at 6 months postoperatively.

Patient characteristics and preoperative imaging

Demographic and perioperative data including age, sex, affected side, body mass index (BMI), number of patellar dislocations, operative time, length of postoperative hospital stay, and follow-up duration were recorded for all patients.

Preoperative imaging comprised a true lateral radiograph to measure the Caton-Deschamps index (CDI) for patellar height, and CT scans to assess the tibial tubercle-trochlear groove (TT–TG) distance, patellar tilt angle (PTA), and trochlear morphology using the Dejour classification. Given the generally slight to fair interobserver reliability of the original 4-type Dejour classification for trochlear dysplasia, cases were categorized into low-grade (Dejour type A) and high-grade (Dejour types B, C, and D) dysplasia to improve reliability and clinical interpretability [21].

Clinical outcome measures

Patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) were assessed preoperatively and at final follow-up using the International Knee Documentation Committee (IKDC) subjective knee score, Kujala score, Lysholm score, and Tegner activity scale. Subjective patient satisfaction was evaluated with a numeric rating scale (0 = complete dissatisfaction, 10 = full satisfaction). Return-to-sports rates were documented.

Postoperative complications in both groups were recorded and included recurrent patellar dislocation, patellar subluxation, wound infection, knee stiffness, persistent anterior knee pain, patellar fracture, and physeal growth disturbances.

Physeal injury was assessed by clinical evaluation. At the final follow-up, patients were examined for limb-length discrepancy, genu varum, or other signs of abnormal lower-extremity growth.

Clinical, radiographic, and outcome data were collected during scheduled in-person clinic visits, with mailed questionnaires or structured telephone interviews used as needed.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics 25.0 (IBM Corp, NY, USA). Categorical variables (sex, affected side, complications) were compared between groups using Fisher exact test. Continuous variables, including age, BMI, follow-up duration, PROM scores, and radiographic parameters were compared using independent-samples t-test. Preoperative and postoperative PROM scores within groups were compared using paired samples t-test. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Results

Patient demographics

A total of 23 skeletally immature patients (24 knees) were included in the analysis (Fig. 2). In this study, all patients completed the follow-up. The proximal physis group comprised 12 knees in 12 patients (7 males, 5 females), with a mean age of 14.1 ± 0.9 years (range, 13–16 years) and a mean follow-up of 24.4 ± 4.3 months (range, 17–33 months). The distal physis group included 12 knees in 11 patients (5 males, 6 females), with a mean age of 13.8 ± 1.6 years (range, 12–16 years) and a mean follow-up of 22.8 ± 4.7 months (range, 17–30 months).

There were no significant differences between the groups in preoperative demographic characteristics, including age, sex, affected side, proportion of recurrent versus first-time dislocation, body mass index, Caton-Deschamps index, Dejour trochlear dysplasia type, patellar tilt angle, or TT-TG distance (P > 0.05) (Table 1). In the proximal group, 5 knees had recurrent patellar dislocation and 7 had first-time acute dislocation. In the distal group, 6 knees had each type.

Clinical outcomes

All PROMs improved significantly from preoperative to final follow-up in both groups (P < 0.001) (Table 2).

In the proximal physis group, the Kujala score increased from 43.5 ± 10.6 to 89.9 ± 7.8, the Lysholm score from 42.9 ± 8.9 to 90.3 ± 7.4, the Tegner score from 1.5 ± 0.5 to 3.5 ± 0.7, and the IKDC score from 37.4 ± 9.1 to 85.9 ± 7.5. In the distal physis group, the Kujala score increased from 44.3 ± 8.2 to 91.3 ± 5.9, the Lysholm score from 44.8 ± 9.3 to 91.4 ± 5.7, the Tegner score from 1.4 ± 0.5 to 3.7 ± 0.8, and the IKDC score from 38.4 ± 7.4 to 85.2 ± 4.3.

No significant differences were found between the proximal and distal physis groups in any postoperative PROM scores or subjective satisfaction. Eleven of 12 patients (91.7%) in each group successfully returned to sports.

Complications

No cases of recurrent instability, growth disturbance, patellar fracture, or stiffness occurred in either group (Table 3).

In the proximal physis group, 3 patients (25%) reported knee pain during flexion that resolved with rest, and 2 patients (16.7%) had quadriceps weakness with thigh muscle atrophy. In the distal physis group, 1 patient (8.3%) developed a postoperative infection that resolved after debridement, 1 patient (8.3%) had knee pain, and 1 patient (8.3%) had quadriceps weakness. The overall complication rate was higher in the proximal physis group (5 complications in 12 knees, 41.7%) than in the distal group (3 complications in 12 knees, 25.0%), although the difference was not statistically compared in the original analysis. There was no statistically significant difference in complication rates between the two groups (41.7% vs. 25.0%, P = 0.667).

Discussion

The principal finding of this study was that there was no significant difference in the overall complication rate between proximal and distal femoral fixation (41.7% vs. 25.0%, P = 0.667) in skeletally immature patients with patellar instability. Nevertheless, both techniques yielded comparable improvements in PROMs, including Kujala, Lysholm, IKDC, and Tegner scores, as well as high rates of return to sport (91.7% in both groups). However, due to the limited sample size of this study, its conclusions should be interpreted with caution, and future studies with larger sample sizes and longer follow-up periods are warranted to further validate these findings. Additionally, isolated MPFL reconstruction proved effective regardless of preoperative TT-TG distance, patellar height, or trochlear dysplasia.

Femoral tunnel placement in skeletally immature patients is technically demanding due to the proximity of the distal femoral physis to the native MPFL femoral origin. Soft tissue fixation techniques, such as the medial collateral ligament (MCL) pulley, avoid physeal injury but have been associated with higher revision rates [22]. Lind et al. [23] reported a 21% revision rate with soft-tissue femoral fixation in this population. In contrast, bony fixation techniques generally provide more reliable stability. Mountney et al. [24] demonstrated that through-tunnel graft fixation achieved a tensile strength of 195 N, closely approximating the native MPFL (208 N). In the present study, no recurrent instability occurred in either group during a mean follow-up of 23.6 months, supporting the reliability of bony fixation when properly positioned.

Anatomical and radiographic studies consistently show that the femoral origin of the MPFL lies distal to the distal femoral physis in skeletally immature patients, typically 4 to 9 mm distal (mean approximately 7 mm) [25, 26]. Nelitz et al. [27] first reported favorable outcomes with bony fixation distal to the epiphyseal line, with no growth disturbances. Subsequent work by Nguyen et al. [28] found that to minimize damage to the physis and distal femoral cartilage, the distal femoral drill tunnel should be angled appropriately 15° to 20° distally and anteriorly. Irarrázaval et al. [29] further expanded this safe zone and found that the optimal direction for femoral tunnel drilling in anatomical MPFL reconstruction is achieved when aiming distally at 30°-40° and anteriorly at 5°-35°. The current study corroborates these findings: no growth disturbances, recurrent instability, patellar fractures, or stiffness were observed with distal fixation.

The higher complication rate in the proximal group, particularly chronic knee pain during flexion, aligns with evidence linking nonanatomic femoral tunnel placement to adverse outcomes [30]. Proximal malposition can increase patellofemoral contact stresses, reduce graft isometry, and contribute to anterior knee pain or early cartilage deterioration [31]. Hu et al. [17] and Kaya et al. [32] reported that proximal femoral tunnel displacement with higher postoperative pain scores and complications. Yoon et al. [33] further reported worse cartilage status and International Cartilage Repair Society (ICRS) grade progression with proximal versus distal tunnel position. In growing patients, proximal fixation may also lead to proximal migration of the attachment site with longitudinal growth, potentially resulting in graft laxity or failure, as described by Kupczik et al. [34] in two cases. However, Belkin et al. [35] demonstrated that in patients with significant patella alta, proximal femoral fixation during MPFL reconstruction may provide better graft isometry. In the current study, both the proximal and distal groups had a high prevalence of patella alta (CDI: 1.52 vs. 1.56). It may explain why the proximal fixation group did not show inferior clinical outcomes compared with the distal fixation group.

Conversely, anatomical fixation better restores native anatomy and patellofemoral kinematics [36]. Biomechanical data indicate that distal-to-physis placement provides more isometric graft behavior in immature knees [16]. Clinical series have shown high satisfaction, rapid return to sport, and improved activity levels with this approach [9]. While one case report noted physeal arrest after distal epiphyseal fixation [37], the overall literature and the present study supports its relative safety when performed with fluoroscopic guidance and appropriate technique. Nevertheless, distal placement is not risk-free, factors such as drill trajectory, implant stress, or local vascular changes warrant individualized planning and careful postoperative monitoring.

Isolated MPFL reconstruction has demonstrated efficacy in adults even with bony abnormalities, including increased TT-TG distance, patellar alta, and trochlear dysplasia [38, 39]. In the current study cohort, 29.2% of patients had an increased TT-TG distance (> 20 mm), 87.5% had patellar alta (CDI > 1.3), and 70.8% had high-grade trochlear dysplasia (Dejour types B, C, and D). Despite these risk factors, isolated MPFL reconstruction produced significant PROM improvements and low failure rates, consistent with reports by Husen et al. [9] and others. This suggests that soft-tissue stabilization alone may suffice in many skeletally immature patients, potentially delaying or avoiding more invasive bony procedures until skeletal maturity.

This study has limitations. Its retrospective, non-randomized design introduces potential selection bias. The patient grouping was based on the surgeon’s preference, which might lead to selection bias. Post hoc power was only 7.6%, reflecting insufficient power due to the small sample size. The small sample size limits statistical power for detecting differences in uncommon events such as growth disturbance or recurrent instability. The cohort mixed patients with first-time and recurrent dislocation without performing subgroup analysis, which may affect the results. In addition, advanced imaging follow-up (CT or MRI) was not routinely performed due to cost and radiation concerns, only plain radiographs were obtained postoperatively to assess alignment. Moreover, chondral lesions were not systematically assessed using MRI and intraoperative management of chondral lesions was not recorded. Furthermore, the exact timing of return to sport was not systematically collected, as some patients could not accurately recall this information at follow-up. Similarly, the postoperative time of onset of complications was not systematically recorded. Longer-term follow-up is needed to evaluate growth-related changes and potential late complications.

Conclusion

In skeletally immature patients undergoing MPFL reconstruction, distal epiphyseal femoral fixation offers similar complication rates and clinical outcomes to proximal fixation. Isolated MPFL reconstruction is an effective option for patellar instability in this population, even in the presence of bony risk factors.
