Developing and Testing Electronic Versions of the Advance QoL Tools for Collecting Self‐Reports of Quality of Life in Children and Adolescents With Advanced Cancer
Authors: Lye‐Ann Robichaud, Nikita Guarascio, Michel Duval, Bruno Michon, Marianne Olivier‐D'Avignon, Mathias Tyo‐Gomez, Émélie Rondeau, Marc‐Antoine Marquis, Serge Sultan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionTo provide appropriate interventions for young people with advanced cancer, self‐reported questionnaires assessing quality of life (QoL) are crucial. This study aims to develop and test electronic versions of two short self‐reported questionnaires (for children aged 8–12 and adolescents aged 13–18).MethodsWe adapted the paper‐and‐pencil versions of Advance QoL into electronic versions using the REDCap platform. We tested the electronic versions and evaluated their social validity (acceptability, relevance, and satisfaction) through semi‐structured cognitive interviews with 20 children (median age 9) and adolescents (median age 15) with cancer. Recruitment and data collection involved two refinement phases, using cognitive interviews to guide adaptations. To analyse responses from the social validity questionnaire, we used the content validity index (CVI), median and range.ResultsFrom the interviews, we identified 22 codes, organised into five themes: (1) content‐related elements, such as lack of information regarding a radar chart displayed in the questionnaire; (2) format‐related elements, including the unnoticed 24‐h time window; (3) technology‐related elements, such as the correct date format; (4) positive features (e.g., length, comprehensiveness, user‐friendliness); and (5) children‐specific elements such as distractibility and attention spans. These insights guided modifications to optimise Advance QoL 's electronic use. CVI indices showed high social validity among the participants (children: two out of three CVI indices were ≥ 0.78; adolescents: all three CVI indices were ≥ 0.78).ConclusionFeedback was largely positive. We addressed minor issues using REDCap's features. In its present state, Advance QoL is comprehensible, usable, and considered pertinent by patients. It offers a unique opportunity to collect the self‐reported data of a very ill population, thereby providing targets for care.

Introduction

Advanced paediatric cancer refers to a stage of illness in which the disease no longer responds to standard curative treatments, and progresses or recurs, limiting the chances of remission [1, 2]. In highly developed countries, including Canada, about 17% of children and adolescents with cancer will have advanced paediatric cancer. In other regions, this percentage may be much higher (e.g., 50% in Latin America) [3, 4, 5]. Each year, over 1000 young people (aged 0–18) are diagnosed with cancer in Canada, with overall one‐ and five‐year survival rates of 93% and 84%, respectively [5]. These young people and their families are likely to benefit from paediatric palliative care (PPC), which focuses primarily on optimising quality of life (QoL) and well‐being, as well as reducing symptom burden. PPC aims to meet the unique needs of young people with advanced cancer and their families—medical, physical, psychological, emotional, social, familial or spiritual—enabling them to live as actively as possible [6, 7].

To provide appropriate interventions for these young people, self‐reported questionnaires assessing their QoL are essential. Many questionnaires have limitations when used with young people with advanced cancer, including inappropriate recall periods, incomplete coverage of QoL domains, limited stakeholder involvement in their development, and insufficient psychometric validation [8, 9, 10].

The need for a questionnaire specifically addressed to this population has led to the creation of the Advance QoL research program [1, 8, 11, 12]. It aimed to use a bottom‐up approach to describe QoL domains specific to advanced paediatric cancer [11]. By integrating the perspectives of young patients, their parents, and healthcare professionals, a group of researchers developed a model encompassing seven key domains for this population [1]. It includes the well‐described domains of physical, psychological and social well‐being and original domains such as pleasure, autonomy, achievements, and the feeling of being heard and considered. The data collected enabled the development of a proxy‐rated questionnaire (rated by parents or healthcare providers) to assess the QoL of young people with advanced cancer [1]. To allow young people to describe their feelings and QoL, two self‐report versions of the Advance QoL questionnaire have recently been developed (8–12‐years‐old; 13–18‐year‐old) [10] following a comprehension study conducted with young cancer patients to validate the statements and vocabulary [8, 12, 13, 14]. Age ranges for the self‐reported versions were established based on other QoL assessment tools [7, 8, 15, 16]. The questionnaires are available in the study's repository [17]. In line with recommendations from the literature, the next phase of the research program is to adapt the questionnaires to an electronic format.

Indeed, there are many arguments in favour of developing electronic versions of questionnaires. Electronic patient‐reported outcome measures (ePROM) are feasible, relevant, and convenient for assessing the QoL of young people with advanced cancer [18, 19]. They allow for collecting honest and reliable data on sensitive information while considering patient preferences [20, 21]. ePROMs also reduce response bias, offer flexibility in timing, and facilitate follow‐ups through electronic profiles [20, 22]. Studies show strong correlations with paper‐and‐pencil data [23, 24, 25] and fewer missing responses [23]. Thus, it is recommended that electronic versions of questionnaires be made available to facilitate completion by young people [18]. ePROMs provide access to sensitive information, enhancing our understanding of the patient and improving support.

The main objective of this study, as a continuation of the Advance QoL research program [1, 8, 11, 12], is to develop and test electronic versions of the two self‐reported Advance QoL questionnaires (for children aged 8–12 and adolescents aged 13–18). Three phases guided this process (Figure 1): (1) Adapt the paper‐and‐pencil questionnaires into electronic versions, (2) Test the use of Advance QoL electronic versions and evaluate its social validity (acceptability, relevance, and satisfaction) with children and adolescents with cancer, and (3) Refine the final electronic versions of Advance QoL. This usability study evaluates how respondents interact with the ePROM, identifying and addressing usage errors, which is necessary before using the ePROM to collect real data [26]. We assessed its accuracy, effectiveness, user satisfaction, ease of use, and completion time. Results obtained will guide modifications to better align with young people's preferences. These findings, combined with those from the comprehensive study [12], should enhance the quality of data collected by Advance QoL and prevent missing data by reducing the burden on young people [26].

Development process for electronic versions of Advance QoL.

Methods

Phase 1: Adaptation

Five young collaborators, a resource patient and healthcare professionals developed the ePROM using the Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) platform [27, 28]. Data were securely collected and managed with REDCap hosted at CHU Sainte‐Justine. This platform's flexibility offers options for customising online questionnaires. Following recommendations, we focused on ease of use as a criterion over visual equivalence [26]. We ensured the ePROM was user‐friendly from the age of 8 and compatible with various devices. Before Phase 2, the ePROM was repeatedly pretested to ensure the tool operated without technical issues.

Phase 2: Testing and Evaluating the Electronic Versions

Participants and Recruitment

Given the limited number of children and adolescents with advanced cancer and the recruitment challenges faced in the previous study [12], we used convenience sampling [29] to recruit young cancer patients from our tertiary hospital (CHU Sainte‐Justine, Montreal, Canada) and a non‐profit support organization for children with cancer and their families (Leucan, Qc, Canada) from January to May 2024. As the objectives focused on evaluating electronic tools, we believe that broadening the inclusion criteria beyond advanced cancer did not impact the data collected. Prior to the study, the participants and the research team had no pre‐existing relationship. Eligible patients, aged 8 to 18 years old, were diagnosed with cancer at least 3 months before enrollment, underwent cancer treatment, were proficient in French, were able to communicate verbally, and had access to an electronic device connected to the Internet. No exclusion criteria were applied.

Leucan informed eligible patients by email. Interested families contacted a research team member (LAR, a female PhD candidate in psychology) for details. At CHU Sainte‐Justine, the clinical team provided a list of eligible patients who were approached by a research team member (LAR) by phone. Prior to data collection, the research team obtained parental written consent and young patients' assent. Participants received a CAD 50 gift card as appreciation for their involvement. The first author (LAR) conducted, recorded and transcribed the interviews. Participants were assigned a code number and code name. The study received ethical approval from the CHU Sainte‐Justine Research Ethics Committee (#MP‐21‐2022‐3550).

Multiple data collection rounds help address smaller, previously unnoticed questionnaire issues, reducing measurement errors [26]. Recruitment and data collection occurred in two rounds. We aimed to recruit 10 children (ages 8–12) and 10 adolescents (ages 13–18) to test the two self‐reported versions. In Round 1, five participants per age group tested Advance QoL, with data analysis leading to revisions of the questionnaires. In Round 2, the research team recruited another 10 participants (five per age group) for further testing. This method is recognised as a powerful means for gathering comprehensive usability information. Round 2 also ensured that targeted problems were resolved without raising new ones [26]. This method and number of participants are deemed adequate, as approximately 85% of problems are identified in each round, ensuring efficient coverage [26, 30].

Data Collection

The data collection procedure was consistent across both rounds.

Sociodemographic

At the end of the interview, a brief self‐reported questionnaire collected participants' sociodemographic and clinical data (age, sex, gender, education level, years since diagnosis, and paediatric cancer condition).

Interview

Data were collected through virtual semi‐structured cognitive interviews lasting up to 45 min using the Zoom Health platform [31]. Interviews focused on age‐appropriate tool versions. Participants accessed the questionnaire via a REDCap link provided during the interviews. We developed an interview guide, incorporating think‐aloud techniques and closed‐ and open‐ended questions to explore questionnaire usage (see Supporting Information for more information) [32]. Participants completed the questionnaire based on their current status. The interviewer offered encouragement and used prompts if needed [26, 32]. Technology‐related questions, such as device used and computer proficiency, were included to understand participants' experiences [26]. We also asked about their preferred questionnaire format. Before data collection, we piloted the interview schedule with young collaborators from Phase 1.

Social Validity

In each round, we assessed social validity using a questionnaire developed for the previous study focusing on the comprehension of Advance QoL self‐reported versions [12]. Based on two social validity measures previously used in health research [33, 34], this questionnaire was completed after Advance QoL to appraise its acceptability, importance, and degree of satisfaction [35, 36]. Consisting of 11 questions on a five‐point scale (1 = totally disagree to 5 = totally agree), it evaluates the overall clarity, relevance, utility, ease of use, and satisfaction (e.g., “The tool is useful for assessing my well‐being.”). We added a specific item focusing on the ease of accessing the questionnaire on electronic devices. Participants were encouraged to provide verbal feedback, especially if they answered ≤ 3 (disagree or neutral), to enhance understanding of their perspectives and facilitate refinement [37].

Data Analysis

Cognitive Interview

We adapted an existing protocol for analysing and interpreting cognitive interviews for instrument development [12, 38]. This method systematically identifies issues and potential improvements for Advance QoL. Items or features that were understood and interpreted consistently among participants were kept, while others were revised. First, we transcribed the interviews and used an item‐by‐item approach to extract segments from each interview using Microsoft Excel. Second, we assigned a code to each segment that highlighted a problematic element, a positive aspect, or a suggestion. We clearly defined each code, whether based on the literature or generated inductively, to avoid ambiguity (e.g., misunderstood word). Third, we grouped these codes into main themes. To ensure the analysis' reliability, two team members (LAR and NG) independently coded the interviews.

Social Validity

To analyse responses from the social validity questionnaire, we used the content validity index (CVI) [37]. A CVI ≥ 0.78 with 10 experts indicates that the social validity questionnaire items assess the acceptability, relevance and satisfaction of Advance QoL [37]. In our study, youth with cancer are experts to evaluate and critique Advance QoL. We calculated three indices to analyse the CVI: (1) For each item, we calculated the item‐level content validity index (I‐CVI) – that is, the proportion of experts who judge the item as relevant; (2) we calculated the scale‐level content validity index based on the average method (S‐CVI/Ave)—that is, averaging all proportions of I‐CVI; (3) we calculated the scale‐level content validity index based on the universal agreement method (S‐CVI/UA)—that is, the proportion of items deemed relevant by all the experts [37]. Median and range were used to analyse the social validity questionnaire. A score of ≤ 3 (disagreement or neutral evaluation) would indicate a need for refinement.

Phase 3: Final Refinement

To review the electronic questionnaires, we used REDCap features (e.g., branching logic). All changes aimed to alleviate respondent burden, facilitate Advance QoL usability, and display a layout that clearly informs participants.

Results

Sample Characteristics

We recruited 11 children (no girls) aged 8 to 12 years (M = 9.6 years) and 10 adolescents (6 girls) aged 13 to 18 years (M = 14.7 years). One child withdrew from the study after signing the consent form, making the final study sample n = 10 for each age group. Participants were in 2nd to 11th grade. The median time since cancer diagnosis was 9 months (range: 4–135 months). Cancer diagnoses included leukaemia (n = 6), Hodgkin lymphoma (n = 5), brain tumour (n = 4), and others (n = 5). Results Overview

In Round 1, we conducted 10 semi‐structured cognitive interviews (5 per age group; average: 36 min). We had to adapt the think‐aloud instructions to adjust for difficulties with divided attention and concentration. Devices used included computers (n = 5), tablets (n = 3), and smartphones (n = 2). Three children used an electronic questionnaire for the first time. Preferred formats were electronic (n = 5), none (n = 3), and paper‐and‐pencil (n = 1), with one missing data. In Round 2, we conducted another 10 interviews (5 per age group; average: 34 min). Devices included computers (n = 5), tablets (n = 4), and smartphones (n = 1). All children were new to electronic questionnaires. Preferred formats were electronic (n = 6), paper‐and‐pencil (n = 3) and none (n = 1). In both rounds, the preference for the paper‐and‐pencil format was primarily attributed to participants' personal inclination toward handwriting over typing.

The qualitative analysis of responses revealed 22 codes, allowing us to better understand how participants used Advance QoL (Figure 2). Codes were identified to ensure they captured the participants' ideas and aligned with the study objectives. No major discrepancy was noted between the two coders. The code “Problems encountered when using the questionnaire” only appeared in Round 1, while five new codes emerged in Round 2 (i.e., apprehension about self‐disclosure, conscious non‐compliance with a time window, confidentiality, asking for their opinion, relevance). These codes were grouped into five main themes: (1) elements related to the questionnaire's content; (2) elements related to the questionnaire's format; (3) technology‐related elements; (4) positive features; and (5) elements specific to children. These themes were developed based on the focus of each code, with the theme names representing the broader categories that group the individual codes.

Issues identified during Rounds 1 and 2 regarding Advance QoL electronic self‐reported questionnaires: results from cognitive interviews with 20 participants.

Between Rounds 1 and 2, Advance QoL was modified following participant feedback. Changes included adding some information (e.g., detailed explanation of the radar chart) and changing the layout of specific information (see Table S3—Examples of changes made to the questionnaire between data collection rounds 1 for more information). Some codes did not require immediate changes but could guide the questionnaire's future use (see Discussion).

Finally, we produced two figures to compare themes and codes across rounds and age groups. Figure 2 presents the codes identified during Rounds 1 and 2, regardless of the age group. Figure 3 displays the same 22 codes that emerged from the analyses of Rounds 1 and 2, categorised by age group. This comparison highlights the similarities and differences between the age groups when using Advance QoL to assess their QoL. It also underscores the necessity to test and validate electronic questionnaires with children and adolescents.

Participants also mentioned difficulties related to a lack of information, misunderstanding words and concepts (e.g., achievements) and a feeling that some items did not apply to their situation. In Round 2, participants reported apprehension with self‐disclosure, as the questionnaire addresses several important aspects of their private lives. Some participants expressed reluctance to reveal personal information due to embarrassment or shyness. The last code related to the questionnaire's content was raised by adolescents only: despite an awareness of the 24‐h time window, some adolescents answered the questions according to a different time window. So maybe someone who relies on their last 24 h won't talk about the fact that, in general, things aren't going so well. (A27, Round 2)

Participants mentioned elements related to the questionnaire's format (Theme 2). They suggested modifications to the questionnaire's visual aspects: “Maybe add a little more colour, pictures, that kind of thing […] because it was a bit boring and monotonous. Like always the same colour, always the same questions, always the same thing (A21, Round 1).” Participants' feedback also suggested that the time window information was not clearly displayed, leading it to be occasionally overlooked or forgotten.

Participants also mentioned technology‐related elements (Theme 3). They identified actual and potential issues with the electronic questionnaire. The main challenge was the date field, which required a specific format (YYYY‐MM‐DD). Errors occurred if the format was not respected, preventing further progress. This issue was resolved between Rounds 1 and 2, and no additional problems were reported. The last code in Theme 3 addressed children's computer skills. Some needed parental assistance to type on the keyboard, which may have influenced their use of the questionnaire.

Participants reported several positive aspects of the electronic questionnaire. They considered the examples as useful and relevant. The electronic format was perceived as easier, clearer, quicker and more motivating compared to the paper‐and‐pencil questionnaire. The length of Advance QoL was deemed acceptable, and it was considered user‐friendly, relevant, and comprehensive. The adolescents particularly appreciated the questionnaire's confidentiality, and both age groups appreciated being asked for their opinions. Here is a verbal statement:Yes, it didn't have too many questions, and then it didn't have “not enough” either. I think it was a reasonable number of questions. Sometimes, I find that if it has too many questions, then, at some point, I'm kind of bored with doing the questionnaire, and then I can't wait for it to be over. […] I'm more in a hurry to write them down. Then, I make fewer developed answers. (A27, Round 2)

Finally, the last theme addresses codes specific to children. Some preferred having a parent present during the interview, which helped guide them through Advance QoL and may have influenced the answers. Children were easily distracted by their environment or thoughts. We also observed that their answers to open‐ended questions were sometimes unrelated to the questions, or the connection was tenuous. For example, E21, 12 years old (Round 1), answered having a good physical well‐being and said: “Well, I don't know [why]… I've never really felt bad about my body. So, I'd say because I have family and friends who love me.”

Social Validity

For children, two out of three calculated CVI indices were ≥ 0.78; for adolescents, all three CVI indices were ≥ 0.78, indicating high social validity among users. For children only, the proportion of social validity items deemed relevant by all children was low (S‐CVI/Ave = 0.18), possibly due to the electronic modality. Notably, 8 out of 10 children were using an electronic questionnaire for the first time. Detailed CVI results are presented in Table 2. Despite this, Table 3 shows that the median score for each item was consistently ≥ 4. Therefore, both children and adolescents “agree” or “totally agree” that Advance QoL is an acceptable, relevant, clear, and useful measure.

Supplementary Observations

During the interviews, we had the opportunity to hear comments from participants that could inform the future clinical use of the tool. Participants were able to describe their well‐being and provide ideas to improve the questionnaire. Table 4 summarises examples of participant responses. I was able to […] express my emotions, say how I was feeling, say how… I've never been asked about my autonomy or things like that, and I think it's fun to go further.”

Final Refinement

As a result of this process, we made the same minor changes to both versions of Advance QoL (8–12 and 13–18 years old). One specific change addressed the codes of “cognitive load, do not relate to the item, and computer skills,” while other changes targeted “appearance and aesthetics” and “time window not noticed.” We used REDCap's advanced functions to make the completion as seamless as possible [27, 28]. We added a few images and highlighted the 24‐h time window in bold to make it more salient. Discussion

The primary objective was to adapt the Advance QoL self‐reported paper‐and‐pencil questionnaires to an electronic format using REDCap [27, 28], and to test the usability and social validity of these electronic versions. The main results of this study, based on 20 cognitive interviews with children and adolescents with cancer, show that the participants appreciated the electronic tools. Several strengths of the Advance QoL self‐reported questionnaires were identified, including the comprehensive QoL domains assessed, the ease and simplicity of questionnaire completion, and the fact that they are addressed specifically to young people. More than a third of the codes identified in the analyses (8/22; 36.36%) describe elements that young people perceived positively. Only one real problem (the date field) was encountered when using the electronic questionnaires. The modifications made between Rounds 1 and 2 resolved this issue and did not introduce any new problems in Round 2. Modifying the questionnaires, such as adding information for the radar chart, implementing branching logic, and incorporating images, allowed us to refine Advance QoL. These modifications enhance both its usability and the validity of the collected data. Our results show that the methods used in this usability study are feasible, original, relevant and effective for eliciting high‐quality feedback from young people with cancer [13, 14, 26, 32].

Assessing the Advance QoL tools' social validity showed that children and adolescents found the questionnaire acceptable, relevant, clear, and useful. High social validity suggests our measure can positively impact QoL improvement [35]. However, one CVI index (S‐CVI/Ave) for children was below the recommended threshold, indicating response variability [37]. While median scores were ≥ 4 out of 5, children's scores varied more than those of adolescents. This variability may be due to the electronic format, which was new for 80% of the children. Maintaining both paper and electronic versions of Advance QoL is recommended, allowing children to choose based on their ease with typing and individual preference [18].

The codes grouped under the theme “elements related to the questionnaire's content” identified issues that complicated task completion for participants without entirely preventing it. For example, open‐ended questions sometimes felt redundant and caused cognitive load, while the lack of information left some participants unsure. Multiple‐choice questions were easier than open‐ended questions. When using a questionnaire with young people with advanced cancer, it is crucial to minimise their burden, as they fatigue easily [9]. Nevertheless, the literature supports the collection of both quantitative and qualitative QoL data [10]. Thus, balancing questionnaire feasibility with the need for valid and relevant information is essential. It also contributes to the sustainability of the approach [22]. In the final version, we aimed to minimise cognitive load while ensuring the collection of relevant information.

An interesting finding from the “elements related to the questionnaire's content” theme is the deliberate non‐compliance with the 24‐h time window. Although adolescents noticed this information, some intentionally disregarded it. Two hypotheses can explain this result. First, some participants felt that 24‐h could not fully capture their QoL, which aligned with previous observations [39]. Second, some participants noted that it was the first time being asked about their QoL, leading them to express a need to discuss their illness experience. The experience of responding to Advance QoL could meet a deeply rooted need for sharing and disclosing such information in some participants [40].

The themes “elements related to the questionnaire's format” and “technology‐related elements” underscore the relevance of this usability study. Comments highlighted the layout's importance, the information's visual appearance, and the instructions' clarity in an electronic questionnaire, as mentioned in the literature [26, 41]. In our first electronic versions, we mainly reproduced the information from the paper‐and‐pencil questionnaire. Since some tasks were not identical, there was some misunderstanding, especially in Round 1. For example, in the paper‐and‐pencil version, respondents draw the chart themselves, whereas in the electronic questionnaire, the chart is programmed to automatically reflect their responses. This led to minor modifications, such as adding information to clarify the meaning of the data displayed in the chart. This example emphasises that visual equivalence between paper‐and‐pencil and electronic versions does not ensure data quality or ease of use, consistent with the literature [26].

Among the eight codes describing the tool's positive features, two aspects are particularly noteworthy, as they address participants' concerns. First, questionnaire length, a critical factor in assessing burden, was deemed appropriate by participants. Some were pleasantly surprised by its brevity, as they had anticipated a much longer questionnaire. Second, the tool provided a sense of security, encouraging participants to disclose sensitive information. Since young cancer patients often experience reduced control and privacy, supporting their confidentiality and autonomy is essential [42, 43, 44]. Nevertheless, some participants emphasised the need to clearly explain the questionnaire's purpose. They highlighted the importance of balancing support with respecting their needs to avoid feeling watched. Allowing them to complete the questionnaire independently, in their preferred format and at their convenience aligns with a patient‐centred approach, enhancing both the therapeutic alliance and the quality of care [45].

One important result was that some children expressed technical difficulties while completing the instrument, most probably because of difficulty typing responses. While the literature advocates for electronic versions [18], both paper‐and‐pencil and electronic formats should be maintained. This enables format flexibility to improve accessibility and respect young people's preference and autonomy [18, 19, 20].

Children‐specific elements and clinical observations prompt reflection on using questionnaires in advanced paediatric cancer. Consistent with previous research, our results suggest that PROMs and ePROMs are feasible and acceptable tools for collecting health‐related and QoL data in paediatric medical settings [22]. Although this study focuses on the development of electronic tools, the findings provide relevant insights for the future clinical implementation of Advance QoL. The care setting (e.g., home or hospital) and the care trajectory (e.g., ongoing treatment or follow‐up appointments) influence the questionnaire's perceived relevance. Depending on the young person's situation, certain QoL domains may be assessed differently. For instance, an adolescent noted that hospitalisation limits autonomy and friendships more than at home, suggesting that a questionnaire completed in this context might better identify immediate needs. Some participants also stressed the importance of timing when completing this questionnaire: considering multiple aspects of their QoL might be too challenging if they already focus their energy and attention on treatments or survival. This suggests that the clinical context should be considered before assessing life domains. Finally, much of the data collected with Advance QoL depended on the participant's developmental stage, as self‐reporting on QoL requires some degree of verbal expression, reflexivity, and self‐assessment [13]. The results suggest that to collect QoL data effectively in this context, we must assess young people's ability to discuss their QoL and adjust our approach accordingly. It also stresses the value of collecting data at various times throughout an individual's illness [46, 47]. This study showed that Advance QoL is suitable for ages 8 and up.

We should recognise the present study's limitations. First, the convenience sample may introduce selection bias, as participants may have a better QoL than the targeted population. Despite this, the sample is heterogeneous regarding age, condition, and computer skills, providing diverse insights into electronic questionnaire use. Participants tested Advance QoL on smartphones, tablets, and computers, demonstrating its feasibility across different platforms, which increases external validity. Although no girls in the 8–12 age group were recruited, no gender‐specific patterns were observed in the feedback collected from the other age group. Second, the research context may have introduced response bias. Participants completed the Advance QoL while being interviewed and recorded, which limited the data's ecological validity. To reduce this bias, we emphasised that there were no right or wrong answers and that the study was not evaluative like a school assessment. Participants were encouraged to freely express their thoughts and suggest improvements. Third, we acknowledge that the questionnaire might be influenced by a North American perspective, particularly regarding autonomy, one of the domains assessed. It has been suggested that the development and understanding of autonomy may vary across different cultural backgrounds [48].

Conclusion

In this usability study with 20 childhood advanced cancer patients, we tested two electronic versions of the Advance QoL tools (ages 8–12 and 13–18) using two rounds of cognitive interviews and a social validity survey. Participants valued the electronic versions, identifying several positive aspects. Minor issues were resolved using REDCap functionalities. The output of this research is a usable electronic QoL assessment tool for young people with advanced cancer. Further research should assess its validity, reliability and sensitivity. Advance QoL have significant potential to improve communication between patients, healthcare teams, and families, ultimately enhancing QoL through targeted interventions.
