Molecular mechanisms of RACK1-driven metastasis in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma revealed by single-cell RNA sequencing
Authors: Hangyu Liu, Tingxin Wang, Yuying Cui, Biao Zhang, Dong Shang, Huiyi Song
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) is a highly aggressive malignancy with significant cellular heterogeneity, which poses a significant challenge for treatment. We integrated three single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) datasets to construct an expression matrix, identify cell types, and assess copy number variation (CNV) scores, with a particular focus on ductal cells. Our study found that ductal cells are the primary source of malignant cells in PDAC and identified a high-malignancy subtype—ductal cell-3—where RACK1 was the most significantly differentially expressed gene. Through the analysis of public and clinical data, along with RT-qPCR and immunohistochemistry validation, we confirmed the high expression of RACK1 in PDAC tumors, which correlated with lymph node metastasis, clinical staging, histological differentiation, and prognosis. Further investigation revealed that RACK1 promotes PDAC cell proliferation, migration, and invasion. Sequencing analysis indicated that RACK1 inhibits the FOXO signaling pathway, promotes epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), and facilitates the progression and metastasis of PDAC. This study provides new insights into the role of RACK1 in PDAC and its underlying mechanisms.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-025-03679-6.Keywords: Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, scRNA-seq, RACK1, RNA-seq, EMT

Introduction

Despite significant advancements in cancer diagnosis and treatment over the past few decades, the 5-year survival rate for patients with pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) remains alarmingly low, below 10% [1]. The poor prognosis of PDAC is primarily attributed to late-stage diagnosis, as the majority of patients do not exhibit noticeable symptoms until the disease has progressed. Over 80% of patients are diagnosed at a stage when the disease is either unresectable or metastatic [1, 2]. This outcome is largely driven by the high intratumoral heterogeneity of PDAC, making the identification of the primary source of malignant cells crucial. This understanding opens new avenues for the discovery of early diagnostic biomarkers [3]. Additionally, the complex mechanisms underlying early metastasis in PDAC present substantial challenges to effective treatment [4, 5]. To improve early diagnosis and extend survival in PDAC patients, it is imperative to further investigate the cellular heterogeneity and biological characteristics of PDAC and to elucidate the mechanisms of its metastasis.

In recent years, single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) has emerged as a powerful approach for investigating the heterogeneity of pancreatic cancer tissues. This technique enables high-resolution mapping of cellular heterogeneity and dynamic alterations within the tumor microenvironment, offering novel perspectives on the complex biology of PDAC [6–11].

In this work, we analyzed scRNA-seq data from 106,330 individual cells derived from PDAC and liver metastasis tissues, as well as normal pancreatic tissues, to construct a comprehensive cell atlas of PDAC. Through bioinformatics analysis, we identified that the primary source of malignant cells in PDAC is ductal cells, and discovered subpopulations of cells that may promote cancer progression and metastasis. Additionally, we identified a differentially expressed gene, RACK1. By analyzing public data and clinical information, we further investigated the correlation between RACK1 and the pathological features and prognosis of PDAC. Through cellular experiments and RNA-seq, we explored the impact of RACK1 on PDAC cell biological functions and its underlying mechanisms. These findings enhance our understanding of PDAC progression and metastasis mechanisms and may lay the foundation for early diagnosis and precision therapy for PDAC.

Results

Single-cell transcriptomic atlas and cell typing of primary PDAC tumors, liver metastases, and normal pancreatic tissue

To explore the cellular diversity of PDAC, we performed principal component analysis (PCA) on the variably expressed genes across all cells for dimensionality reduction, identifying 17 major cell clusters. Using specific cell markers, we further classified these 18 clusters into 9 distinct cell types, including T cells, stellate cells, macrophages, ductal cells, endothelial cells, acinar cells, fibroblasts, B cells, and mast cells (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Fig. 1). In most cases, we utilized well-established cell type markers to identify the cell clusters. For instance, the cluster with high expression of CD3E was identified as T cells [12], the cluster expressing high levels of RGS5 was classified as stellate cells [13], the cluster expressing AIF1 was categorized as macrophages [14], the cluster with high expression of CFTR was identified as ductal cells [15], the cluster expressing CDH5 was classified as endothelial cells [16], the cluster expressing CPA1 was identified as acinar cells [17], the cluster with high expression of COL3A1 was classified as fibroblasts [18], the cluster expressing MS4A1 was categorized as B cells [19], and the cluster with high expression of TPSB2 was classified as mast cells [20] (Fig. 1b and Supplementary Fig. 2). Furthermore, we analyzed the distribution of these cell types across different tissue origins (Fig. 1c), revealing that most cells originated from PDAC tissues, with nearly equal proportions of cells derived from normal pancreatic tissue and liver metastatic lesions. Ductal cells exhibited the most prominent representation among these origins.

To more precisely distinguish and analyze malignant and non-malignant cells, we calculated the large-scale chromosomal copy number variations (CNVs) for each cell type using copyKAT, based on the average expression patterns across the entire genome. The results revealed that the CNV levels in ductal cells were significantly higher compared to other cell types (Fig. 1d).

The heterogeneity and malignancy of ductal cells during the progression of PDAC

As ductal cells may exhibit distinct differentiation and transformation characteristics during PDAC progression, and based on the aforementioned results, we were prompted to further investigate their heterogeneity to determine whether subpopulations with more pronounced malignant features exist. PCA of ductal cells revealed three ductal cell subtypes, namely Ductal cell-1, Ductal cell-2, and Ductal cell-3 (Fig. 2a, b). To further define the malignant cell subtypes within ductal cells, we calculated the large-scale chromosomal CNVs for each cell subtype using the average expression patterns across the entire genome with inferCNV. The results showed that the CNV levels in Ductal cell-2 and Ductal cell-3 were significantly higher than those in Ductal cell-1 (Fig. 2c). In a single-cell sequencing study that only included pancreatic cancer and adjacent normal pancreatic tissues, two subtypes of ductal cells were identified. These subtypes, ductal cell-1 and ductal cell-2, expressed marker genes consistent with those observed in this study [21]. However, upon integrating single-cell sequencing data from liver metastatic tissues, a third subtype of ductal cells, ductal cell-3, emerged. We analyzed the tissue origins of three subtypes of ductal cells and found that ductal cell-3 is largely absent in normal pancreatic tissue, predominantly originating from PDAC tissue and liver metastases (Fig. 2d).

Based on the above findings and in conjunction with previous studies by Puram et al. [22], we applied three criteria to identify the malignant cell origin in PDAC: (1) expression of epithelial cell marker genes, (2) the presence of high levels of CNVs, and (3) high expression of genes associated with malignant cell behavior. Ductal cells express epithelial cell marker genes such as CFTR and TFF2 [23, 24], exhibit higher CNVs, and show elevated expression of genes related to malignant cell behavior. Therefore, ductal cells may serve as the primary source of malignant cells in PDAC. In ductal cells, as shown in Fig. 2a, Ductal cell-3 also significantly expresses TFF2, has higher CNVs compared to the other two subtypes, and shows high expression of genes associated with malignant cell behavior. Thus, Ductal cell-3 may represent the subtype of ductal cells with a higher degree of malignancy.

Next, we investigated the gene expression profiles within the three subtypes of ductal cells and identified differentially expressed genes between ductal cell-3 and the other two cell subtypes. Functional enrichment analysis revealed that the upregulated genes in ductal cell-3 were predominantly enriched in cancer-related functions, such as fibrinolysis, negative regulation of response to wounding, further supporting the malignant state of this subtype (Fig. 2e). We also identified a significantly differentially expressed gene, RACK1, which can be used to distinguish aberrant and malignant gene expression profiles in ductal cells (Fig. 2f). To better understand the functional states and relationships of ductal cells, we employed the Monocle 2 algorithm to elucidate the developmental trajectory of ductal cells. Pseudotime analysis revealed a branching structure starting from ductal cell-1, gradually diverging into two directions, giving rise to ductal cell-2 and ductal cell-3. Subsequent pseudotime analysis, in conjunction with the expression pattern of RACK1, indicated its appearance in the late stages of ductal cell differentiation, predominantly concentrated in ductal cell-3, thereby further corroborating the role of RACK1 in tumor progression (Fig. 2g).

To verify whether the characteristic of increased RACK1 expression during PDAC progression is commonly observed in PDAC tissues, we analyzed the expression of RACK1 in all cell types. The results showed that RACK1 is primarily expressed in ductal cells, cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), macrophages, and T cells (Supplementary Fig. 3). Based on this, we focused on the expression of RACK1 and further analyzed the other three cell types, obtaining results consistent with those in ductal cells (Supplementary Figs. 4–6).

The clinical significance of RACK1 expression

We first explored the clinical significance of RACK1 expression using public databases. Analysis of The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) data revealed that RACK1 expression was significantly higher in pancreatic cancer tissues (T) compared to normal pancreatic tissues (N), consistent with our previous findings (Fig. 3a). To determine whether RACK1 expression is specific to pancreatic cancer, we further analyzed its expression across multiple cancer types using public datasets. Results demonstrated that RACK1 expression was generally elevated in tumor samples compared to their corresponding normal tissues across various cancer types (Fig. 3b). Additionally, we evaluated the impact of RACK1 expression on patient survival time, revealing that patients with high RACK1 expression exhibited shorter overall survival (Fig. 3c). However, the survival curve did not show significant differences beyond 5 years, likely due to the extremely low 5-year survival rate of pancreatic cancer, making it difficult to observe statistically significant distinctions.

To validate the above data analysis, we conducted molecular biology experiments confirming the overexpression of RACK1 in tumor tissues, assessed through RNA quantification and protein expression levels. First, we performed Reverse Transcription-Quantitative Polymerase Chain Reaction (RT-qPCR) on RNA extracted from 10 randomly selected pairs of pancreatic cancer tissues and adjacent non-cancerous tissues. The results revealed a significantly higher level of RACK1 RNA in pancreatic cancer tissues compared to adjacent non-cancerous tissues (Fig. 3d). Next, we performed immunohistochemical (IHC) staining for RACK1 on 51 PDAC tissues (PT) and adjacent non-tumor tissues (NT). We found that RAC1 expression was significantly higher in PDAC tissues compared to adjacent non-tumor tissues (Fig. 3e).

To further explore whether RACK1 may be a significant determinant of clinical outcomes in PDAC, we investigated the correlation between upregulated RACK1 expression and clinicopathological features. PDAC samples were divided into two groups based on the Remmele scoring system [25, 26]: low RACK1 expression (scores 0–4) and high RACK1 expression (scores > 4). Clinical pathological data of patients were summarized in Table 1. As shown in Table 1, the expression level of RACK1 was significantly positively correlated with histological differentiation (p = 0.009), lymph node metastasis (p < 0.01), and TNM staging (p = 0.019). However, we found no significant association between RACK1 expression and other clinicopathological features, including age and location. This suggests that RACK1 may play a role in the occurrence and development of pancreatic cancer, and its high expression may be associated with the tumor's high invasiveness or low differentiation.

Downregulation of RACK1 expression inhibits cell proliferation, migration, and invasion in PANC-1 cells

Downregulation of RACK1 expression in PANC-1 cells divided the cells into the downregulation group (SI group) and the negative control group (NC group) [27]. Clonogenic assays revealed that downregulation of RACK1 notably suppressed the proliferative capacity of the cells compared to the NC group (Fig. 4a, b). Additionally, wound healing and Transwell assays were performed to evaluate cell migration and invasion. In PANC-1 cells, the SI group exhibited a marked reduction in both migration and invasion compared to the NC group (Fig. 4c–f).

Upregulated expression of RACK1 promotes cell proliferation, migration, and invasion in PANC-1 cells

Overexpression of RACK1 in PANC-1 cells divided the cells into the overexpression group (OE group) and the negative control group (NC group). Clonogenic assays demonstrated that RACK1 overexpression enhanced the proliferative capacity of the cells relative to the NC group (Fig. 4g, h). Selection of differentially expressed genes

To elucidate the molecular impact of RACK1 downregulation on PDAC cells, we conducted differential gene expression (DEG) analysis to identify gene expression alterations in PANC-1 cells following RACK1 knockdown. Among the differentially expressed genes, 806 were upregulated, and 532 were downregulated. The selection criteria for differential genes involved eliminating genes with low expression or zero values, with a threshold set at |log2FC|> 2. Notably, in addition to RACK1, the differentially expressed genes included a significant number of genes associated with epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), such as CDH1, FOXO1, MMP1, LAMC2, and others [28–31] (Fig. 5a).

Functional annotation and classification

We performed Gene Ontology (GO) analysis to uncover the regulatory effects of RACK1 downregulation on cellular activities. Among the top 15 enriched MF terms, we identified five that are closely associated with EMT, including Endoderm formation [32], Extracellular matrix organization [33], Formation of primary germ layer [34], Extracellular structure organization [35], and External encapsulating structure organization [36] (Fig. 5b).

Subsequently, we performed Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis to explore the pathways with the highest DEG enrichment. In the top 10 most enriched pathways, four were strongly related to EMT, namely Glycolysis [37], Glutathione metabolism [38], FOXO signaling pathway [30], and Pentose phosphate pathway [39]. Notably, the FOXO signaling pathway exhibited the most significant association with cell proliferation and EMT. Combining these findings with previous studies, we hypothesize that RACK1 may influence the progression and metastasis of PDAC by modulating the FOXO signaling pathway, thereby regulating the EMT process. We will proceed to validate this hypothesis in subsequent experiments (Fig. 5c).

E-cad serves as a marker for epithelial cells, while N-cad and VIM are markers for mesenchymal cells, all of which play critical roles in the EMT process. Discussion

Despite continuous advancements in the diagnosis and treatment of PDAC, patient survival has not improved due to the persistent high rate of early distant metastasis. Although studies have reported a high degree of similarity between primary PDAC tumors and liver metastases in terms of somatic mutations, copy number variations, and transcriptomic profiles, the cellular-level homogeneity and heterogeneity between primary and metastatic PDAC remain unresolved [40].

Most PDAC patients lose the opportunity for surgical intervention upon the detection of metastasis, thereby hindering a comprehensive understanding and assessment of the entire tumor microenvironment in metastatic PDAC through surgical resection. To date, studies exploring the cellular ecosystem of PDAC metastasis using single-cell sequencing methods have been extremely limited [7, 41]. This study incorporates single-cell sequencing data from multiple PDAC liver metastasis tissues and provides a more comprehensive analysis of both primary PDAC tumors and metastatic lesions, offering a detailed single-cell transcriptomic landscape of PDAC. Our research not only delineates the cellular diversity within tumor, stromal, and immune components at high resolution but also identifies ductal cells as the primary origin of malignant PDAC cells, consistent with previous studies [42, 43]. Furthermore, we characterize markers associated with the developmental processes of both primary and metastatic PDAC cells. These findings partially address the existing gaps in our understanding of PDAC metastatic tissues.

An increasing body of research indicates significant intratumoral heterogeneity in PDAC, posing substantial challenges for designing precise diagnostic and therapeutic strategies [44–50]. Therefore, it is essential to study different cell types individually rather than as a collective population, particularly to investigate the primary origins of malignant cells. The aim is to identify their commonalities as precisely and comprehensively as possible, thereby exploring more reliable diagnostic markers and developing more targeted therapeutic strategies. Building on the identification of ductal cells as the primary source of malignant cells, this study further uncovered a more aggressive cell subtype within them, Ductal cell-3, and revealed that RACK1 may play a critical role in promoting tumor progression and metastasis. Consistent results were also observed in other cell types.

Interestingly, recent studies have reported that RACK1 plays a role in promoting tumor progression in other types of cancer [51–54]. As shown in our study on the expression of RACK1 in pan-cancer, RACK1 expression is generally higher in pan-cancer tissues compared to the corresponding normal tissues, especially in prostate cancer, colon cancer, and PDAC [55, 56]. This validates the functional relevance of our bioinformatics analysis. However, the role and mechanisms of RACK1 in PDAC have rarely been reported. Therefore, we believe it is essential to further explore its functions and underlying mechanisms.

RACK1 belongs to the WD repeat protein family (UniProt P63244) with a molecular weight of 37 kDa [57–59]. The WD repeat domain contains a characteristic tryptophan-aspartic acid dipeptide motif, enabling RACK1 to function as a scaffold protein involved in the assembly of supramolecular complexes [60]. As a widely expressed protein in mammals, RACK1 participates in regulating cellular signal transduction, growth, and development [61–64]. Since its discovery as an interacting protein of protein kinase C (PKC) [57], the role of RACK1 in tumorigenesis and progression has attracted significant attention. Studies have shown that RACK1 exerts pro-tumorigenic effects in various cancers through its unique structural and functional properties.

Post-translational modifications (PTMs), particularly ubiquitination, play a crucial role in regulating RACK1 function [51, 65]. For instance, in ovarian cancer, downregulation of SMURF2 reduces RACK1 ubiquitination, thereby enhancing its stability and promoting tumor progression [66–68]. Similarly, in breast cancer, RACK1 stabilizes β-catenin by interacting with PSMD2, facilitating tumor cell proliferation [51, 68]. These findings align with the results of the present study: elevated RACK1 expression enhances the proliferation, migration, and invasion capabilities of PDAC cells. Notably, the pro-tumorigenic effects of RACK1 are widespread. Beyond PDAC, RACK1 has been demonstrated to promote tumor cell proliferation and metastasis in various malignancies, including prostate cancer, lung cancer, cervical cancer, liver cancer, melanoma, and neuroblastoma [69–76]. These studies collectively underscore the importance of RACK1 as a potential therapeutic target. Zhang et al. reported in their study that they used CircVPRBP to target and inhibit RACK1 O-GlcNAcylation and stability to suppress lymph node metastasis in cervical cancer. The article by Challal et al. proposed that by inhibiting PARP14-mediated RACK1 MARylation and blocking stress granule formation, the growth of ovarian cancer cells is slowed down [77, 78].

In this study, RNA-seq analysis of transfected PDAC cells, integrated with bioinformatics, revealed that RACK1 likely promotes PDAC progression and metastasis by suppressing the FOXO signaling pathway. The FOXO pathway, a critical tumor-suppressive pathway, is regulated by upstream signals such as PI3K/AKT and IL-6/STAT3, and exerts its anti-tumor effects by modulating downstream target genes involved in cell cycle regulation, apoptosis, metabolism, and oxidative stress [79–81]. Research has demonstrated that in prostate cancer, SUV39H2 inhibits FOXO3a function through the AKT/FOXO pathway, reducing apoptotic capacity and promoting tumor growth and drug resistance [82–84]. In liver cancer, the FOXO pathway suppresses tumorigenesis by inhibiting cell proliferation, promoting apoptosis, enhancing oxidative stress responses, and inducing ferroptosis [85]. Notably, cyclin-dependent kinases (CKs) enhance FOXO1 transcriptional activity by inhibiting its phosphorylation, thereby suppressing liver cancer cell growth and promoting ferroptosis [86].

Moreover, the FOXO pathway plays a pivotal role in tumor EMT. FOXO proteins regulate EMT-related transcription factors (e.g., Snail, Twist, and ZEB1) [87–90], modulate cell adhesion molecules and cytoskeletal dynamics [91–94], and inhibit inflammatory and cytokine signaling [95–98], thereby suppressing the EMT process through multiple mechanisms. Based on cellular and molecular biology experiments, we propose that RACK1 may act as an upstream regulator of the FOXO pathway, promoting PDAC EMT by suppressing FOXO signaling, thereby driving tumor progression and metastasis.

Our study has several limitations. Firstly, the single-cell sequencing data were not directly generated but were derived from the integration and analysis of high-quality databases. Secondly, due to the high heterogeneity of the tumor microenvironment in metastatic multifocal tumors, even within the same patient, the current dataset may not fully capture the complete ecosystem of metastatic PDAC. For instance, despite utilizing multiple neural cell markers, we were unable to identify neural cell populations in the present dataset. Therefore, establishing an independent single-cell sequencing database for PDAC and metastatic tissues is warranted in future research. Additionally, although RACK1 exhibited similar patterns across other cell types, its specific mechanisms remain unexplored, and its potential role in cell–cell interactions requires further validation through high-dimensional multiplexed in situ profiling. Finally, the lack of integrated spatial transcriptomic data limited our ability to fully resolve the spatial distribution of tumor cells. Incorporating such data in future studies will provide a more precise understanding of tumor progression patterns.

In summary, our findings provide novel insights into the dynamic changes during the malignant progression of PDAC. We identified a new ductal cell subpopulation (Ductal cell 3) and elucidated the critical role of the oncogenic gene RACK1. Through in vitro experiments and RNA-seq analysis, we demonstrated that RACK1 promotes PDAC progression and metastasis by suppressing the FOXO signaling pathway, thereby influencing tumor EMT. These results not only deepen the understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying PDAC but also offer valuable insights for developing new diagnostic markers and targeted therapeutic strategies, potentially paving the way for improved patient prognosis.

Methods

scRNA-seq data collection

The research involves the acquisition of three single-cell RNA sequencing datasets from public databases. The dataset GSE197177 of the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database comprises 6 single-cell data, including one normal tissue sample, two pancreatic cancer tissue samples in situ and three metastatic tissue samples [99]. The dataset GRA001160 of the GSA database contains 35 single-cell data, including 11 normal tissue samples and 24 groups of pancreatic cancer tissue samples in situ [11]. The dataset SCP1644 from the Single Cell Portal database contains 14 untreated metastatic tissue samples [21].

Human PDAC samples for RT-qPCR and IHC

All patient samples were obtained from Department of General Surgery of Dalian Medical University Affiliated First Hospital. The human PDAC tissues and adjacent nontumorous samples were obtained from 61 patients who underwent surgery without preoperative systemic chemotherapy at the Department of General Surgery of Dalian Medical University Affiliated First Hospital. The diagnosis of all cases of PDAC was confirmed histologically, based mainly on examination of sections stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). Pancreatic cancer and adjacent tissues from 10 patients were used for RT-qPCR, while postoperative pathological sections from the remaining patients were used for IHC. The clinical characteristics of PDAC patients were listed (Supplementary Table S1).

This study was approved by Ethics Committee of Dalian Medical University Affiliated First Hospital (Approval No. 2024-346) and conducted in accordance with ethical guidelines (Declaration of Helsinki). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Cell culture and transfection

Human PDAC lines PANC-1 was purchased from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, VA, USA). Cells were maintained in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) with high gluco supplemented and 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2 at 37 °C.

siRNA for gene knockdown was synthesized by GenePharma (China). The sense strand of siRACK1 (5'–3') was GCAGCAACCCUAUCAUCGU, and the antisense strand (5'–3') was ACGAUGAUAGGGUUGCUGCTT. The sense strand of the negative control (siNC) was UUCUCCGAACGUGUCACGU, and the antisense strand was ACGUGACACGUUCGGAGAATT. In addition, plasmids overexpressing RACK1 and the corresponding negative control (NC) plasmid were also synthesized. PANC-1 cells were seeded at a density of 1.0 × 105 cells per well in 6-well plates and cultured for 24 h. Once the cells reached 50–70% confluence, 8 μl of siRACK1, siNC, RACK1 overexpression plasmid, or NC plasmid was added to 100 μl of DMEM medium and mixed (Solution A). Meanwhile, 8 μl of Transfect-mate reagent was diluted in 100 μl of DMEM medium (Solution B) and allowed to stand for 5 min. Solutions A and B were then combined and incubated at room temperature for 20 min. Finally, the mixture was added to 2 ml of complete DMEM medium. After 24 h of incubation, the cells were ready for further experiments.

Ribonucleic acid preparation

Total RNA was extracted from PANC-1 cells using Trizol reagent (ADAMAS LIFE, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The RNA samples were then treated with DNase I (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) in the absence of RNase to remove any residual genomic DNA. The digestion products were purified using magnetic beads (Axygen, Union City, CA, USA). Finally, the concentration, quality, and integrity of the total RNA were assessed using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, Wilmington, DE, USA) and a Qsep400 Bioanalyzer (BiOptic, China). Total RNA with a concentration ≥ 200 ng/mL, quality ≥ 10 μg, and RNA integrity number (RIN) ≥ 8.0 was used for RNA-Seq analysis.

Library construction

mRNA from each sample was enriched using oligo(dT) magnetic beads and then fragmented into short pieces (approximately 200 bp) in fragmentation buffer. The first strand cDNA was transcribed from the fragmented RNA using random hexamer primers, and the second strand complementary cDNA was synthesized in a reaction containing buffer, dNTPs (deoxyribonucleotide triphosphates), RNase H, and DNA polymerase I. The double-stranded cDNA was purified using magnetic beads and subjected to end repair and 30 single adenylation. Integrity and size were assessed using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Finally, the cDNA library was sequenced on a paired-end flow cell using the Illumina HiSeq 4000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA).

Ribonucleic acid sequencing data processing

The raw reads were pre-processed using FastQC software. PCR duplicates, reads containing only adapters, reads with poly-N, and low-quality reads (score ≤ 5) were removed. Clean reads were then used for subsequent analyses. Transcript expression levels were estimated using fragments per kilobase per million reads (FPKM) values and quantified with RSEM software.

Identification of differentially expressed genes

Differential expression analysis was performed using the "DESeq2" R package, with data normalized using the median ratio method, to identify genes with significant expression differences between different groups (control group and knockout group). The criteria for selecting differentially expressed genes were |log2 Fold Change|> 2 and p < 0.05. Differentially expressed genes were visualized using heatmaps generated with the "pheatmap" R package.

Gene ontology and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes enrichment analysis

The DEGs were further subjected to gene functional annotation, with enrichment analysis performed using the Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) databases to explore their potential roles in biological processes, molecular functions, and signaling pathways. In this study, the GO and KEGG enrichment analysis of the DEGs was conducted using the "cluster Profiler" R package, and the results were visualized through bar plots and bubble plots generated with the "ggplot2" R package.

scRNA-seq data processing and cluster annotation

Single-cell sequencing analysis was conducted using the "Seurat" R package [100]. Initially, scRNA-seq data underwent stringent quality control, excluding cell populations with gene counts less than the lowest 2% and greater than the highest 2%, and those where mitochondrial gene composition exceeded 20% [21] method. The data were normalized using the "NormalizeData" function and scaled using the " ScaleData " function. To avoid batch effects among samples and experiments, integration of single-cell data was performed using "harmony" integration. Finally, 106330 single cells remained, and they were applied in downstream analyses.

Clustering of cells was achieved via principal component analysis (PCA), employing "FindNeighbors" and "FindClusters" functions with a resolution parameter set to 0.5. For nonlinear dimensional reduction and visualization, we employed Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP). For subgroup cell clustering, cells of different types were extracted separately and clustered by their respective first 30 principal components (PCs) using different resolutions based on visual inspection.

Identification of cell types and cluster markers

We applied the "FindAllMarkers" function in Seurat to identify specific genes for each cell subset and characterized the identities of cell types of these groups based on expression of known markers. For the selection of marker genes specific to each cell cluster, we calculated the log2 fold change between two groups using the "FindMarkers" function with the Wilcoxon rank-sum test (default parameters).

CNV estimation

Copykat and Infer CNV are both tools used for analyzing chromosome copy number variations (CNVs) in single-cell transcriptome data. Copykat [101] analyzes the relationship between gene copy numbers and gene expression at the single-cell level to infer chromosome-level CNVs. It identifies abnormalities in chromosome numbers or local genomic imbalances within cells and provides cell-level CNV information. Infer CNV relies on statistical models and algorithms to infer cell-level chromosome copy number variations based on single-cell gene expression data. Infer CNV [102] may employ more complex algorithms to analyze and infer CNVs at a finer granularity, typically providing detailed CNV analysis results and visualizations.

Pseudotime analysis by Monocle

Cell developmental trajectories were inferred using Monocle2 with default parameters as recommended by the developers [103]. Firstly, integrated gene expression matrices from specific cell type were exported from Seurat into Monocle to construct a Cell Data Set. Secondly, the "set Ordering Filter" function was applied to sort cells with the variable genes identified by the function of "differential Gene Test". Finally, after dimensionality reduction using the "reduce Dimension" function (employing the "DDRTree" method), the "plot_pseudotime_heatmap" function was used to display the different differentiation stages of "RACK1".

Pathway analysis

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were detected by the "Find All Markers" function in Seurat, using |FC|> 2 and adjusted p < 0.05 as the cut-off values. GO enrichment analysis on DEGs in this study were performed by the cluster Profiler package [104]. Differences between different cell groups were calculated by the "Find Markers" function in the Seurat package.

Analysis of TCGA database

TCGAplot [105] provides several functions to perform pan-cancer paired/unpaired differential gene expression analysis, pan-cancer correlation analysis between gene expression and TMB, MSI, TIME, and promoter methylation. Moreover, gene set based pan-cancer and tumor specific analyses were also available.

Cell growth assay

Two thousand PANC-1 cells (overexpressing RACK1, RACK1 knockdown cells, and their corresponding NC group cells) were seeded in 3 cm diameter culture dishes and cultured for 14 days. The DMEM medium was refreshed every other day. After 14 days, the medium was removed, and the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature for 15 min. Following fixation, the cells were stained with 0.1% crystal violet solution at room temperature for 5 min. Finally, the cells were washed three times with PBS to remove any excess stain. The crystal violet-stained cells were then dried at 37 °C and photographed using a Tanon 5200 automated chemiluminescence imaging system.

Cell wound scratch assay

Cells were seeded in 6-well plates and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. A wound was created in the center of the plate using a sterile 20 µl pipette tip. The cells were gently washed three times with PBS, and then serum-free medium was added. Cell migration was observed using an inverted microscope at 0 and 24 h. The wound area was measured using ImageJ software.

Cell invasion assay

The Matrigel was diluted to a concentration of 200 µg/mL using serum-free medium. 100 µL of the mixture was added to the Transwell chambers and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. Afterward, 200 µL of transfected cell suspension at a concentration of 1.0 × 10^6/mL was added to the Matrigel-coated Transwell chambers. In each well of a 12-well plate, 800 µL of DMEM complete medium was added, and the Transwell chambers were carefully placed into the wells. After 24–48 h, the chambers were removed and fixed in 4% formaldehyde for 15 min, followed by staining with 0.1% crystal violet for 10 min. Cell invasion was observed, counted, and statistically analyzed using an Olympus optical microscope.

Reverse transcription‑quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT‑qPCR)

Total RNA from 10 randomly selected primary tumor and adjacent non-tumor tissue samples were extracted using TRIzol reagent (ADAMAS LIFE, China) according to the manufacturer's protocol. RNA (2 µg) from each sample was used for complementary DNA synthesis using the Primescript RT reagent kit (Yugong Biotech Co., Ltd., China). To amplify the spliced form of human RACK1, the following primer sequences were used: forward, 5'-ACCAGCAGCAAGGCAGAACC-3' and reverse, 5'-GTTGTCCGTGTAGCCAGCAAAC-3'. β-actin was used as an internal control and the primer sequences were as follows: forward, 5'-CCACGAAACTACCTTCAACTCCATC-3' and reverse, 5'-AGTGATCTCCTTCTGCATCCTGTC-3'. PCR was performed on the LineGene 9600 Plus sequence detection system (Bioer technology, Inc., China) and using SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Yugong Biotech Co., Ltd., China), the thermocycler conditions were as follows: 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles at 95 °C for 10 s and 60 °C for 30 s. The relative expression levels of RACK1 was normalized to that of β-actin using the comparative 2−ΔΔCt method.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Slides were deparaffinized through graded xylenes (45 min) and graded ethanol (15 min), and then antigen retrieval was performed using citrate buffer at pH 6.0 (Service bio, China) for 3 min. Slide were then washed in PBS (pH 7.4) for 15 min with moderate rotation. After washing, slides were blocked with 0.3% H2O2 (25 min). After PBS washing, slides were then blocked with 3% BSA (30 min), followed by staining with primary antibody at 4 °C overnight (diluted 1:500; Santa Cruz, USA). After PBS washing, slides were incubated with HRP conjugated Goat Anti-Mouse IgG (H + L) (diluted 1:200; Service bio, China) at room temperature for 50 min, followed by using DAB staining solution (Service bio, China), positive signals appeared as brownish-yellow, finally counterstained using hematoxylin solution for 3 min. The sections were examined under a white light microscope (ECLIPSE E100, Japan) to determine the outcome.

Immunohistochemical evaluation

Stained sections were observed under a microscope. All of the immunostained sections were evaluated in a blinded manner without any information about the clinical and pathological information of the patients by three independent pathologists. Five high-power fields were randomly chosen for assessment of RACK1, and at least 500 cells were counted per field. For density evaluation: 0: no staining; 1: weak staining; 2: moderate staining; 3: strong staining. Also, the cells were scored as follows: 1 (25% tumor cells stained), 2 (26–50% tumor cells stained), 3 (51–75% tumor cells stained), 4 (76–100% tumor cells stained). The immunostaining score was calculated as the staining intensity score × the percentage positive score. Each section was classified as low/no RACK1 expression (0–4) or high RACK1 expression (> 4) based on the final score.

Immunofluorescence

After the cells were allowed to adhere to coverslips, they were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min. Subsequently, the cells were blocked with 3% BSA at room temperature for 30 min. After removing the blocking solution, the cells were incubated with the primary antibody-RACK1(diluted 1:200, Servicebio, China), FOXO1, E-cad, N-cad and VIM(diluted 1:400, CST, USA) overnight at 4 °C. The next day, the corresponding secondary antibody(diluted 1:400, Servicebio, China) was applied and incubated at room temperature for 50 min. Finally, the cells were stained with DAPI for 10 min to label the nuclei. The results were observed under a fluorescence microscope.

Statistical analysis

Images were quantified using ImageJ, and statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 9.5 software (GraphPad; CA, USA), along with the creation of graphs. For comparisons between two groups, an unpaired t-test (for normal distribution) or a Mann–Whitney U-test (for non-normal distribution) was used. The Chi-square (χ2) test was used to evaluate the association between RACK1 expression and clinicopathological features. These experiments were repeated at least three times with similar results. Statistical significance: *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, **** p < 0.0001.
