Multidimensional analysis reveals the potential of ACSL3 as a cancer biomarker: from pan-cancer exploration to functional validation in hepatocellular carcinoma
Authors: Qun Niu, Yu Mou, Yuanqian Yao, HaiJian Dong, Kaixin Wang, Zijian Zeng, Yujing Tao, Xueqing Gong, Hui Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractACSL3 converts long-chain fatty acids into acyl-CoA, a key step in lipid metabolism. Since this process is linked to cancer development and progression, this study investigates the specific roles and underlying mechanisms of ACSL3 across various cancer types. We examined ACSL3 by analyzing its expression, transcriptomic variations, single-cell localization, prognostic significance, and biological roles using various databases and perspectives. We also studied its relationship with the immune microenvironment, epigenetic changes, and drug responsiveness. The effects of ACSL3 on liver cancer were investigated through experiments conducted both in vivo and in vitro experiments. ACSL3 was found to be abnormally expressed in most cancers, with a higher expression ratio in macrophages, and is associated with the prognosis of certain cancers, such as kidney cancer, esophageal cancer, head and neck cancer, liver cancer, lung cancer, thyroid cancer, and ovarian/cervical cancer. Its expression is closely linked to immune cell infiltration, the anti-cancer immune cycle, major histocompatibility complex, immune checkpoints, tumor mutation burden, microsatellite instability, RNA modifications like m1A, m5C, and m6A, as well as DNA methylation. ACSL3 is commonly involved in pathways related to the tumor microenvironment and cell cycle across various cancers. Experiments both in vitro and in vivo showed that reducing ACSL3 levels significantly decreased liver cancer cell growth, indicating its potential as a therapeutic target. Our research underscores the promise of ACSL3 expression as a new biomarker for forecasting the outcomes of various human cancers and assessing the effectiveness of immunotherapy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10238-025-01882-x.Keywords: ACSL3, Pan-cancer analysis, Cancer microenvironment, Computational biology and bioinformatics

Introduction

Cancer remains a major global health challenge and is one of the leading causes of death worldwide [1]. As a polygenic disorder influenced by multiple factors, its initiation and progression are driven by both genetic and environmental factors [2]. Although traditional treatments like surgery, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy have improved outcomes, issues such as treatment resistance, tumor recurrence, and suboptimal responses to new therapies persist [3, 4]. Therefore, identifying novel therapeutic targets and reliable biomarkers is crucial. The advent of high-throughput sequencing has recently revolutionized cancer research, and while many studies focus on individual cancer types, pan-cancer analyses provide a comprehensive view by revealing both commonalities and differences across various cancers. This strategy not only deepens our understanding of shared biological features but also supports the development of treatment approaches that can be applied across different cancer types [5, 6].

Characteristic metabolic alterations in cancer cells are closely associated with bioenergetic imbalance, with lipid metabolism abnormalities being a key driver of tumor progression that supports malignant phenotypes through various mechanisms, including abnormal proliferation, metastasis, and immune resistance [7, 8]. Beyond standard energy production pathways, processes involving fatty acids—such as de novo synthesis, uptake, oxidation, and storage—are essential for tumor development [9, 10]. The breakdown of fatty acids relies on their activation by acyl-CoA synthetase (ACS), with the enzyme’s substrate selectivity depending on the fatty acid’s chain length [11]. Since long-chain fatty acids are frequently found in typical diets, the long-chain acyl-CoA synthetase (ACSL) family serves a crucial role in managing fatty acid metabolism [12].

ACSL3 plays a crucial role as part of the acyl-CoA synthetase long-chain family, encoding a transmembrane protein that catalyzes the combination of long-chain fatty acids (such as arachidonic acid and eicosapentaenoic acid) with coenzyme A to generate acyl-CoA, directly regulating lipid metabolism, membrane phospholipid synthesis, and the biosynthesis of eicosanoid signaling molecules [13, 14]. Its central role in energy homeostasis and cell signal transduction closely links it to tumor occurrence and development. Research suggests that ACSL3 is abnormally expressed in various cancers and drives malignant phenotypes through metabolic reprogramming and signal pathway interactions. For instance, ACSL3 inhibits breast cancer progression and malignant behavior by obstructing lipid metabolic reprogramming and through the inhibition of pathways mediated by the phosphorylation of YES1 leading to YAP1 and downstream pathways [15]; simultaneously, ACSL3 promotes oleic acid secretion from adipocytes, thereby protecting triple-negative breast cancer cells from ferroptosis [16]; ACSL3 is upregulated in prostate cancer and promotes cancer cell growth by enhancing endogenous steroid synthesis [17]; ACSL3 promotes renal clear cell carcinoma progression through a dual mechanism of regulating lipid droplet accumulation and sensitivity to ferroptosis [18]. Although studies have highlighted ACSL3’s tumor-promoting functions in certain cancers, a thorough investigation into its variable expression across diverse cancer types, its epigenetic modification profile, and its connection with genomic instability is still lacking.

In this research, we performed an extensive pan-cancer analysis of ACSL3 utilizing data from various databases and cohorts. We confirmed ACSL3’s enhancing effect on liver cancer cells through experiments involving animals and cell cultures. Additionally, we investigated the mechanisms by which ACSL3 operates in pan-cancer and assessed its potential as a cancer biomarker.

Materials and methods

Data collection

Expression profiles covering multiple cancer types, along with related clinical details, were obtained from the TCGA database (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/). Information on survival outcomes, methylation status, copy number alterations, transcript abundance, microsatellite instability, and tumor mutation burden was gathered from the UCSC Xena repository (https://xenabrowser.net). Additionally, normal tissue expression data were sourced from the GTEx database (https://gtexportal.org/home/). Furthermore, we collected thirty bulk gene expression datasets and fifteen single-cell transcriptomic datasets from the GEO database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). Supplementary Table 1 includes all details concerning cohort composition and accession numbers.

Prognostic and diagnostic analysis

The R software facilitated the investigation of ACSL3 expression across various cancers and their normal tissue counterparts. To investigate the relationship between ACSL3 expression and cancer prognosis, we employed univariate Cox regression analysis and Kaplan–Meier survival estimates using the “survival” package. We also examined how ACSL3 levels correlate with clinical variables such as age, tumor size (T), metastasis (M), lymph node involvement (N), and tumor stage. Moreover, the diagnostic sensitivity and accuracy of ACSL3 in cancer were evaluated by generating Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curves with the “timeROC” package.

Single-cell expression distribution

To investigate the distribution of ACSL3 expression at the cellular level across various cancers, we examined 15 single-cell RNA sequencing datasets. The datasets were refined using the “Seurat (Version 5.2.1)” package with criteria: more than 300 genes, sequencing depth under 30,000, and mitochondrial content below 20%. The workflow included NormalizeData, FindVariableFeatures, ScaleData, and RunPCA for data normalization, identification of highly variable genes, and dimensionality reduction. To address batch effects and integrate the datasets, we used the Harmony method through the IntegrateLayers function. Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) facilitated dimensionality reduction. Cell clustering was conducted using FindNeighbors and FindClusters. Finally, cell types were identified based on marker gene expression.

Gene set enrichment analysis

To investigate the biological functions of ACSL3 in different cancer types, we divided the cancer samples into high and low expression groups based on median ACSL3 levels. We then conducted differential mRNA expression analysis and performed Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) using the “clusterProfiler” package. The study utilized KEGG and Hallmark gene sets as reference frameworks to calculate normalized enrichment scores (NES) and false discovery rates (FDR).

Tumor immune microenvironment and genetic alteration analysis

To investigate the differing immune activities between high and low ACSL3 expression cohorts, anticancer immune state data across diverse cancers were gathered from the TIP database. We applied the “ssGSEA” and “ESTIMATE” methods to quantify immune cell infiltration within tumor samples. Additionally, data concerning tumor mutational burden (TMB), microsatellite instability (MSI), and methylation were extracted from the UCSC database, and corresponding scores for pan-cancer cohorts were calculated. Expression profiles of immune-related genes—including those for the major histocompatibility complex (MHC) and immune checkpoints—as well as genes associated with RNA modifications (m1A, m5C, and m6A) were also compiled. Spearman correlation analyses were conducted to determine the relationship between ACSL3 expression and both the tumor immune microenvironment and genetic alterations. Moreover, the frequency of ACSL3 genetic modifications was examined using the cBioPortal database.

Potential therapeutic drug analysis

For each type of cancer, we identified the top 100 mRNA genes that were most significantly upregulated and downregulated in the high and low ACSL3 expression groups. This selection emphasizes the distinct gene expression patterns linked to ACSL3 levels. We then uploaded these differentially expressed genes and their fold changes to the SPIED3 platform. Using the CMAP database, which contains data on 1309 compounds as the reference dataset, correlation analysis was performed. Compounds with negative correlations in the results were identified as potential therapeutic drugs capable of reversing the expression trends of the differentially expressed genes. Additionally, we obtained drug response scores and RNA-seq expression data from the CellMiner database for the NCI-60 dataset. We focused on FDA-approved and clinically validated drugs to investigate their correlations with ACSL3. A higher drug response score indicates greater sensitivity to the drug.

Cell culture

HepG2, Huh-7 and HEK293T cells, obtained from ProCell (Wuhan, China), were cultured in DMEM enriched with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). All cell types were incubated at 37 °C within a humidified environment containing 5% CO2. For transient gene delivery, transfections were performed using either FuGENE 6 (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) or jetPRIME (Polyplus-transfection, Illkirch, France), following the protocols specified by the manufacturers.

Generation of knockdown cell lines

To stably deplete ACSL3 expression, Huh-7 and HepG2 cells were transduced with lentiviral particles carrying ACSL3-specific shRNAs. These shRNAs were cloned into the pLKO.1 shRNA vector (Thermo Fisher Scientific) based on the following sequences corresponding to the human ACSL3 coding region: shACSL3-1: 5’- CTCGATGTCTTAGTTCCAGTA -3’; shACSL3-2: 5’- CTCGATGTCTTAGTTCCAGTA -3’; shACSL3-3: 5’- CTCGATGTCTTAGTTCCAGTA -3’. The shRNA constructs were subsequently subcloned into the GV493 lentiviral vector (GeneChem, China) and subjected to DNA sequencing for verification. Lentiviral packaging and subsequent transduction of target cells followed established procedures detailed previously [19].

Real-time PCR and immunoblotting

Employing the TRNzol Universal reagent (Tiangen, China), we isolated total RNA. For cDNA synthesis, 1 μg RNA was reverse transcribed using the PrimeScriptTM RT reagent kit with cDNA Eraser (Takara, Japan). Subsequent qPCR amplification was carried out with SYBR green reagent (Takara, Japan) utilizing a real-time PCR thermocycler (Agilent, USA). The following primer pairs were designed for qPCR: ACSL3: Forward-GCCGAGTGGATGATAGCTGC, Reverse-ATGGCTGGACCTCCTAGAGTG; GAPDH: Forward—GAGCTGAACGGGAAGCTCACTG, Reverse—TGGTGCTCAGTGTAGCCCAGGA. Western blot analysis followed previously published methodology [16]. Primary antibodies comprised anti-ACSL3 (20,710–1-AP, Proteintech).

Apoptosis assay

Following seeding in 12-well plates at 50,000 cells per well and a 72-h incubation, cells were harvested. Apoptotic cells were identified using an Annexin V detection kit (Invitrogen) followed by quantification of FITC-positive populations via flow cytometry (BD Canto II).

Wound healing assay

Cell monolayers grown in 6-well plates at approximately 80% confluence were disrupted by scraping with a sterile 200 μL pipette tip. The debris was removed with two PBS washes, and then a medium lacking serum was applied. An inverted microscope was used to record wound closure at 0, 24, and 48 h.

Cell viability assay

Cells plated in 96-well plates (10,000 cells/well) underwent continuous viability monitoring from days 0–4. Relative growth rates were normalized to day 0 values and assessed with the Enhanced CCK-8 Kit (Beyotime, C0042, China).

Cell migration assay

Cell migratory capacity was evaluated in Transwell® chambers (8-μm pore; MilliporeSigma, USA) following supplier guidelines. Migrated cells adherent to the membrane underside were counted in two randomized fields per chamber.

Mouse xenograft models

BALB/c nude mice (4–6 weeks, 18–22 g; GemPharmatech, China) were housed under SPF conditions. Huh-7 cells stably expressing sh-ACSL3 or negative control (2 × 10⁶ cells in 100 μL PBS) were subcutaneously injected into the right flank. Tumor volume was calculated every 2–3 days using caliper measurements and the standard formula: V = (length × width2)/2. Body weights were tracked throughout the study. On day 21, tumors were excised, weighed, and imaged post-euthanasia. Institutional Animal Ethics Committee approval (Chengdu University of TCM; No. 2025069) was obtained.

Statistical analysis

Relationships between variables were evaluated using Spearman’s rank correlation. Differences between two groups were compared with the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, while the Kruskal–Wallis test was used for comparisons involving more than two groups. Survival analyses were carried out using Kaplan–Meier curves alongside Cox proportional hazards models. When conducting multiple hypothesis tests, we used the Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) method to adjust the p-values. All statistical tests and data visualizations were performed using R v4.3.2, with a significance threshold set at P < 0.05.

Results

Abnormal expression of ACSL3 in pan-cancer

Based on TCGA and GTEx expression datasets, we analyzed ACSL3’s differential expression patterns across a range of tumors versus normal tissues. Overall, ACSL3 was found to be dysregulated in the majority of tumors, predominantly showing upregulation. Tumors with significantly higher ACSL3 expression included BRCA, CESC, CHOL, COAD, DLBC, ESCA, GBM, KICH, LGG, LIHC, LUSC, OV, PAAD, PRAD, READ, SKCM, STAD, THCA, THYM, UCEC, and UCS. Conversely, ACSL3 expression was downregulated in KIRC, KIRP, LAML, and TGCT (Fig. 1A and B). Validation of ACSL3 expression levels across 17 cancer cohorts from the GEO database yielded consistent results (Fig. 1C).

Due to the extensive dysregulation of ACSL3 observed across cancer types, we examined its transcript levels in both normal and tumor tissues. Our analysis uncovered notable variations in transcript abundance among different cancers. Remarkably, one transcript linked to nonsense-mediated mRNA decay (NMD) (ENST00000407441) showed marked upregulation in several tumor samples, which may result from NMD evasion, gene mutations, or irregular splicing. Furthermore, the dysregulation of several non-coding ACSL3 transcripts suggests that ACSL3 might influence tumor development and progression through non-coding RNA pathways (Fig. 1D).

Expression distribution of ACSL3 in single cells

Following our preliminary investigation of ACSL3 levels in different cancers, we observed that the gene is broadly dysregulated, with a tendency toward higher expression in many tumors. To better understand the cell-specific distribution of ACSL3 in these cancers, we examined single-cell transcriptomic datasets. The results showed that ACSL3 is widely expressed in various immune cells and malignant clusters; however, its expression patterns are specific to different cancer cells (Fig. 2). Notably, ACSL3 has high expression levels and proportions in macrophages across all cancers, suggesting that ACSL3 may influence tumor development through dysregulated expression in macrophages, potentially reflecting the lipid metabolism reprogramming needs of tumor-associated macrophages.

Prognostic and diagnostic value of ACSL3 in pan-cancer

We assessed the prognostic value of ACSL3 across a range of cancers by implementing univariate Cox regression and Kaplan–Meier survival methods. These analyses examined how ACSL3 expression relates to four key survival metrics: OS, DSS, DFI, and PFI. Our findings indicate that increased ACSL3 expression is significantly associated with reduced survival in cancers such as ACC, CESC, HNSC, KICH, LAML, LGG, LIHC, LUAD, and UVM, while it appears linked to better outcomes in KIRC, OV, THCA, THYM, UCEC, and UCS (Fig. 3A). To further explore the connection between ACSL3 and clinical characteristics, we evaluated its expression in relation to patient age, TNM staging, and overall tumor stage. The analysis revealed that ACSL3 expression correlates with specific clinical features in various cancer types; for example, its levels are notably elevated in advanced stages of LIHC, LUAD, and UCS, whereas lower levels are observed in KIRC and OV (Fig. 3B). In addition, ROC analysis based on ACSL3 expression demonstrated robust diagnostic capability in several cancers, particularly in LAML, PAAD, READ, and SKCM (Fig. 3C).

Gene set enrichment analysis

To examine the biological functions of ACSL3 in cancer progression, we divided cancer samples based on ACSL3 expression levels into high and low groups. We performed differential gene expression analysis and conducted gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA). The KEGG analysis revealed that several cancers were enriched in pathways such as calcium signaling, cAMP signaling, and ECM-receptor interaction, which are upregulated and associated with cancer. Conversely, pathways like primary immunodeficiency were downregulated across multiple cancers (Fig. 4A). In the Hallmark analysis, cell cycle-related pathways, including G2M checkpoint and E2F targets, were significantly activated in numerous cancers. The activation of genome stability pathways, such as mitotic spindle, may contribute to uncontrolled tumor cell growth. Other pathways related to cancer, such as KRAS signaling, interferon alpha response, and inflammatory response, were also enriched (Fig. 4B). Interestingly, certain cancers like TGCT exhibited notable heterogeneity, with enrichment patterns differing from other cancers.

Immunological characteristics of ACSL3 in the pan-cancer microenvironment

The progression of malignant tumors is closely linked to the immune microenvironment surrounding them. To understand how ACSL3 affects immune status in different cancers, we assessed the activity scores of the anti-cancer immune cycle. Our analysis revealed that ACSL3 expression is positively associated with cancer cell antigen release (Step 1), antigen presentation (Step 2), and T cell recognition of cancer cells (Step 6). Conversely, it is negatively associated with priming and activation (Step 3), immune cell trafficking to tumors (Step 4), and immune cell infiltration into tumors (Step 5) (Fig. 5A). The ESTIMATE analysis showed that ACSL3 has a negative correlation with immune and stromal scores in several cancers, indicating that ACSL3 might influence the tumor microenvironment by affecting immune cell infiltration or stromal components (Fig. 5B). Additionally, analysis of immune cell infiltration levels across cancers indicated that, apart from T helper cells, Tcm, Tem, and Th2 cells, most immune cells exhibit a negative correlation with ACSL3 expression (Fig. 5C).

B ESTIMATE analysis assessing the correlation between the tumor immune microenvironment and ACSL3. C ssGSEA analysis evaluating the relationship between tumor immune cell infiltration and ACSL3

Regulatory factors related to the immune system are essential in shaping the tumor microenvironment and affecting the success of cancer immunotherapy. Therefore, we also studied the association between ACSL3 and MHC molecules and immune checkpoints. The results showed significant associations, although the correlations varied among different cancers and molecules. Among MHC molecules, ACSL3 is positively correlated with TAPBP, TAP2, TAP1, and B2M in most cancers, while it is largely negatively correlated with other molecules (Fig. 6A). Immune checkpoints generally showed a positive correlation with ACSL3 across most cancers, while TMIGD2, TNFRSF14, TNFRSF18, and TNFRSF25 were positively correlated with ACSL3 (Fig. 6B). Overall, cancer patients with high ACSL3 expression exhibit inactive immune cell activity, presenting an immunosuppressive state.

Genetic alteration analysis of ACSL3 in pan-cancer

Genomic stability influences gene expression and tumor progression, and is linked to immunotherapy responses. Analyses of TMB and MSI showed a positive correlation between ACSL3 expression and TMB scores in cancers like SARC, PAAD, THYM, STAD, SKCM, GBM, UCEC, COAD, OV, LUSC, and LUAD. Similarly, ACSL3 expression positively correlates with MSI scores in cancers such as READ, MESO, UCEC, KIRC, COAD, SARC, LUSC, and STAD. In contrast, ACSL3 expression is negatively correlated with TMB scores in THCA, PRAD, KIRC, and KIRP, and with MSI scores in BRCA, THCA, PRAD, and DLBC (Fig. 7A and B). These results indicate that ACSL3 might be a predictive biomarker for cancer immunotherapy effectiveness in certain cancers.

To explore the mechanisms underlying ACSL3 dysregulation in pan-cancer, we analyzed the genetic alterations of ACSL3. We observed diverse types and frequencies of genetic alterations in ACSL3 across different cancers. Most cancers exhibited ACSL3 mutations, especially UCEC, while copy number amplification predominated in PAAD and UCS (Fig. 7C). ACSL3 expression showed a significant correlation with DNA methylation sites across various cancers, suggesting that DNA methylation influences ACSL3 regulation (Fig. 7D). Notably, compared to promoter regions, more methylation sites in gene body regions were associated with ACSL3 expression. Generally, promoter region methylation silences gene expression, while gene body methylation is sometimes linked to gene activation and transcription maintenance, although further research is needed to clarify these findings. Overall, these results highlight the complex regulatory mechanisms of ACSL3 expression.

RNA modifications, as key post-transcriptional regulatory mechanisms, play critical roles in gene expression regulation. Dysregulation of RNA modification enzymes has been shown to be closely associated with tumor initiation, progression, and metastasis. Our pan-cancer analysis revealed that ACSL3 expression levels were significantly positively correlated with various RNA modification enzymes, including those involved in m1A, m5C, and m6A modifications (Fig. 7E). This finding suggests that ACSL3 expression may be profoundly influenced by an aberrantly active RNA modification regulatory network.

Analysis of potential therapeutic drugs

To explore therapeutic agents targeting ACSL3-related tumor-promoting pathways, we analyzed the top 100 most significantly differentially expressed genes for each cancer type from a pan-cancer differential analysis. These genes underwent correlation analysis with gene expression data from the CMAP database. This approach identified the top 30 compounds with negative correlations, such as GW-8510, lanatoside_C, and 6-azathymine, which may potentially counteract cancer-associated gene expression patterns (Fig. 8A).

Exploration of Potential Therapeutic Agents. A Identified 30 compounds from the CMAP database showing negative correlation with ACSL3 expression. B Conducted drug sensitivity analysis using data from the CellMiner database

Additionally, we examined FDA-approved and clinically validated drugs from the CellMiner database. Six anti-tumor drugs were found to have expression sensitivity correlations with ACSL3. Notably, HYPOTHEMYCIN exhibited a positive correlation, indicating that patients with elevated ACSL3 expression might respond better to HYPOTHEMYCIN treatment (Fig. 8B).

Knockdown of ACSL3 inhibits proliferation and migration of liver cancer cells

To enhance the reliability of our research, we conducted experimental validation in liver cancer. First, we verified the significant upregulation of ACSL3 expression in HCC across 13 independent cohorts (Fig. 9A). We explored the expression levels of ACSL3 in various liver cancer cell lines using the Cancer Cell Line Encyclopedia (CCLE) database, and found that ACSL3 has higher expression levels in Huh-7 and HepG2 cell lines (Supplementary Table 2). Subsequently, we constructed ACSL3 knockdown cells in liver cancer cell lines Huh-7 and HepG2. The knockdown efficiency was validated using real-time quantitative PCR and Western blot (Fig. 9B and C). Based on the knockdown efficiency, we selected Huh-7 cells for further experiments. Colony formation assays showed that knockdown of ACSL3 reduced the number and size of colonies formed by Huh-7 cells, indicating weakened long-term proliferation ability (Fig. 9D). Transwell assays demonstrated that knockdown of ACSL3 decreased the migration and invasion capabilities of Huh-7 cells (Fig. 9E). CCK8 assays also indicated that knockdown of ACSL3 reduced the proliferation ability of Huh-7 cells (Fig. 9F). Wound healing assays confirmed that ACSL3 silencing significantly reduced the wound healing rate at designated time points, suggesting impaired migration (Fig. 9G). Finally, AV/PI staining analysis showed that inhibiting ACSL3 expression significantly promoted apoptosis in Huh-7 cells (Fig. 9H).

Discussion

The nutrient supply critically influences both tumor expansion and dissemination. Within the cancer microenvironment, a buildup of various lipids promotes tumor progression and metastatic behavior [20]. As an essential enzyme in lipid metabolism, ACSL3 transforms free long-chain fatty acids into corresponding fatty acyl-CoA esters. These activated esters are further metabolized into different lipid species and derivatives, supporting cellular nutrition, signaling pathways, and membrane biogenesis [21]. Moreover, ACSL3 is instrumental in the formation of lipid droplets in the tumor milieu. Acting as storage depots, these droplets accumulate neutral lipids—such as polyunsaturated fatty acids that are especially susceptible to peroxidation—thus allowing malignant cells to harness energy from lipid metabolism while concurrently mitigating oxidative damage due to peroxidation [20]. ACSL3 can also activate monounsaturated fatty acids and replace polyunsaturated fatty acids to alter cell membrane properties, granting cancer cells resistance to ferroptosis and radiotherapy [22, 23]. Currently, the role of ACSL3 in certain types of cancer has been reported, but some contradictory findings have emerged. For example, in breast cancer, ACSL3 can promote cancer cell proliferation by inhibiting ferroptosis [16], while also suppressing cancer cell proliferation through the YES1/YAP1 axis [15]. In kidney cancer [18], prostate cancer [17], and lung cancer [24], ACSL3 exhibits pro-cancer effects. These findings suggest that the biological functions of ACSL3 may have cancer-type specificity and complexity. Accordingly, our study assessed variations in ACSL3 expression across multiple cancer types, evaluated its epigenetic alterations, investigated immune microenvironment features, and explored its associations with genomic instability.

Analysis of pan-cancer expression datasets from TCGA, GTEx, and GEO indicates that ACSL3 exhibits elevated levels in 21 cancer types while showing reduced expression in four cancers, namely KIRC, KIRP, LAML, and TGCT. In addition, one NMD isoform of ACSL3 appears upregulated in most cancers, a change that may be related to evasion of the NMD process. The tumor microenvironment, particularly under hypoxic conditions, is known to dampen the NMD pathway, thereby modulating cellular stress responses and facilitating tumor progression [25]. Further insights from single-cell transcriptomic studies across 15 cancer types reveal that ACSL3 expression is cell-type specific within the tumor milieu. Notably, high levels of ACSL3 are predominantly observed in macrophages in most cancers, suggesting a role for ACSL3 in steering tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) toward a phenotype that supports tumor growth. Macrophages are key drivers of pro-tumor inflammatory responses, and TAMs promote tumor progression through multiple mechanisms, including inducing genomic instability, supporting tumor stem cell survival, creating a favorable microenvironment for metastasis, and suppressing protective adaptive immune responses [26]. TAMs can advance tumor progression through lipid metabolism reprogramming. Specifically, TAMs promote lipid droplet accumulation via lipid uptake, utilize fatty acid oxidation (FAO) to sustain their survival, and secrete immunosuppressive metabolites (e.g., arginase-1, which depletes arginine), thereby reducing T-cell proliferation and cytokine production [27, 28]. As an important lipid metabolism-related gene, ACSL3 may be involved in TAM lipid metabolism reprogramming. In conclusion, our analysis highlights the widespread dysregulation of ACSL3 across cancers, suggesting its involvement in molecular regulation across different cancer types through alternative splicing mechanisms and TAM lipid metabolism reprogramming.

We employed GSEA enrichment analysis to probe how ACSL3 might influence cancer development across different tumor types. Our findings show that several pathways—including Calcium signaling, cAMP signaling, ECM-receptor interactions, KRAS_SIGNALING, G2M_CHECKPOINT, E2F_TARGETS, and MITOTIC_SPINDLE—are generally activated in various cancers. KRAS, one of the most commonly mutated oncogenes in human cancers, binds GTP to control key pathways such as RAF/MEK/ERK, PI3K/AKT/mTOR, and RHOA, thereby continuously promoting cell proliferation, survival, and metabolic reprogramming [29]. In lung cancer, studies have indicated that mutant KRAS promotes the uptake, retention, and oxidation of fatty acids through an ACSL3-dependent mechanism, ultimately enhancing cell growth [24]. Conversely, our analysis identified that pathways such as Ribosome, INTERFERON_ALPHA_RESPONSE, and IL6_JAK_STAT3_SIGNALING are generally suppressed across cancers. IFNα, for instance, has been observed to inhibit tumor progression while boosting immune responses [30]. Moreover, there is a well-established link between ribosome biogenesis and tumor development; dysregulation of ribosome synthesis—evident by increased RNA polymerase I activity, rRNA overexpression, and surplus ribosomal protein production—leads to a higher number of ribosomes, thereby boosting protein synthesis and supporting the energy and biosynthetic needs for rapid tumor cell proliferation [31]. In summary, ACSL3 appears to participate widely in cancer-associated signaling pathways. However, in some cancers such as BRCA, PRAD, STAD, and TGCT, most of these pathways are downregulated. These context-dependent effects may be influenced by multiple underlying mechanisms. First, variations in the tumor microenvironment may alter fatty acid availability, immune cell infiltration profiles, and cytokine expression patterns, thereby modifying the metabolic dependencies of tumor cells [32]. Second, co-expression or differential expression of other lipid metabolism-related enzymes and regulatory genes—such as ACSL1, CPT1A, and SCD1—could lead to functional compensation or pathway rewiring, consequently influencing ACSL3’s net effect on tumor behavior [33].

Our analysis of immune cell infiltration revealed that elevated ACSL3 levels are linked to reduced presence of CD8 + T cells, cytotoxic cells, NK cells, and dendritic cells (DCs) in many cancers, whereas higher expression of ACSL3 corresponds with increased levels of T helper cells, T central memory cells (Tcm), and Th2 cells. Previous studies have indicated that crucial tumor-eliminating cells like CD8 + T cells and NK cells demonstrate diminished tumor infiltration, which contributes to the immunosuppressive environment created by cancer cells [34]. In contrast, Th2 cells are recognized for their role in fostering tumor progression and mediating immune suppression [35]. Moreover, we detected marked correlations between ACSL3 expression and various genes that regulate immune responses, including those associated with the major histocompatibility complex (MHC) and immune checkpoint pathways. MHC encodes a series of proteins responsible for initiating cellular immune responses and serves as the core molecule for antigen presentation and immune response activation, potentially enhancing the efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors [36]. Immune checkpoints, which exert immunosuppressive effects, can be exploited by tumor cells to evade immune destruction and have become major targets for the development of immunotherapy-related drugs [37]. In summary, ACSL3 exhibits extensive associations within the pan-cancer immune microenvironment, showing complex and diverse regulatory relationships across different immune cells and cancer types. These associations are closely linked to the dynamic changes in the immune microenvironment during tumor progression. For instance, in cancers such as KIRC, KIRP, LAML, and PCPG, ACSL3 expression was positively correlated with most MHC molecules as well as immune checkpoints, consistent with the findings from the cancer immunity cycle analysis.

Genetic alterations play a critical role in the initiation, progression, and treatment of cancer. Cancer development often arises from genetic alterations in key genes within the genome, such as mutations, rearrangements, amplifications, or deletions, leading to uncontrolled cell growth, abnormal proliferation, and evasion of normal biological regulation [38, 39]. In our pan-cancer analysis, we observed the types and frequencies of ACSL3 genetic alterations, such as mutations being predominant in UCEC, while copy number amplifications were more common in UCS. ACSL3 was also found to be correlated with TMB and MSI. Both TMB and MSI have emerged as important indicators for predicting responses to immunotherapy, with higher scores generally associated with better immune responses [40]. Epigenetic regulation is widespread in cancer, with RNA modifications and DNA methylation being common types. These modifications influence gene expression and cellular functions, thereby contributing to tumor initiation and progression, and they hold promise as potential targets for cancer therapy [41, 42]. Notably, ACSL3 exhibited significant positive correlations with most RNA modification enzymes across all cancers, including those associated with m1A, m5C, and m6A. This suggests that ACSL3 expression may be profoundly influenced by an aberrantly active RNA modification regulatory network.

In summary, our findings suggest that ACSL3 exhibits abnormal regulation in most cancer tissues, most often with elevated expression, and is linked to factors such as prognosis, diagnosis, the tumor immune microenvironment, and epigenetic regulation. Both in vivo and in vitro experiments supported the cancer-promoting function of ACSL3 in hepatocellular carcinoma. However, our study has several limitations. First, it is largely based on data from various public databases and statistical analyses, which can be affected by heterogeneity. Therefore, the bioinformatics results should be interpreted with caution. For instance, although some correlations were statistically significant, the correlation coefficients were relatively low. Second, experimental validation was only performed in liver cancer, and specific mechanistic pathways were not explored. In the future, we aim to conduct more in-depth studies.
