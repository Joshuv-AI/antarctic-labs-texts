A Diagnostic Study Using STARD Platform on Liquid-Based Cytology in Cervical Smear and Its Positivity Rate among Females with Abnormal Vaginal Conditions Attending a Tertiary Care Center
Authors: Mohd Afzal, Abhishek Sharma, Vikas Tiwari, Jaishree Karnwal Tiwari
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Liquid-based cytology (LBC) is a newer method of preparing cervical cell samples. This technique involves collecting cells in a liquid fixative and preparing and evaluating them.Aim:This study aims to investigate cervical smears prepared using the Ezi-Prep LBC method and analyze the positivity rate for cervical cancer and assess the diagnostic accuracy of LBC in detecting cervical abnormalities among females with abnormal vaginal conditions attending a tertiary care center.Materials and Methods:The present diagnostic study was performed in a tertiary care hospital over a period of 1 year (April 1, 2021–November 30, 2021). A total of 230 women, aged 21–70 years, presented at the gynecology outpatient department for either routine cervical screening or with complaints of vaginal bleeding, discharge, or pelvic pain. Cervical smears were prepared using the Ezi-Prep method and stained with ULTRA-PAP stain.Results:LBC smears are monolayer smears and are without nuclear overlapping with spread over a diameter of 16 mm. Minimum cellular overlapping and crowding were observed in LBC smears. The mean age of the participants was 35.5 ± 5.1 years. The smears were studied by using nine morphological parameters.Conclusion:The majority of the smears were inflammatory smears. A total of 16 cases and three cases were found to be bacterial vaginosis and candidiasis, respectively. In an 8-month study, HSIL was found in two cases, whereas LSIL was found in only one case. The positivity rate was observed to be 0.9% in HSIL cases and 0.4% in LSIL cases, 0.9% ASC-US case, and 0.4% invasive carcinoma case.

INTRODUCTION

Cervical cancer causes the death of approximately 1 million women worldwide every year.[1] Breast cancer is the leading malignancy affecting women, while cervical cancer ranks as the second most common malignancy.[2] Early diagnosis and treatment of cervical cancer can reduce mortality rates by up to 80% and lower the incidence of morbidity by as much as 70%.[3] Cervical cancer is a preventable disease, despite being the most common cancer affecting women.[4] Timely screening and detection of precancerous lesions can prevent the spread of cervical cancer throughout the body.[5] Due to the long preinvasive stage and the availability of several screening techniques, early detection of cervical cancer is possible.[6] As cervical cancer develops slowly on the cervix, various screening procedures have the potential to effectively prevent its development. The high mortality rate of cervical cancer is a significant public health problem.[7]

Cervical cancer affects the cell lining of the cervix and can lead to cervical diseases. The cell lining of the cervix is located in the lower part of the uterus. The cervix is the connection point between the upper part of the uterus, where a fetus grows, and the birth canal, or vagina.[8] The cervix is a narrow, cylindrical structure that forms the lower part of the uterus and protrudes into the vagina, creating a passageway between the uterus and the outside of the body.[9] The cervix has two parts: the endocervix (opening to the uterus with glandular cells) and the exocervix (outer part visible during a speculum exam with squamous cells).[10] These cells meet in the transformation zone, which moves with age and childbirth. Cervical cancer often develops in cells in the transformation zone.[8,9] Cervical cancers are classified by their appearance under a microscope, with squamous cell carcinoma and adenocarcinoma being the main types. Squamous cell carcinomas (90% of cases) develop from cells in the exocervix, usually in the transformation zone. Adenocarcinomas develop from glandular cells in the endocervix. Adenosquamous carcinomas, characterized by features of both types, are less common.[11] Bacterial vaginosis is a frequent vaginal infection that results in discharge, odor, and discomfort. The diagnostic criteria for bacterial vaginosis are straight-forward, and Pap screening tests are affordable. Since most bacterial vaginosis patients are unaware of the illness, a Pap smear analysis of vaginal discharge is necessary for diagnosis.[12]

The Pap test, also known as cervicovaginal cytology or the Papanicolaou smear, has been the primary screening tool for cervical cancer for more than six decades. In addition to its effectiveness in detecting cervical cancer, it has also been used as an incentive for women to undergo annual physical exams.[13] It is recommended that individuals between the ages of 21 and 70 years undergo Pap smear screening every 3 years. If an abnormal Pap smear is found, a repeat test may be necessary in 6–12 months.[14] Liquid-based cytology (LBC) is a newer method of preparing cervical cell samples. Samples are collected using a cytobrush, rinsed in preservative fluid, and transported to the lab. There, the cells are dispersed, the debris is removed, and a thin layer of cells is deposited on a microscope slide and stained. The diagnostic criteria and categories are the same as for the conventional Pap smear.[15] LBC allows for cells to be suspended in a single layer, improving the accuracy of morphological assessment.[20,22] This technique involves collecting cells in a liquid fixative and preparing and evaluating them. Developed countries are adopting LBC for increasing the sensitivity of the Pap test.[16]

This study aims to evaluate the diagnostic precision of LBC in the detection of cervical abnormalities in females presenting with abnormal vaginal conditions at a tertiary care center. Furthermore, it also aims to ascertain the effectiveness of LBC in identifying cervical abnormalities within the specific study population.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Ethical considerations

The present diagnostic cross-sectional study was performed in a tertiary care hospital over a period of 1 year (April 1, 2021–November 30, 2021). A total of 230 women in the age group of 21–70 years presenting to the gynecology outpatient department for either routine cervical screening or with complaints of vaginal bleeding, discharge, or pelvic pain were enrolled in the study. Ethical approval was taken from AMCH-IEC (Institutional Ethical committee) with Ref. no. of AMCH-IEC/21-91. Sample size was calculated as follows: Population: N = zα2 × P (1 - P)/E2 where zα = 1.96, P = 95% and E = 0.05, N is the population size.

Selection criteria

Inclusion criteria

Individuals with satisfactory LBC smears, ensuring the quality and reliability of the collected samples, were enrolled. The age range for inclusion spanned from 20 to 70 years, encompassing mostly adult women. Additionally, adequate number of samples had to be collected during the screening process, contributing to the robustness of the data obtained.

Exclusion criteria

Unsatisfactory LBC smears, which might compromise the accuracy of the cytological assessment, led to the exclusion of individuals failing to meet this criterion. Unmarried women, pregnant women, and individuals below the age of 20 or above the age of 70 were also excluded from the study, ensuring a more focused and relevant participant group. Furthermore, individuals who either refused or were unable to provide informed consent were not included, emphasizing the importance of ethical considerations and participant autonomy in the research process. These inclusion and exclusion criteria collectively aimed to ensure the integrity of the study’s data and the appropriateness of the participant cohort for obtaining the research objectives.

Procedure

This study conducted cervical smear analysis using the Ezi-Prep method. Before collecting samples, patients were required to provide informed consent. The samples were collected from females who were attending the gynecology department, with a total of 230 samples collected in the outpatient department.

Data analysis

The data are analyzed using descriptive statistics such as frequencies and percentages. An identity number was assigned to each sample. The findings were manually entered into the database. The current study was analyzed using IBM SPSS Version 20.0.

RESULTS

In the present study, a total of 230 LBC smears were analyzed. All the smears were prepared by the Ezi-Prep method. LBC smears are monolayer smears and without nuclear overlapping with spread over a diameter of 16 mm; minimum cellular overlapping and crowding were observed in LBC smears. The mean age of the participants was 38.5 years. The smears were studied by using nine morphological parameters. DISCUSSION

Pap smear is worldwide accepted tool for early detection of precancerous lesions of the cervix. LBC is a very useful, easy, and safe tool for detecting precancerous cervical epithelial lesions. Minimal screening of cervical smears is carried out in India due to lack of proper knowledge.[16]

The LBC test is a routine cancer screening method that should be done every 3 years, and a PAP smear with an HPV DNA test is recommended as a screening method every 5 years.[15]

In the present study, most of the inflammatory smears were detected in the 31–40 year age group and compared with other studies.

Inflammatory smears were found in 52.1% of screened women, which is similar to the results of other studies.[16,18,19] Inflammatory smears were predominant in comparison with other studies.[16,18,19] A study was conducted in Arogyam Medical College, Roorkee, and many of the patients underwent LBC tests as a part of the routine test and many preferred by gynecologist.

In the present study, bacterial vaginosis was identified in 6.9% of the total cases. When compared to the findings of Lakshmi and Gouri,[16] where it was reported at 3%, and Phani Meghna et al.,[19] where it was found in 3.13% of the smears, our study exhibited a higher prevalence. The predominant infection was bacterial vaginosis, which is similar to earlier references.[16,17,18,19] Normal cervical smears show rod-shaped lactobacilli. In bacterial vaginosis, clue cells were found, which were squamous cells covered by a layer of Coccobacillus obscuring the cellular outlines [Figure 3]. There is an associated absence of lactobacilli and scant inflammatory infiltrate.[12] Moderate-to-severe inflammatory infiltration should exclude the diagnosis of bacterial vaginosis. Bacterial vaginosis is associated with pelvic inflammatory disease, preterm birth, postoperative gynecological infections and abnormal cervical cytology.[16] Trichomonas was not detected in our studied smears. Candidiasis was found in 1.9% cases in the present study, which is similar to an earlier study by R.K Sherwani et al. 7 (4.3%).[21]

In the current investigation, the prevalence of LSIL was observed in only 0.4% of cases. This finding contrasts with the results reported by Sachan et al.,[18] who identified LSIL in 5.1% of smears, and Lakshmi and Gouri,[16] where the detection rate was higher at 7.5% of smears. Notably, LSIL smears in our study exhibited distinct characteristics, including squamous cells displaying an abundance of cytoplasm, nuclear expansion exceeding three times the typical area of intermediate nuclei, anisonucleosis, and discrete nucleoli, as illustrated in [Figure 5]. HSIL was found in 0.9% of cases, which differs with the findings of Sachan et al.,[18] who found it in 0.5% of smears, and Lakshmi and Gouri,[16] who found it in 6% of smears. In our investigation, HSIL smears showed smaller dysplastic cells with a high nucleus-to-cytoplasm (N/C) ratio, nuclear hyperchromatism, and irregular nuclear outlines [Figure 8].

ASC-US were identified in 0.9% of cases, revealing a disparity from the observations of Sachan et al.,[18] where ASC-US was detected in 2.9% of smears, and Lakshmi and Gouri,[16] where the prevalence was reported at 2.5%. The characteristic features of ASC-US include squamous differentiation, an elevated Nuclear-to-Cytoplasmic (N/C) ratio, and minimal nuclear changes, encompassing hyperchromasia, chromatin clumping, irregularity, smudging, and occasional multinucleation. The presence of abnormal appearing nuclei is pivotal for ASC interpretation, as depicted in [Figure 7].

Regarding invasive carcinoma, the present study found a rate of 0.4%, contrasting with the absence of such cases in the study by Sachan et al.[18] and a 1% detection rate reported by Lakshmi and Gouri[16] On the other hand, the identification of NILM/NORMAL in 21.6% of smears in our study differed significantly from the higher prevalence reported by Sachanet al.[18] (48.8%) and the lower rate observed by Lakshmi and Gouri[16] (4%). Considering that cervical cancer typically develops in women aged 40–50 years, with precursor lesions appearing 5–10 years earlier, it is advisable for women to undergo at least one Pap smear test before reaching the age of 45. This proactive measure is crucial for early detection and effective management of cervical abnormalities.

Limitations

The study has several limitations that warrant consideration. The reliance solely on conventional smears presents a notable gap in the diagnostic approach. The omission of alternative methods may have limited the comprehensive assessment of infectious organisms, as conventional smears might not have captured certain nuances or provided a complete diagnostic picture. Additionally, the removal of mucus during processing could have potentially hindered the effective diagnosis of infectious agents, leading to an underrepresentation of these organisms in the findings. The relative small sample size may not adequately reflect the observations in the broader population.

Future recommendations

Researchers should consider incorporating a more comprehensive array of diagnostic techniques beyond conventional smears. Utilizing advanced technologies or alternative methods could provide a more nuanced understanding of infectious organisms, contributing to a more accurate and holistic diagnosis. In terms of sample size, future studies should strive for larger and more diverse participant pools to improve the external validity of findings. This approach would allow for a more robust exploration of the research questions across varied demographic groups, yielding results that are more generalizable to those of the broader population.

CONCLUSION

The majority of the smears suggested inflammation, and 16 cases and 3 cases were found of bacterial vaginosis and candidiasis, respectively. Positivity of activity in the center inflammatory smears most common. In the 8-month study, HSIL was found in two cases and LSIL found only one case. The positivity rate was observed to be 0.9% in HSIL cases and 0.4% in LSIL cases, 0.9% ASC-US case, and 0.4% in invasive carcinoma case. In the present study, HSIL smears were of the 41–50 year age group, LSIL 61–70 years age group, and LBC smear testing is a very useful, simple, economical, and safe tool for detecting precancerous cervical epithelial lesions. It is a routine screening procedure to reduce the treatment burden, morbidity, and mortality. Every woman above the age of 30 years should undergo routine cervical cancer screening, even into the postmenopausal period.
