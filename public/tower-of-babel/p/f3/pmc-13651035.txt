The role of social media in career choices of first-year dental students: a cross-sectional study on digital homogeneity
Authors: Hatice Zehra Bodur Güney, Musa Acartürk
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe aim of this study is to evaluate the role of social media in the career choices of first-year dental students in Turkey and to examine the relationship between this effect and demographic characteristics and social media usage habits.MethodsThis descriptive cross-sectional study included first-year dental students enrolled in different dental faculties in Turkey during the 2025–2026 academic year. Data were collected through a two-part online questionnaire developed in line with the literature, with internal consistency assessed as a preliminary step (α = 0.84); formal psychometric validation was not conducted. The questionnaire assessed demographic characteristics, social media usage habits, exposure to dentistry-related content, and factors influencing career choice. Descriptive statistics and Chi-square test were used to analyze the data, and the significance level was set at p < 0.05.Results64.9% of the study sample reported being influenced by social media content during the career decision-making process; this figure reflects the study sample only and should not be interpreted as a population prevalence estimate. No statistically significant associations were found between social media influence and gender, type of high school graduated from, or year of university admission (p > 0.05). Among students influenced by social media, 47.9% reported using social media for 3 h or more per day, and TikTok was the most frequently used platform (20.3%). Animations were the most attention-grabbing content type (22.9%), while social prestige (22.9%) was the most common expectation associated with social media. Across all participants, the most frequently reported reasons for choosing dentistry were guidance counselor recommendations (15.5%) and the prestige of the profession (14.2%).ConclusionsThe findings indicate that a considerable proportion of first-year dental students in the study sample reported being influenced by social media during their career decision-making, and this reported influence was not significantly associated with demographic variables. Idealized professional representations on social media, particularly those emphasizing prestige and lifestyle expectations, may shape students’ perceptions of the profession. These findings highlight the need for dental faculties to develop realistic and balanced digital content strategies, and for guidance services to incorporate media literacy training, so that prospective students can critically evaluate social media content during the career choice period.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12909-026-09927-9.

Introduction

Career choice is a multidimensional decision-making process that shapes not only an individual’s economic future but also life satisfaction, social status, and psychological well-being. Health science professions are widely perceived as attractive career options due to the professional autonomy, social prestige, and employment opportunities they offer. Among these professions, dentistry is considered a prominent discipline characterized by its strong clinical orientation, opportunities for independent practice, and a professional profile often associated with aesthetic outcomes [1, 2].

In Turkey, dental faculties require a high ranking in the national university entrance examination and are consistently among the most competitive programs [1, 2]. Therefore, the information sources that shape prospective students’ perceptions and expectations about the dental profession may play an important role in their adaptation to dental education, motivation levels, and long-term professional satisfaction [3].

In recent years, the career decision-making process has undergone substantial changes with the expansion of digital technologies. For younger generations often described as digital natives, social media platforms have become a major source of information during career exploration and university selection [4]. Studies show that dental students frequently use social media not only for social interaction but also to access professional and educational content related to dentistry [5, 6]. Prospective students increasingly rely on vlogs, daily life videos, and clinical content shared on platforms such as Instagram, YouTube, and TikTok to gain insight into professional lifestyles, working conditions, and perceived prestige [7, 8]. However, the selective and visually oriented nature of such content may present idealized representations of professional life, which may shape expectations in ways that do not fully reflect the realities of the profession [3, 8, 9].

George Gerbner’s Cultivation Theory suggests that repeated exposure to mass media can shape individuals’ perceptions of reality over time [10]. Within this framework, portraying the dental profession on social media as associated with aesthetic achievements, high earnings, and a prestigious lifestyle may create an idealized professional image among prospective students [11]. Studies in the field of health sciences also indicate that social media can influence career choice and institutional perception [8, 9, 12]. In this study, Cultivation Theory serves as a theoretical lens to interpret the findings rather than as a framework being directly tested.

The increasing visibility of health professions on social media platforms suggests that these platforms may influence how prospective students perceive professional identities [13]. As digitalization expands, individuals from different socio-demographic backgrounds are increasingly exposed to similar online content, which may contribute to comparable professional perceptions. This phenomenon has been described as the homogenizing effect of digital platforms, referred to as “digital homogeneity”. A multicenter study involving 1577 dental students from nine countries reported notable similarities in social media representations of career motivations despite different cultural and economic contexts [14].

Although social media offers new opportunities for accessing professional information, it may also introduce certain risks in the career decision-making process. Because most online content is not subjected to scientific review, prospective students’ perceptions may be influenced by incomplete or selective information [15–17]. This issue has been described as the “signal-to-noise ratio”, where viral or misleading content may overshadow reliable information [17]. Additionally, constant exposure to others’ achievements and lifestyles may contribute to social comparison processes that could influence how young individuals perceive career expectations [4, 18].

Although previous studies have examined socioeconomic and academic factors influencing dental students’ career choices in Turkey, empirical research specifically investigating the role of social media in shaping career perceptions—particularly among first-year dental students and in relation to demographic characteristics and social media usage habits—remains limited [1, 11]. In recent years, access to reliable educational information and career guidance has also been discussed within the framework of the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (SDG), particularly Goal 4 (Quality Education). In the digital era, social media platforms function as informal learning environments that may influence young individuals’ professional perceptions and expectations. Therefore, examining the potential role of social media in career choice is important not only for understanding individual decision-making processes but also for supporting informed educational choices. These theoretical perspectives are used in this study primarily as conceptual frameworks to interpret potential patterns in students’ perceptions rather than as variables directly tested within the empirical analysis.

The aim of this study is to evaluate the role of social media in the career choices of first-year dental students in Turkey and to analyze the relationship between this influence and demographic characteristics as well as social media usage habits. In this context, the H₀ hypothesis of the study is that social media does not have an independent effect on the career choices of first-year dental students in Turkey when demographic characteristics and social media usage habits are considered.

Methods

Study design and ethical approval

This study was planned as a descriptive and cross-sectional research. The research was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Helsinki Declaration, and the necessary ethical approval for the implementation of the study was obtained from the Scientific Research Ethics Committee of Giresun University Giresun Training and Research Hospital (dated December 24, 2025, decision no. 16). Informed consent was obtained online from all participants in the study.

Study population and sample size

The study’s sample consisted of first-year dental students enrolled in dental faculties across Turkey during the 2025–2026 academic year. Due to the convenience sampling nature of the data collection process, the exact number of participating institutions and their geographic distribution could not be determined. Data collection was conducted between January 1 and 31, 2026, following ethical committee approval.

The sample size was calculated using G* Power 3 software [19]. In the power analysis, a 95% confidence level (α = 0.05), 90% power (1 − β) and moderate effect size (Cohen’s w = 0.30) [19] were used, and it was determined that a minimum of 400 participants should be reached. The targeted statistical power was achieved with the recruited sample.

Inclusion criteria for the study were: being a first-year dental student, voluntarily agreeing to participate in the study, and providing informed consent. Questionnaires with incomplete responses and responses from students outside the first year were excluded from the study.

Sampling method and data collection process

Data collection was based on convenience sampling, prioritizing accessibility and voluntary participation. An online survey form created using the Google Forms platform was used as the data collection tool. The survey link was shared through digital communication networks (WhatsApp groups) and social media communities of first-year dental students.

The survey form was designed with a mandatory answer option to ensure participants answered all questions completely. During the data collection process, technical restrictions were implemented within the Google Forms infrastructure to prevent multiple responses from the same participant (only one email address allowed). The responses of participants who provided informed consent digitally and completed the survey were included in the analysis. Due to the nature of convenience sampling and distribution through digital student groups, the exact number of students who received the survey link could not be determined; therefore, a response rate could not be calculated.

Data collection instrument

Data was collected using a questionnaire consisting of two sections and 10 items, developed through a literature review. The first section of the questionnaire included questions about participants’ demographic characteristics (gender, type of high school graduated from, year of university admission) and whether social media influenced their career choices. Participants who answered “Yes” to the relevant question in this section were directed to the next question, while those who answered “No” were directed to the final question of the questionnaire (Question 10), which asked about the primary factors influencing their decision to choose dentistry. Social media influence was operationalized as a single-item binary variable based on participants’ responses to the question ‘Did social media content influence your decision to choose dentistry during the university application period?’ (Yes/No). While this approach enabled a straightforward classification of participants, a single binary item may not fully reflect the complexity of social media influence.

The second part of the survey investigated students’ social media usage times during the university application period, the social media platform they most actively used during the university application period, the frequency of their exposure to dentistry-related content, the types of content that caught their attention, their expectations regarding the profession, and the factors that influenced their decision to choose dentistry as a profession.

Questionnaire development and internal consistency

The survey instrument was developed based on previously published literature addressing career choice and social media use among dental students. Face validity was ensured through internal review by the research team, during which the clarity, wording, and relevance of the questionnaire items were evaluated. Formal content validity indices (CVI/CVR) were not calculated, as an external expert panel was not convened. Internal consistency was assessed only as a preliminary descriptive step using Cronbach’s alpha (α = 0.84). However, because the questionnaire included heterogeneous item formats and no formal factor structure was established, this coefficient was not interpreted as evidence of psychometric validation or construct reliability.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis of the obtained data was performed using the SPSS 26.0 software package. Descriptive statistical methods were used in the evaluation of the data. The Chi-Square test was used to examine the relationships between categorical variables, and the statistical significance level was accepted as p < 0.05 in all analyses.

When examining the impact of social media on the decision to choose dentistry as a field of study, which was the main question of the study, it was found that 64.9% (n = 315) of the participants reported being influenced by social media content during the career decision-making process However, given that the survey was distributed primarily through digital platforms and social media networks, this figure reflects the study sample only and should not be interpreted as a population prevalence estimate. The remaining 35.1% (n = 170), who reported no social media influence, were directed only to the final survey question regarding their primary reasons for choosing dentistry.

Social media influence was independent of gender (χ²=0.384; p = 0.536), type of high school (χ²=1.394; p = 0.707), and year of university admission (χ²=1.248; p = 0.742) (p > 0.05). 63.9% of female students and 67.4% of male students were influenced by social media.

The usage habits of 315 students affected by social media are presented in Table 2. It was determined that 47.9% of the students used social media for 3 h or more per day. The most frequently reported platform during the university application period was TikTok (20.3%). 41.6% of the students who stated they were affected by social media reported that they were rarely or never exposed to specific dentistry content. The most notable type of content was animations (22.9%), while social prestige (22.9%) was the most common expectation stemming from social media. No statistically significant relationship was found between the time spent on social media and the type of expectation (χ²=11.220; p = 0.510).

Discussion

This study examines the role of social media in the career choices of first-year dental students. The study findings show that approximately two-thirds of the participants were influenced by social media content during their career decision-making process. This relatively high reported rate suggests that university and career decisions are increasingly associated with digital platforms, and that social media may play an influential role in students’ perceptions of the profession. Similar studies in the literature on medical and health sciences students have also reported high rates of influence of social media on career choice [8, 12, 13]. In this study, Chi-Square tests were used to examine whether social media influence was associated with demographic variables.

In recent years, increased use of digital media, particularly during the COVID-19 pandemic, has transformed students’ access to information and decision-making processes. Uma et al. [20] reported that social media use among dental students increased significantly during the pandemic, reaching up to 11 h per day. The findings of the present study suggest that these intensified digital habits may have continued after the pandemic and that social media may play a central role in career decision-making processes.

The findings suggest that social media may function as an important informal information source during the career decision-making process. Previous studies have reported that Generation Z students frequently rely on user-experience-based digital content rather than traditional guidance resources when exploring career options [21]. Consistent with earlier findings, visually oriented content such as vlogs and animations appears to attract considerable attention among prospective students [8, 9]. Interestingly, 41.6% of students who reported being influenced by social media also indicated rare or no exposure to dentistry-specific content. This finding may indicate that professional perceptions are shaped not only through direct profession-related information but also through indirect exposure to lifestyle-oriented content shared on digital platforms.

Our study hypothesized that the influence of social media would be independent of demographic variables, and the findings were consistent with this hypothesis. Traditional factors in career choice, such as gender, type of high school, and year of university admission, did not alter the level of social media influence. Chi-Square analyses revealed no significant relationships between demographic variables and social media influence. These findings may be interpreted in light of the concept of digital homogeneity; however, the absence of statistically significant differences should not be interpreted as direct evidence of homogeneous influence across all groups. A multicenter study conducted in nine countries by Riad et al. [14] similarly reported the strong influence of digital platforms on students’ career motivations. However, it should also be considered that this homogeneity may carry certain educational risks, as algorithm-driven popular content may dominate decision-making processes rather than individual interests, abilities, and values.

Another noteworthy finding of our study is the change in career choice motivations among dental students in Turkey over time. A 2018 study reported that family expectations, job security, and economic factors played a determining role in dental students’ career choices [1]. Similarly, a 2022 study reported that external motivations such as financial security, independence, and professional prestige were prominent [2]. In contrast, in our study the most dominant expectation among students influenced by social media was social respect and prestige (22.9%). This shift may reflect a transition from necessity-driven motivations to lifestyle-oriented expectations. This trend is consistent with Gerbner’s Cultivation Theory [10], which suggests that repeated media exposure may shape idealized perceptions of professions over time.

The finding that TikTok, Facebook, and YouTube were the most frequently used platforms suggests that students prefer short, visually oriented digital content. TikTok’s prominence is particularly noteworthy given its algorithm-driven and rapidly consumed content structure. However, the relatively even distribution across platforms is noteworthy. The near-uniform distribution observed not only across platforms but also across content types and expectation categories may itself reflect the homogenizing effect of social media exposure, whereby students’ preferences and perceptions converge regardless of the specific platform or content type encountered. However, the large volume of content on these platforms may also create a “signal-to-noise ratio” problem. Studies examining social media use among dental students indicate that although these platforms play an important role in information acquisition, the prevalence of non–peer-reviewed content may increase the risk of misleading information [5, 6, 20]. Students’ interest in animation and treatment videos may reflect their desire to understand the technical aspects of dentistry visually; however, such content often highlights successful cases while overlooking complications and clinical challenges.

The finding that 70.3% of the participants were female reflects the current gender distribution in dental faculties in Turkey. Previous studies also report that female students now outnumber male students in dental education and that this trend is increasing [22–24]. This shift indicates a change in the gender perception of the dental profession. The dominance of female dentists in social media content and the emphasis on aesthetically focused treatments may further reinforce this gender pattern [15]. These observations suggest that social media may influence not only career choices but also the gender profile of the profession.

The relatively balanced distribution of reasons for choosing dentistry suggests that career decisions are the result of a multifactorial process. Among the reported reasons, guidance counselor advice, professional prestige, and the desire to help were among the more frequently cited factors, consistent with previous studies [2, 25–27]. However, the relatively high proportion of responses in the ‘Other’ category (14.0%) suggests that the predefined response options may not have fully captured the range of reasons influencing students’ career decisions. While external motivations such as professional prestige were cited somewhat more often than intrinsic motivations such as the desire to help, the overall distribution remained relatively even across categories [23].

This study offers several important contributions to the literature. First, it draws on Gerbner’s Cultivation Theory as an interpretive lens to discuss how social media may shape career perceptions among dental students [10]. Second, by addressing concepts such as FOMO and the signal-to-noise ratio in the context of career choice, the study suggests that social media may function not only as an information source but also as a mechanism that may influence expectations and perceptions [18]. When considered alongside multinational studies, these findings suggest that social media may be transforming the way health professions are perceived and preferred globally [14].

The findings of this study have important implications for dental education and career guidance policies. Faculties should consider sharing high-quality and balanced content on their official websites and social media accounts to present a more realistic picture of the profession [6, 20]. In addition, high school guidance services may benefit from incorporating media literacy training to help students critically evaluate information obtained from social media [28]. Dental schools could also enhance existing orientation programs within their first-year curricula to better explain the realities of the profession and support students in adjusting their expectations [26, 29]. Finally, raising awareness about the ethical responsibilities of dentists and social media influencers may help reduce misleading or exaggerated professional representations online [15, 30].

These findings can also be interpreted within the broader framework of Sustainable Development Goal 4 (Quality Education), which emphasizes equitable access to reliable and high-quality educational information. In the digital era, the reliability and balance of information sources may influence students’ career perceptions and expectations. Idealized representations of professions on social media may shape expectations within an unrealistic framework and potentially undermine informed career decisions. Therefore, improving the accuracy and balance of digital career-related content should be considered an important component of contemporary educational policies.

This study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design does not allow causal interpretation, and the use of convenience sampling with voluntary participation may limit the representativeness and generalizability of the findings. Second, the use of self-reported data may introduce recall bias and social desirability bias. Third, because only first-year students were included, changes in social media-related expectations throughout the educational process could not be assessed. Furthermore, recruiting participants through social media platforms to study social media influence may have introduced self-selection bias toward digitally active students, potentially inflating the reported rate of social media influence; therefore, the 64.9% figure should be interpreted with caution. Formal content validity indices (CVI/CVR) were not calculated for the questionnaire. Moreover, a formal response rate could not be determined because the survey link was distributed through multiple student networks, and the exact number of participating dental schools could not be identified due to the convenience sampling nature of the data collection process. Future research should consider longitudinal designs, qualitative approaches, and multicenter studies including students from different academic years. In addition, since the questionnaire was developed specifically for this study, formal psychometric validation in larger and multicentre cohorts is needed. Such validation should include procedures such as exploratory factor analysis and confirmatory factor analysis to support the instrument’s broader use in comparative dental education research.

Conclusions

This cross-sectional study indicates that a substantial proportion of first-year dental students in Turkey reported being influenced by social media during the career decision-making process, although no significant associations were observed with the demographic variables examined. The absence of statistically significant associations may indicate that the reported influence of social media was relatively comparable across the demographic groups examined; however, these findings should be interpreted cautiously given the study design and sampling limitations. Among those influenced, animations and vlogs were the most attention-grabbing content types, and social prestige/status emerged as a prominent expectation. These findings suggest that idealized professional portrayals on digital platforms may shape perceptions of dentistry and contribute to unrealistic expectations. Given that prospective students increasingly rely on user-experience-based digital content rather than traditional guidance services, dental faculties and educational institutions should promote realistic and evidence-informed professional information through their official digital channels. In addition, guidance services should incorporate media literacy training to help students critically evaluate social media content during career decision-making.
