Characterization of defective coffee beans and blends differentiation based on 1H qNMR technique
Authors: Gui-Lin Hu, Chen-Xi Quan, Hao-Peng Dai, Ming-Hua Qiu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Defective coffee beans (DCB) are one of the main reasons for poor coffee quality. In the current research, chemical difference of three common DCB including sour beans (SCB), black beans (BCB), and mold beans (MCB) were clarified using 1H qNMR method and compared with that of non-defective beans (NDCB). The results indicated that DCB has lower sugar and lipid content compared to NDCB, yet it boasts a higher acetate concentration. The 1H NMR from water-soluble content was shown to be more effective than that of oil fraction for qualitative of DCB blends, regardless of whether partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) or machine learning (ML) algorithms were used. Support vector machine (SVM) was proved to be excellent for distinguishing DCB blends. Finally, a partial least squares regression (PLS) model was built for quantitative analysis of DCB blends. In summary, current research will not only help to reveal the material basis of DCB and their impact on coffee flavor, but also provide feasible strategies for the identification of DCB.

Introduction

Defective coffee beans (DCB) primarily include (sour beans) SCB, (balck beans) BCB, (mold beans) MCB, immature beans, round beans, shell beans and broken beans, etc., which exist in a high proportion of unscreened coffee beans (Silva et al., 2019, Silva et al., 2019). Typically, in green coffee beans that have not undergone screening, the proportion of DCB can reach 10%–30% (Craig et al., 2012a). The existence of DCB directly impacts the taste of coffee; specifically, the presence of black beans in a roasted batch usually imparts a heavy flavor to the beverage; sour beans contribute to sour and oniony tastes, while immature beans impart astringency. The proportion of DCB in coffee is one of the most important factors determining the grading of coffee beans(Craig et al., 2012a). The Speciality Coffee Association of America (SCAA) method for coffee grading qualifies five different coffee grades, according to the number of defects (per 350 g of green coffee sample) (SCAA). The International Coffee Organization (ICO) recommends that a maximum of 86 defects/300 g for Arabica variety and 150 defects/300 g for Robusta variety should not be exceeded for exportation purposes (ICO,).

The quality difference in taste between DCB and NDCB is ultimately depends on the chemical compositions of their roasted products, which can be attributed to three aspects: i) The first factor arises from differences in physical properties such as density, moisture content, and shape between DCB and NDCB (Mazzafera, 1999; Vasconcelos et al., 2007), resulting in uneven heating during roasting. ii) Another factor can be attributed to the differences in the contents of flavor precursor compounds in DCB and NDCB, such as amine, sugars, and caffeine etc (Vasconcelos et al., 2007). Both of these factors can lead to fewer flavor substances or unbalanced combination of flavor components in the final coffee beverage of DCB (Toci and Farah, 2008, 2014; Vasconcelos et al., 2007), resulting in weird sensory traits. iii) The third worst-case scenario is that DCB may contain microorganisms that can spoil the fruit and produce toxins such as ochratoxin A (Taniwaki et al., 2014), which can have direct negative health effects.

In recent years, the chemical ingredients of four defective DCB that have the greatest negative impact, including SCB, BCB, MCB, and immature beans, have received the most attention. Gas chromatography mass spectrometry (GC-MS) is the most commonly used method for the detection of volatile components in DCB. A study conducted by Toci and Farah (2014) showed that DCB contains higher amounts of volatile compounds, especially pyrazines, pyrroles and phenolic compounds. In addition, DCB displayed a higher content of some volatile carbonyl compounds than that of NDCB (Santos and Rodrigues, 2020). While for non-volatile components, a series of methods like high performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) (Franca et al., 2005), electrospray ionization mass spectrometry (ESI-MS) (Mendonca et al., 2008), Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) (Craig, Franca and Oliveira, 2012a), diffuse reflectance infrared fourier transform spectroscopy (DRIFTS) (Craig, Franca and Oliveira, 2012b), etc. have been adopted. These studies indicated that there are differences in water-soluble components such as caffeine, amines, phenolic acids, reducing sugars, trigonelline and so on between DCB and NDCB. Although the existing research have some sporadic knowledge about the chemical compositions and distinction of DCB, these understandings are still limited, for example, the fat-soluble components in DCB have not been clarified yet. Therefore, it is necessary to introduce other detection methods to carry out more extensive research on the chemical compositions of DCB.

Given that DCB has the potential to impact not just the quality and price of coffee, but also the well-being of consumers, the proficient detection and elimination of DCB proves advantageous in enhancing both the flavor of coffee and human health. Commonly, DCB can be discerned through physical attributes like weight and visual characteristics. During the primary sorting phase, a machine can sift out DCB by leveraging their weight differential with NDCB. Nonetheless, a fraction of DCB may persist, relegating the beans at this stage to be marketed solely as commercial beans at a lower price. For high-quality specialty coffee, in addition to machine screening, the remaining DCB must be further screened manually, which is usually a laborious process. In recent years, some machine vision based artificial intelligence methods like generative adversarial networks (GAN) have been developed to help this process (Chou et al., 2019). DCB can be well distinguished at the green bean stage according to appearance, however, once roasted and ground into powder, they will have a similar appearance to NDCB, which makes distinguishing by appearance no longer feasible. In such cases, chemometrics can provide alternative methods. Specifically, the chemical profiles of the bean samples were detected using instruments. Then, multivariate analysis methods such as principal component analysis (PCA), partial least squares regression (PLS) or partial least squares regression discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) were employed for analysis and modeling of the data and identifying markers for DCB. In previous studies, techniques such as fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), electrospray ionization mass spectrometry (ESI-MS), and GC-MS have been utilized as instrumental characterization methods for identifying DCB (Mendonca et al., 2008; Craig, Franca and Oliveira, 2012a). Although FTIR has the advantage of non-destructive testing, it can only provide fingerprint spectra rather than detailed spectra of compounds; Mass spectrometry based methods are usually time-consuming and have low repeatability. Therefore, at present, these methods have not been widely applied in the identification of defective bean doping.

1H qNMR (quantitative nuclear magnetic resonance) is a method of quantitative analysis using nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) technology (Iwasaki et al., 2023). The principle of 1H qNMR is based on the fact that the peak area of the corresponding spectral peak of a specific molecular group is proportional to the number of protons, regardless of the chemical environment in which it is located. This allows it to use a known amount of chemical substances with different structures from the analyte as reference standards for determination (Li and Hu, 2017). This method has the advantages of not requiring expensive standards, non-destructiveness, high accuracy, and good reproducibility. In 2010, Wei et al. first applied 1H qNMR technology to identify water-soluble chemical components in green coffee beans. Since then, this technology has been widely used for the analysis of differences in the main components of coffee under different maturity, variety, fermentation, and other conditions (Aswathi et al., 2023; de Paulo et al., 2023; dos Santos et al., 2020; Febrina et al., 2021; Hu et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2021, Wang et al., 2021; Y. B. Wang et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2024). In addition, it has also been applied for quantitative detection of the main components in coffee pulp or silverskin (Wang et al., 2021). Nevertheless, 1H qNMR has not been employed for DCB identification thus far. Therefore, to achieve a clearer understanding of DCB based on existing research, in the current study, 1H qNMR was applied for the analysis of the oil and water-soluble contents of the green DCB, and compared them with that of NDCB. Further, the chemometric method (PLS-DA) and ML methods including K-nearest neighbor classification (KNN), random forest classification (RFC) and support vector machine (SVM) were performed on the qualitative distinguish of DCB blends in roasted coffee powder. Finally, the PLS model based on 1H NMR signal was built for the quantification of DCB blends.

Material and methods

Instruments and chemicals

The grinder (YB-2500A) for crushing coffee samples was purchased from Yongkang Sufeng Industry and Trade Co., Ltd. All the NMR data were acquired by the Bruker DRX-600 instruments (Bruker, Zurich, Switzerland). All the deuterated solvents for NMR experiments were provided by Beijing Yinuokai Technology Co., Ltd, People's Republic of China. The internal standards 3,4,5-trimethoxybenzaldehyde (98 %) and TMSP-D4 (98 %) were purchased from Aladdin Reagent Co., Ltd.

Characterization of non-volatile components in green coffee beans

Materials. Five batches of Arabica coffee beans were randomly collected from Pu'er City, Yunnan Province, China in October 2021. Four types of coffee beans, including NDCB, SCB, BCB and MCB were manually selected from these five green beans samples according to the defective bean classification rule of American Specialty Coffee Association (SCAA), and a total of 20 coffee samples were obtained, each with a weight of more than 15 g.

Extract of oil contents. Accurately weighed 100 mg coffee powder into the EP tube, added 1.0 mL CDCl3 (with 3,4,5-trimethoxybenzaldehyde as internal standard), and ultrasonically extract at 25 °C for 30 min. The extract was passed through a 0.8 μm microporous membrane, 600 μL were transferred into 5 mm NMR tube and kept at 4 °C for following NMR analysis.

Extract of water-soluble contents. The sample is crushed, dried and passed through an 80 mesh sieve, weighed 60 mg into the 1.5 mL EP tube, added 800 μL D2O (with 0.03 % TMSP-D4 as internal standard), and ultrasonically extract at 60 °C for 30 min. Then, the EP tube was centrifuged at 13000 rpm for 5 min, 450 μL D2O were transferred into 5 mm NMR tube and kept at 4 °C for following NMR analysis.

1H qNMR experiments. The 1H qNMR experiments refers to the methods previously reported (Wang et al., 2023). Within 24 h of sample extraction, 1H qNMR experiments were conducted. 1H qNMR were conducted on a 600 MHz NMR and under 30° pulse with 12019 Hz (20 ppm) of spectral width. The number of scans was 64 and the number of dummy scans was 2. The acquisition time was 2.76 s. The collection temperature of the sample is 273K. The relaxation delay (D1) was 10.0 s, which was longer than five times of the maximum longitudinal relaxation time (D1>5T1max) (Li and Hu, 2017).

Qualitative characterization of DCB blends in roasted coffee beans

Materials. Ten NDCB samples roasted into medium degree were randomly collected from Puer City, Yunnan Province, China. The ten samples were ground into powder and mixed with the powder of two roasted DCB samples (SCB：BCB：MCB = 1:1:1) according to the ratio of NDCB: DCB = 70 : 30 to afford 20 adulterated samples. A total of 30 samples divided into two groups (10 non-defective and 20 adulterated samples) were obtained.

The characterization of oil and water-soluble content. The same procedures as described in 2.2 section were adopted to the sample pretreatment and 1H qNMR detection for the CDCl3 extract (oil content) and D2O extract (water-soluble content). Each sample was tested with two repetitions. Finally, 60 1H NMR spectra belonging to 40 blend samples and 20 non-defective samples were acquired.

Quantitative characterization of DCB blends in roasted coffee beans

Materials. The ten NDCB samples described above were separately mixed with the powder of one roasted DCB sample (SCB：BCB：MCB = 1:1:1) with DCB rates of 0, 0.15, 0.3, 0.45, 0.6, respectively. A total of 50 samples with DCB rates range from 0 to 0.6 were obtained.

The characterization water-soluble content. The same procedures as described in 2.2 section were adopted to the sample pretreatment and 1H qNMR detection for the D2O extract (water-soluble content).

Results

Flavor precursors in green DCB

Studies have shown that there may be differences between DCB and NDCB in terms of the major components in green beans and in volatile components after roasting (Franca et al., 2005; Santos and Rodrigues, 2020). However, many important chemical constituents in coffee, such as coffee oil components, have not been clarified in previous DCB studies. Additionally, the difference in chemical composition of DCB and NDCB may change with the origin, variety and other factors (Ossa et al., 2018; Souard et al., 2018), as a result, some previous studies have not reached consistent conclusions. Therefore, in the current study, 1H qNMR was used to clarify the content of precursor compounds in DCB. To have a comprehensive understanding of the chemical profile of DCB, the oil and water-soluble contents of three kinds of DCB (SCB, BCB and MCB) together with NDCB were clarified. By comparing with reference, four kind of compounds from oil (caffaldehyde, cafestol, kahweol and lipids) and six types of water-soluble components (trigonelline, caffeine, CGAs, 5-CQA and acetate) belonging to the four kind of coffee beans were identified from the mixed 1H NMR profile. As shown in Fig. 1, the strength of characteristic signals of these compounds varies from bean types. Furthermore, the characteristic proton of the compounds (kahweol, δH 6.30 ppm; caffaldehyde, δH 10.18 ppm; cafestol, δH 6.21 ppm; lipids, δH 0.87–0.90 ppm; caffeine, δH 7.81 ppm; trigonelline, δH 9.13 ppm; 5-CQA, δH 5.35 ppm; sucrose δH 5.43 ppm; acetate, δH 1.94–2.00 ppm) were selected for quantitative calculation (Ciaramelli et al., 2019; Hu et al., 2020; Wei et al., 2010). Previously, the contents of C&K were reported to vary from 419 to 906 mg cafestol/100 g and 289–908 mg kahweol/100 g in arabica coffee beans (Moeenfard and Alves, 2020). However, to the best of our knowledge, their contents in DCB have not been clarified yet. The content of these two kinds of diterpenoids (esters) exhibited similar trend in NDCB, SCB, BCB and MCB, with the significant highest content in NDCB (cafestol 0.36 ± 0.03%; kahweol 0.61 ± 0.06%) and the lowest content in BCB (cafestol 0.04 ± 0.03%; kahweol 0.08 ± 0.06%). Interestingly, another kind of coffee dierpenoid, caffaldehyde, displayed a reverse trend compared to C&K, which with the highest content in BCB (0.13 ± 0.03%). In addition to diterpenoids, the total content of lipids in coffee oil was also quantified. Since there are too many types of fatty acids to calculated the percentage content of a certain fatty acid, the molar content of the total fatty acids was calculated. Overall, the lipid level in NDCB, which was reaching 0.4 ± 0.02 mol/1000g, were higher than that of other three groups, consistent with the study by Vasconcelos et al. (2007). Although the total content of lipids in SCB, DCB and MCB is lower than that of NDCB, they still have considerable presence, and the content reaches 0.35 ± 0.01, 0.34 ± 0.02 and 0.37 ± 0.03 mol/1000 g, respectively.

Among the soluble reducing sugars in coffee, sucrose exhibits the highest content (Wei et al., 2010), which is why it was selected as a representative ingredient in the present study. Current research results showed that the sucrose contents of SCB (9.66 ± 0.28%, P < 0.05) and BCB (0.63 ± 1.53%, P < 0.05) were significantly lower than that of NDCB, with the sucrose content in BCB being only half of that in NDCB. The sucrose in MCB was not significantly different from that in NDCB. In the study by Mazzafera (1999), similar results were also found: The sucrose content of BCB was significantly lower than that of NDCB. From the 1H NMR profile shown in Fig. 1, the proton signals of other sugars have the same trend as sucrose. However, due to the severe signal overlap, the current study did not perform quantitative analysis on them.

As shown in Fig. 2, caffeine and trigonelline exhibited similar trends in all types of beans. The caffeine and trigonelline content of BCB reached 2.18 ± 0.10 % and 1.31 ± 0.06 %, respectively, which were significantly higher than that of NDCB (1.88 ± 0.12% P < 0.001 and 1.13 ± 0.07% P < 0.001) and the other two kinds of DCB. In previous studies, Franca et al. (2005) found that the caffeine in BCB was higher than that in NDCB via HPLC method, which is consistent with the results of the current study. However, the trigonelline content detected by Franca et al. was lower than that of NDCB. Current research indicated 5-CQA content in BCB (P < 0.05) was significantly higher than that in NDCB, and the content of MCB is significantly (P < 0.01) lower than that in NDCB. While in the results of Franca et al., the 5-CQA content of SCB was higher than that of NDCB, and the content of BCB was significantly lower than that of NDCB. Qualitative of roasted DCB blends based on chemometric and ML

Subsequently, a series of NDCB samples and 30% DCB adulterated samples were tested for oil and water-soluble components using 1H NMR. To enhance data processing efficiency, rather than manually integrating the characteristic signals for each compound, we utilized MestReNova software to integrate all samples simultaneously in increments of 0.004, thereby obtaining the integrated matrix in one go. PLS-DA analysis is the most commonly used supervised chemometric classification algorithm. In addition, it can also be employed to quickly find makers between groups. Variable importance in projection (VIP) value serves as the main indicator for markers, with a VIP value greater than 1.0 typically considered indicative of a marker. Therefore, PLS-DA was adopted to analyze the 1H NMR integral matrix of the CDCl3 and D2O extract, respectively. In the PLS-DA model based on the 1H NMR integral matrix of the CDCl3 extract, a total of three components were fitted with the R2X = 0.484 R2Y = 0.863, Q2 = 0.599 and P < 0.001. As shown in Fig. 4, the VIP values of all features in 0–10 ppm are presented. Each feature cannot carry a chemical component, but represents an integration interval of 0.004. The features with VIP value greater than 1 are mainly concentrated in the characteristic proton signal area of coffee diterpenoids and the proton signal area of fatty acids. For the PLS-DA model of D2O extract data, UV scaling was performed on the data matrix. Then a model (R2X = 0.796 R2Y = 0.931 Q2 = 0.647, P < 0.001) with six components was fitted. The results of the permutation test showed that the intercept of R2 on the Y-axis is less than 0.4, and the intercept of Q2 on the Y-axis is less than 0.05, suggesting that the model does not appear to be overfitting (Fig. S4). Among them, the content of soluble sugars played the most important role in distinguishing DCB blends.

In recent years, ML algorithms have been widely used in the fields of food blends identification, traceability, and variety identification, achieving promising results (Khan et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2022; Zhou et al., 2019; Okamura et al., 2021; Sarkar et al., 2023; Ma et al., 2023). Therefore, based on the 1H NMR data matrix of CDCl3 and D2O, the current study initially explored the performance of three common ML methods in distinguishing DCB blends.

First, PCA analysis was performed on the 1H NMR data of CDCl3 and D2O extract, respectively (Fig. S5). For the PCA results of CDCl3 data, the top 11 PCs which added up to a total explained variances of 99.5%, were selected for subsequent ML research. In contrast, for the D2O data, the cumulative explained variance of the top 20 PCs also reached 99.5%, with the PCs beyond the top 20 contributing little to the total explained variance. Therefore, the first 20 dimensions of data analyzed by PCA were used for the training of subsequent ML models.

KNN is the simplest ML classification algorithm. Its core idea is to judge the category of the unknown sample by the dominant category of the K samples closest to the unknown sample in the feature space. The KNN model was established using the KNeighborClassifier package in sklearn basing on the 1H NMR data after dimensionality reduction with the PCA method. In the KNN model based CDCl3 data, only a best accuracy of 0.67 on test set was obtained (Table 1). In the KNN model basing on D2O data, when the important parameter n_neighbors = 5, the model has the best performance on the test set, which with a highest accuracy of 0.83. RFC combines several decision trees to form an ensemble estimator, is a popular ML algorithm for ensemble learning to solve classification problems. RFC's core parameter n_estimators is optimized by drawing a cross-validated learning curve. In the CDCl3 data based RFC model, a best accuracy value of 0.83 was obtained (Table 1). While the model achieves a perfect recall value (score = 1.0), its accuracy is not optimistic, only reaching 0.8. The model basing on D2O data has the best performance on the test set when its n_estimators is 10, which with accuracy and precision values of 0.89 and 0.93, respectively.

In the experiments of SVC, the GridSearchCV package was used for cross-validation and parameter optimization. Then, the optimal parameters were reused for model training basing on training set, and the performance of the model was finally evaluated on the test set. For the D2O data, the optimal SVC hyperparameters obtained was: kernel = “rbf”, C = 10, gamma = 0.0001. Under the best parameters, the accuracy, precision and recall of the model all reach 1 (Table 1), indicating an excellent classification model. Compared with the D2O data based SVC model, the CDCl3 data showed a worse classification result, which only obtained the accuracy of 0.83 under the optimal parameters (kernel = "linear",C = 10).

Quantitative of roasted DCB blends

The aforementioned studies show that it is feasible to qualitatively analyze DCB blends by chemometrics and ML method. Further, we hope to explore the possibility of quantitative analysis of DCB blends. Considering that in previous studies, the 1H NMR data of the water-soluble extract from D2O extract performed better than that from the oil fraction, only the 1H NMR data of D2O extract was selected in the following quantitative studies.

Similarly, 1H NMR detection is first performed on fifty samples with DCB blends rates ranging from 0 to 0.6 and integrated in steps of 0.04 ppm to obtain a 50 × 2365 integral matrix. Then, the PLS method was selected for the establishment of the discriminant model. The Q2 of the model was 0.868, indicating that the model is also good in terms of predictive power. Discussion

Cafestol and kahweol (C&K) primarily exist in esterified form (99.6%) in the oil and are distributed as 24 different esters (n-C14:0, C16:0, C16:1, C17:0, C18:0, C18:1, C18:2, C18:3, C20:0, C20:1, C22:0, C24:0, mainly palmitate and linoleate) (Kurzrock and Speer, 2001; Novaes et al., 2018). These diterpenoids may impart bitterness to coffee beverage (Lang et al., 2020). The similar polarity between C&K esters makes them difficult to achieve chromatographic separation. Generally, to detect the total content of C&K using chromatographic methods, it is necessary to perform saponification of C&K esters to obtain the diterpene fraction (Kurzrock and Speer, 2001). 1H qNMR is an effective method which can achieve rapid quantitative analysis of the C&K without tedious sample preparation process (Finotello and Schievano, 2017; Schievano and Navarini, 2014). In the current study, 1H qNMR was used for the first time to characterize the diterpenoid components in DCB, among which caffaldehyde was quantitatively studied for the first time in roasted coffee beans. Results showed significant differences in the content of C&K and caffaldehyde between the DCB and NDCB. Interestingly, In BCB, the overall level of C&K is relatively low, while the content of caffaldehyde is significantly higher than other groups. From the perspective of formation pathways, caffaldehyde was formed by the ring opening and oxidation of kahweol. Therefore, it can reasonably conclude that the form process of the BCB will promote the aforementioned oxidation reaction, resulting to a high level of caffaldehyde.

Previous research has demonstrated that lipids possess the highest capacity for retaining aroma compared to proteins and carbohydrates, primarily due to the affinity of certain aromatic compounds for fats. Meanwhile, lipids have the ability to absorb hydrophobic flavor molecules, thereby making a substantial contribution to the overall taste profile of the final product (Mao et al., 2018; Zhu et al., 2023). Current study reveals that lipids in NDCB are maintained at higher levels than in DCB, which may be one of the reasons for their higher quality. In addition, it has been shown that coffee lipids are valuable materials for the production of essential fatty acids (Fernandes et al., 2022), antifoams (Ishwarya and Nisha, 2021), antioxidants, and radiation protectors (Boger et al., 2021; Faria et al., 2020). Although the total lipid content in SCB, DCB, and MCB is lower than that of NDCB, they still have a considerable presence, suggesting the potential utilization value of lipids in these products.

Carbohydrates are the most important type of flavor precursor compounds in coffee. During the roasting process, the Maillard reaction between carbohydrates and ammonia compounds produces a series of flavor compounds that enhance the quality of coffee (Reineccius, 1995). Similar to the changes in lipid content, the current study detected higher levels of sugars in NDCB compared to DCB, indicating that more flavor compounds may be produced in NDCB during the roasting process. Therefore, this may be one of the key reasons for the higher quality of NDCB. Another noteworthy factor is that acetate, a substance that may be produced during the fermentation process, has a relatively high overall content in defective beans, with the most prominent levels found in SCB. This suggests that defective beans may undergo more fermentation during growth or processing. Additionally, acetic acid may also serve as an important marker for detecting defective beans.

Overall, although the composition of the main flavor precursor compounds between DCB and NDCB is the same, there are significant differences in their content. From the results of PCA analysis, it can be seen that the differences in water-soluble extracts among different categories of DCB are more pronounced. Obviously, this difference may directly leads to quality changed in roasted coffee. Nevertheless, It must be realized that 1H qNMR can only detect the main flavor precursors, and those trace substances which can lead adverse quality may be ignored. Combining more technologies to reveal the material basis of DCB is a topic worthy of continuous research in the future.

If DCB such as SCB, BCB, and MCB are mixed in the green coffee beans, they can be distinguished simply by their appearance. However, once they have undergone roasting, it is hard to distinguish them by appearance, which makes the new techniques for the identification of DCB blends become a necessity. In recent years, instrumental-based characterization of chemical profile combined with multivariate analysis proved to be effective for distinguishing DCB blends. Among them, the chemical characterization methods that have been tried are infrared-photo-acoustic spectroscopy (FTIR-PAS) (Craig et al., 2012b; Craig et al., 2012a), Laser-induced breakdown spectroscopy (LIBS) (Silva et al., 2019), DRIFTS and ESI-MS (Mendonca et al., 2008) etc. Considering that 1H NMR can quickly obtain stable signals and the sample processing is simple (Wei et al., 2010), the feasibility of using 1H NMR combined with chemometric methods and ML methods to identify DCB blends was explored.

Compared to the PLS-DA method, ML algorithms are more suitable for distinguishing defective bean doping. Overall, there is a significant difference in performance among the three ML algorithms on different datasets. KNN performs relatively well in processing D2O data, but overall accuracy is still low. The KNN algorithm assumes that similar sampels exist in close proximity. However, in the context of blended coffee beans, the feature space from 1H NMR data was highly complex and non-linear. This means that the distance metrics used by KNN might not accurately capture the similarity between samples. In such cases, more sophisticated algorithms that can handle non-linear relationships may outperform KNN. RFC has demonstrated certain capabilities on both datasets, especially on D2O data, showing excellent performance; SVC achieved perfect classification performance on D2O data, but performed poorly on CDCl3 data. These results emphasize the impact of data features and solvent environment on the performance of classification models, providing important guidance for subsequent research. Overall, the 1H NMR data from D2O extract performed better than that from CDCl3 in distinguishing blends of DCB. Compared with KNN, RFC exhibited a better classification effect. The SVC model trained basing on the 1H NMR data from D2O was the best ML model to distinguish blends of DCB. Future research could consider exploring other feature engineering and model optimization techniques to further enhance the classification ability of DCB mixtures.

In addition, 1H NMR combined with PLS method was used for effective quantitative analysis of DCB mixture, and the results showed good model fit and predictive ability. The PLS method performs well in processing high-dimensional data, especially when there is multicollinearity between the independent and dependent variables. Due to the high-dimensional nature of 1H NMR data, PLS can effectively extract the main features, reduce dimensionality, and maintain the core information of the data. This is crucial for accurate prediction of DCB content. Although this study demonstrates the effectiveness of the model, future research can focus on exploring its universality and adaptability. For example, considering the impact of different mixture components on the predictive ability of the model, as well as its stability under different experimental conditions.

Conclusions

The three types of coffee DCB, including SCB, BCB, and MCB, exhibited a series of non-negligible differences in the content of flavor precursor compounds from NDCB. Overall, NDCB showed higher content of sugars, diterpenoid esters and lipids than the three other DCB, which may directly contribute to their superior taste. Acetate may be the primary chemical responsible for the sour taste of SCB. Although 1H NMR detection is stable and reliable, it is limited to detecting high-concentration chemical components. The presence of other trace components produced during the processing of DCB requires additional detection methods, such as LC-MS or GC-MS. Furthermore, PLS-DA and ML methods were employed to discriminate the blends of roasted DCB. The model based on the 1H NMR data of the D2O extract displayed a better discriminative effect than that of the CDCl3, promoting that in the quality control of coffee beans, it is more reliable to use water-soluble components as markers for identifying DCB blends. Among all tried ML algorithms for qualitative identification, SVC showed the best application prospects to distinguish DCB. Finally, 1H NMR profile based on water-soluble components, combined with PLS modeling, was attempted for quantitative detection of DCB blends and proved to be a feasible solution. This study provides valuables insights for high-quality coffee quality control.
