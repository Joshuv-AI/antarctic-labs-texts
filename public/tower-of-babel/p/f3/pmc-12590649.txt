Association between gender inequality and anaemia prevention knowledge: a cross-sectional study in a Peri-urban community in Ghana
Authors: Safianu Apalebilah, Ama Frimpomaa Oware, Sarkodie Freeman, Joel Karikari Nyarkoh, Emmanuel Jingbeja, Patrick Adu, Joseph Boachie
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAnaemia remains a global health burden, affecting about 1.8 billion diverse groups of people worldwide. However, anaemia disproportionately impacts women and children, with nearly twice as many women as men affected. Addressing gender inequality is essential for improving knowledge and awareness of anaemia and its prevention and management. The aim of this study was to determine the association between gender and anaemia prevention/management knowledge and to assess how socioeconomic and cultural factors, including cultural permission and subordination, shape gender inequality in anaemia-related health knowledge within a Peri-urban Ghanaian community.MethodsThe study adopted a cross-sectional design, involving the administration of pre-tested questionnaires to about 385 recruited participants residing in a Peri-urban community in the central region of Ghana. Participants’ socio-demographics, gender inequality assessment among socioeconomic and cultural factors, and knowledge of anaemia were assessed. Bivariate analysis using a Chi-square or Fisher’s exact test and adjusted odds ratios (AOR) with multivariate logistic regression were performed, and p-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.ResultsThe majority of participants identified as Christian (62.3%) and belonged to the Akan ethnic group (69.6%). Most participants were also married (62.9%), and household sizes of more than five members were common (64.2%). Gender inequality was significantly associated with cultural permission (p = 0.019), defined as the ability of participants to determine daily meal preparation, and subordination (p = 0.007) which in this study was defined as requiring permission before visiting a health facility. Unemployed participants (aOR = 2.463; p = 0.028) and students (aOR = 3.253; p = 0.018) were about two and three times more likely, respectively, to require cultural permission. Similarly, unemployed individuals were about twice as likely to experience subordination compared to employed individuals (aOR = 2.410; p = 0.014). Knowledge of anaemia prevention and management was significantly associated with sex, with females demonstrating higher awareness than males regarding iron-rich foods (57% vs. 43%, p = 0.018), vitamin B12 supplementation in strictly vegan diets (60.1% vs. 39.9%, p = 0.015), and avoiding the habit of eating pica (59.2% vs. 40.8%, p = 0.048).ConclusionSignificant gender differences were observed in anaemia knowledge, with females demonstrating slightly better awareness of preventive measures than males. The study emphasizes the necessity for specific interventions, such as education, that tackle the intersecting factors sustaining gender inequalities in anaemia awareness. Additional research is needed to identify geographically specific factors contributing to gender inequality in anaemia knowledge within the sub-region.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25087-9.

Introduction

Anaemia remains a global health burden affecting about 1.8 billion people worldwide [1]. Anaemia disproportionately impacts women and children, with nearly twice as many women as men affected by the condition. This disparity may reflect underlying gender-based differences in access to nutrition, healthcare, and education, highlighting potential gender inequality. Comparatively, most anaemia-prevalent or related studies in Ghana predominantly target pregnant mothers [2], non-pregnant women [3] and adolescent females [4–6], than men. This suggests that females are a relatively high-risk group in Ghana, necessitating gender-specific interventions to reduce the anaemia burden. Likewise, the World Health Organization (WHO) has, over the past decade, aimed to reduce the prevalence of anaemia in women of reproductive age by 50% by the year 2025 [7]. The persistence of anaemia might partly be due to the multifaceted demographics associated with its aetiology, which includes—but is not limited to—genetic, nutritional, geographic, gender-related, infectious, socioeconomic, and cultural factors [8]. Among these factors, gender plays a key role in shaping social hierarchies and unequal access to resources and opportunities. Gender inequality refers to disparities between men and women caused by restrictive social norms and practices that limit rights, opportunities, and well-being [9]. This has become a critical focus in global health due to mounting evidence of health disparities, necessitating urgent attention and action to ensure that every individual has an equal opportunity to attain their highest level of health.

A deeper theoretical perspective holds that gender, as a social determinant of health, influences exposure to health risks, access to resources, and autonomy over health-related decisions at multiple societal levels. Gender inequality operates through power relations, institutional practices, and social norms, reinforcing disparities not only in economic participation and education but also in control over household resources and healthcare decisions [10–12]. In many settings, including Ghana, these gendered structures result in women often having less control over nutrition, fewer opportunities to receive health information, and greater challenges in seeking and utilizing healthcare services—all of which contribute to observed disparities in anaemia risk and knowledge. Addressing anaemia thus requires moving beyond biological factors to also confront the social systems and power dynamics that perpetuate gender-based health disadvantages [13].

A critical dimension of these social systems and power dynamics is reflected in the concepts of cultural permission and subordination. Cultural permission refers to the extent to which individuals (particularly women) are granted autonomy through social norms and intra-household relations to make health-related decisions, such as determining daily meal preparation or participating in health education activities [14, 15]. In contrast, subordination describes the expectation or requirement that women seek approval from a spouse or other household member before taking actions that impact their health, including accessing healthcare services [16]. Both constructs can constrain women’s agency and health-seeking behaviour and are key drivers of persistent gender disparities in health knowledge and outcomes [17].

Gender-based health inequality plays a significant role in anaemia prevalence, perpetuated by various social determinants, including limited access to nutrition, healthcare, and education, as well as economic and cultural disadvantages. Understanding the association between gender inequality and knowledge about anaemia prevention and management is essential for developing effective public health strategies that address disparities, promote health education, and improve outcomes among vulnerable groups. Uneven access to education perpetuates gender-based health disparities [18], particularly in the context of anaemia. A robust body of evidence indicates an association between educational attainment and health knowledge, literacy, and overall well-being [19, 20]. Specifically, women with limited education are disproportionately affected, lacking essential health knowledge, including nutrition and healthcare-seeking behaviours [21]. Gender inequality also restricts access to healthcare services for a particular gender. In many societies, particularly patriarchal ones, healthcare access may be skewed in favour of men due to decision-making power, control of resources, and social norms that prioritize male health needs over female ones [22]. Due to limited access to healthcare services, women may have fewer opportunities to receive information or counselling on anaemia prevention, which can contribute to lower awareness [23]. Individuals’ socioeconomic status, employment, and economic independence play a critical role in their ability to access nutritious food and healthcare services. Studies have shown that women in lower-income households are at higher risk of anaemia due to inadequate dietary intake and limited healthcare access [24, 25].

Addressing gender inequality is essential to ensure equitable access to health education and interventions that promote awareness and knowledge of anaemia prevention and management. Tackling the geographic-specific root causes of gender disparities is key to enhancing health literacy and reducing anemia prevalence. Therefore, the aim of this study was to determine the association between gender and anaemia prevention/management knowledge and to assess how socioeconomic and cultural factors, including cultural permission and subordination, shape gender inequality in anaemia-related health knowledge within a Peri-urban Ghanaian community.

Methods

Study site and setting

The study was conducted in the Peri-urban town of Moree, a small coastal town located in the Abura-Asebu-Kwamankese district in the Central Region of Ghana (Fig. 1). This district has a 5.2 km coastal strip stretching along the Gulf of Guinea in the southern sector of Ghana. The district is also in close proximity to other well-known towns, including Cape Coast to the southwest, the Mfantseman District to the east, the Assin South District to the north, and the Twifo-Hermang Lower Denkyira Districts to the northwest.Fig. 1Map of Abura-Asebu-Kwamankese district

Map of Abura-Asebu-Kwamankese district

There are approximately 262 communities within the Abura-Asebu-Kwamankese district, with the district capital being Abura Dunkwa. Anaemia remains prevalent in several low- and middle-income countries, including Ghana, due to limited access to proper sanitation, educational facilities, healthcare services, and other socioeconomic factors. The Peri-urban town of Moree is no exception. The Abura-Asebu-Kwamankese district comprises both rural and Peri-urban areas. Moree, a Peri-urban coastal town within the district, was purposively selected for its diverse socio-demographic characteristics and accessibility, making it suitable for examining cultural and gender-related influences on health knowledge and decision-making. Likewise, in terms of accessibility and logistical feasibility, Moree is more accessible than remote rural villages, allowing for easier data collection while still capturing the challenges faced by less urbanized populations. This ensures a balance between practicality and research relevance. Moreover, the town’s coastal location and mixed livelihoods (such as fishing and trading) introduce unique gender roles in health decision-making (e.g., male-dominated fishing economies versus women’s caregiving roles). These dynamics are critical for understanding how anaemia interventions might be perceived and adopted across genders.

Since the study adopted a purposive sampling technique and focused on cultural and gender influences rather than district-wide prevalence, Moree’s selection may be justified as a case study to explore these themes in depth, even if it might not be statistically representative of all 262 communities. However, broader surveys would be needed for district-wide generalizations.

Study design

The current study adopted a cross-sectional design to examine the relationship between gender and participants’ knowledge of anaemia prevention and management, as well as to assess the socioeconomic and cultural factors associated with gender inequality. A cross-sectional approach was chosen because it allows data to be collected from a wide range of participants at one time, making it suitable for determining the prevalence and associations between gender disparities and anaemia knowledge in a community-based context. Additionally, among the socioeconomic and cultural factors significantly associated with gender inequality, the study sought to identify the demographic characteristics of participants in Moree Township that may predict cultural and socioeconomic differences between men and women. The study was conducted from June to December 2024 using well-structured, close-ended questionnaires.

Study population sampling technique

The study adopted a purposive sampling technique to recruit participants. The target population consisted of adults aged 20 years or older, including both men and women who were inhabitants and/or long-term residents of Moree Township (having lived there for more than 10 years). This population primarily comprised adults who were expected to be economically independent. Economically independent adults may have greater autonomy in making health-related decisions, including choices regarding nutrition and healthcare access.

Moree Township has several social amenities, such as a clinic, a market, information centers, and a community playground. At these public locations, the study recruited consenting adults aged 20 or older who confirmed they had been residents of Moree Township for over a decade. Participants were selected based on predefined characteristics rather than randomly.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Only consenting adult residents of Moree Township were recruited for the current study. Participants aged 20 years and above were included, while individuals outside this age range—including children and teenagers—were excluded. Non-residents of the Moree community, such as those living in neighbouring towns or individuals on temporary visits, were also excluded from the study.

Data collection

Data collection in this study involved the use of a pre-tested, structured questionnaire with closed-ended questions. The questionnaire was adapted with slight modifications from a similar study, which had already undergone expert validation [27]. To ensure the validity of the constructs, the questionnaire was pre-tested in a Peri-urban community within the same district, a setting sharing similar characteristics with Moree, before being deployed for large-scale data collection.

The questionnaire was divided into subsections, including socio-demographics, gender inequality assessment, assessment of participants’ knowledge of the risk factors, causes and effects of anaemia as well as the assessment of participants’ knowledge about the management and prevention of anaemia. To maximize participant responsiveness, the questionnaire was designed to be as concise as possible without compromising data quality. Care was taken to minimize respondent fatigue while ensuring all key categories relevant to the study’s objectives were captured.

The questionnaire included items to assess access to education, cultural beliefs regarding female education, and gender-specific health education exposure based on literature linking these factors to gendered health disparities. To explore household decision-making power related to nutrition, participants were asked whether their culture permitted them to decide which meal to prepare daily at home. Additionally, participants were asked: Whether gender-specific health education initiatives were organized in their community (to assess the presence of gender-targeted health programs that might influence anaemia knowledge and prevention). Participants were also asked whether their community’s cultural norms allowed individuals to pursue higher education than their spouse or partner, regardless of gender (to examine cultural attitudes toward family educational hierarchy, which may reflect underlying gender inequality).

Ethical consideration

Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of the University of Cape Coast (ID: UCCIRB/CHAS/2023/02) before the study commenced. Written informed consent was obtained from participants prior to their inclusion in the study. The researchers ensured data confidentiality and anonymity. Participation was voluntary and uncompensated, and participants could withdraw at any stage without consequences.

Data analysis

The data were first coded, entered into SPSS Version 28.0, and analyzed using descriptive statistics. Frequencies and percentages were used to report the data. Bivariate analyses were conducted to explore associations between variables using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Additionally, adjusted odds ratios (AORs) with their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were determined using multivariate logistic regression analysis. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The main predictor variables included sex (male, female), key socio-demographic factors (age, marital status, employment status, income level, education level), and two central sociocultural constructs: cultural permission and subordination.

Cultural permission was defined as the individual’s perceived ability to decide which meal to prepare daily at home, assessed by the question: “Does your culture permit you to determine which meal to prepare daily at home?” (Yes/no).Subordination was defined as the self-reported need to obtain permission from someone at home before visiting a health facility, measured by the question: “Do you need to obtain permission at home before visiting any health facility?” (Yes/no).

Participants’ knowledge of anaemia prevention, management, and risk factors was assessed using two sets of 10 questions each. Each correct response was awarded one point, while incorrect or “not sure” answers received zero points. Results

Sociodemographic characteristics of participants

This study collected data on the sociodemographic characteristics of the participants (Table 1). Most participants were between the ages of 20 and 29 years (48.6%), with females representing a slightly higher proportion (55.6%) than males (44.4%). A majority of participants identified as Christian (62.3%), while the Akan ethnic group was the most prevalent (69.6%). Most participants were married (62.9%), and household sizes of more than five members were common (64.2%). Additionally, more than half of the participants reported being employed (54.3%) and having a low income level (55.6%).

A significant proportion of participants did not self-fund their education (87.5%) and reported being economically dependent (65.5%). Additionally, most participants indicated they had the freedom to attend health education programs of their choice (76.9%), but only 18.2% reported that their community organized health education programs specifically for their gender.

A notable finding is that 87% of participants stated that their culture does not permit them to be more educated than their spouse/partner. Significant differences were observed in two areas: cultural permission to determine meal preparation at home (p = 0.019) and the requirement to obtain permission before visiting a health facility (p = 0.007). These socioeconomic and cultural inequalities, particularly the need for cultural permission and experiences of subordination, restrict individuals’ autonomy in making independent dietary and healthcare decisions. Limited autonomy reduces opportunities to access reliable health information and educational programs, which in turn influences participants’ knowledge of anaemia prevention and management. This effect is evident in the sex-specific differences in awareness of iron-rich foods, vitamin B12 supplementation, and pica documented in Tables 3, 4 and 5.

Regarding knowledge of risk factors, causes, and effects of anaemia, about seven in ten participants had generally heard about anaemia (70.4%), and more than half (55.6%) understood that anaemia results from a reduction in blood/haemoglobin levels. However, about four in ten (44.2%) held the misconception that men were at greater risk of anaemia than women, while about seven in ten (67.3%) were unsure whether eating non-nutritious substances (pica) could cause anaemia. Additionally, a large majority—about eight in ten (80%)—correctly identified that untreated anaemia could result in death.

A significant gender difference was observed in participants’ awareness that avoiding pica (a habit of eating non-nutritious substances) can prevent anaemia (p = 0.048), with females (59.2%) more likely than males (40.8%) to demonstrate this knowledge. Overall, the majority—about six in ten (55.8%)—were unsure about this, with females being more predominant than males (53% vs. 47%). A significant gender difference was also observed in participants’ awareness that strict vegetarians may require vitamin B12 supplementation to prevent or manage anaemia (p = 0.015), with females (60.1%) more knowledgeable than males (39.9%). There was also a notable difference in the understanding that anaemia can be prevented by consuming iron-rich foods (p = 0.018), with females showing slightly better awareness than males (57% vs. 43%). Similarly, the majority of participants—about six in ten (61.6%)—were unsure of the importance of vitamin B12 supplementation in strict vegetarians for preventing or managing anaemia, with more than half being female (52.7% vs. 47.3%).

Discussion

Anaemia remains a major global health concern, disproportionately affecting women and children—two vulnerable groups—due to gender-associated sociocultural and economic inequalities. This study found that gender inequality significantly influenced knowledge of anaemia prevention and management, with cultural permission and subordination emerging as key predictors of disparities. The study findings align with key global data documenting contributing determinants of gender-based inequalities and their association with anaemia prevention knowledge.

The majority of participants were young adults (20–29 years old), primarily female, Christian, and of Akan ethnicity. These findings highlight the entrenched relationship between family structure and cultural norms in the study community [19]. One of the study’s key findings was the association between gender inequality and knowledge of anaemia. Compared to men, women demonstrated a better understanding of dietary interventions, such as consuming iron-rich foods. This aligns with research by Awuah et al. (2021), who found that women who participated in focused health education programs had greater nutritional awareness [29]. Similar patterns were observed in a study conducted in low- and middle-income countries, where access to comprehensive nutritional education remains limited [24]. These disparities highlight the need for targeted educational programs to address specific gaps in knowledge about anaemia prevention. Although vegetarianism is uncommon in this community, education about vitamin B12 remains relevant for populations adopting plant-based diets due to urbanization.

In this study, cultural permission and subordination were treated as key predictor variables, assessed for their influence on participants’ knowledge of anaemia prevention and management, as well as their association with sociodemographic characteristics. Cultural norms, particularly the requirement for permission to access healthcare, strongly influenced gender-based differences in anaemia knowledge. This finding aligns with that of Seidu et al. (2020), who noted similar barriers to women’s healthcare autonomy [30]. Participants who were older (40–49 years) and had higher incomes were less likely to need cultural permission, suggesting that decision-making autonomy and economic independence are positively correlated [31]. However, variations in sampling methods and geographical locations may lead to disparities in these findings. For instance, research conducted in more urbanized areas often finds that women have greater autonomy due to better access to employment and educational opportunities [32].

The need to obtain permission before visiting a medical facility, referred to as subordination, showed significant associations with religion, number of children, and employment status. Subordination was more common among Muslims and among individuals with either no children or large families, highlighting the influence of family structure and cultural norms [17].

Participants demonstrated varying levels of understanding about anaemia prevention and treatment. While most recognized the importance of consuming iron-rich foods, misconceptions persisted—particularly regarding preventive measures such as vitamin B12 supplementation and the risks associated with pica. These findings align with a study on the global burden of anaemia, which identified similar knowledge gaps [1].

A deeper interpretation of these findings requires situating them within robust theoretical frameworks of gender and gender inequality. Gender is not merely a biological classification but a set of socially constructed roles, behaviours, expectations, and relationships that significantly shape opportunities for education, access to healthcare, and the distribution of resources [33, 34]. Gender inequality refers to the systematic disparities in power, resources, decision-making, and social status that arise from culturally embedded gender roles and societal norms [10, 12]. In practice, gendered systems influence not only household resource allocation and access to health services but also who receives health information, nutritional support, and the autonomy to act upon health knowledge [11, 35]. In many contexts, including our study setting, these inequalities manifest in requirements for women to seek permission before accessing health facilities or making household nutrition decisions. These limitations are not biologically inherent but are rooted in social constructs and power relations.

Women’s biological predispositions, such as menstruation and pregnancy, increase their risk of anaemia. However, the most significant factors influencing gaps in awareness, prevention, and management of the condition are the socially constructed roles of gender and the associated power dynamics [13].

However, as Awuah et al. (2021) suggest, the gender-based disparities in this study—with women showing slightly better knowledge than men—may reflect the effectiveness of focused health education initiatives [29]. Our finding that females demonstrated better awareness of iron-rich foods for anaemia prevention (57% vs. 43%) aligns with a similar study [36] which attributes this progress to educational components integrated into antenatal care services, benefiting women during their multiple pregnancies.

Misconceptions about anaemia risk factors persisted (e.g., 44.2% believed men were more susceptible than women). Further research should explore the drivers of these beliefs, as such gaps may perpetuate gender disparities in prevention knowledge. These misconceptions could stem from entrenched cultural prejudices and limited access to reliable health information. For instance, patriarchal norms that prioritize male health-seeking behaviours [21] or restrict female participation in community health programs may reinforce these false beliefs. Anaemia prevention initiatives could be strengthened through community-based education programs. For example, workshops co-led by local leaders could directly address misconceptions, while broader prevention efforts (e.g., iron supplementation) require systemic support. Public health education on anaemia should also cover a wider range of causes. Although 56.6% of respondents reported autonomy in meal decisions (Table 2), men’s involvement remains critical, as they often control household resources [20]. Interventions should engage men without undermining women’s reported autonomy.

Limitations

It is important to acknowledge several limitations, even though this study provides insightful information about the connection between gender disparity and anaemia knowledge. Although the cross-sectional approach offers a snapshot of current trends, it may not fully capture the evolving nature of sociocultural and economic factors influencing anaemia awareness. The relatively small sample size could limit the generalizability of the findings, and the cross-sectional design restricts the ability to establish causation. Future studies in similar settings could validate these associations using longitudinal data. Additionally, while the findings may not generalize to purely rural or urban settings, they offer transferable insights into Peri-urban contexts.

Conclusion

The research findings indicate significant knowledge gaps and misconceptions regarding the prevention and management of anaemia among individuals of both genders. Significant gender differences were observed in anaemia knowledge, with females demonstrating slightly better awareness of preventive measures than males. These differences, particularly in areas such as pica and iron-rich foods, suggest that tailored education may benefit women and men differently. To reduce gender-based health disparities, it is necessary to address cultural norms that limit women’s autonomy in healthcare and to foster economic empowerment through job opportunities and educational options. The research emphasizes the necessity for targeted interventions that tackle the intersecting factors sustaining gender inequalities in anaemia awareness. Community-based education programs that target both men and women could also help dispel misconceptions and enhance anaemia prevention efforts. Follow-up studies in contrasting communities (e.g., inland rural villages) are recommended to validate these trends.
