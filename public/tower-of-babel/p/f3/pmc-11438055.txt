Impact of repeated heat-pressing on the microstructure and flexural strength of lithium disilicate glass-ceramics
Authors: Jiale Li, Ding Li, Qi Wang, Fu Wang, Zhenzhen Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe leftover material from the heat-pressing of IPS e.max Press ceramic is often discarded, despite some laboratories exploring its potential for reuse. However, there is a lack of data on the performance of IPS e.max Press ceramic when combined with the button portions. This study investigated the impact of repeated heat-pressing on the crystal structure and flexural strength of lithium disilicate glass-ceramic (LDGC).MethodsSpecimens (N = 30, n = 10 per group) were categorized based on the number of heat-pressing cycles: G0 (control group, no heat-pressing), G1 (one cycle of heat-pressing), and G2 (two cycles of heat-pressing). The crystal structure of LDGC bars was characterized using X-ray diffraction (XRD). Flexural strength was tested, and microstructures were analyzed via scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and the ImageJ processing program. Data were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), and multiple pairwise comparisons of means were performed with Tukey’s post-hoc test.ResultsG2 exhibited significantly lower flexural strength and crystallinity, as well as larger crystal size, compared to G1 and G0 (p < 0.05). Flexural strength values decreased significantly with an increased number of heat-pressing cycles.ConclusionsThe mechanical properties of LDGC significantly degraded after repeated heat pressing. Therefore, it is not clinically advisable to repeatedly press the lithium disilicate ingot together with the leftover material.

Background

The pressable lithium disilicate ceramic, IPS Empress 2, was introduced in 1998 [1]. Unlike the leucite-reinforced glass-ceramic IPS Empress Esthetic, IPS Empress 2 exhibits superior mechanical strength due to its lithium disilicate crystal composition [2]. In 2007, the advanced pressable lithium disilicate glass-ceramic (LDGC), IPS e.max Press, was launched, offering enhanced mechanical properties and improved translucency. This ceramic boasts approximately 70% crystalline phase and an interlocking microstructure, delivering exceptional strength and aesthetic performance [3–5].

In dental laboratories, the button and sprue portions of IPS e.max Press ceramic are typically discarded after a single heat press. However, some dental laboratories have found these materials useful for re-pressing. Consequently, it is essential to evaluate the mechanical properties of re-pressed glass ceramic material to determine the feasibility of repeated heat-pressing treatments. Chung has reported that repeated heat-pressing significantly increases the flexural strength of IPS Empress 2 [6]. By comparing fracture toughness values of Empress 1 and Empress 2 before the pressing procedure, after it, and after the repressing procedure, Albakry has found no significant change in fracture toughness and biaxial flexural strength after the pressing and repressing procedures [7, 8]. However, after the repressing procedure, the Empress 2 lithium disilicate crystal scale increases significantly. Cracks propagating parallel to the aligned crystals exhibit lower toughness than those propagating perpendicularly. Tang’s studies have shown that, compared to a single heat-pressing treatment, the density of IPS e.max Press glass-ceramic material decreases, and the porosity increases after two heat-pressing events. Vickers hardness and fracture toughness significantly decrease, with flexural strength reducing from 354.46 MPa after one cycle of heat-pressing to 247.37 MPa after two cycles of heat-pressing. Crystals become oriented, and the crystal size increases after two cycles of heat-pressing [9]. Gorman has reported that in lithium disilicate ceramic (IPS e.max Press) specimens reused up to four times, there is no significant difference in biaxial flexural strength and fracture toughness. However, the hardness decreases, the crystal scale increases, and the types of crystals remain constant [10].

Previous studies have shown that the lithium disilicate crystal size increases and the crystal arrangement becomes more directional after repeated heat-pressing [9, 10]. However, the mechanical properties observed in these studies have been inconsistent. Most studies have used the button and sprue portions after one heat-pressing for repeated heat-pressing material, whereas the properties of the lithium disilicate ingot combined with the leftover material have not been studied. This study hypothesized that the flexural strength of LDGC is not affected by repeated heat-pressing.

Methods

Three groups of glass-ceramic

LDGC specimens undergoing no heat-pressing (G0), one cycle of heat-pressing (G1), and two cycles of heat-pressing (G2) were fabricated. Organic glass specimens with dimensions of 22 mm × 5 mm × 2 mm (length × width × thickness) were created to replace the wax pattern. IPS e.max Press ceramics (HT, Ivoclar Vivadent) were then fabricated into bar specimens (22 × 5 × 2 mm, N = 10, G1) in the machine of Programat® EP3000 (Ivoclar Vivadent) following the manufacturer’s instructions [4]. After pressing, the button and sprue portions were adjusted by grinding to ensure proper insertion into the refractory molds. To thoroughly eliminate the surface investment material, the button portions were etched with 1% hydrofluoric acid for 12 min, followed by a water rinse. Subsequently, both the lithium disilicate ingot and the remaining material were pressed together, resulting in an additional 10 specimens (G2).

Using IPS e.max Press LDGC as the raw material, a bulk glass foundation was created by melting it at 1,450 °C for 30 min using the fusion method. The molten material was then immediately transferred to a furnace set at 500 °C and allowed to cool to room temperature, forming 10 bar specimens, each measuring 22 × 5 × 2 mm. To determine the crystallization characteristics of the as-prepared glass, differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) experiment was conducted using a SETARAM LabsysTM TG DSC at a heating rate of 40 K/min. There were two exothermic peaks at 616 °C and 810 °C, respectively. The end temperatures of the second peak were estimated to be ~ 855 °C. The glass ceramic without heat-pressing (G0) was produced through a two-step heat treatment process, with the first step at 616 °C and the second at 855 °C.

The lateral surfaces of all glass-ceramic specimens were meticulously wet-ground using SiC grinding papers and then mirror-polished with diamond suspensions. Following the ISO 6872 (2015) guidelines [11], the specimens were refined to final dimensions of 20 × 4 × 1.5 mm (length×width×thickness) in preparation for mechanical testing and microstructural analysis.

Flexural strength test

The three-point flexural strength of 30 specimens was measured following the guidelines of ISO 6872 (2015) [11]. The specimens were tested dry at room temperature. Each specimen was placed on two rollers set 15 mm apart and loaded axially with a cylinder in a Universal Testing Machine (AGS-10KNG, Japan) at a cross-head speed of 0.5 mm/min until failure. The three-point flexural strength was calculated using the following formula:

To observe the crack propagation of glass ceramics, ceramic fragments after flexural strength test were examined using nanoindentation equipped with a Berkovich diamond tip in load-control mode up to a maximum load of 300 mN.

X-Ray Diffraction (XRD) analysis

Briefly, 10 fractured segments of each type of specimen were randomly chosen for XRD analysis after the three-point bending test. The crystalline phases of the glass-ceramic specimens were analyzed by X-ray diffraction analysis (XRD) using a X’Pert Pro diffractometer with Cu-Kα radiation (λ = 0.15418 nm) over a 2θ-range of 10° ~ 80° using a 0.033° step interval. Based on the XRD data, the crystallinity (%) of the respective glass-ceramics was calculated using the following Eq. [4]:

Where Ic is the integral intensity of the crystalline fraction, representing the area under the diffraction peaks and background curve; and Ia is the intensity scattered by the amorphous fraction, corresponding to the area of the diffractogram outside the peaks.

Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM)

To examine the microstructure of samples subjected to no heat-pressing, one cycle of heat-pressing, and two cycles of heat-pressing, several fractured segments from the three-point bending test were etched using 4% hydrofluoric acid for 20 s and then coated with gold for SEM imaging (S-4800, Hitachi High-Technologies Corporation, Tokyo, Japan). Subsequently, 10 photomicrographs were taken per etched specimen to assess crystal size using the ImageJ processing program.

Statistical analysis

The data distributions were assessed using normal probability plots and the Shapiro-Wilk test, confirming that the measurements were approximately normally distributed (IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 22.0, Armonk, NY: IBM Corp). A one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted to compare the means of crystal size and flexural strength across different materials, with a statistical significance set at 5%. Multiple pairwise comparisons of means were performed using Tukey’s post-hoc test, utilizing GraphPad Prism version 10 software (GraphPad Software, San Diego, California, USA).

Results

XRD analysis

Figure 1 presents the XRD patterns of LDGC for groups G0, G1, and G2. The majority of the higher-intensity diffraction peaks in all three groups corresponded to the orthorhombic lithium disilicate crystalline phase. Some weaker peaks might correspond to the orthorhombic lithium metasilicate crystalline phase and the lithium phosphate phase, indicating that lithium disilicate was the primary crystalline phase in the samples. Additionally, the diffraction peaks of LDGC in the G1 group (represented in red) and the G0 group (represented in green) were significantly higher than those in the G2 group.

Analysis of crystal volume fraction

Based on the diffraction intensity of the glass matrix and crystalline phases, the crystallinity of the glass-ceramics was estimated. The crystallinity of the G1 glass-ceramic was approximately 64% ± 4.8%, the G0 glass-ceramic was around 64% ± 1.8%, and the G2 glass-ceramic was about 48% ± 4.3%. The crystal volume fraction in G2 was significantly lower than in G1 and G0 (p < 0.05).

Observation of crystalline Phase structure

Figure 2 shows the SEM images (5000 X and 20000 X magnifications) of the crystalline phase structures of the three groups of LDGC after acid etching. The lithium disilicate crystalline phases in G1, G2, and G0 all appeared as rod-like structures forming an interlocking network. However, the crystal sizes differed significantly among the groups. The G0 crystals were finer and denser, while the G1 crystals were significantly larger, and the G2 crystals were even larger, exhibiting long needle-like shapes with more pronounced directionality.

Analysis of crystal dimensions

Figure 3 illustrates the length, width, and aspect ratio of the crystals in the three groups of LDGC. The average crystal length in the G2 group, following repeated casting, was significantly greater than that in the G1 and G0 groups (p < 0.05). The G0 group exhibited short rod-like crystals with an average length of (0.92 ± 0.14) µm, the G1 group showed rod-like crystals with an average length of (3.22 ± 0.41) µm, and the G2 group had significantly larger crystals with an average length of (4.42 ± 0.88) µm. The average crystal width in the G2 group was lower than that in the G1 group following repeated casting (p < 0.05). Consequently, the average aspect ratio of the crystals in the G2 group was markedly higher than in the other two groups, increasing from 4.91 to 8.16. This finding indicated that repeated heat-pressing contributed to an increase in crystal dimensions.

Flexural strength

Figure 4 presents the flexural strength test results for the three groups of LDGC. It was evident that the flexural strength of the ceramics in the G1 and G0 groups was significantly higher than that in the G2 group, with the statistical analysis showing a significant difference (P < 0.05).

Discussion

XRD analysis

XRD analysis indicated that the content of the lithium disilicate crystalline phase was higher after the initial heat-pressing compared to after repeated heat-pressing. This might suggest phase transitions between Li2Si2O5 and Li2SiO3 during the heat treatment process. Several studies have shown that repeated heat-pressing significantly affects the microstructure of LDGC materials, causing an increase in crystal size, a blunter shape, and more directional crystal alignment [9, 10]. These findings are consistent with our study. The directional alignment of the crystals induced by pressing is a result of plastic deformation of the glass matrix during the plunger extrusion process. The formation of larger, blunter rod-like lithium disilicate crystals can be attributed to Ostwald ripening [7, 8]: during thermodynamic processes, smaller crystals tend to aggregate on the surface of larger crystals. This likely explained the larger crystal size observed in the G2 group.

Crystal volume fraction analysis

In this study, the crystallinity of the G1 glass-ceramic was approximately 64% ± 4.8%, the G0 glass-ceramic was around 64% ± 1.8%, and the G2 glass-ceramic was about 48% ± 4.3%. The crystal volume fraction in G2 was significantly lower than in G1 and G0. Due to Ostwald ripening [12], repeated heat pressing temperature exceeded the initial melting point of the crystals which was 900 °C [13], causing partial melting of the initial crystals and further growth. Consequently, the crystal size in G2 increased, but the crystal content decreased [14]. Previous research has indicated that an increase in crystal volume fraction enhances mechanical properties. Continuous heat treatment of glass-ceramics results in a hierarchical structure characterized by macroscopic-scale voids at the glass matrix and crystal interfaces, microscopic-scale crystal sizes, and nanoscale lattice defects. This pronounced structural hierarchy plays a decisive role in the mechanical properties of glass-ceramics [15–19]. For glass-ceramics, when the glass matrix volume fraction is high, the mechanical properties are determined by the volume fraction of crystals dispersed within the glass matrix. Therefore, the lower crystal volume fraction in G2 leads to a decline in its mechanical performance.

Analysis of flexural strength

The results of this study indicated that the flexural strength of the ceramics in the conventional G1 group was significantly higher than that in the G2 group. The higher strength observed in the G1 group might be attributed to the uniformly crystallized interlocking needle-like crystals, which possess a consistent aspect ratio [20–22]. In contrast, the G2 group exhibited larger crystal sizes, narrower profiles, and fewer crystals, which facilitated crack propagation through the surrounding glass matrix, ultimately resulting in reduced strength [19].

Previous studies examining the flexural strength, fracture toughness, hardness, and microstructure of repeatedly pressed ceramics consistently indicate that repeated pressing enlarges crystal size and promotes more directional crystal alignment [7–10]. However, the impact on mechanical properties remains inconclusive. Some researchers have observed a notable decrease in flexural strength, while others report no significant change [7–10]. Most research has concentrated on the repeated pressing of IPS e.max Press ceramics using initial pressing ingots. There is a noticeable gap in studies exploring the performance of commercial ceramics combined with ingots after repeated pressing.

Tang’s research has demonstrated that repeated pressing results in a decrease in crystal density, an increase in porosity, and significant reductions in three-point bending strength, microhardness, and fracture toughness, indicating a notable decline in mechanical properties after repeated pressing [9]. The increase in crystal size and stronger crystal directionality have also been observed. Tang has attributed the reduction in hardness to increased porosity and decreased density after repeated pressing. The reduction in strength is attributed to the growth and decreased number of crystals, which makes cracks more prone to propagating within the glass matrix. Similarly, the decline in fracture toughness results from the more directional and sparse crystal distribution, further facilitating crack propagation within the glass matrix.

Gorman’s study on the mechanical properties of LDGC after four repeated pressings has found no significant differences in biaxial flexural strength or fracture toughness, although hardness decreases, and crystal size increases, while the crystal types remain unchanged. These results suggest that LDGC can be reused multiple times [10]. Gorman has noted that the flexural strength of 243.3 MPa is lower than the manufacturer’s recommended value due to the use of 180-grit sandpaper for polishing and suggested that the manufacturer’s recommended strength value is obtained after different heat treatments to reduce residual stress.

Chung’s research has found that the flexural strength of Empress 2 ceramics after repeated pressing is significantly higher than that after the initial pressing [6]. Albakry has compared the fracture toughness of Empress 1 and Empress 2 heat-pressed ceramics before and after pressing and repeated pressing. He has found no significant changes in fracture toughness or biaxial bending strength after initial pressing and repeated pressing. However, the crystal size of Empress 2 lithium disilicate increases significantly after repeated pressing, with crack propagation perpendicular to the crystal direction being noticeably smaller than that parallel to the crystal direction [7, 8].

Crystals can inhibit crack propagation through crack deflection [23]. In this study, the SEM images of crack propagation obtained by the indentation method (Fig. 5) showed that cracks within the LDGC did not propagate in a straight line. Instead, they exhibited phenomena such as turning and crack deflection when encountering crystals, making cracks more likely to propagate within the glass matrix. This further indicated that the higher crystal volume fractions in the G1 and G0 groups were better at preventing crack propagation [24–26], resulting in higher flexural strength compared to those subjected to repeated pressing.

The oral environment is highly complex, with variations in chewing force, temperature, and pH that can increase the wear and mechanical load on restorations, thereby reducing their longevity [27–30]. Consequently, lithium disilicate restorations must possess exceptionally high mechanical properties to minimize these complications. However, the study results indicated that the mechanical properties of LDGC significantly declined after repeated heat-pressing. Therefore, the simultaneous re-pressing of IPS e.max Press ceramic blocks and ingots cannot be used clinically.

Conclusion

Despite the limitations of this study, several key conclusions can be drawn:

Additionally, this process led to a significant decrease in flexural strength.
