Successful combined treatment for primary central nervous system lymphoma with massive hemorrhage: Illustrative case
Authors: Yusuke Kobayashi, Yuma Miki, Daisuke Tanioka, Yuta Sakuragi, Atsushi Terajima, Yosuke Sato, Hitomi Kera, Kouzou Murakami, Yoichi Morofuji
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background: Primary central nervous system lymphoma (PCNSL) is a rare tumor, accounting for only 1–2% of all primary brain tumors, with most cases being diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL). Because these lymphomas can rapidly worsen, prompt and accurate histological diagnosis, followed by early treatment initiation, are essential for improved prognosis. Extensive surgical resection has not demonstrated a clear survival benefit, and a minimally invasive biopsy is recommended. Hemorrhage within lesions is exceedingly rare, with few reports available.Case Description: A 73-year-old woman presented with progressive aphasia and right-sided hemiparesis. Imaging revealed a lesion from the left basal ganglia to the frontal lobe, suggestive of glioblastoma but not ruling out PCNSL. She developed deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism, requiring anticoagulation therapy. Steroid therapy for acute brain edema provided transient improvement, but a massive intratumoral hemorrhage occurred. We performed a frameless robot-guided stereotactic biopsy and craniotomy for hematoma evacuation in a single operation. Final pathology confirmed DLBCL. Subsequent high-dose methotrexate plus rituximab therapy and radiation led to marked tumor shrinkage.Conclusion: Combining robot-guided stereotactic biopsy with craniotomy in PCNSL with hemorrhage ensures precise diagnosis, effective decompression, and optimal treatment planning.

INTRODUCTION

Primary central nervous system lymphoma (PCNSL) is a rare intracranial neoplasm typically manifesting as diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL).[1,3,12] Unlike many other brain malignancies, PCNSL is managed with chemotherapy and radiotherapy rather than extensive resection, as surgical removal has not shown a clear survival benefit.[3] Minimally invasive biopsy followed by prompt chemo-radiotherapy remains the standard of care.[1,2] Recent advances such as frameless robot-assisted stereotactic biopsy systems have enhanced diagnostic accuracy for deep-seated lesions, achieving diagnostic yields of 96–99% and permanent complication rates below 3%.[13,23,25]

Intratumoral hemorrhage in PCNSL is exceedingly rare. While up to 26% of high-grade gliomas or metastatic tumors present with hemorrhage,[9,24] only 14 cases of hemorrhagic PCNSL have been reported.[4,5,7,8,11,16,19,20,22] Its presence often leads to misdiagnosis as glioblastoma or stroke. We describe an immunocompetent elderly patient with PCNSL who developed massive hemorrhage after anticoagulation for venous thromboembolism and high-dose steroids – a previously unreported iatrogenic trigger. To manage this emergency, we performed frameless robot-guided stereotactic biopsy and open craniotomy for hematoma evacuation in a single session. This is among the first reported cases of simultaneous robot-assisted stereotactic brain biopsy and planned craniotomy for hematoma evacuation in hemorrhagic PCNSL, highlighting critical considerations for anticoagulant/steroid use in lymphoma management.

ILLUSTRATIVE CASE

Case presentation

A 73-year-old woman with no history of immunodeficiency or malignancy developed aphasia and right-sided weakness approximately 1 month before admission (day 1 of symptom onset). Over the following 2 weeks, she exhibited progressive aphasia, right hemiparesis, and gait disturbances. On day 18, a magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) at the referring hospital identified two lesions in the left basal ganglia and frontal lobe, raising suspicion of a brain tumor. Her condition deteriorated further, leading to a bedridden state and inability to take oral intake; she was then transferred to our hospital on day 21. On arrival, neurological examination revealed impaired consciousness (Glasgow Coma Scale E3V1M4), severe aphasia, and right hemiparesis (manual muscle test [MMT] 1/5). Computed tomography (CT) and MRI at our facility [Figures 1a-d] showed lesion enlargement compared to the previous MRI, with increased edema and midline shift. Glycerol infusion was initiated to reduce intracranial pressure. Although the lesion’s ring-enhancing, multi-focal appearance strongly suggested glioblastoma, we also considered PCNSL given the patient’s rapid clinical decline.

A ring-enhancing lesion and extensive edema are observed, causing a mild midline shift. Although the patient already had profound aphasia and right-sided paralysis, there was no abrupt deterioration in consciousness level.

Initial management

We initially planned an early stereotactic biopsy, suspecting PCNSL as a differential. However, on the 1st day at the hospital, the patient’s D-dimer level was 7.53 μg/mL, and a CT scan revealed bilateral lower-leg deep venous thrombosis (DVT) and a non-massive pulmonary thromboembolism in the right pulmonary artery branches. Due to the elevated anesthesia and surgical risk, we started a direct oral anticoagulant (DOAC; apixaban) for thrombosis; once stabilized, we would proceed with a robot-guided biopsy under general anesthesia.

On hospital day 2 (day 23 of symptoms), her level of consciousness worsened, and a repeat MRI revealed increased edema; we administered dexamethasone, which improved her symptoms. Two days later (day 25), soluble interleukin-2 receptor rose to 1,172 U/mL, further supporting the likelihood of lymphoma. CT and MRI performed 48 h after steroid initiation showed tumor shrinkage, consistent with PCNSL’s characteristic steroid responsiveness; however, there was now an intratumoral hemorrhage along with the left putaminal bleeding [Figures 1e-h]. The midline shift increased slightly from 10 mm to 11 mm, but no abrupt neurological deterioration was seen – her deficit was already severe. Although the hematoma volume was 20 mL, it caused significant mass effect with worsening herniation and was, therefore, considered clinically massive. This situation called for both urgent decompression and a definitive diagnosis. Because the tumor lay deep in the dominant hemisphere, we deemed a single craniotomy approach for both resection/biopsy and hematoma evacuation too risky. Instead, we planned two separate surgical trajectories to conduct stereotactic biopsy and concurrent hematoma removal [Figures 2a and b].

Steroids and apixaban were discontinued, and we administered andexanet alfa to reverse the anticoagulation. After confirming no hematoma expansion for 48 h, we proceeded to surgery on hospital day 6 (day 27). Intraoperative neurophysiological monitoring was not feasible due to the patient’s pre-existing severe aphasia and hemiparesis. However, to avoid further neurological deterioration, we carefully selected a surgical strategy that minimized the risk of additional deficits. Under the guidance of a frameless robotic system (Medtronic Stealth Autoguide), we performed a small burr hole craniotomy for deep-seated stereotactic needle biopsy using a 2.2 mm diameter needle, followed by a left frontotemporal craniotomy with a trans–Sylvian fissure approach to evacuate the left putaminal hematoma. Intraoperative frozen pathology strongly suggested malignant lymphoma, so we avoided aggressive tumor resection and removed approximately 70% of the hematoma [Figure 2c]. The hematoma volume was estimated at 20 mL preoperatively and was reduced to 6 mL on postoperative imaging. Further surgical resection of the residual tumor was not pursued, as PCNSL typically responds well to chemotherapy and radiotherapy, and the lesion’s deep location within the dominant basal ganglia posed a high risk of neurological morbidity. No new neurological deficits emerged postoperatively. Postoperative imaging showed an improvement in midline shift from 11 mm preoperatively to 8 mm. MRI confirmed adequate tumor sampling [Figure 2d].

Postoperative course

Final histopathology revealed CNS-DLBCL. The patient underwent eight cycles of high-dose methotrexate plus rituximab, resulting in tumor shrinkage. She then received whole-brain radiation (30 Gy) followed by a 10 Gy/5-fractions local boost; subsequent MRI showed no noticeable enhancing lesions. Her aphasia partially improved, allowing for some word-level speech, and her right-sided hemiparesis improved to MMT 3/5 in the upper extremity and 2/5 in the lower extremity. After discharge, the patient was transferred to a private nursing facility, and no further clinical or imaging follow-up data were available, as she was no longer under the direct care of our institution. This case report was conducted in accordance with the ethical guidelines for medical and health research involving human subjects in Japan. According to our hospital’s policy, individual Institutional Review Board approval was not required for single case reports. Publication-related information was disclosed through an opt-out process, and written informed consent for publication was obtained from the patient and her family.

Histopathologic examination

A diffuse proliferation of large atypical lymphoid cells was observed [Figures 3a and b]. The tumor cells were positive for the B-cell marker CD20 [Figure 3c] and negative for the T-cell marker CD3 [Figure 3d]. Ki-67 staining revealed a high proliferation index [Figure 3e]. Vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) immunoreactivity was detected in the cytoplasm of tumor cells [Figure 3f].

DISCUSSION

We present a rare occurrence of PCNSL with acute massive intratumoral hemorrhage, which we successfully managed through a combined stereotactic biopsy and open craniotomy. PCNSL is a highly malignant yet chemo-sensitive tumor, often deteriorating rapidly if untreated. Standard management prioritizes confirming the diagnosis through minimally invasive biopsy and then promptly starting chemotherapy, since extensive surgical resection has not been shown to provide a survival benefit in PCNSL.[1] Recent multicenter retrospective studies have reported prolonged overall survival in selected younger patients (<65 years) and those with superficial, solitary lesions who underwent resection compared to those managed with biopsy alone.[18] However, as these findings are subject to selection bias and the inherent limitations of retrospective analyses, current guidelines continue to recommend resection only in life-threatening situations.[2]

In our case, the lesion was deep in the dominant basal ganglia, a location where open surgery or resection would carry high risk. Under normal circumstances, a stereotactic biopsy alone would have been the preferred approach for diagnosis. However, the development of a large hemorrhage mandated urgent decompression to relieve intracranial pressure. This posed a therapeutic dilemma: how to decompress the hematoma while still obtaining a diagnostic tissue sample from a deep-seated lymphoma, all without causing additional harm. Our solution was to perform both procedures in the same operative session – a strategy not previously described in the literature for hemorrhagic PCNSL. Using a Medtronic Stealth Autoguide frameless robotic system, we first performed a stereotactic biopsy through a small burr hole to target the deep tumor core. Importantly, we obtained a frozen-section diagnosis during surgery: once lymphoma was identified, we avoided any attempt at total resection of the enhancing lesion.

Debulking lymphoma is unnecessary and could risk new deficits. Instead, we proceeded with a limited open craniotomy to evacuate the intratumoral hematoma and reduce mass effect. This combined approach achieved both goals – diagnosis and decompression – in one operation. The use of robot-assisted navigation was critical for accuracy and efficiency: contemporary series report diagnostic yields of approximately 96–99% with such systems[24,13,23,25] and low rates of morbidity (permanent neurological complications <3%).[13,23] Performing the biopsy before opening the cranium also helped mitigate the issue of brain shift (which can average ~1 cm during craniotomy),[15] thereby ensuring our biopsy target remained accurate. Relying only on tissue from the hematoma cavity or attempted blind resection could have led to inadequate viable tumor for diagnosis as blood clot and necrosis predominated the cavity. Thus, the combined biopsy + evacuation technique was pivotal for optimal management. Notably, the procedure was completed safely with no new postoperative deficits, illustrating the feasibility of this innovative strategy. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first reported case of a simultaneous stereotactic biopsy and hematoma evacuation for a hemorrhagic brain tumor, adding a new paradigm in neurosurgical management of such cases.

Although PCNSL classically does not present with hemorrhage, this case underscores that significant bleeding can occur under certain conditions. Only a few case reports document PCNSL with intracerebral hemorrhage [Table 1], and such presentations are considered extremely rare. A stroke-like hemorrhagic onset in an immunocompetent patient is so atypical for lymphoma that it often leads clinicians toward other diagnoses initially.[11] It is now recognized; however, that small foci of hemorrhage or hemosiderin deposition are sometimes present in PCNSL if one looks carefully (e.g., with susceptibility-weighted MRI). Such microhemorrhages are usually clinically silent and do not exclude the diagnosis of lymphoma.[14,17]

In our patient, by contrast, the hemorrhage was acute, large, and symptomatic – an event that occurred after PCNSL was already suspected and after certain therapies had begun. We initiated high-dose dexamethasone on admission to reduce life-threatening edema, and also initiated an anticoagulant (apixaban, a DOAC) due to the discovery of extensive DVT and pulmonary embolism (PE). We propose that this combination of factors significantly contributed to the massive bleeding.

Corticosteroids are known to cause rapid cytoreduction and necrosis in lymphomas, which might have rendered the tumor’s vasculature fragile. Yamada et al.[22] reported that PCNSL pathology can show numerous fragile, thin-walled microvessels that predispose the tumor to hemorrhage. Our patient’s tumor cells also showed high expression of VEGF, a pro-angiogenic factor that has been linked to intracerebral bleeding in PCNSL cases.[16]

In addition to such tumor-specific risks, the patient’s anticoagulation therapy would be expected to elevate the chance of any intracranial hemorrhage. Patients with brain tumors have a known susceptibility to venous thromboembolism (occurring in up to 20–30% of cases),[6,21] and treating such patients with anticoagulants will balance preventing thrombosis against the danger of intracerebral bleeding.

Notably, even in patients with brain metastases post anticoagulation treatment, studies report a ~5–10% incidence of intracranial hemorrhage within 1 year.[10] Our case aligns with these concerns: the hemorrhage occurred shortly after starting apixaban, suggesting the anticoagulation to have been a precipitating factor (though it was medically necessary).

Although reversing anticoagulation may increase the risk of thromboembolic events such as PE or DVT, we carefully weighed these risks against the potential for hematoma progression and life-threatening herniation and, therefore, decided to administer andexanet alfa. This chain of events highlights the clinical dilemma of managing concomitant brain tumors and coagulopathies. Clinicians should remain vigilant for hemorrhagic complications when using anticoagulants in patients with intracranial neoplasms, even those like PCNSL that are traditionally thought to bleed rarely.

This case illustrates an important management strategy for a complex scenario. It emphasizes caution when anticoagulation and steroids are required in patients with brain tumors, as even typically non-hemorrhagic tumors like PCNSL can unpredictably bleed under such conditions. It also showcases the utility of combining advanced surgical techniques to address competing priorities: obtaining a diagnosis and alleviating mass effect. By leveraging robotic stereotactic guidance, we safely integrated a biopsy with an emergent craniotomy, avoiding the need for separate procedures on different days. Early diagnosis through frozen section allowed us to tailor the surgical extent, which likely prevented unnecessary brain injury. The patient subsequently received appropriate chemo-immunotherapy and had a favorable recovery, which we attribute in part to the timely diagnosis and decompression achieved by our approach.

One limitation of this report is the lack of long-term follow-up, as the patient was transferred to a private nursing facility and could not be monitored beyond the initial treatment period. However, we were able to confirm that the patient was alive 1 year after surgery. This limitation restricts our ability to draw conclusions about long-term outcomes or functional recovery. While this is a single case and such hemorrhagic presentations of PCNSL are exceedingly uncommon, our experience suggests a proof of concept: In rare situations, a multidisciplinary surgical plan can be carried out safely to both treat the complication and confirm the pathology.

Further case accumulation and multicenter studies will be needed to assess the safety, feasibility, and generalizability of this combined surgical approach. Going forward, the integration of robotic tools in neurosurgery may enable similar combined interventions for other challenging cases. Further reports or series will be helpful to determine the broader applicability and outcomes of this strategy.

CONCLUSION

Robot-guided stereotactic biopsy combined with craniotomy enabled both precise diagnosis and effective decompression in hemorrhagic PCNSL. This case underscores the value of advanced neurosurgical approaches and vigilant risk management for patients with lymphoma and hemorrhagic complications. Further studies are warranted to evaluate the safety and utility of similar combined surgical approaches in comparable hemorrhagic cases of PCNSL.
