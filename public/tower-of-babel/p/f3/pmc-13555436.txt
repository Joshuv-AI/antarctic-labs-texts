Atrioventricular Valve Replacement With Monovalvatation Technique for Single Ventricle With Two Atrioventricular Valve Orifices
Authors: Hisashi Yoshida, Takeshi Shinkawa, Akiko Yamagata, Hiroshi Niinami
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Atrioventricular valve (AVV) replacement for patients with single-ventricle physiology and 2 AVV orifices can be technically challenging when both annuli are too small to place adequately sized prosthesis. We report 2 adult Fontan patients who underwent AVV replacement with monovalvatation technique, in which 2 AVV orifices were integrated into a single annulus by incising an interventricular septum between 2 AVV orifices. They had prior AVV replacement with relatively small prosthesis or AVV repair with complete annuloplasty in childhood. Postoperative courses were uneventful, with good prosthetic function. This technique can be an effective surgical option in such anatomically challenging cases.

Approximately 9% of Fontan patients require atrioventricular valve (AVV) surgery after the Fontan operation.1 AVV replacement in an adult with 2 AVV orifices after Fontan completion can be challenging, because implantation of an adequately sized prosthesis can be limited by a small annulus or previous surgical procedures. Because most previous repairs/replacements are performed in childhood, the AVV orifices may become fixed in size, particularly after complete annuloplasty, ring placement, or valve replacement. As these patients grow into adulthood, the fixed annular dimension maybe too small for an adult-sized prosthesis. However, implantation of a prosthesis that is large enough in size is crucial to prevent prosthesis-patient mismatch, valve malfunction, and long-term morbidity. Case Reports

Patient 1

Patient 1 was 24-year-old woman, weighing 49 kg, with heterotaxy syndrome, double-outlet right ventricle, and hypoplastic left ventricle. She had surgical histories of modified Blalock-Taussig shunt at age 6 years, Glenn procedure, mitral and tricuspid valve repair at 7 years, Fontan operation (extracardiac-conduit total cavopulmonary connection), and subsequent mitral and tricuspid valve replacements with 16 and 20 ATS mechanical prostheses (Medtronic) soon after the Fontan operation due to uncontrollable regurgitations at 9 years. At age 22 years, she presented with sick sinus syndrome, severe patient-prosthesis mismatch, with tricuspid and mitral valve stenoses (mean gradient of 11.7 mm Hg at the mitral prosthesis and 11.2 mm Hg at the tricuspid prosthesis), and protein-losing enteropathy. She was indicated for AVV repeat replacement with pacemaker implantation. Preoperative computed tomography revealed tricuspid and mitral prosthetic valves were aligned and facing anteriorly (Figure 2A).Figure 2(A) Preoperative computed tomography revealed tricuspid (20mm) and mitral (16mm) prosthesis valves were aligned and facing anteriorly. (B) Postoperative computed tomography showed new prosthesis at the single integrated annulus.

(A) Preoperative computed tomography revealed tricuspid (20mm) and mitral (16mm) prosthesis valves were aligned and facing anteriorly. (B) Postoperative computed tomography showed new prosthesis at the single integrated annulus.

At the operation, a redo sternotomy was performed and a normothermic cardiopulmonary bypass was established with bicaval cannulation. After aortic cross-clamp and adequate prostheses and pannus resection, the tricuspid and mitral orifices looked smallish, with severe calcification, and were measured as 14 and 18 mm, respectively. After explanting the prosthetic valves, a relatively large ventricular septal defect (VSD) was identified beneath the common annulus of the 2 AVV orifices. Consequently, the tricuspid/mitral common annulus was incised toward the VSD to create 1 large AVV orifice. An AVV repeat replacement with a 25/33 On-X mechanical prosthesis (Artivion) was performed without additional VSD enlargement. Although a pacemaker implantation was planned preoperatively, an atrioventricular block developed.

Her postoperative course was uneventful. The postoperative echocardiography revealed a mean AVV gradient of 3 mm Hg, and the postoperative computed tomography showed new prosthesis at the single integrated annulus (Figure 2B). She was discharged home on postoperative day 27 with no recurrence of protein-losing enteropathy.

Patient 2

Patient 2 was 35-year-old man, weighting 45 kg, with double-inlet right ventricle, double-outlet right ventricle, and hypoplastic left ventricle. He had surgical histories of original Blalock-Taussig shunt at age 1 year and an atriopulmonary connection Fontan operation with mitral annuloplasty at 7 years. At age 34 years, atrial arrhythmia and severe tricuspid (annulus diameter: 13 mm) and moderate mitral (annulus diameter: 16 mm) regurgitations developed, and was indicated for total cavopulmonary connection conversion with AVV replacement. At the operation, both AVV orifices were small, with mitral annulus calcification due to previous annuloplasty, and measured as 10 mm and 20 mm. The common tricuspid/mitral annulus was incised to create 1 AVV orifice, and AVV replacement with 25/33 On-X mechanical prosthesis (Artivion) was performed with no VSD enlargement. He was discharged home on postoperative day 21 with no atrioventricular block. The postoperative mean AVV gradient by echocardiogram was 4 mm Hg, and the postoperative computed tomography showed a prosthesis placed by the mono-valvatation technique (Figure 3B).

Comment

The AVV anomaly is associated with an increased risk of failure of the Fontan circulation and mortality.2 Morgan and colleagues3 reported that mechanical AVV replacement in Fontan patients with preserved ventricular function salvages approximately half of patients, with substantially improved survival outcomes early after replacement. However, a multicenter report from Japan noted that among 56 Fontan patients with AVV replacement, 36% died during follow-up (3.7 [SD, 2.6] years) with younger age and smaller prosthesis emerging as risk factors for death.4 These findings highlight the importance of implanting a large enough sized prosthesis in Fontan patients.

Several techniques have been suggested to address the size mismatch between the annulus and prosthesis. Supra-annular valve replacement using a polytetrafluoroethylene tube graft is one of the solutions. Sung and colleagues5 proposed that one of advantages of supra-annular valve replacement is the avoidance of direct insertion of a rigid mechanical ring into a small and friable annulus. Even though their report was for a small child, they claimed their technique can also be applied to adult patients with a small mitral annulus. However, there is a concern of creating stagnant flow around the valve in the left atrium leading to left atrial thrombus formation.

In addition, Yang and colleagues6 reported that the mean pressure gradient of a mitral valve may remain high in patients with a small mitral annulus after supra-annular mitral valve replacement. They revealed that follow-up echocardiography showed moderate-severe stenosis of the mitral valve prosthesis (mean pressure gradient >10 mm Hg) in the short-term. They suspected that blood flowing through the valve may impact the atrial wall under the composite graft and create a turbulent flow that generates a high-pressure gradient. Nevertheless, midterm results are sufficient, with no atrioventricular block and a 5-year survival rate of 75%.

In our patients, we adopted the monovalvatation technique, excising pannus and subvalvular apparatus as much as possible and incising the common tricuspid/mitral annulus to create a single integrated annulus. This approach enabled implantation of an adult-sized prosthesis in the “true” AVV annulus, with low postoperative gradients, while theoretically optimizing inflow hemodynamics compared with the supra-annular valve replacement. Importantly, we avoided VSD enlargement to reduce the risk of conduction system injury. Although atrioventricular block developed in 1 patient, this was most likely related to the high-risk nature of valve repeat replacement rather than to the septal incision.7 We believe careful incision of the muscle band toward the preexisting VSD and avoiding VSD enlargement will minimize injury of the conduction system.

In conclusion, AVV replacement with the monovalvatation technique enabled implanting a sufficiently large prosthesis in 2 Fontan patients with too small AVV orifices. This technique provides favorable hemodynamics and can be a good surgical option.
