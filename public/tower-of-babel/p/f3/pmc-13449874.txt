Association between intraoperative cerebral desaturation detected by near-infrared spectroscopy and early postoperative cognitive changes in patients undergoing laparoscopic major oncologic surgery: a preliminary exploratory study
Authors: Hazal Özkaya Hız, Dilek Kalaycı, Seher Altınel, Süheyla Ünver, Özlem Şen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe Trendelenburg position and pneumoperitoneum cause cardiovascular, cerebrovascular, metabolic, and respiratory changes. This preliminary study aimed to evaluate the effects of the Trendelenburg position and pneumoperitoneum on cerebral tissue oxygen saturation (rSO₂) and their association with early postoperative cognitive changes in patients undergoing laparoscopic major abdominal oncological surgery.MethodsThis preliminary exploratory study included 55 patients scheduled for elective laparoscopic major abdominal cancer surgery. Preoperative cognitive status was assessed using the Mini-Mental Test (MMT). Intraoperatively, regional cerebral oxygen saturation (rSO₂) was monitored using near-infrared spectroscopy (NIRS). Cerebral desaturation was defined as a decrease of ≥20% from baseline rSO₂ lasting at least 15 seconds. Hemodynamic parameters, pneumoperitoneum pressure, and Trendelenburg angle were recorded. Hemodynamic parameters and rSO₂ were recorded at 16 predefined time points: baseline (T0), after preoxygenation (T-preox), after induction (T-postind), after CO₂ insufflation (Ti), after Trendelenburg position (Tt), and every 20 minutes intraoperatively. Cognitive function was re-evaluated using the MMT one week postoperatively. Early postoperative cognitive change was defined as either a decrease of 2 or more points in the postoperative day 7 MMT score compared with baseline, or a postoperative MMT score of 23 and below.ResultsA significant decrease in rSO₂ was observed after pneumoperitoneum and the Trendelenburg position. Patients were divided into two groups: those with a >20% decrease in rSO₂ (Group 2, n=6) and those without (Group 1, n=47). Group 2 had significantly lower median rSO₂ values at baseline and most intraoperative time points compared to Group 1 (p<0.05), which may be related to underlying cardiac comorbidities. The incidence of early postoperative cognitive changes was 11.3% (6/53 patients). All patients in Group 2 (100%) developed early postoperative cognitive changes, compared to 17% in Group 1 (p=0.002).Postoperative MMT scores were significantly lower in Group 2 (22.6 ± 2.2 vs. 26.1 ± 2.4, p=0.002). A negative correlation was found between postoperative MMT scores and age, ASA score, and comorbidity (p<0.05).ConclusionsIn conclusion, although the Trendelenburg position and pneumoperitoneum are generally expected to increase cerebral oxygenation, this may not be evident in high-risk patients with cardiac compromise. In this specific population, these factors may pose a significant risk for cerebral desaturation and appear associated with early postoperative cognitive changes. Therefore, monitoring rSO₂ using NIRS should be considered a beneficial tool for identifying vulnerable patients and potentially reducing the risk of cognitive decline.Trial registrationClinical Trial: NCT04714346.

Introduction

Laparoscopic major abdominal cancer surgery requires a combination of pneumoperitoneum and Trendelenburg positioning to optimize surgical exposure. Although these positions are mandatory for the surgical procedure, they lead to a number of physiological challenges, especially in oncology patients. In this patient group, factors such as advanced age and cardiovascular comorbidities may impair physiological reserve and increase the risk of postoperative complications, including cognitive changes.

The most prominent hemodynamic changes due to pneumoperitoneum and Trendelenburg position are observed after insufflation [1]. The increase in intra-abdominal pressure due to the pneumoperitoneum causes a decrease in venous return and cardiac output. The negative cardiovascular effects of pneumoperitoneum are alleviated to some extent by the effects of the Trendelenburg position. Complex changes occur in brain perfusion during prolonged Trendelenburg position and pneumoperitoneum. These complications are rare in short-term interventions in healthy people, but are more likely to occur in older, comorbid patients undergoing long, complicated surgery [2].These changes are likely to affect cerebral dysfunction and early postoperative cognitive changes. Therefore, monitoring of cerebral tissue oxygen saturation during the operation may be valuable [3]. The method known as Near Infrared spectroscopy (NIRS) offers a stable, non-invasive and safe method of detecting cerebral desaturation. Monitoring intraoperative cerebral oxygen saturation may help identify patients at higher risk for postoperative cognitive changes. This may help reduce postoperative cognitive issues and decrease the length of hospital stay.

The aim of this study is to observe the NIRS changes due to the Trendelenburg position and pneumoperitoneum in patients undergoing laparoscopic major abdominal oncologic surgery and to evaluate their association with early postoperative cognitive changes.

Materials and methods

The study was conducted after approval from the Ethics Committee of SBÜ Ankara Dr. Abdurrahman Yurtaslan Oncology Education and Research Hospital (Protocol No: 2019-08/384, Date: 07.08.2019, Clinical Trial: NCT04714346). This study was designed as a preliminary, prospective, and exploratory observational study was conducted between August 2019 and August 2020. Fifty-five patients scheduled for oncologic surgery, classified as ASA I-IV, aged 18 years and above, and scoring 24 or higher on the MMT test at baseline were included in the study. The sample size calculation was based on the study by Casati et al. ( 4) with a 5% margin of error and 90% power. Accounting for a potential 30% patient loss, 52 consecutive patients were planned to be enrolled; ultimately, 55 patients were included. The Mini-Mental Test (MMT) was used as a screening tool to evaluate early cognitive changes. Early postoperative cognitive changes were assessed by comparing preoperative and postoperative MMT scores.In our study, early postoperative cognitive change was defined as a decline of ≥ 2 points in the MMT score on postoperative day 7 compared to the baseline, or a total score of ≤ 23, which is a widely accepted threshold for cognitive impairment in the literature [4].

Written informed consent forms were obtained from all participants. Preoperative demographic data, comorbidities, American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical score, agents used for anesthesia induction and their doses, diagnosis, type and duration of operation, Trendelenburg position angles, pneumoperitoneum pressure, and amount of intraoperative blood loss were recorded. After administering the preoperative mini-mental test (MMT), Cerebral oxygen saturation (rSO₂) was continuously monitored throughout the surgical procedure using a near-infrared spectroscopy (NIRS) device (INVOS 5100 C; Somanetics, Troy, MI, USA). Sensors were placed on the bilateral frontal areas of the patient’s forehead before induction. Preoperative baseline rSO₂ values were recorded while the patient was awake and breathing room air before anesthesia induction. To account for inter-individual variability, each patient served as their own control, and cerebral desaturation was defined as a decrease of ≥ 20% in rSO₂ relative to the individual baseline value, consistent with the methodology described by Casati et al. [4], to exclude transient artifacts, this reduction was required to be sustained for at least 15 consecutive seconds to be recorded as a desaturation event. In addition, invasive arterial blood pressure was monitored via the radial artery using a calibrated transducer .Vital parameters (at specific intervals), demographic data, comorbidities, anesthesia method, operation time, and amount of bleeding were also recorded. MMT was repeated in the 1st week postoperatively. The relationship between NIRS changes and MMT changes in patients with cerebral tissue oxygen desaturation was examined.

Perioperative NIRS measurement values (rSO₂-right / rSO₂-left), invasive arterial pressure (IAP), mean arterial pressure (MAP), heart rate (HR), and peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO₂) were recorded at the following intervals .All parameters were recorded at the following time points: T0 (baseline), T-preox (after preoxygenation), T-postind (after induction), Ti (after CO₂ insufflation), Tt (after Trendelenburg positioning), and every 20 min thereafter up to T80 (T20, T40, T60, T80), as well as after returning to the supine position (Ts) and after desufflation (Td). In the desaturation group, additional measurements were recorded at the time of desaturation (Tdo1–Tdo3). The duration of cerebral desaturation was defined as the cumulative time during which rSO₂ values remained ≥ 20% below the individual baseline. The duration of cerebral desaturation was defined as the cumulative time during which rSO₂ values remained ≥ 20% below the individual baseline, calculated by documenting the precise onset (when rSO₂ first dropped ≥ 20% from baseline for ≥ 15 s) and recovery times (when rSO₂ returned to within < 20% of baseline) of each desaturation episode. Arterial blood gas measurements of pH, PaO₂, PaCO₂, and EtCO₂ were recorded after insufflation and at the time of desaturation or at the 30th minute of pneumoperitoneum. Intraoperative management during episodes of cerebral desaturation was based on routine clinical practice, and no standardized NIRS-guided intervention protocol was applied. Due to the observational design of our study, intraoperative management during episodes of cerebral desaturation was left to the discretion of the attending anesthesiologist, and routine clinical practices were performed. Responses to desaturation included optimizing mean arterial pressure, adjusting ventilation parameters (FiO₂,tidal volume, respiratory rate), reducing pneumoperitoneum pressure, or modifying patient positioning as deemed appropriate by the clinical team.

Intraoperative fluid management was standardized for all patients in accordance with our institutional protocol. Isotonic crystalloid solution (0.9% NaCl) was administered at a rate of 6–8 ml/kg/h. Additional fluid boluses were given based on clinical judgment (e.g., mean arterial pressure < 60 mmHg or heart rate > 100 bpm). The total volume of administered fluid was recorded for each patient. To standardize postoperative analgesia, all patients received 1 g of paracetamol and 100 mg of tramadol intravenously 30 min before the end of the surgery. In the recovery room, patients with a Visual Analog Scale (VAS) score > 4 were planned to receive rescue analgesia. None of the patients in either group required additional opioid administration in the early postoperative period. The intra-abdominal pressure was maintained at a standardized level of 12 mmHg throughout the laparoscopic procedure.

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 20.0. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation, median (minimum–maximum), or n(%), as appropriate. Continuous variables (e.g., age, BMI, MMT) were compared using the Student’s t-test. Categorical variables (e.g., ASA scores) were compared using the Chi-square or Fisher’s exact test. All correlations were unadjusted and assessed using the Spearman correlation test. Univariable logistic regression was performed to evaluate the association between cerebral desaturation (≥ 20% rSO₂ reduction) and early postoperative cognitive changes, and results are presented as odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). Given the small number of patients in the desaturation group, we limited our regression analysis to univariable assessments to maintain statistical reliability. The sample size (n = 55) was based on feasibility and the anticipated incidence of cerebral desaturation in this surgical population. With only 6 patients experiencing cerebral desaturation (10.9%), the study may be underpowered to detect statistically significant associations with narrow confidence intervals. Therefore, findings should be considered preliminary and hypothesis-generating rather than definitive. The wide confidence intervals observed in our logistic regression analyses reflect this limitation. Future adequately powered studies are required to confirm these preliminary observations.

In Group 2, cerebral desaturation was defined as a ≥ 20% reduction in rSO₂. The mean cumulative duration of cerebral desaturation in these patients was 17.0 ± 3.1 min, calculated based on the precise onset and recovery times of each episode. No desaturation was observed in any patient during the early period, from baseline (T0), including preoxygenation (T-preox), until after anesthesia induction (T-postind).Desaturations started at the earliest with CO2 insufflation (Ti) and the assumption of the Trendelenburg (Tt) position. Most of the desaturation events were concentrated in the process between Tt(Trendelenburg) and T80. In the six patients classified as having cerebral desaturation (≥ 20% rSO₂ reduction in either hemisphere), the maximum decrease ranged from 18 to 28% on the right and 17–25% on the left.The lowest recorded rSO₂ values were 44.3 ± 3.4 on the right and 46.3 ± 6.6 on the left.

Comorbidities were similar between the groups (Table 2). Although not statistically significant, the incidence of cardiovascular disease was higher in Group 2.

Discussion

In this preliminary exploratory study, six of 53 patients (11.3%) undergoing laparoscopic major abdominal oncologic surgery experienced a ≥ 20% decrease in rSO₂ lasting a mean duration of 17.0 ± 3.1 min during intraoperative NIRS monitoring. The incidence of early postoperative cognitive changes detected by the Mini-Mental Test was found 11.3%. Our findings suggest that the application of the Trendelenburg position and pneumoperitoneum during laparoscopic major oncologic surgery may be associated with alterations in cerebral oxygenation.

It has been reported that during laparoscopic surgery, both the Trendelenburg position and CO₂ pneumoperitoneum may affect cerebral oxygen metabolism and lead to secondary neurological changes due to alterations in intracranial pressure (ICP) and cerebral blood flow (CBF) [2]. In healthy populations previous studies, such as the one by Kalmar et al. [5], have reported an increase in regional cerebral oxygen saturation (rSO₂) but this physiological mechanism may differ in high-risk groups. Intraoperative NIRS monitoring may provide additional information about cerebral oxygenation changes in patients who appear to be more vulnerable during surgery. Lee et al. [6] reported a significant decrease in rSO₂ after Trendelenburg positioning, with desaturation improving after position correction. While Harvey et al. [7] suggested that the decrease in rSO₂ may result from impaired venous return. Furthermore, Chen et al. [8] reported that cerebral perfusion pressure decreases if the position is maintained for more than 165 min Consistent with these findings, we observed a statistically significant decrease in rSO₂ after Trendelenburg positioning. This decrease may be related to factors such as impaired lung mechanics, patient age, ASA physical status, and accompanying comorbidities. In our study, the mean Trendelenburg time in the desaturation group was 138.3 ± 30.6 min. The mean Trendelenburg angle in the desaturation group was 24.2° ± 7.3°, which was comparable to Group 1 (25.1° ± 7.6°, p = 0.782). Despite the absence of a significant difference in Trendelenburg angle between the groups, the advanced age of the patients and the high prevalence of cardiovascular comorbidities (83.3%) in Group 2 may have contributed to the occurrence of desaturation in this vulnerable population.

During pneumoperitoneum PaCO₂ is the primary regulator of cerebral arterial tone; hypercapnia causes cerebral vasodilation, while hypocapnia may lead to cerebral hypoxia due to vasoconstriction, even with normal oxygen saturation [9]. In our study, the patients remained normocapnic. Unlike most reports in healthy populations, we observed a decrease in rSO₂ in Group 2 after pneumoperitoneum. In the desaturation group, the mean PaCO₂ in simultaneously drawn blood gases was 43 mmHg. We found no correlation between EtCO₂ and rSO₂.The characteristics of the patient group we studied may have been influential in this difference. The vast majority of studies in the literature have reported that rSO2 is maintained or increased during pneumoperitoneum and Trendelenburg positioning in healthy or low-risk patient groups [10, 11]. In contrast, Özgün et al. found that rSO2 values decreased significantly at 20 and 60 min during surgery in the Trendelenburg position and determined that cerebral saturation decreased as age increased [12]. In this specific patient population, the combination of the venous load created by the Trendelenburg position and the effect of pneumoperitoneum, instead of providing the expected increase in cerebral perfusion, may have led to a decrease in rSO₂ values by straining the pumping capacity of the heart.This situation suggests that NIRS monitoring may be more stimulatory compared to routine follow-up parameters, especially in high-risk oncology patients.

Current literature continues to demonstrate the relationship between intraoperative cerebral desaturation and postoperative cognitive changes, but findings remain heterogeneous. A 2024 systematic review emphasized that NIRS effectively measures brain oxygen saturation noninvasively and may be useful in preventing complications such as postoperative cognitive dysfunction, especially in high-risk surgical patients [13].In contrast, a large-scale retrospective study found that while age, cerebrovascular disease, and preoperative cognitive impairment were associated with delirium, neither baseline nor intraoperative rSO2 parameters were directly associated with postoperative delirium [14].These findings demonstrate the ongoing debates regarding the clinical utility of NIRS monitoring and indicate the need for standardized intervention protocols and outcomes.

A reduction in rSO₂ of 20% or more has been associated with an increased risk of postoperative neurological and cognitive deterioration. Trafidlo et al. demonstrated that postoperative cognitive performance was better in patients in whom cerebral desaturation was detected and promptly addressed [15]. Casati et al. [4] reported an association between NIRS monitoring and reduced cognitive impairment, Similarly, De Tournay-Jetté et al. [16] showed that intraoperative cerebral oxygen desaturation was associated with early and late postoperative cognitive decline in elderly patients. Accordingly, prolonged rSO₂ desaturation may reflect increased vulnerability to postoperative cognitive changes. In our study, although significant declines were observed in both rSO2 and MMT scores, no direct statistical correlation was found between the severity of intraoperative desaturation and the degree of postoperative cognitive change (p > 0.05). Therefore, our findings should not be interpreted as a dose-response relationship between the severity of desaturation and the severity of cognitive change. In the absence of a significant correlation, we believe that it is primarily due to the limited sample size, which restricts the statistical power. Furthermore, a standardized NIRS-guided intervention protocol was not applied during desaturation episodes; instead, intraoperative management was determined by the clinical judgment of the anesthesiologist in the operating room. This lack of a standardized protocol may have influenced the duration of desaturation and subsequent cognitive outcomes. potentially affecting the reproducibility of our findings.

In our study, preoperative rSO2 levels were lower in the desaturation group compared to the non-desaturation group. Low preoperative baseline rSO2 levels may have contributed to this finding. We consider that the lower baseline rSO2 values in Group 2 may have been caused by the significantly higher prevalence of cardiovascular comorbidities (83.3% vs. lower rates in Group 1), ASA III or higher classification, and advanced age. Cardiovascular diseases, especially with aging, may compromise baseline cerebral oxygen delivery through reduced cardiac output, atherosclerotic changes in cerebral vasculature associated with aging, and impaired compensatory mechanisms; this may have predisposed the patients in this group to more pronounced desaturation when exposed to the hemodynamic stresses of Trendelenburg positioning and pneumoperitoneum. Additionally, the lack of recovery in rSO2 values after position correction or desufflation in this group supports our consideration that pre-existing cardiovascular and cerebrovascular disorders may play a significant role. Consistent with previous reports, age, comorbidity, and ASA physical status were associated with postoperative cognitive outcomes [17, 18].

This study has several important limitations. The primary limitation is the small sample size (n = 55) and low event rate (n = 6 desaturation cases, 10.9%), which limited statistical power and resulted in wide confidence intervals (OR = 24.3, 95% CI: 2.2–264.1). This underpowered design precludes definitive conclusions, and our findings should be considered preliminary and hypothesis-generating. The limited sample also precluded multivariable regression analysis to adjust for confounders such as age, ASA status, and comorbidities. We acknowledge that the Mini-Mental Test (MMT) has limited sensitivity for detecting subtle cognitive dysfunction and does not replace comprehensive neuropsychological testing. Performing the MMT assessment on the 7th postoperative day constitutes another limitation of our study. This single assessment conducted within the first postoperative week may have overlooked potential changes in cognitive functions occurring at different times. In future studies, the use of assessments at multiple time points (e.g., postoperative days 1, 3, and 7) may provide more comprehensive information about the relationship between early postoperative cognitive changes and intraoperative cerebral desaturation. Additionally, education levels were not adjusted for in the MMT analysis. A standardized NIRS-guided intervention protocol was not applied during desaturation episodes, which may have influenced desaturation duration and outcomes. Preoperative carotid Doppler ultrasonography and longer-term follow-up could have provided additional information regarding baseline cerebrovascular status and cognitive status.

Conclusion

In conclusion, although Trendelenburg positioning and pneumoperitoneum are generally expected to increase cerebral oxygenation, this response may not occur in high-risk patients with cardiac compromise. In this specific group, these intraoperative factors may pose a risk for cerebral desaturation and appear to be associated with early postoperative cognitive changes. Therefore, monitoring rSO₂ using NIRS should be considered a useful tool for identifying vulnerable patients and potentially reducing the risk of cognitive decline. However, given the small number of patients with desaturation in this study (n = 6), these findings should be viewed as preliminary and hypothesis-generating. Further studies with larger and more balanced cohorts are required to confirm these associations.
