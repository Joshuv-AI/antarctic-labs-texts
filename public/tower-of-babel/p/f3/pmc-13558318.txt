Combining Guselkumab with Upadacitinib Is Safe and Effective in the Treatment of Medically Complex Inflammatory Bowel Disease
Authors: Navneet Natt, David Choi, Asher Shafrir, Sushila R Dalal, Noa Krugliak Cleveland, Russell D Cohen, David T Rubin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and AimsDual-targeted therapy (DTT) combines medications with different anti-inflammatory mechanisms to manage immune-mediated conditions. We describe our experience of adding guselkumab (GUS) to upadacitinib (UPA) in complex inflammatory bowel disease (IBD).MethodsWe reviewed our real-world registry of adult patients with IBD who had GUS added to UPA. Prospectively collected outcomes included clinical remission (Harvey–Bradshaw Index (HBI) < 5 or Simple Clinical Colitis Activity Index (SCCAI) < 3), biochemical remission (normalization of fecal calprotectin (FCP)/CRP), corticosteroid-free remission, and adverse events (AEs). Patient-reported activity of extraintestinal manifestations (EIMs) was retrospectively reviewed.ResultsWe identified 11 patients with Crohn’s disease (CD) and 4 with ulcerative colitis (UC) who added GUS to UPA for luminal inflammation (n = 12) and/or EIMs (n = 5). Five of nine patients with active CD achieved biochemical remission. Of the four with symptomatic CD (HBI > 5), two achieved clinical remission. Four patients received DTT for CD-associated EIMs and two had partial improvement of symptoms after GUS. All 3 patients who started DTT for active UC achieved clinical remission and one with active enteropathic arthropathy had partial improvement of symptoms after DTT. AEs included minor skin irritation, fatigue, and headaches. 14/15 patients remain on DTT after median 6 months’ follow-up.ConclusionThe addition of GUS to UPA led to clinical remission in 50% and 100% of patients with CD and UC, and biochemical remission in 55.6% and 66.7% of patients with CD and UC, respectively. 60% noted improvement of their EIMs after adding GUS. No serious AEs were reported highlighting the safety of this DTT strategy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s10620-026-09995-3.Keywords: Dual-targeted therapy, Guselkumab, Upadacitinib, Crohn’s Disease, Ulcerative colitis, Extraintestinal manifestations

Introduction

Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) is a chronic immune-mediated disorder in which dysregulation of the immune system leads to inflammation in the gastrointestinal (GI) tract and often has co-existing extraintestinal manifestations (EIMs) [1, 2]. Early treatment of IBD has been shown to reduce disease progression, minimize the development of complications such as strictures, abscesses, and fistulas in Crohn’s disease (CD), and lower the risk of dysplasia and surgery in ulcerative colitis (UC) [3, 4]. Despite the expansion of the therapeutic armamentarium available to treat IBD, long-term clinical and endoscopic remission rates remain relatively low, suggesting that a therapeutic ceiling has been reached with our current drugs [5, 6]. In network meta-analyses (MA) comparing the relative efficacy of advanced therapies and small molecule treatments for IBD, upadacitinib (a selective Janus kinase (JAK)-1 inhibitor) has consistently ranked among the highest in its ability to induce both clinical and endoscopic response and remission in bio-exposed patients with CD and UC [7–9]. While these results are promising, these data underscore that one-quarter to one-half of patients will remain symptomatic despite treatment with even our most effective therapies.

Dual-targeted therapy (DTT) is a treatment paradigm that involves combining advanced therapies that target different inflammatory pathways for synergistic effects [5]. DTT provides a mechanistic rationale for overcoming the current therapeutic ceiling in IBD, and treating EIMs of IBD by targeting aberrant immunological pathways [2]. We previously demonstrated clinical remission in 80% of patients with medically refractory CD who were treated with the unique DTT combination of upadacitinib (UPA) and ustekinumab (UST) and similar results of DTT with UPA and risankizumab (RISA) [10, 11].

Guselkumab (GUS) is an IgG1 monoclonal antibody and interleukin-23 (IL-23) inhibitor approved by the U.S Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for the treatment of moderately to severely active CD and moderate to severe UC [12–14]. It is the first IL-23 inhibitor with both subcutaneous (SC) induction and maintenance dosing available in the USA. In randomized controlled trials (RCTs), subcutaneous (SC) induction followed by escalated maintenance dosing of GUS 200 mg every 4 weeks was associated with clinical remission rates of 58.3% at 24 weeks and 66.1% at 48 weeks in CD [13]. In patients with UC, those who received SC induction and high dose SC maintenance had a 36% clinical remission rate at 24 weeks, while IV induction with high dose SC maintenance was associated with a 50% clinical remission at 44 weeks [12, 14].

To date, the safety and efficacy of combining upadacitinib (UPA) with GUS in the treatment of medically refractory IBD have not been investigated. We hypothesize that the combination of GUS with UPA may facilitate improved rates of clinical remission and treatment of EIMs. A single case report has demonstrated success with this combination regimen in the treatment of psoriatic arthritis; however, the data for IBD are lacking [15]. Herein, we report our single-center tertiary care experience of combining GUS with UPA for the treatment of medically complex IBD and associated EIMs.

Methods

We performed a retrospective case series of individuals who had GUS added to UPA for medically complex IBD between March 25, 2025, and Dec 20, 2025.

We included adult patients, aged 18 or older, with a confirmed diagnosis of IBD (CD or UC) who had active luminal disease (defined by the presence of symptoms, elevated biochemical markers including C-reactive protein (CRP) and fecal calprotectin (FCP), imaging, and/or endoscopy) or active EIMs despite therapy with dose-optimized UPA. Eligible participants were those who had previously consented to have their de-identified medical data stored in the University of Chicago’s real-world prospective database as part of the IBD Catalyst Project. We extracted the following data from patient charts: baseline demographics, disease characteristics, prior IBD surgeries, and IBD treatment history. We prospectively collected data on clinical disease activity including EIM status, adverse events (AEs), rates of treatment discontinuation, and need for surgery after GUS was added to UPA. A clinical pharmacist called patients to prospectively collect HBI and SCCAI scores at week 0 (baseline), 4, 8, 12, and 24; to provide reminders to complete bloodwork for CRP and submit stool samples for FCP; and to elicit any AEs. In the event a patient could not be reached, a second attempt was made to contact the patient either via telephone or via a secure patient messaging system (Epic) at the University of Chicago.

We investigated the following efficacy outcomes: clinical response (reduction in Harvey–Bradshaw Index (HBI) or Simple Clinical Colitis Activity Index (SCCAI) by ≥ 3 points), clinical remission (HBI < 5 or SCCAI < 3), biochemical response (≥ 50% reduction in baseline FCP or CRP), and biochemical remission (normalization of CRP < 5 mg/L and/or FCP < 150mcg/g). In those with active EIMs at baseline, we looked at improvement of EIM activity as a subjective patient-reported outcome. In patients who were receiving systemic corticosteroids (CS) at baseline, we also examined CS-free remission which was defined as clinical remission of IBD or improvement in EIM activity in the absence of systemic CS use for 3 months and 6 months, respectively. AEs were defined based on the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) adverse event reporting system. Where available, we described the results of follow-up endoscopy, histology, and/or radiographic assessment with intestinal ultrasound (IUS) after GUS was added to UPA; however, this was not a formal study outcome given the limited dataset and short duration of patient follow-up.

Results

We identified fifteen patients with IBD, eleven with CD and four with UC, who had GUS added to UPA between March 25, 2025, and December 20, 2025. The median duration of patient follow-up was 6 months (IQR 3, range 1–8 months).

The baseline characteristics of included participants are summarized in Table 1 (see supplemental Table 1 for further descriptions of each individual participant). Of the eleven patients with CD, ten (90.9%) had ileocolonic involvement, nine (81.8%) had either a stricturing and/or penetrating phenotype, and five (45.4%) had concurrent perianal disease. Eight patients (72.7%) had undergone at least one CD-related surgery prior to DTT initiation, with total number of surgeries ranging from one to six (median 2.5, IQR 1.3). Although the median HBI across participants was 2 (IQR 7.5), nine patients (81.2%) had active luminal disease based on symptoms, biochemical markers, endoscopy, and/or radiographic imaging, and this was the primary indication for starting DTT. Eight patients had EIMs at baseline, of which four were well managed on UPA monotherapy. The remaining four patients required DTT for active EIMs (n = 1 with orofacial granulomatosis and n = 3 with enteropathic arthropathy). Of the four patients with UC, half were women with a median disease duration of 8.5 years (IQR 1.3). All the patients with UC were non-smokers and did not have any prior bowel surgeries nor any prior IL-23 exposure before starting DTT with GUS. Three patients had active UC as the primary indication for DTT, while the remaining patient (case 12) required DTT for better control of their joint pains.

All the included participants were on UPA at baseline (median duration 21 months, IQR 20.5), prior to adding GUS. The median number of advanced therapy exposures prior to starting DTT was 5 (IQR 2). The most common drug class prescribed prior to UPA was an anti-TNF-α agent, with more than one anti-TNF-α exposure documented in ten patients (76.9%). Four patients with CD had previously received a DTT regimen including UPA + ustekinumab (cases 1 and 6) and UPA + risankizumab (cases 2, 5, and 6). Crohn’s Disease

Impact of DTT on Luminal Disease

The efficacy and safety of UPA and GUS are outlined in Table 3. Of the eleven patients with CD, nine had evidence of active luminal disease at baseline which was the primary indication for adding GUS to UPA. Five of the nine patients with luminal CD had an HBI < 5 at baseline and all five (100%) maintained clinical remission in follow-up. Of the four patients with active luminal disease and an HBI > 5 at baseline, two (50%) achieved clinical remission at the end of follow-up. The two patients (case 1 and 2) who did not achieve clinical response or remission had known stricturing CD on a background of a prior ileocecectomy. Both patients had previously been exposed to DTT regimens with UPA and ustekinumab (UST) or UPA and risankizumab (RISA). In both cases, minimal active inflammation and stenotic disease were identified on follow-up IUS (case 1) or endoscopy (case 2), prompting a referral for surgical evaluation. Only one patient with CD (case 5) was using oral CS for bowel inflammation at baseline. They stopped their prednisone one week after adding GUS to UPA and maintained CS-free remission after 6 months of follow-up. Five of the nine patients with active CD (55.6%) achieved biochemical remission in follow-up.

Impact of DTT on EIMs

Four patients required DTT for management of CD-associated EIMs (n = 1 orofacial granulomatosis (OFG), n = 3 enteropathic arthropathy). Three of these patients also had active luminal CD (cases 2, 5, and 8), of which two (cases 5 and 8) exhibited improvement of both their EIM and luminal CD after starting DTT. One (33.3%) patient noted subjective improvement of their arthropathy eight weeks after starting GUS, while the other patient had partial healing of his OFG as early as four weeks after adding GUS to UPA. The two patients with ongoing active EIMs despite DTT had active arthropathy both at baseline and in follow-up. One (case 6) had no active bowel inflammation but had tried prior DTT regimens including UPA + UST and UPA + RISA for joint pains. The other participant (case 2) had CS-dependent hip pain and remained on prednisone for this indication.

Ulcerative Colitis

Impact of DTT on Luminal Disease

The three patients with active luminal UC achieved clinical remission (100%), with results seen as early as two weeks after starting DTT (cases 13 and 14). Two patients (66.7%) achieved biochemical remission after DTT (cases 13 and 15), and one had an IUS at week 12 which showed sonographic improvement, but not normalization, in the bowel wall thickness of the sigmoid and descending colon (case 13).

Impact of DTT on EIMs

One patient with UC started DTT for management of CS-dependent enteropathic arthropathy and psoriatic arthritis. While she had partial improvement of her EIMs and was able to taper her prednisone dose from 7.5 to 5 mg daily, she did not achieve CS-free remission of her arthropathy and remained on prednisone at 6 months of follow-up.

Safety Results

AEs of DTT included minor facial skin irritation (n = 1) and fatigue (n = 1), which coincided with starting UPA and continued after GUS was added. One patient with a history of migraines reported headaches at the time of GUS injections. One patient (case 4) with known stricturing CD was hospitalized for a bowel obstruction two weeks after starting DTT and required an ileal resection but remained on DTT post-operatively. The patient with fatigue discontinued UPA ten weeks after starting DTT and remained in clinical remission on GUS monotherapy at a 12-week follow-up. The other fourteen patients remain on DTT at the end of follow-up, corresponding to a treatment persistence rate of 93%. There were no notable cytopenias, liver enzyme derangements, or renal dysfunction identified in the included participants (see Supplemental Table 3).

Discussion

We demonstrate the short-term efficacy and safety of adding GUS to UPA in the treatment of medically refractory IBD and/or associated EIMs. Of the 9 patients with active luminal CD, clinical and biochemical remission were achieved in 50% and 55.6% of patients, respectively, after adding GUS to UPA. All three patients with active UC achieved clinical remission (100%) and 66.7% achieved biochemical remission after the addition of GUS. Three of five patients (n = 2 CD, n = 1 UC) reported improvement of their EIMs after combining GUS with UPA but not remission. No serious AEs such as infection, malignancy, or death were documented, and all but one patient remained on DTT at the end of follow-up, highlighting the safety, tolerability, and patient acceptability of this regimen.

DTT is a treatment paradigm that involves combining advanced therapies with unique mechanisms of action to target inflammation via synergistic pathways [5]. In theory, this should circumvent the therapeutic ceiling that has been reached with modern IBD therapies and allow for attainment of higher clinical remission rates, reduced risk of secondary loss of response, and simultaneous treatment of immune-mediated conditions that are associated with IBD due to shared genetic haplotypes (e.g. psoriasis) [6]. Preliminary results from the DUET-CD and DUET-UC trials demonstrate promising safety and efficacy data for the concurrent inhibition of IL-23 p19 and anti-TNF-alpha when treating patients with CD and UC who have failed 2 or more drug classes [16, 17]. These RCTs support the use of IL-23 p19 inhibitors, like GUS, in parallel with other anti-inflammatory treatment pathways when managing refractory, difficult to treat IBD. While no RCTs have specifically looked at DTT with UPA, we previously published on our experience of using UST or RISA in combination with UPA for the treatment of medically refractory CD and demonstrated clinical remission in five out of six patients with active CD using this strategy [10, 11]. Alternative UPA-based DTT strategies with vedolizumab and UST have also been described in small retrospective studies conducted in Australia and Taiwan with similar short-term efficacy and safety data [18, 19]. Here, we present the first clinical study to investigate the unique combination of GUS and UPA in patients with complex, highly refractory IBD and associated EIMs. In a recent systematic review (SR) and meta-analysis of 279 patients with IBD, use of DTT was associated with pooled clinical remission rates of 59% [20]. Although none of the patients in this SR were treated with UPA and GUS, we demonstrate analogous clinical remission rates with the combination of UPA + GUS at our tertiary care center.

In the current study, we included four patients with a history of DTT exposure including UPA in combination with either UST or RISA (and one patient who had received both UPA + UST and UPA + RISA). Of the three patients with active luminal CD, only one achieved clinical, CS-free, and biochemical remission after switching to UPA + GUS suggesting that this unique combination may be effective in carefully selected individuals who have previously been exposed to DTT with an IL-23 inhibitor. The remaining two patients had objective evidence of reduced inflammation on an IUS at month 4 (case 1) and mucosal healing on colonoscopy at month 8 (case 2) but remained symptomatic due to the presence of anastomotic strictures at their neo-terminal ileum. These results highlight that anti-inflammatory treatment should not be a substitute for a timely surgical consultation in patients with fibrostenotic disease, and DTT has limited utility in this setting. Three of the seven patients with stricturing CD (42.9%) in this study ultimately required referral to a colorectal surgeon due to sonographic and endoscopic evidence of stenoses at their previous surgical sites, which further underscores the importance of early surgical consultation in those with post-operative recurrence of stricturing CD.

The addition of GUS in those with inadequate control of EIMs with UPA monotherapy is a novel strategy that has not been previously investigated. The rationale for using UPA in the concurrent treatment of IBD and inflammatory arthritis rests in its ability to block Janus kinase (JAK)-dependent cytokine signaling which propagates inflammation in the gut and joints [21, 22]. Given that UPA is a selective JAK-1 inhibitor, there is a theoretical rationale for combining its use with an IL-23 inhibitor, as IL-23 signaling is regulated by JAK-2 and remains an actionable target for controlling luminal and extraluminal inflammation [22]. GUS is currently approved for the treatment of psoriasis and psoriatic arthritis, but it has not been specifically studied in the treatment of peripheral spondyloarthropathy [23]. In mouse models, the IL-23p19 gene was expressed by inflammatory cells in the joint synovium and administration of neutralizing antibodies against this IL-23p19 subunit was associated with decreased arthritic pain and suppressed disease activity. These findings support a mechanistic role for the use of IL-23 inhibitors like GUS in the management of enteropathic inflammatory arthritis [24]. As many EIMs parallel intestinal disease activity, it is possible that those who saw benefit in their EIM from the addition of GUS did so due to the additive benefit of this therapy in controlling their luminal disease as these same patients also had active IBD at baseline (cases 5 and 8). Conversely, two of the three patients (cases 2 and 12) with inactive luminal disease required ongoing prednisone to control their joint pains after consulting with a rheumatologist, which highlights the importance of multidisciplinary collaboration when treating those with debilitating enteropathic arthropathy despite well-controlled IBD.

Safety remains a pivotal concern when combining advanced therapies due to the potential risk of heightened immunosuppression. This is especially important when combining advanced therapies with a JAK inhibitor which has been associated with infections such as herpes zoster and biochemical abnormalities including neutropenia, hepatic disorders, and creatine kinase elevation in large IBD clinical trials [25, 26]. In this study, we report three minor AEs, of which one started after the addition of GUS (headache) and two started while on UPA monotherapy and persisted with the combination of GUS (facial skin irritation and headache). No infections or biochemical abnormalities were observed in any of the included patients. Seven of the fifteen patients (46.7%) included in this study were on UPA 45 mg daily and continued this dose, even after GUS was added, with no adverse events reported. This is in line with previous data published at our institution which demonstrated the safety of extended induction dosing of UPA and reinforces the safety of this regimen even when combined with other therapies [27]. While promising, these data should be interpreted with caution in light of the small sample size and short duration of patient follow-up which ranged from 1 to 8 months. Large cohort studies with extended patient follow-up are necessary to accurately ascertain the long-term safety of UPA and GUS combination therapy.

Our literature review suggests that this is the first published study to assess the unique combination of UPA and GUS in patients with CD and UC. We describe a cohort of highly treatment-refractory patients, many of whom had multiple prior IBD-related surgeries, high-risk disease phenotypes, failure of multiple classes of advanced therapies, and active EIMs, who were treated with DTT without any major AEs after a median follow-up of 6 months. While the heterogeneity of the study cohort may, on the one hand, limit interpretation of the data, it also serves for hypothesis generation as to the multiple potential uses for UPA and GUS combination therapy in the management of luminal IBD and EIMs. We also included several patients with a prior history of malignancy and metabolic risk factors for liver disease, which further highlights the safety and tolerability of upadacitinib when combined with other immune-mediated therapies. Despite this being a retrospective study, a notable strength was the prospective data collection of clinical and biochemical outcomes.

Despite the novelty of this study, the generalizability of the results is limited by its retrospective design and small sample size (n = 15). Even with proactive reminders sent to patients to submit stool samples, there was still substantial missing FCP data, which further limited the sample size from which we could draw inferences regarding objective outcomes like biochemical response and remission. The lack of endoscopic endpoints was also a major limitation to this study. This was largely owing to the relatively short duration of follow-up in which clinical and biochemical data were more readily available than endoscopic or sonographic assessment. Future research should explore the impact of DTT on long-term treat-to-target outcomes in IBD such as endoscopic and transmural healing. As this was a case series, there was no control group, and thus, the efficacy of combination treatment with UPA and GUS compared to either monotherapy or alternative DTT regimens remains unclear. Lastly, the subjective reporting of EIM improvement as a patient-reported outcome is a major limitation to this study and one that requires further investigation with the use of validated scoring systems rather than binary assessment.

Conclusions

In conclusion, combining GUS with UPA appears to be a safe and effective strategy in the treatment of medically refractory IBD. This approach was associated with clinical remission in 50% and 100% of patients with CD and UC, respectively, and biochemical remission in 55.6% and 66.7% of patients with CD and UC, respectively. Of the five patients who started DTT for management of EIMs, 60% reported improvement with the addition of GUS. Further large-scale prospective studies are needed to validate the long-term impact of DTT, and appropriate step-down strategies for those who achieve deep remission on this combination approach.
