Evaluating the Prognostic Value of the EQUAL Candida Score and a Nomogram‐Based Approach for Candidaemia‐Related Mortality
Authors: Elif Mukime Saricaoglu, Melike Inan Hekimoglu, Ezgi Gulten, Irem Akdemir, Gule Cinar, Afife Zeynep Yilmaz, Duygu Ocal, Irem Kar, Kemal Osman Memikoglu, Fugen Yoruk
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionCandidaemia is a life‐threatening infection with a persistently high mortality rate, despite significant advances in antifungal therapy and supportive care. The European Confederation of Medical Mycology developed the EQUAL Candida Score as a standardised tool to evaluate adherence to guideline‐based management; however, its prognostic value has not been consistently demonstrated in different patient populations. This study aimed to evaluate the clinical impact of adhering to guidelines and determine the predictive value of the EQUAL Candida Score for mortality risk in candidaemia patients.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included adult patients with candidaemia who were treated at a tertiary care hospital. Patients were classified as survivors or nonsurvivors based on 90‐day candidaemia‐related mortality. We identified independent predictors of mortality using multivariable Cox regression analysis and subsequently developed a prognostic nomogram based on the final model.ResultsA total of 189 patients with candidaemia were included in the study, of whom 88 (46.6%) died within 90 days. The median EQUAL Candida Score was significantly lower among nonsurvivors compared with survivors (8 vs. 13, p < 0.001). This prognostic association remained consistent in subgroup analyses, both in patients with (10 vs. 13, p < 0.001) and without (10 vs. 13, p = 0.022) central venous catheters. An optimal cut‐off score of 12 was identified across all groups, yielding a sensitivity of 70%–80% and a specificity of 79%. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis further confirmed that patients with an EQUAL Score ≥ 12 had significantly higher survival rates in all subgroups. In multivariable Cox regression, immunosuppressive treatment (HR 1.728), septic shock (HR 2.035), lack of source control (HR 2.013) and an EQUAL Score < 12 (HR 3.503) were identified as independent predictors of candidaemia‐related mortality. Based on these variables, a nomogram was developed to estimate individualised survival probabilities at 1, 3 and 6 months. External validation in an independent cohort (n = 64) confirmed the model's prognostic performance, with a Harrell's C‐index of 0.704 (95% CI: 0.587–0.821), despite the limited sample size.ConclusionThe EQUAL Candida Score serves as a reliable prognostic marker for candidaemia. When combined with clinical parameters, it enhances the accuracy of mortality risk estimation. Our novel nomogram provides a practical framework for early risk stratification and may optimise management strategies for high‐risk patients.Keywords: candidaemia, EQUAL score, guideline adherence, mortality, nomogram, prognostic factors, risk prediction

Introduction

Candida species are among the leading pathogens responsible for hospital‐acquired bloodstream infections and are associated with substantial morbidity and mortality, particularly in immunocompromised and critically ill patients [1, 2]. Major risk factors for candidaemia include broad‐spectrum antibiotic use, central venous catheters (CVCs), total parenteral nutrition (TPN) and prior surgery [3, 4]. Timely initiation of appropriate antifungal therapy is critical in the management of candidaemia, as delays in targeted treatment have been shown to significantly increase mortality [5].

The European Society of Clinical Microbiology and Infectious Diseases (ESCMID) and the Infectious Diseases Society of America (IDSA) have established standardised algorithms for the diagnosis and management of candidaemia [6, 7, 8]. However, adherence to these guidelines in routine clinical practice, and the extent to which guideline compliance translates into improved outcomes, remains uncertain [9]. To address this issue, the European Confederation of Medical Mycology (ECMM) developed the Quality of Clinical Candidemia Management Score (EQUAL Candida Score)– the first structured tool designed to quantify adherence to clinical guidelines. The score assigns weighted points to diagnostic, therapeutic and follow‐up interventions according to the strength of their respective recommendations [9, 10].

Findings on the association between EQUAL Candida scores and mortality have varied across studies. Hoenigl et al. and Huang et al. demonstrated that higher scores—indicative of better adherence to clinical guidelines—were significantly associated with improved survival outcomes [9, 11]. However, Zakhem et al. found no statistically significant relationship between EQUAL Candida scores and mortality in the overall candidaemia population. Nevertheless, the score showed potential utility in stratifying outcomes among patients with CVCs [12]. These conflicting findings underscore the necessity of further research into the prognostic value of the EQUAL Candida Score in various patient populations and clinical settings. In this context, the present study aimed to evaluate the impact of guideline adherence and to determine the predictive value of the EQUAL Candida Score for estimating mortality risk in patients with candidaemia.

Material and Methods

Study Design and Patient Selection

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at a university medical centre comprising two affiliated hospitals between January 2019 and December 2023. Patients admitted to the First Hospital of XXX University were designated as the training cohort (n = 189), and patients from the Second Hospital of XXX University served as the external validation cohort (n = 64). Eligible participants were adults (≥ 18 years) with a confirmed diagnosis of candidaemia who received systemic antifungal therapy for at least 48 h. For patients with multiple episodes of candidaemia, only the first episode was analysed. Exclusion criteria included incomplete clinical data, receipt of antifungal therapy for invasive fungal infections other than candidaemia, death within the first 72 h after initiation of antifungal therapy, and concurrent bacterial bloodstream infection.

Data Collection

Demographic characteristics, comorbidities, clinical conditions and microbiological data were collected from electronic medical records. The presence of risk factors such as diabetes mellitus, hypertension and chronic pulmonary disease was documented. Data on hospitalisation in the intensive care unit (ICU), the presence of a CVCs, administration of TPN, infection source and prior surgical interventions were also collected.

Variables and Definitions

Candidaemia was defined as the isolation of Candida species from at least one blood culture in the presence of systemic signs of infection. Follow‐up blood cultures were performed daily or every other day after the initiation of antifungal therapy and continued until Candida was cleared. Microbiological cure was defined as sustained negative blood cultures following appropriate antifungal therapy. Identification and antifungal susceptibility testing of Candida isolates were performed using the Vitek 2 Yeast Biochemical Card (bioMérieux, Durham, NC, USA), with results interpreted according to the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) breakpoints [13].

Immunosuppressive treatment was defined as ongoing corticosteroid therapy (≥ 20 mg/day of prednisone or equivalent for ≥ 2 weeks) and/or receipt of immunosuppressive agents—such as calcineurin inhibitors, antimetabolites, mTOR inhibitors, biologic agents, cytotoxic chemotherapy, or other targeted immunosuppressive therapies—administered within the 30 days prior to the diagnosis of candidaemia. The length of hospitalisation prior to the onset of candidaemia was defined as the duration (in days) from the date of hospital admission to the date of the first positive Candida blood culture. Septic shock was defined according to the Sepsis‐3 criteria [14]. Time to positivity was defined as the time interval between initiating blood culture incubation and the automated detection of microbial growth by the blood culture system.

Empirical antifungal therapy consisted of fluconazole, echinocandins, or amphotericin B. Treatment modification was defined as switching from one antifungal class to another during treatment. Information on the initial empirical therapy, subsequent treatment modifications and the underlying reasons for these changes (e.g., lack of clinical response, antifungal susceptibility results and adverse drug effects) was systematically collected from patient records. Adherence to candidaemia management guidelines was assessed using the ECMM EQUAL Candida Score [10]. Patients were classified as survivors or nonsurvivors based on candidaemia‐related mortality, which was defined as death within 90 days of the first positive blood culture attributable to candidaemia. Early mortality was defined as death occurring within 3–7 days of admission.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics, version 30.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages and compared using Pearson's Chi‐square test or Fisher's exact test, as appropriate. Continuous variables, including ordinal‐scale scores, were summarised as medians with interquartile ranges (Q1–Q3) and compared using the Mann–Whitney U test due to their non‐normal distribution.

Univariable and multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression analyses were conducted to identify factors independently associated with candidaemia‐related mortality. Results were reported as hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Variables with a p value < 0.10 in univariable analyses were considered for inclusion in the multivariable model. The proportional hazards assumption was verified prior to model fitting.

The prognostic performance of the EQUAL Candida Score was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. The area under the curve (AUC), optimal cutoff value (determined by the Youden index), sensitivity and specificity were calculated for the overall cohort and for subgroups stratified by CVC status. Kaplan–Meier survival curves were generated to compare mortality between groups defined by EQUAL Candida Score categories, with statistical significance assessed using the log‐rank test.

A nomogram was constructed based on the final multivariable Cox regression model to estimate individualised 1‐, 3‐ and 6‐month survival probabilities. External validation of the model was performed in an independent cohort. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

This study has been approved by the Ethics Committee of XXX University Faculty of Medicine (approval number: 2,023,000,688–2; date of approval: 9 January 2025).

Results

A total of 189 patients with candidaemia were included in this study, comprising 101 (53.4%) survivors and 88 (46.6%) nonsurvivors. The median age of the overall cohort was 66 years (IQR, 55–75), and 102 patients (54.0%) were female. A substantial proportion of the cohort was immunosuppressed, including those with solid organ malignancies (33.9%), haematological malignancies (15.3%), solid organ transplants (3.7%) and bone marrow transplants (6.9%). Early mortality was observed in 12.1% (23/189) of patients. The 30, 60 and 90‐day mortality rates were 41.3%, 45% and 46.6% respectively. Candida albicans was identified in 56 patients (30.3%), whereas nonalbicans species were found in the majority (69.7%), with C. parapsilosis (33.5%) and C. glabrata (15.7%) being the most prevalent. A comparison of the clinical characteristics of survivors and nonsurvivors revealed distinct differences. Nonsurvivors had a higher prevalence of ICU admission, septic shock, a history of TPN use and infection with C. glabrata . Conversely, survivors were more likely to undergo timely source control interventions (69.3% vs. 36.4%, p < 0.001) and achieve a microbiological cure (98.0% vs. 36.4%, p < 0.001). In our cohort, most of the patients (70.9%) received echinocandins as empirical antifungal therapy, followed by fluconazole (23.8%) and liposomal amphotericin B (5.3%). The distribution of empirical therapy did not differ significantly between survivors and nonsurvivors (p = 0.546). Notably, echinocandin use was consistently predominant across both groups (Table 2). Of the 73 patients who required antifungal treatment modification, the most common reason was lack of clinical response (56.2%), followed by antifungal susceptibility results (42.5%), and adverse drug effects (1.4%). In the nonsurvivor group, antifungal treatment was most frequently modified due to lack of clinical response (79.4%) and antifungal susceptibility results (20.6%). By contrast, treatment modifications in survivors were most often related to antifungal susceptibility results (61.6%), followed by lack of response (35.9%) and adverse drug effects (2.6%). The rate of antifungal drug modification due to antifungal susceptibility results was significantly higher in survivors compared to nonsurvivors (p < 0.001).

Several components of the EQUAL Candida Score were significantly associated with survival. Survivors underwent susceptibility testing (p = 0.04), echocardiography (p < 0.001) and ophthalmoscopic examination (p = 0.003) more frequently. Follow‐up blood cultures (p = 0.002) and CVC removal (p < 0.001) were also more commonly performed in survivors. The median EQUAL Candida Score was significantly lower in nonsurvivors than in survivors (8 vs. 13, p < 0.001). This prognostic value remained consistent across subgroups, with significantly lower scores among nonsurvivors both in patients with (10 vs. 13, p < 0.001) and without (10 vs. 13, p = 0.022) CVCs (Table 3).

Prognostic Performance of the EQUAL Candida Score

The EQUAL Candida Score demonstrated strong discriminatory ability in predicting outcomes for candidaemia patients, with an overall AUC of 0.760 (95% CI: 0.690–0.830; p < 0.001). Stratified analyses revealed consistent prognostic performance, with an AUC of 0.761 (95% CI: 0.686–0.835; p < 0.001) among patients with CVCs and 0.779 (95% CI: 0.584–0.974; p = 0.022) among those without CVCs. Across all groups, a cut‐off score of 12 was identified as optimal, yielding a sensitivity of 70%–80% and a specificity of 79% (Figure 1).

Further confirmation of the prognostic value of the EQUAL Candida Score was provided by Kaplan–Meier survival analysis (Figure 2). Patients with an EQUAL Score ≥ 12 exhibited significantly higher survival rates compared to those with a score < 12, regardless of CVC status. To assess the potential influence of early mortality on time‐dependent investigations such as echocardiography and ophthalmoscopy, a sensitivity analysis was performed, excluding patients who died within the first 3–7 days. Multivariable Cox Regression and Nomogram

In the multivariable Cox regression analysis, immunosuppressive treatment (HR: 1.728, p = 0.029), septic shock (HR: 2.035, p = 0.004) and lack of source control (HR: 2.013, p = 0.007) were identified as independent predictors of candidaemia‐related mortality. Furthermore, an EQUAL Candida Score < 12 was strongly associated with increased mortality (HR: 3.503, p < 0.001) (Table 4). Based on these findings, a nomogram was developed incorporating the four independent predictors—immunosuppressive treatment, septic shock, absence of source control and the EQUAL Candida Score (Figure 3). The total points derived from individual variables correspond to the predicted probabilities of survival at 1, 3 and 6 months. Patients with an EQUAL Score ≥ 12, no septic shock and no immunosuppressive treatment have lower cumulative risk scores, which translate to higher predicted survival probabilities. Conversely, patients with septic shock, immunosuppressive therapy and an absent source control accumulate higher scores, corresponding to substantially lower predicted survival probabilities. Thus, this tool provides individualised risk stratification and may support timely clinical interventions in high‐risk patients.

Discussion

Invasive candidiasis predominantly affects individuals with predisposing conditions such as immunosuppression, solid organ/haematological malignancy, transplantation and total parenteral nutrition, resulting in substantial mortality. Most of these conditions were also present in our study population. This highlights the critical importance of early recognition and management of candidaemia in high‐risk groups [15]. The EQUAL Candida score is a structured, point‐based tool that has been shown to facilitate guideline adherence. It has also emerged as an independent predictor of mortality. The use of the EQUAL Candida score has been examined across diverse patient groups. Notably, Bal et al. [16] developed a paediatric modification (paed‐EQUAL) for neonates and children, and studies in geriatric cohorts have identified opportunities to improve the management of candidaemia [17]. Importantly, in our cohort, a considerable proportion of patients were immunosuppressed—encompassing those with malignancies and transplant recipients—underscoring the particular relevance of accurate risk stratification and guideline‐based management in this vulnerable subgroup.

Our study identified a 90‐day mortality rate of 46.6% for candidaemia, which is consistent with previously reported rates of 40.4%–43% [11, 18]. This finding underscores the persistently high mortality burden associated with this invasive infection. In a large observational cohort study involving 64 hospitals across 20 European countries, the EQUAL score was shown to be independently associated with mortality in patients both with and without CVCs [11]. Consistent with these observations, our findings revealed significantly improved survival rates among all subgroups of patients with an EQUAL Candida Score ≥ 12. This result reinforces the clinical utility of the EQUAL Candida Score as a reliable tool for risk stratification and outcome prediction.

Previous studies have reported considerable variation in the EQUAL Candida scores among different populations. Huang et al. reported a mean score of 8, Zakhem et al. 11.5, and Leepattarakit et al. a median of 8.91 [9, 12, 19]. Another study demonstrated median scores of 11 and 6 in patients with and without CVCs [20]. Additionally, Calderón‐Parra et al. retrospectively validated the EQUAL score, reporting a median adherence of 17 points [21]. Such variability in guideline compliance likely reflects differences in patient characteristics, socioeconomic factors, hospital complexity and logistical constraints. Our study demonstrated a median EQUAL Candida score of 12 across all groups, including patients with and without CVCs. This higher level of adherence, compared with previously published data, likely reflects a more standardised diagnostic and therapeutic approach, as well as heightened awareness of guideline‐based management of candidaemia in our setting.

According to the EQUAL Candida scoring system, patients with CVCs can inherently achieve up to three additional points compared to those without CVCs, potentially resulting in higher total scores [10]. However, in our study, the median EQUAL scores were similar between patients with and without CVCs. This finding may be explained by the relatively low adherence to guideline‐recommended timing for CVC removal in our cohort. Specifically, only 18.3% of CVCs were removed within the first 24 h of diagnosis and 35% within 24–72 h, reflecting suboptimal compliance. These rates are considerably lower than those reported in prior studies, such as Huang et al., who observed adherence rates of 64.6% [9]. Several factors may have contributed to this discrepancy. A substantial proportion of our patients had haematological malignancies or solid tumours. Many of these patients were immunosuppressed and experienced haemodynamic instability, thrombocytopenia and other laboratory abnormalities, which may have further limited the feasibility of early catheter removal. Moreover, the necessity of removing long‐term (tunnelled) catheters in an operating room, coupled with logistical barriers at the first hospital—where clinical units are located in separate buildings—further complicated timely interventions. Transferring clinically fragile patients with candidaemia across facilities under these conditions posed additional challenges. Together, these factors specific to the healthcare setting likely explain the low rates of early catheter removal observed in our study.

The shorter duration of antifungal therapy and hospital stay observed in the nonsurvivor group, along with the higher prevalence of septic shock, suggests that severely ill candidaemia patients may have died during the initial phase of their illness despite receiving appropriate antifungal therapy. These findings are consistent with previous studies [18, 22]. Moreover, the lower frequency of diagnostic investigations such as echocardiography and ophthalmoscopy in the nonsurvivor group is likely attributable to early mortality, which prevented the completion of recommended evaluations. To account for this potential bias, we performed a sensitivity analysis excluding patients who died within the initial days of illness. While no statistically significant differences were observed, this lack of significance may, at least in part, reflect the limited sample size of our study.

Our data revealed that survivors were more likely to have their treatment adjusted based on susceptibility results. In contrast, modifications for nonsurvivors were primarily prompted by a lack of clinical response. This divergence underscores the prognostic importance of early, evidence‐based treatment adjustments. Shah et al. reported that despite the availability of fluconazole‐susceptible isolates, more than half of the patients were not de‐escalated from echinocandins to fluconazole, citing reasons such as concerns about drug–drug interactions, delays or lack of awareness of susceptibility results and clinicians' reluctance to change therapy mid‐course [23]. In our study, the lower rate of susceptibility‐guided treatment modifications observed in nonsurvivors may similarly reflect the same factors. Additionally, clinical instability in critically ill patients may have precluded de‐escalation to narrower‐spectrum agents, even when isolates were susceptible.

In recent decades, the proportion of nonalbicans Candida species responsible for candidaemia has increased. Reports indicate that fluconazole‐resistant C. glabrata bloodstream isolates are being detected more frequently worldwide [24]. In our study, C. parapsilosis was the most frequently isolated species, followed by C. albicans and C. glabrata . Fluconazole‐resistant C. glabrata bloodstream infections pose significant clinical challenges because they often require echinocandins or polyenes for effective treatment. Prior exposure to fluconazole has notably been identified as a significant risk factor for developing such resistant infections [25]. In our cohort, C. glabrata was not found to be an independent predictor of mortality; however, it was significantly more prevalent among nonsurvivors. C. glabrata isolates demonstrated a high rate of fluconazole resistance (85.7%), and resistance to echinocandin was observed in 23.5% of isolates. Given that a substantial proportion of our patient population consisted of individuals with malignancies and/or a history of organ or stem cell transplantation—conditions that frequently require azole prophylaxis—we believe that the high rate of fluconazole resistance may reflect the selective pressure associated with widespread azole exposure in this high‐risk group.

Previous studies have demonstrated the prognostic utility of the EQUAL Candida score, both as an independent predictor of outcomes in broad patient cohorts [9, 11, 12] and in species‐specific analyses. In particular, candidaemia due to C. tropicalis has been shown to correlate with lower EQUAL scores as an independent predictor of 30‐day mortality, thereby reinforcing its value in high‐risk nonalbicans infections [19]. However, to our knowledge, no prior study has incorporated this score into a comprehensive, individualised prediction tool. In our cohort, four easily ascertainable factors—immunosuppressive treatment, septic shock, lack of source control and an EQUAL Candida Score < 12—emerged as independent predictors of candidaemia‐related mortality. Based on these variables, we developed a nomogram that integrates host status, acute illness severity, procedural management and adherence to quality‐of‐care benchmarks, allowing for individualised estimation of 1‐, 3‐ and 6‐month survival probabilities. Importantly, this framework delineates both modifiable targets, such as timely source control and EQUAL‐guided management, and nonmodifiable prognostic indicators, including immunosuppression and shock. Together, these findings provide a clinically pragmatic and evidence‐based approach to risk‐stratified management and structured follow‐up in invasive candidiasis.

Our study incorporated an external validation cohort from a separate hospital, thereby strengthening the generalisability of our findings. Evaluating the model in different patient populations and healthcare settings confirms its performance in various clinical settings, thereby overcoming the limitations of a single‐centre design. Although the small sample size of the external validation cohort limits statistical power, the results provide preliminary support for the model's generalisability. Future prospective multicentre studies are essential to validate this nomogram across diverse patient populations and confirm its broader clinical utility.

This study has several limitations, primarily due to its retrospective design and small sample size. Consequently, residual confounding factors due to unmeasured variables cannot be entirely ruled out. The disease's high mortality rate introduced survivorship bias, limiting the implementation of certain interventions throughout the course of the infection. The relatively low adherence to the recommended blood sampling volume (40 mL) in this study, coupled with the limited sensitivity of blood cultures (approximately 50%) [26, 27], may have contributed to an underestimation of the true incidence of candidaemia. Furthermore, the possibility that not all cases of candidaemia were identified limits the generalisability of our findings.

The EQUAL Candida Score is a valuable prognostic tool for patients with candidaemia. When combined with relevant clinical variables, it enables accurate prediction of mortality risk, regardless of the presence or absence of a central venous catheter. The strength of our study lies in the integration of the EQUAL Candida Score into a newly developed nomogram, which offers a practical approach to early risk stratification in candidaemia patients—including a substantial proportion of immunosuppressed individuals—and may facilitate more structured management strategies in this high‐risk population. The nomogram enables the timely identification of high‐risk patients and has the potential to guide clinical decision‐making, optimise management strategies and improve patient outcomes in this population.
