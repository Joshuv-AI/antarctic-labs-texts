A Patient with Benign Prostatic Hyperplasia Treated with Prostatic Arterial Embolization: Case-report with Review of the Literature from Asia
Authors: Shigeo Ichihashi, Daisuke Gotoh, Naoki Fujimura, Yuto Chanoki, Tatsuya Yokota, Katsuhiko Taira, Kazumasa Torimoto, Kiyohide Fujimoto, Tiago Bilhim, Toshihiro Tanaka
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Prostatic artery embolization is a minimally invasive treatment for lower urinary tract symptoms caused by benign prostatic hyperplasia. We report our first case of prostatic artery embolization in a 74-year-old male with urinary retention requiring intermittent catheterization. Bilateral prostatic artery embolization using Embosphere microspheres was successful, with no major complications. By 2 months post-procedure, intermittent catheterization was no longer required, and at 6 months, symptoms and prostate volume showed marked improvement. A review of Asian studies confirmed prostatic artery embolization's safety and efficacy, with clinical success in 78%-100% of cases and technical success rates comparable to those in Western countries. Despite the smaller vessel sizes in Asians, outcomes were unaffected. Prostatic artery embolization appears to be an effective treatment for benign prostatic hyperplasia and requires further validation to support its broader use in Japan.

Introduction

Prostatic arterial embolization (PAE) has emerged as a widely adopted minimally invasive treatment for lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS) secondary to benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH). It offers an effective alternative to traditional transurethral resection of the prostate (TURP) [1, 2] or open prostatectomy [3], and various studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of PAE in significantly improving symptoms, with benefits that persist from the medium to long term [4-7]. However, PAE has yet to be covered by the public insurance system in Japan, limiting its widespread application. Most published studies on PAE come from Western countries, resulting in limited data on the procedure's effectiveness in Asian populations. To address this, we report our initial experience with PAE, accompanied by a literature review of relevant studies from Asia.

Case Presentation

The patient was a 74-year-old male with a history of frequent urination, requiring clean intermittent catheterization 5-6 times daily and medical therapy with silodosin 8 mg and vibegron 50 mg. His initial International Prostate Symptom Score (IPSS) was 11 points, and his quality of life (QOL) score was 5 points. Preoperative PV was 99.0 mL, and intravesical prostatic protrusion (IPP), a useful parameter to predict bladder outlet obstruction [8, 9], was 30.7 mm. In the urodynamic study, the bladder outlet obstruction index (BOOI) was 104, the bladder contractility index (BCI) was 120.5, the maximum urinary flow rate (Qmax) and post-void residual (PVR) were 2.5 mL/s and 248 mL. The prostate-specific antigen (PSA) level was 10.2 ng/mL 5 months prior to the PAE procedure. MRI revealed an enlarged prostate without any indication of prostate cancer. CTA was performed using an Aquilion ONE CT scanner (Canon Medical Systems, Tochigi, Japan). A bolus injection of 100 mL of contrast agent was administered, and image acquisition was initiated using the bolus tracking method. Scanning commenced when the attenuation in the abdominal aorta reached 200 Hounsfield units. Three-dimensional images were reconstructed using a dedicated 3D workstation (Synapse Vincent, Fujifilm, Tokyo, Japan). CTA identified that the right prostatic artery originated from the internal pudendal artery, while the left prostatic artery stemmed from the obturator artery (Fig. 1). At our institution, PAE is performed under the approval of the Advanced Medical Technology Evaluation Committee of the university. The procedure is offered exclusively on a self-pay basis, with all associated medical costs borne directly by the patient. Endovascular treatment was performed under local anesthesia. The right common femoral artery was accessed under ultrasound guidance, and a 5-Fr long sheath was inserted. Based on CTA anatomical mapping, both prostatic arteries were selectively catheterized using a Carnelian PIXIE microcatheter (Tokai Medical Products, Kasugai, Aichi, Japan) and a 0.014-inch guidewire Meister (Asahi Intecc Co, Seto, Aichi, Japan). Prior to embolization, the patient received an intravenous dose of acetaminophen. Following the injection of 100 μg of nitroglycerin into each prostatic artery, PAE was performed. Embosphere particles (300-500 μm; Merit Medical Japan, Shinjuku, Tokyo, Japan), diluted 10-fold, were used for embolization. A total of 6/20 mL was injected into the left prostatic artery, and 8/20 mL into the right (Fig. 2). The final angiogram confirmed the absence of prostatic enhancement. The patient was discharged the day after the procedure without any complications or symptoms. By 2 months postoperatively, intermittent catheterization was no longer required. At 3 months, the IPSS improved to 8, with a QOL score of 2, allowing for the discontinuation of medical therapy. MRI also demonstrated a significant reduction in prostate volume (Fig. 3). The prostate-specific antigen level decreased to 1.3 ng/mL. By 6 months, the IPSS and QOL scores further improved to 7 and 2, respectively. PV and IPP improved significantly, decreasing from 99.0 mL and 30.7 mm to 30.6 mL and 21.0 mm, respectively. The BOOI improved to 25 and BCI remained at 115. The Qmax increased to 12.8 mL/s, and the PVR was 0 mL, indicating a marked improvement in lower urinary tract function.

Review of the literature from Asia

An extensive electronic search of English-language literature was conducted using the MEDLINE (PubMed) database, covering the period from January 2010 to December 2024. Our search strategy combined free-text terms with relevant Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) to identify studies on PAE for BPH, using terms “benign prostatic hyperplasia [MeSH terms]” and “embolization” or “embolisation.” This search yielded 387 articles, of which 32 were published in the Asian region. We excluded case reports, studies primarily focused on imaging evaluations, and duplicate patient populations from the same author groups. Ultimately, 11 studies met our inclusion criteria (Table 1). The mean age of patients in these studies ranged from 67.5 to 85.7 years. In most studies, PAE was performed via a transfemoral approach [7, 10-19]. Selective cannulation of the bilateral prostate artery was possible in 55.9-100% of cases [1, 7, 10-19]. Studies from Korea demonstrated that PAE via transradial access was equally effective and safe compared to the transfemoral approach, with the added benefit of fewer complications [11, 16]. Microspheres and polyvinyl alcohol were used as the embolic agents. Clinical success was obtained in 78%-100% of the cases at 12-month follow-up. Leng et al. [14] reported a 90.3% success rate in catheter removal and symptom relief following PAE in patients with acute urinary retention caused by BPH. Li et al. [10] reported significant clinical improvements with the use of smaller polyvinyl alcohol particles, suggesting enhanced gland penetration and improved clinical outcomes. In a comparative study between PAE and TURP, Qiu reported that although PAE was effective, TURP resulted in superior outcomes regarding symptom scores and flow rate [12]. No major adverse events were reported after PAE. Minor complications included acute urinary retention (0%-32%), urinary tract infection (0%-8.8%), and rectal bleeding (0%-14%). There were no major complications requiring surgical intervention.

PAE was shown to be safe for elderly patients. Xu et al. [15] reported the safety and efficacy of PAE in patients over 80 years of age with large prostate volumes who were considered unsuitable for open or endoscopic surgical procedures due to cardiopulmonary insufficiency. Technical success was achieved in 27 patients (96.4%) who underwent PAE. Significant improvements were observed at all postoperative time points in mean IPSS, mean QOL score, mean Qmax, mean PVR urine volume, and mean prostate volume. Similarly, Wang et al. [18] demonstrated the effectiveness of PAE in elderly patients aged ≥75, showing comparable technical success and clinical improvement to those under 75.

Discussion

PAE has been increasingly performed worldwide as a minimally invasive treatment option for BPH. Numerous clinical studies have highlighted the safety of the procedure and its effectiveness in improving symptoms, even though objective measures of improvement, such as reduction in prostate volume and Qmax, are generally less pronounced compared to TURP [20-23]. Published data have shown that PAE provides greater improvement in urinary symptoms and sexual function than medical treatment for up to 24 months [24], along with durable long-term LUTS improvement, with re-intervention rates of 20% within the first 5 years and 30%-60% beyond 5 years post-PAE [6].

We successfully performed the first case of PAE in our facility. In this case, a preoperative CTA was performed, which provided valuable information on the anatomy of the prostatic arteries. Given our limited experience, the CTA facilitated identification of the origin of the prostatic artery. The information allowed us to determine the optimal fluoroscopic angles for accurately identifying the prostatic artery ostium. Additionally, this imaging helped us select the appropriate shapes for the guidewire and catheter, contributing to reduced procedure time and enhanced technical success. CTA can help predict technical challenges during the PAE procedure, such as vascular stenosis, calcification, and tortuosity, which are likely to increase technical difficulty and radiation exposure [25]. Cone-beam CT during the PAE procedure can provide similar information; however, pre-procedural CTA offers the advantage of allowing sufficient time to develop a treatment strategy. This approach can enhance technical success or facilitate the selection of alternative treatment options for patients with unfavorable anatomy for PAE. A known anatomical difference between Asian and Caucasian populations is vessel size, with Asian individuals often having smaller coronary and aortoiliac arteries [26, 27]. There are no data regarding the size difference of the prostatic artery; however, Asians may have a smaller prostatic artery and more tortuous iliac arteries, considering the anatomical difference in other vascular territories. This anatomical characteristic could potentially impact the technical success rates of PAE in Asian patients. Interestingly, technical success rates reported in Asian studies are comparable to those in Western countries, indicating that smaller vessel size does not significantly hinder procedural outcomes.

Additionally, PAE is considered a safe procedure. In a meta-analysis of 662 patients, only two adverse events classified as Society of Interventional Radiology class C, events requiring therapy and short-term hospitalization (<48 hours), were reported [28]. The most observed adverse events were minor, occurring in 3%-7% of patients, and included transient urinary retention, dysuria, hematuria, hematospermia, and transient rectorrhagia. Adverse events such as bladder ischemia and penile ulceration, attributable to non-target embolization, have been rarely reported. However, such complications can be effectively minimized, particularly by operators with limited experience, through pre-procedural assessment using CTA or cone-beam CT to obtain detailed anatomical information.

For embolization, we selected Embospheres microspheres (300-500 μm) as the embolic agent. Limited data exist regarding the superiority of specific embolic materials or particle sizes [6]. Evidence suggests that smaller particles may be more effective, as they penetrate deeper into tissue than larger ones. Liquid embolic agents have also been reported to be effective and safe [29, 30]. More data is needed to guide the selection of optimal embolic materials.

The transradial approach has gained increasing attention worldwide across various interventional procedures, including PAE. Its primary advantage lies in enhanced patient comfort and early ambulation, as patients can mobilize immediately after the procedure. Several recent studies from Korea have shown that transradial PAE offers comparable efficacy and safety to the traditional transfemoral approach, with the added benefit of fewer access-site complications [11, 16]. Although there have been theoretical concerns regarding the risk of cerebral embolism with transradial access, current evidence does not support an increased risk. A prospective, multicenter, open-label registry evaluating transradial access in embolization procedures, including PAE, uterine fibroid embolization, and liver tumor embolizations, demonstrated a high safety profile, with no reported cases of stroke, hand ischemia, or serious access-related adverse events at 30-day follow-up [31]. These findings support the growing evidence endorsing transradial access as a safe, effective, and patient-friendly alternative approach for PAE.

Conclusions

The first case of PAE for BPH was successfully performed at our institution. A review of Asian studies demonstrated that the safety and efficacy of PAE are comparable to those in Western countries. Further data accumulation is essential to promote PAE as a viable alternative to surgical procedures for Japanese patients with LUTS.
