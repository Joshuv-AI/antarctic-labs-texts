Cystic Artery Hemorrhage as a Rare Complication of Acute Gangrenous Cholecystitis
Authors: Ye Wang, Bin Cui, Xu Jiang, Xinglin Jin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis report presents a case of cystic artery hemorrhage resulting from acute gangrenous cholecystitis. During an emergency laparoscopic cholecystectomy, the surgical team unexpectedly encountered hemorrhage from the cystic artery. Subsequent pathological examination confirmed the diagnosis of acute gangrenous cholecystitis. As a severe infectious condition of the gallbladder, acute gangrenous cholecystitis is typically associated with a high risk of complications; however, cystic artery hemorrhage, as a direct complication, is exceedingly rare. This article seeks to investigate both common and uncommon complications of acute gangrenous cholecystitis through the analysis of this case, emphasizing the clinical features, pathogenesis, and management strategies related to cystic artery hemorrhage to improve clinicians’ capacity to recognize this rare occurrence.Keywords: acute gangrenous cholecystitis, cystic artery, cystic artery hemorrhage, gallbladder perforation, laparoscopic cholecystectomy, complications

Introduction

Acute calculous cholecystitis is one of the most common causes of acute abdominal pain encountered in clinical practice. Its local complications include gallbladder empyema, pericholecystic abscess, perforation, and progression to gangrenous cholecystitis. Acute gangrenous cholecystitis represents a progressive form of acute cholecystitis, typically resulting from obstruction of the gallbladder duct, such as that caused by cholelithiasis. This obstruction leads to ischemia, necrosis, and bacterial infection of the gallbladder wall.1 The incidence of acute gangrenous cholecystitis ranges from 2% to 30% of acute cholecystitis cases and is frequently observed in elderly individuals, diabetic patients, and those with compromised immune systems.2 This condition poses significant risks, with complications that may include gallbladder perforation, abscess formation, biliary peritonitis, and systemic sepsis, resulting in a mortality rate of 22%.3 Vascular-related complications, such as cystic artery hemorrhage, are rarely documented. As a branch of the right hepatic artery, the cystic artery is generally at a lower risk of erosion during inflammatory processes, making bleeding events uncommon. This report, alongside a specific case study, systematically reviews the pertinent literature to underscore the uniqueness and clinical importance of cystic artery hemorrhage in the context of acute gangrenous cholecystitis.

Case Description

Patient Information

A 56-year-old female patient presented to the emergency department with persistent right upper abdominal pain lasting one day. She reported no significant medical history, abdominal trauma, prior surgical interventions, or relevant family or genetic history. On admission, physical examination revealed right upper quadrant tenderness accompanied by rebound tenderness and mild abdominal muscle guarding. Murphy’s sign was positive. An emergency full abdominal CT scan was indicated due to the presence of an enlarged gallbladder, a thickened gallbladder wall, and a suspected gallbladder rupture. Additionally, fluid accumulation surrounding the liver was noted, resulting in hepatic compression (Figure 1). The patient’s family declined surgical intervention. Consequently, an emergency color Doppler ultrasound-guided gallbladder puncture and drainage were performed, along with puncture and drainage of the perihepatic effusion. The drainage fluid was entirely purulent, and the characteristics of the fluids in the two drainage tubes were approximately identical. One week later, both the gallbladder and abdominal drainage fluids became bloody. Upon examination, the patient’s heart rate was recorded at 104 beats per minute. No significant jaundice was noted in the skin, mucous membranes, or sclera. However, the eyelids, conjunctiva, and lips appeared pale. Physical examination indicated abdominal distension, tenderness in the upper right abdomen, rebound tenderness, and muscle rigidity. Furthermore, Murphy’s sign was strongly positive, and bowel sounds were detected at a rate of approximately two per minute. Laboratory tests showed: red blood cell count 1.52 × 1012/L, hemoglobin 57 g/L, hematocrit 16.8%, neutrophils 87.8%, procalcitonin 5.65 ng/mL, C-reactive protein 77.8 mg/L, interleukin-6 8.5 pg/mL, alanine aminotransferase 128 U/L, aspartate aminotransferase 158 U/L, total bilirubin 6.7 μmol/L, and direct bilirubin 13.9 μmol/L. Both plain and contrast-enhanced abdominal CT scans revealed fusiform slightly hyperdense shadows and patchy slightly hypodense shadows at the liver’s periphery, suggestive of a hematoma. The gallbladder exhibited an irregular shape, accompanied by local mixed-density shadows of both high and low attenuation. Gallbladder perforation was considered, along with the possibility of bleeding and infection (Figure 2). Based on the clinical manifestations, laboratory findings, and imaging features, the patient was diagnosed with acute gangrenous cholecystitis with gallbladder perforation and intra-abdominal hemorrhage.

Treatment Process

Given the rapid deterioration of the patient’s condition and the emergence of pronounced signs of peritoneal irritation, an emergency laparoscopic cholecystectomy was deemed necessary. Intraoperative exploration disclosed extensive and dense adhesions within the abdominal cavity, obscuring the anatomical landmarks. A significant quantity of dark red, bloody, purulent fluid was observed at the lateral margin of the right lobe of the liver. The gallbladder wall exhibited extensive necrosis and a dark green hue, accompanied by severe adhesions to adjacent tissues. Additionally, a substantial amount of milky white purulent material and pus was noted surrounding the gallbladder. Numerous dark red blood clots were present beneath the neck of the gallbladder. Despite removal of the clots, unexplained bleeding persisted, complicating the identification of the Calot’s triangle. Although a conversion to laparotomy was initially planned, the family expressed a desire to continue with laparoscopic surgery. Further exploration revealed active bleeding from the cystic artery on the anterior wall. This bleeding was successfully controlled by clamping with an absorption clamp. Subsequently, the gallbladder duct was clamped, and the gallbladder was excised to complete the cholecystectomy, during which three stones were identified within the gallbladder. The surgical site was irrigated, and no fresh bleeding was detected. Postoperatively, the patient’s vital signs remained stable The estimated blood loss was approximately 1500 mL. Following the procedure, a regimen of high-gradient antibiotics was administered alongside anti-infective therapy, and both plasma and red blood cells were transfused.

Postoperative Pathology and Follow-Up

Gross pathological findings of the resected gallbladder were consistent with acute gangrenous cholecystitis with perforation (Figure 3). The patient experienced a smooth recovery following the operation, exhibiting no signs of rebleeding or infection. At the 3-month follow-up, the patient demonstrated no recurrence of symptoms, and laboratory indicators had returned to normal. The re-examination of the blood test showed: white blood cells at 5.73×109/L, red blood cells at 4.01×1012/L, hemoglobin at134g/L, hematocrit at 39.8%, neutrophils at 52.7%, aspartate aminotransferase at 22 U/L, alanine aminotransferase at 17 U/L, albumin at 48 g/L.

Discussion

There is no large body of literature concerning acute gangrenous cholecystitis.4 Complications of acute gangrenous cholecystitis primarily stem from ischemic necrosis of the gallbladder wall, bacterial toxin release, and local inflammatory dissemination.5 Common complications encompass gallbladder perforation, typically due to necrotic tissue rupture resulting in bile leakage and peritonitis, peri-gallbladder abscess necessitating percutaneous drainage or surgical intervention, and systemic infections like sepsis or septic shock, which are primary causes of mortality.6 Additionally, biliary obstruction or pancreatitis may arise from stone displacement or inflammation spread. Conversely, vascular complications like cystic artery hemorrhage are exceedingly rare, with only isolated case reports documented.7 This rarity may be attributed to the relatively small diameter of the cystic artery (averaging 1.91mm)8 and its deep location in the Calot’s triangle, rendering it less susceptible to direct erosion by inflammation. Nonetheless, necrosis involving the vascular wall can precipitate sudden bleeding.

In this instance, the direct cause of cystic artery hemorrhage was vascular erosion resulting from acute gangrenous cholecystitis. The underlying pathophysiological mechanisms include: 1. Ischemic necrosis: The disruption of blood flow within the gallbladder wall induces tissue hypoxia, leading to the release of proteases and free radicals that damage the vascular endothelium.9 2. Bacterial invasion: Common pathogens, including Escherichia coli and Klebsiella, produce collagenase and elastase, which compromise the structural integrity of blood vessel walls.10 3. Local inflammatory response: The upregulation of cytokines, such as TNF-α and IL-6, facilitates the formation of microthrombi and contributes to vascular rupture.11

It is also possible that the initial gallbladder drainage contributed to delayed cystic artery erosion and subsequent hemorrhage by altering local inflammatory conditions and exposing the vessel wall.

The infrequency of gallbladder artery hemorrhage can be attributed to several factors: 1. Anatomical protection: The cystic artery is primarily shielded by adipose tissue and fascia, which tend to remain relatively isolated during the initial stages of inflammation. 2. Self-limiting process: Most instances of bleeding can spontaneously resolve through thrombosis, with active hemorrhage typically occurring only in cases of severe necrosis. 3. Diagnostic challenges: Preoperative imaging techniques, such as CT or ultrasound, often struggle to identify microvascular lesions, which are frequently discovered unexpectedly during surgical procedures.

In cases of severe inflammation and uncontrolled bleeding, bailout procedures such as subtotal cholecystectomy or conversion to open cholecystectomy should be considered to avoid bile duct injury and achieve secure hemostasis. This case underscores that, although cystic artery hemorrhage is uncommon, it poses a significant risk to life. In the management of acute gangrenous cholecystitis, it should be considered in the differential diagnosis, particularly when accompanied by gallbladder perforation. This case further emphasizes that in the management of such challenging scenarios, surgeons should adhere to fundamental surgical principles while adopting flexible operative strategies and performing meticulous intraoperative exploration.

Conclusion

Acute gangrenous cholecystitis is a complex condition. Although cystic artery hemorrhage is a rare complication with a low incidence, it carries substantial clinical significance. This case illustrates its presentation and management during emergency laparoscopic surgery, highlighting the necessity of early diagnosis, tailored surgical approaches, and multidisciplinary collaboration. Future efforts should focus on accumulating more cases and conducting research to enhance prevention and treatment strategies, thereby reducing morbidity and mortality in affected patients.
