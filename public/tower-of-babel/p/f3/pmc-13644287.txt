Sterile peripheral corneal infiltration mimicking infectious keratitis following chemical injury: a case report
Authors: Jae Hwan Jeong, Junkyu Chung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSterile corneal infiltration is an inflammatory condition typically associated with contact lens wear, staphylococcal hypersensitivity, or corneal procedures. Chemical injuries usually present with diffuse epithelial defects or stromal haze, whereas the development of a discrete sterile corneal infiltrate following chemical injury appears to be uncommon. We describe an unusual case of unilateral sterile peripheral corneal infiltration following accidental chemical injury caused by contact lenses soaked in a makeup remover.Case presentationA 35-year-old woman presented with severe ocular pain and decreased vision after briefly wearing soft contact lenses that had been mistakenly soaked overnight in a makeup remover solution. Slit-lamp examination revealed near-complete epithelial defects in both eyes. In the left eye, a ring-shaped peripheral stromal infiltrate spanning approximately 270° was observed, with a distinct lucid interval and intact overlying epithelium. Microbiological investigations, including bacterial and fungal corneal cultures and viral polymerase chain reaction testing, were negative for infectious pathogens. The patient was diagnosed with bilateral chemical injury and unilateral sterile corneal infiltration. The patient was managed with antimicrobial coverage, cautious anti-inflammatory treatment, autologous serum, and intensive lubrication, followed by gradual epithelial healing and complete resolution of the infiltrate within two months.ConclusionsChemical toxicity from contaminated contact lenses may trigger a localized sterile immune-mediated corneal infiltrate that can mimic infectious keratitis. Careful slit-lamp examination and recognition of clinical features such as a lucid interval and intact epithelium are important for distinguishing sterile from infectious infiltrates and for guiding appropriate treatment.

Background

Sterile corneal infiltration (SCI) is an inflammatory condition often associated with contact lens (CL) wear, staphylococcal hypersensitivity, corneal refractive surgery, and collagen cross-linking (CXL) [1–3]. These infiltrates are typically immune-mediated, resulting from an antigen-antibody reaction to bacterial toxins or other environmental triggers [3–5]. Differentiating SCIs from infectious keratitis is critical as management strategies differ markedly; SCIs necessitate anti-inflammatory therapy, whereas infectious keratitis requires prompt and aggressive antibiotic treatment [3, 4, 6–8].

Chemical injuries to the cornea usually present as diffuse epithelial defects, ischemia, or stromal opacities [9]. However, discrete sterile corneal infiltration following chemical injury appears to be uncommon. We report an unusual case of a young woman who developed a unilateral sterile peripheral infiltrate in conjunction with bilateral chemical injuries after inadvertently soaking her contact lenses in a makeup remover.

Case presentation

A 35-year-old woman was referred with acute bilateral ocular pain and extensive corneal epithelial defects that had not improved during the first 4 days after chemical exposure. The patient had no significant medical history. She regularly wore soft contact lenses with no history of ocular surgery, ocular surface disease, allergies, or autoimmune disorders. The night before symptom onset, she mistakenly soaked her contact lenses in a makeup remover solution (Bioderma Sensibio H2OⓇ, NAOS, France) rather than a multipurpose lens solution. She wore the lenses the following morning and immediately developed severe ocular pain, leading to prompt removal of the contact lenses.

The patient visited a local ophthalmology clinic on the same day. Initial treatment included topical antibiotic eye drops, oral prednisolone (10 mg once daily), therapeutic contact lenses, and oral analgesics. However, the extensive epithelial defects did not show apparent improvement during the first 4 days of treatment. Consequently, the patient was referred to our hospital. The therapeutic contact lenses were removed at the initial visit to our hospital and were not re-applied thereafter. At presentation, best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) was 20/32 in the right eye and 20/100 in the left eye. The intraocular pressure, measured using a non-contact tonometer, was 21 mmHg in the right eye and 20 mmHg in the left eye. Slit-lamp examination revealed nearly complete epithelial defects in both eyes (Figs. 1 A, B). These findings were consistent with severe toxic keratitis. No stromal infiltration or cellular reactions in the anterior chamber were observed in the right eye. However, the left eye demonstrated a distinct clinical feature: a ring-shaped stromal infiltrate involving approximately 270° of the peripheral cornea. Notably, this infiltrate was located outside the central epithelial defect. The corneal epithelium directly overlying the infiltrate remained intact, and a lucid interval was observed between the infiltrate and limbus (Fig. 1C). No inflammatory cells were detected in the anterior chamber. Corneal sensation was not formally assessed at the initial examination.

The right eye was treated with preservative-free sodium hyaluronate 0.15% frequently (HyabakⓇ, Samil Pharm, South Korea), topical levofloxacin 1.5% three times daily (CravitⓇ, Santen, Japan), ofloxacin 0.3% ointment at bedtime (OcufloxⓇ, Samil Pharm, South Korea), topical 20% autologous serum four times daily, and topical cyclosporine 0.1% once daily (IkervisⓇ, Santen, Japan). The left eye was treated with preservative-free sodium hyaluronate 0.15% frequently, topical fortified antibiotics (vancomycin 50 mg/mL and ceftazidime 50 mg/mL) every 2 h. In addition, topical loteprednol etabonate 0.5% four times daily (LotemaxⓇ, Bausch & Lomb, USA) and oral acyclovir 400 mg four times daily were empirically administered to cover possible herpetic keratitis. To evaluate possible infectious keratitis in the left eye, samples for bacterial and fungal cultures were obtained from the leading margin of the epithelial defect adjacent to the peripheral infiltrate. Because the epithelium directly overlying the infiltrate was clinically intact and no ulceration was present at that site, it was left undisturbed to avoid unnecessary epithelial disruption. Tear film viral polymerase chain reaction (PCR) testing was also performed because herpetic keratitis with an immune-mediated stromal reaction was included in the initial differential diagnosis. During the first week, the viral PCR results were negative, and oral acyclovir was discontinued. As the clinical course suggested a low likelihood of bacterial infection and stability of the infiltrate, the fortified antibiotics were tapered from every 2 h to every 4 h and eventually three times daily. Simultaneously, loteprednol etabonate was tapered to three times daily, while cyclosporine 0.1% once daily and autologous serum eye drops four times daily were added to the left eye regimen.

In the second week, the final corneal cultures for bacterial and fungal pathogens were negative. Based on this confirmation of the sterile etiology, we discontinued the fortified antibiotics and switched to levofloxacin 1.5% three times daily. Moreover, loteprednol etabonate three times daily, cyclosporine 0.1% once daily, and autologous serum four times daily were administered.

By the third week, autologous serum therapy was discontinued in both eyes, and levofloxacin was stopped in the left eye. The right eye was maintained on sodium hyaluronate 0.15% frequently and cyclosporine 0.1% once daily; meanwhile, the left eye was treated with sodium hyaluronate 0.15% frequently, cyclosporine 0.1% once daily, and loteprednol etabonate three times daily. This therapeutic regimen was maintained until the 2-month follow-up.

The corneal epithelial defects in both eyes gradually resolved, and the stromal infiltrate density decreased over the treatment period (Fig. 2). Two months after presentation, the corneal epithelium had healed completely, with visual acuity recovering in both eyes. Faint peripheral corneal opacity persisted in the left eye; however, the defect was not visually significant (Fig. 3). No adverse events or recurrences were observed during the follow-up. The patient attended follow-up visits as scheduled and reported that she used the prescribed medications as instructed. During follow-up, the patient reported improvement in ocular discomfort and was satisfied with the recovery of vision. Discussion

To the best of our knowledge and based on a review of the available literature, discrete sterile corneal infiltration following chemical injury appears to be uncommon. In this case, a unilateral sterile peripheral infiltrate developed following a brief but severe chemical injury caused by makeup remover. Chemical injuries typically cause diffuse epithelial defects, ischemia, and stromal haze [9]. However, distinct sterile infiltrates are not typically observed. The patient reported immediate removal of the contact lens due to severe pain. Although the exposure was brief, the ingredient list of the makeup remover confirmed that it contained a surfactant/solubilizing agent (polyethylene glycol-6 caprylic/capric glycerides) and preservative or excipient components (cetrimonium bromide, disodium ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid, and propylene glycol). Irritants, such as household detergents and surfactants, are known to cause corneal de-epithelialization [9]. Furthermore, studies have demonstrated that even a single brief exposure to a surfactant can cause rapid and direct corneal cell death [10]. In addition, ophthalmic preservatives such as cetrimonium bromide are known to induce toxic epithelial damage and inflammatory cell infiltration in the cornea [11].

We propose that the soft contact lenses absorbed these chemicals and subsequently delivered a high concentration of toxic agents directly to the cornea upon insertion [12]. When corneal epithelial cells are damaged and undergo rapid cell death due to severe toxic insults, they release proinflammatory cytokines. These cytokines recruit inflammatory cells from limbal vessels into the cornea [13]. Consequently, the immediate yet highly concentrated chemical toxicity from the contaminated contact lens likely triggered a severe immune-mediated reaction, leading to discrete sterile infiltration.

The differential diagnosis included infectious keratitis, such as Acanthamoeba keratitis and herpetic keratitis, with an immune-mediated stromal reaction or immune ring, marginal keratitis, peripheral ulcerative keratitis, toxic keratopathy, and sterile immune-mediated infiltration. Toxic keratopathy was considered the primary cause of the bilateral extensive epithelial defects, given the immediate onset of symptoms after exposure to the makeup remover-contaminated contact lenses. However, toxic keratopathy alone did not fully explain the unilateral peripheral stromal infiltrate involving approximately 270° of the corneal periphery with a lucid interval. Acanthamoeba keratitis was considered because of the contact lens history, but the abrupt onset immediately after chemical exposure, absence of typical findings such as radial keratoneuritis, and subsequent clinical course made it less likely. Marginal keratitis and peripheral ulcerative keratitis were also considered, but there was no evidence of blepharitis or meibomian gland dysfunction, progressive stromal thinning, scleritis, or systemic autoimmune history. Herpetic keratitis with an immune-mediated stromal reaction was considered in the initial differential diagnosis; therefore, empirical oral acyclovir and viral polymerase chain reaction testing were performed. Distinguishing SCIs from infectious keratitis was particularly important in this case because the treatment strategies differ markedly. Infectious keratitis requires aggressive antibiotics, whereas SCIs require anti-inflammatory drugs such as steroids [3, 7]. In our case, several clinical features favored a sterile inflammatory process rather than active infectious keratitis. These included a lucid interval between the limbus and the infiltrate, intact epithelium overlying the peripheral infiltrate at the time of our initial examination, minimal anterior chamber reaction, negative microbiological work-up, and lack of clinical progression under close follow-up [4, 7]. These clinical features guided management and helped prevent unnecessary interventions.

At the initial visit, infectious keratitis could not be definitively excluded in the left eye because of the peripheral stromal infiltrate and large epithelial defect. Therefore, intensive fortified antibiotics and empirical oral antiviral therapy were initiated. At the same time, the infiltrate had several features suggestive of a sterile immune-mediated process, including intact overlying epithelium at the time of examination, a lucid interval from the limbus, and minimal anterior chamber reaction. Topical corticosteroids were therefore administered cautiously in the left eye under broad antimicrobial coverage and close follow-up, rather than after complete exclusion of infection. After viral polymerase chain reaction testing and bacterial and fungal cultures returned negative results, antiviral therapy and fortified antibiotics were discontinued, and anti-inflammatory treatment was continued with gradual tapering according to the clinical course. Previous reports of sterile corneal infiltrates after corneal procedures have described improvement with topical corticosteroid therapy [6, 8]. In the present case, topical corticosteroids were continued cautiously in the left eye after the likelihood of infection was considered low, and the stromal infiltrate gradually resolved during follow-up.

In contrast, the right eye showed no stromal infiltrate or anterior chamber reaction. Treatment in the right eye was therefore directed primarily toward epithelial recovery and ocular surface stabilization rather than suppression of stromal inflammation. Topical cyclosporine was used once daily as an adjunctive steroid-sparing anti-inflammatory agent for ocular surface inflammation, rather than as a primary treatment for acute chemical injury. Although experimental and review studies suggest that cyclosporine may help modulate ocular surface inflammation after corneal injury, direct clinical evidence supporting its early use in acute chemical injury remains limited [14, 15]. To minimize potential epithelial toxicity from multiple topical medications, preservative-free formulations were used whenever available, and the dosing frequency was reduced as soon as the clinical condition allowed.

Autologous serum was used in both eyes as an adjunctive epithelial-supportive treatment in the setting of extensive epithelial damage. Previous studies and reviews have described autologous serum as one of the biological treatments used to support ocular surface stabilization in chemical burn-related epithelial defects [16–18]. However, in the present case, autologous serum was not used as treatment for a formally defined persistent epithelial defect, and its individual contribution cannot be determined.

Interestingly, in our case, the chemical injury was bilateral, whereas the SCI was unilateral. Unilateral SCIs after bilateral refractive surgery have been reported previously [2, 7]. The precise mechanism underlying this observation remains unclear. However, severe post-operative pain or microtrauma can induce local inflammation and unilateral infiltration [2]. In our patient, the right contact lens was removed first, followed shortly by removal of the left lens. This difference in exposure time may have resulted in greater chemical toxicity in the left eye. However, the exact reason why the sterile infiltrate occurred only in one eye remains unclear. A higher concentration of makeup remover may have remained in the left contact lens or asymmetric mechanical stress may have contributed to this localized immune response.

This report has several limitations. First, as a single case report, it cannot establish a definite causal relationship between chemical exposure and sterile corneal infiltration. Second, the amount and concentration of makeup remover retained in each contact lens could not be quantified; therefore, the proposed explanation for unilateral infiltration remains speculative. Third, although the microbiological work-up was negative, infectious keratitis cannot be completely excluded. Because the peripheral infiltrate was covered by clinically intact epithelium at presentation, bacterial and fungal cultures were obtained from the adjacent leading margin of the epithelial defect rather than directly from the infiltrate itself to avoid unnecessary epithelial disruption. In addition, in vivo confocal microscopy was not performed, which may have provided further information regarding infectious or inflammatory etiologies. Fourth, corneal sensation was not formally assessed, limiting the evaluation of possible neurotrophic or herpetic components. Fifth, the possibility that the epithelium overlying the peripheral infiltrate had healed before presentation cannot be completely excluded. Finally, because multiple topical agents were used concurrently, the individual therapeutic contributions of topical corticosteroids, autologous serum, and cyclosporine could not be determined.

This case suggests that chemical exposure from contaminated contact lenses may cause not only epithelial toxicity but also secondary sterile corneal infiltration.

Conclusion

This case describes an unusual presentation of sterile peripheral corneal infiltration following chemical injury caused by contact lenses contaminated with makeup remover. Ophthalmologists should be aware that such chemical exposure may lead not only to epithelial toxicity but also to secondary sterile corneal infiltration that can mimic infectious keratitis. Careful slit-lamp examination and recognition of features such as a lucid interval and intact epithelium are important for distinguishing sterile from infectious infiltrates and for guiding appropriate treatment.
