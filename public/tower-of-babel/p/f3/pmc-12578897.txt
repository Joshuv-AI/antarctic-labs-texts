Machine learning-driven classification and prognostic prediction of kidney renal clear cell carcinoma using APOBEC family expression signatures
Authors: Zhen Ren, Yaru Zhu, Xiaochen Qi, Jianing Zhao, Shuhong Tang, Qichao Wang, Haizhe Ji, Jia He, Wei Tian, Xinru Tang, Peizhi Yang, Huanhuan Wu, Tianliang Zheng, Jingyuan Zhao, Hong Yuan, Jundong Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractApolipoprotein B mRNA-editing enzyme, catalytic polypeptide-like (APOBEC) cytidine deaminases are a highly evolutionarily conserved protein family. Their members are closely associated with DNA damage repair and involved in the genesis and progression of various tumors. Additionally, DNA damage contributes to the onset and progression of kidney renal clear cell carcinoma (KIRC). We collected KIRC samples from public datasets and applied clustering and machine learning methods to analyze the correlation between APOBEC families and KIRC prognosis. Based on APOBEC family expression, we classified KIRC patients into two clusters, which showed significant differences in drug sensitivity and survival. Subsequently, we compared the difference between the traditional coxph model and the machine learning model in predicting the prognosis of KIRC patients, and the results showed that the machine learning has better predictive ability. In addition, the study also predicted potential small-molecule drugs for KIRC treatment. This study proposes a method for KIRC classification and prognosis prediction based on APOBEC family expression. It may help clinicians predict patient drug sensitivity and survival, thereby guiding clinical medication and long-term treatment follow-up.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-21989-1.Keywords: Apolipoprotein b mRNA editing enzyme, Catalytic polypeptide-like (APOBEC), Kidney clear cell carcinoma, Machine learningSubject terms: Bioinformatics, Gene expression analysis, Cancer, Urological cancer

Introduction

Apolipoprotein B mRNA-editing enzyme, catalytic polypeptide-like (APOBEC) cytidine deaminases are a highly evolutionarily conserved protein family1. In humans, the APOBEC family comprises 11 members, including AID, APOBEC1, APOBEC2, APOBEC3A, APOBEC3B, APOBEC3C, APOBEC3D, APOBEC3F, APOBEC3G, APOBEC3H, and APOBEC4. APOBEC1 and AID are located on chromosome 12, APOBEC2 on chromosome 6, APOBEC4 on chromosome 1, and the rest on chromosome 222. The APOBEC family plays important physiological roles in humans. AID drives antibody isotype switching and enhances antigen affinity, thereby mediating critical immune responses2. APOBEC1 is primarily known as an RNA editor, mostly distributed in the digestive tract and involved in lipid transport2,3. APOBEC2 is predominantly distributed in skeletal and cardiac muscle, where it can regulate myocyte proliferation and differentiation, and may be associated with mitochondrial function4,5. The APOBEC3 subfamily is characterized by its viral restriction activity, and is mainly present in immune centers, peripheral blood cells, and IFN-γ-activated liver cells2,6. Beyond its immune role, mutations in this subfamily are important oncogenic factors2,7. There are fewer studies on APOBEC4, and only one study has reported that it is highly expressed in testicular tissue, but its function is unclear8.

Current studies suggest that cancer arises from the monoclonal expansion of cells with accumulated mutations. While mutations have long been considered random events induced by DNA replication errors or by DNA-damaging agents, genome sequencing led to the discovery of non-random mutation signatures in many human cancers9. These common non-random mutations include DNA strand-biased mutation showers and mutations restricted to specific DNA motifs—phenomena recently attributed to the catalytic activity of APOBEC family DNA deaminases9. Growing evidence indicates that the APOBEC family participates in the progression of a broad spectrum of tumors through multiple mechanisms. With the widespread application of multi-omics technologies, the APOBEC family has been recognized as an important marker in numerous tumors. Whole-genome sequencing revealed that APOBEC-associated mutational signatures were the dominant mutational features detected in esophageal squamous cell carcinoma samples, suggesting that APOBEC-mediated cytidine deamination may be a major driver of mutations in this cancer type10. In lung neuroendocrine carcinomas, the APOBEC family was found to influence tumor microenvironment heterogeneity and immunotherapy vulnerabilities11.

Increased TP53 mutation rate is associated with overexpression of AID in human lung cancer cell lines12. AID has also been implicated in the development of ovarian, gastric, and liver cancers13–16. APOBEC1 may promote carcinogenesis in the early stages of esophageal cancer17. Hyperactivation of APOBEC2 contributes to liver and lung tumorigenesis18. The APOBEC3 subfamily has a more prominent role in tumor progression. mutational signatures associated with APOBEC3 activity were found in more than over half of all tumors and were associated with tumor drug resistance and metastasis19. The incidence of kidney renal clear cell carcinoma (KIRC) is on the rise globally, especially in the younger population20,21. In 2020, there were over 431,000 new kidney cancer cases and 179,000 deaths worldwide22. While surgery remains the mainstay of KIRC treatment, a large proportion of patients still experience postoperative disease progression and recurrence23. Current research confirms that non-random DNA mutations drive KIRC progression24–26. Meanwhile, APOBEC family activity correlates with the occurrence of these non-random mutations9. This suggests that the APOBEC family may be involved in KIRC initiation and progression. There are few studies on the APOBEC family in KIRC. Hase’s team found that APOBEC3C promotes KIRC progression by mediating NF-κB activation27, and Tan’s team reported that the role of APOBEC3A functional polymorphisms in KIRC is regulated by tumor necrosis factor-α and the transcriptional repressor ETS128. Thus, the function of the entire APOBEC family in KIRC remains poorly understood, and the underlying mechanisms require further investigation.

Machine learning refers to techniques that enable computer systems to “learn” by analyzing large datasets: they automatically extract patterns and make predictions or decisions without explicit programming. By processing large volumes of imaging and clinical data, machine learning can provide clinicians with valuable references for disease diagnosis and prognosis prediction29–31.

In this study, we collected KIRC samples from public datasets and used clustering and machine learning methods to analyze the correlation between APOBEC family expression and KIRC prognosis. We hope to identify specific molecular mechanisms underlying KIRC progression and improve the clinical management of KIRC, as well as to provide a referenceable method for KIRC prognosis prediction.

Materials and methods

Data collection and pre-processing

RNA-seq, CNV data and clinical data of KIRC samples were all from TCGA-KIRC cohort in the TCGA database, with a total of 537 samples, including 72 cases of tumor adjacent tissues. After excluding the samples with survival time less than 30 days and missing clinical data, we obtained a total of 470 KIRC samples. We standardized the RNA-seq data and converted the count format to FPKM format for further analysis. Then we used the same processing pipeline to simultaneously obtain RNA-seq data, CNV data and clinical data of all cancer types in TCGA database for pan-cancer analysis.

Pan-cancer analysis and co-expression analysis

Based on COX regression, we calculated the risk coefficients of all gene members of the APOBEC family (AID, APOBEC1, APOBEC2, APOBEC3A, APOBEC3B, APOBEC3C, APOBEC3D, APOBEC3F, APOBEC3G, APOBEC3H and APOBEC4) in different cancer types. Meanwhile, based on the CNV frequency of APOBEC family genes in different cancer species, the correlation between mutation and mRNA expression was also shown in the form of line plots and heatmaps. Pan-cancer analysis can effectively and intuitively demonstrate the results and extent of the role of the APOBEC family in most cancers, which is conducive to improving the universality of the results obtained in subsequent analyses of single cancer species. In KIRC, we calculated the co-expression relationships among all APOBEC family genes using the Pearson correlation algorithm based on mRNA expression levels, and visualized their correlation coefficients as heatmaps.

Clustering analysis

We identified the expression level of each APOBEC family gene by comparing its expression in KIRC tumor tissue samples with that in adjacent tissues. After determining the optimal number of clusters (k = 3) based on the CDF (Cumulative Distribution Function) curve—with 50 iterations and 10 resampling rounds—we performed unsupervised consensus clustering on all samples. Based on gene expression patterns, we obtained three clusters in total (C1, C2, and C3), which represent expression clusters of different APOBEC family-related pathways. Using the GSVA algorithm, we calculated the expression level of the overall APOBEC family pathway for each sample, which was used to verify inter-group differences post-clustering. Survival differences between different groups were assessed from a clinical point of view by K-M curve analysis.

Immunoinfiltration analysis and immunotherapy response prediction

Based on TIMER, CIBERSORT, Cibersort-ABS, QUANTISEQ, Xcell, MCPCOUNTER and EPIC algorithms, we calculated the infiltration level of each immune component in different KIRC clusters under various algorithms, and presented the results in the form of heatmap. The infiltration of immune components in all KIRC samples was calculated by ssGSEA algorithm, and the correlation coefficients among all genes in the APOBEC family were calculated by pearson algorithm, and the correlation coefficients were displayed in the form of heatmap. At the same time, we re-calculated the correlation between the APOBEC-score calculated based on GSVA and the mRNA expression of PDCD-1 and CTLA-4, and presented the results in the form of scatter plots and heatmaps. TIDE algorithm (http://tide.dfci.harvard.edu/) and submap algorithm (https://cloud.genepattern.org/gp) were used to calculate the correlation between the APOBEC family cluster CTLA-4 and PD-1 therapy results based on mRNA expression similarity, and the results were displayed in the form of heatmap.

Correlation analysis of APOBEC family with histone modification and mitochondrial genetic function

HDAC is one of the important epigenetic factors, which can inhibit gene transcription by catalyzing histone deacetylation, leading to the compression and/or closure of local chromatin structure, and can also catalyze the deacetylation of non-histone proteins, thus playing a regulatory role on biological macromolecules. The SIRT family is a well-known group of deacetylases that rely on nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+). Among them, mitochondrial SIRTs (SIRT3, SIRT4, and SIRT5) are deacetylases located in mitochondria that regulate the acetylation levels of a variety of key proteins to maintain mitochondrial function and redox homeostasis. Mitochondrial SIRTs have been reported to exert a Janus-like role in tumorigenesis, acting both as tumor suppressors and oncogenes. Both histone modification and mitochondrial inheritance play important roles in tumor metabolism. Therefore, we extracted mRNA expression levels of HDAC, SIRT, and KIRC and used heat maps to show the differential expression of key histone modified genes between clusters with APOBEC expression differences. After that, we obtained mitochondria-related pathways and genes from the Mitocarta database, and calculated the correlation between APOBEC family pathway score and different mitochondrial function-related pathways.

Cmap analysis

Based on the Cmap algorithm and using the genes obtained after screening the APOBEC family, potential compounds with APOBEC molecular characteristics in KIRC patient samples were identified, with the Cmap score calculated via the Xsum algorithm—Yang et al.32. demonstrated that the eXtreme Sum (XSum) method for CMap analysis is most likely to yield meaningful results. Through sequential arrangement of CMAP-SCORE values, the 5 compounds with the lowest scores were selected as drug candidates.

Construction of APOBEC family 8 gene prediction model by machine learning

To investigate the APOBEC family’s prognostic prediction capability for KIRC patients and translate this into clinical applications, we employed machine learning models to construct multiple combined models through feature screening and model-building processes. To avoid model overfitting, we discarded models with fewer than 5 filtered features and used the ArrayExpress database and GSE22541 cohort for external validation. The training set: validation set = 3:1 where 5-fold Cross-Validation is performed. The results showed that various machine learning models performed well, with the support vector machine (SVM), ridge regression, and random forest models demonstrating the best performance.

In the prognostic model construction, we selected the random forest model, constructed an 8-gene prediction model after LASSO regression screening, and compared it with the COX regression model. Evaluated by AUC and C-index, the random forest model exhibited superior predictive ability compared to the COX regression model. The AUC measurement was time-accumulation-based, and the features included APOBEC family genes selected via LASSO regression. Subsequently, using the C-index as the evaluation criterion, we constructed a combined screening and prediction model with multiple machine learning models, incorporating the same LASSO-selected APOBEC family genes as features, and validated the model using the external ArrayExpress database. Finally, we developed a nomogram of the 8-gene COX regression model for clinical regression analysis.

Results

Correlation of the APOBEC family with KIRC

We evaluated the effect of APOBEC family on a variety of tumors by Cox risk-proportional modeling and LogRank modeling, and our results indicated that APOBEC family members serve as either risk or protective factors in a variety of tumors. Among them, APOBEC3B/D/H was recognized as a risk factor for poor prognosis of KIRC (Fig. 1A). At the same time, our team analyzed the copy number variation (CNV)of the APOBEC family in various tumors, and the results showed that some degree of variation in this family is common in various tumors (Fig. 1B). In addition, the results show that APOBEC family members are generally highly expressed in KIRC and the correlation coefficient between CNV of APOBEC2 and its mRNA expression level was high in KIRC. All of these results suggest a significant correlation between APOBEC family and KIRC (Fig. 1C&D).

(B) CNV profiling of APOBEC members across 33 cancer types (GISTIC2.0 analysis, TCGA pan-cancer atlas). (C) Tumor-normal expression disparities validated by Wilcoxon test in 15 solid tumors (paired/non-paired). Construction of tumor clusters and their characterization

The internal correlation of APOBEC family genes was analyzed and the results showed a low correlation coefficient within the APOBEC family (Fig. 2A). Unsupervised cluster analysis by R software was performed to classify the KIRC patients into three clusters, obtain an APOBEC-score and analyze their survival. The results showed that there were significant intergroup differences among the three clusters(p = 0.022), C1 and C2 are more similarly characterized, and the patients in the C3 group, which had the fewest APOBEC family high-expression profiles, had the best survival (Fig. 2B&C). RiskScore, TIMER and other multi-methods were used to assess the immune cell infiltration among the three groups. Overall, the immune infiltration profiles of C1 and C2 clusters were generally similar, while they differed significantly from C3. C1 and C2 had a higher degree of immune infiltration, in which CD8 + T cells and macrophages were significantly higher than in the C3 cluster (Supplementary Fig. 1). In response to this phenomenon, the investigators suggested that the large number of exhausted T cells and the imbalance of the M1/M2 macrophage ratio may be important reasons for the poor prognosis of C1 and C233,34.The correlation between APOBEC score and immune cell infiltration was fitted according to the ssGSEA algorithm, and the results indicated that APOBEC family members generally have a role in regulating immune cell infiltration in KIRC. This conclusion is obviously consistent with the above two results (Fig. 2D). Besides, we analyzed the expression of SIRT family, HDAC family and common proto-oncogenes and oncogenes in kidney cancer among the three clusters. It is well known that SIRT family members play a complex role in tumor progression. SIRT3 and SIRT5, which are more studied in renal cancer, are markers of good prognosis35,36, and within the three clusters of this study, it is evident that the expression of SIRT3 and SIRT5 is higher in Cluster3 compared to Cluster1 and Cluster2, while SIRT7, which is considered to be a proto-oncogene, SIRT737, on the other hand, has a lower expression in Cluster3, which is consistent with the results of the survival analysis(Fig. 2E). The histone deacetylase (HDAC) family, which is closely related to epigenetics, is associated with the progression of several tumors. Consistent with previous studies, our results showed that HDAC3,9,10, which are associated with poor prognosis in renal cancer, were highly expressed in Cluster1 and Cluster238–40. However, HDAC11 is more highly expressed in Cluster3(Fig. 2F). Unlike the remaining genes, HDAC11 has been less studied in renal cancer, but it demonstrates different roles in different tumors. In pancreatic neuroendocrine tumors and breast cancer, high expression of HDAC11 is associated with longer survival41,42, but in colon and lung cancer, HDAC11 represents a poor prognosis41. Differences in the expression of these genes may be another reason for the differences in survival of the three clusters.

Reconstruction of tumor clusters

Based on this characterization, the three clusters were subsequently subjected to a Kruskal-Wallis test, which showed that there was no significant difference between Cluster1 and Cluster2, but both were significantly different from Cluster3 (Fig. 3A). Subsequently, Cluster1 and Cluster2 were merged and again subjected to a Kruskal- Wallis test; the new cluster Cluster1 + 2 was significantly different from Cluster3. Survival analysis also showed significant differences between the new clusters (Fig. 3B&C). Based on the above results, Cluster1 + 2 was defined as an APOBEC-enriched APOBEC-active cluster, and Cluster3 was defined as APOBEC-inactive cluster. The responses of the two clusters to common immune checkpoint inhibitors were analyzed, and the results showed that the APOBEC-inactive cluster had a better response to PD-1 inhibitors (Fig. 3D). On the basis of unsupervised clustering of DEGs, the APOBEC score was computed to quantify the APOBEC model for each KIRC individual (Fig. 3E). Then, the APOBEC score was correlated with the expression of PD-1 and CTLA-related genes, and the results showed a positive correlation between the score and the expression of these genes, suggesting the significance of the subgroup and the score in guiding the clinical use of drugs. In addition, we used the database to predict the potential small molecule drug mercaptopurine (Fig. 3F).

Correlation of the APOBEC family with mitochondrial activity

Studies have shown that the APOBEC family is closely related to mitochondria5,43,44. Therefore, we performed a correlation analysis of the APOBEC family with mitochondrial central law-related pathways and genes (Fig. 4A). In the process of mtDNA maintenance, APOBEC score was mainly positively correlated with mtDNA replication and negatively correlated with mtDNA modifications. At the gene level, the genes that were strongly positively correlated with APOBEC scores included PIF1, MGME1, and POLQ, and there were also some genes that were negatively correlated with APOBEC scores, although the correlation was not very strong, such as METTL4, EXOG, and APEX1 (Fig. 4B). Similarly, during mtRNA metabolism, APOBEC score was negatively correlated with mtRNA granules, mt-tRNA modifications, mt-rRNA modifications, mt-mRNA modifications, and mtRNA stability and decay were all negatively correlated. Interestingly, unlike the mtDNA maintenance process, genes in the mtRNA metabolism process were mainly strongly negatively correlated with APOBEC scores, e.g., DHX30, PDE12, TRMT5, MTPAP, while some genes were positively correlated with APOBEC scores but not strongly, e.g., METTL8, THG1L, and others. THG1L, etc. (Fig. 4C). During translation, APOBEC scores were mainly negatively correlated with mitochondrial ribosome assembly and fMet processing. Despite the large number of genes involved in the translation process, only five genes, LARS2, MRPL50, TIMM21, EXD2, and DHX30, were strongly and negatively correlated with APOBEC scores (Supplementary Fig. 2).

Machine learning prognostic modelling

Previous studies have mostly used Cox proportional hazards regression (coxph) for survival analysis, and this study aims to explore prognostic models with better predictive power. Two models, coxph and random survival forest (rfsrc) model, were constructed to compare their predictive ability. Comparing the predictive ability of the two models, the results show that the Brier score values of rfsrc are consistently lower compared to coxph over the course of time, while the C/D AUC values are consistently higher than those of coxph, suggesting that the predictive ability of rfsrc is better (Fig. 5A). Similarly, the overall predictive ability of the model was assessed and the C-index value and Integrated C/D AUC of rfsrc were higher than those of coxph, while the Integrated Brier score value was lower, which indicates that the overall predictive ability of rfsrc is better(Fig. 5B). To improve the performance and interpretability of the model, Lasso was used for feature selection and regularization, thus avoiding overfitting and underfitting. The gene family was screened to nine (Fig. 5C&D). The effect of this gene family on the risk of developing KIRC was assessed using Hazard Ratio (HR). The results showed that APOBEC3B, APOBEC3D and APOBEC3A increased the risk of KIRC, except for APOBEC3F, which had a HR < 1(Fig. 5E).

Long-term prognostic prediction of KIRC based on APOBEC family

Then, we used the two methods to examine the importance of time-dependent variables for the whole cohort. The coxph model showed the greatest effect of APOBEC3D on KIRC prognosis at different time points, and rfsrc model showed it to be APOBEC3H, which is consistent with our previous findings (Fig. 6A). Two samples were randomly selected to study the time-dependent survival contribution of each risk factor to OS in the selected patients using SurvSHAP(t) plots. Under the coxph model, both samples showed that APOBEC3D had the greatest effect on survival probability at different time points. Under the rfsrc model, the analyses for the two samples were APOBEC3C and APOBEC3H (Fig. 6B&C). External data were evaluated using rfsrc, ROC curves were plotted, and AUCs were calculated. The results showed that the model had a fair predictive ability for external data (Fig. 6D). In addition, APOBEC family-based constructed nomogram to predict patient survival at 3, 5, and 7 years (Fig. 6E). Overall, the predictive power of machine learning models is superior to that of conventional coxph. In order to explore models with better predictive performance, this study integrated multiple combinations of 10 machine learning algorithms (CoxBoost, StepCox, Lasso, Ridge, Enet, survival-SVMs, GBM, SuperPC, plsRcox, and RSF) to build Consensus Machine Learning-Driven Models (CMLS) and tested all validation cohorts and calculated the average C-index for each model, where the model with the highest value was considered the optimal model (Fig. 6F). The results showed that survivalSVM is the optimal machine learning model, which may be a direction we explore in the future.

Discussion

The incidence of KIRC is increasing worldwide20,21. Despite the fact that surgical treatments for KIRC have become more sophisticated, a large number of patients still experience postoperative recurrence and metastasis23. The APOBEC family plays an important physiological role in the human body. Current research suggests that cancer arises from monoclonal expansion of certain cells following mutations that are associated with APOBEC family activity9. The APOBEC family has been shown to be involved in tumorigenesis and progression in a variety of tumors, but the significance of this family in KIRC is not yet clear.

The combination of machine learning and clinical medicine is gradually opening up a new era in the medical field. By intelligently analyzing a large amount of clinical data, machine learning can assist doctors in diagnosing diseases more accurately, predicting the development of the disease and formulating personalized treatment plans. A machine learning model that analyzes CT images with clinical data shows good performance in predicting recurrence and metastasis of primary gastrointestinal mesenchymal stromal tumors29. Machine learning can also serve as a complementary tool to provide clinicians with a second opinion for the prognostic prediction of psychiatric disorders30.

This research identified the association of APOBEC family members with a variety of tumors in public databases and confirmed that APOBEC3B/D/H are risk factors for poor prognosis in KIRC. Previous studies have also shown that these three genes promote tumor progression through different mechanisms. APOBEC3B was confirmed to regulate R-loops and promote transcription-associated mutagenesis in cancer45. APOBEC3D was shown to enhance the survival of pancreatic cancer cells after treatment with gemcitabine by deamidating cytosine in the cell’s nuclear genome and ensuring the restart and repair of DNA replication forks46. In lung cancer, deamidation of APOBEC3H haplotype I increases mutation generation and thus promotes tumor progression47. However, the roles of these genes in the progression of KIRC is unclear and needs to be demonstrated experimentally, but we speculate that it may be related to mutation accumulation or DNA damage repair.

We subsequently divided the KIRC samples into 2 clusters by means of clustering, and confirmed that different clusters had significant differences in immune infiltration, survival time, expression of proto-oncogenes and oncogenes, and drug sensitivity, etc. This gives a scientific reference for clinical guidance of drug administration. In addition, we explored the possible application of mercaptopurine, a small molecule drug, for the treatment of KIRC. It is an immunosuppressant and antitumor agent belonging to the thiopurine class of drugs, which is currently used mainly in the treatment of leukemia and has not yet been widely applied to kidney cancer48. Relevant study has shown that low-dose mercaptopurine has the ability to improve drug resistance in triple-negative breast cancer49. In addition, it has also been shown that loading mercaptopurine via a nano-delivery system can improve its short half-life and low water solubility, thereby increasing its efficacy for the treatment of other tumors50,51. This may be a feasible way to apply mercaptopurine to KIRC in the future.

In order to explore a better prognostic model, this research constructed a machine learning model, which was superior to the previously routinely used coxph in terms of prediction ability and had a more favorable prediction ability to external data. Additionally, the genes identified by coxph and random forest for survival regression (rfsrc) as major contributors to KIRC prognosis were largely consistent with the previously identified APOBEC3B, APOBEC3D, and APOBEC3H. Additionally, this study integrated multiple machine learning methods to establish the CMLS, which lays the foundation for future research.

Overall, this study could help doctors predict kidney cancer progression earlier and develop personalized treatment plans. However, this study also has certain limitations. First, this study lacks validation in clinical samples, as well as at the cellular and animal levels—leading to limited exploration of the underlying mechanisms. Second, the limited number of datasets included introduces a certain degree of selection bias. Furthermore, advances in multi-omics technologies and continuous improvements in algorithms have paved the way for more accurate prognostic predictions52,53. Simultaneously, the integration of artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning has emerged as a major trend54. Therefore, in the future, it may be possible to improve prognostic prediction accuracy by integrating multiple types of data, including genomics, transcriptomics, metabolomics, imaging data, and clinical data. Alternatively, the validity and reliability of the model could be verified through large-scale clinical trials.

Conclusion

In this study, tumor clusters and a disease prognostic prediction model were developed using a clustering method combined with a machine learning approach to assess drug sensitivity and prognosis in KIRC patients. The role of APOBEC family-related genes in KIRC progression was clarified, and potential small-molecule drugs for KIRC treatment were identified. This study provides a referenceable basis for guiding clinical medication and assessing prognosis in KIRC patients.
