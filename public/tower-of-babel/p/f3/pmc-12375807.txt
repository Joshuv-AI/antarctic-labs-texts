Hirayama disease: a rare cause of cervical myelopathy
Authors: Amin Khan, Aditya Duhan, Rajiv Mangla, Manisha Mangla, Sajjad Ghanim Al-Badri
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractHirayama disease (HD) is a rare cervical myelopathy involving lower cervical myotomes, causing asymmetric or unilateral distal upper extremity muscle atrophy. Diagnosis relies on clinical findings, nerve conduction studies, and cervical spine MRI in neutral and flexion positions. We present a 17-year-old male with painless, progressive weakness and atrophy of the left forearm and hand muscles, along with hyperhidrosis. Initial cervical spine MRI revealed loss of cervical lordosis, lower cervical hemicord atrophy, and increased T2 signal intensity in the anterior horn cells. Flexion MRI demonstrated anterior shifting of the posterior dura, cervical cord compression, venous plexus congestion, and posterior epidural space widening with enhancement, confirming Hirayama disease. In adolescent males with cervical cord atrophy and asymmetric flattening on routine MRI, Hirayama disease should be suspected. Flexion MRI is crucial for identifying anterior dural shifting and related changes, ensuring accurate diagnosis.

Background

Hirayama disease (HD), is a rare sporadic cervical myelopathy that usually involves the lower cervical myotomes and causes a muscular atrophy disorder of the distal upper extremities. The disease primarily occurs in adolescent males. Common clinical characteristics include a gradual onset and a slow progression of muscle atrophy that is usually unilateral or asymmetric, affecting mainly the muscles of the forearm and hands while sparing the brachioradialis muscle. This results in a distinctive appearance known as oblique amyotrophy [1].

The pathophysiology of HD is yet to be completely delineated. It is postulated that in utero there is an uneven growth between the developing vertebral column and the dura mater, which results in the dura mater becoming shorter and more stretched out. As a result, when one flexes their neck, it can cause the posterior dural sac and cervical cord to shift anteriorly, which then results in compression of the cervical cord against the vertebral column. As the cord is compressed against the bony vertebrae anteriorly, the anterior vertebral plexus is also compressed. This causes the flow to be redirected to the posterior venous plexus with resultant congestion. As the congestion progresses, it increases the intramedullary pressure, and this increase in intramedullary pressure is thought to lead to microcirculatory changes in the anterior horn of the spinal cord leading to focal areas of ischemia [2, 3]. The fact that Cervical collar therapy in order to ensure neutral neck positioning has been shown to improve outcomes favors this hypothesis [4].

The diagnosis of HD is difficult and requires high clinical suspicion and the help of various different modalities in order to make the diagnosis. This includes a robust clinical history, physical exam findings, nerve conduction tests (NCT), electromyography (EMG), and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the cervical spine with and without contrast [5]. Lastly, as we will discuss in our case presentation, it is paramount for the diagnosis of HD that a dynamic MRI cervical spine should be performed in both the neutral and flexed positions.

Case presentation

We present the case of a 17-year-old male patient who presented with painless progressive weakness atrophy of the muscles of the left forearm and hand, and hyperhidrosis. Upon physical exam a C7-T1 myotomal distribution of involvement was noted. The flexor carpi radialis and ulnaris, interossei, thenar and hypothenar muscles showed wasting and were tested for strength and was rated at an average of 2.7/5 on the medicine research council manual muscle testing method scale. Of note, the brachioradialis was spared and displayed normal strength. The patient also exhibited hyperhidrosis and cold paresis, a common finding in patients with Hirayama disease The patient’s physical exam endorsed increased motor weakness, brisk reflexes, and fasciculations in the left deltoid. Of note sensory examination was done and was negative for any alterations in vibration, temperature, sharp, dull, or light touch. EMG findings revealed a high amplitude of action potentials with prolonged duration and an absence of positive sharp waves or fibrillation potentials in the C7-C8 and T1 innervated muscles. This was consistent with chronic denervation without active denervation, consistent with chronic active motor neuronopathy. The clinical picture was suggestive of a juvenile muscular atrophy disorder. An MRI of the brain with contrast and a lumbar puncture was performed which was unremarkable for any findings concerning primary demyelinating disorders. A decision was made to perform an MRI of the cervical spine with contrast.

Images were acquired on a 1.5 Tesla scanner (Achieva, Philips Medical Systems ©, Best, The Netherlands). The MRI protocol initially included imaging in a neutral position only, however imaging in a hyperflexed position was also performed after suspicious findings on initial imaging. Hyperflexion of the neck was achieved by asking the subject to first extend the head as far back as possible and then to tuck his chin towards his chest. The subjects’ shoulders were placed as far caudal as possible.

Neutral MRI of the cervical spine with and without contrast showed loss of normal cervical lordotic curvature, focal atrophy, and flattening of the hemicord, and an associated left-sided asymmetric increase in T2 signal intensity of the anterior horn grey matter (Figure 1). Importantly, there was no evidence of loss of dural attachment. There was an increased suspicion of Hirayama disease and a decision was made to perform an MRI cervical spine on flexed neck positioning. A Flexion MRI was done and demonstrated an increase in the posterior epidural space, along with anterior translation of the posterior dura and cervical spinal cord, with resultant pressure of the cervical spine against the vertebral column (Figure 2). There was also evidence of likely venous congestion, as the MRI displayed prominent flow voids and enhancement in this region, on the postgadolinium fat-suppressed T1-weighted images. The maximum forward shifting of posterior dura, measured on flexion MR postgadolinium fat-suppressed T1 WI in the mid-sagittal plane was 6.7 mm.

Discussion

HD is a rare neurological condition involving the lower cervical spine, characterized primarily by unilateral or asymmetric muscular atrophy and progressive in a unique myotomal distribution, most commonly the C7-T1 myotomes. As such, it is important to differentiate HD from other more common conditions that may cause upper limb muscle atrophy. The more common conditions include cervical spondylosis, peripheral nerve compression secondary to the thoracic outlet, supinator, cubital tunnel and carpal tunnel syndromes, intramedullary tumors, ossification of the posterior longitudinal ligament, and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) [6–8].

A robust clinical history and physical exam, consideration of the first age of onset, and utilizing a wide variety of diagnostic modalities allow for distinguishing between the above-mentioned conditions including electrophysiological testing and imaging. Peripheral nerve compression disorders of the upper limbs can be ruled out with the help of electrophysiological testing, and the combination of EMG testing imaging is instrumental in ruling out intramedullary tumors, cervical spondylosis, and ALS.The unique myotomal distribution can be helpful in allowing us to differentiate HD from MMN and other compressive neuropathies. It is important to note that cervical spondylotic amyotrophy and ALS can often be mistaken for HD in the early stages of the disease [3, 9, 10].

Imaging is crucial for diagnosing HD. Conventional MRI in a neutral position might reveal atrophy and asymmetric flattening of the cervical cord, with or without abnormal T2-weighted hyperintensities, particularly in the anterior horn cells as was seen in this case. We postulate that if there are findings of atrophy and asymmetric flattening of the cervical cord on a routine neutrally positioned MRI of the cervical spine, then Hirayama disease should be considered, and in such cases, a Flexion MRI should be performed due to its increased sensitivity and specificity. As was seen in our case, specific findings include posterior dural detachment, widening of the posterior epidural space, and moderate to severe forward shifting of the posterior dural sac and cervical cord. Post-gadolinium flexion MRI can also help by displaying increased enhancement in the region of the engorged posterior epidural venous plexus, which is thought to increase intramedullary pressure and cause regions of focal ischemia within the anterior horn grey matter, thus the signal abnormalities and hemiatrophy. Some studies have highlighted the utility of 3D-CISS for better visualization of epidural flow voids in MRI [5, 10].

It is important to note that studies have found that forward shifting of the posterior dural sac is a relatively non-specific finding and found in healthy patients. However, in healthier patients, the extent of forward shift is typically mild in healthy subjects (1.0 to 4.2 mm) compared to patients with HD (6.1 to 7.8 mm). Another important point to consider is the ratio of the Laminodural space to spinal canal diameter should increase in HD patients during flexion, while the ratio of spinal cord dimensions should decrease [5, 9].

Early diagnosis of HD is crucial, as it allows for recommendations to limit neck flexion, thereby potentially preventing disease progression [9]. Such recommendations may include the application of a cervical brace [4]. A high degree of suspicion by the radiologist is essential for diagnosing HD, as a neutral position MRI may not always reveal the diagnosis.
