Impact of Baseline Cardiogenic Shock Severity on Outcomes in Patients Treated With Impella Device
Authors: Hiroaki Yokoyama, Shinobu Hosokawa, Masataka Sata, Koichi Kishi, the J‐PVAD Investigators
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRisk stratification of patients with cardiogenic shock (CS) is of considerable importance. We aimed to test the utility of the Society for Cardiovascular Angiography and Intervention shock staging system recently refined by the CS Working Group in an Asian population and to investigate its association with in‐hospital adverse events.MethodsFrom the Japanese nationwide registry of Impella, the patients with CS who successfully received Impella support between February 2020 and December 2023 were included and assigned to any stage according to the CS Working Group–refined Society for Cardiovascular Angiography and Intervention staging system. We set the primary end point as in‐hospital death and explored it within each stage, with stage B as a reference.ResultsIn total, 4643 patients were analyzed (median age, 69 years; women, 23.3%; CS due to acute myocardial infarction, 61.7%), and 122, 588, 1905, and 2058 patients were in stages B, C, D, and E, respectively. After adjustment with potential confounders, stage C had no significant difference in in‐hospital death (odds ratio, 1.12 [95% CI, 0.69–1.80]; P=0.653). However, with adjusted analysis, stages D and E had a significantly higher rate of in‐hospital death (stage D: odds ratio, 2.41 [95% CI, 1.55–3.77]; P<0.001; stage E: odds ratio, 4.90 [95% CI, 3.14–7.66]; P<0.001).ConclusionsThe CS Working Group–refined Society for Cardiovascular Angiography and Intervention staging system at baseline has a satisfactory association with in‐hospital death in the more progressive stage; however, the system seems to have a lower power in the earlier phase of CS. Further investigation is warranted to establish a more accurate staging system.Keywords: cardiogenic shock, Impella, mechanical circulatory support, percutaneous ventricular assist deviceSubject Categories: Cardiopulmonary Resuscitation and Emergency Cardiac Care, Myocardial Infarction, Heart Failure

Cardiogenic shock (CS) is a fatal condition that begins with impaired cardiac function, leading to systemic hypoperfusion and resulting in hemodynamic, neurohormonal, and metabolic changes that progressively worsen without treatment. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 Despite therapeutic advances in drug and mechanical circulatory support (MCS) devices over the past 2 decades, the short‐term mortality rate in patients with CS remains largely unchanged and high, ranging between 30% and 60%. 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 In CS management, the treatment effect varies according to the mixed pathogeneses, phenotypes, and clinical presentations, and consistent parameters used to accurately assess shock severity were lacking. 8 , 9 Recent publications have reported that the degree of hemodynamic and metabolic responsiveness to comprehensive therapies depends on the patient profile. 4 , 9 , 10

Impella (Abiomed, Danvers, MA), a percutaneous transvalvular microaxial flow pump, has recently emerged as a new device for temporary MCS in CS management, in addition to the intra‐aortic balloon pump and venoarterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VA‐ECMO). Overall, retrospective data on Impella use in CS compared with standard therapy are heterogeneous. 11 However, the DanGer Shock (Danish–German Cardiogenic Shock) trial demonstrated that Impella use in addition to standard therapy reduced the risk of all‐cause death at 180 days compared with standard therapy alone among patients with CS complicated with ST‐segment–elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI). 12 Furthermore, a previous large‐scale study using propensity score matching demonstrated that patients treated with a combination therapy of VA‐ECMO and Impella had significantly lower mortality rates than those treated with VA‐ECMO alone. 13 The feasibility of using the Impella in CS is expected to increase in the coming years.

The Society for Coronary Angiography and Intervention (SCAI) proposed a staging system for CS based on a multidisciplinary panel of clinical experts in 2019. 4 , 14 SCAI stage was categorized into 5 classes of CS (A–E), ranging from “at risk” to “extreme” CS, and provided robust in‐hospital mortality risk stratification. 15 Subsequently, the Cardiogenic Shock Working Group (CSWG) proposed a modified SCAI stage based on the treatment intensity of drugs (vasopressors and inotropes) and MCS devices reflected the clinical course (=CSWG‐modified SCAI stage). 4 Furthermore, the CSWG refined the modified staging system using selected clinical parameters reflective of hypotension and hypoperfusion and defined specific ranges of each parameter to define each SCAI stage. 9 However, the staging system has been evaluated for in‐hospital death only in Western countries and not in Asian countries. Therefore, the primary objective of this study was to evaluate whether the staging system successfully stratifies patients with CS at risk of in‐hospital adverse events in the Asian population as well, to assess associations between baseline patient profiles and in‐hospital adverse events in various settings within each stage, and to further refine the staging system.

METHODS

Data Source

We used the data set of the J‐PVAD (Japanese Registry for Percutaneous Ventricular Assist Device) to investigate outcomes according to CS severity. The J‐PVAD registry is a nationwide multicenter observational study, and all patients who underwent Impella device insertion at qualified centers certified by the Impella Committee were enrolled. The J‐PVAD registry prospectively collects clinical characteristics and outcome data during index hospitalization and is registered with the University Hospital Medical Information Network (UMIN000033603). This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Central Institutional Review Board of Osaka University (approval number: 17232). The J‐PVAD registry was funded by the Impella Committee. The requirement for written informed consent was waived owing to the implementation of complete data anonymization and the retrospective observational nature of the present study. 16 Regarding data availability, the deidentified participant data will not be shared.

Study Population

Between February 2020 and December 2023, all patients who attempted to receive the Impella device at 279 hospitals across Japan were included. The exclusion criteria included Impella use due to factors other than cardiogenic shock, such as supplementary use at high‐risk percutaneous coronary intervention, unsuccessful Impella insertion, missing data on patients' status at discharge and laboratory data defining the SCAI stage.

CS was defined as systolic blood pressure ≤90 mm Hg for ≥30 minutes and cardiac index <2.2 L/min per m2 if measured or the need for intravenous vasopressors and inotropes to maintain the blood pressure and cardiac index, accompanied by end‐organ hypoperfusion. When a patient had received another MCS device (eg, extracorporeal membrane oxygenation and intra‐aortic balloon pump) before Impella insertion, the patient was considered to have CS, irrespective of the indications for other MCS devices. 16

Only 1 primary indication for Impella use at the time of insertion was selected from the following diseases by the treating physicians at each anticipated institution in the J‐PVAD registry: acute myocardial infarction (AMI) (with or without mechanical complications), myocarditis, nonischemic cardiomyopathy, ventricular arrhythmia, valvular heart disease, and others. The choice of primary indication was left to the discretion of the local physicians. 16

Impella Devices Used in the Present Analysis

In this registry, the types of PVADs included Impella 2.5, CP, 5.0, and 5.5. In Japan, Impella 2.5 and 5.0 were approved by the Pharmaceuticals and Medical Devices Agency of the Japanese Ministry of Health, Labour, and Welfare in September 2016; Impella CP in March 2019; and Impella 5.5 in November 2021. Impella 2.5 and 5.0 became commercially available in September 2017, Impella CP in May 2019, and Impella 5.5 in February 2022. 1 , 16

SCAI Classification and Novel Framework

The patients were assessed before the Impella use, according to the definition of SCAI stages refined by the CSWG (=CSWG‐refined SCAI stage). 9 The staging criteria were defined on the basis of the key publication. 9 pH ≤7.2 was included in the original criteria of stage E. However, we had no information on pH in this registry data set; thus, we could not consider pH levels.

We had concern about whether all patients who experienced out‐of‐hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) were included in stage E was appropriate. In the above key publication, 9 the difference in in‐hospital death between patients with OHCA with and without return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC) was not evaluated. In fact, among the patients with OHCA in this study cohort, those without ROSC had significantly higher rates of in‐hospital death than those with ROSC (with ROSC, 48.7%; without ROSC, 70.5%; P<0.001). Moreover, according to previous reports, in‐hospital cardiac arrest (IHCA) can adversely affect the prognosis of patients with CS. 17 , 18 In this study cohort, patients with IHCA had significantly higher in‐hospital mortality rates than those without IHCA (with IHCA, 58.9%; without IHCA, 40.2%; P<0.001). Using logistic analysis, the odds ratio for in‐hospital death in OHCA and IHCA were 1.70 (95% CI, 1.49–1.94) and 2.14 (95% CI, 1.89–2.42), respectively. Thus, we simply categorized the patients with IHCA or with OHCA and without ROSC as new stage “F,” which was a more progressive stage than original stage E (Figure S1).

End Points and Definitions

The primary interest of this study was in‐hospital death and complications during the index hospitalization, which were compared for each SCAI stage. In‐hospital death and complications were defined as composite events. Complications were defined as any bleeding, stroke, transient ischemic attack, access site–related infection, vascular injury necessitating repair, lower limb ischemia, hemolysis, cardiac tamponade, ventricular perforation, aortic valve regurgitation, and aortic valve injury.

As a subgroup analysis of the impact of SCAI stage on outcomes in the AMI subset, we conducted the following: (1) The difference in in‐hospital death between the AMI and non‐AMI populations was evaluated for each SCAI stage; and (2) in the AMI population, the effect of previous coronary artery disease (CAD) and types of AMI (STEMI or non‐STEMI) on in‐hospital death was evaluated for each SCAI stage. In addition, within each SCAI stage, the differences in background characteristics between survivors and nonsurvivors during the index hospitalization were evaluated.

CSWG‐modified SCAI stage was previously proposed on the basis of the treatment intensity derived from the total number of drugs, such as inotropes or vasopressors, and MCS devices. 4 We also evaluated the effect of treatment intensity before the Impella implantation on in‐hospital death in this registry.

Statistical Analysis

All the data were collected from the J‐PVAD registry database. The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was used to assess data normality. Continuous and categorical variables are expressed as means±SD or medians (interquartile ranges [quartiles 1–3]), as appropriate, and as numbers (percentages), respectively. For continuous variables, data were compared between 2 groups using Student's t test or the Mann–Whitney U test, and 1‐way ANOVA or the Kruskal–Wallis test was conducted for comparisons among >3 groups. Categorical variables were analyzed using the χ2 or Fisher's exact test. The odds ratio with 95% CI for in‐hospital adverse events within each SCAI stage was evaluated using logistic regression models with stage B as the reference. The confounders used to adjust the odds ratio calculations were age, sex, body mass index, AMI or non‐AMI, types of Impella device, previous CAD, previous heart failure, and previous stroke or transient ischemic attack. Moreover, we compared the prognostic power for in‐hospital death between the current CSWG‐refined SCAI stage and our novel framework using a receiver operating characteristic curve. A P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 27 (IBM Corp., Chicago, IL) and EZR version 1.68 (Saitama Medical Center, Japan).

RESULTS

Patient Characteristics

Of the 8808 patients who attempted to receive the Impella device, 4165 were excluded, and 4643 patients with CS and successful Impella insertion were analyzed (median age, 69 years; women, 23.3%; AMI‐CS, 61.7%; Table 1, Figure 1). Of these, 122 (2.6%) were classified as stage B, 588 (12.0%) stage C, 1905 (41.0%) stage D, and 2058 (44.3%) stage E (Figure 2). The baseline characteristics of the overall and within stage B through E subgroups are shown in Table 1. Stages B and C had more AMI‐CS than stages D and E (B, 73.8% and C, 71.7% versus D, 58.1% and E, 61.9%). The pathogeneses of CS in the non‐AMI population were myocarditis (10.3%), arrhythmia (6.4%), cardiomyopathy (6.2%), and valvular disease (2.8%) (Figure 3). The number of IHCA tended to be higher in more severe populations (B, 8.2% and C, 13.8% versus D, 27.8% and E, 47.6%), and such populations had a larger number of previous CAD and previous heart failure history, and worse renal and liver function at baseline laboratory data (median of serum creatine [mg/dL]: B, 0.97 and C, 1.05 versus D, 1.28 and E, 1.28; median alanine aminotransferase) ([U/L]: B, 25 and C, 30 versus D, 44 and E, 78). The median lactate levels at stages B through E were 1.56, 2.86, 4.22, and 11.0 mmol/L, respectively. Overall (n=4106), the median time from shock onset to Impella insertion was 152 minutes, and the patients who were supported by Impella within 180 minutes had a significantly lower in‐hospital mortality rate than those >180 minutes (P=0.037) (Tables S1 and S2). Regarding the primarily used Impella device, Impella 2.5/CP was inserted in >95% of patients within each stage.

In‐Hospital Outcomes

Overall, the in‐hospital mortality rate in this study cohort was 46.6% and tended to be higher in the more severe populations (stage B, 23.8% and C, 24.9% versus D, 41.9% and E, 58.3%; Figure 2). Regarding adverse events during the index hospitalization, the composite events of in‐hospital death and complications occurred in 54.1%, 56.6%, 65.2%, and 74.8% of patients with stages B, C, D, and E, respectively, and more severe cases had higher rates (Table 2). Overall, the most common complication was bleeding (24.7%), of which 93.0% required transfusion. Other common complications included hemolysis (13.9%), cerebrovascular events (6.9%), lower limb ischemia (5.6%), infection at the Impella access site (2.5%), and cardiac tamponade (2.5%) (Table 2). Major complication rates were similar across SCAI stages; however, with respect to hemolysis, stages B and C had higher rates (23.0% and 22.2%, respectively) than stages D and E (13.8% and 11.2%, respectively). After Impella insertion, 599 (12.9%) patients additionally received VA‐ECMO; after Impella removal, 86 (1.9%) and 243 (5.2%) patients required VA‐ECMO and intra‐aortic balloon pump, respectively.

Associations Between SCAI Stages and In‐Hospital Outcomes

The unadjusted and adjusted adverse event rates of patients within each SCAI stage, with stage B as a reference, are shown in Table 3. At baseline, with unadjusted analysis, there were no significant differences in composite events, in‐hospital death, and complications between stages B and C. These results were consistent even after adjusting with several clinical confounders. However, in both unadjusted and adjusted analyses, stages D and E had significantly higher rates of composite events and in‐hospital death than stage B. Regarding complications, compared with stage B, in both unadjusted and adjusted analyses, stages D and E showed no significant differences, which was consistent with stage C.

Subanalysis: AMI Subset

Overall, there was a comparable rate of in‐hospital mortality between the AMI‐CS and non–AMI‐CS groups (AMI‐CS, 47.6%; non–AMI‐CS, 45.1%; P=0.109), and within each SCAI stage other than stage D, a similar tendency was observed (stage B, P=0.145; C, P=0.664; D, P=0.010; and E, P=0.290; Figure 3). In the AMI population, patients with previous CAD (n=607) had a higher in‐hospital mortality rate than those without previous CAD (n=2078) (CAD positive, 50.6%; CAD negative, 44.9%; P=0.016); within each SCAI stage other than stage E, a similar tendency was observed (stage B, P=0.104; C, P=0.301; D, P=0.718; and E, P=0.004; Figure S2). However, patients with STEMI (n=2269) showed no significant difference in the in‐hospital mortality rate compared with patients with non‐STEMI (n=474) (n=127 missing data on types of AMI) (STEMI, 46.8%; non‐STEMI, 44.7%; P=0.418), and within each SCAI stage other than stage C, a similar tendency was observed (stage B, P=0.061; C, P=0.019; D, P=0.755; and E, P=0.112; Figure S2).

Difference in Characteristics of Survivors Versus Nonsurvivors Within Each SCAI Stage

We examined the baseline characteristics of the survivors and nonsurvivors (Table S3). Within each stage, the survivors had a comparable number of women, with significantly younger age and higher body mass index than the nonsurvivors. Regarding vital signs and laboratory data, except for stage B, the survivors had significantly higher blood pressure and lower serum lactate and creatinine levels than the nonsurvivors. However, alanine aminotransferase levels did not correlate with shock severity.

Novel Framework

All the patients were assigned to a novel framework. In total, 112 (2.4%) were classified as stage B, 481 (10.4%) stage C, 1376 (29.6%) stage D, 910 (19.6%) stage E, and 1764 (38.0%) stage F. The areas under the curve (AUCs) using receiver operating characteristic curves for in‐hospital death in CSWG‐refined SCAI stage and novel framework were 0.624 and 0.641, respectively (P=0.009). Similar to the CSWG‐refined SCAI staging system, in‐hospital mortality rate tended to be higher in more severe populations (B, 24.1% and C, 23.7% versus D, 37.4%, E, 48.7%, and F, 60.4%; Figure S3).

In CS management, the SCAI staging system was proposed and upgraded to help clinicians make decisions regarding treatment alternatives. 19 However, this system might have limited usefulness. Numerous observational studies have demonstrated a correlation between increasing SCAI stage and increased risk of death; however, the SCAI staging system did not provide an initial estimate of death compared with other risk scores. 20 , 21 Moreover, shock status changes rapidly, and its temporal change cannot be reflected in a rigid classification. 22 Thus, how this system could be used most effectively to prognosticate remained to be determined. 20 Subsequently, the CSWG set stage‐defining parameters and clarified the usefulness of the refined staging system, 9 , 17 , 23 , 24 and the SCAI staging system can be helpful in more easily communicating the severity of the current clinical status of patients with each physician. 20

Validation of the SCAI staging system has been investigated only in Western countries, and few reports assessing the SCAI staging system with statistical significance have been published to date. Compared with this study, which targeted the Asian population, previous reports had several differences in patient profiles and CS pathogenesis, and their in‐hospital mortality rate was relatively lower. For instance, in a comparison between this study and the key publication (n=3455; overall in‐hospital mortality rate, 35%) that established the CSWG‐refined SCAI staging system, 9 in particular, this study included a greater number of patients with OHCA (25.4% versus 6.4%). As mentioned above, in patients with CS, OHCA is strongly associated with a poor prognosis. Nevertheless, within each SCAI stage at baseline, the pattern of in‐hospital death appeared to be similar to that observed in this study (no significant difference between stages B and C, which were earlier phases of CS). The AUC using the receiver operating characteristic curve in the CSWG‐refined SCAI staging system revealed a weak correlation with in‐hospital death (AUC, 0.624); therefore, we proposed a simple and novel framework that differentiated patients with cardiac arrest. The AUC in our novel framework significantly increased (P=0.009) but still had low power to predict in‐hospital death (AUC, 0.641). We believe that it will be a future challenge to reconsider the stage‐defining parameters that would allow for a slightly greater differentiation of the earlier stages.

We assessed the impact of the CS pathogenesis on in‐hospital death. According to previous reports, AMI‐CS had a relatively higher in‐hospital mortality rate than non–AMI‐CS, 25 , 26 , 27 unlike this study. In this study, only stage D showed a significant difference in the in‐hospital mortality rate (AMI‐CS, 44.4%; non–AMI‐CS, 38.4%; P=0.01). In an observed stage D cohort in detail, patients with AMI‐CS were significantly older (median age, 73 years versus 66 years) and had a higher proportion of IHCA (31.2% versus 23.0%) than patients with non–AMI‐CS (not shown), probably leading to a significant difference in in‐hospital death. Furthermore, with respect to complications after Impella insertion, hemolysis was significantly different, with a tendency to occur more frequently during the milder shock stage. The reason for this is probably that Impella 2.5/CP was more frequently used in milder shock stages, which might cause a higher rate of hemolysis. Although it is a prerequisite to consider the patient's background, including age, activities of daily living, and frailty, and to discuss the indications for Impella use in multidisciplinary teams, our findings on adverse events after Impella insertion support the idea that physicians need not hesitate to place Impella, any more than necessary, in CS management within any SCAI stage, regardless of CS pathogenesis.

The treatment intensity analysis, including the number of drugs and MCS devices, revealed that the patients with ≥1 MCS had a considerably higher rate of in‐hospital death, which is consistent with a previous report. 4 , 9 The use of vasopressors or inotropes is a key component of the pharmacological treatment strategy for CS status, improving perfusion by increasing cardiac output and altering systemic vascular resistance and providing time to treat the underlying disease implicated in CS and to stabilize hemodynamics. 28 However, these agents increase myocardial oxygen demand and might further aggravate the clinical situation. 6 , 29 Therefore, MCS devices have been suggested to increase cardiac output without negative consequences from them. 6 , 29 Conversely, the situation with the need for MCS devices is more serious; thus, our findings can be easily understood.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report to provide information on the usefulness of the SCAI staging system in the Asian population using a nationwide Impella database. This system can provide helpful decision‐making information to physicians facing CS management; however, further studies are warranted to refine the system.

Limitations

This study has some limitations. First, all patients included in this study were limited to those receiving Impella; thus, at baseline, they appeared to be in a more severe stage, leading to fewer patients in the earlier CS phase. Moreover, the time for the stage assessment was not the initial recognition of the CS. Second, data on serial parameters that defined shock severity were available only at the insertion of the Impella. According to a previous report, serial assessment of the shock stage improves risk stratification. 21 However, this could not be conducted in this study. Third, in the original CSWG‐refined SCAI staging system, pH ≤7.2 was defined as 1 of the stage‐defining parameters as stage E. However, we had no data on pH in the database; thus, it might be relatively possible to over‐ or underestimate the stage determination.

CONCLUSIONS

In the Asian CS population, the CSWG‐refined SCAI staging system has an adequate association with in‐hospital death in more severe stages; however, the system seems to have lower power in the earlier phase of CS. Therefore, further investigations are warranted to establish a more accurate shock staging system.
