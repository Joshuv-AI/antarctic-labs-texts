A totally laparoscopic strategy combining microwave ablation and autologous blood transfusion for patients with initial WSES grade IV traumatic splenic rupture in selected transient responders: a proof-of-concept study
Authors: Zhiqi Feng, Dejin Xie, Weiqin Hong, Chufa Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeSplenic artery embolization (SAE) is the standard intervention for transient responders with high-grade splenic trauma. When SAE is unavailable, contraindicated, or declined, alternative minimally invasive approaches may be warranted. This study evaluates a totally laparoscopic strategy combining microwave ablation (MWA) for hemostasis with intraoperative autologous blood transfusion (ABT) in selected transient responders with initial WSES Grade IV splenic rupture.MethodsThis retrospective study included 10 consecutive transient responders (October 2021–June 2024) with initial WSES Grade IV splenic rupture who underwent totally laparoscopic MWA combined with intraoperative ABT. All patients exhibited transient hemodynamic response to limited resuscitation (≤ 1500 mL fluids) and preoperative shock index > 0.9. Intraoperative injury was graded by the AAST system. Outcomes included technical success, transfusion requirements, complications, and follow-up.ResultsAll 10 patients underwent successful spleen-preserving laparoscopy without conversion. AAST grades: II (n = 2), III (n = 4), IV (n = 4). Mean operative time was 191.3 ± 101.7 min. Mean blood salvage was 1587.5 ± 663.9 mL, yielding 490.8 ± 80.2 mL autologous red cells. No patient required postoperative allogeneic transfusion. Hemoglobin, platelets, and lactate improved significantly by postoperative day 3 (P < 0.05). No splenic abscess, overwhelming infection, or mortality occurred. At 12–18 months follow-up, no splenic atrophy or immune dysfunction was observed.ConclusionIn selected transient responders with initial WSES Grade IV splenic rupture, a totally laparoscopic approach integrating MWA and ABT is technically feasible. The discrepancy between WSES and AAST grades suggests some WSES IV patients have less severe injuries, supporting laparoscopic exploration in selected cases.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00068-026-03225-1.

Introduction

Traumatic splenic rupture remains a life-threatening condition requiring prompt intervention. Over the past decades, management has shifted from routine splenectomy to spleen-preserving strategies, driven by the recognition of the spleen’s critical immunological role [1, 2]. Non-operative management (NOM), including splenic artery embolization (SAE), is now the standard of care for hemodynamically stable patients [3–5]. For unstable patients, however, immediate surgery is often mandated [6].

SAE is recommended as the first-line treatment for splenic trauma due to its high success rates [7–10]. Traditionally, laparoscopic surgery in trauma has been reserved for stable patients or for diagnostic purposes [11]. However, SAE is not without limitations, including a finite failure rate and potential complications [12]. Moreover, in certain clinical scenarios—such as patient refusal, unavailability of interventional radiology, or specific contraindications—SAE may not be feasible. For such patients, a minimally invasive surgical alternative that achieves hemostasis while preserving the spleen could offer a valuable therapeutic option.

Microwave ablation (MWA) has been used effectively for hemostasis in solid organ injuries, including the spleen, by inducing coagulative necrosis of bleeding parenchyma. Intraoperative autologous blood transfusion (ABT) using cell salvage can reduce or eliminate the need for allogeneic blood, avoiding its associated risks. Combining these techniques within a laparoscopic approach might offer a unique solution for selected unstable patients.

This study presents a proof-of-concept case series evaluating the feasibility and clinical outcomes of a totally laparoscopic strategy integrating MWA and ABT in patients with World Society of Emergency Surgery (WSES) Grade IV splenic rupture [13] who were transient responders but in whom SAE was not pursued because of patient refusal after multidisciplinary consultation. We also examine the relationship between preoperative WSES grade and intraoperative American Association for the Surgery of Trauma (AAST) anatomic grade [4], to better understand the selection of candidates for this approach.

Materials and methods

This study was reported in line with the STROCSS 2024 criteria [14]. This retrospective case-series study was conducted after obtaining approval from the Hospital’s Institutional Review Board.

Preoperative workup and fast-track protocol

Upon arrival, a multidisciplinary fast-track protocol led by the general surgery trauma team was activated, involving coordinated collaboration among the Emergency Department, Imaging Department, Laboratory Department, Anesthesiology, Operating Room, Nursing Team, Intensive Care Unit (ICU), and the Blood Bank (Establishment of priority intravenous access and fluid resuscitation, blood transfusion, laboratory tests, imaging examinations, anesthesia and surgical preparation, early ICU bed reservation). The goal was to achieve surgical intervention within 3–6 h of admission. All patients underwent contrast-enhanced CT; active bleeding was defined by contrast extravasation (“blush”), progressive abdominal distension, or recurrent instability with dropping hemoglobin [15].

Surgical technique

All procedures were performed under general anesthesia with invasive monitoring. Patients were placed in a split-leg, head-elevated, right-tilted position. Pneumoperitoneum was established at 12 mmHg. A 10-mm supraumbilical port was used for the camera; a 10-mm port at the left midclavicular line (umbilical level) and a 5-mm epigastric port served as working ports.

Intraoperative decision-making

Based on the rate of bleeding, priority was given either to immediate blood salvage (if bleeding was controllable) or to rapid hemostasis (if bleeding was profuse). Blood salvage was performed using a continuous autotransfusion system (Cell Saver Elite®, Haemonetics, USA) with heparinized saline anticoagulation; collected blood was washed and concentrated according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The recovery rate (volume of concentrated red cells reinfused) was recorded.

Microwave ablation

A microwave ablation system (ECO-200E, Nanjing ECO Microwave System, China) with a 14-gauge cooled-tip antenna was used (videos). For parenchymal lacerations, the antenna was inserted along the edges and into the depth of the injury. Ablation was performed at 65–70 W for 5–10 min per application, until tissue firmness and a coagulative eschar were achieved. Representative intraoperative laparoscopic views of active hemorrhage and post-ablation hemostasis are shown in Fig. 1. For injuries involving segmental hilar vessels, these were first clipped with absorbable polymer clips before parenchymal coagulation.

Irrigation and drainage

The peritoneal cavity was thoroughly irrigated with warm saline, and a closed suction drain was placed near the splenic bed.

Outcome measures

Primary outcomes: technical success (completion of laparoscopy without conversion to open surgery), avoidance of allogeneic transfusion, and complication rates (including rebleeding, splenic abscess, pleural effusion, and mortality). Secondary outcomes: operative time, volume of blood salvaged and reinfused, changes in laboratory parameters (hemoglobin, platelets, lactate) from admission to postoperative day 3, time to first flatus, postoperative hospital stay, and follow-up findings at 12–18 months (clinical assessment, abdominal CT, and immune function tests).

Statistical analysis

Continuous data are presented as mean± standard deviation or median (range). Preoperative and postoperative laboratory values were compared using paired t-tests or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests as appropriate. A P-value < 0.05 was considered significant. Analyses were performed with SPSS version 27 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY).

Results

Patient characteristics

10 patients (4 males, 6 females; mean age 47.6 ± 21.5 years) met the inclusion criteria. All were classified as WSES Grade IV based on admission CT and hemodynamic instability with transient response. Preoperative shock index (SI) was moderate (SI 0.9–1.2) in 6, and severe (SI > 1.2) in 4 cases. No patient had a history of hypertension; two patients had diabetes.

Preoperative management

All patients received ≤ 1500 mL of crystalloid/colloid resuscitation in the emergency department. Seven patients also received vasopressors, 1.5–2 units of packed red blood cells, and 200–400 ml of fresh frozen plasma before transfer to the operating room. All patients were evaluated by the interventional radiology team; all explicitly refused SAE after detailed counseling, and signed refusal forms were obtained.

Intraoperative findings and surgical outcomes

Laparoscopic spleen-preserving surgery was successfully completed in all 10 patients. Intraoperative AAST injury grades were distributed as follows: grade II (n = 2), grade III (n = 4), and grade IV (n = 4). This discrepancy between preoperative WSES IV and lower anatomic grades in some patients is discussed below.

The mean operative time was 191.3 ± 101.7 min—encompassing cell salvage (washing, concentration, and reinfusion) and postoperative stabilization—whereas the actual dissection and ablation were substantially shorter. No conversion to laparotomy was required.

A mean of 1587.5 ± 663.9 ml of shed blood was salvaged, yielding 490.8 ± 80.2 ml of concentrated autologous red cells for reinfusion. No patient required postoperative allogeneic blood. Six patients required ICU admission (mean ICU stay 3.2 ± 1.8 days). Individual demographic and clinical data for the 10 patients are detailed in Table 1.

Complications and follow-up

No patient required reoperation for bleeding. One patient developed a left pleural effusion requiring ultrasound-guided drainage. There were no cases of splenic abscess, overwhelming post-splenectomy infection (OPSI), or mortality. At a median follow-up of 15 months (range 12–18 months), no patient developed splenic atrophy, pseudocyst, or immune dysfunction on laboratory testing. Discussion

This case series demonstrates that a meticulously planned totally laparoscopic (TL) strategy, incorporating microwave ablation (MWA) and autologous blood transfusion (ABT) within a multidisciplinary fast-track framework, represents a viable therapeutic option for managing traumatic splenic rupture in selected transient responders. Crucially, this approach relies on the precise identification of the “unstable but laparoscopic-tolerable” patient—a concept that challenges the traditional dichotomy between minimally invasive surgery and the imperative for rapid hemorrhage control in acute trauma settings.

An important observation from our study is the discrepancy between preoperative WSES Grade IV classification (based on clinical instability) and intraoperative AAST anatomic grades (ranging from II to IV). This finding is not unexpected: the WSES classification integrates both clinical and anatomic factors, whereas the AAST system grades anatomic injury alone. High-resolution CT may overestimate parenchymal injury depth in the presence of active bleeding, or the time interval from injury to imaging may affect the appearance. Importantly, the observation that some patients classified as WSES IV had AAST II–III injuries suggests that hemodynamic instability in these patients may have been driven more by active bleeding from a limited injury rather than by massive parenchymal destruction. This provides a rationale for laparoscopic exploration and spleen preservation in carefully selected cases, as the anatomic damage is often less severe than clinically suspected.

Our selection criteria focused on transient responders to initial resuscitation (≤ 1500 ml crystalloid/colloid, shock index > 0.9), indicating preserved physiological reserve that allows time for pneumoperitoneum establishment and precise dissection. This nuanced patient selection likely explains our 100% laparoscopic success rate and distinguishes our cohort from patients with profound, refractory shock. The decision to proceed laparoscopically was continuously re-evaluated intraoperatively, with a pre-defined low threshold for conversion to laparotomy—strictly adhering to the “life over spleen” principle.

The successful application of this strategy hinges on a synergistic triad of factors that collectively overcome the traditional barriers to laparoscopy in unstable trauma. First, process assurance is ensured by the multidisciplinary fast-track protocol, which minimizes system-wide delays by streamlining triage, preoperative resuscitation, and operating room activation. This orchestrated effort effectively compresses the time from the decision to operate to definitive surgical control, making a time-sensitive laparoscopic approach for high-grade splenic rupture logistically feasible. Furthermore, the proactive reservation of ICU beds provides a critical safety net for patients in the postoperative period.

Second, the synergistic combination of ABT and MWA formed the technical backbone of the procedure. The significant volume of autologous blood salvaged and re-transfused (1587.5 ± 663.9 ml) served as a deliberate resuscitative maneuver within a damage control philosophy [16, 17]. It directly mitigated the risks associated with massive allogeneic transfusion—including coagulopathy, immunomodulation, and resource strain [18]—while providing the physiological stability and clear visual field necessary for meticulous laparoscopic hemostasis. This aligns with the principles of early goal-directed therapy and damage control resuscitation [19, 20].

Third, microwave coagulation offered a rapid and reliable method for definitive parenchymal sealing. The technical strategy was adapted according to the injury grade: for deep parenchymal cracks, interstitial microwave application created a robust eschar; for injuries involving segmental vessels, we found the sequence of clip-and-coagulate to be most effective, consistent with techniques described in other laparoscopic splenic procedures [21]. For grade IV injuries involving partial splenic tissue devitalization, performing a partial splenectomy after establishing hemostasis via microwave ablation is technically feasible, thereby maximizing the chances of spleen preservation—an approach consistent with the functional application of microwave ablation in partial splenectomy [22, 23]. This adaptability is key for managing higher-grade injuries.

Our findings warrant comparison with splenic artery embolization (SAE), which remains the cornerstone of treatment for transient responders according to current WSES guidelines. In our cohort, SAE was offered to all patients but was declined after extensive counseling—a scenario that, although uncommon, does occur in clinical practice due to patient preference, logistical constraints, or contraindications. While SAE offers high success rates (85–95%) in appropriately selected patients, our TL approach enables simultaneous exploration, resuscitation via autologous blood transfusion, and definitive hemostasis in a single procedure. The mean operative time of 191.3 ± 101.7 min, while longer than typical SAE, includes non-surgical components. The actual surgical hemostasis time was substantially shorter.

The complete avoidance of postoperatively allogeneic transfusion and the absence of splenic abscess or OPSI underscore the potential immunologic and infectious benefits of this strategy. The rapid recovery of bowel function and the absence of adhesive complications highlight the advantages of the minimally invasive approach and thorough peritoneal lavage. Follow-up CT demonstrated preserved splenic morphology without atrophy, pseudocyst, or abscess formation, confirming the durability of spleen preservation. No patient developed immune dysfunction on laboratory testing during follow-up.

Safety disclaimers and clinical prerequisites

Given the application of this technique in a cohort of hemodynamically compromised patients, it is essential to explicitly delineate the safety boundaries and prerequisites for its implementation. The safe translation of this approach beyond the index institution is predicated upon adherence to the following core principles. First, patient selection is paramount: this technique is strictly limited to Transient Responders, as defined in our inclusion criteria; non-responders or patients with refractory shock are not candidates for this laparoscopic approach and should proceed directly to damage-control laparotomy. Second, team expertise is non-negotiable: the surgical team should possess experience with at least 10 cases of laparoscopic microwave ablation for hepatic or splenic lesions and demonstrate full proficiency in laparoscopic splenectomy. Concurrently, the anesthesia team must be well-versed in restrictive resuscitation strategies and the intraoperative management of autologous blood transfusion for trauma patients. Third, dynamic intraoperative reassessment is mandatory: continuous evaluation of hemodynamic stability, quality of visualization, and adequacy of hemostasis must guide all intraoperative decisions. Any element of uncertainty at any stage—whether due to deteriorating physiology, inadequate exposure, or unsatisfactory hemostasis—should prompt immediate conversion to laparotomy. Specific criteria for conversion include: (1) severe abdominal distension with inadequate visualization despite evacuation of hemoperitoneum; (2) a shattered spleen with incomplete hemostasis following microwave ablation, accompanied by anesthesiologist-reported hemodynamic instability; and (3) iatrogenic exacerbation of hemorrhage that proves difficult to control laparoscopically. Finally, the “life over spleen” principle remains inviolable: under all circumstances, preservation of life takes precedence over splenic salvage, and splenic salvage takes precedence over the pursuit of minimally invasive access.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. The retrospective design, small sample size, and single-center setting inherently limit the strength of conclusions and generalizability. The stringent selection criteria and requirement for a highly experienced surgical team introduce significant selection bias and may affect generalizability. The follow-up period of 12–18 months, while adequate for assessing early complications and splenic preservation, is insufficient to fully evaluate very late sequelae. Furthermore, the requirement for patient refusal of SAE introduces potential confounding, although this reflects real-world heterogeneity. The lack of a control group precludes direct comparison with SAE or open surgery. However, these criteria precisely define the ‘boundary’ patient cohort for which this approach might be feasible, serving as a proof-of-concept rather than a definitive therapeutic strategy.

Conclusion

This study provides the first systematic report of an integrated totally laparoscopic strategy for managing high-grade splenic rupture in select unstable trauma patients, expanding the minimally invasive arsenal within damage control surgery. By uniquely combining MWA hemostasis with intraoperative ABT within a coordinated fast-track protocol, this approach proves technically feasible and safe in carefully selected transient responders. It effectively achieves rapid hemorrhage control while preserving splenic function and entirely avoiding allogeneic transfusion. The observed discrepancy between WSES and AAST grades highlights the importance of considering laparoscopic exploration in selected unstable patients, as the actual anatomic injury may be less severe than anticipated. Future research should focus on prospective multicenter registries to validate this approach, standardize the technique, refine patient selection criteria, and conduct comparative effectiveness analyses against SAE.
