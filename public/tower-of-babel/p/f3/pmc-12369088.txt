Obesity indicators and female asthma risk: the mediating role of white blood cell count based on NHANES data analysis
Authors: Xu Qianqian, Zhang Qingmei, Jia Jin, Chen Yalin, He Chenyu, Zhang Huaiwen, Chen Jie
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study used data from the 2009–2018 National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) to examine the associations between obesity indicators (body mass index [BMI] and body roundness index [BRI]), the inflammatory marker white blood cell count (WBC), and asthma in women. Furthermore, it explored the potential mediating role of WBC in the relationships between BMI, BRI, and asthma.MethodsThis cross-sectional study included 3,792 eligible female participants. Logistic regression, restricted cubic spline (RCS), nonlinear curve fitting, threshold effect analysis, and mediation analysis were applied to evaluate the associations of BMI, BRI, and WBC with asthma.ResultsWeighted analyses showed that women with asthma had significantly higher BMI, BRI, and WBC levels compared to those without asthma (all P < 0.001). After adjusting for all potential confounders, these indicators remained significantly associated with asthma risk. Quartile-based analysis indicated that women in the highest quartile (Q4) of BMI, BRI, and WBC had a significantly higher risk of asthma. RCS and nonlinear curve fitting analyses supported nonlinear associations between these indicators and asthma, suggesting potential threshold effects. Mediation analysis demonstrated that WBC partially mediated the associations between BMI, BRI, and asthma risk.ConclusionThis study identified significant and nonlinear associations between BMI, BRI, WBC, and asthma risk in women. WBC was shown to play a partial mediating role in these relationships, providing new evidence for understanding the inflammatory mechanisms underlying obesity-related asthma.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12890-025-03885-6.

Introduction

Asthma is a common chronic inflammatory airway disease characterized by persistent airway inflammation, airway hyperresponsiveness, and reversible airflow limitation, which seriously affects patients’ health and quality of life. Globally, asthma affects approximately 334 million people, and its prevalence continues to rise, making it a major public health concern [1, 2]. The pathogenesis of asthma is complex and involves genetic susceptibility, environmental exposure, immune dysregulation, and airway remodeling. In recent years, epidemiological and experimental studies have suggested that metabolic disorders and chronic low-grade systemic inflammation may play important roles in obesity-related asthma, particularly in non-eosinophilic and steroid-resistant phenotypes [3]. Obesity, characterized by metabolic dysregulation and chronic inflammation, has been recognized as an independent risk factor for the development and progression of asthma, attracting widespread attention [4].

BMI is a traditional indicator for assessing overall obesity, and numerous epidemiological studies have confirmed that elevated BMI is significantly associated with increased asthma risk [5]. However, BMI cannot fully reflect the impact of fat distribution on respiratory function. In recent years, the BRI, as a representative indicator of abdominal fat distribution, has received increasing attention. Studies have shown that higher BRI is closely associated with asthma risk, particularly in individuals with significant abdominal fat accumulation, who often present with more severe respiratory symptoms [6].

Obesity influences asthma risk through multiple mechanisms, including mechanical and inflammatory pathways. On the one hand, abdominal fat accumulation may reduce lung volume and ventilatory function, directly contributing to asthma-like symptoms [7]. On the other hand, obesity can induce chronic low-grade systemic inflammation, contributing to airway inflammation and remodeling [8, 9]. WBC, a common marker of systemic inflammation, is prominently elevated in obesity-related low-grade inflammation. In obese individuals, adipose tissue secretes pro-inflammatory factors (e.g., leptin, interleukin-6[IL-6]), while adiponectin levels decrease, further exacerbating inflammation. These changes promote the aggregation of neutrophils and monocytes and stimulate the release of interleukin-8(IL-8) and tumor necrosis factor-α(TNF-α), which aggravate airway inflammation, hyperresponsiveness, and remodeling [3, 10]. Studies have shown that elevated WBC levels are closely associated with non-eosinophilic asthma phenotypes, airway injury, and disease progression, particularly in obese populations, and can predict the risk of asthma exacerbations [3, 11–14].

In addition, asthma exhibits notable differences in epidemiological characteristics and pathogenesis between sexes. Globally, asthma prevalence is consistently higher in women than in men, especially after puberty and in adulthood [15]. This difference may be closely related to sex-specific hormonal fluctuations (e.g., estrogen, progesterone), fat distribution patterns (e.g., greater abdominal and subcutaneous fat accumulation), and stronger immune responses [16, 17]. During special physiological stages, such as the menstrual cycle, pregnancy, and menopause, significant hormonal changes may affect airway inflammation, immune regulation, and asthma phenotypes. Furthermore, pregnancy and postpartum conditions, such as diastasis recti, may lead to abnormal fat distribution, altered metabolic status, and immune dysregulation, thereby potentially increasing asthma risk or affecting disease progression.

The NHANES dataset includes variables specific to women, such as menopausal status and reproductive history, which facilitate systematic evaluation of their potential impact on asthma risk. These factors may indirectly contribute to asthma development through modulation of hormone levels, fat distribution, and immune function. Studies have shown that menopause and related hormonal changes are associated with asthma risk, potentially mediated by changes in immune function and fat redistribution [18–20]. In addition, estrogen fluctuations have been implicated in the regulation of airway inflammation and asthma phenotypes [21].

In summary, this study utilized data from NHANES 2009–2018 to systematically evaluate the associations of BMI, BRI, and WBC with asthma risk in women, aiming to elucidate the links between obesity, inflammation, and asthma in women, and to provide epidemiological evidence for exploring potential mechanisms.

Methods

Study population

The study initially included 49,693 participants from the NHANES database covering the period from 2009 to 2018. NHANES conducted comprehensive physical measurements and complete blood count (CBC) tests for individuals aged 20 and above, and participants self-reported a physician diagnosis of asthma by a doctor or other health professional. The primary aim of the study was to assess the nutrition and health status of women in the U.S. population and to further explore the epidemiological characteristics of asthma and its related factors. Inclusion criteria included: ① complete baseline data on waist circumference (WC), height, BMI, smoking and drinking habits, and history of hypertension and diabetes; ② complete five-part blood count data; ③ U.S. women aged ≥ 20 years; ④ complete data on age at menarche, age at first and last live birth, and hormone use history; ⑤ complete self-reported asthma diagnosis data. A total of 3,729 women who met these criteria were included in the analysis (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Diagram illustrating the study population´s inclusion and exclusion. BMI, BRI, and inflammatory index

BMI (kg/m²), WC (cm), and standing height (cm) were extracted from the NHANES physical examination dataset (file name: “BMX.Doc”). The BRI [22] was calculated using WC (in meters) and height (in meters) as follows:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{BRI}=364.2-365.5\times\sqrt{1-\left(\frac{\mathrm{WC}}{2\mathrm\pi\times{\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{Height}}2}}\right)^2}$$\end{document}

Using the NHANES laboratory dataset (file name: “CBC.Doc”), the NLR [23] and platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR) [24] were calculated. The relevant indicators included WBC, lymphocyte count, neutrophil count, monocyte count, and platelet count, with units expressed as thousands of cells per microliter (×10³/µL) [25–27]. The formulas were:NLR = Neutrophil count/Lymphocyte count.PLR = Platelet count/Lymphocyte count.

These indicators were selected because they are standardized parameters consistently collected during the NHANES study cycles and have been commonly used in epidemiological studies to explore associations related to obesity and systemic inflammation.

Asthma

Asthma information was obtained from the medical condition questionnaire module (file name: “MCQ.Doc”) using question MCQ010: “Have you ever been told by a doctor or other health professional that you have asthma?” Women who answered “yes” were classified as having a history of asthma, while non-respondents were excluded. Previous studies have shown that this self-reported method has high accuracy [28–31].

Data covariates

To control for potential confounding factors, several covariates were included in the study. Demographic variables included age, race, education level, marital status, and poverty-income ratio (PIR). Lifestyle variables included smoking status (defined as having smoked ≥ 100 cigarettes in a lifetime) and drinking status (defined as having consumed any form of alcohol or ≥ 12 alcoholic beverages in a lifetime). Health status variables, including hypertension and diabetes, were self-reported through the questionnaire, with the question “Have you ever been diagnosed with hypertension or diabetes by a doctor or other health professional (yes/no)?”. Women’s reproductive variables included age at menarche, use of exogenous sex hormones, and age at first and last childbirth, with data from the reproductive health module, specifically variables RHQ010, RHQ540 (yes/no), RHD180, and RHD190.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using the DecisionLinnc version 1.0 software platform [32], which integrates multiple programming language environments and supports data processing, statistical modeling, and visual analysis. Given NHANES’s complex sampling design, sample weighting was applied throughout all analyses. The weighted variables used the two-year cycle examination sample weight (WTMEC2YR) for each period, multiplied by 1/5 to obtain the weight for the 10-year combined sample [33, 34]. The analysis was limited to women participants with complete exposure and outcome variable data.

The study first described the characteristics of the asthma and non-asthma groups: categorical variables were presented as percentages; continuous variables were tested for normality, and if normally distributed, presented as mean ± standard deviation (Mean ± SD). For non-normally distributed variables, medians (IQR) were reported.

Three multivariate logistic regression models were constructed to assess the independent associations between BMI, BRI, and WBC with asthma risk. Model 1 was the unadjusted model; Model 2 adjusted for age, race, education level, marital status, and PIR based on Model 1; Model 3 further included age at menarche, age at first and last childbirth, hormone use, smoking and drinking behaviors, hypertension, and diabetes status based on Model 2. The general form of the logistic regression models was as follows:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \log\left(\mathrm P/\left(1-\mathrm P\right)\right)=&\mathrm\beta0+\mathrm\beta1\times\left(\mathrm{BMI}/\mathrm{BRI}/\mathrm{WBC}\right)\\&+\Sigma\beta k\times\mathrm C\mathrm o\mathrm v\mathrm a\mathrm r\mathrm i\mathrm a\mathrm t\mathrm e{\mathrm s}_{\mathrm k} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}

where P represents the probability of asthma occurrence, β0 is the intercept, β1 is the coefficient for the primary exposure (BMI, BRI, or WBC), and βk are the coefficients for covariates included in each model.

Simultaneously, BMI, BRI, and WBC were grouped by quartiles, and trend tests (P for trend) were calculated for sensitivity analysis to verify the stability of the analysis results.

To improve the robustness of the model, multicollinearity tests were performed on all covariates before modeling, using the variance inflation factor (VIF) to assess correlations between variables. If a variable’s VIF > 10, it was considered to have severe multicollinearity and was excluded from the final model [35].

To explore potential nonlinear relationships between BMI, BRI, WBC and asthma risk, restricted cubic spline (RCS) models were constructed with weighting applied, and generalized additive models (GAMs) were used for smoothing curve fitting, both based on Model 3. Threshold effect analysis was conducted to identify inflection points, with model differences before and after the inflection point assessed using likelihood ratio tests. Based on this, subgroup analyses and interaction tests were conducted to explore heterogeneous effects in different populations.

Mediation analysis was performed to assess the indirect effects of BMI and BRI on asthma through WBC. To validate the assumptions of the mediation model, we additionally conducted weighted linear regression analyses to examine the associations of BMI and BRI (continuous values and quartiles) with WBC and log10-transformed WBC. These analyses applied the same covariate adjustment strategies as in the logistic regression models. Trend tests were also conducted to assess dose-response relationships.

Finally, to assess the discriminatory ability of each indicator for predicting asthma risk, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were plotted, and the area under the curve (AUC) was calculated to compare the predictive performance of BMI, BRI, and WBC.

Additionally, to validate the robustness of the results, sensitivity analyses were performed by excluding current smokers. Descriptive analyses and multivariate logistic regression were repeated under weighted conditions, using model settings consistent with the main analysis. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

This study, based on data from the 2009–2018 NHANES database, systematically evaluated the associations of BMI, BRI, and WBC with asthma risk among adult women in the United States. The results showed that higher levels of BMI, BRI, and WBC were significantly associated with an increased asthma risk, with consistent findings across various models and analytical methods. RCS analysis revealed nonlinear dose-response relationships between BMI, BRI, and asthma risk, with clear threshold effects. Subgroup and sensitivity analyses further confirmed the robustness of these associations. Mediation analysis indicated that WBC may partially mediate the association between obesity and asthma risk, providing clues to the underlying inflammatory mechanisms. However, its biological significance requires validation in prospective studies. In addition, ROC analysis showed that BMI, BRI, and WBC had limited discriminatory power as individual indicators (AUCs of 0.593, 0.590, and 0.564, respectively), and their predictive value warrants further evaluation.

Obesity, particularly abdominal obesity, is a well-established risk factor for asthma. Women generally have lower lung capacity and poorer airway ventilation, with a more active and responsive immune system [36, 37]. With age, and through physiological processes such as pregnancy and childbirth—especially in multiparous women—visceral fat accumulation increases, further elevating asthma risk [38]. Previous studies have shown that weight gain and increased waist circumference are associated with a higher asthma risk in women, consistent with our findings [39–41]. Visceral fat secretes pro-inflammatory factors (e.g., TNF-α, IL-6, leptin) that activate immune cells, promoting low-grade systemic inflammation, airway hyperresponsiveness, and remodeling [42–48]. Obesity can also impair T and B cell (T lymphocyte and B lymphocytes) function, induce Th2(T helper type 2 cells) dysfunction, and, together with abnormal fatty acid metabolism and oxidative stress, contribute to airway smooth muscle proliferation, basement membrane thickening, and collagen deposition, ultimately leading to airflow obstruction [5, 49–52].

WBC, as a marker of systemic inflammation, not only reflects airway inflammatory activity but may also contribute directly to asthma pathogenesis. In individuals with obesity, elevated levels of pro-inflammatory adipose-derived factors (such as leptin and IL-6) can trigger a state of chronic low-grade systemic inflammation, while reduced levels of the anti-inflammatory factor adiponectin may further exacerbate this process [4, 44, 53, 54]. Evidence suggests that decreased adiponectin may accelerate immune imbalance and airway inflammation through multiple signaling pathways, including the modulation of adipose-derived cytokines, miRNAs, lipid mediators, and extracellular vesicles (EVs) [4, 54–56]. This inflammatory environment can promote WBC release and activate neutrophils, monocytes, and other immune cells, leading to the secretion of reactive oxygen species, proteases (e.g., neutrophil elastase), IL-8, and TNF-α, ultimately leading to epithelial damage, mucus hypersecretion, and airway hyperresponsiveness [57–60]. Within this complex inflammatory network, extracellular EVs act as important mediators of inter-tissue signaling and may play a critical role in the pathological link between obesity and asthma.

EVs are capable of transporting bioactive molecules—including miRNAs, lipids, and cytokines—and regulating airway inflammation, remodeling, and steroid resistance through immunometabolic pathways [55, 56]. Miethe et al. reported that adipose-derived EVs modulate macrophage and epithelial cell function, thereby linking obesity-associated systemic inflammation to airway pathology [55]. Alashkar Alhamwe et al. proposed that EVs are not merely biomarkers of disease but active participants in disease progression that may represent potential therapeutic targets [56]. These complex immunometabolic networks and signaling pathways not only reveal potential mechanisms by which obesity promotes asthma development but also provide a theoretical basis for understanding the different clinical phenotypes of obesity-related asthma.

Asthma is a heterogeneous disease comprising type 2 (Th2-driven) and non-type 2 inflammatory phenotypes [61]. Type 2 asthma is typically associated with eosinophilic airway inflammation and good responsiveness to inhaled corticosteroids, while non-type 2 asthma is often characterized by neutrophil-dominant inflammation, steroid resistance, and poor disease control. Obesity-related asthma in adult women is frequently reported as a non-type 2, neutrophil-dominant phenotype marked by low-grade systemic inflammation, poor corticosteroid responsiveness, and persistent airway inflammation [62]. Such immune features, including elevated neutrophils, increased NLR, and lymphocyte subset abnormalities, contribute to airway remodeling and steroid resistance [63], reflecting an immunometabolic phenotype of obesity-related asthma. A better understanding of this phenotype and its underlying mechanisms is essential for developing targeted interventions and personalized treatment strategies.

Despite the strengths of this study, including a large sample and comprehensive adjustment for confounders, the cross-sectional design limits causal inference. The use of self-reported data may have introduced recall bias, and unmeasured confounders (e.g., genetic background, environmental exposures) may have influenced the results. Single-point measurements and the lack of correction for multiple testing may also have affected the robustness of the findings. Future prospective cohort studies are needed to validate these associations and further elucidate the underlying mechanisms.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that BMI, BRI, and WBC were nonlinearly and positively associated with asthma risk among adult women in the United States. Additional statistical and sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of these findings. Future prospective studies with larger cohorts are needed to validate these results and to further elucidate the interactions between these biomarkers, immune responses, and inflammatory factors in the development and progression of asthma in women, thereby providing a stronger scientific basis for early screening and personalized prevention strategies.
