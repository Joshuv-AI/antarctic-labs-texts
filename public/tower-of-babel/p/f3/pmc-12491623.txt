Leveraging catechol chemistry to tackle toughness-softness-work capacity tradeoff in reprogrammable liquid crystal actuators
Authors: Enjian He, Yahe Wu, Huan Liang, Hongtu Xu, Jiujiang Ji, Zhijun Yang, Yixuan Wang, Yen Wei, Yan Ji
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Dynamic chemistry endows liquid crystal elastomers (LCEs) with reprogrammability, enabling the reversible modulation of actuation modes to adapt to diverse tasks and enhancing sustainability and lifecycle management. However, balancing toughness, softness, and work capacity remains challenging due to their inherent tradeoff, as these properties are essential for achieving high-performance and stable actuation. Here, inspired by mussel coordination chemistry, we design a macromolecular crosslinker that combines covalent crosslinking with coordination bonds to tackle this challenge. The optimized LCE achieves exceptional toughness of 28.5 MJ/m³ and low Young’s modulus of 3.1 MPa, with high-temperature toughness exceeding 9.3 MJ/m³ at 90 °C (25 °C above phase transition temperature, Ti) and reaching 5.5 MJ/m³ at 120 °C (55 °C above Ti), while maintaining work capacity of 416 kJ/m³. Increasing coordination bond content further improves toughness (up to 67.0 MJ/m³) without significantly altering modulus or work capacity. Additionally, incorporating different metal ions provides a strategy akin to stem cell differentiation, transforming a single base material into variants with distinct properties. This enables spatially heterogeneous materials, paving the way for highly integrated actuators with multifunctionality.

Introduction

Liquid crystal elastomers (LCEs) have garnered significant attention in recent years for their exceptional ability to convert external stimuli into mechanical responses, making them ideal candidates for a wide range of applications, particularly in soft robotics1–5. In particular, much effort has been focused on introducing dynamic bonds into LCEs to enhance their reprogrammability. Dynamic bonds refer to chemical bonds or physical interactions that can break, reform, and exchange under specific conditions6–9. Various types of dynamic bonds including dynamic covalent bonds (such as Diels-Alder10,11, transesterification12, and disulfide exchange13) and dynamic non-covalent bonds (such as metal coordination bonds14 and hydrogen bonds15) have been incorporated into LCEs. The resultant reprogrammability not only allows for repeated modulation of actuation modes to accommodate diverse tasks on demands, but also enables reprocessability, welding, and self-healing, which satisfies the needs of sustainability and life cycle management16–19.

However, the current reprogrammable LCE materials are far from meeting the mechanical and actuation requirements for widespread applications in soft robotics. The first vital hurdle is the conflicts between toughness and softness. On one hand, the material must exhibit high toughness to withstand deformation as required for navigating in complex, unstructured environment. Toughness is a comprehensive metric that reflects both strength (resistance to stress) and elongation at break (deformability), both of which are essential for soft robotic applications. So far, the toughness of conventional LCEs typically remains below 3 MJ/m3, which not only limits deformation capabilities but also risks catastrophic failure20. On the other hand, for safe human-machine interaction and fragile items manipulation, the actuator must remain soft, ideally, the Young’s modulus should at least be lower than 10 MPa21–23. In the field of LCE, although approaches such as interpenetrating networks24 or carbon nanotube25 incorporation can improve toughness, without exception, they often lead to significant increase in the modulus of materials. For instance, the reported toughest LCE formed by an interpenetrating network achieved toughness of 17.2 MJ/m3, but its modulus soared to 560 MPa24.

The second hurdle is poor work capacity. Work capacity is defined as the mechanical work output per unit volume generated during actuation, and serves as a critical metric for assessing the practical performance of soft actuators. To achieve high work capacity, high toughness is necessary at both room and high temperature, for the actuation is driven by liquid crystalline order–disorder phase transition triggered by heating and cooling. This makes it extremely difficult to apply the common strategies used to improve the toughness of ordinary LCEs. For example, while hydrogen bonds can improve toughness26,27, they tend to dissociate at high temperatures, leaving the material less tough when needed at temperatures above the nematic-isotropic temperature (Ti). Furthermore, achieving high work capacity should not compromise actuation stability. The soft actuators must endure repeated actuation cycles under the presence of heavy loads. For reprogrammable LCEs, both dynamic covalent bonds and non-covalent interactions contribute to reprogrammability but often result in insufficient actuation stability at high temperatures28. For reprogrammable LCEs, when the modulus is lower than 10 MPa, the highest work capacity reported so far is 140 kJ/m3 with toughness of 9.6 MJ/m3 at room temperature29.

To address the above hurdles, we designed and synthesized a class of reprogrammable LCEs that exhibit high toughness at both high and low temperatures, as well as high work capacity, while maintaining low Young’s modulus. Our approach is inspired by previous work, in which mussel-inspired iron-catechol complexes were incorporated into a covalently cross-linked epoxy network to mitigate the tradeoff between stiffness and extensibility30. Unlike most coordination interactions, the iron-catechol bonds possess exceptionally high dissociation energy and thermal stability, making them ideal for maintaining toughness at elevated temperatures31,32. However, as with other strategies aimed at toughening elastomers, the introduction of iron-catechol cross-linking points led to an exceptionally high Young’s modulus (184 MPa) due to the increased cross-linking density from iron-catechol interactions and the formation of ionomeric nanodomains, which restricted chain mobility. We hypothesize that, with proper molecular design, the integration of liquid crystal (LC) domains can significantly enhance toughness while maintaining low modulus. This is because the LC domains are on the micron scale, whereas the ion-catechol domains are on the nanoscale30,33. In LCEs with a relatively low cross-linking density, the modulus is primarily governed by the LC domains rather than the ion-catechol domains.

According to this hypothesis, we designed a kind of LCEs cross-linked by both covalent and coordination bonds. They all exhibit modulus below 5 MPa. With 4 mol% of coordination bonds, the low-degree covalently cross-linked LCE achieves toughness of 28.5 MJ/m3 (6-fold improvement compared to its non-coordinated counterpart). Increasing the ion-catechol content does not lead to drastic increase in modulus. Even at 10 mol%, the modulus remains below 5 MPa, while the toughness increases to 67.0 MJ/m³ (22-fold improvement compared to its non-coordinated counterpart). Due to the exceptional stability of the coordination bonds, which show minimal dissociation at high temperatures, the materials still have high toughness at temperatures above Ti. At 90 °C (above Ti 25 °C), the toughness of the material with 4 mol% coordination bonds exceeds 9.3 MJ/m³, and it remains at 5.5 MJ/m³ even at 120 °C (above Ti 55 °C). Furthermore, the presence of covalent bonds ensure the material’s actuation stability, enabling it to lift loads up to 10,000 times its own weight while maintaining stable work capacity of 416 kJ/m³.

This material introduces a novel strategy for deriving a range of materials with distinct properties from a single base material, analogous to the differentiation of stem cells34. Unlike the tempering process described in the previous work34, our approach allows for the customization and tuning of optical and mechanical properties by simply altering the coordinating metal ions, without modifying the fundamental network structure35,36. Integrating diverse metal ions within a single film enables the creation of materials with highly integrated, diverse performance characteristics, analogous to the synergistic interplay of tissues with varying mechanical properties in biological systems. Benefiting from in-situ coordination through brush-coating, local coordination can modulate the distribution of coordinating ions, achieving heterogeneity in network dynamic behavior and mechanical properties. Combined with intrinsic photo-thermal effect of the metal coordination bonds, this offers more control over the actuation, programming, and motion of LCE.

Results

Synthesis and characterization of the tough but soft LCE

With the aim to effectively maintain low Young’s modulus, modified linear polysiloxane was selected as the macromolecular cross-linker. This was achieved by grafting silyl-protected catechol (SPCAT) units onto the long chains of polysiloxane containing thiol-functionalized side groups ((mercaptopropyl)methylsiloxane homopolymer, PMMS) via thiol-ene click reaction (Figs. 1a and S1). The catechol unit was subsequently utilized for coordinating iron(III) ions following the formation of the LCE network. The SPCAT-functionalized PMMS with multi-thiol group was incorporated as macromolecular cross-linker into classic soft LCE formulation. This formulation consisted of di-acrylate liquid crystalline mesogen ((4-(3-acryloyloxypropyloxy) benzoic acid 2-methyl-1,4-phenylene ester, RM257) and di-thiol spacer (3,6-dioxa-1,8-octanedithiol (EDDET). Unlike traditional formulations, no trifunctional or tetrafunctional thiol-based small molecules were used as cross-linkers. Instead, the synthesized macromolecular cross-linker was employed, which contained both covalent cross-linking site for network formation and coordinating site for subsequent metal coordination (Fig. 1a). The covalently cross-linked LCE films were synthesized via classical thiol-acrylate Michael addition reaction of monomers under the catalysis of dipropylamine (DPA) (Fig. S2). The molar ratio of thiol to acrylate groups was maintained at 1:1. Specifically, thiol groups were contributed by the spacer and the cross-linker at a molar ratio of 7:1, ensuring nearly consistent covalent cross-linking density across all LCEs. By tuning the molar ratio of CAT to PMMS, a series of LCEs with different ligand contents (CATx-ori, x = 2, 4, 6, 8 mol%, relative to the mesogen content) were synthesized (Table S1). Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy revealed the disappearance of the typical IR signals assigned to thiols (S-H stretching at 2571 cm−1) and acrylates (C=C stretching at 1636 cm−1 and =C–H bending at 810 cm−1) after the reaction (Fig. S3), indicating a high conversion rate of monomers. To exclude the influence of iron oxide formation on the mechanical performance, a series of characterizations were conducted. Scanning electronic microscope (SEM, Figs. S16 and S17) images revealed no significant changes in the surface structure of the films before and after coordination, without iron oxide particles observed. Additionally, Energy dispersive spectrometer (EDS, Fig. S18) testing on CAT4-Fe showed a uniform distribution of elements, indicating homogeneous distribution of coordination bonds within the network. These results confirm that the enhancement in mechanical performance arises from iron–catechol coordination rather than the formation of inorganic fillers.

Reprogrammability and work capacity of CATx-Fe

The reversible exchange of coordination bonds necessary for the reprogrammability was confirmed by the stress relaxation test (Figs. 3a and S20–23). As shown in Fig. 3a, CAT4-Fe films exhibited excellent stress relaxation behavior, whereas CAT4-ori and CAT4-dep showed minimal stress relaxation (Fig. S20). At elevated temperatures, coordination bonds underwent rapidly reversible exchange, facilitating internal stress relaxation and dissipation. In addition, the stress relaxation behavior was further analyzed using the stretched exponential Kohlrausch–Williams–Watts (KWW) model41,42. The fitting results indicated that the stress decayed to near zero at infinite time, confirming that the networks were capable of full relaxation due to dynamic coordination bond exchange (Fig. S21). The apparent activation energy value was calculated as 101.2 kJ mol−1 based on the fitting of an Arrhenius plot for the stress-relaxation time at σ/σ0 = 1/e (Fig. S22). As the coordination bond content increased, the rate of stress relaxation accelerated (Fig. S23). By heating the CAT4-Fe film at 120 °C for 60 min, most of the stress could be relaxed (Fig. 3a). Leveraging this characteristic, we could change the orientation of mesogens within CATx-Fe to fabricate actuators (Fig. 3b)43,44. The detailed procedure of actuator fabrication can be found in Methods section of Supplementary information. As shown in Fig. 3c and Movie S1, CAT4-Fe film as example, the network rearrangement and mesogens orientation fixation led to the formation of monodomain LCE, achieving maximum actuation strain of approximately 41% under load-free condition. The orientation of the LCE after programming was confirmed using wide-angle X-ray scattering (WAXS, Fig. S24). To eliminate the influence of other factors, CAT4-ori film was subjected to the same programming process. It remained in polydomain state without effective actuation during the heating and cooling cycle (Fig. S25), confirming that mesogens orientation in CATx-Fe resulted from coordination bond exchange. The actuation strain varied with the coordination bond content. Specifically, CAT2-Fe achieved the maximum actuation strain of 47%, while the actuation strain values for CAT4-Fe, CAT6-Fe, and CAT8-Fe decreased progressively to 41%, 34%, and 21%, respectively (Fig. 3d). Benefiting from the exchangeability of coordination bonds, different actuators with diverse actuation modes could be created by reprogramming the orientation of mesogens. As shown in Fig. 3e, a small-diameter spiral actuator could be reprogrammed into a large-diameter spiral actuator through the exchange of coordination bonds via direct heating. Furthermore, by repeating the reprogramming process, the spiral actuator could be reprogrammed into a helical 3D actuator combining rotation and uniaxial length change. Reprogramming could also be achieved through light irradiation. The iron-catechol coordination bonds endowed the LCE with an exceptional photothermal effect. Unlike methods involving the incorporation of secondary photothermal agents, this in-situ coordination approach addressed concerns related to filler dispersion, compatibility within the matrix, and the detachment of coatings from the matrix45,46. The photothermal effect and the photo controlled actuation are detailed in Figs. S26–28 and Movie S2. Under laser irradiation at 0.33 W cm−1, the equilibrium temperature reached 120 °C (Fig. S27), which was used for reprogramming. In Fig. 3f and Movie S3, the LCE film was first stretched into a helical shape, followed by laser irradiation at two points at the center. This process fixed the mesogens orientation in these two regions, thereby enabling actuation only in the middle two regions of the film. We also designed a light-driven robot that can effectively manipulate its motion direction by simply controlling the direction of light exposure (Fig. S29 and Movie S4).

CATx-Fe exhibited superior and robust mechanical work capacity. Under load, the CATx-Fe film performed mechanical work during the heating and cooling cycles. Given the excellent photothermal effect of the films, 808 nm IR light was employed to generate indirect heat and assessed the mechanical work capacity (Fig. 3g). As shown in Fig. 3h, the measured work capacity exhibited a trend of first increasing and then decreasing with increasing iron content24. Specifically, the work capacities of CAT2-Fe, CAT4-Fe, CAT6-Fe, and CAT8-Fe were 380, 416, 357, and 310 MJ/m³, respectively, with CAT4-Fe exhibiting the highest value of 416 MJ/m³. This indicated that an optimal level of coordination bonds could effectively enhance the LCE network, thereby improving its energy output. However, excessive coordination bonds led to an overly dense cross-linked network, limiting the mobility of LC domains and consequently reducing work capacity. Given its superior work capacity, CAT4-Fe was selected for further investigation and demonstration in this study.

The actuators demonstrated excellent actuation stability, a critical parameter for monodomain LCEs in practical applications, where insufficient stability may result in operational failure47,48. Under load-free condition, the CAT4-Fe actuator maintained an actuation strain of ~41% over the first five cycles, with only a slight decrease to ~40% after 500 cycles (Fig. 3i). Under load, CAT4-Fe actuator was able to lift weight 10,000 times its own while maintaining stable work capacity of 416 kJ/m³ (Fig. 3g and Movie S5). During cyclic actuation under load, a slight increase in actuator length was observed over the initial cycles, attributable to creep effects under sustained stress and heating (Fig. S30). The length stabilized after approximately 20 cycles, indicating that the combination of robust catechol–iron coordination bonds and permanent covalent cross-links effectively suppressed further creep and ensured reliable working performance49.

Mechanical properties of the CATx-Fe

Upon coordination with iron, CATx-Fe networks exhibited remarkable toughness enhancement while maintaining material softness. Taking CAT4 films as example (Fig. 4a), uniaxial tensile tests revealed that after deprotection, CAT4-dep exhibited slight increase in toughness, rising from 4.5 to 5.3 MJ/m³. This improvement was attributed to weak hydrogen bond interactions among catechol units. After coordination with iron ions, the toughness markedly increased to 28.5 MJ/m3—a 6-fold enhancement compared to CAT4-ori. This trend became more pronounced at higher coordination contents, CAT8-Fe achieved toughness of 67.0 MJ/m3, representing a 22-fold increase over CAT8-ori (Fig. 4b, Figs. S31 and S32). This performance originates from the molecular design, in which both covalent cross-linking sites and dynamic coordinating sites were integrated into the same macromolecular cross-linker, promoting spatial separation between micron-scale LC domains and nanoscale iron–catechol coordination domains (Fig. 1a). During stretching, the LC domains first responded to deformation through reorientation and energy dissipation (Fig. S33)50,51. As strain increased, stress was transferred to the coordination-rich domains, where dynamic coordination bonds acted as sacrificial bonds that reversibly dissociate and reform, further dissipating energy30. Moreover, the dynamic coordination bonds contribute to improvements in both tensile strength and elongation at break simultaneously. In contrast to conventional toughening strategies, such as filler addition or increasing cross-linking density, which often sacrifice extensibility and increase modulus substantially, thus restricting their applicability for safe human-machine interaction and fragile object handling. The CATx-Fe series overcome this tradeoff, achieving enhanced toughness significantly while maintaining Young’s modulus below 5 MPa (Fig. 4c and Table S2).

Although the toughness of monodomain CAT4-Fe was comparable to that of its polydomain one (28.7 MJ/m³, Fig. S32). The monodomain LCE, where the mesogens were already aligned along the stretching direction, exhibited a stiffer mechanical response during stretching (Figs. S33 and S34). Notably, in polydomain LCE films under uniaxial tension, a stress plateau is observed after the linear elastic region. This plateau corresponds to the alignment of mesogens along the tensile direction, a phenomenon commonly referred to as the soft elasticity of LCEs21,51. With increasing coordination bond content, the soft elasticity plateau relating to mesogen domains reorientation in CATx-Fe networks became narrower, indicating that the higher cross-linking density restricted the reorientation of mesogens (Fig. S35)51,52.

Besides enhancing toughness at ambient temperature, the iron-coordinated films significantly improved toughness at elevated temperatures (above Ti). One of the major challenges for the practical application of LCEs is the significant decline in mechanical properties at temperatures above Ti, where they transition into isotropic phase and actuate. Therefore, enhancing the toughness of the network at high temperatures is crucial to ensure that actuators can effectively perform actuation functions and mechanical work. At 90 °C (25 °C above Ti), CATx-Fe exhibited remarkable toughness (Fig. 4d). Specifically, CAT2-Fe achieved toughness of 2.6 MJ/m³, while CAT4-Fe exceeded 9.3 MJ/m³, surpassing the instrument’s measurement limit due to extensive elongation. Materials with higher coordination bond content showed even greater high-temperature toughness. For instance, CAT8-Fe exceeded 15.1 MJ/m³. For CAT4-Fe, the material with the optimal work capacity, toughness at higher temperature was further investigated. At 120 °C (55 °C above Ti), the toughness remained substantial, reaching 5.5 MJ/m³, representing 6-fold improvement compared to CAT4-ori (0.98 MJ/m³) (Fig. 4e). Notably, the toughness of the CAT4-Fe film at both room temperature and elevated temperatures (>Ti), stands out among previously reported LCEs with Young’s modulus below 10 MPa (Fig. 4f and Table S3).

Modulation and integration of properties through metal ion tuning

The concept of performance differentiation has long been a key area of research, enabling the generation of materials with diverse properties from a single precursor via simple stimuli. This approach allows for the customization of material properties without the need for complex design or synthesis. Recent studies have demonstrated this via processes like tempering, which produce materials with varying mechanical properties34. Here, we present a simple yet effective method to achieve performance differentiation and integration for actuators. By coordinating CATx-dep with different metal ions, we create films with tunable properties, offering a unique opportunity to derive multiple materials from a single base, akin to stem cell differentiation. This enables personalized customization of material properties without altering the original chemical composition. All LCEs coordinated with different ions here showed rapid stress relaxation behavior and could be prepared into monodomain state through thermal programming (Figs. 5a and S36). Specifically, CAT4-Fe exhibited low actuation strain of only 41%, LCEs coordinated with Zn2+ and Bi3+ achieved actuation strain of around 58%, LCEs coordinated with Co2+, Ni2+, and Cu2+ showed larger actuation strain, reaching approximately 70%. This variation is primarily attributed to differences in coordination strength and bond exchange dynamics between metal ions and catechol ligands, which influence the network mobility and mesogen reorientation53,54. In terms of mechanical properties, coordination with metal ions resulted in notable improvements in both toughness and stiffness (Fig. 5b, c), which showed similar trend as previously reported36,55. These enhancements varied depending on the strength of the interactions between the metal ions and catechol ligands (including charge density of metal ion, coordination geometry and kinetics)55, leading to material-specific differences in mechanical properties. Specifically, Co2+ ated with Co2+, Ni2+, provided moderate enhancement, increasing the toughness to 1.5 times that of the original film. In contrast, Cu2+, Zn2+, Bi3+, and Ni2+ resulted in 2.4–3.5-fold improvements, while Fe3+ achieved the most significant enhancement, achieving 6.4-fold increase in toughness (Fig. 5c). Integrating diverse metal ions within a single film enables the creation of materials with highly integrated, diverse performance characteristics, analogous to the synergistic interplay of tissues with varying mechanical properties in biological systems56,57. First, mechanical heterogeneity can be achieved within a single sample. For example, the left part of a CAT4-dep film was coordinated with Ni2+ while its right part was coordinated with Fe3+, the film exhibited asymmetric deformation under uniaxial tension (Fig. 5d and Movie S6). The Fe3+-coordinated region, which formed stronger cross-links, displayed a smaller strain (~25%), whereas the Ni2+-coordinated region stretched to a much greater extent (~135%). Secondly, different ions in different areas generate more flexible control on the actuation mode (Figs. 5e, S37 and Movie S7). A LCE film with one side coordinated by Cu2+ and the other by Fe3+ was subjected to two different programming processes. In the “uniform” stretching process, the film was stretched to 150% of its original length and fixed at both ends. Due to differences in stiffness, the Fe3+ side exhibited smaller pre-strain, this strain difference persisted under heating (T > Ti). After programming, Fe3+ side ultimately showed lower actuation strain (18%) than the Cu2+ side (65%), highlighting the influence of mechanical asymmetry on actuation behavior. In contrast, when the film was “proportionally” stretched (Figs. S38, S39 and Movie S7), both regions were independently stretched to 150% of their original lengths and fixed at the midpoint by tape before thermal programming. This method ensured equal pre-strain in both regions, resulting in actuation strains of 36% and 64% for the Fe and Cu parts, respectively.

Local in-situ coordination within a single LCE film through brush-coating method offers an even more flexible way to tailoring material properties on demand (Methods section of Supplementary information for detailed procedures). In one example, partial coordination by coating following stretching can create different shapes. As shown in Fig. 5f and Movie S8, local coordination process was applied to specific regions of a LCE cross, while the other areas remained unprocessed. The coated surface became stiffer than the uncoated the surface, inducing mechanical heterogeneity, which led to bending upon stretching. As another example, employing local coordination method to pattern LCEs films also introduces heterogeneous dynamic behaviors. As shown in Fig. 5g and Movie S9, in a flower-shaped LCE film, partial brush-coating followed by programming endows the reversible actuation during heating and cooling cycles specifically to the coating regions as only the regions containing iron-catechol coordination bonds, capable of dynamic exchange. Additionally, the coordinated regions exhibited strong photothermal effects, enabling for reversible actuation upon light exposure (Fig. S40 and Movie S10). Utilizing advanced techniques like precise masking techniques combined with spray coating58,59, we believe that greater intricacy and precision in patterned coordination can be achieved, promising the development of soft actuators with biomimetic properties and more complex structural configurations. The details of those demonstration as well as more patterns can be found in Figs. S41–43.

Discussion

By designing macromolecular cross-linkers, we integrate mussel-inspired metal coordination chemistry with covalent cross-linking into LCEs, significantly enhancing the toughness and work capacity of actuator materials while maintaining low modulus. This approach addresses inherent tradeoff between toughness, softness, and work capacity faced by reprogrammable LCEs. By tuning the coordination bond content, the mechanical properties and work capacity of the network are readily tailored. The optimal network, CAT4-Fe, demonstrates exceptional performance, capable of lifting weights 10,000 times its own while maintaining consistent work capacity of 416 kJ/m³. It exhibits room-temperature toughness of 28.5 MJ/m³ and high-temperature toughness exceeding 9.3 MJ/m³ at 90 °C (25 °C above Ti), with toughness of 5.5 MJ/m³ retained even at 120 °C (55 °C above Ti). For networks with higher coordination bond content, the toughness reaches 67 MJ/m³, while the work capacity remains above 310 kJ/m³. All these reprogrammable LCEs exhibit modulus below 5 MPa. A variety of actuators with on-demand motions can be prepared, and the actuation modes can be reprogrammed via coordination bond exchange. The coordination with different ions, patterned coordination, and local ‘in situ’ coordination not only provide a new method to differentiate the same base material into materials with different properties but also offer a new way to seamlessly integrate varied mechanical and actuation properties into one customized soft actuator. The macromolecular cross-linker design in this work is only a demonstration to solve the inherent tradeoff of LCE, more ligand and metal ions remain to be explored, other strong non-covalent interactions, and more LCE systems are expected to further improve the performance of LCE. We hope that the above strategy promises the development of next-generation adaptive soft actuators and robots with flexible performance adjustments and multifunctional integration capabilities.

Methods

Materials

Eugenol (Bide Pharmatech, 98%), triethylsilane (Bide Pharmatech, 98%), tris(pentafluorophenyl)borane (Energy, 98%), (mercaptopropyl)methylsiloxane homopolymer (PMMS, 75–150 cSt, molecular weight range of 4000–7000 g mol−1, Gelest), 2,2-dimethoxy-2-phenylacetophenone (DMPA, Adamas, 99%), 3,6-dioxa-1,8-octanedithiol (EDDET, Energy, 95%), 1,4-bis-[4-(3-acryloyloxypropyloxy) benzoyloxy]-2-methylbenzene (RM257, Hunan Binghang New Materials, 98%), dipropylamine (DPA, Meryer, 98%), ferric nitrate nonahydrate (Macklin, 99.9%), nickel chloride hexahydrate (Macklin, 99.9%), cobalt acetate tetrahydrate (Macklin, 99.9%), copper chloride (Macklin, 99.9%), zinc chloride (Macklin, 99.9%) and bismuth nitrate pentahydrate (Macklin, 99.9%) were used as purchased. Tetrahydrofuran (THF, Bei Jing Tong Guang Fien Chemicals Company, AR), trichloromethane (CHCl3, Sinopharm Chemical Reagent, AR), hydrochloric acid (HCl, Bei Jing Tong Guang Fien Chemicals Company, AR) and deionized water (Macklin) were also used as received.

Synthesis of silyl-protected catechol (SPCAT)

SPCAT was synthesized following earlier reported procedure. Eugenol (4.98 g, 30.0 mmol, 1.00 equiv.), Triethylsilane (10.68 g, 90 mmol, 3.00 equiv.) were added into a round-bottom flask in reflux with a condenser, stirred until homogeneous, and then a catalyst, Tris(pentafluorophenyl)borane (0.03 g, 0.06 mol, 0.002 equiv.) was added. The mixture was stirred at room temperature for 6 h, and the reaction process was monitored by thin-layer chromatography. The catalyst was filter out by a neutral alumina column chromatography using dichloromethane as eluent solvent. Unreacted triethylsilane and solvent were then removed by rotary evaporation, resulting a colorless liquid SPCAT (Figs. S5 and S6).

Synthesis of SPCAT-functionalized PMMS (PMMS-CATx)

PMMS, CAT with required mole ratio (Table S1) and the photoinitiator DMPA (1 wt%) were added into a vial, dissolved with about 1 mL CHCl3, mixed until a homogeneous solution was achieved. The vial was exposed to UV light (0.2 W cm−1) about 5 min under stirring to promote the reaction between sulfhydryl group and double bond, resulting in PMMS-CAT2, PMMS-CAT4, PMMS-CAT6, and PMMS-CAT8, respectively (Fig. S1).

Synthesis of SPCAT-functionalized LCE (CATx-ori)

PMMS-CATx (0.3 mmol of thiol group), RM257 (0.6062 g, 1.2 mmol), and EDDET (0.1504 g, 1.05 mmol) were added into a vial, dissolved with about 3 mL THF, mixed until a homogeneous solution was achieved. Then, diluted DPA (1 wt% to total mass) as a catalyst for thiol–acrylate Michael addition reaction was added into the vial, vibrated again to get a homogeneous mixture. The mixed solution was cast in a Teflon mold and left to cure overnight. After the reaction, the obtained CATx-ori films were dried for 48 h at 35 °C under vacuum to completely remove the remaining solvent.

Deprotection of SPCAT

SPCAT (0.116 g, 1 mmol) was added into HCl and THF mixed solution under rapid stirring. Deprotection reaction proceeded quickly, which was monitored by TLC. Then, the organic layer was extracted into DCM and washed with H2O three times, then DCM was dried over Na2SO4. After filtration, rotary distillation and drying in a vacuum oven, a pure product was obtained as a white solid (Figs. S5 and S6).

Deprotection of CATx (CATx-dep)

The CATx films were deprotected by submerging in HCl and THF mixed solution under mildly stirring for 12 h. After deprotection treatment, the samples were washed with immersed in H2O and THF mixed solution 3 times for 15 min each time. Then, the films were dried for 48 h at 35 °C under vacuum to completely remove the remaining solvent, and CATx-dep were obtained. CATx-dep for subsequent iron treatment do not need to be dried, iron treatment can be carried out immediately.

Process of iron-treated CATx (CATx-Fe)

The as-prepared CATx-dep films were immersed in 0.01 M Fe(NO3)3 9H2O/THF solution for 24 h. The films were washed with THF solution 3 times to completely remove the unreacted ferric ion. Then, the obtained samples were dried for 48 h at 35 °C under vacuum to completely remove the remaining solvent. LCEs with different metal coordination were processed like the protocol of CATx-Fe above.

General characterization

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) data were collected on an ESCALAB 250 Xi electron spectrometer from ThermoFisher Scientific using 1487 eV Al Kα radiation, and the carbon (C), oxygen (O), sulfur (S), silicon (Si), Ferrum (Fe) elemental contents were tested. Raman spectroscopy tests were conducted on Horiba LabRAM HR Evolution with a 785-nm laser excitation source, raman shift ranging from 300 to 1800 cm−1. Water contact angle of LCEs were produced on DataPhysics OCA-15. Static water contact angle measurements were performed on flat sample surfaces using a goniometer equipped with automated droplet control. A micro-syringe coupled with a motorized actuator was employed to dispense deionized water at a volume of 3 μL per droplet. To minimize dynamic effects, the needle was positioned just above the sample surface during dispensing, and subsequently retracted gently after droplet deposition. The contact angle was determined using the instrument’s proprietary image analysis software, with image resolution of 768 × 576 pixels. For each sample, measurements were conducted at three different locations, and the average value was reported as the final result. Wide-angle X-ray scattering (WAXS) and small angle X-ray scattering (SAXS) tests were performed using a Ganesha 300 XL SAXS system (SAXSLAB) equipped with a multilayer focused Cu Kα radiation source (Genix 3D Cu ULD, 50 kV, 0.6 mA) and a 2D semiconductor detector (Pilatus 300 K, DECTRIS, Switzerland). Differential Scanning Calorimetry (DSC) analyses were carried out on a TA Instruments Q2000 calorimeter. Samples were subjected to a heating–cooling–reheating cycle from −40 °C to 120 °C at a scanning rate of 10 °C/min under a nitrogen atmosphere. The first heating cycle was used to erase the thermal history of the samples, and the second heating curve was used for data analysis. Uniaxial tensile tests were performed on a universal testing machine (MTS SYSTEMS CMT6503) at a constant speed of 5 mm min⁻¹, at room temperature (25 ± 1 °C) and ~30% relative humidity. For each sample, three unnotched rectangular specimens (n = 3) were tested to ensure reproducibility. The values reported in both the main text and Supplementary Figs. represent the average results, and the error bars correspond to the standard deviation across the three independent measurements. Dynamic mechanical analysis (DMA) was conducted on a TA Instruments Q800 using tensile film geometry. For temperature-dependent dynamic mechanical analysis, samples were tested over a temperature range from –60 °C to 150 °C at a heating rate of 3 °C min−1. The oscillation frequency was set to 1 Hz, and the strain amplitude was fixed at 0.05%. For stress relaxation experiments, samples were first equilibrated at the target temperature for 10 min before applying a 10% constant uniaxial strain. Stress relaxation was then recorded over time. To simulate the programming and actuation process, samples were tested in controlled force mode. The protocol included ramping the temperature to 120 °C at 30 °C min−1, equilibrating for 10 min, and applying stress at a rate of 0.25 MPa min−1 up to 0.25 MPa for 60 min. The temperature was then decreased to 90 °C at 15 °C min−1 and held for 10 min under constant force. A final thermal cycle from 25 °C to 90 °C at 15 °C min−1 followed by 15 min of thermal equilibrium was applied to simulate actuation behavior. For high temperature uniaxial tensile test, samples were first equilibrated at the target temperature for 10 min before applying a uniaxial strain at a constant speed of 5 mm min−1. 1H NMR and 13C NMR spectra were performed in dry NMR tubes on a JEOL JNM-ECA400 (400 MHz) spectrometer for all samples. All Fourier Transform Infrared Spectrometer (FTIR) data were collected on PerkinElmer Spectrum 100 FT-IR Spectrometer with wavenumber ranging from 550 to 4000 cm−1 with scan number of 4. Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was performed on Bei Jing Bei Guang Hong Yuan Yi Qi WRT-121 under air atmosphere with a heating rate of 10 °C min−1. Scanning electron microscope (SEM) cross sectional morphologies (5 kV) and elements imaging (15 kV) of LCEs were obtained on HITACHI SU-8010. The 1D X-Ray Diffractometer (XRD) data were obtained on a Bruker D8 Advance diffractometer with Cu-Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å) at 40 kV and 40 mA. Polarizing optical microscope (POM) measurement was conducted using a Nikon ECLIPSE LV100POL polarizing optical microscope equipped with crossed polarizers. Photothermal effect was tested by NIR laser source (Hi-Tech Optoelectronics Co. Ltd., China) with a wavelength of 808 nm was used to photothermal experiments. The temperature was measured by a thermal imaging camera (FLIR E40).
