Cannabidiol as an adjunct in refractory paroxysmal sympathetic hyperactivity following severe traumatic brain injury: a case report
Authors: Sai Sriram Swamiyappan, Jeevan Kumar Reddy, Rithvik Ramesh, Harini Sivaji, Vivek Visvesvaran
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundParoxysmal Sympathetic Hyperactivity (PSH) is a well-recognized complication following severe traumatic brain injury (TBI), with an incidence of 5–33% in the acute phase, characterized by episodic autonomic and motor hyperactivity. Management is often challenging, and a subset of patients develop refractory PSH despite optimized first- and second-line therapies. Cannabidiol (CBD) possesses neuroregulatory and autonomic-modulating properties demonstrated in preclinical TBI studies and epilepsy trials including Epidiolex studies, but its role in PSH has not been previously described.Case presentationWe report the case of a 44-year-old South Indian gentleman with severe TBI following a road traffic accident (GCS 5: E1V1M3) with CT brain showing bilateral frontotemporo-parietal acute subdural hematoma with mass effect. He underwent emergency bilateral decompressive craniectomy and required mechanical ventilation with tracheostomy. Three weeks post-injury, he developed recurrent PSH episodes (4–6 episodes per day) characterized by severe tachycardia (heart rate 140–180 bpm), hypertension (systolic blood pressure > 180 mmHg), hyperthermia (up to 40 °C), diaphoresis, and dystonic posturing. The diagnosis of PSH was established using the Paroxysmal Sympathetic Hyperactivity Assessment Measure (PSH-AM), with a total score of 28 (Clinical Feature Scale: 18, Diagnosis Likelihood Tool: 10), indicating probable PSH. Infective, metabolic, epileptic, and structural causes were excluded. Despite treatment with multiple conventional agents at maximum tolerated doses—including bromocriptine (titrated from 1.25 mg twice daily to 40 mg/day), baclofen (10 mg/day), gabapentin (titrated from 150 mg/day to 300 mg/day), propranolol (15 mg three times daily), clonidine (0.2 mg/day), dexmedetomidine infusion (72-h infusion), and fentanyl (infusion followed by patches)—the autonomic storms persisted, fulfilling criteria for refractory PSH. Cannabidiol oil (100 mg/mL) was therefore initiated as adjunctive therapy at 100 mg twice daily (approximately 3 mg/kg/day) and titrated to a 100–150–100 mg/day regimen over one week via nasogastric tube. Within the first week, there was a marked reduction in episode frequency (from 4 to 6 per day to less than 1 per 48 h) and severity, with PSH-AM scores decreasing from 28 (CFS: 18, DLT: 10) to 16 (CFS: 6, DLT: 10), and opioid and sedative infusions were successfully withdrawn. By the second week, complete resolution of PSH episodes was achieved with a PSH-AM score of 4. No adverse effects were observed, including no hepatic dysfunction, excessive sedation, or hemodynamic instability.ConclusionsThis case highlights a potential adjunctive role for cannabidiol in refractory PSH following severe TBI. Introduction

Paroxysmal Sympathetic Hyperactivity (PSH) syndrome is characterized by episodic hypertension, tachycardia, hyperthermia, diaphoresis, and dystonia in patients following severe TBI. Variously labelled as diencephalic seizures, paroxysmal sympathetic storms and midbrain dysregulatory syndrome, Blackman et al. in 2004 proposed the name Paroxysmal Sympathetic Hyperactivity [5]. The condition occurs in 5–33% of patients with severe TBI during the acute phase, significantly impacting ICU length of stay and neurological recovery [5]. Refractory PSH syndrome is defined as persistent paroxysmal sympathetic and dystonic episodes that do not adequately respond to optimized first line and second line drugs (including beta-blockers, alpha-2 agonists, dopaminergic agents, gabapentinoids, and GABAergic drugs), often requiring continuous opioid or sedative support to control autonomic storms, after exclusion of alternative causes of instability [5, 9]. Cannabidiol has neuroregulatory, anti-inflammatory and autonomic-stabilizing properties [1], but its role in PSH syndrome has not been described. We present a case where CBD provided significant improvement in refractory PSH symptoms which to the best of our knowledge is the first instance of a potential therapeutic role.

Background

Strich, in his report on patient with severe traumatic brain injury in 1956 described the occurrence of episodic agitation, tachycardia, diaphoresis, tachypnea and hyperthermia and claimed they were ‘brainstem attacks’ [12]. The constellation of symptoms can be explained by dysfunction of diencephalic autonomic centers and or their connections to the cortical and subcortical loci such as the paraventricular hypothalamic nucleus, lateral periaqueductal gray substance, lateral parabrachial nucleus, or rostral ventricular medulla [6, 7].

Baguley et al. in 2014 [3] proposed a diagnostic criteria- the paroxysmal sympathetic hyperactivity assessment measure (PSH AM)-which included the Clinical Feature Scale (CFS) which included scores from 0 to 3 for heart rate, respiratory rate, systolic pressure, temperature, sweating and posturing during the episodes-ranging from 0 to 18 and a Diagnosis likelihood Tool (DLT) which included 11 parameters each scored either 0 or 1 depending on the presence or absence. The PSH-AM score was the sum of CFS score and DLT score. A score of more than 17 was considered probable for diagnosis of PSH.

Case report

A 44-year-old South Indian gentleman presented with alleged history of a road-traffic accident (fall from motorcycle) with GCS 5 (E1V1M3). CT brain showed bilateral fronto-temporo-parietal (FTP) acute subdural hematoma with mass effect, midline shift, and diffuse axonal injury. The patient underwent emergency bilateral decompressive craniectomy with evacuation of SDH. Postoperatively, he required aggressive ICP-directed therapy and mechanical ventilation. A tracheostomy was performed on postoperative day 7 for ventilatory support. Gradual weaning from the ventilator was achieved over the next 2 weeks.

Onset of PSH Syndrome: By postoperative week 3, the patient developed recurrent episodes (4–6 per day) of severe tachycardia (heart rate 140–180 bpm), with markedly elevated blood pressure (systolic blood pressure 180–200 mmHg), high-grade fever (up to 40 °C), profuse sweating, and dystonic posturing involving all 4 limbs. Each episode lasted 20–40 min. EEG showed no epileptic activity and repeat imaging showed no features of raised ICP. Infective, metabolic and drug induced causes were excluded through comprehensive workup including blood cultures, metabolic panels, and drug screens. The clinical pattern was consistent with PSH syndrome- with a PSH AM Clinical Feature Scale (CFS) of 18 and Diagnosis Likelihood Tool (DLT) of 10, bringing the total score to 28, indicating probable PSH. Conventional Management: The patient was started on bromocriptine (1.25 mg twice a day) initially for 5 days, sequentially titrated to maximum dose (40 mg/day) combined with baclofen (10 mg/day). However, PSH episodes continued to persist (CFS: 16, DLT: 10, total PSH-AM: 26). Subsequently the patient was started on gabapentin (150 mg/day escalated to 300 mg/day), propranolol (15 mg, initially twice and then thrice a day reaching 45 mg/day) and clonidine (0.2 mg/day), all titrated to maximum tolerated dosages. Despite this multi-agent approach, the episodes persisted with minimal improvement (CFS: 15, DLT: 10, total PSH-AM: 25). He was then started on dexmedetomidine infusion at 0.5–1.0 mcg/kg/h. Despite the 72-h infusion, there was only modest reduction in the frequency of the episodes which continued to occur at 2–3 episodes per day (CFS: 14, PSH-AM: 24). Addition of fentanyl infusion (25–50 mcg/h), followed by transdermal patches (25 mcg/h) reduced the episode frequency slightly, but the episodes persisted (CFS: 13, DLT: 8, total PSH-AM: 21).

Introduction of Cannabidiol: After more than 2 weeks since the onset of PSH and 7 days of fentanyl therapy, given the refractory nature of the autonomic storms, a trial of cannabidiol oil solution (CBD, 100 mg/mL concentration) was initiated at a dose of 100 mg twice daily (total 200 mg/day, approximately 3 mg/kg/day for this 70 kg patient), after discussion with the neurology team and obtaining informed consent from the patient’s family. The dose was then titrated to a 100–150–100 mg/day regimen (total 350 mg/day, 5 mg/kg/day) over the subsequent week. The oil was administered through Ryle's tube (nasogastric route). The initial dosage used was in accordance with the recommended weight-dependent dose for seizure management. It was increased to the maximum allowable dose for the patient’s weight based on established epilepsy protocols.

Clinical response: Within the first week of CBD therapy, there was a significant decrease in both frequency (from 4 to 6 episodes per day to less than 1 episode in 48 h) and intensity of PSH episodes. The PSH-AM score decreased from 28 to 16 (CFS: 6, DLT: 10). Vital sign stability improved markedly with heart rate normalizing to 80–100 bpm and blood pressure to 120–140/70–90 mmHg. Fentanyl was gradually tapered over 3 days and stopped. The patient no longer required fentanyl or mechanical ventilation thereafter. By the end of the second week on CBD, PSH episodes had completely resolved, with stable hemodynamic parameters and improved arousal. The final PSH-AM score was 4. No adverse drug effects such as hepatic dysfunction (monitored by liver function tests), excessive sedation or hemodynamic instability were noted. Over the next 11 days, other medications administered for PSH were gradually tapered and discontinued. The patient was discharged on cannabidiol (350 mg/day) and bromocriptine (10 mg/day). At last follow-up (1 month since discharge), he remained free of PSH symptoms but had severe neurological deficits (Glasgow Outcome Scale Extended score: 3). Discussion

In patients with severe TBI, PSH remains a well-recognized complication which can prolong ICU stay due to the need for increased sedatives and opioids. It has also been noted to delay neurological recovery. Despite the numerous names this condition has been reported by, the underlying mechanisms proposed have remained constant- disruption of cortical inhibitory control over subcortical and spinal sympathetic centers, resulting in excessive excitatory output [2, 8, 10]. However, the disconnection theory alone cannot account for the entire clinical spectrum—especially when there is cortical and subcortical inflammatory involvement in the absence of brainstem involvement. The excitatory/inhibitory ratio (EIR) model has gained wider acceptance [2], conceptualizing PSH as a two-stage process in which initial disconnection of descending inhibitory pathways leads to excessive excitation, followed by termination of paroxysms with partial recovery of inhibitory control. The EIR framework also suggests that sympathetic paroxysms may reflect structural or functional midbrain impairment.

Given the underlying mechanism, drugs used in the management of PSH include, bromocriptine, clonidine, lorazepam, morphine, propranolol, baclofen, pregabalin, gabapentin and dantrolene. Failure to control the symptoms with these medications warrant use of fentanyl or dexmedetomidine infusions. Such situations requiring continuous infusion where the routinely used medications of more than 2 differing groups in their maximum tolerable doses have failed to ameliorate the symptoms has been called refractory PSH. Several cases of refractory PSH have been described. But in these situations, use of fentanyl has abated the symptoms. Our patient had the characteristic phenotype of PSH, high PSH-AM scores and potential mimics such as sepsis, seizures, neuroleptic malignant syndrome were ruled out. Despite the addition of multiple first and second line medications at their maximum tolerated doses, symptoms couldn’t be ameliorated—fulfilling criteria for refractory PSH [8]. Unfortunately, for our patient, fentanyl produced only temporary marginal improvements.

Cannabidiol is a non-psychoactive phytocannabinoid with neuroregulatory properties [11]. Although its use in PSH has not been previously reported, several receptor-level mechanisms provide biological plausibility. The role of CBD in traumatic brain injury has been extensively reported in the literature with positive benefits in preclinical studies demonstrating neuroprotective effects, reduced inflammation, and modulation of excitotoxicity [4]. Furthermore, CBD has shown autonomic stabilizing properties in epilepsy trials, including the landmark Epidiolex studies in Dravet syndrome and Lennox-Gastaut syndrome, where seizure reduction was accompanied by improved autonomic stability.

Literature shows extensive distribution of the CB1 and CB2 receptors across the limbic system, hypothalamus and the brainstem autonomic regulatory areas like the peri-aqueductal grey and the spinal cord. But interestingly CBD does not exert its action via the CB1 and CB2 receptors which are more relevant for the endocannabinoid system. The sympatho-inhibitory or modulatory effects potentially stem from its modulation of other receptors. First, the serotonin 5-HT1A receptor, for which CBD acts as a direct agonist. The 5-HT1A receptors are distributed across the autonomic center neurons with significant presence in the hypothalamus, brainstem autonomic nuclei (nucleus tractus solitarius, dorsal vagal complex and periaqueductal gray) and the limbic and paralimbic regions. Its activation can reduce sympathetic output. Second is the Transient Receptor Potential Vanilloid 1 (TRPV1) ion channel, which CBD, after transient activation, desensitizes. This can potentially mute the trigger-induced autonomic episodes. Apart from this, CBD can allosterically modulate GABA A receptors and antagonize the GPR55 receptor which cumulatively reduce the brain's excitability.

In this patient, initiation of cannabidiol was temporally associated with a marked reduction in the frequency and severity of PSH episodes, accompanied by a significant decrease in PSH-AM scores (from 28 to 4 over two weeks) and successful withdrawal of opioid and sedative support. No adverse effects attributable to cannabidiol were observed. The sustained clinical response following failure of multiple conventional therapies suggests a potential adjunctive role for cannabidiol in refractory PSH. Further prospective studies are required to define its safety, efficacy, and optimal dosing in neurocritical care settings. Our study aligns with sustainability development goal 3—good health and well-being. The patient's wife expressed relief at the improvement of the stormy episodes of PSH that warranted ICU care even though the general neurological condition of the patient did not improve significantly.

Limitation of the study: As a single-patient observation, spontaneous resolution cannot be fully excluded, and causality cannot be inferred. The lack of a control group and the observational nature of this case report limit definitive conclusions about CBD’s efficacy.

Ethics and consent: Institutional Review Board approval was waived for this single case report as per institutional policy. Written informed consent was obtained from the patient's legally authorized representative (wife) for both the treatment and publication of this case report including any accompanying images and clinical data.

Conclusion

This case highlights the potential adjunctive use of cannabidiol in a patient with refractory paroxysmal sympathetic hyperactivity following severe traumatic brain injury, in whom conventional therapies had failed. Initiation of cannabidiol was temporally associated with sustained reduction in autonomic episodes and successful withdrawal of sedative and opioid support. While causality cannot be inferred from a single case, this observation suggests a potential role for cannabidiol in refractory PSH and warrants further prospective evaluation in larger controlled studies.
