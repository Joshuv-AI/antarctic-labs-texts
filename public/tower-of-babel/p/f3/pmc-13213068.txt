Retrospective cohort study of body fat percentage and incident impaired fasting glucose among Chinese adults
Authors: Duo Yang, Renzhe Lin, Sen Li, Shujun Ye, Zitian Luo, Huankai Zhang, Si Wu, Longsheng Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDiabetes poses a growing global public health burden, with impaired fasting glucose (IFG) representing a key prediabetic stage. Body fat percentage (BF%) is a commonly used indicator of adiposity and has been shown to be a better predictor of metabolic risk than BMI. However, evidence regarding the association between BF% and the risk of IFG in Chinese populations remains limited. This study aims to explore the relationship between BF% and the risk of incident IFG in Chinese adults. The study analyzed data from 184,308 Chinese adults with normal fasting glucose, derived from a large retrospective cohort database. The exposure was BF%, estimated using a previously validated prediction formula incorporating BMI, age, and gender. The primary outcome was new-onset IFG, defined as a fasting plasma glucose level between 5.6 and 6.9 mmol/L. The relationship between BF% and IFG risk was examined using multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression models. Restricted cubic splines (RCS) were used to explore nonlinearity, and subgroup analyses were conducted to assess consistency. During a median follow-up of 3.0 years, 11.28% of participants developed IFG. Higher BF% levels were independently associated with increased IFG risk: per 1-unit increase, the HR was 1.03 (95% CI: 1.02–1.03) in the fully adjusted model. A dose-response relationship was observed across BF% quartiles. RCS analysis revealed a nonlinear association with an inflection point at BF% = 30.205; below this point, the HR per 1-unit increase was 1.013 (95% CI: 1.009–1.017), and above it, the HR was 1.046 (95% CI: 1.038–1.054). Elevated BF% was consistently and positively associated with an increased risk of IFG across all predefined subgroups, with all corresponding HRs being greater than 1, indicating a robust and consistent association. Elevated BF% is an independent risk factor for IFG in Chinese adults, exhibiting a dose-response and nonlinear relationship. Introduction

Diabetes mellitus has emerged as one of the most pressing global public health challenges of the 21 st century. According to recent epidemiological estimates, over 500 million adults are currently affected worldwide, a staggering figure that is projected to approach 900 million by 20501,2. China, in particular, bears the highest absolute burden of diabetes globally, driven by rapid urbanization, shifting dietary patterns, and increasingly sedentary lifestyles. Within the continuum of diabetes pathogenesis, impaired fasting glucose (IFG)—clinically defined by the American Diabetes Association as a fasting plasma glucose (FPG) level between 5.6 and 6.9 mmol/L3—represents a crucial prediabetic state. The transition from normal glucose tolerance to IFG is primarily characterized by the onset of hepatic insulin resistance and early progressive β-cell dysfunction. Identifying individuals at this specific IFG stage is of paramount clinical importance. It not only serves as a strong predictor for future clinical diabetes and cardiovascular complications but also provides a highly actionable, critical window where progression to diabetes can be halted, and dysglycemia has the potential to revert to normoglycemia4–6.

Obesity is universally recognized as a primary, reversible driver of both IFG and type 2 diabetes7,8. However, the traditional anthropometric indices routinely utilized in primary care to assess obesity, such as body mass index (BMI) and waist circumference (WC), possess significant inherent limitations in accurately evaluating true cardiometabolic risk. BMI, calculated simply as weight divided by height squared, is fundamentally a measure of excess weight rather than excess adiposity. It cannot distinguish between fat mass and non-fat mass (e.g., skeletal muscle and bone mineral content)9,10. This limitation is particularly detrimental in clinical practice, as it frequently underestimates metabolic risk in individuals who have experienced a loss of lean muscle mass but have concomitantly accumulated ectopic or visceral fat. This specific phenotype, commonly recognized as “normal-weight obesity” (NWO), is highly prevalent yet easily overlooked11,12. The inadequacy of BMI is even more pronounced in Asian populations. Evidence suggests a distinct “Asian phenotype” characterized by a higher propensity for abdominal and visceral fat deposition at much lower BMI thresholds compared to Caucasians. Furthermore, while WC effectively captures central adiposity, it fails to fully quantify the systemic body fat burden and does not account for the profound, naturally occurring age- and sex-related variations in total body composition.

In contrast, body fat percentage (BF%) provides a much more direct, comprehensive reflection of an individual’s total body fat load13,14. From a pathophysiological perspective, excess adipose tissue is not merely an inert energy storage depot; it is a highly active endocrine organ. Excessive body fat accumulation triggers the hypersecretion of free fatty acids and pro-inflammatory cytokines (such as interleukin-6 and tumor necrosis factor-alpha), while downregulating protective adipokines like adiponectin. This lipotoxicity and chronic low-grade inflammatory state directly impair insulin signaling pathways in target tissues, propelling the development of fasting dysglycemia. Consequently, multiple studies have demonstrated that a higher BF% is significantly associated with an increased risk of metabolic disorders, providing vital risk stratification information far beyond that offered by BMI15–17. For instance, Gómez-Ambrosi et al. demonstrated that individuals categorized with a “normal” BMI but high BF% exhibited significantly higher rates of dysglycemia18. Similarly, Zhao et al. emphasized that maintaining a healthy BF% is a more critical determinant for preventing diabetes than merely maintaining a normal BMI16.

Despite this compelling evidence, integrating accurate BF% measurements into large-scale epidemiological studies and routine primary care remains challenging. Gold-standard measurement techniques, such as dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DEXA), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), or bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA), are hindered by high costs, the need for specialized equipment, potential radiation exposure, and time constraints. To bridge this critical translational gap, a formula-derived BF% offers a highly practical, cost-effective alternative. Crucially, rather than being a mere mathematical transformation of basic anthropometrics, this validated formula provides essential biological and clinical incremental value. Age and gender are the fundamental biological determinants that dictate the muscle-to-fat ratio. Biologically, older adults experience age-related sarcopenia (gradual muscle loss) and increased fat infiltration, while females naturally possess a significantly higher proportion of essential and storage adipose tissue for a given BMI compared to age-matched males due to hormonal differences. The predictive formula explicitly recalibrates the raw BMI to reflect these physiological realities. By mathematically integrating these biological variables, a formula-derived BF% actively flags high-risk patients (e.g., postmenopausal women or elderly individuals with low muscle mass) whose “normal” BMI masks a dangerously high metabolic fat load. This provides primary care physicians with a zero-cost, easily accessible, and biologically accurate metabolic screening tool that requires only routinely collected clinical data18–20.

Although recent evidence has demonstrated a significant longitudinal association between estimated BF% and the incidence of clinical diabetes19,20, research extending this association to the earlier, critical stage of IFG remains markedly limited. Previous large-scale cohorts have predominantly focused on the endpoint of diabetes, potentially missing the earliest stages of metabolic deterioration where interventions are most effective. Therefore, we conducted a secondary analysis utilizing a robust, large-scale retrospective cohort database comprising 184,308 Chinese adults with normal baseline fasting glucose. Our primary objective is to thoroughly explore the longitudinal relationship between formula-derived BF% and the risk of new-onset IFG. Furthermore, we aim to investigate potential non-linear dose-response relationships and identify specific clinical thresholds (inflection points). We hypothesize that elevated BF% is an independent risk factor for IFG, and validating this accessible biomarker could establish a superior, earlier screening strategy for prediabetes in routine clinical practice, ultimately facilitating more timely and targeted primary prevention efforts.

Method

Data source

The original dataset utilized in this study was sourced from the Dryad data repository platform, which operates under the principles of open science. This platform serves as an open-access digital repository that integrates a wide range of publicly available research data across multiple disciplines. The dataset referenced in this study is associated with a large cohort study focusing on Chinese adults, published in BMJ Open in 2018, titled “Association of body mass index and age with incident diabetes in Chinese adults: a population-based cohort study”21. Researchers can access the complete data resource via its Digital Object Identifier link (10.5061/dryad.ft8750v). This dataset contains longitudinal clinical follow-up records from individuals who participated in a health screening program between 2010 and 2016. In accordance with Dryad’s policies, data hosted on the platform may be used for secondary analyses to explore new scientific questions. Since the original study had already obtained ethical review and approval from the Ethics Committee of the Rich Healthcare Group in China, this secondary analysis of the same dataset did not require separate ethical approval or renewed participant informed consent. Furthermore, all procedures in this study strictly adhered to the ethical standards outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and complied fully with relevant national laws, regulations, and institutional guidelines.

Study population

This study conducted a secondary analysis based on a retrospective cohort previously established by Chen et al. In the original study, the baseline cohort comprised adult participants who underwent at least two health assessments between 2010 and 2016. From this initial pool of 211,833 individuals, we applied strict pre-defined criteria to select the analytical sample for our current study. The inclusion criteria for our secondary analysis were: (1) mainland Chinese adults aged > 20 years; and (2) individuals with a normal baseline FPG < 5.6 mmol/L. The exclusion criteria were strictly defined as follows: (1) individuals with a baseline FPG ≥ 5.6 mmol/L; (2) participants who were diagnosed with diabetes mellitus during the follow-up period, as our primary outcome was new-onset IFG; and (3) individuals with missing data for key variables essential for BF% calculation (i.e., age, gender, and BMI). After applying these sequential screening steps, a final total of 184,308 participants were included in the analysis (Fig. 1).

Flowchart of participant selection.

Definitions of the exposure variable

BF% was the primary exposure variable in this study, treated as a continuous variable. It was estimated according to a formula proposed in the literature22, as follows: BF% = (1.20 × BMI) + (0.23 × Age) − (10.8 × Gender) − 5.4, where gender is coded as 1 for males and 0 for females. The BF% estimation formula has also been validated in studies of Chinese populations19.

Definitions of the outcome variable

The primary outcome of this study was defined as new-onset IFG. According to relevant guidelines from the American Diabetes Association (ADA), IFG was defined as an FPG concentration between 5.6 mmol/L and 6.9 mmol/L in participants without a prior diagnosis of diabetes mellitus. The duration of follow-up was calculated from the date of the baseline health assessment to the date of the last available test.

Other covariates

The covariates considered in this study encompassed the following categories: (1) demographic characteristics, including age and gender; (2) anthropometric measures, namely height, body weight, and blood pressure (BP); (3) blood biochemical parameters, specifically FPG, total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), and serum creatinine (Scr); (4) behavioral factors, such as smoking and drinking status; and (5) family history of diabetes.

Information on demographic characteristics, lifestyle behaviors, and family disease history was collected via structured questionnaires. Anthropometric measurements, including height, body weight, and BP, were obtained by uniformly trained staff following standardized protocols. BMI was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by the square of height in meters. Based on the categorical definitions in the original database, smoking and drinking statuses were classified as “current,” “ever,” or “never.” Venous blood samples were collected by professional personnel from all participants after a fast of at least 10 h. The relevant biochemical parameters were measured using a Beckman Coulter AU5800 fully automated biochemical analysis system.

Missing data processing

The dataset used in this study contained observations with missing values, which were addressed using a standard handling strategy in observational epidemiological research. Among the variables included in the analysis, a total of 13 variables contained missing data, comprising 11 continuous and 2 categorical variables. The specific proportion of missing data for each variable is listed in Supplementary Table 1. To reduce potential bias introduced by missing data, imputation was performed for the following variables: height, systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), HDL-C, LDL-C, ALT, AST, BUN, Scr, TC, and TG. The imputation was conducted in the R statistical environment using the MICE package23. The multiple imputation by chained equations method was applied, generating 5 complete datasets after 5 iterations. This method is a commonly used and robust statistical approach for handling arbitrary missing patterns, as it can reasonably account for the potential randomness in the missing data mechanism.

For the missing records in the categorical variables of smoking and drinking status, this study adopted the strategy of uniformly classifying them as “not recorded.” To assess the reliability of the multiple imputation method and test the robustness of the analytical results, the primary regression analysis results reported in the main text are based on the pooled datasets after imputation. To comprehensively verify the stability of the conclusions, this study also provides, in the supplementary materials, the results from analyses using only the original complete-case data (pre-imputation) and the data obtained after excluding participants with incomplete covariate information (i.e., complete-case analysis). The direction and magnitude of the association between BF% and the risk of IFG incidence were generally consistent across the three analytical datasets (post-imputation, pre-imputation, and after excluding cases with missing covariates). This indicates that the main conclusions of this study are not sensitive to the choice of missing data handling method, demonstrating good robustness of the findings.

Statistical method

The BF% calculated in this study followed a normal distribution, as illustrated in Fig. 2, which shows the distribution of BF% across all participants. Based on the quartiles of BF%, all participants were divided into four groups: Q1 (≤ 21.408), Q2 (≤ 25.732), Q3 (≤ 30.018), and Q4 (≤ 58.460). The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Normally distributed variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation, while non-normally distributed variables are described as median (interquartile range) [M (IQR)]. Categorical variables are expressed as frequency (percentage) [n (%)]. For between-group comparisons, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for normally distributed continuous variables with homogeneity of variance; otherwise, the Kruskal-Wallis H test was applied. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test.

The distribution of the BF%.

To examine the association between different levels of BF% and the risk of IFG incidence, Kaplan-Meier (K-M) survival curves were plotted according to BF% quartiles, and the significance of between-group differences was assessed using the log-rank test. Subsequently, multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression models were established to quantify the association between BF% and the risk of developing IFG, estimating HRs and their 95% CIs. Prior to interpreting the models, the proportional hazards (PH) assumption was statistically verified using the Schoenfeld residuals test. To systematically evaluate the robustness of this association at different levels of adjustment for potential confounders, we constructed two hierarchically adjusted regression models: Model I adjusted for baseline FPG, SBP, DBP, smoking status, drinking status, and family history of diabetes; Model II (the fully adjusted model) further included ALT, AST, LDL-C, HDL-C, TC, TG, BUN, and Scr based on Model I, aiming to comprehensively control for potential confounding factors. Crucially, to prevent severe mathematical coupling and multicollinearity, the component variables used to calculate the estimated BF% (i.e., age, gender, and BMI) were intentionally excluded from all multivariable adjustment models. Furthermore, collinearity diagnostics were performed for all covariates included in the fully adjusted model (Model II) by calculating the variance inflation factor (VIF), with a VIF value > 5 considered indicative of significant multicollinearity. The diagnostics confirmed the statistical stability of our model, as the VIFs for all adjusted variables were strictly below 5.0 (ranging from 1.010 to 2.619; Supplementary Table 2).

Furthermore, to explore the potential nonlinear relationship between BF% and the risk of IFG, a smoothing curve was fitted using RCS. If the statistical test indicated a significant nonlinear trend (P for nonlinearity < 0.05), a piecewise Cox proportional hazards regression model was further applied to identify a potential inflection point (threshold). The strength and direction of the association between BF% and IFG risk were then estimated separately on both sides of the inflection point. Finally, this study also conducted subgroup analyses. Participants were stratified by gender, age, BMI, SBP, DBP, LDL-C, HDL-C, TC, TG, smoking status, drinking status, and family history of diabetes. Within each subgroup, Cox regression analyses were uniformly adjusted for all other covariates except the stratification variable itself. This was done to evaluate the stability and consistency of the primary association across populations with different characteristics.

Statistical software

All results are reported in accordance with the STROBE statement. Statistical analyses were performed in the R environment (version 4.2.2; https://www.R-project.org/) and the Free Statistics Analysis Platform (version 2.1.1; https://www.clinicalscientists.cn/freestatistics). A two-tailed P-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. As the present study constitutes a secondary analysis of pre-existing data, no formal a priori sample size calculation was performed.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the study population

A total of 184,308 Chinese adults with normal baseline FPG were included in this study. During a median follow-up period of 3.0 years, a total of 20,785 (11.28%) participants developed incident IFG. Participants were divided into four groups according to the quartiles of BF%: Q1 (n = 46012), Q2 (n = 46090), Q3 (n = 46077), and Q4 (n = 46129). The baseline characteristics of each group are presented in Table 1. The mean age of the overall population was 41.02 ± 12.10 years, and 53.07% were male. With increasing BF% quartiles, participants showed a trend of progressively older age (Q1: 33.98 ± 6.82 years; Q4: 50.08 ± 12.73 years), while the proportion of males decreased significantly (Q1: 98.27%; Q4: 12.67%). Significant differences (all P < 0.001) were observed across BF% quartile groups in height, weight, BMI, SBP, DBP, FPG, TC, TG, HDL-C, LDL-C, ALT, AST, BUN, and Scr, as well as smoking status, drinking status, and family history of diabetes. The incidence of new-onset IFG increased significantly with higher BF% quartiles (P < 0.001), rising progressively from 8.55% in the Q1 group to 15.29% in the Q4 group.

Association between BF% and IFG risk

We employed Cox proportional hazards regression models to assess the association between BF% and the risk of new-onset IFG. The Schoenfeld residuals test indicated no significant deviation from the proportional hazards assumption for BF% (P = 0.174), confirming that the assumption was well met (Supplementary Fig. 1). As shown in Table 3, in the crude model without adjusting for any covariates, each 1-unit increase in BF% was associated with a 4% higher risk of developing IFG (HR 1.04, 95% CI: 1.04–1.04, P < 0.001). Compared to the lowest BF% quartile group (Q1), the HRs for the Q2, Q3, and Q4 groups were 1.20 (95% CI: 1.15–1.25), 1.20 (95% CI: 1.15–1.25), and 1.78 (95% CI: 1.71–1.85), respectively, with a significant trend (P for trend < 0.001). After adjusting for baseline FPG, SBP, DBP, smoking status, drinking status, and family history of diabetes (Model I), the HR for each 1-unit increase in BF% decreased to 1.02 (95% CI: 1.02–1.02, P < 0.001), and the adjusted HR for the Q4 group was 1.42 (95% CI: 1.37–1.48). After further adjustment for ALT, AST, LDL-C, HDL-C, TC, TG, BUN, and Scr (Model II), the association strength between each 1-unit increase in BF% and IFG risk was an HR of 1.03 (95% CI: 1.02–1.03, P < 0.001). Using the Q1 group as the reference, the HR for the Q4 group was 1.48 (95% CI: 1.42–1.54), and the trend remained statistically significant (P for trend < 0.001).

Nonlinear relationship between BF% and IFG risk

A nonlinear association was observed between BF% and the risk of IFG incidence (Fig. 5). After adjusting for a series of covariates including FPG, SBP, DBP, ALT, AST, LDL-C, HDL-C, TC, TG, BUN, Scr, smoking status, and family history of diabetes at baseline, the curve indicates a nonlinear increasing trend in IFG risk with rising BF% levels. A significant inflection point was identified (Table 4). The analysis revealed a threshold effect in the association between BF% and the risk of IFG incidence, with an inflection point at 30.205. The relationship between BF% and IFG risk exhibited different strengths on either side of this inflection point. When the BF% level was below 30.205, each 1-unit increase in BF% was associated with a 1.3% higher risk of developing IFG (HR 1.013, 95% CI: 1.009–1.017, P < 0.001). However, when BF% reached or exceeded this critical point of 30.205, its effect on IFG risk was significantly amplified. For each 1-unit increase in BF% above the threshold, the IFG risk increased by 4.6% (HR 1.046, 95% CI: 1.038–1.054, P < 0.001). The likelihood ratio test for the threshold effect was statistically significant (P < 0.001).

Subgroup analysis

To evaluate the generalizability of the association between BF% and the risk of new-onset IFG across different populations, we conducted subgroup analyses. The results are presented in a forest plot (Fig. 6), which displays the multivariable-adjusted HR and its corresponding 95% CI for each 1-unit increase in BF% within each subgroup. Participants were stratified by age, gender, BMI, smoking status, drinking status, family history of diabetes, SBP, DBP, TG, TC, LDL-C, and HDL-C. The results demonstrated that elevated BF% was consistently and positively associated with an increased risk of IFG across all predefined subgroups, with all corresponding HRs being greater than 1, indicating a robust and consistent association.

Sensitivity analysis

Supplementary Table 3​ presents the results of the analysis using the original dataset. These results are highly consistent with those of the primary analysis (Table 3), further validating the robustness of the aforementioned association. To assess the impact of missing data on the results, a sensitivity analysis was conducted by excluding participants with incomplete covariate data. As shown in Supplementary Table 4, in this dataset, the results from the fully adjusted Model II still indicated a significant 3% increase in IFG risk for each 1-unit increase in BF% (HR 1.03, 95% CI: 1.02–1.03, P < 0.001). Using the Q1 group as the reference, the Q4 group had a significantly higher risk of developing IFG (HR 1.52, 95% CI: 1.40–1.64, P < 0.001), with a significant dose-response relationship (P for trend < 0.001).

Discussion

In this large-scale longitudinal retrospective cohort investigation, we explored the association between BF% and the risk of developing IFG in a Chinese adult population with normal baseline FPG. Over the follow-up period, we observed an independent and significant positive correlation between BF% and the risk of new-onset IFG among the 184,308 participants. The main findings can be summarized as follows. First, a higher BF% is an independent risk factor for new-onset IFG. Even after full multivariable adjustment, participants with higher body fat burdens exhibited a substantially increased risk of IFG compared to those in the lowest quartile, establishing a clear dose-response relationship. Second, a crucial finding is the revelation of a nonlinear, J-shaped relationship between BF% and IFG risk, characterized by a significant threshold effect. We identified an inflection point at a BF% of approximately 30.205%. Below this threshold, the risk of IFG increased gradually; however, once BF% exceeded this critical point, the magnitude of the risk amplified sharply. This indicates that the impact of BF% on IFG risk is not a simple linear progression but rather escalates sharply after body fat accumulation reaches a specific level. Furthermore, extensive subgroup analyses demonstrated that this positive association remained consistent across diverse populations stratified by age, gender, BMI, and metabolic profiles, highlighting its generalizability. Finally, multiple sensitivity analyses—including those using multiply imputed datasets and complete-case data—further supported the reliability of these primary findings, demonstrating that our conclusions are highly robust and not sensitive to data handling methods.

The sharp escalation in IFG risk observed once BF% exceeds the 30.205% threshold can be plausibly explained by a series of pathophysiological mechanisms triggered by excessive adipose tissue accumulation, potentially related to the following processes. First, insulin resistance represents the central link connecting obesity to fasting dysglycemia. Excess body fat, particularly visceral adipose tissue, is not an inert energy store but a highly active endocrine organ24. Hypertrophic adipocytes secrete excessive amounts of free fatty acids (FFAs) and various pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) and interleukin-6 (IL-6), while also reducing levels of beneficial adipokines like adiponectin25,26. This chronic, low-grade inflammatory state and lipid overflow can interfere with insulin signaling in target tissues such as the liver and skeletal muscle via the circulation. In the liver, insulin resistance leads to unsuppressed hepatic glucose output, directly elevating FPG levels27. In the present study, the association between BF% and IFG remained significant even after adjusting for baseline blood lipids (TG, TC, etc.) and liver enzymes (ALT, AST), although the effect size was attenuated. This suggests that these indicators, which reflect hepatic fat deposition and lipid metabolism disorders, may partially mediate the effect of BF% on IFG risk. Second, ectopic fat deposition may play a key role in the threshold effect identified in this study. When the storage capacity of subcutaneous adipose tissue is saturated, excess lipids are deposited ectopically in non-adipose tissues such as the liver, pancreas, and skeletal muscle28. Ectopic fat deposition in the liver (i.e., non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, NAFLD) exacerbates hepatic insulin resistance and glucose output. Elevated serum levels of ALT and AST often indicate hepatocyte injury or steatosis, which aligns with the trend observed in the baseline characteristics of this study where higher BF% was associated with higher ALT/AST levels. Intramuscular lipid deposition impairs insulin-stimulated glucose uptake. Therefore, the steep increase in risk beyond the inflection point may signify a critical shift in body fat distribution from relatively benign subcutaneous storage to harmful ectopic deposition29. Third, adipose tissue dysfunction and impaired β-cell function are also important mechanisms. Chronic exposure to high levels of FFAs and glucose (i.e., glucolipotoxicity) and an inflammatory environment exerts toxic effects on pancreatic β-cells, impairing their insulin secretory function30. Although the IFG stage is primarily characterized by hepatic insulin resistance, early defects in β-cell glucose sensitivity and secretory capacity are already present, failing to secrete sufficient insulin to adequately compensate for insulin resistance. Excess body fat may accelerate this β-cell functional decline through the aforementioned mechanisms of glucolipotoxicity and inflammation, collectively contributing to elevated fasting glucose. Therefore, the association between BF% and IFG risk observed in this study, particularly the discovery of the nonlinear threshold effect, may involve multiple interrelated pathological processes triggered by excessive body fat. These include insulin resistance, the ensuing chronic inflammatory state, lipotoxicity, and ectopic fat deposition that occurs when body fat accumulation surpasses a specific critical point31. These mechanisms act in concert to disrupt normal glucose homeostasis, ultimately leading to the development of IFG.

The central finding of this study—that a higher BF% is an independent risk factor for new-onset IFG in Chinese adults with normal baseline FPG—aligns strongly with extensive prior evidence on the association between obesity and dysglycemia, while deepening and extending this knowledge in several key aspects. First, the positive association between BF% and IFG risk reinforces the clinical significance of body fat accumulation as a key modifiable risk factor for the prediabetic stage. This association remained significant even after adjusting for various potential confounders, including BMI, blood lipids, and liver enzymes. This suggests that the adverse impact of the fat load represented by BF% on glucose homeostasis is independent of traditional obesity metrics and baseline metabolic status19. As summarized in Table 5, the prognostic value of BF% has been well-documented in previous literature. For instance, Gómez-Ambrosi et al.18 demonstrated in a cross-sectional study that BF% provides crucial risk information beyond BMI, successfully identifying high-risk individuals even within the normal BMI category. Longitudinally, Zhang et al.20 and Pan et al.19 confirmed that elevated adiposity significantly increases the incidence of diabetes. However, a critical distinction and contribution of our research is the targeted focus on the earlier prediabetic stage. While the aforementioned studies primarily evaluated clinical diabetes as their primary endpoint (Table 5), our study utilized a massive cohort (n = 184,308) to exclusively assess the risk of new-onset IFG among individuals with strictly normal baseline fasting glucose. Furthermore, while Pan et al.19 reported a general nonlinear relationship for diabetes, our study extends this concept by pinpointing a specific nonlinear inflection point (approximately 30.205%) for the onset of IFG. This distinction is pathophysiologically significant: it suggests that the adverse metabolic effects of excessive body fat manifest prominently well before the clinical diagnosis of diabetes. By establishing this strong association and specific threshold at the IFG stage, our findings fill a critical gap in the existing literature, emphasizing an earlier and highly precise window for targeted clinical intervention. Regarding the potential biological significance of the inflection point, we hypothesize that it may reflect a critical state in the body’s fat storage and distribution. When total body fat accumulation reaches this level, the storage capacity of subcutaneous adipose tissue may approach saturation, leading to the deposition of excess lipids in visceral or ectopic sites (e.g., liver or skeletal muscle)28. Visceral and ectopic fat deposits possess stronger lipolytic activity and pro-inflammatory cytokine secretion capacity, making them more likely to induce severe insulin resistance and β-cell dysfunction29. A study by Rao et al. in a hypertensive population also observed gender differences in the significant association between BF% and diabetes risk32, indirectly supporting the complexity of body fat distribution and its metabolic impact mechanisms. Therefore, the inflection point identified in our study may mark a key transition in metabolic risk from a supplementary to a qualitative change. Beyond this threshold, the pathophysiological role of adipose tissue undergoes a significant alteration, leading to a sharp exacerbation of metabolic disorder risk. By confirming the longitudinal association between BF% and IFG risk in a Chinese population and identifying its risk inflection point, this study not only strengthens the evidence for body fat accumulation as a risk factor for prediabetes but also deepens the understanding of this issue from “whether an association exists” to “the pattern of the association’s strength.” This provides a critical foundation for formulating future targeted diabetes prevention strategies based on BF% thresholds.

These findings carry significant practical implications. At the clinical level, the identified threshold of approximately 30.205% for body fat percentage provides a quantitative tool for screening individuals at high risk for impaired fasting glucose, particularly those presenting with normal-weight obesity33. For this demographic, regular fasting plasma glucose monitoring and targeted lifestyle interventions focused on fat reduction, such as resistance exercise34, are highly recommended. At the public health level, our results suggest that body fat management should be emphasized alongside traditional weight management in primary diabetes prevention. Integrating body composition assessments into community screening guidelines could facilitate the earlier and better identification of high-risk individuals, thereby potentially curbing the progression to diabetes.

This study possesses several important methodological strengths that enable it to provide reliable and generalizable evidence regarding the association between BF% and the risk of IFG. First, the study is based on a large-scale cohort of Chinese adults and employs a longitudinal retrospective design. The large sample size provides sufficient statistical power to precisely detect the association between BF% and IFG risk, particularly in identifying a relatively subtle nonlinear relationship (threshold effect). The longitudinal design ensures that the exposure factor (BF%) was assessed prior to the occurrence of the outcome (IFG), establishing a temporal sequence and allowing for more effective inference of risk association compared to cross-sectional studies. Second, the study applied rigorous and comprehensive statistical strategies in data analysis. To address missing data, we employed multiple imputation, a method that helps reduce potential selection bias associated with complete-case analysis and makes more efficient use of the available data. In analyzing the association, we constructed progressively adjusted multivariate Cox regression models, systematically controlling for a wide range of confounders, including baseline blood glucose, BP, blood lipids, liver and kidney function, and lifestyle factors, thereby enhancing the credibility of the results. Furthermore, the highly consistent results from sensitivity analyses using both the original dataset and the dataset with missing values excluded fully demonstrate the robustness of the study’s core findings. Third, the data for this study were sourced from a health screening program covering multiple regions across China. The study participants included adults of varying ages, genders, and BMI ranges. Compared to studies based on specific patient populations or single geographic areas, our study population exhibits greater diversity and representativeness. Consequently, the findings are likely more applicable to the broader Chinese adult population, providing a valuable reference for formulating public health strategies for prediabetes prevention. While this study possesses several strengths, it also has certain limitations. First, as an observational study, despite employing a longitudinal design and adjusting for a wide range of known confounding factors (such as blood lipids and liver enzymes), it cannot completely rule out the possibility of residual confounding. Unmeasured or imprecisely measured factors (e.g., genetic background, detailed dietary patterns, sleep quality, and socioeconomic status) may potentially influence the estimated association between BF% and IFG. Therefore, the findings primarily demonstrate a significant association between BF% and IFG risk and cannot establish a strict causal relationship. Second, the BF% used in this study was an estimated value calculated using a formula incorporating BMI, age, and gender, rather than a direct measurement obtained by more precise methods such as DEXA or BIA. Although this formula is widely used and validated in large epidemiological studies, it differs from the “gold standard” for body composition measurement, potentially introducing measurement error. This could lead to an underestimation or bias in the true strength of the association. Third, the study participants were sourced from individuals undergoing voluntary health screenings, which may introduce selection bias. This population typically has higher health awareness and may exhibit healthier lifestyles and better health status compared to the general population. This “healthy volunteer effect” may limit the generalizability of the findings to the broader general population, particularly those who do not participate in regular health check-ups. Finally, although we adjusted for numerous covariates, the dataset lacked certain important behavioral and physical activity factors, such as specific macronutrient intake composition, the intensity and frequency of physical activity, and sedentary time. These are key variables influencing body fat accumulation and glucose metabolism, and their absence may constitute a source of residual confounding. Future research should aim to collect more comprehensive behavioral data to establish more robust risk prediction models.

Conclusion

This large-scale retrospective cohort study demonstrates that elevated BF% is an independent risk factor for new-onset IFG in Chinese adults. Notably, we identified a nonlinear, J-shaped relationship with a critical inflection point at approximately 30.205%, beyond which the risk of IFG escalates sharply. These findings highlight the importance of incorporating body fat assessment into routine prediabetes screening to help identify high-risk individuals, suggesting the potential value of body composition management in primary prevention strategies.
