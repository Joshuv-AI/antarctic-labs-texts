Trigeminal neuralgia: from clinical challenges to molecular insights
Authors: Yijia Xu, Yujie Fan, Ruiding Li, Binbin Tan, Bo Li, Jianfang Sun, Shuai Han, Jianqi Wu, Anhua Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTrigeminal neuralgia (TN) is a paroxysmal, recurrent, electric shock‑ or knife‑like severe pain localized to the sensory distribution of the trigeminal nerve branches in the face. Often referred to as “the most painful affliction known to humankind”. First-line drugs carbamazepine and oxcarbazepine are associated with long-term side effects and diminishing efficacy, highlighting an urgent need for new therapeutic strategies. This review systematically summarizes the current progress in TN animal models (ranging from chronic constriction and inflammatory models to genetically engineered models), the multi-brain-region interactive pain transmission circuitry, molecular targets (voltage-gated sodium/calcium/potassium channels, TRP channels, chemokines, and epigenetic regulators), and drug development. Current treatments primarily rely on ion channel modulators, while emerging strategies involve Nav1.7/Cav3.2 inhibitors, CGRP antibodies, NRF2 activators, and botulinum toxin. Future efforts should focus on establishing more clinically translational precision models, dissecting the spatiotemporal dynamics of neural circuits, promoting multi-target combination therapies based on molecular subtyping, and accelerating the transition from symptomatic control to curative treatment.

Background

Trigeminal neuralgia (TN) is a paroxysmal, recurrent, electric shock‑ or knife‑like severe pain localized to the sensory distribution of the trigeminal nerve branches in the face. Often referred to as “the most painful affliction known to humankind”, it imposes profound physical and psychological distress on patients [1].

Pharmacotherapy serves as the cornerstone of all treatment modalities and occupies the most critical position. Among them, the first‑line agents carbamazepine and oxcarbazepine efficiently suppress abnormal high‑frequency neuronal firing by blocking voltage‑gated sodium channels [2], thereby providing reliable and rapid symptom control for most newly diagnosed patients. However, long‑term use is associated with dose‑related adverse effects, drug interactions, and potential hematological and hepatic enzyme abnormalities, rendering some patients intolerant or experiencing diminished efficacy over time [3]. This clinical challenge has driven continued efforts to explore alternative or combination regimens. In this context, agents such as lamotrigine, gabapentin, and pregabalin, leveraging their distinct mechanisms of action and relatively milder side effect profiles, have gradually established their roles as second‑line options or key components of combination therapy, particularly for patients intolerant to first‑line drugs, those with contraindications, or those who respond poorly [4, 5].

The decline in drug tolerance, accumulation of adverse effects, and the emergence of refractory cases during long‑term pharmacotherapy have prompted the implementation of interventional procedures [4]. In clinical practice, interventional treatments achieve precise targeting of the trigeminal ganglion or nerve roots by physically or chemically ablating portions of the nerve. These include percutaneous balloon compression, radiofrequency thermocoagulation, and glycerol rhizolysis [4, 6]. For classic cases with definitive neurovascular compression that are refractory to medication and interventional approaches, surgical intervention in the form of microvascular decompression, which involves placing a spacer between the vessel and the trigeminal nerve root via craniotomy remains the only treatment option that offers long‑term efficacy [5–8]. Nevertheless, ablating sensory afferents carries risks of sensory deficits and nerve function impairment, while craniotomy demands stringent patient fitness, incurs substantial costs, and requires prolonged postoperative recovery [9, 10].

Despite the established status of pharmacotherapy, the limitations of current drugs underscore the urgent necessity to gain a deeper mechanistic insight into TN. Recent research indicates that the pathogenesis of TN involves not only abnormal expression of ion channels in the trigeminal ganglion, but also intricate pain transmission circuits spanning multiple brain regions. These novel molecular and circuit-level perspectives are paving new ways for therapeutic intervention. Accordingly, this manuscript reviews recent advances in animal models, pain transduction pathways, molecular pathological mechanisms, and potential drug targets associated with TN, thereby establishing a theoretical foundation for the treatment of TN and the development or refinement of therapeutic agents.

Classifying trigeminal neuralgia: bridging clinical subtypes, therapeutic strategies, and their corresponding animal models

Clinical classification and therapeutic strategies of trigeminal neuralgia

According to the third edition of the International Classification of Headache Disorders (ICHD-3) by the International Headache Society (IHS), TN is categorized into the following three subtypes [10]: classical TN (characterized by neurovascular compression at the trigeminal root entry zone, accompanied by morphological changes of the nerve), secondary TN (caused by underlying diseases such as multiple sclerosis or tumors; notably, multiple sclerosis is associated with an approximately 20-fold increased risk of TN [11]), and idiopathic TN (without identifiable etiology). Although not listed as a separate subtype in the ICHD-3, familial TN has been increasingly recognized, often presenting with similar clinical features to sporadic cases and, in some instances, associated with specific genetic mutations [12–14, 69].

The treatment of TN primarily comprises pharmacotherapy and surgical interventions. First-line drugs include carbamazepine and oxcarbazepine, which inhibit high-frequency ectopic discharges of the trigeminal nerve by blocking voltage-gated sodium channels [10, 15]. Second- and third-line agents include gabapentin, pregabalin, lamotrigine, and baclofen. Regarding surgical treatments, microvascular decompression (MVD) is considered a curative procedure, whereas other ablative surgeries include radiofrequency thermocoagulation, balloon compression, glycerol injection, and gamma knife radiosurgery [15].

Animal models of trigeminal neuralgia and their clinical correspondence

Given the complex etiology of TN and the difficulty in obtaining clinical specimens, animal models serve as essential tools for investigating TN mechanisms and screening therapeutic agents. Based on etiological and pathological characteristics, these models can be classified into several categories, including trigeminal nerve root/ganglion compression models, peripheral nerve injury models, chemically induced models, secondary TN models, and genetically engineered models (Fig. 1; Table 1).

Trigeminal nerve root/ganglion compression models

The most classic model is the chronic nerve root compression model. Luo et al. first reported a rat model in which a plastic filament was retrogradely inserted through the infraorbital fissure to compress the trigeminal nerve root (TNR) [16]. Under stereotaxic guidance, a plastic filament with a diameter of 1 mm was inserted retrogradely into the cranial cavity via the infraorbital fissure along the infraorbital nerve (ION). Subsequent studies confirmed that this model induces glial activation and peripheral sensitization [17, 18].

In recent years, Ding et al. established the foramen lacerum impingement of trigeminal nerve root (FLIT) model, which exhibits greater clinical relevance [19, 20]. This model utilizes the naturally occurring foramen lacerum at the skull base, and via a cervical approach, an absorbable gelatin sponge is precisely placed between the TNR and the auditory bulla, resulting in sustained and localized nerve root compression. This design directly simulates the most common etiology of classical TN, namely neurovascular compression. Moreover, the compressive material is absorbable, conferring reversibility to the model. Behaviorally, this model manifests a richer repertoire of TN-like behaviors, including paroxysmal, asymmetric facial grooming (resembling “tic douloureux” --the most characteristic pain syndrome in TN patients). Furthermore, model animals exhibit a series of toothache-like behaviors, including overgrowth of incisors, deviation of the head to the contralateral side during feeding, active avoidance of solid food, reduced ability to gnaw wooden blocks, and postoperative weight loss, thereby closely mimicking oral trigger pain in clinical TN patients.

In 2025, Abdulrahim et al. established a novel intradural trigeminal nerve compression (idTNC) mouse model [21]. This model adopts a trans-supraorbital fissure approach to precisely place a silicone-coated filament into the intradural region of the TNR, directly simulating the core etiology of classical TN–neurovascular compression of the intradural segment of the TNR. Behavioral characterization revealed that postoperative model animals exhibited significant mechanical and thermal hyperalgesia, while calcium imaging demonstrated a marked increase in spontaneous activity of trigeminal ganglion (TG) neurons.

Ahn et al. employed a stereotaxic injection method to deliver a 4% agar solution into the rat TG via a transcranial approach, thereby creating a chronic compression model of the TG [22]. This model displayed mechanical allodynia and hyperalgesia in the vibrissal pad area from days 3 to 40 post-surgery, without an observed increase in facial grooming behavior.

Peripheral nerve injury models

Vos et al. created a chronic constriction injury (CCI) model by loosely ligating the ION via a midline facial incision in rats. This model is currently one of the most widely used animal models of TN [23]. Ding et al. subsequently modified this approach to establish a distal ION-CCI (dION-CCI) model, which avoids retraction of orbital contents and thus reduces surgical trauma [24, 25]. The above peripheral nerve injury models exhibit TN-like behaviors following surgery, including mechanical allodynia, cold/heat hyperalgesia in the ION-innervated region (vibrissal pad and upper lip), as well as increased facial grooming. Owing to their ease of operation and stable phenotypes, these models are often employed as surrogate models for drug screening in TN research.

Building upon the ION-CCI model, researchers have proposed two nerve injury models with smaller surgical incisions. The Foramen Rotundum Inflammatory Constriction Trigeminal Infra Orbital Nerve model (FRICT-ION) involves placing a 3-mm chromic gut suture into the foramen rotundum via a small buccal mucosal incision, positioning the suture along the course of the ION to produce sustained inflammatory compression [26]. This model involves minimal surgical trauma and no bleeding, and it can induce mechanical allodynia lasting up to 10 ~ 14 weeks, accompanied by anxiety- and depression-like behaviors. Another model is the distal infraorbital nerve injury (DIONI) model [27], in which the distal ION is ligated and transected after exiting the infraorbital foramen. This model is minimally traumatic, spares the orbital contents, and induces persistent cold and mechanical allodynia. Behavioral assessments can detect both cold and mechanical allodynia, rendering this model particularly suitable for studying cognitive and affective changes associated with persistent facial pain.

In addition, inferior alveolar nerve/mental nerve injury models have been developed to simulate iatrogenic nerve injury, like post-extraction or post-implantation pain [28, 29]. These models manifest as allodynia in the mandibular region and reduced bite force.

Chemically induced models

A variety of chemical agents have also been employed to establish TN models. These include the cobra venom injection model, in which cobra venom is injected into the nerve sheath of the ION to induce demyelination and nerve conduction block [30]; the subarachnoid injection model of central excitatory substances, wherein picrotoxin, strychnine, or other central excitatory substances are injected into the dorsal subarachnoid space of the caudal medulla to elicit paroxysmal pain [31]; and the formaldehyde-induced model, in which a diluted formaldehyde solution is subcutaneously injected into the upper lip or vibrissal pad area of rodents to induce typical trigeminal neuralgia-like facial wiping/rubbing behavior [32]. Another representative model is the focal demyelination model using lysophosphatidylcholine (LPC), which is stereotaxically injected into the trigeminal root entry zone to provoke paroxysmal tic-like pain [33]. In addition, photochemically induced models and penicillin injection models have also been used to establish trigeminal neuralgia models mimicking different etiologies [34].

Secondary TN models

Secondary trigeminal neuralgia models mainly include the experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis (EAE) model and the varicella-zoster virus (VZV) model [35, 36]. The EAE model is established by immunizing C57BL/6 mice with myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein (MOG) to recapitulate multiple sclerosis (MS)-related secondary TN. The VZV model is generated by injecting varicella-zoster virus into the vibrissal pad of rats to simulate postherpetic trigeminal neuralgia (PHN), which manifests as persistent allodynia.

Genetically engineered models

With the maturation of gene-editing technologies such as CRISPR/Cas9, researchers have introduced mutations in genes associated with TN dysfunction into the mouse genome to construct specific genetically engineered models. For instance, Dong et al. used CRISPR/Cas9 technology to generate a point mutation mouse model of the GABA-A receptor γ1 subunit carrying the GABRG1 gene p.Cys188Trp missense mutation [37]. In addition, mouse models with mutations in ion channel genes such as Nav1.7, Nav1.3, Nav1.8, TRPM8, and CACNA1G have also been successively established [38–41]. The most distinctive feature of genetically engineered models is their ability to directly simulate the genetic susceptibility underlying familial or idiopathic TN. For example, mutations in GABRG1 and CACNA1G were originally identified through gene sequencing in TN patients.

Furthermore, the oligodendrocyte-specific apoptosis model represents another genetically engineered approach. In this model, a recombinant adeno-associated virus (rAAV2/9) carrying Cre recombinase driven by the myelin basic protein (MBP) gene promoter, together with a Cre-inducible apoptotic execution gene (taCasp3-TEVp), is precisely injected into the trigeminal root entry zone of adult mice to induce demyelination and TN-like pain [33].

Trigeminal neuralgia-related pain transmission circuits and molecular targets

Pain transmission circuits associated with trigeminal neuralgia

The pathological mechanisms underlying trigeminal neuralgia (TN) are complex and involve network-level dysfunctions characterized by abnormal amplification, integration, and maintenance of pain signals across multiple brain regions. Previous studies have also indicated that TN pain transmission does not follow a single linear pathway; rather, it constitutes an intricate network of parallel branches, ascending facilitation, descending modulation, and positive feedback amplification. Therefore, a systematic dissection of the multilevel circuits involved in TN pain transmission, along with their interrelationships, represents a necessary prerequisite for elucidating the core mechanisms of TN and identifying key nodes for therapeutic intervention (Fig. 2).

Peripheral input from the trigeminal ganglion and the first-order relay in the brainstem

The TG, as a cluster of primary sensory neuron somata, serves as the first station.

for orofacial sensory afferents. In TN models, TG neurons exhibit heightened excitability and upregulated expression of neuropeptides such as calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP). Studies have confirmed that the TG not only transmits signals via the classical spinal trigeminal nucleus caudalis (Sp5C) but also directly projects to the parabrachial nucleus (PBN), thereby contributing to emotional pain processing [42]. In TN models, astrocytes in the Sp5C become activated, with increased expression of GFAP, and the P2 × 3 receptor is upregulated in Sp5C astrocytes, suggesting that glial cells are involved in the amplification and maintenance of pain signals [43].

As the first-order relay station for orofacial pain, the spinal trigeminal nucleus caudalis (Sp5C) receives inputs from the TG and relays them to higher brain centers. Studies have shown that Sp5C neurons expressing tachykinin 1 (Tac1) project specifically to the PBN, forming a TG→Sp5CTac1→PBNTac1 pathway. This circuit plays a critical role in the pathogenesis of TN and modulates pain-like behaviors in TN models [44]. In addition, Sp5C neurons also project to the ventral posteromedial nucleus (VPM) of the thalamus, constituting the classical trigeminothalamic tract [45]. Recent studies have further revealed that within the Sp5C, wide dynamic range (WDR) neurons and non-specific neurons exhibit increased excitability in TN models. Their glutamatergic projections to the lateral parabrachial nucleus (LPBN) activate rostral ventromedial medulla (RVM) on cells, which in turn facilitate Sp5C neuronal activity through descending projections, thereby constituting an amplification circuit. Moreover, inhibition of the glutamatergic pathway from the Sp5C to the LPBN reverses chronic orofacial pain induced by experimental occlusal interference (EOI) [46].

Brainstem-midbrain circuit

The PBN serves as a key relay station for TN signal transmission, integrating inputs from the Sp5C and TG, and projecting to multiple higher brain regions. Tac1-positive neurons within the PBN exhibit enhanced activation in TN models, and chemogenetic inhibition of PBNTac1 neurons effectively alleviates TN-induced mechanical allodynia [44]. The PBN also sends projections to the ventral tegmental area (VTA), forming an Sp5C-PBN-VTA pathway that mediates TN-related depressive-like behaviors [47]. Furthermore, the projection from the PBN to the RVM constitutes the Sp5-LPBN-RVM circuit, which enhances RVM descending facilitation through a positive feedback amplification mechanism, thereby promoting the chronification of orofacial pain [46].

The periaqueductal gray (PAG) is a core node of the descending analgesic system. In the ventrolateral PAG (vlPAG), the activity of CaMKIIα positive neurons is decreased in TN models, whereas optogenetic activation of vlPAG glutamatergic neurons alleviates pain hypersensitivity and aberrant thalamic discharge in rat models of TN [48]. The vlPAG exerts descending modulatory effects via projections to the RVM and the locus coeruleus (LC). Recent studies have shown that activation of the vlPAG-LC pathway concurrently relieves mechanical allodynia and anxiety-like behaviors in mice with spared nerve injury (SNI). The analgesic effect depends on α2-adrenergic receptors in the spinal dorsal horn, whereas the anxiolytic effect is achieved through the vlPAG-LC-ACC pathway [49]. However, the activity of the vlPAG-LC pathway in TN models exhibits dynamic changes, with increased activity at early stages and potential adaptive alterations at later stages.

Thalamocortical circuit

The VPM of the thalamus serves as the principal thalamic relay nucleus for orofacial pain information. In TN models, VPM neurons exhibit increased firing frequency and augmented burst discharge, which correlate with enhanced input from the spinal trigeminal nucleus caudalis (Sp5C) [45]. VPM glutamatergic neurons, upon receiving projections from the Sp5C, further project to the dysgranular posterior insular cortex (DPIC), forming an Sp5C→VPMGlu→DPICGlu pathway that contributes to the maintenance and spread of TN [45]. Optogenetic inhibition of DPIC glutamatergic neurons reduces neuronal discharge in both the trigeminal nucleus caudalis (TNC; synonymous with Sp5C) and the VPM, and alleviates pain hypersensitivity as well as anxiety-like behaviors in TN rats [50].

The mediodorsal thalamus (MD) serves as another important thalamic nucleus and plays a critical role in pain processing in TN. CaMKIIα-positive neurons within the MD respond to both mechanical and thermal stimuli; activation of the MD induces hyperalgesia and aversive behaviors, whereas inhibition of the MD alleviates SNI-induced mechanical allodynia and anxiety-like behaviors [51]. The MD sends dense projections to the ACC, forming the MD-ACC circuit. Under neuropathic pain conditions, plastic changes occur in the MD-ACC input, leading to a shift in the excitation/inhibition balance of subcortically projecting neurons (SC neurons) within the ACC toward inhibition, thereby exacerbating pain-related aversion [52]. Conversely, projections from the ACC to the MD also participate in pain modulation, and activation of the ACC-MD pathway induces hyperalgesia and anxiety-like behaviors [51].

The paraventricular thalamus (PVT) plays a critical role in the emotional integration of TN. Glutamatergic neurons in the anterior PVT (aPVT) are specifically activated in the chronic constriction injury of the infraorbital nerve (CCI-ION) model; optogenetic activation of the aPVT exacerbates TN pain hypersensitivity, whereas its inhibition alleviates pain [53]. The aPVT sends projections to the ACC, forming the aPVT-ACC circuit. Activation of this circuit augments TN pain by increasing glutamate release and N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) receptor activation in the ACC [53]. In contrast, the posterior PVT (pPVT) participates in the descending facilitation of neuropathic pain via projections to the zona incerta (ZI) and the vlPAG [54]. The PVT-basolateral amygdala (BLA) circuit is involved in both the nociceptive and anxiety-like behavioral responses associated with TN [55].

Cortex-involved circuits

The ACC is a core brain region for emotional processing in TN. In TN model mice, layer V pyramidal neurons in the ACC exhibit reduced excitability and enhanced inhibitory input, leading to decreased ACC output [56]. Vasoactive intestinal polypeptide (VIP)-expressing interneurons within the ACC show reduced activity in TN models; chemogenetic activation of these VIP neurons restores ACC output and alleviates TN-related pain hypersensitivity as well as anxiety- and depressive-like behaviors [57]. The ACC also receives projections from the ventral hippocampus (vHPC), forming the vHPC-ACC circuit. This circuit activates corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH)-positive GABAergic interneurons in the ACC, which in turn inhibit layer V pyramidal neurons, thereby mediating TN-related anxiety- and depressive-like behaviors [56]. In addition to receiving inputs, the ACC is also involved in bidirectional projections with the VTA, forming a positive feedback loop: ACC glutamatergic neurons project to the VTA, where they activate local GABAergic interneurons, which indirectly inhibit VTA dopaminergic neurons; the latter project back to the ACC, forming an ACCGlu→VTAGABA→VTADA→ACCGlu circuit. Under neuropathic pain conditions, the activity of this circuit is enhanced, leading to the mutual exacerbation and chronification of pain and emotional symptoms.

The medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) also undergoes plastic changes in TN. Specifically, in TN model mice, layer V pyramidal neurons in the mPFC exhibit reduced excitability, and consequently, the projection from the mPFC to the vlPAG is weakened [58]. Dopaminergic projections from the VTA to the mPFC participate in pain modulation, and accordingly, activation of the VTA-mPFC circuit alleviates SNI-induced mechanical allodynia [59]. Glutamatergic projections from the nucleus reuniens (RE) of the thalamus to the mPFC are enhanced in TN models, Conversely, inhibition of the RE-mPFC circuit concurrently relieves both pain hypersensitivity and depressive-like behaviors in SNI mice [60]. The medial orbitofrontal cortex (mOFC) is activated in TN, In particular, its glutamatergic neurons projecting to the MD to regulate pain sensitivity, whereas projections to the basolateral amygdala (BLA) to modulate anxiety- and depressive-like behaviors [61], suggesting a functional dissociation of the cortico-thalamo-amygdala circuit.

The insular cortex (IC) is also important in TN processing. For example, the posterior insular cortex (PIC) exhibits increased activity in TN models. Moreover, optogenetic inhibition of glutamatergic neurons in the DPIC ameliorates aberrant firing in the TNC and VPM, as well as alleviates pain hypersensitivity and anxiety-like behaviors [50]. In addition, the PIC also receives projections from the VPM, forming a VPM-PIC pathway that participates in pain perception.

A comprehensive understanding of molecular targets associated with TN is essential for the development of safer and more effective drugs.

Ion channels as molecular targets in TN research

Voltage-gated sodium channels

Voltage-gated sodium channels (VGSCs) represent the primary target of carbamazepine, a first-line drug for TN treatment. Studies have revealed aberrant expression of multiple sodium channel subtypes in the trigeminal ganglion (TG) and trigeminal nerve root of TN patients. Nav1.3 is upregulated in TN patients and animal models [62, 63], whereas Nav1.7 is downregulated in gingival tissue of TN patients, for Nav1.8, no significant change was detected in human gingival tissue or in a rat TN model [62, 64]. Such altered expression patterns may contribute to neuronal hyperexcitability and ectopic discharge. Furthermore, Nav1.9 mediates mechanical and thermal hyperalgesia in the ION-CCI mouse model [65]. A gain-of-function mutation (M136V) in Nav1.6 increases resurgent current density and lowers current threshold, leading to hyperexcitability of TG neurons and is associated with familial TN [66]. The above findings on altered VGSC expression should be interpreted with caution. First, mRNA expression levels do not necessarily correlate linearly with functional protein levels [67]. Second, highly specific and reliable antibodies for precise quantification of VGSC subtypes are currently lacking; even recent efforts have faced challenges, including the retraction of a widely cited Nav1.7 antibody study [68]. Therefore, although altered VGSC expression remains a plausible hypothesis in the pathogenesis of TN, high-quality human data supporting this claim are still limited. Acknowledging these limitations is essential for a rigorous and balanced interpretation of the current evidence [62–64].

Voltage-gated calcium channels

Voltage-gated calcium channels (VGCCs) play a critical role in neurotransmitter release and regulation of neuronal excitability. Cav3.2 (T-type calcium channel) is the most extensively studied calcium channel subtype in TN. Multiple missense mutations (E286K, H526Y, G563R, P566T) in the CACNA1H gene have been identified in TN patients; among these, the G563R and P566T mutations result in a gain of channel function, increasing calcium current density and TG neuron excitability [69, 70]. In the CCI-ION mouse model, Cav3.2 is upregulated in the spinal trigeminal subnucleus caudalis (Sp5C), but not in the peripheral TG. The T-type calcium channel blocker Z944 produces anti-allodynic effects by targeting Cav3.2, and this effect is absent in Cav3.2 knockout mice [70]. Additionally, a CACNA1A mutation (P2455H) in Cav2.1 has also been associated with familial TN [71]. Epidemiological studies show that trigeminal neuralgia is more common in women [72–74]. The mechanism remains unclear, but estrogen is known to modulate voltage-gated calcium channels and opioid receptors, potentially affecting pain signaling and drug response [75].

Voltage-gated potassium channels

Dysfunction of voltage-gated potassium channels (VGPCs) can lead to neuronal hyperexcitability. Among these, Kv2.1 (encoded by KCNB1) is downregulated in the TG of CCI-ION rats. Its downregulation results in a lowered action potential threshold and increased firing frequency. When miR-323-3p targets Kv2.1 to inhibit its translation, TN pain is significantly alleviated [76]. The inwardly rectifying potassium channel 4.1 (Kir4.1) is predominantly expressed in satellite glial cells (SGCs). In the CCI-ION rat model, Kir4.1 expression is significantly downregulated, which in turn leads to activation of SGCs and upregulation of glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP). Further studies have demonstrated that SGC-specific overexpression of Kir4.1 alleviates CCI-ION-induced mechanical allodynia [77]. SGCs in the TG tightly envelop neuronal somata and play an important role in TN.

TRP channels

The transient receptor potential (TRP) channel family serves as important pain sensors. Among these, TRPA1 completely mediates non-evoked nocifensive behaviors as well as mechanical, cold, and chemical allodynia in the CCI-ION mouse model, a process involving monocyte/macrophage infiltration [78]. TRPM3 mediates spontaneous pain and mechanical allodynia in an orofacial neuropathic pain mouse model, and its antagonist reverses these pain behaviors [79]. The A931T mutation of TRPM7 (located at the interface between transmembrane domain S3 and voltage sensing region S4) has been identified as a risk factor for familial TN. This mutation generates a non-canonical “omega pore” that mediates persistent Na⁺ influx, leading to hyperexcitability of TG neurons [80]. TRPM8, a cold‑activated TRP channel, has been implicated in orofacial pain conditions, although its specific role in trigeminal neuralgia remains to be established [38].Furthermore, TRPV4 is expressed in spinal microglia and promotes neuropathic pain by releasing lipocalin 2 (LCN2), which enhances excitatory synaptic transmission [81].

Chemokines and their receptors serve as bridging mediators in neuron-glia interactions. Following CCI-ION, CCL2 is upregulated in the TG and enhances Nav1.8 current density via the CCR2/ERK pathway [82, 83]. CXCL1 released from spinal dorsal horn astrocytes enhances NMDA receptor activity and COX-2 expression via CXCR2 [84]. CXCL13 is upregulated in the TG, spinal cord, and anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), mediating neuron-neuron (in the TG and ACC) or neuron-astrocyte (in the spinal cord) interactions through CXCR5, thereby contributing to pain and pain-related negative affect [85, 86]. The CX3CL1/CX3CR1 axis mediates neuron-microglia interactions and plays an important role in neuropathic pain [87].

The MAPK signaling pathway is highly activated in TN. Following CCI-ION, phosphorylation levels of ERK, JNK, p38, and ERK5 are elevated in the TG, and the scorpion neurotoxin Syb-prII-1 dose-dependently inhibits these pathways [88]. Nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (NRF2) exerts a protective role in TN, as its activation reverses oxidative stress and produces analgesic effects [89]. The peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor (PPAR) family (PPARα, PPARβ/δ, and PPARγ) exerts anti-inflammatory and analgesic effects through both transcription-dependent and transcription-independent mechanisms [90].

In addition, epigenetic regulation has garnered increasing attention in TN. For instance, TET3-mediated demethylation of the Cbln2 promoter leads to upregulation of CBLN2 in TG neurons, which enhances neuronal excitability and pro-inflammatory cytokine release via activation of the ERK signaling pathway [91]. PRMT2-mediated H3R8me2a increases the binding of the transcription factor FOXA2 to the miR-323-3p promoter, upregulating miR-323-3p expression, which in turn targets and inhibits Kv2.1 [76]. L3MBTL2 exerts a protective role through epigenetic regulation and alleviation of endoplasmic reticulum stress, and its expression is downregulated in TN [92].

Recently, cannabinoid receptors have emerged as potential targets for orofacial pain. Activation of CB1 receptors by synthetic cannabinoids attenuates mechanical and thermal hypersensitivity in a rat TN model [93]. Consistently, intranasal administration of a CB2 agonist ameliorates cold allodynia in a trigeminal neuropathic pain model by suppressing microglial activation [94]. These findings suggest that targeting the endocannabinoid system may represent a novel therapeutic strategy for TN.

Research progress on pharmacotherapeutic agents for trigeminal neuralgia

In clinical practice, the therapeutic strategy for TN is typically initiated with pharmacotherapy, with surgical intervention considered only when necessary (Fig. 3). First-line pharmacotherapies are predominantly represented by carbamazepine and oxcarbazepine, whereas second-line agents include lamotrigine, gabapentin, pregabalin, baclofen, and botulinum toxin type A [5]. However, currently available drugs are commonly associated with dose-limiting adverse effects (e.g., dizziness, somnolence, and liver dysfunction) as well as diminished efficacy over time [95]. Consequently, to overcome the limitations of existing therapies, numerous novel drug candidates are currently under clinical and preclinical investigation.

Marketed drugs for TN treatment

Ion channel modulators in TN therapy

Ion channel modulators constitute the predominant class of first- and second-line pharmacotherapies for TN (Table 4). VGSCs are core molecular determinants of neuronal action potential generation and propagation. Under the pathological state of TN, focal demyelination in the trigeminal root entry zone leads to aberrant accumulation and functional upregulation of VGSCs, thereby resulting in neuronal hyperexcitability and ectopic discharge. Among the various VGSC subtypes, Nav1.3, Nav1.7, and Nav1.8 exhibit upregulated expression in TN models and serve as important analgesic targets. Currently recognized VGSC blockers demonstrate the following representative characteristics. For instance, carbamazepine achieves an efficacy rate of 70 ~ 90% for paroxysmal TN pain; however, it loses effectiveness in 50% of patients after 5 ~ 10 years of long-term use, and is accompanied by a range of side effects, with major adverse reactions including dizziness, ataxia, somnolence, hyponatremia, hepatic dysfunction, and bone marrow suppression [96, 97]. This “loss of efficacy” is carbamazepine resistance, which mechanistically can be divided into pharmacokinetic tolerance and true pharmacodynamic resistance. Therefore, the long-term decline in efficacy should not be attributed solely to disease progression or pharmacodynamic tolerance; rather, attention should also be paid to pharmacokinetic mechanisms [98–102].Voltage-gated calcium channel α2-δ auxiliary subunits (Cav α2-δ) are located on the presynaptic membrane, where they modulate the trafficking and functional properties of calcium channels. In neuropathic pain conditions, α2-δ subunit expression is upregulated, leading to increased presynaptic calcium influx and enhanced release of excitatory neurotransmitters (e.g., glutamate and substance P). α2-δ ligands act by binding to this subunit, thereby inhibiting calcium channel trafficking and function. Currently known Cav α2-δ subunit modulators exhibit the following representative characteristics: they offer better tolerability than sodium channel blockers, with a lower incidence of adverse effects (dizziness, somnolence, peripheral edema); nevertheless, their efficacy against paroxysmal pain is weaker than that of carbamazepine, although they may be more effective for concomitant persistent pain. Moreover, co-administration with ropivacaine can enhance the analgesic effect [103].

Other types of TN therapeutic drugs

GABA (gamma-aminobutyric acid) serves as the primary inhibitory neurotransmitter in the central nervous system [104]. GABAA receptors are chloride channels whose activation induces neuronal hyperpolarization; in contrast, GABAB receptors are G protein-coupled receptors that reduce neurotransmitter release by activating potassium channels and inhibiting calcium channels. In TN, a decline in GABAergic inhibitory function leads to central disinhibition. Baclofen, clonazepam, and topiramate are representative drugs of this class. Notably, these agents are particularly suitable for TN associated with multiple sclerosis, as they also possess antispasmodic properties [37, 104]. However, their efficacy is limited when used as monotherapy; they are therefore often combined with carbamazepine. Abrupt discontinuation may precipitate withdrawal symptoms, including hallucinations and seizures.

In addition to GABAergic receptor inhibitors, other agents-such as synaptic vesicle protein 2 A (SV2A) modulators and prostaglandin E1 analogs-are also clinically applied for the treatment of different types of TN [3, 105] (Table 5). The PGE1 analogue misoprostol has shown efficacy in refractory TN, possibly via immunomodulatory mechanisms including inhibition of T lymphocyte function and reduction of perineural inflammatory activity, as indicated by clinical studies in MS‑associated TN; the SV2A modulator levetiracetam exerts its effect by binding to the presynaptic vesicle protein SV2A, thereby reducing excessive neurotransmitter release and dampening neuronal hyperexcitability within trigeminal nociceptive pathways [3, 105].

TN therapeutic drugs in clinical and preclinical research

Ion channel modulators

Given the central role of VGSCs in neuronal action potential generation and propagation, drug development efforts targeting VGSCs remain continuously ongoing, primarily focusing on the discovery of novel agents acting as selective sodium channel inhibitors [2]. Current research indicates that Nav1.7-selective blockers offer certain theoretical advantages, including the potential to avoid central nervous system (CNS) side effects. Nevertheless, they also present clinical challenges, as phase II trials have failed to meet primary endpoints [3]. This observation further suggests that the pathological mechanisms underlying trigeminal neuralgia (TN) likely involve multiple sodium channel subtypes, and that selective blockade of a single subtype may be insufficient to achieve complete pain control. Interpreting negative outcomes from Nav1.7‑selective blocker trials requires caution, as conclusions on single‑channel blockade depend on pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic evidence of target engagement and are complicated by autonomic side effects that limit dose escalation (e.g., MK‑2075) [106]. Therefore, such results should not be taken as definitive proof that Nav1.7 plays no role in TN pain, and alternative sodium channel subtypes (e.g., Nav1.8 or Nav1.9) warrant continued investigation [39].Nav1.8 is another promising analgesic target that is primarily expressed in peripheral nociceptive neurons. Unlike Nav1.7, its inhibition is not limited by autonomic side effects. In 2025, the selective Nav1.8 blocker suzetrigine received FDA approval for acute pain [107]. In TN, Nav1.8 expression is abnormal [62, 63]. Given that Nav1.8, Nav1.7, and Nav1.9 are co‑expressed in the trigeminal ganglion, monotherapy with a single blocker may provide limited efficacy, and combination with multi‑channel sodium channel blockers represents a strategy worth exploring.Representative ion channel modulators currently in clinical development are summarized in Table 6.

Other agents

In addition to ion channel modulators, numerous other types of TN therapeutic agents continue to be identified (Table 7). For example, calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP) is a 37-amino-acid neuropeptide synthesized and released by trigeminal ganglion neurons. Upon binding to its receptor (comprising a calcitonin-like receptor and receptor activity-modifying protein 1), CGRP mediates vasodilation and neurogenic inflammation. Although CGRP plays a central role in migraine, its involvement in TN remains controversial [108]. Erenumab, fremanezumab, and rimegepant are representative agents currently under investigation.

Botulinum toxin type A (BoNT-A) acts as an inhibitor of SNARE proteins. The soluble N-ethylmaleimide-sensitive factor attachment protein receptor (SNARE) complex-comprising SNAP-25, VAMP/synaptobrevin, and syntaxin-mediates the fusion of synaptic vesicles with the presynaptic membrane. BoNT-A, a zinc-dependent metalloprotease, specifically cleaves SNAP-25, thereby blocking vesicular release [109].

NRF2 is a transcription factor that regulates the expression of antioxidant genes. Under physiological conditions, NRF2 binds to KEAP1 and is targeted for ubiquitin-mediated degradation. Upon oxidative stress, cysteine residues of KEAP1 are modified, leading to the release of NRF2, which then translocates into the nucleus and activates downstream antioxidant genes (e.g., NQO1, GSTA2, HMOX1). In the cerebrospinal fluid of TN patients, oxidative stress markers (4-HNE, MDA, and protein carbonylation) are significantly elevated, and reactive oxygen species (ROS) can directly activate the TRPA1 pain channel. Exemestane, by inhibiting KEAP1, stabilizes NRF2 and activates antioxidant genes, thereby exerting anti-TN effects [89]. The remaining agents listed in Table 7 are at earlier stages of preclinical or clinical evaluation; although their mechanisms or targets are not discussed in detail here, they represent emerging directions that warrant future investigation in TN.

Medications for acute exacerbation episodes

In addition to conventional TN pharmacotherapies, the primary goal of treatment during acute exacerbation episodes is rapid relief of severe pain (Table 8). Such medications are typically administered via intravenous, topical, or intranasal routes. Lidocaine, a sodium channel blocker, achieves rapid onset of action when administered intravenously, topically, or via nasal spray [113, 103]. Fosphenytoin, a prodrug of phenytoin, is converted to phenytoin after intravenous administration to exert sodium channel-blocking effects [114]. Phenytoin, administered orally or intravenously, blocks sodium channels and enhances GABAergic inhibition [115, 104]. Sumatriptan, a 5-HT1B/1D receptor agonist, can be administered subcutaneously or via nasal spray [116, 117]. Magnesium, an NMDA receptor antagonist, is given intravenously to inhibit central sensitization [118]. Ropivacaine, an amide-type local anesthetic, reversibly blocks sodium channels in nerve fibers upon local injection, thereby inhibiting action potential generation and propagation [119].

Conclusions and future perspectives

In summary, TN is a severe form of facial neuropathic pain. Although its clinical management is primarily based on sodium channel blockers such as carbamazepine and oxcarbazepine, the long-term side effects and diminishing efficacy of these drugs have driven continuous exploration of new therapeutic targets and intervention strategies. Based on the understanding of the molecular mechanisms of TN [120], this review further systematically summarizes the core research advances in the field of TN. In terms of animal models, the evolution from the classical chronic constriction injury model to more clinically relevant models such as the foramen lacerum impingement model, the intradural trigeminal nerve compression model, and genetically engineered models has provided powerful tools for dissecting the pathological mechanisms underlying different etiological subtypes. Regarding neural circuits, research has expanded from the simple “trigeminal ganglion-spinal trigeminal nucleus-thalamus-cortex” pathway to a complex network involving multi‑brain‑region interactions and positive‑feedback amplification, incorporating areas such as the parabrachial nucleus, periaqueductal gray, anterior cingulate cortex, and insular cortex, revealing a deep coupling between pain signals and emotion/cognition. At the molecular target level, in addition to voltage‑gated sodium, calcium, and potassium channels, the discovery of emerging targets-including TRP channels, chemokines and their receptors, the MAPK signaling pathway, and epigenetic regulators-has greatly enriched our understanding of the molecular pathogenesis of TN. In drug development, although selective channel inhibitors such as Nav1.7 blockers face challenges in clinical translation, novel intervention strategies targeting CGRP, NRF2, the SNARE complex, among others, are showing promise.

Nevertheless, despite these significant advances, current research still faces several key challenges, and future breakthroughs can be anticipated from multiple perspectives. These include in‑depth dissection of the spatiotemporal dynamics of multi-region circuit dysregulation, a shift from “single-target” to “multi-target” modulation strategies, acceleration of the translation of novel drugs from preclinical studies to clinical practice, and the continuous development animal models with greater clinical translational value. Regarding the first three aspects, considerable progress has already been made. With respect to animal models of higher translational value, a significant gap remains between preclinical findings and human TN. For instance, most existing models primarily produce evoked pain behaviors, whereas human TN presents as spontaneous, paroxysmal electric‑shock‑like pain that can be triggered by innocuous stimuli or occur without an obvious trigger [34, 21]. Even more clinically relevant compression models cannot fully recapitulate the exact location of intradural neurovascular compression or the long-term paroxysmal nature of TN [21]. Furthermore, traditional preclinical assessments rely on reflex‑based tests measuring thresholds to evoked stimuli rather than spontaneous pain, which dominates the clinical presentation of TN [121]. Over‑reliance on such endpoints partly explains why many analgesic candidates that show efficacy in animal models fail in clinical trials [15]. Species differences further widen the translational gap: human dorsal root ganglion neurons exhibit extensive co‑expression of CGRP and P2 × 3, whereas these populations are largely segregated in rodents [122]. To bridge this gap, future studies should incorporate multi‑model validation, measures of spontaneous pain, and human‑based novel approach methodologies [123]. Integrating these complementary platforms is expected to improve the predictive validity of preclinical research and accelerate the translation of novel analgesics into clinical practice for TN patients.
