Wonder, Awe, and Imagination in Nursing Professional Practice Education
Authors: Darlaine Jantzen, Michael Morelli, Angela C Wolff
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTEducation for nursing entered universities based on the assumption that the complexity of nursing practice required an education beyond apprenticeship‐style training focused mostly, if not wholly, on technical knowledge and skills. Although there were calls for academic education as early as 1932, this shift as an entry‐to‐practice did not happen in Canada until the early 2000s. In the intervening years, the argument for a liberal‐arts education defined the expectations for academic nursing leadership, the institutions that provide BSN education, and nurse educators. The value of both science and the humanities was clear. Over the past decades, economic and ideological positions have generated doubts about this clarity and re‐ignited resistance to a liberal arts and humanities education. These reservations have eroded the foundations for nurses' academic education. In contrast to the call for humanities‐informed nursing, academic nurse leaders are enticed to shift the focus from educating for human flourishing to producing market‐ready graduates. As part of a multi‐method study on educating nursing students for dispositions deemed important for safe, competent, and ethical nursing practice, we have engaged in philosophical enquiry. In this study, we are extending the work of Derek Sellman, who draws from Alasdair MacIntyre's After Virtue, to develop an account of what makes a good nurse. Here we compare the MacIntyrean approach with that of Kristján Kristjánsson, an educationalist whose work has informed research related to character education for the professions. For Kristjánsson, wonder, awe, and epiphanies are crucial parts of this approach, particularly in relation to enchanted human flourishing. In this article, we pose the following question and formulate a preliminary answer: Is the integration of Kristjánsson's view into nursing education complementary to Sellman and not only possible, but promising, for nursing in the twenty‐first century?

Nothing could be farther from most nurse educators' minds than wonder and awe. At least, that is how it seems. Nursing educators frequently remark about biomedical dominance, lack of time, content overload, pressure to produce research coupled with teaching, and limited clinical placements. Even in a rationalised context, to educate students for good nursing in all its facets, nurse educators and leaders cannot neglect the moral formation of nurses (Grace 2024; Milliken and Grace 2017). We examine two approaches to this educational endeavour, both of which draw on virtue ethics and a deep tradition of moral philosophy. We compare the work of Sellman (1996, 1997, 2009, 2011) and his Macintyre (2007) virtue ethics with Kristjánsson (2015, 2020). Following Kristjánsson (2020), we consider the possibility and promise of integrating a commitment to wonder, awe, and epiphanic experiences in pursuit of ‘extended, enchanted human flourishing’ (p. 91). We then suggest integrating Johnson's (1993) work, which is particularly relevant to moral imagination. Ultimately, this is an invitation for further exploration and discussion.

Educational Context

Education for nurses entered universities based on assumptions that the complexity of practice required an education beyond apprenticeship‐style training focused mostly, if not wholly, on technical knowledge and skills as well as compliance. Calls for a baccalaureate degree as entry‐to‐practice began as early as 1932 in Canada (Rovers and Banjok 1988), and although there was some university‐based nursing education in the 1920s, this shift did not occur until the early 2000s (Wolff et al. 2010). The mid‐20th century drive to establish nursing as a discipline, accompanied by nursing theory development, resulted in many programmes using specific nursing theories to structure their curriculum. In the UK, Project 2000 shifted approaches to nursing education, including ‘establishing ethics as a core component of the nursing curriculum’ (Sellman 1996, 44). The expectation for baccalaureate‐prepared nurses signalled a shift from traditional models emphasising technical abilities and compliance to contemporary views of the nurse as an autonomous, critical‐thinking professional (Wolff et al. 2010). During the transition, the argument for a liberal‐arts education shaped expectations for academic nursing leadership, institutions providing BSN education, and nurse educators. The value of both science and the humanities was clear. Few have articulated this as eloquently as Myra E Levine (Levine 1999), who, near the end of her career, drew attention to the fact that scientific and technical knowledge are important for learning nursing, but ‘imperfectly achieved without the intellectual skills’ of the humanities (p. 19). In addition to developing intellectual skills, we suggest with Rolfe (2014) that the humanities are essential for developing ‘empathic imagination’ (p. 1459), empathic engagement, compassion, courage, and commitment (p. 1460). With ‘her ultimate and persistent goal to educate good nurses’ (Stafford 1996, 5; italics added for emphasis), Levine noted that attempts to integrate nursing ethics, history, and philosophy into the curriculum were ‘meager and restrictive’ (Levine, p. 19). As nursing in Canada was finally moving toward BSN rather than diploma education, Levine (1999) recognised some of the barriers. Levine posits that nurses appear duty‐bound to acknowledge a limited vision of ‘practicality’ which hampers the introduction of the humanities into the curriculum. Being practical is commonly expressed as if there is no higher‐level cognition involved, and that practical considerations leave no room for intellectual ones, and vice versa. While we reject this dichotomy, we observe that instrumental matters hold power. Additionally, across the subsequent decades, because of economic and ideological positions, such as the technical rationalism (Kinsella 2007), neoliberalism (Myrick 2004), managerialism (Grace 2022), anti‐intellectualism (Racine and Vandenberg 2024), and standardisation (Slemon 2018) and the practices of placing higher premiums on science and technical skills over the humanities, the initial intentions of a liberal‐arts education with attention to both science and humanities have been replaced1 (Grace and Perry 2013). This has resulted in the privileging of other intentions, including evidence‐informed practice, skills‐oriented learning, and critical thinking, and the devaluation of more philosophical, formative, and imaginative resources of the humanities. This is not to suggest that these more science‐driven approaches are not important for nursing education. However, we propose that they and the ideologies listed above that generate these foci risk the erasure of understanding self, relationships, and morality in ways offered by learning through the humanities and historical approaches to nurses' moral formation (M. D. Fowler 2021). Nursing students may learn the principles and frameworks for hypothetical decisions in moments of ethical dilemma, but nurse educators have shied away from engaging in the ‘who’ – the character, capacities, and comportment – of the nurse (Sellman 1996, 2011). While bioethics often offers principles, a humanities‐oriented education can provide the foundation to help nurses interpret those principles in the complex situations, specifically where right answers are not obvious.

Various educational philosophies have informed nursing curricula over the past century. Liberal adult education has a long history, and emphasises rational thought, ‘liberal learning, organised knowledge, and the development of the intellectual power of the mind’ (Elias and Merriam 1995, 13). Liberal arts education is aimed at the formation of citizens who are wise morally, aesthetically, and spiritually, as well as intellectually. However, we proceed with caution, given the critiques of decolonial perspectives and anti‐racist practices and the ways in which liberal education was shaped by problematic strains of early Christianity, Augustinianism, Jesuit education, and Eurocentric values rooted in Ancient Greek philosophy (Elias and Merriam 1995). Liberal arts or liberal education shaped the dream of professionalisation; however, behaviourism held dominance through institutional and educational resources, as arguably it still does (Handwerker 2012). Humanistic and emancipatory (radical) philosophies have had some influence, particularly through the caring curriculum that follows Watson's nursing theory (Bevis and Murray 1990; Bevis and Watson 1989; Tanner 1990, 2007). Within this mix of philosophies in nursing education, the influence of John Dewey and progressivism on educational approaches, such as constructivism, currently favoured for their student (consumer) centredness, cannot be ignored. Notably, although considered a cornerstone of the move to academic education for nurses, liberal arts education does not feature in contemporary nursing curriculum texts at all (e.g., Page‐Cutrara and Bradley 2020). Given the pressures mentioned above and the hegemonic influence of behaviourism in post‐secondary institutions, it is not entirely surprising that the value of both science and the humanities, and a liberal‐arts education for baccalaureate‐prepared nurses has virtually disappeared.

Over the past decade, nurse educators and leaders have been enticed to shift from the idea of liberal arts education and educating for human flourishing to producing market‐ready graduates in the Canadian setting (Wolff et al. 2010). We see evidence of this globally (Benner et al. 2010; Peterson et al. 2022). We will not debate the problems of creating a binary between market‐ready graduates versus nurses committed to human flourishing, exemplary ethical comportment, and relational practice (Doane and Varcoe 2021). However, we are concerned that educating nurses solely with the market in mind ignores critical questions posed by those who speak, write, and work within the humanities. For instance, what is ‘the market’? How should healthcare look within this market? And how can nurses be prepared to enter this market with the knowledge, skill, and dispositions needed not only to perform their work effectively, but to work in ways that foster real and lasting flourishing? Rolfe (2014a) also counters arguments that the ‘profession of nursing should end its flirtation with higher education and return to a clinically based apprenticeship method of teaching’ (p. 1459). He proposes nurses need more, not less, education in the humanities to learn to care about, and ‘imagine and identify with the suffering of others to the extent that they are motivated to respond to that suffering’ (p. 1459). Yet, given the persistent suspicion that integrating the humanities into nursing education is little more than a romantic ideal, proposals like Rolfe's are unlikely to gain purchase.

Moral Development

BSN students arrive in our programmes already shaped by familial, cultural, educational, and sociopolitical experiences. Their moral orientations may harmonise with—or in some cases conflict with—the professional values of nursing. The making of a good nurse, to borrow Sellman's (2011) phrasing, is a long process. Since Aristotle, Confucius, and others, people have theorised how this process of moral development can be explained, with an eye toward achieving the valued ends: a good person who contributes to the well‐being of those around them. Following World War II, in an effort to make sense of the horrors that came to light, moral philosophers and psychologists made new efforts to explain moral development. One such theorist, Kohlberg, dominated Western understandings for decades, despite his theory being challenged early on (e.g., Gilligan) and despite his own misgivings (Kristjánsson 2015). In his stage‐bound theory, individuals traverse sequentially across each stage, culminating, for some, in the advanced stage of ‘post‐conventional autonomous Kantian moral reasoning’ (Kristjánsson 2020, 118). Kohlberg himself observed that his theory did not predict actual behaviour particularly well. Developmentalists such as Kohlberg theorise that students' understandings of right, wrong, and what to do for the good of others are strongly dependent on cognitive developmental processes consistent with a rationalist paradigm (Kristjánsson 2015) and what Johnson (1993) describes as, and critiques, a normative dimension of reason.

How, then, do our understandings of moral development based on developmental theory intersect with character, nursing education, and ideas of wonder and awe? Kristjánsson (2015, 2020) contrasts his reconstructed Aristotelian approach to moral development directly with Kohlberg's. Sellman does not address early moral development explicitly however, by drawing on MacIntyre and an Aristotelian‐influenced approach to virtue ethics, he similarly diverges from a developmentalist framework(see also Johnson 1993). In brief, Kohlberg's developmentalist view stands in contrast with both Aristotelian character education (Kristjánsson 2015) and also to growing understandings of humans as dependent, embodied knowers situated within connected, relational communities (Johnson 2014; see also Narvaez 2014).

Kohlberg and other developmentalists have also been challenged by scholars in the field of spiritual development, a field that addresses wonder, awe, and epiphanies through a spirituality lens. Scholars who have focused on spiritual development, including Edward Robinson (1977), David Hay, Rebecca Nye and others, suggest that, contrary to the developmental approach, religious and spiritual experience is not controllable, predictable, or linear (Hay and Nye 1998). Robert Coles (1986, 1997), writing in the 80s and 90s, also studied children's moral and spiritual development and concluded, similarly to Robinson, Hay and Nye, that children describe a sense of connectedness to self, others, and/or a higher power or to nature, quite distinct from their psychosocial or developmental stage (Kristjánsson 2020). The hegemony of understanding the moral development of children, youth, and potential nursing students from a disembodied, autonomous moral reasoning perspective needs to be continually challenged – particularly if nursing education is to take the role of character formation seriously as a central educational aim.

Character Education

In the past two decades, there has been increased attention to character education, particularly in the United States, the United Kingdom, and Europe (Kristjánsson 2015). This renewed focus reflects broader concerns about moral formation that extend beyond cognitive accounts of moral reasoning to include questions of character, dispositions, and the kind of persons professionals are becoming. Yet, despite this growing interest, it remains difficult to find particular and robust explorations of the philosophical underpinnings of character education as they relate specifically to nursing education and practice. Although not all scholars or educators would endorse Aristotelian or MacIntyrean assumptions regarding the intrinsic, irreducible moral nature of character, and although nursing professional bodies may not explicitly articulate such commitments, in nursing, we nonetheless act as if this is the case. This assumption is evident across nursing recruitment, education, and regulation. The public expects nurses to do good rather than harm. Recipients of care trust nurses to be honest about medication administration, competent in treatments, respectful in their language, anti‐racist, non‐discriminatory, attentive to the dignity of those in care, and so on. These expectations reflect an expectation that nurses are not merely skilled technicians or autonomous moral reasoners, but rather they are embodied, relational practitioners whose character is revealed in everyday actions, relationships, and responses to vulnerability. Such expectations align more closely with virtue‐based and relational understandings of moral formation than with stage‐based developmental models that prioritise abstract reasoning. Therefore, exploring the philosophical underpinning of character education, particularly as they apply to a professional practice such as nursing, is crucial. These underpinning philosophies become the architectonic philosophy (or philosophies) that inform both the aims of nursing education and the pedagogical strategies employed to meet those aims.

Character education, like liberal arts education, is often considered to follow from Aristotle in some way. There are significant variations within this broad characterisation, including moral, psychological, political, health, and religious character education, creating a semantic minefield (Kristjánsson 2015). Despite the heterogeneity, educators and parents are demonstrating a re‐emerging interest in ‘the flourishing child’ (Kristjánsson 2015, 11). Kristjánsson (2020), whose work we will explore in depth later, examines various approaches to education broadly – not solely on moral education – and foregrounds attention to flourishing, and the importance of ‘awe and epiphanic experiences’ in education (p. 31). The quality and focus of nursing students' prior moral education, whether formal or informal, will vary. This is an important reminder that nurse educators will always encounter a diverse range of learners.

In this invitation to further exploration, we begin by summarising the philosophical perspectives that frame two cases for professional (nursing) practice education that can facilitate the development of dispositions for nursing as a moral practice. Our choice of the word dispositions, rather than virtues, attitudes, or character traits, is consistent with Sellman (2011)2, Johnson (2014, p. 32), and the educationists Billett and Henderson (2011). Billett and Henderson (2011) discuss three aspects of professional education, namely, ‘Conceptual, procedural, and dispositional capacities that comprise the canonical knowledge of the occupation’ (p. 1). In our view, this taxonomy is particularly useful for distinguishing it from commonly used terms such as ‘ways of knowing’ or defaulting character formation to affective learning. Dispositional capacities are not relegated to emotions but comprise some of the nursing canon. While our preferred term for those capacities is dispositions, in this paper, we use the language of the authors cited. Additional taxonomy challenges abound. With Groenhout (2014), we critique the Standard Taxonomy of ethics education, noting that not all moral theorising that is not deontological or utilitarian is then considered a virtue ethics. Not all character education is rooted in Aristotelian philosophy (e.g., ethics of care). In what follows, we explore two approaches to teaching ethics that are, to differing degrees, categorised as both virtue ethics and Aristotelian. This is not to suggest, however, that other approaches are not worthy of deeper consideration. We begin with Sellman (2011) and Macintyre (2007) and provide some details about a project we are undertaking. We then present Kristjánsson (2015, 2020) (reconstructed) Neo‐Aristotelian view, which has informed another study, similar to our own, and has been informed by Kristjánsson (Peterson et al. 2022). We draw a key distinction between these philosophical perspectives in how they approach human flourishing and the place of awe and epiphanies. We offer some preliminary observations regarding the implications for nurse educators. And with that in view, we aim for what follows to be received as an invitation for dialogue.

We are aware of the critiques of the notion of virtues and the related virtue script (Nelson 2006; Holland 2012). On the one hand, we are sympathetic to these critiques and to the need for redefining nursing. If the definitions, circumstances, and/or systems promote historically colonising and gendered notions of nursing, they ought to be redefined. For example, Bradshaw (2017) revisits the historical foundations of nursing, considering the Francis Report, advocating for a return to the Nightingale model—a virtue‐based, practice‐driven approach to nurse education. This model, rooted in morally grounded professionals and standardised care, is presented as a remedy to modern uncertainties about nursing identity. Because of the impossibility of embracing Nightingale, with her decidedly colonising values (Power et al. 2025), we eschew Bradshaw's advice. On the other hand, we are wary of dismissing an emphasis on character, virtue, and practices of care for nursing altogether. If a hyperfocus on technical knowledge and skills eclipses serious consideration of the person providing care, including what could be described as their character and dispositions, nursing will continue to be overcome by market demands for ever‐greater efficiency. Nurses will not receive the preparation and ongoing resources needed not only to meet those demands but to thrive in such contexts, and in so doing, recast nursing as something that can extend beyond the efficiency demands of a gruelling neoliberal market. For this reason, we looked to Sellman, MacIntyre, and Kristjánsson for philosophical grounding for moral formation.

What Makes a Good Nurse

Historical analyses demonstrate that nursing ethics was relational, virtue‐based, and rooted in the profession's long‐standing moral commitments (M. Fowler 2024). Across publications from 1996 to 2011, Sellman developed a case for attending to specific professional virtues (2011,. 21) as a response to the nurse's role in encouraging human flourishing for recipients of care who are, by the nature of their need for healthcare, vulnerable in a more‐than‐ordinary manner (Sellman 2009). Drawing heavily on Alasdair MacIntyre's After Virtue (2007) and integrating Aristotelian concepts (e.g. phronesis), Sellman provides a basis for exploring the value of virtues in informing ethical nursing practice, codes of ethics, and most pointedly, moral formation for nurses. Throughout his work, Sellman refers to the character of the nurse and requisite character qualities as virtues (Sellman 2011), including protective virtues and professional virtues; with professional phronesis as a ‘centrally important virtue’ (p. 19). He calls for nurse educators to take the cultivation of virtue, not merely the teaching about virtue or the contrasting of ethical theories, very seriously (Sellman 1997, 2011). For Sellman, there is an important distinction between acting ethically and being ethical. Acting ethically amounts to doing what is perceived as right in order to conform to certain social, cultural, and/or institutional norms, and then being rewarded for that conformity. Being ethical is concerned with discerning what is right to do, even when it may not conform to social, cultural, and/or institutional norms. For Sellman, then, human flourishing, for both the patient and the nurse, even in challenging circumstances, is the primary aim of nurses' moral practice (see also Peterson et al. 2022). Within institutions with an austerity mindset, where efficiency over compassion is normalised, nurses need to be able to envision – imagine – that human flourishing is possible.

From the 1990s forward, Sellman (1996) amongst others (e.g., Gastmans 2002; Bradshaw 2017; Rolfe 2014b, 2015) observed that ethics as a core component of the curriculum would provide nurses with language to justify their decisions in practice, and vocabulary to identify uncomfortable feelings about issues in practice; develop critical and analytical thinking; strengthen self‐awareness (e.g. of values), become reflective practitioners, and to recognise that the whole of healthcare is morally fraught (Sellman 1996). Sellman draws on Macintyre (2007), who proposes a virtue ethics that is derived from cultural context through community narratives and the extant moral tradition (Jantzen 2026). Sellman (1997) makes an important point that may help clarify the Aristotelian roots of current character education in nursing. Difficulty arises when failing to recognise distinctions between virtue/virtuousness, virtue character/intellect, and the modern division of other and self. As abstract as these philosophical categories may seem, their outworking in nursing contexts is quite concrete and practical, with important implications for the flourishing of patients, nurses, and those connected to both. Here, one Aristotelian idea becomes helpful when construing virtues as both other‐ and self‐directed. Yet, in current parlance, different virtues tend to be limited to one or the other, and are often focused on the flourishing of either self or other/s. For example, justice tends to be other‐directed, whereas courage tends to be self‐directed. But must it be so? Sellman offers a compelling answer. We agree with Sellman that one's own flourishing is, or at least ought to be, ‘equated with the good life for all’ (Sellman 1997, 4).

Sellman, drawing on MacIntyre, focuses on the professionally wise practitioner. Here, practices, habit formation, and virtue cultivation come to the fore—as in, nursing is a practice that inherently cultivates virtues and goods (e.g., human flourishing) when practised in particular ways, with moral exemplars as guides in these practices. From this perspective, nursing is not merely a set of tasks but a sustained moral practice in which habit formation, role modelling, and virtue cultivation are indispensable for fostering the profession's internal goods, including human flourishing. Sellman highlights how moral exemplars within practice communities guide novices into these ways of being by embodying the virtues to which learners aspire (Sellman 2009, 86–87). One example is the cultivation of self‐awareness, which Sellman identifies as foundational to becoming a phronimos, a wise practitioner capable of discerning what is right, done in the right way, at the right moment, and for the right reasons. This emphasis on situated learning resonates with Benner et al. (2010) calls for a transformation of US nursing education grounded in coaching, community, and experiential formation.

Importantly, Sellman (2009) also argues for cultivating a shift from a reliance on altruism, often an internal motivator for students entering nursing, toward a more robust and enduring moral orientation rooted in goodwill, in a ‘strong sense’ (p. 86). This shift is necessary because altruism, while admirable, can be too idealistic, and therefore elusive, as the ideal crumbles when confronted with the less‐than‐ideal realities of the healthcare environment. For example, Price et al. (2013) reported that many Millennial nurses enter the profession with strong altruistic ideals, only to find that workplace realities undermine these initial expectations. Likewise, Rognstad et al. (2004) reveal a duality in students' motivations—balancing altruism with a desire for patient acknowledgement—suggesting that professional identity is shaped simultaneously by internal virtues and external forms of validation. Furthermore, altruism and the related notion of ‘I want to help people’ can produce unequal and problematic power dynamics wherein the caregiver becomes too paternalistic. Ultimately, this can lead to resentment and unethical behaviour when idealised expectations are unmet. In contrast, goodwill offers a stable, other‐oriented, enduring ethos that incorporates the best of altruism. Together, these insights underscore that cultivating professional wisdom in nursing requires intentional formation within communities of practice that nurture virtues capable of enduring the moral complexities of contemporary healthcare.

Over the past two decades, since engaging in academic nursing education, we have been troubled by the same situation Sellman (2011) aptly describes. ‘[N]ursing is an inherently moral practice, and this places moral obligations on individual nurses to cultivate the sorts of dispositions necessary to ensure nursing actions enable rather than diminish human flourishing’ (2011, p. 17). Yet, nurse educators are unable to cultivate these dispositions – at least overtly or easily – in the current context. We have taught nursing in diverse settings and have observed that evaluating a student's character is fraught and primarily limited to egregious, unethical, unprofessional, or illegal activity. Hoyer et al. (1991), over 30 years ago, addressed one of the most serious breaches of ethical conduct—clinical cheating—emphasising the need for moral development to be integrated throughout nursing education, as fostering professional integrity requires a proactive approach to ethics education that goes beyond rule enforcement to cultivate moral reasoning and character. This underscores the importance of aligning educational strategies with both the personal values students bring and the professional virtues they must develop. A comprehensive approach that includes value clarification, ethical reflection, and character formation is essential for preparing nurses who are not only clinically competent but also morally grounded. Falling back on professional standards alone fails to provide enough warrant to identify a lack of open‐mindedness, unless it is explicitly connected to racism. Highly relativistic societal values, strongly critiqued by Macintyre (2007) in his case against emotivism and by Johnson (2014) in his case against Moral Law theories grounded in cognitive science, are directly at play here.

Nursing Character Education

For nursing character education, students' moral formation and development as a good nurse is a multifaceted process that integrates virtue ethics and the cultivation of moral character (e.g., justice, honesty, and courage), with the goal of promoting human flourishing (Begley 2006; van Hooft 1990). This contrasts with teaching about moral theories. The evolving identity of nursing students and the cultivation of virtue and character within nursing education are central themes across various scholarly works. However, connecting virtue ethics or character explicitly with nursing education is a relatively under‐researched area. Crigger and Godfrey (2014) emphasise that virtue ethics plays a foundational role in nursing education by fostering the internalisation of professional values and moral character. Their approach to teaching professional identity formation encourages students to cultivate virtues such as integrity, compassion, and responsibility from within, rather than merely adhering to external codes of conduct. This inward‐outward development aligns with the goals of virtue ethics, which prioritise character and moral disposition as essential to ethical nursing practice. Brennan and Timmins (2012) explore the shift in student identities from compliant, dependent thinkers in traditional, hospital‐based programmes to more autonomous, critical‐thinking professionals in university settings. Such a transition can leave students underprepared for the moral complexities of practice. Underscoring character formation as a dynamic process, Aydin Er et al. (2017) observed that students' perceptions of a ‘good nurse’ evolve, with senior students valuing empathy, honesty, and responsibility more than first‐year students (see also, Peterson et al. 2022). Zic (2025) argues that upholding virtue ethics in nursing education is essential for fostering a morally grounded and intellectually rigorous learning environment. Encouraging students to engage with questions about virtue and the good life helps them to see nursing not just as a technical profession but as a moral profession committed to human flourishing (Gonzalez‐Sanz 2020). Russell (2014) argues for embedding virtue ethics into nursing curricula to address widespread incivility in the profession. She proposes that teaching civility through a virtue ethics framework can foster respectful, collegial relationships and healthier work environments. Furthermore, while clinical skills are essential, nursing must also nurture virtue formation through reflective and humanistic education (Lim 2013). Complementing this, Vanlaere and Gastmans (2007) highlight the importance of cultivating moral character in nursing students through critical companionship and reflective practice, aligning closely with the principles of virtue ethics. They argue that ethical competence in nursing is not solely about rule‐following but about developing the capacity for moral sensitivity and judgement through lived experiences and reflection. These findings highlight the importance of sustained moral formation and role modelling in shaping good nursing professionals. Together, these limited studies advocate for a virtue ethics approach to nursing education – one that balances technical competence with character development – to ensure that students emerge not only as clinically competent but as morally grounded professionals. All this being the case, there remain lukewarm, and in some cases oppositional, responses to these proposals for and accounts of character education for nursing.

The challenge of educating for moral practice, specifically related to character education in nursing, informed our research, which was funded by a university‐based small grant. Because human flourishing featured prominently in the institution's strategic plan, applicants were encouraged to demonstrate how their work engaged this concept. We familiarised ourselves with ideas of relevant concepts, specifically eudemonia, professional wisdom, phronesis, and professional virtues, and grounded our understanding in Sellman (2011) exploration of education for ethical competence, and Macintyre (2007) account of ethics. With the grant, we began a study of how BSN students learn select dispositions for good nursing and human flourishing. The study involves a metanarrative review and data collection across three diverse post‐secondary settings, one of which is a liberal arts university. Our literature review led us to Kristjánsson (2015, 2020) when reading about a similar project (Peterson et al. 2022). In what follows, we outline some of the unique features of Kristjánsson's reconstructed Aristotelian approach, with his unique contributions regarding wonder, awe, and epiphanic experiences.

Kristjánsson's Neo‐Aristotelian

Like Sellman, Kristján Kristjánsson has written extensively over several years, developing his approach to moral education as a reconstruction of an Aristotelian ethics, and informing the work of the Jubilee Centre for Character and Virtues (2020) and Peterson et al. (2022) examination of ethics and the good nurse. Two key Aristotelian ideas are clearly articulated by Peterson et al. (2022): virtues are contextually fitting traits developed over time through habituation and deliberation that consistently guide individuals; and, secondly, phronesis is a meta‐virtue that guides both moral and intellectual virtues. This conceptualisation aligns well with Sellman's interpretation of MacIntyre. Kristjánsson (2015) observes that,

There remains something quintessentially Aristotelian about seeing moral development as a process of learning to exhibit virtues in action … considering proper emotional regulation an intrinsic part of the good life … [and] considering the creation of certain socio‐political mechanisms a necessary precondition for the sustenance of moral education (p. 11).

Educating for human flourishing is more than character development, and it cannot be relegated to a course or discrete subjects. For Kristjánsson, human flourishing is more culture and context‐dependent. He views Aristotle as too analytical, if not constricted, and therefore Kristjánsson seeks to reconstruct Aristotle's thinking with an ‘extended and enchanted’ theory for human flourishing. In other words, if the Aristotelian gaze looks intently at the world, Kristjánsson's Neo‐Aristotelian, extended, and enchanted view calls on educators to role model gazing at the world with awe. And while both gazes are of import for human flourishing, it is the gaze of awe that can yield self‐transcendence.

Kristjánsson, in contrast to MacIntyre and Sellman, translates his Neo‐Aristotelian view into pedagogical method: advocating for Socratic dialogue, in the manner that Socrates claimed – as ‘sophisticated midwifery’ (2015, p. 120); constructive classroom dialogue (contrasting with ‘barren conversation’ in classroom spaces – p. 128); ‘character friendships’ and role‐modelling (p. 124); and ‘educative use of the arts’ (p. 127). Kristjánsson's work has been integrated into a series of research projects aimed at prospective professionals, students in professional practice programmes, and educators led by James Arthur (Jubilee Centre for Character and Virtues 2020; Peterson et al. 2022). Arthur, with colleagues, launched a multi‐method study of nurses in the UK called Virtuous Practice in Nursing, examining the extent to which first‐year and final‐year students and 5‐year graduate nurses relied on codes, and how these codes intersected with their beliefs regarding professional virtues (Peterson et al. 2022). Although strongly connected to Kristjánsson's work, a significant component of the data collection involved examining ethical dilemmas, in which participants were asked to ‘explain their reasoning and judgement’, albeit ‘virtue‐based reasoning’ (p. 53).

Nurse educators have paid little attention to what Sellman (Jantzen 2026), Kristjánsson and Peterson et al. (2022) have to offer. In fact, to our knowledge, no attention has been given to it in Canadian nursing education. We find this perplexing because Canadian nursing education rightly emphasises decolonising nursing education and anti‐racist work, which requires particular attention to a way of being that arguably is connected to character, dispositions, and virtue. Yet, this language rarely, if ever, surfaces in that discourse. While aware of reservations regarding an Aristotelian nursing ethics (Newham 2014; Holland 2012) and take related reservations seriously, as have Kristjánsson and MacIntyre, we think it is possible and promising to address the opportunities and challenges related to Aristotelian thinking; to make use of certain themes in Aristotle's treatment of character, virtues, habits, and practices without taking Aristotle's work wholesale and uncritically. In fact, Macintyre (2007) proposes ‘a living tradition [is] a historically extended, socially embodied argument, and an argument precisely about the goods which constitute that tradition’ (p.222). If this is so, then virtue ethics can be a living tradition that welcomes and meaningfully incorporates the critiques and constructive resources offered by feminist (Groenhout 2014), decolonial and anti‐racist thinking and practice, as well as other historical and contemporary perspectives and practices. To sum up, if our aim is to cultivate moral formation, then ‘the teaching of ethics is a form of moral education and suggests a whole different pedagogy that transcends our usual understandings of the role of the teacher both in higher education and in practice settings’ (Sellman 2009, 90, italics added). We acknowledge that most who have examined moral formation for nursing connect their philosophy and pedagogy with virtue ethics and notions of human flourishing, variously defined. If we accept the obligation of cultivating character and a ‘good’ (i.e., moral) nurse through nursing education, we want to ask the following question: how might 21st‐century and future nursing education achieve this end? While some argue character development and virtue education do not belong in post‐secondary settings, there are scholars – including Kristján Kristjánsson and his colleagues at the Jubilee Project — who observe that universities do make claims, even promises, about the kind of person their institution will graduate; and, that to fulfil our obligations to society and the public good, we most certainly need to be concerned with more than intellectual development and the acquisition of knowledge and skills (Jubilee Centre for Character and Virtues 2020). Educationists often connect character development with a sense of wonder and awe because they tend to focus on teaching children and youth (Kristjánsson 2020). But what, if any, is the place of wonder, awe, and imagination in the education of maturing and mature adults? And, if moral development is connected to awe and wonder, how does all of this intersect with learning to be a good nurse in formal nursing education?

Wonder, Awe, and Epiphanies

One precondition for human flourishing, by all accounts, is a sense of meaning and purpose (Kristjánsson 2015; Peterson et al. 2022). For elementary and high school teachers, this may be a tall order. But it seems to us that for nurse educators, this is low‐hanging fruit. Beyond romantic notions of caring for others, nursing, whether it involves the individual, the community, or the planet, is important for recipients of care. There is inherent meaning and purpose in nursing, but these only emerge with clarity when ethical practices are introduced to nursing students in both their academic and practice settings. However, Kristjánsson (2020) extended, enchanted human flourishing as an aim of education goes further, arguing for a sense of self‐transcendence. This is a key distinction between Sellman, MacIntyre and Kristjánsson. This is a ‘more capacious goal’ than mere character education (Kristjánsson 2020, 96). While nurse educators are able to facilitate a new sense of meaning and purpose within nursing by presenting the aims and goals of the discipline (Grace and Perry 2013), we consider the possibility that nurturing wonder, awe and imagination in their educational programme could be complementary to the making of a good nurse that Sellman proposes. This is human flourishing and moral formation in a new set of clothes.

Wonder has long been considered the origin, the very essence, and even the aim of philosophy (McGilchrist, 2009). In a highly regarded book, McGilchrist (2009) draws on neurobiology, philosophy, anthropology, and more to outline the implications of the divided human brain, and to argue for the primary role of the right hemisphere for, amongst many other functions, empathy, imagination, vigilant sustained attention, and living well with others. He writes,

Philosophy shares the trajectory that I have described as typical of the relationship between the hemisphere. It begins in wonder, intuition, ambiguity, puzzlements and uncertainty; it progresses through being unpacked, inspected from all angles and wrestled into linearity by the left hemisphere; but its endpoint is to see that the very business of language and linearity must themselves be transcended, and once more left behind. The progression is familiar; from the right hemisphere, to the left hemisphere, to the right hemisphere again.

(McGilchrist, p. 178)

From ancient philosophy to modern, advanced neuroscience, wonder has perennially appeared as a starting point for being and flourishing in the world.

Kristjánsson (2020) goes ‘beyond traditional wisdoms by arguing that the pedagogy of education for flourishing requires the facilitation of awe and epiphanic experiences’ (p. 31). Presenting the various relevant literature, or Kristjánsson's understanding of wonder, awe, and epiphanies, is far beyond the confines of this paper. In brief, wonder can be conceptualised as an intellectual virtue, directed toward objects, and a curiosity about understanding and contemplating the workings of the object; to have enduring intellectual interest in the other (Kristjánsson 2020, 96, drawing on Tobia's conceptual analysis of wonder). Wonder, a disposition that combines curiosity, openness, self‐awareness, and intellectual humility when encountering people, circumstances, contexts, and/or things, not only creates space for effective and good nursing but it also creates space for a good life, or human flourishing.

Awe, then, is considered a virtuous emotion, directed toward both the object and its value existentially, and with self‐reflexivity. ‘Awe prompts us to self‐consciously reflect upon ourselves, for example, by re‐evaluating our status in the universe’ (Kristjánsson 2020, 97), and prompts existential questions, potentially triggering deep personal transformation. We are reminded of Cameron (2006) exploration of the ineffable in nursing practice, here. Facilitating educational experiences for awe is the antidote to both the disenchanted human flourishing of Aristotle and the post‐enlightenment disenchantment thoroughly examined by Charles Taylor (Kristjánsson 2015, 2020). Taylor places blame on scientism, mechanism, and instrumentalism (Kristjánsson 2015, 2020), problems we began with in our analysis of contemporary nursing education. Awe, a sibling of wonder, teaches people (including nurses) to catch and savour moments that make life worth living, and make nursing a practice one commits to with perseverance and courage—even when it is extremely difficult.

Imagination and specifically moral imagination (Johnson 1993) is essential for moral deliberation (Johnson 1993)3, moral development (Narvaez 2014), and imitation – for McGilchrist (2009) the ‘ultimately most important human skill’ (p. 248). While we argued elsewhere for the importance of moral imagination and for nursing to engage more attentively with Johnson's work (Jantzen et al. 2023), imagination is also central to the process of learning from the professional phronimos (Peterson et al. 2022; Sellman 2011). Learning from role models involves imitation. ‘Imitation is imaginatively entering into the world of the one that is imitated … not to copy slavishly’ (McGilchrist, p. 247). Imitation is ‘founded on empathy and grounded in the body … a marker of empathy’ (McGilchrist, p. 248). McGilchrist, all the while connecting imitation, imagination, and empathy to the right hemisphere, concludes that imitation is ‘not mechanical reproduction but an imaginative inhabiting of the other, which is always different because of its intersubjective betweenness’ (p. 249). To nurture wonder, awe, and imagination for all the reasons outlined above, nurse educators have the enormous challenge of engaging the right hemisphere in a milieu dominated by left hemisphere perspectives and imperatives.

Briefly, we return to the less dominant theories of spiritual development, including Hay and Nye (1998) and Robinson (1977). Academic educators continue to prepare students for licensing exams by teaching developmentalist approaches, including Kohlberg. Often, this is content‐heavy preparation for caring for children and families. Teaching for awe might entail presenting the extensive critiques of developmentalist approaches and raising awareness of counter‐proposals for both moral and spiritual formation. Central authors in character education and moral philosophy have provided ample evidence to support this important shift. By returning to Hay and Nye, Kristjánsson (2020) argues that helping children, youth, and we add nursing students to become aware of their awareness and of something beyond themselves, without relying on religion or spirituality, falls squarely within the remit of any good character education, or education towards flourishing. Nursing students often undergo social and cognitive changes, especially young adults. Therefore, nurse educators are well‐positioned to raise their gaze, to see and vicariously experience something beyond the moment, and importantly, beyond themselves; essential, as it happens, for good nursing.

In addition to inspiring wonder and awe, Kristjánsson (2020) argues that a philosophy for character education should also ‘allow for moral elevation and radical gestalt‐switches (epiphanic psycho‐moral conversions)’ (p. 2). Clearly, the Enlightenment created the conditions for us to see this as both foreign and wrong‐headed. That is to say, there is a deep‐rooted wariness within many of us towards anything or anyone that hints at irrationality and/or emotionalism. Epiphanies are, generally, abrupt, swift, emotionally laden, reconfigurations of mental structures toward self‐change in the direction of moral improvement or reform, and flourishing (Kristjánsson 2025). The extent to which educators plan for such experiences remains unclear (Jonas 2025; Kristjánsson 2025). While this remains minimally explored in nursing (Mitchell and Pereira‐Edwards 2022; Trueman 2017; see Gallagher et al. 2017 for residential caregivers), transformative learning theory has been taken up fairly extensively (Ryan et al. 2022). We conclude this paper by moving this exploration of (some) philosophical foundations for nursing education in moral formation to raise practical questions about its implications. As we began, nothing could be further from most nurse educators' minds than wonder, awe, and, potentially, imagination, character education, and the facilitation of dispositions considered central to nursing as a moral practice. Kristjánsson (2015) offers four tests for a moral education philosophy (p. 147–148). Briefly, is it relevant and useful for the sociocultural context? Does it meet and address the sociopolitical context? Is it underpinned by respectable philosophical underpinnings? To this, we would add: is it underpinned by contemporary cognitive science (i.e., Johnson 2014)? Is it supported by dominant psychological theory? To these questions, we propose Sellman's offering of a MacIntyrean virtue ethics (Sellman 2011); together with Kristjánsson's extended, enchanted Neo‐Aristotelian attention to wonder and awe; and a deep understanding of the aretaic turn (Jantzen 2026), passes the test and therefore has purchase for future nursing education. To this, we add the integration of imagination as a pedagogy and a practice for promoting moral deliberation (Jantzen et al. 2023; Johnson 1993).

Conclusion

Cognitive science suggests that nurses need ‘imaginative rationality’ with creativity, discernment, and deliberative capacities (Johnson 1993, 187; see also Narvaez 2014, 107−125). McGilchrist (2009) argues, There are, it seems to me, four main pathways to the truth: science, reason, intuition, and imagination. Kristjánsson (2015), with his extended, enchanted view of human flourishing, agrees and adds that teaching for human flourishing requires learning to wonder and to cultivate awe, in ways that lead to epiphanies, transforming beliefs, values, and reasoning for moral decisions. Sellman (2011) contends that educating for professionally wise practitioners requires learning professional virtues, habituation, through the ‘role modelling’ of other phronimos in the context of a moral tradition. Taking all this together, we propose that nurse educators consider adopting integrated models of learning that combine the best of technical know‐how and evidence‐based practices with the philosophical, moral, and aesthetic resources offered by the humanities. We also contend that nursing students need more meaningful dialogue, not more ‘Kohlbergian dilemmas’ (Kristjánsson 2015, 128) with what Noddings (1994) calls the rough and tumble conversations of real people. And with Rolfe, we conclude that both science and philosophical exploration show that the making of a good nurse involves what has been historically associated with the humanities: spending time in the wonder‐generating, awe‐inspiring, epiphany‐sparking worlds of philosophy, art, literature, theatre, music, and learning to attend—all of which, as it happens, are crucial for the development of the kinds of imagination and dispositions integral not only to nursing practice, but also human flourishing. In conclusion, we propose further exploration and dialogue among nurse educators, academic and healthcare leaders, and practitioners, while resisting the impulse to disintegrate and demoralise what nursing, an inherently integrated practice, is and can be. We reject the either/or dichotomy between romantic idealism and cold rationality, and instead, urge people to consider, perhaps even embrace, a model for nursing education and practice that integrates the best that all disciplines and sources of knowledge have to offer.
