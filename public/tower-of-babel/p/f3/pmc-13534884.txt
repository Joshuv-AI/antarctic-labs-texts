Pediatric home‐based central venous access device dressings: Medical liquid adhesive versus standard care to reduce dressing disruption: The STICKY‐kids RCT protocol
Authors: Tricia M. Kleidon, Mari Takashima, Victoria Gibson, Robert S. Ware, R. N. Nicole Marsh, Amanda J Ullman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCentral venous access devices (CVADs) are frequently used in Hospital‐At‐Home models to facilitate prolonged intravenous treatment for children. Premature CVAD dressing disruption is common and contributes to device failure. This protocol describes a trial evaluating the adjunctive use of medical liquid adhesive at the dressing–skin interface to improve dressing adhesion and maintain dressing integrity.MethodsThis single‐site, two‐arm, parallel‐group, superiority randomized controlled trial undertaken in a children's Hospital‐at‐Home will evaluate whether adding medical liquid adhesive to standard CVAD dressings, compared with standard dressing alone, reduces dressing disruption. Children aged ≤18 years requiring a CVAD for ≥7 days will be randomized 1:1 to standard dressing plus medical liquid adhesive or standard dressing alone. Dressing integrity will be assessed daily, with dressing‐related outcomes assessed after 7 days or following dressing change (whichever occurs first). At each dressing change, participants are eligible for repeat participation, retaining their original randomization. The primary outcome is dressing disruption requiring premature replacement or reinforcement before the scheduled 7‐day change. Secondary dressing‐related outcomes include dressing dwell‐time, skin adverse events, CVAD complications, patient, caregiver, and clinician satisfaction. Secondary outcomes assessed once per participant are healthcare utilization and costs. Analyses will follow the intention‐to‐treat principle.DiscussionEnsuring the safety and durability of invasive devices beyond the in‐hospital setting is an increasing priority in pediatric care. This trial will provide rigorous data to inform the clinical‐ and cost‐effectiveness of the addition of medical liquid adhesive to standard dressings to improve CVAD dressing integrity, supporting care across the hospital‐to‐home continuum.

INTRODUCTION

Background

Central venous access devices (CVADs) play a central role in the delivery of a broad range of intravenous therapies, including parenteral nutrition, anti‐cancer medications, and prolonged treatment courses such as antibiotics. 1 , 2 , 3 While these devices are essential to modern pediatric hospital medicine, they carry well‐established risks, including catheter dislodgement, infection, and medical adhesive‐related skin injury (MARSI), many of which are precipitated by dressing disruption. 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 Premature dressing disruption compromises the sterile barrier and is a common contributor to unplanned dressing changes, device failure, which often results in treatment interruption, unplanned healthcare encounters, and increased resource utilization. 9 , 10 , 11 , 12

Beyond the acute in‐hospital setting, CVADs facilitate early transition to alternative care models, including Hospital‐At‐Home programs, which are increasingly used to deliver complex intravenous therapies. 13 , 14 , 15 For pediatric hospitalists, these models extend hospital‐level care into the home environment. Compared with in‐hospital care, Hospital‐At‐Home care involves greater patient mobility, variable environments, reduced continuous clinical monitoring, and increased caregiver responsibility, which may heighten the risk of CVAD dressing disruption and its consequences, including emergency visits, readmission, and family burden. 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 16 , 17 As Hospital‐At‐Home models expand to enable earlier discharge and reduce in‐hospital bed pressure, high‐quality evidence to guide strategies to improve CVAD dressing durability is needed to ensure complex intravenous therapies can be delivered safely. 17 , 18 , 19

Medical liquid adhesive (MLA) is a nonwater‐soluble gum mastic adhesive designed to enhance dressing adhesion and integrity. 20 In a randomized controlled trial (RCT) of 160 adults in an intensive care unit, MLA reduced initial central venous catheter (CVC) dressing failure from 50% to 28% (odds ratio: 0.39, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.20–0.76), prolonged time to first dressing failure (median 58.5 vs. 23.8 h), and reduced dressing changes (incidence rate ratio 0.74, 95% CI: 0.55–0.99), with infrequent MARSI (1.3%) and no catheter‐related bloodstream infections reported. 21

Supporting evidence from adult in‐hospital quality improvement studies demonstrated similar benefits in clinical practice. In an adult quality improvement study evaluating peripheral intravenous catheter (PIVC) dressing integrity, education and audit alone resulted in minimal improvement with the percentage of dressings remaining fully intact rising from 55% (baseline) to 57% (76/134). Adding MLA to the standard dressing protocol increased fully intact dressings to 83% (19/23) and 93% (26/28) when used with a new dressing and across sequential phases, with no increase in MARSI, suggesting the gains were primarily driven by the adhesive intervention rather than education alone. 22 Similarly, an adult CVAD‐focused quality improvement initiative reported a 34% improvement in dressing integrity and an increase in dressing dwell time from 2.7 to 6.5 days following MLA implementation, alongside a reduction in dressing‐related centrla line associated blood stream infection rates and no reported MARSI. 23

Despite widespread clinical use in adults, the safety and effectiveness of MLA remain unknown in pediatrics. Rigorous, practice‐relevant evidence is required to inform strategies that improve device safety across the hospital‐to‐home continuum while assessing downstream impacts on complications, healthcare utilization, and costs.

Objectives

The objective of the Supplemental Tackifier to Improve CVAD dressings in Kids Year‐round (STICkY Kids) trial is to determine whether, in children receiving in‐home care, the use of MLA in conjunction with standard dressing practices, compared with standard dressing practices alone, reduces dressing disruption and decreases CVAD complications.

METHODS

Study design

STICKY Kids is a two‐arm, parallel‐group, superiority RCT comparing novel MLA (plus Standard Care) with Standard Care alone. Participants may have multiple dressing changes (to a maximum of eight) while enrolled. Dressing‐related outcomes will be assessed at each dressing change, usually at 7 days or sooner if complications occur. Other outcomes will be recorded once per participant. Initial consent covers all potential dressing changes, and participants retain their original randomization. Population

Study setting

Recruitment commenced on June 1, 2025 and is expected to be completed by December 2027. The study is being conducted at Queensland Children's Hospital (QCH) within the Hospital‐At‐Home program, a pediatric quaternary‐referral home care service in Queensland, Australia. The Hospital‐At‐Home program supports early hospital discharge for children requiring ongoing intravenous therapy or entirely home‐based management. Clinical populations managed through Hospital‐At‐Home include acute and chronic medical conditions (e.g., cystic fibrosis, empyema), renal, liver, cardiac, general medical (including neonatal sepsis), gastroenterology (including long‐term parenteral nutrition), and oncology/hematology. Study procedures have been adapted to the home environment to support participation of children and their families, including integration with routine care visits, flexible assessment timing, and minimization of additional study‐specific procedures.

Eligibility criteria

Children are eligible for inclusion in the trial if they are 18 years of age or younger, are expected to require a CVAD for 7 days or more, are receiving intravenous therapy through the Hospital‐At‐Home program and have a vein size suitable for a catheter of 3fr or larger size. Children are excluded if they are receiving end‐of‐life care, have a known hypersensitivity to MLA, have significant skin compromise at the insertion site, or have previously participated in the trial using the same device. Children can be re‐recruited if they have a new CVAD during a different Hospital‐At‐Home admission. There was no defined washout period between interventions.

The inclusion of a broad pediatric age range (0–18 years) reflects the clinical population managed within Hospital‐at‐Home programs. While skin characteristics, mobility, and activity levels may vary across developmental stages and influence dressing integrity, these factors will be addressed analytically through stratification by age group during randomization and analysis.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and their caregivers, and Hospital‐At‐Home nursing staff were consulted during the development of the STICKY Kids trial to ensure that study procedures, outcomes, and data collection methods were feasible and acceptable in the home environment. Feedback from families informed the design of participant‐facing materials, the frequency and method of outcome assessments, and strategies to minimize burden during dressing changes. Families will continue to be engaged throughout the study via regular check‐ins. Study results in the form of a plain language summary will be shared with participants and caregivers who provide an email on the consent document.

Recruitment and consent

A dedicated Research Nurse (ReN) will liaise with the Hospital‐At‐Home co‐ordinator to identify eligible children. The ReN will provide the parents/guardians of eligible children with written information about the study, allow adequate time for them to review the information, and answer any questions prior to obtaining informed consent from a parent or legal guardian. Age‐appropriate information is provided to the child, and child assent is obtained where appropriate. The initial consent covers all potential dressing changes, and participants retain their original randomization. Recruitment feasibility is supported by historical Hospital‐in‐the‐Home admission volumes at Queensland Children's Hospital, with an anticipated enrolment rate of approximately six participants per month. At each study endpoint, the ReN will check in with participants and their caregivers to confirm continued willingness to participate, ensuring ongoing engagement and adherence to study procedures.

Interventions

Participants will be randomly allocated to receive either standard CVAD dressing care alone (Tegaderm™ 1685, or Tegaderm™ 1688 [dependent on patient size]; Solventum) or MLA (Mastisol®; Ferndale) plus standard dressing care.

Participants allocated to the control care group will receive standard CVAD dressing practices as per institutional policy, typically comprising a transparent semipermeable dressing with adjunctive securement consisting of a subcutaneous anchor securement system (SecurAcath®; Interrad Medical, Inc.), or a sutureless securement device (Statlock™). Additional standard products were used according to institutional practice recommendations and international guidelines. Skin barrier preparations (Cavilon™; Solventum) were applied as indicated, and tissue adhesive (SecurePort IV®) was applied at the insertion site only to seal the cutaneous entry point in accordance with institutional practice recommendations practice, 25 and international guidelines. 3

Participants allocated to the intervention group will receive CVAD dressing and securement in accordance with standard institutional policy, with the adjunctive application of MLA. Following routine skin antisepsis, MLA will be applied immediately prior to dressing placement using the manufacturer‐supplied applicator as a single‐layer border under the anticipated perimeter of the bordered transparent semipermeable dressing, in accordance with manufacturer instructions and institutional guidelines. The adhesive will be allowed to become tacky before dressing application to optimize adhesion. MLA will be reapplied at each scheduled dressing change during the study period unless contraindicated by skin integrity concerns.

Permitted and prohibited concomitant cares

Except for the study intervention, all CVAD insertion, maintenance, assessment, and removal practices—including skin antisepsis, dressing changes, securement, flushing, and complication management—will be standardized per local and international guidelines. 3 , 25 No restrictions will be placed on routine standard care, such as selecting an alternative skin antiseptic in the presence of sensitivity or allergy or temporarily removing or adjusting dressings to facilitate imaging or other necessary interventions without compromising patient care.

Strategies to improve adherence to the intervention

Standardized education will be provided to research and clinical staff before study commencement to ensure consistent intervention delivery. Where feasible, the ReN or Nurse Practitioner will oversee the initial MLA application, provide ongoing education and point‐of‐care support, and update Hospital‐At‐Home handover sheets. Coloured stickers will indicate the participant's intervention arm on the CVAD dressing. Missed MLA applications will be recorded as protocol deviations and reapplied at the next clinically appropriate dressing change.

Randomization and allocation concealment

Participants will be randomized 1:1 using a secure, central, web‐based randomization system implemented through REDCap (Research Electronic Data capture, Vanderbilt 27 ). The randomization schedule, with randomly varied block sizes of 4 and 6, was generated by an independent statistician. Randomization will be stratified by age group (0–2 years, ≥2–12 years, ≥12–18 years) and timing of intervention (initial dressing application at time of CVAD insertion vs. subsequent dressing at time of patient discharge to Hospital‐At‐Home). Allocation concealment is maintained by randomizing immediately before dressing application.

Masking

Masking of participants, caregivers, clinicians, and outcome assessors is not feasible due to the visible nature of the intervention. To minimize assessment bias, outcome evaluation will be guided by prespecified, standardized definitions and supported by photographic documentation. Statistical and economic analyses will be conducted by investigators masked to treatment allocation.

Outcomes

Primary outcome

The primary outcome is premature dressing disruption, defined as the requirement for any CVAD dressing to be replaced prematurely and/or re‐enforced due to disruption in dressing integrity (e.g., lifted/detached on any border edge or within the transparent portion of dressing; visibly soiled; presence of moisture, drainage, or blood) before scheduled dressing change at 7 days postapplication (or catheter removal if earlier). Each dressing application initiates a new 7‐day observation period; participants may contribute multiple outcomes. 3

The outcome is based on standardized, objective clinical criteria, supported by photographic documentation to enhance reliability, and is appropriate across pediatric age groups, including nonverbal children.

To ensure standardized and consistent outcome measurement, dressings will be photographed and stored in REDCap when lifting, requiring replacement/reinforcement, or associated with compromised skin. This enables primary outcome validation and skin injury monitoring. Agreement in outcome assessment will be evaluated by masked Hospital‐At‐Home nurses and vascular access specialist nurses, with a recommended sample size of 50 per COnsensus‐based Standards for the selection of health status Measurement Instruments (COSMIN) guidelines. 28

DATA COLLECTION AND MANAGEMENT

Apart from the adjunctive use of MLA in the intervention group, all CVAD insertion, management, and removal practices will follow local clinical guidelines with any variations documented but not considered protocol deviations. 25 Data will be collected by ReNs from the patient and medical records and directly input into a purpose built form in REDCap (Research Electronic Data capture, Vanderbilt 27 ), hosted by the University of Queensland on password protected devices. Before analysis, data will be checked for completeness and plausibility, and reasonable efforts will be made to resolve missing or inconsistent data from source records. All study data will be de‐identified (pseudonymized) using a unique study identifier. A secure screening log linking identifiers to study IDs will be maintained, and consent forms and logs will be stored in locked cabinets or password‐protected drives. Identifiable data are accessible only to site staff and project managers unless required by legislation or the Human Research Ethics Committee (HREC). Any data breaches will be reported to the HREC and the study sponsor.

Sample size

Sample size calculations were based on the binary primary outcome of premature CVAD dressing disruption (no disruption vs. any disruption requiring unplanned replacement or reinforcement before the scheduled 7‐day dressing change). Based on local clinical data and prior observational studies in pediatric Hospital‐At‐Home programs, we estimated that approximately 30% of children receiving standard CVAD dressing care would experience premature dressing disruption. We hypothesized that the addition of MLA will reduce the absolute risk of dressing disruption to 15%, corresponding to a clinically meaningful 15% absolute risk reduction. 23

Assuming a two‐sided α of 0.05% and 80% power, detection of this between‐group difference would require outcome data from 242 participants if each participant contributed a single observation. However, because dressing integrity is assessed repeatedly within individuals on multiple occasions, the effective sample size was adjusted to account for within‐participant correlation. A design effect of 1.075 was applied, assuming an intraclass correlation coefficient of 0.05 and a mean of 2.5 primary outcome observations per participant. After accounting for this design effect, a total of approximately 260 primary outcome observations are required. This corresponds to a target recruitment of 104 individual participants.

Statistical analysis

The primary estimand is defined at the dressing‐episode level, where each dressing application constitutes a discrete observation period with its own outcome assessment. Participants may contribute multiple episodes over the study period (up to a maximum of eight), and observations within the same participant are therefore not independent. Within‐participant correlation is accounted for using a participant‐level random intercept in mixed‐effects models.

All analyses will follow the intention‐to‐treat principle. Primary outcome data collected from randomization until CVAD removal, discharge from Hospital‐At‐Home, or completion of 8 weeks of follow‐up will contribute to the primary analysis. The primary outcome will be analyzed using mixed‐effects logistic regression, with treatment group included as the main effect and stratification factors (age group, and timing of intervention) included as covariates, and participant included as a random intercept to account for repeated assessments at each dressing change. Results will be presented as odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals. Model‐derived marginal predicted probabilities will also be reported for each treatment arm to allow estimation of the absolute risk difference on a clinically interpretable scale, consistent with the sample size assumptions.

A sensitivity analysis will be conducted in which dressing changes prompted by the presence of blood alone, or other reasons to change dressing early (e.g., ultrasound assessment) without accompanying dressing lifting, will be excluded from the primary outcome definition of premature dressing disruption. A per‐protocol analysis of the primary outcome will be conducted, with the exclusion of major protocol deviations such as events that could substantially affect the integrity of the intervention or the primary outcome, including enrolment of participants who do not meet eligibility criteria, failure to receive the assigned intervention at a dressing change, use of the intervention contrary to allocation, or incomplete or invalid assessment of the primary outcome.

Secondary outcomes will be analyzed using appropriate regression models based on outcome distribution, with random effects included where repeated measurements occur. The extent and patterns of missing data will be examined, and where missingness is substantial or potentially informative, sensitivity analyses using multiple imputation will be conducted to assess the robustness of findings to departures from the missing‐at‐random assumption. No interim analyses or stopping rules for efficacy, safety, or futility are planned.

Cost‐effectiveness analyses

A per‐person cost‐effectiveness analysis will be conducted from the hospital perspective, incorporating direct intervention costs, staff time, dressing changes, and CVAD‐related complications. Resource use will be collected for a subset of approximately 25 participants, including product counts, the number of staff per dressing change, and time required for setup, removal, and application of dressings.

A probabilistic decision‐analytic model will simulate clinical pathways for MLA plus standard care versus standard care alone, estimating incremental costs, cost offsets, incremental cost‐effectiveness ratios, and net monetary benefit over the expected CVAD dwell period (2024 AUD). Costs will include complications, dressing changes, reinsertions, treatment of complications, staff time, and equipment use. Total cost per patient will be analyzed using a generalized linear model with trial group as the predictor, adjusting for confounders, with a gamma family log‐link assumed for skewed cost data. Monte Carlo simulation will quantify uncertainty in the economic outcomes, informing confidence intervals for cost‐effectiveness and net monetary benefit.

The primary estimand targets children receiving Hospital‐At‐Home care with a CVAD expected to remain in situ for ≥7 days, estimating the between‐group difference in the probability of premature dressing disruption. Interim analyses

No interim analysis is planned/or will be conducted.

Monitoring

Risk assessment and clinical management

A risk‐based approach to governance, monitoring, and safety will follow National Health and Medical Research Council (NHMRC) guidelines, supported by standardized procedures, investigator training, and predefined data dictionaries to ensure consistency and data quality. The intervention carries low risk, primarily local skin reactions, which will be managed with individualized dressing plans by the Vascular Assessment and Management Service. The intervention does not introduce procedures beyond routine care; potential discomfort is limited to standard dressing application and removal. To minimize distress, clinicians will use age‐appropriate communication, involve caregivers, and apply gentle adhesive removal techniques. All skin reactions will be photographed, monitored, and documented, and any systemic adverse reactions will be treated clinically and reported as a serious adverse event (SAE).

Participant burden

There is no additional burden to participants, as the intervention is delivered during routine CVAD care, and no study‐specific visits are required. All study procedures are embedded within usual clinical care pathways to avoid disruption or additional appointments. Participation is not expected to impact the child's usual routines. Participants will not receive financial incentives; however, families will be acknowledged through the provision of a plain language summary of study findings.

Adverse events (AEs) and SAE

AEs and SAEs will be monitored per institutional, regulatory, and ethical requirements. Catheter‐ and skin‐related complications will be recorded as AEs without additional reporting. SAEs including systemic allergic reactions, death, life‐threatening events, or CVAD‐related unplanned hospitalization, will be reported to the PI and Medical Monitor within 48 h, and serious, unexpected, intervention‐related events will be reported to the HREC. All adverse events will be managed by the treating team, and an annual SAE summary will be submitted to the HREC.

Data Safety Monitoring Board (DSMB)

An independent DSMB, comprising a trialist, statistician, and vascular access specialist, will be established before study commencement. It will review safety data, oversee trial conduct per International Council for Harmonisation (ICH) Good Clinical Practice, monitor recruitment, and may recommend protocol changes or early trial cessation. The DSMB will convene after approximately 25% of participants complete the study and annually thereafter, with recommendations communicated to the PI and HREC.

Auditing

No independent auditing of trial conduct is planned beyond routine monitoring by the sponsor (University of Queensland [UQ]) and HRECs (Children's Health Queensland and UQ).

This study will be conducted in compliance with the current version of the protocol. Any change to the protocol document that affects the scientific intent, study design or patient safety is considered an amendment, and therefore, will be written and filed as an amendment to this protocol. All such amendments will be submitted to the HREC and local governance office at each site for approval.

Trial management and governance

The Trial Management Group (TMG) will oversee the day‐to‐day conduct of the trial and include the PI, statistician, trial coordinator/research nurse, and data manager. The TMG is responsible for operational oversight, ensuring safety processes are followed, and confirming that the Medical Monitor is informed of all SAEs.

Dissemination plan

Study findings will be disseminated locally via hospital seminars and departmental forums and presented at national and international scientific meetings. Results will be published in peer‐reviewed journals relevant to vascular access, pediatrics, and home‐based care, and reported in accordance with the CONSORT 2010 statement 36 and template for intervention description and replication checklist. 37 Investigators will leverage professional networks in infusion therapy and infection prevention for broader knowledge translation. Authorship will follow NHMRC guidelines, and study results will be shared with participants’ families where appropriate guidelines. 38

DISCUSSION

Dressing disruption is a common and consequential problem in home‐based care, contributing to catheter dislodgement, infection risk, unplanned healthcare encounters, and increased caregiver burden. 4 , 5 , 6 , 39 The STICKY Kids trial will provide rigorous randomized evidence evaluating whether the addition of Mastisol Liquid Adhesive to standard CVAD dressings reduces premature dressing disruption in children receiving care at home. By focusing on a clinically meaningful, patient‐centred primary outcome, the study addresses an important evidence gap relevant to everyday pediatric practice. Repeated outcome assessments will allow evaluation of dressing performance over time under real‐world home conditions.

In addition to dressing integrity, this trial will examine downstream effects on CVAD complications, skin safety, healthcare utilization, and costs. 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 These outcomes will enable a comprehensive assessment of the balance between potential benefits, harms, and value associated with routine MLA use. If MLA improves dressing durability without increasing skin injury, it may support adoption of a low‐cost, scalable strategy to reduce avoidable complications; if not, the trial will provide robust evidence to discourage unnecessary use and costs. The study uses standardized outcomes, prospective data collection, and mixed‐effects modelling for repeated measures, with predefined outcomes, photographic documentation, and masked analysis mitigating bias despite lack of blinding.

This trial has several limitations. First, blinding of participants, caregivers, and clinicians is not feasible due to the visible nature of the intervention, which may introduce performance and detection bias; however, the use of standardized outcome definitions, photographic documentation, and masked statistical analysis mitigates this risk. Second, the single‐site design within a well‐established Hospital‐At‐Home program may limit generalizability to settings with different models of care, resources, or patient populations. Third, although repeated dressing‐episode assessments enhance statistical efficiency and reflect real‐world practice, participants with longer CVAD dwell times may contribute more observations. Finally, although the intervention is low cost, the economic evaluation is based on a subset of participants and modelling assumptions, which may limit the precision of cost‐effectiveness estimates.

In summary, the STICKY Kids trial will generate high‐quality evidence to guide CVAD dressing strategies for children managed at home. By addressing device safety across the hospital‐to‐home continuum, the study aligns with the evolving scope of pediatric hospital medicine and aims to support safer, family‐centred care.
