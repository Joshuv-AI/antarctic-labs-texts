Surgical-site infections in infants undergoing surgery for congenital heart disease during their first year of life
Authors: WooJin Shin, Ami Patel, Luis Alba, Maria Messina, Cynthia Okerfelt, Amelia O'Connell, Erin Driscoll, John Babineau, Eva Cheung, Anita I. Sen, Angelica Vasquez, Diana Vargas, Nimrod Goldshtrom, Ganga Krishnamurthy, Churl-Su Kwon, Emile Bacha, Andrew Goldstone, Lisa Saiman

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesInfants undergoing cardiothoracic surgery for congenital heart disease (CHD) are at risk for surgical-site infections (SSIs). Two widely used surveillance systems, the Society of Thoracic Surgeons (STS) and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention's National Healthcare Safety Network (NHSN), use different surveillance definitions for SSIs. Thus, we compared SSIs meeting STS and NHSN case definitions; evaluated SSI trends; identified risk factors; determined outcomes; and described causative pathogens.MethodsFrom January 2020 to December 2023, we conducted a retrospective cohort study of infants undergoing cardiothoracic surgery during their first year of life at a single academically affiliated children's hospital in New York City. We calculated the number of SSIs identified using STS versus NHSN definitions; assessed demographic characteristics and pre-, intra-, and postoperative risk factors for SSIs using multivariable logistic regression; and assessed susceptibility to cefazolin perioperative prophylaxis.ResultsDuring the study period, 867 infants underwent 1126 procedures. Sixty-one (7.0%) infants experienced SSIs identified by STS surveillance, of which 25 (41.0%) SSIs did not meet NHSN surveillance definitions. SSI rates showed a nonsignificant downward trend during the study period. Independent risk factors for SSIs were cyanotic lesions, hospitalization >7 days before surgery and surgical duration ≥5 hours. Infants with SSIs had longer hospitalizations and greater all-cause, in-hospital mortality. Twenty-seven (44.3%) SSIs included pathogens resistant to cefazolin.ConclusionsYoung infants undergoing surgery for CHD are at risk for SSIs. National alignment of SSI surveillance definitions is needed to more accurately capture burden and inform targeted prevention in high-risk infants.

Central Message

The SSI rate for infants ≤365 days old undergoing surgery for congenital heart disease was 7.0%. Risk factors were cyanotic lesions, hospitalized >7 days before surgery, and surgery duration ≥5 hours.

Perspective

Overall, 41% of SSIs in young infants undergoing cardiothoracic surgery identified by Society of Thoracic Surgeons (STS) surveillance were not captured by CDC surveillance, which is used for public reporting. These discrepancies in national surveillance highlight the need to align strategies to improve inclusive reporting and better inform targeted prevention efforts in this high-risk population.

Infants undergoing surgery for congenital heart disease (CHD) are at an increased risk of surgical-site infections (SSIs) as a result of their relative immunologic immaturity, the inherent complexity of their cardiac lesions, and frequent need for prolonged hospitalization before and after surgery.1, 2, 3 Because of this heightened vulnerability, reliable surveillance for SSIs is critical to obtain accurate rates and to inform and monitor the impact of prevention efforts. However, surveillance remains fragmented in the United States because of the use of 2 different systems: the Society of Thoracic Surgeons (STS)4 and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention's (CDC) National Healthcare Safety Network (NHSN).5 These surveillance systems have somewhat-different SSI case definitions and include different surgical procedures. They are often used by different stakeholders with different reporting goals at the same hospital, which can create confusion, additional work, and potential mistrust.6

This study sought to increase our understanding of the recent epidemiology of SSIs in infants undergoing cardiothoracic surgery during their first year of life to inform future surveillance efforts. We chose to study this age group because our program performs 250 to 300 procedures annually in children younger than 1 year of age, and we demonstrated the greatest SSI rate in young infants.7 The objectives of the current study were to (1) compare the number of SSIs meeting STS surveillance definitions versus NHSN definitions; (2) evaluate trends in SSI rates and procedure volume from 2020 to 2023, which allowed assessment of the potential impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on SSIs; (3) identify pre-, intra-, and postoperative risk factors for SSIs; and (4) characterize the pathogens causing SSIs including their susceptibility to cefazolin perioperative prophylaxis.

Methods

Study Design, Setting, and Population

We conducted a retrospective cohort study of infants undergoing cardiothoracic surgery at our academically affiliated children's hospital from January 2020 to December 2023. Included infants underwent 1 or more cardiothoracic surgical procedures during their first year of life. This study was approved by the institutional review board of Columbia University Irving Medical Center on March 16, 2022 (AAAU0806, approved March 16, 2022), with a waiver of informed consent.

Case Definitions for SSIs

Infants with SSIs were identified by the Department of Infection Prevention and Control using review of culture reports from wound, tissue, body fluid, or blood and readmissions; the cardiothoracic care team; and/or “infectious complications” collected in the STS database. We used both STS4 and NHSN5 surveillance definitions to identify SSIs (Table E1). STS definitions include SSIs that occur within 30 days postoperatively or during the hospitalization for the procedure associated with the SSI. The NHSN definitions, used by the Department of Infection Prevention and Control for reporting, include superficial SSIs diagnosed within 30 days postoperatively and deep incisional or organ-space (mediastinitis) SSIs diagnosed within 90 days postoperatively. NHSN excludes SSIs that follow manipulation of the surgical site, eg, SSIs in infants who underwent a procedure for sternal closure after their initial surgery.

Included and Excluded Surgery Procedures

STS includes SSI surveillance after all cardiothoracic surgeries. NHSN restricts surveillance to procedures included in the 'NHSN CARD' operative procedure category and excludes surgical procedures performed on vessels, cardiac transplantation, pacemaker placement, and procedures performed outside the operating room (Table E1).

In this study, we excluded procedures unrelated to the repair of CHD, such as exploration of the mediastinum; thoracic procedures such as vascular rings, tracheal procedures, pulmonary artery sling repairs; patent ductus arteriosus closures; procedures related to ventricular assist devices or extracorporeal membrane oxygenation; pericardial procedures; pacemaker implantation; and procedures for delayed sternal closures.

Data Collection and Analysis

Data were obtained from the STS database, supplemented by electronic medical record review for missing data or data not collected by STS, eg, SSI pathogens. STS collects the fundamental diagnoses, defined as the most complex congenital cardiac condition requiring surgical intervention, and the primary procedures, defined as the most representative procedure for the underlying cardiac lesion. If an infant underwent more than 1 procedure, the SSI could be associated with a procedure other than the primary procedure. Thus, we defined the attributable surgical procedures as those temporally linked to SSIs, using dates for positive cultures or the cardiothoracic team's diagnoses of SSIs.

Potential risk factors were collected, including those associated with the primary procedures for infants without SSIs and for the primary and attributable procedures, when relevant, for infants with SSIs. Demographic characteristics (eg, age at the time of procedure, sex, race, ethnicity, small for gestational age8) and preoperative factors, such as cyanotic cardiac lesions with a preoperative oxygen saturation of ≤85% and timing of preoperative prophylaxis were collected from STS. Intraoperative factors (eg, duration of surgery, cardiopulmonary bypass time, and crossclamp time) and postoperative factors (eg, temperature upon intensive care unit [ICU] arrival and delayed sternal closure) were also collected from STS, as were the outcomes of duration of overall hospitalization, postoperative length of stay and all-cause, in-hospital mortality.

The pathogens causing SSIs were categorized as Gram-positive cocci, Gram-negative bacilli, or fungi (yeast and molds). Antimicrobial susceptibility to cefazolin perioperative prophylaxis was assessed using relevant breakpoints (minimal inhibitory concentration ≤2 μg/mL).9 Nonsusceptibility was defined as intermediate or resistant breakpoints or included pathogens never susceptible to cefazolin, eg, fungi. For polymicrobial SSIs, if 1 or more pathogens were non-susceptible to cefazolin, that SSI was categorized as caused by non-susceptible pathogens.

The proportion of SSIs meeting STS and NHSN definitions was determined. Bivariate analyses were performed for potential risk factors and outcomes associated with SSIs, using t tests or the Wilcoxon rank-sum test for continuous variables and χ2 or the Fisher exact tests for categorical variables. Variables achieving statistical significance (P < .05) in bivariate analysis were considered for inclusion in the multivariable logistic regression model to identify the independent effect of the risk factors. To avoid model instability, we excluded bivariate predictors tested with the Fisher exact tests and the Wilcoxon rank-sum test and excluded variables with a variance inflation factor >5 due to collinearity (eg, surgery length, cardiopulmonary bypass duration, and crossclamp time). Observations with unknown values were excluded from the analysis. Statistical analyses were performed using SAS Studio 3.81 (Enterprise Edition) and R, version 4.4.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

Results

From 2020 to 2023, 1126 included surgical procedures for CHD were performed in infants during their first year of life and 61 (7.0%) of 867 infants undergoing surgery were diagnosed with SSIs meeting STS criteria. Of these 61 SSIs, 25 (41.0%) did not meet NHSN criteria, often as a result of procedures not included in the 'NHSN CARD' procedures or SSIs after postoperative manipulation of the sternal wound, usually after delayed sternal closure.

Overall, the most common surgical procedures were septal defects (25.2%) and right heart lesions (20.2%) (Table E2). Procedures for left heart lesions were more common in those with SSIs than those without SSIs (24.7% vs 8.2%, respectively; P < .001), and procedures for septal defects were less common in those with SSIs than those without SSIs (8.2% vs 24.2%, respectively; P = .004).

Outcomes Associated With SSIs

Discussion

We identified discrepancies identifying SSIs using STS versus NHSN. All SSIs that met NHSN SSI surveillance definitions met STS surveillance definitions, but 41% of SSIs that met STS definitions did not meet NHSN definitions. Cardiothoracic teams generally use STS definitions for reporting and reputational recognition, whereas the NHSN definitions are used for national public health surveillance reporting by CDC. The use of 2 surveillance systems with different surveillance strategies consumes valuable resources and can impede engagement in multidisciplinary quality improvement efforts, as many clinically recognized SSIs captured by STS are excluded from NHSN reporting. This can foster confusion among multidisciplinary teams and potentially contribute to mistrust of NHSN data, especially when publicly reported metrics differ from institutional experience. Thus, our observations further support the benefits of aligning NHSN with STS surveillance,6 as alignment would more completely capture the breadth of pediatric cardiothoracic surgery and facilitate consistency and reliability for clinical practice, quality improvement, and epidemiological surveillance.

During the pandemic, the NHSN reported an increase in healthcare–associated infections, but early observations suggested a possible decrease in SSI rates largely attributable to the substantial reduction in surgical procedures.10,11 However, we noted stable surgical volumes, likely due to the urgent need for surgery in young infants with CHD as well as a downward, albeit nonsignificant, trend in SSI rates. Our findings are consistent with those of the CVD-COVID-UK Consortium, which demonstrated stable surgical volumes for infants with CHD without increased postprocedural complications or mortality.12 Furthermore, our participation in Solutions for Patient Safety during the study period improved adherence to quality improvement strategies likely contributing to decreased SSIs.13,14

Three clinically important risk factors for SSIs were identified by multivariable analysis. As others have shown, cyanotic lesions were associated with SSIs because chronic hypoxemia and tissue ischemia can impair local immune function and wound healing.15,16 We also found that >7 days of preoperative hospitalization was a risk factor for SSIs. Similarly, previous studies demonstrated that prolonged preoperative hospitalization increased the SSI risk likely because prolonged preoperative hospitalization is needed for clinical optimization before surgery due to increased existing severity of illness and complex comorbidities which are themselves SSI risk factors.17,18 Extended preoperative hospitalization also increases exposures to invasive procedures, antibiotics, and hospital-acquired flora, leading to colonization with resistant and/or more virulent organisms.18,19 As others have shown, longer duration of surgery was associated with an increased risk of SSIs.3,20 The reasons for this observation are likely multifactorial as prolonged surgery increases the risk of intraoperative skin and wound contamination, can cause oxidative stress activation and cortisol release thus impairing immune function, and can adversely impact tissue oxygenation and neutrophil bactericidal activity.3,20 However, although age ≤30 days at the time of surgery7 and temperature <36 °C at ICU arrival21 are biologically plausible risk factors for SSIs, our sample size of 61 SSIs may have limited the power of the multivariable analysis to detect smaller effect sizes.

The SSI rate in our cohort was 7.0% using STS case definitions in a population restricted to infants undergoing CHD-related surgical procedures within their first year of life. Previous pediatric studies have reported SSI rates ranging from 1.2% to 4.0%.1,7,22,23 Differences among rates likely reflect differences in the study populations and SSI case definitions used. For example, some studies included patients 0 to 18 years of age, some included both congenital and acquired heart disease, some excluded superficial SSIs, and most used NHSN case definitions.

The all-cause, in-hospital mortality rate in the current study was 13.1% which was similar to previously reported mortality estimates for SSIs following pediatric cardiac surgery which ranged from 7-20%.22,23 However, our findings suggest that increased duration of hospitalization and increased length of stay after surgery were related to complex postoperative courses rather than directly attributable to SSIs. As evidence, the median hospital day of death was similar in those with and without SSIs (129 and 94 days, respectively) as was the median number of days from surgical procedure to death (62 and 45 days, respectively).

The predominance of Gram-positive cocci, particularly methicillin-susceptible Staphylococcus aureus, causing SSIs is consistent with prior studies.1,7 However, a substantial proportion of SSIs were caused by less-common organisms, as 13.1% of SSIs were caused by Gram-negative bacilli, 8.2% were polymicrobial infections, and 4.9% were caused by fungi.1,24 Less-common pathogens and polymicrobial infections may reflect complex wound environments, the risk of contamination of the wound from delayed sternal closure, and the impact of prolonged hospitalization, including antibiotic exposures exerting selective pressure on microbial flora. The high prevalence (45.8%) of pathogens not susceptible to cefazolin warrants further investigations of risk factors and potential preventive strategies, including effective antimicrobial stewardship during hospitalization.

The study's limitations included that it was a single-center, observational cohort study, which limits causal inferences and generalizability to other programs with different case mixes, perioperative protocols, or surveillance practices. Missing data, inconsistent documentation, or variability in clinical recognition of SSIs are inherent challenges of retrospective data collection from medical records. Our identification of SSIs was based on the date of a positive culture, which may not reflect the onset of signs and symptoms consistent with infection.

In conclusion, our findings highlighted the need to harmonize the STS and NHSN SSI surveillance systems for children. We identified cyanotic cardiac lesion, longer hospitalization before surgery, and longer duration of surgery as independent SSI risk factors in infants undergoing cardiothoracic surgery during their first year of life. We also noted a wide array of less-common causative pathogens for SSIs. Our findings suggest the importance of future studies of factors associated with prolonged hospitalization before surgery, eg, nutritional status, previous infections, and/or antibiotic exposures. Future studies, including registry studies, could provide larger sample sizes and identify modifiable risk factors to inform more personalized prevention strategies in this vulnerable population.
