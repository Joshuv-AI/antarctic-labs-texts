Targeted inhibition of RGS19 alleviates renal fibrosis by restoring autophagy and modulating immune cell infiltration
Authors: Xinhao Niu, Yufeng Zhao, Long Li, Xiaoqing Xu, Cuidi Xu, Jiaheng Wu, Xiaohan Yu, Yanbo Chen, Ruiming Rong, Bin Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRenal fibrosis, a progressive pathological feature of chronic kidney disease (CKD), is driven by impaired autophagic processes and persistent immune activation. The molecular mechanisms that interconnect these pathways remain inadequately understood. This study investigates the role of regulator of G-protein signaling 19 (RGS19), a novel autophagy-associated gene, in the pathogenesis of renal fibrosis. By analyzing transcriptomic data from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) and applying machine learning algorithms, RGS19 was identified as a key fibrosis-related gene. In both in vitro and in vivo renal fibrosis models, we validated its functional role, focusing on autophagic flux and immune responses. We observed that RGS19 expression was elevated in fibrotic kidneys and correlated with increased CD8 + T cell infiltration. Knockdown of RGS19 using siRNA led to reduced p62 accumulation, suppressed rapamycin (p-mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR)) activity, and restored LC3B-II levels, reflecting enhanced autophagic flux. Additionally, the secretion of T cell chemoattractants, such as C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 9 (CXCL9) and C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 10 (CXCL10), was diminished. Notably, targeted delivery of RGS19 siRNA via RDYH58 nanoparticles effectively alleviated renal fibrosis in murine models by reducing collagen deposition and immune cell infiltration. These findings suggest that RGS19 plays a central role in linking autophagy dysfunction with immune activation in renal fibrosis and highlight its potential as a therapeutic target for CKD.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-026-04098-7.Keywords: Renal fibrosis, Autophagy, Immune activation, RGS19, Chronic kidney disease, Nanoparticle therapy

Introduction

The prevalence of Chronic Kidney Disease (CKD) is steadily increasing worldwide, and it is projected to become the fifth leading cause of death globally by 2040 [1]. Despite the varying etiologies, renal fibrosis, characterized by tubular atrophy, myofibroblast activation, and extracellular matrix (ECM) deposition, represents the final stage of CKD [2]. Although research has identified several pathological mechanisms underlying renal fibrosis, including cell death, oxidative stress, and inflammation [3–5], existing therapeutic strategies have not effectively halted disease progression. Therefore, there is an urgent need to explore novel molecular mechanisms and therapeutic targets to improve the prognosis of patients with renal fibrosis.

Autophagy is a cellular process that plays a crucial role in both physiological and pathological conditions of the kidney by degrading and recycling damaged cellular components [6]. The role of autophagy in renal fibrosis is multifaceted. It acts as a protective mechanism by modulating cellular responses to oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and programmed cell death, thereby reducing kidney damage and maintaining renal integrity [5, 7, 8]. Impaired or dysfunctional autophagy is associated with enhanced oxidative stress and mitochondrial damage, which exacerbates cell death and accelerates the progression of fibrosis [3]. Conversely, aberrant activation of autophagy may promote fibrosis. For example, research has shown that autophagy activates the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK)/extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK) pathway in renal tubular cells, leading to the transactivation of early growth response 1 (EGR1), which in turn upregulates the secretion of fibroblast growth factor 2 (FGF2) into the stroma. FGF2 then stimulates fibroblasts to promote fibrogenesis [9]. Therefore, the role of autophagy in renal fibrosis is complex, involving a dynamic balance between protective and pathological mechanisms.

In recent years, the immune microenvironment has emerged as a key focus in renal fibrosis research. Various immune cell types, including macrophages, neutrophils, and T lymphocytes such as CD8+ T cells and CD4+ T cells, are activated in injured renal tissues, suggesting that immune dysregulation may contribute to the development of CKD [8, 10, 11]. Studies have consistently observed increased infiltration of CD8+ T cells in unilateral renal ischemia-reperfusion (UIRI) mice, and the depletion of CD8+ T cells significantly improved the loss of peritubular capillaries and renal fibrosis [11]. Therefore, investigating the role of CD8+ T cells in renal fibrosis may provide novel insights for future therapeutic strategies.

Regulator of G protein Signaling 19 (RGS19) is a G-protein signaling regulator that plays a critical role in intracellular signal transduction, metabolic regulation, and inflammatory responses. Recently, RGS19 has attracted considerable attention as a potential autophagy regulator [12–14]. However, research on how RGS19 modulates the renal fibrosis microenvironment, immune cell infiltration, and disease progression remains limited. Nanomedicine holds great clinical potential due to its advantages, including prolonged drug half-life, targeted delivery, controlled release, and the ability to penetrate biological barriers (e.g., the Blood-Brain Barrier (BBB)) [15]. Studies have reported that polydopamine (PDA), a major component of the natural pigment melanin, is used to synthesize nanomaterials with excellent stability, biodegradability, biocompatibility, and photothermal conversion properties [15].

Based on the aforementioned background, this study aims to investigate the role of RGS19 in renal fibrosis and its mechanism in modulating disease progression through the regulation of autophagic flux and CD8+ T cell infiltration. By integrating bioinformatics analysis with in vivo and in vitro experiments, we will comprehensively explore the function of RGS19 in autophagy and immune regulation. Furthermore, we will develop a nanoparticle-based targeted delivery system, RDYH58 Nanoparticles Loaded with small interfering RNA (siRNA) Targeting RGS19 (RDYH58-NPs@si-RGS19), to evaluate its potential application in antifibrotic therapy. Our findings will not only uncover the critical role of RGS19 in renal fibrosis but also provide new strategies to improve the fibrotic microenvironment and slow disease progression. More importantly, the targeted intervention approach based on nanomedicine offers a novel direction for the precision treatment of renal fibrosis, potentially contributing to the development of more effective therapeutic strategies, reducing the incidence of end-stage renal disease, and improving patient outcomes.

Materials and methods

Data acquisition

In this study, we utilized the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) to obtain datasets related to renal fibrosis. GSE76882 was selected as the primary training dataset, while GSE12682 and GSE22459 were merged as the testing datasets. The GSE76882 dataset includes 99 samples with normal renal function post-transplantation and 135 samples exhibiting interstitial fibrosis and tubular atrophy. The GSE12682 dataset consists of 13 samples with normal renal function post-transplantation and 23 samples with interstitial fibrosis and tubular atrophy. The GSE22459 dataset contains 25 samples with normal renal function post-transplantation and 40 samples with interstitial fibrosis and tubular atrophy. After data integration, batch effects were removed using the removeBatchEffect function from the R package limma, resulting in a batch-normalized expression matrix.

Additionally, 232 autophagy-related genes were extracted from the Human Autophagy Database (https://www.tanpaku.org/autophagy/) to construct the autophagy gene list for subsequent analysis.

Selection of differentially expressed genes (DEGs)

DEGs were identified using the Limma method based on microarray data. The analysis was conducted using the “limma” package in the R environment. The selection criteria included an absolute log2 fold change (|log2(FC)|) > 1 and an adjusted p-value (FDR) or p-value < 0.05. This stringent threshold ensured that only the most significantly altered genes were included, ultimately identifying the key DEGs between the experimental and control groups.

Optimization of candidate gene selection using machine learning techniques

Candidate gene selection was optimized by applying the Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression using the glmnet package, with sample phenotype (interstitial fibrosis and tubular atrophy/fibrosis vs. normal renal function after transplantation) defined as the supervised learning label. The optimal penalty parameter (λ) was selected under 10-fold cross-validation to identify robust predictive features. The resulting candidates were further refined and ranked using SVM–recursive feature elimination (SVM-RFE) and Random Forest (RF) variable-importance scores.

Validation of candidate genes

The diagnostic performance of the candidate genes was assessed using Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) analysis. The Area Under the Curve (AUC) and its Confidence Interval (CI) were calculated using the R package pROC to ensure the reliability of the statistical results. The gene expression patterns in the training and testing groups were compared using boxplots to verify the consistency and significance of gene expression across different datasets.

Subgroup analysis based on candidate genes

Renal fibrosis samples were stratified into high- and low-expression groups according to candidate gene expression, and subgroup-specific differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using limma. Functional enrichment of DEGs was performed with org.Hs.eg.db and clusterProfiler for Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analyses, with KEGG pathway annotations retrieved via the KEGG Representational State Transfer application programming interface (REST API). Pathway-level functional shifts were further evaluated by Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA) using clusterProfiler::gseKEGG, with genes ranked as sign(log2FC) × −log10(p-values) and parameters set to minGSSize = 5, maxGSSize = 5000, pAdjustMethod = “Benjamini–Hochberg (BH)”, and FDR < 0.05. Gene sets were obtained from Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB) v7.5.1 (collections c2.cp.kegg and c5.go.bp). In addition, pathway activity scores were calculated by GSVA (GSVA v1.44.2; method = “gsva”, kcdf = “Gaussian” for microarray data, mx.diff = TRUE); enrichment scores were compared between groups using linear models or non-parametric tests with BH correction (FDR < 0.05).

Immune correlation analysis in subgroups

Immune infiltration was estimated using CIBERSORT with the official LM22 signature matrix; the number of permutations was set to 1,000. Quantile normalization was enabled for microarray data. Only samples with CIBERSORT output p < 0.05 were retained for downstream statistical analyses and visualization. Associations between candidate gene expression and the inferred proportions of the 22 immune cell types were evaluated using Spearman correlation, and multiple testing across the 22 × genes comparisons was adjusted using the BH method (FDR < 0.05). Pairwise correlations among immune cell types were visualized as a correlation matrix heatmap using corrplot to depict immune cell interaction patterns.

Animal model construction and grouping

All animal experiments were designed and reported in accordance with the ARRIVE 2.0 guidelines [16]. Male and female mice were included in equal numbers and randomly allocated to experimental and control groups using block randomization to minimize batch effects. All surgical procedures and data collection were performed blinded to group assignment. Experiments were conducted using 8–12-week-old C57BL/6J mice (Hunan SJA Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd., Changsha, China). The mice were housed in a controlled SPF-grade animal facility with 45%−50% humidity and a temperature range of 25–27 °C for 1 week, with a 12-h light/dark cycle, to acclimate to the experimental environment. Prior to the experiment, the mice were fasted for 12 h but had ad libitum access to food and water at other times. All animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Ethics Committee of Shanghai Ninth People’s Hospital and conducted in strict accordance to the standard of the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.

For the unilateral ureteral obstruction (UUO) injury model, mice were first anesthetized with 3% isoflurane (792632, Sigma Aldrich, USA). A midline abdominal incision was made to expose the left ureter, which was ligated twice using 5 − 0 silk sutures. Mice in the sham-operated group underwent the same surgical procedure, except that the ureter was not ligated. Mice were euthanized 10 days post-surgery. For the UIRI model, after anesthesia, a left abdominal incision was made, and the left renal pedicle was clamped for 45 min at 37 °C. Mice were euthanized 14 days after reperfusion. Kidney tissue was then collected for further analysis [5].

Groups: (1) Sham group, UUO group; (2) Sham group, UIRI group; (3) Sham + sh-NC group, Sham + sh-RGS19 group, UUO + sh-NC group, UUO + sh-RGS19 group: 30 min after UUO model establishment, mice were intravenously injected with 100 µL of sh-RGS19 or sh-NC lentiviral solution (1 × 109 U) [17]; (4) Sham + sh-NC group, Sham + sh-RGS19 group, UIRI + sh-NC group, UIRI + sh-RGS19 group: 30 min after UIRI model establishment, mice were intravenously injected with 100 µL of sh-RGS19 or sh-NC lentiviral solution (1 × 109 U); (5) UUO group (UUO+Phosphate-Buffered Saline (PBS)), UUO + si-RGS19 group, UUO + NPs@si-RGS19 group, UUO+RDYH58-NPs@si-RGS19 group: 30 min after UUO model establishment, mice received a single tail-vein injection of PBS, free/naked si-RGS19 (0.5 µg/g body weight, in PBS), or NPs@si-RGS19/RDYH58-NPs@si-RGS19 (5 mg/kg, formulated in PBS containing 0.5 µg/g body weight of si-RGS19), with a final injection volume of 150 µL per mouse. Mice were euthanized 10 days after UUO surgery, and kidney tissues were collected for subsequent analysis [4].

Histology and immunohistochemistry staining

Kidney tissue was fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and subsequently embedded in paraffin. The tissue samples were sectioned into 4 μm thick slices. Following the manufacturer’s protocol, the paraffin-embedded kidney tissue sections were stained with Picrosirius Red (ab150681, Abcam, UK) and Masson’s Trichrome Staining (G1006, Servicebio, Wuhan, China). The positive areas for Masson’s Trichrome (blue) and Sirius Red (red) staining were quantified using ImageJ.

Immunohistochemistry Staining: The paraffin sections were deparaffinized with xylene and rehydrated through a gradient series of ethanol (100%, 95%, and 75%, 3 min each). Antigen retrieval was performed by heating the sections in 0.01 M citrate buffer at 100 °C in a microwave for 10 min. Afterward, the sections were incubated at room temperature for 30 min with 3% hydrogen peroxide to block endogenous peroxidase activity. Goat serum blocking solution was applied for 20 min at room temperature, followed by the removal of excess liquid. The sections were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: anti-RGS19 (rabbit; 1:200, PA5-23210, Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), anti-p62 (rabbit; 1:200, ab91526, Abcam, UK), and anti-p-mTOR (rabbit; 1:200, ab109268, Abcam, UK). Afterward, the sections were incubated with Horseradish Peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (1:1000, ab6721, Abcam, UK) for 1 h at room temperature. The immunoreaction was visualized using 0.05% diaminobenzidine (DAB) (DA1010, Solarbio, Beijing, China) with 0.01% hydrogen peroxide. The sections were then counterstained with hematoxylin for 5 min and washed with distilled water. The sections were briefly washed in 1% hydrochloric acid ethanol for 4 s and subsequently blued in distilled water for 20 min. Images were captured using a Nikon ECLIPSE Ti microscope (Fukasawa, Japan) and processed with Nikon software. After image acquisition, sections were processed in Nikon software using identical settings across groups, including background subtraction and intensity adjustment. The positivity threshold was defined as the mean background signal of the control group plus 2 standard deviations, and quantitative analyses were performed blinded to group allocation.

Immunofluorescence staining

Kidney frozen Sect. (3 μm) were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature for 15 min. TKPTS cells cultured on glass slides were fixed with a 1:1 mixture of cold methanol and acetone at room temperature for 15 min. After fixation, the sections and cells were blocked with 10% donkey serum at room temperature for 60 min. Immunostaining was then performed using the following primary antibodies: anti-RGS19 (mouse; 1:100, sc-271810, Santa Cruz, USA), Lotus Tetragonolobus Lectin (LTL, FL-1321, VECTOR Laboratories, USA), anti-fibronectin (FN) (rabbit; 1:100, 15613-1-AP, Proteintech, China), anti-vimentin (rabbit; 1:100, ab92547, Abcam, UK), anti-Alpha-Smooth Muscle Actin (α-SMA) (rabbit; 1:100, 14395-1-AP, Proteintech, China), anti-E-cadherin (mouse; 1:100, ab231303, Abcam, UK), and anti-LC3B (rabbit; 1:100, ab192890, Abcam, UK).

After overnight incubation, the sections were washed in 0.01 M PBS and then incubated with secondary antibodies: goat anti-rabbit Cyanine 3 (Cy3)® (1:1000, ab6939, Abcam, UK), goat anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor® 488 (1:1000, ab150077, Abcam, UK), and goat anti-mouse Alexa Fluor® 647 (1:1000, ab150115, Abcam, UK). Nuclei were stained with 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) (C1006, Beyotime, Shanghai, China), and the slides were mounted with Vectashield antifade mounting medium (H-1900, VECTOR Laboratories, USA). Images were acquired using a fluorescence microscope (Confocal Laser Scanning Microscopy (CLSM); LSM 510 META, Carl Zeiss AG).

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining

Mouse organ tissues were stained using an H&E staining kit (C0105S, Beyotime, Shanghai, China). The tissues were first rinsed with saline and then fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 30–50 min. Following fixation, the tissues were washed, dehydrated, cleared, infiltrated with wax, embedded, and sectioned. The tissue sections were then mounted onto glass slides and dried in an incubator at 45 °C. After dewaxing with xylene, the sections were gradually rehydrated using a series of alcohol solutions (from high to low concentration), followed by a 2-min wash in distilled water. The sections were stained with hematoxylin for 5 min, washed with running water to remove excess stain, differentiated in 1% hydrochloric acid ethanol for 3 s, and then stained with eosin for approximately 2 min. Finally, the sections were dehydrated, cleared, and mounted. Tissue pathology was observed under an optical microscope.

Flow cytometry

Mice were euthanized, and kidney tissue was dissociated to generate a single-cell suspension using the Multi-Tissue Dissociation Kit 2 (130-110-203, Thermo Fisher Scientific, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The cell suspension was incubated with Fc Block (553141, BD Biosciences, USA) on ice for 15 min. The cells were then stained with Live/DEAD Fixable Violet Dead Cell Stain 780 (565388, BD Biosciences, USA) for 15 min on ice, followed by two washes. Next, the cells were incubated with the following antibodies: CD45-Brilliant Violet 510 (563891, BD Biosciences, USA), CD3-Brilliant Violet 421 (562600, BD Biosciences, USA), and CD8-PerCP-Cy™5.5 (551162, BD Biosciences, USA) for 30 min on ice. After two additional washes, the cells were fixed with BD Cytofix/Cytoperm™ (554714, BD Biosciences, USA) and stained with Ki-67-Alexa Fluor™ 488 (561165, BD Biosciences, USA). Data were acquired using a fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS)Aria II flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, USA) and analyzed with FlowJo 10.8 software.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Each group of mice kidneys (0.1 g) was weighed and homogenized in 900 µL of physiological saline. The tissue was then sonicated and centrifuged at 3,000 g for 15 min to obtain the tissue homogenate. The total protein concentration in the homogenate was measured using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (P0012S, Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Additionally, the cell culture supernatants from all groups were collected, and the levels of CXCL9 (ab203364, Abcam, UK) and CXCL10 (ab260067, Abcam, UK) in both the tissue homogenates and culture supernatants were measured according to the ELISA kit instructions. Absorbance was detected at 450 nm using a microplate reader (Bio-Tek, Winooski, VT, USA). Data analysis was performed using Origin 9.5 software.

Promoter reporter assay

The CXCL9 promoter region containing the putative RGS19-binding site(s) was amplified by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) and cloned into the SacI/XhoI sites of the pGL3-promoter vector (Promega). Cells were co-transfected with pCMV–lacZ and the indicated pGL3 constructs using FuGENE 6 (Roche Applied Science), with 2–5 µg total plasmid DNA per transfection. One hour after transfection, cells were exposed to adenoviral vectors at the indicated multiplicity of infection (MOI). Cells were lysed in Promega Reporter Lysis Buffer, luciferase activity was measured using a luminometer, and β-galactosidase activity was measured in parallel for normalization [18].

Cell culture and treatment

Mouse proximal tubular epithelial cells (TEC) (TKPTS; CRL-3361; RRID: CVCL_LP34) and mouse fibroblast cell line L929 (CCL-1; RRID: CVCL_0423) were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA). The identity of both cell lines was confirmed by STR profiling, and they were tested to be free of mycoplasma contamination prior to use. TKPTS cells were cultured in DMEM/F12 medium (11320033, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (A5256701, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (100 U/mL penicillin and 100 µg/mL streptomycin) in a 37 °C incubator with 5% CO₂ (BB15, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). L929 cells were cultured in MEM medium (11095098, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. The culture medium was replaced every 24 h, and cells were passaged every 72 h using 0.25% trypsin for cell dissociation.

To establish an in vitro fibrosis model, TKPTS and L929 cells were treated with 10 ng/mL TGF-β1 (HY-P70648, MCE, USA) for 24–48 h.

Autophagic flux detection

LC3 Dot Accumulation: TKPTS cells were transfected with the pCMV-GFP-LC3B plasmid (D2815, Beyotime, Shanghai, China). In the absence of autophagy, GFP-LC3B is dispersed throughout the cytoplasm. Upon autophagy induction, GFP-LC3B accumulates on the autophagosomal membrane, forming puncta (LC3B dots or punctae).

mRFP-GFP-LC3B Lentiviral Infection: Autophagic flux was monitored using the mRFP-GFP-LC3B lentivirus (HB-LP210, Hanbio Biotechnology, Shanghai, China), which takes advantage of the pH-sensitive properties of RFP and GFP. TKPTS cells were infected with the mRFP-GFP-LC3B fusion protein-expressing lentivirus, followed by treatment with TGF-β1, or co-treatment with sh-RGS19 or sh-RGS19 combined with 3-methyladenine (3-MA). After various treatments, the cells were observed using confocal microscopy. Autophagosomes were identified by yellow puncta (RFP and GFP signals), while autolysosomes were represented by red puncta (RFP signal).

Isolation, activation, and expansion of mouse CD8+ T cells

Mice were perfused with saline to remove leukocytes from the vasculature. The spleens were harvested and dissociated into a single-cell suspension by passing through a 40 μm mesh. CD8+ T cells were isolated using the Mouse CD8+ T Cell Isolation Kit (B90011, Selleck, Shanghai, China). Flow cytometry sorting was performed using anti-mouse CD8a-eFluor®450 (48–0081-82, Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) to assess the purity of the isolated CD8+ T cells. According to the manufacturer’s protocol, the single-cell suspension was incubated with magnetic beads or antibodies from the enrichment kit for 30 min, ensuring the binding of CD8+ T cells to the beads or antibodies. Unbound cells were removed, and the beads were washed with PBS. The beads were then removed from the magnetic field, yielding high-purity CD8+ T cells. The sorted cells were stimulated overnight with 2 µg/mL anti-mouse CD3 antibody (A27161, ABclonal, China), followed by stimulation with 2 µg/mL anti-mouse CD28 antibody (A20346, ABclonal, China) and rhIL-2 (100 U/mL; 212-12-5UG, PeproTech, USA) for 48 h. After stimulation, the cells were transferred to new culture dishes containing fresh DMEM medium supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% penicillin-streptomycin, 10 mM HEPES, 2 mM L-glutamine, 1 mM sodium pyruvate, MEM non-essential amino acids, and 55 µM β-mercaptoethanol. The culture medium, containing cytokines, was replenished every 2–3 days to maintain a cell density of at least 2 × 10⁶ cells/mL. On day 7, the cells were counted again and maintained under the same culture conditions until day 14.

Co-culture of TKPTS cells with CD8+ T cells and CD8+ T cell detection

TKPTS cells were seeded in a 96-well U-bottom plate at a density of 5 × 10³ cells per well. CD8+ T cells were isolated from the spleen of mice and labeled with 2 µM carboxyfluorescein succinimidyl ester (CFSE, 65–0850-84, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). After treatment, the TKPTS cells were washed and resuspended in 100 µL of DMEM/F-12 medium. Subsequently, 5 × 10⁴ CFSE-labeled CD8+ T cells were added to each well, co-cultured with TKPTS cells at a 1:1 ratio in 200 µL of medium. After a 3-day incubation, the percentage of CFSE-positive CD8+ T cells was analyzed by flow cytometry. Additionally, the levels of T-cell chemotactic factors in the culture medium were measured by ELISA, as previously described.

Fibroblast proliferation assay

Cell proliferation was assessed using the BeyoClick™ EdU-488 Imaging Kit (C0071S, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, L929 cells were seeded in 12-well plates and subjected to RGS19 knockdown treatment, followed by TGF-β1 (5 ng/mL) treatment for 24 h. After treatment, 0.5 mL of EdU was added, and the cells were incubated at 37 °C for 4 h. The cells were then washed three times with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, and permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100. Cell nuclei were stained with Hoechst 33,342. Additionally, cell proliferation was evaluated by flow cytometry or confocal microscopy imaging.

Lentiviral transfection and cell experimental grouping

The RGS19 shRNA lentiviral plasmid (sh-RGS19) was constructed using the lentiviral silencing vector pGLVU6/Puro (C06002, GenePharma, Shanghai, China). The sh-RGS19 plasmid and its control plasmid (sh-NC) were purchased from GenePharma (Shanghai, China). Lentiviruses, along with the packaging plasmids pCMV-dR8.91 (ZT1466, FENGHUISHENGWU, Changsha, Hunan, China) and pMD2.G (#12259, Addgene, USA), were co-transfected into 293 T cells (CRL-3216, ATCC, USA) using calcium phosphate at a 3:3:1 ratio. The 293 T cells were cultured in DMEM medium (11965084, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) containing 10% FBS and 1% antibiotics in a 37 °C incubator with 5% CO2. The knockdown sequences were as follows: sh-RGS19-1 (target sequence: CTCAGAGTCAGACTCATATAA), sh-RGS19-2 (target sequence: GCGACTTATCTATGAGGACTA), and sh-NC (CTCGCTTGGGCGAGAGTAA).

One day after transfection, the medium was replaced with a small amount of serum-free medium and cultured for an additional 24–30 h. The conditioned medium was collected, filtered through a 0.45 μm filter, and concentrated by ultracentrifugation at 1600 g for 2 h at 4 °C. The lentivirus was resuspended in a serum-free medium and titrated using the p24 ELISA kit (L00938, Jiangsu, Nanjing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. TGF-β1-treated TKPTS or L929 cells were cultured with lentivirus and 8 µg/mL puromycin (540411, Sigma-Aldrich, USA), then centrifuged at 850×g for 80 min at 32 °C, and cultured for an additional 9 h. Transduction was repeated for two consecutive days.

The experimental groups were as follows: (1) TKPTS cells: sh-NC group, sh-RGS19#1 group, sh-RGS19#2 group; (2) Ctrl group, TGF-β1 group (cells treated with 10 ng/mL TGF-β1 for 24 h), TGF-β1 + sh-NC group (cells treated with 10 ng/mL TGF-β1 for 24 h, followed by transfection with a control lentivirus for RGS19 knockdown), TGF-β1 + sh-RGS19 group (cells treated with 10 ng/mL TGF-β1 for 24 h, followed by transfection with an RGS19 knockdown lentivirus), TGF-β1 + sh-RGS19 + 3-MA group (cells treated with 10 ng/mL TGF-β1 for 24 h, transfected with the RGS19 knockdown lentivirus, and treated with 5 mM 3-MA for 24 h), TGF-β1 + NPs@si-RGS19 group (cells treated with 10 ng/mL TGF-β1 for 24 h, followed by treatment with NPs@si-RGS19 at a siRNA concentration of 200 nM for 48 h), and TGF-β1 + RDYH58-NPs@si-RGS19 group (cells treated with 10 ng/mL TGF-β1 for 24 h, followed by treatment with RDYH58-NPs@si-RGS19 at a siRNA concentration of 200 nM for 48 h). (3) L929 cells: Ctrl group, TGF-β1 group (cells treated with 5 ng/mL TGF-β1 for 24 h), TGF-β1 + sh-NC group (cells treated with 5 ng/mL TGF-β1 for 24 h, followed by transfection with a control lentivirus for RGS19 knockdown), and TGF-β1 + sh-RGS19 group (cells treated with 5 ng/mL TGF-β1 for 24 h, followed by transfection with an RGS19 knockdown lentivirus). After the treatment period, cells from each group were collected for subsequent analyses.

Preparation of PDA nanoparticles (NPs) modified with fibrotic renal homing peptide loaded with si-RGS19

To prepare the NPs, 800 µL of polyethyleneimine (PEI, 25 kDa, 1 mg/mL; 408727, Sigma Aldrich, USA) and 800 µL of Cy3-labeled si-RGS19 (20 µM) were incubated at room temperature for 30 min. The Cy3-labeled si-RGS19 was prepared using the Silencer™ siRNA Labeling Kit (M1632, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Next, a mixture consisting of 4 mL diethyl pyrocarbonate-treated water (750023, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), 1.5 mL ethanol, and 20 µL ammonium hydroxide (AX1308, Sigma Aldrich, USA) was stirred for 30 min. The siRNA/PEI mixture was then added to the ethanol-water solution containing ammonia. Subsequently, 40 mg of dopamine hydrochloride (H8502, Sigma Aldrich, USA) was added to the mixture. The solution initially became colorless and then darkened, indicating the continuous formation of PDA NPs. The reaction mixture was stirred overnight. Finally, the NPs (NPs@si-RGS19) were collected by centrifuging at 10,000 rpm for 10 min to remove unencapsulated siRNA/PEI.

The fibrotic renal homing peptide RDYH58 (RDYHPRDHTATWGGC) was synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China). RDYH58 was first reduced using a fixed TCEP disulfide bond-reducing gel (77712, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The reduced RDYH58 peptide was then dissolved in an appropriate amount of solution and mixed in a molar ratio of 2:1 with DSPE-PEG(2000)-maleimide (880126P, Sigma Aldrich, USA). The reaction was stirred gently at room temperature for 4 h to promote the specific reaction between maleimide and thiol groups. An excess of free cysteine (Cys; HY-Y0337, MCE, USA) was added to block the unreacted maleimide groups. The resulting DSPE-PEG(2000)-maleimide-peptide complex was obtained by centrifugation (16,220 rpm, 30 min, 25 °C) to remove unbound peptide and excess reagents. Subsequently, NPs@si-RGS19 NPs were conjugated with the RDYH58 peptide-modified DSPE-PEG(2000)-maleimide complex in a 1:2 weight ratio. The final NPs, RDYH58-NPs@si-RGS19, were collected using a 50 kDa molecular weight cut-off ultrafiltration device (UFC9050; Millipore, Merck, MA). RDYH58-NPs@si-RGS19 were then lyophilized overnight using a freeze dryer.

RGS19-targeting siRNA duplexes were designed using the siDirect web tool and synthesized by GenePharma (Shanghai, China). The 21-nt siRNA sequences were: sense, 5′-ACAGUAAGGACAUAUUUCCAA-3′; antisense, 5′-GGAAAUAUGUCCUUACUGUUU-3′. Endotoxin contamination was evaluated using the Thermo Scientific Pierce Rapid Gel Clot Endotoxin Assay Kit (A43879; Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) to confirm that samples were endotoxin-free.

Sterile processing of nanoparticles

RDYH58-NPs@si-RGS19 is a thermosensitive formulation containing the targeting peptide RDYH58, siRNA, and the fluorescent label Cy3. To avoid heat- or radiation-induced degradation, terminal sterilization was performed by sterile filtration. The suspension was gently mixed or briefly sonicated under aseptic conditions to minimize aggregation, and then passed through a 0.22-µm polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membrane filter. After filtration, particle size, ζ potential, siRNA encapsulation efficiency, and Cy3 fluorescence intensity were re-assessed to confirm preservation of physicochemical properties and bioactivity. All procedures were conducted in a biosafety cabinet to maintain sterility [19].

Characterization of RDYH58-NPs@si-RGS19

The size, particle size distribution, and ζ potential of RDYH58-NPs@si-RGS19 were measured at 25 °C using a Malvern Zetasizer (Nano ZS90, Malvern Instruments, UK). A 10 µL suspension of RDYH58-NPs@si-RGS19 was applied to a copper grid coated with Formvar/carbon support film (300 mesh) and incubated at room temperature for 3 min. The samples were negatively stained with 1% uranyl acetate for 15 s, air-dried, and blotted with filter paper. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images were then acquired using a TECNAI-10 microscope (Philips, The Netherlands) at an accelerating voltage of 120 kV.

After rotary evaporation, RDYH58-NPs@si-RGS19 samples were collected to assess the content of free siRNA. A 10 µL solution of RDYH58-NPs@si-RGS19 or NPs@si-RGS19 (both containing si-RGS19 at the same concentration) was mixed with either 117 µL of 1× TE buffer (A) or 2% Triton X-100 (B) and vortexed for 2 min. Free siRNA standards were serially diluted. NP samples and siRNA standards were mixed with an equal volume of 1:200 diluted RiboGreen reagent and incubated for 5 min. Fluorescence intensity was measured using a microplate reader (excitation/emission wavelengths: 480/520 nm). The encapsulation efficiency (EE%) of si-RGS19 in NPs was calculated as: EE% =(Fluorescence value of B - Fluorescence value of A)/Fluorescence value of B × 100%.

Release kinetics of si-RGS19

To evaluate the release kinetics, RDYH58-NPs@si-RGS19 or NPs@si-RGS19 were suspended in PBS or culture medium containing 10% FBS and dialyzed in Float-A-Lyzer dialysis bags (MWCO 300 kDa) at 37 °C under two different pH conditions (pH 7.4 and pH 5.0). At predetermined time points, a certain volume of PBS was removed from the dialysis bags and replaced with an equal volume of fresh PBS. The amount of siRNA released from the NPs was determined by constructing a standard curve of fluorescence intensity versus siRNA concentration. Fluorescence intensity was measured using a multimode microplate reader (excitation/emission wavelengths: 550/570 nm).

Stability of RDYH58-NPs@si-RGS19

The ribonuclease (RNase) stability of free si-RGS19 and RDYH58-NPs@si-RGS19 was assessed using agarose gel electrophoresis. Briefly, free si-RGS19 and RDYH58-NPs@si-RGS19 NPs were incubated with RNase ONE™ ribonuclease (10 µg/mL; M4261, Promega, USA) at 37 °C for 0, 15, 30, 60, 120, or 240 min. After incubation, the NPs were collected by centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 10 min, and the pellet was dissolved in chloroform. siRNA was then extracted using a 0.5 M NaCl solution containing 0.1% sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS). The extracted siRNA was analyzed by electrophoresis on a 3% agarose gel containing GelRed and visualized under UV light.

To evaluate the stability of RDYH58-NPs@si-RGS19 in serum, the NPs were incubated in PBS with 10% FBS at pH 7.4 or 6.8 at 37 °C for 48 h. At predetermined time points, equal aliquots of the NP solution were collected and analyzed for particle size using Dynamic Light Scattering (DLS).

In vitro hemolysis test and safety evaluation

Red blood cells isolated from mouse blood were used for hemolysis evaluation. One milliliter of mouse blood was diluted with 1.25 mL of 0.9% (w/v) NaCl solution to obtain whole blood samples. A 0.1 mL aliquot of this sample was added to solutions of RDYH58-NPs@si-RGS19 at various concentrations (25, 50, 100, 200 µg/mL). A 0.9% NaCl red blood cell suspension served as the negative control (NC), while a red blood cell suspension in an equal volume of distilled water served as the positive control (PC). The mixtures were incubated at 37 °C for 4 h, followed by centrifugation at 3000 g for 10 min. The optical density (OD) of the supernatant was measured at 540 nm using a microplate reader to quantify the hemoglobin released from lysed red blood cells. Hemolysis was calculated using the following formula: % hemolysis = (OD sample - OD NC) * 100/(OD PC - OD NC).

Cell Safety Evaluation: TKPTS cells were seeded at a density of 104 cells per well in 96-well plates. RDYH58-NPs@si-RGS19 at various concentrations (6.25, 12.5, 25, 50, 100, 200 µg/mL) were suspended in a complete culture medium and added to the cells. After 48 h of incubation, the culture medium was removed, and 10 µL of CCK-8 solution was added to each well. The cells were incubated for 1 h at 37 °C in a humidified incubator. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured using an Epoch microplate spectrophotometer to assess cell viability. Untreated cells were used as the control. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

Mouse safety assessment: Blood samples were collected from nude mice for hematology and serum biochemistry. Liver function was assessed by alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST); renal function by creatinine (CRE) and blood urea nitrogen (BUN); and cardiac injury by creatine kinase (CK) and myoglobin (Myo). Hematological toxicity was evaluated by measuring white blood cells (WBC), hemoglobin (HGB), and platelets (PLT) [20].

In vitro targeting of RDYH58-NPs@si-RGS19

TKPTS cells were seeded at a density of 2 × 105 cells per well in 12-well plates. After 24 h of treatment with either TGF-β1 or control (Ctrl), the medium was removed, and a fresh medium containing RDYH58-NPs@si-RGS19 or NPs@si-RGS19 was added to each well. The cells were incubated at room temperature for 30 min, followed by three washes with PBS. The cells were then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min and stained with DAPI to observe the cell nuclei. Fluorescent imaging was performed using an IX53 fluorescence microscope, with Cy3 and DAPI showing red and blue fluorescence, respectively.

Cellular uptake of RDYH58-NPs@si-RGS19

TKPTS cells were seeded in 12-well plates at 2 × 105 cells per well. After 24 h of treatment with TGF-β1 or control, the medium was removed and replaced with 1 mL fresh medium containing RDYH58-NPs@si-RGS19 supplemented with either 10% FBS or PBS (10% FBS omitted). Cells were incubated at room temperature for 30 min, washed three times with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, and counterstained with DAPI to visualize nuclei. Coverslips were imaged using an IX53 fluorescence microscope, with Cy3 and DAPI signals acquired in the red and blue channels, respectively [21].

In vivo biodistribution and biocompatibility of RDYH58-NPs@si-RGS19

C57BL/6 mice from the sham or UUO group were intravenously injected with RDYH58-NPs@si-RGS19 or NPs@si-RGS19 (0.5 mg/kg), with eight mice in each group. After 4 h and 24 h post-injection, the major organs, including the heart, spleen, lungs, kidneys (left and right), and liver, were collected from each group. Fluorescence imaging was performed using the IVIS-200 bioluminescence imaging system (Perkin Elmer, Waltham, MA, USA). To quantify the accumulation of si-RGS19 in tumors and organs, the fluorescence intensity of each tissue was measured using ImageJ.

Biocompatibility of RDYH58-NPs@si-RGS19 in vivo: C57BL/6 mice were administered a high dose of RDYH58-NPs@si-RGS19 (10 mg/kg). On day 14, major organs, including the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys, were harvested for H&E staining analysis.

Reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA from cells or tissues was extracted using Trizol reagent (15596026, Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific), and its concentration and purity were assessed using a Nanodrop2000 micro-UV spectrophotometer (1011U, Nanodrop, Thermo Fisher Scientific USA). RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA according to the protocol provided by the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit (RR047A, Takara, Japan). The reverse transcription conditions were as follows: 42 °C for 30–50 min, followed by 85 °C for 5 s. RT-qPCR was performed using the Fast SYBR Green PCR Kit (RR820A, Takara, Japan) and an ABI PRISM 7300 RT-PCR system (Applied Biosystems). The PCR conditions were: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min; denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, annealing at 57 °C for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 30 s, for a total of 40 cycles. Each sample was run in triplicate. β-actin was used as an internal reference gene, and gene expression levels were analyzed using the 2-ΔΔCt method, where ΔΔCt = (experimental group target gene average Ct - experimental group housekeeping gene average Ct) - (control group target gene average Ct - control group housekeeping gene average Ct). All experiments were performed in triplicate. Primer sequences are shown in Table S1.

Western blot analysis

Cells and tissues from each group were collected and lysed with RIPA buffer containing 1% PMSF (P0013B, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) on ice for 30 min. After centrifugation at 14,000 g for 15 min at 4 °C, the supernatant was collected. The protein concentration of the samples was determined using the bicinchoninic acid assay. The protein samples were then mixed with an appropriate volume of 5× loading buffer and boiled at 100 °C for 10 min to denature the proteins, with 30 µg of protein loaded onto the gel. Separation and stacking gels were prepared for electrophoresis. Following electrophoresis, protein bands of interest were transferred onto PVDF membranes (FFP28, Beyotime, Shanghai, China). The membranes were then blocked with 5% non-fat dry milk at room temperature for 1 h. After blocking, the membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies: anti-RGS19 (mouse, 1:1000, sc-271810, Santa Cruz, USA), anti-FN (rabbit, 1:5000, 15613-1-AP, Proteintech, China), anti-collagen I (Col I) (rabbit, 1:2000, 14695-1-AP, Proteintech, China), anti-α-SMA (rabbit, 1:2000, 14395-1-AP, Proteintech, China), anti-p62 (rabbit, 1:1000, ab91526, Abcam, UK), anti-p-mTOR (rabbit, 1:1000, ab109268, Abcam, UK), anti-mTOR (rabbit, 1:10000, ab134903, Abcam, UK), anti-GAPDH (rabbit, 1:2500, ab9485, Abcam, UK)), anti-LC3A/B (rabbit, 1:1,000; ab62721; Abcam, UK), and anti-vimentin (rabbit, 1:1,000; ab92547; Abcam, UK).

After washing the membranes with PBS containing 0.1% Tween 20 (PBST) at room temperature, the membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (goat anti-rabbit or goat anti-mouse IgG, 1:10,000, BA1054/BA1050, BOSTER, Wuhan, China) for 1 h at room temperature. The membranes were then washed six times with PBST (5 min per wash). enhanced chemiluminescence substrate (AR1172, BOSTER, Wuhan, China) was applied to the membranes, and chemiluminescent signals were detected using the Amersham Imager 600 (USA). The intensity of the protein bands was quantified using ImageJ software. All experiments were repeated three times.

Statistical analysis methods

Data were derived from at least three independent experiments and are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (Mean±SD). For comparisons between the two groups, an independent two-sample t-test was used. For comparisons involving three or more groups, Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) was applied. If the ANOVA results indicated significant differences, post-hoc Tukey’s HSD test was conducted to assess pairwise differences between the groups. For non-normally distributed data or data with unequal variances, the Mann-Whitney U test or Kruskal-Wallis H test was used. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.5 (GraphPad Software, Inc.) and R software. Spearman’s rank correlation analysis was employed to evaluate correlations. A significance level of 0.05 was applied to all tests, with a two-tailed p-value of less than 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

RGS19 as a key gene in renal fibrosis and autophagy

Autophagy plays a critical role in regulating cellular homeostasis and immune responses, making it an important biological process in the pathogenesis of renal fibrosis, which is closely linked to disease progression [22]. Although previous studies have highlighted the significance of autophagy in renal fibrosis, comprehensive analyses of autophagy-related genes and their underlying molecular mechanisms in renal fibrosis remain limited. In our study, we obtained renal fibrosis-related transcriptomic data from the GEO database, specifically dataset GSE76882. Using the Limma method, we identified DEGs, yielding a total of 9,942 DEGs, with 4,316 genes upregulated and 5,626 genes downregulated (Fig. 1A-B). Additionally, we retrieved 232 autophagy-related genes from the Human Autophagy Database. Through a Venn diagram analysis, we successfully identified 123 genes that are significantly associated with both renal fibrosis and the autophagy process (Fig. 1C).

Identification of DEGs and Autophagy-Related Genes in Renal Fibrosis. (A-B) DEGs associated with renal fibrosis identified through Limma differential analysis; (C) Venn diagram showing the overlap between DEGs in renal fibrosis and autophagy-related genes; (D) Results of gene importance scoring using RF; (E-F) Expression patterns of the candidate gene RGS19 in the training and validation cohorts. Training set (GSE76882): Normal = 99, RF = 135; Test set (GSE12682 + GSE22459): Normal = 38, RF = 63

We first applied the LASSO regression method to identify 45 genes closely associated with renal fibrosis and autophagy (as shown in Figure S1A-B). These 45 genes were then further refined using the SVM-RFE algorithm, resulting in the selection of 26 genes highly correlated with disease status (Figure S1C-D). To prioritize these genes based on their relevance, we conducted a gene importance analysis using the RF algorithm. This analysis highlighted RGS19 as the most influential gene, thus establishing RGS19 as the primary candidate for further investigation (Fig. 1D & Figure S1E).

We used the GSE76882 dataset as the training set and the combined GSE12682 and GSE22459 datasets as the test set to further evaluate the expression levels of the candidate gene RGS19 in both sets. The results indicated that, compared to the control group, RGS19 expression was significantly elevated in patients with renal fibrosis in both datasets (p < 0.05) (Fig. 1E-F). To assess the diagnostic potential of RGS19, we performed ROC curve analysis. The results demonstrated strong diagnostic relevance, particularly in the training set, where the AUC for RGS19 reached 0.88, with a CI ranging from 0.83 to 0.92 (Figure S1F). Further validation in the test set also supported the gene’s diagnostic utility, showing an AUC of 0.66 with a CI of 0.55 to 0.77, confirming its stable diagnostic significance (Figure S1G).

RGS19 and its association with immune response in renal fibrosis

In this section of our study, we classified Renal Fibrosis samples from the training group into high and low-expression groups based on the expression levels of the RGS19 gene. Using the Limma differential analysis method, we identified 6,195 genes with significantly differential expression, of which 3,390 genes were upregulatedand 2,805 genes were downregulated (Figure S2A-B). Further correlation analysis revealed that the MYO1F gene showed the strongest positive correlation with RGS19 expression, while the SLC6A19 gene exhibited the most significant negative correlation (Figure S2C).

KEGG pathway analysis indicated that these DEGs were predominantly involved in immune-inflammatory pathways, particularly those related to cytokine-cytokine receptor interactions and chemokine signaling (Fig. 2A). Complementary GO functional analysis demonstrated significant involvement of these genes in immune response regulation, T-cell activation, and immune response signaling pathways (Fig. 2B), further highlighting their close relationship with immune function. GSEA results revealed that high expression of RGS19 was primarily associated with chemokine signaling and cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction pathways (Fig. 2C), whereas low expression of RGS19 was linked to butyrate metabolism and cytochrome P450 pathways (Fig. 2D). GSVA analysis showed consistent results with GSEA, reflecting similar trends (Figure S3A).

(A) KEGG pathway analysis of DEGs in subgroups; (B) GO analysis of subgroup differential genes; (C-D) GSEA analysis of the high and low expression groups of RGS19; (E) Immune functional differences between the high and low expression groups of RGS19 in the training set; (F) Differential expression of immune cell infiltration in the training set; (G) Correlation between RGS19 expression levels and immune cell infiltration

Subsequently, we assessed the immune-related functional activity scores of each individual and compared the differences in immune function between the high and low RGS19 expression groups. The analysis revealed that patients with high RGS19 expression generally exhibited stronger immune function compared to those with low expression. This observation indicates that RGS19 expression levels have a significant impact on immune activity (Fig. 2E & Figure S3B).

Further analysis of immune cell infiltration showed significant differences in the levels of CD8+ T cells, resting memory CD4+ T cells, M0 macrophages, and resting dendritic cells (DC) between the training and validation groups. These findings enhance our understanding of the roles of different immune cell types in renal fibrosis and their potential correlation with RGS19 expression levels (Fig. 2F & Figure S3C). In a more in-depth exploration of the relationship between RGS19 expression and immune cell infiltration, we identified correlations between RGS19 expression and the infiltration levels of ten immune cell types. Notably, eosinophils, neutrophils, activated DC, and CD8 T cells exhibited a significant positive correlation with RGS19 expression, while resting mast cells and activated natural killer cells showed a significant negative correlation (Fig. 2G).

Overall, these analyses suggest that RGS19 may play a role in regulating the immune microenvironment and inflammatory response, laying the groundwork for further exploration of the mechanisms of renal fibrosis and potential therapeutic approaches.

RGS19 expression and CD8+ T cell infiltration in renal fibrosis mouse models

We established two Renal Fibrosis mouse models, UUO and UIRI (Fig. 3A), to investigate the role of RGS19 in Renal Fibrosis. The mRNA level of RGS19 was significantly upregulated following UUO (Fig. 3B). Western blot analysis confirmed that the protein level of RGS19 was also significantly increased in the fibrotic kidneys (Fig. 3C). Immunohistochemical analysis revealed that RGS19 was predominantly localized in the TEC during the progression of fibrosis (Fig. 3D). This distribution was further validated by immunofluorescence co-staining with the proximal tubule marker, LTL (Fig. 3E). Similarly, RGS19 expression was significantly elevated in the kidneys of UIRI mice, exhibiting a comparable pattern (Figure S4A-D).

Masson’s trichrome and Picrosirius red staining showed marked collagen deposition in the tubular interstitium of UUO mice compared to the Sham group (Fig. 3F). Consistently, Western blot analysis indicated significant increases in the expression of FN, Col I, and α-SMA in the kidneys of UUO mice (Fig. 3G). Similar results were observed in the UIRI mouse model (Figure S4E-F).

Previous studies have reported insufficient autophagy in the renal tissues of renal fibrosis (Renal Fibrosis) model mice [7]. Our prior bioinformatics analysis identified RGS19 as an autophagy-related gene. Both renal fibrosis and RGS19 overexpression were associated with increased infiltration of CD8+ T cells, and RGS19 expression was positively correlated with CD8+ T cell infiltration. Therefore, we further investigated autophagy and CD8+ T cell infiltration in the renal tissues of Renal Fibrosis model mice.

Immunohistochemical and fluorescence staining analyses revealed that in UUO and UIRI model mice, the expression of autophagy-related substrates (SQSTM1/p62) and p-mTOR was significantly upregulated, while LC3B expression was downregulated, indicating impaired autophagy in the fibrotic kidneys (Figure S5A-B). Additionally, flow cytometry analysis showed a marked increase in the infiltration and proliferation of CD8+ T cells in the renal tissues of UUO and UIRI model mice (Fig. 3H-I & Figure S4G-H). Previous research suggests that excessive release of inflammatory factors may promote the secretion of T cell recruitment chemokines CXCL9/CXCL10, leading to the accumulation of CD8+ T cells in renal tissues [23, 24]. We also measured the levels of inflammatory cytokines IL-1β, Interleukin 6 (IL-6), Tumor Necrosis Factor-alpha (TNF-α), and chemokines CXCL9 and CXCL10. The results showed significant increases in IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, CXCL9, and CXCL10 levels in the renal tissues of UUO and UIRI model mice (Fig. 3J & Figure S4I & Figure S5C-D).

These findings suggest that RGS19 is highly expressed in the renal tissues of Renal Fibrosis model mice, where autophagy is suppressed and CD8+ T cell infiltration is increased.

Knockdown of RGS19 promotes autophagy, inhibiting ECM Production, and reducing CD8+ T cell proliferation in TEC

TGF-β1, a key driver of fibrosis, actively alters the metabolism of various cell types [5]. To explore the effects of RGS19 on ECM production and CD8+ T cell proliferation in TEC, we utilized the TGF-β1-stimulated mouse proximal TEC line, TKPTS, as an in vitro model of fibrosis. RGS19 knockdown was performed, and its impact on ECM production and CD8+ T cell activation was assessed (Fig. 4A). The results showed that after 24 and 48 h of TGF-β1 stimulation, both the mRNA and protein expression of RGS19 were significantly increased in TKPTS cells (Figure S6A-B). RGS19 knockdown was confirmed by the efficiency test shown in Figure S6C-D, and sh-RGS19#1 was selected for subsequent experiments.

Next, we assessed autophagy in TKPTS cells following TGF-β1 stimulation. Western blot analysis revealed that TGF-β1 stimulation led to a significant upregulation of p62 and p-mTOR protein expression, accompanied by a downregulation of LC3BII expression. Compared to the TGF-β1 + sh-NC group, autophagy was significantly enhanced in the TGF-β1 + sh-RGS19 group. Furthermore, in the TGF-β1 + sh-RGS19 + 3-MA group, autophagy was notably suppressed following treatment with the autophagy inhibitor 3-MA (Fig. 4B-C). To further evaluate autophagic flux, we used an RFP-GFP-LC3B sensor. LC3B-positive, neutral pH autophagosomes were visualized as green fluorescence, while LC3B-positive, acidic pH autolysosomes appeared red. The results showed that TGF-β1 induced the accumulation of autophagosomes, but these did not fuse with lysosomes. In contrast, knockdown of RGS19 triggered autophagic flux, with red puncta appearing as a result of autophagosome-lysosome fusion. However, treatment with 3-MA suppressed autophagic flux (Fig. 4D-E).

Next, we performed immunofluorescence staining to assess the expression of fibrosis markers. The results showed that, upon TGF-β1 stimulation, the expression of FN, vimentin, and α-SMA was significantly increased in TKPTS cells, while E-cadherin expression was markedly reduced. Compared to the TGF-β1 + sh-NC group, the TGF-β1 + sh-RGS19 group exhibited a significant decrease in the expression of FN, vimentin, and α-SMA, and a significant increase in E-cadherin expression. In contrast, in the TGF-β1 + sh-RGS19 + 3-MA group, the expression of FN, vimentin, and α-SMA was significantly elevated, while E-cadherin expression was notably reduced, compared to the TGF-β1 + sh-RGS19 group (Fig. 4F). Under TGF-β1 stimulation, RGS19 overexpression was associated with increased mTOR pathway activity, as indicated by elevated p-mTOR levels, accompanied by changes in autophagy markers (p62 upregulation and reduced LC3B-II). Treatment with the mTOR inhibitor rapamycin (100 nM) decreased p-mTOR and reversed these marker changes, with reduced p62 and increased LC3B-II, suggesting partial restoration of the autophagy-related phenotype (Figure S6E). Together, these data support a link between RGS19-associated alterations in autophagy markers and mTOR signaling.

We further isolated and cultured CD8+ T cells in vitro, confirming their purity to be approximately 85% by flow cytometry (Figure S6F). The various treatment groups of TKPTS cells were co-cultured with CFSE-labeled CD8+ T cells, and their ability to induce CD8+ T cell proliferation was assessed. The results showed that TGF-β1 stimulation significantly enhanced the capacity of TKPTS cells to induce CD8+ T cell proliferation. Compared to the TGF-β1 + sh-NC group, the TGF-β1 + sh-RGS19 group showed a reduced capacity for CD8+ T cell proliferation. In contrast, the TGF-β1 + sh-RGS19 + 3-MA group exhibited an increased ability to induce CD8+ T cell proliferation compared to the TGF-β1 + sh-RGS19 group (Fig. 4G-H). Additionally, ELISA analysis of the chemokine levels in the co-culture system revealed that TGF-β1-stimulated TKPTS cells promoted the accumulation of CXCL9 and CXCL10 in the culture medium. Compared to the TGF-β1 + sh-NC group, the TGF-β1 + sh-RGS19 group showed significantly reduced levels of CXCL9 and CXCL10. However, the TGF-β1 + sh-RGS19 + 3-MA group exhibited significantly higher levels of CXCL9 and CXCL10 compared to the TGF-β1 + sh-RGS19 group (Fig. 4I). After RGS19 silencing, CD8⁺ T-cell migration and proliferation were reduced in the Ctrl + sh-RGS19 group compared with the control group. Compared with Ctrl + sh-RGS19, concurrent CXCL9 overexpression (Ctrl + sh-RGS19 + CXCL9) increased CD8⁺ T-cell migration and proliferation to levels close to the control group (Fig. 4J–K). Promoter reporter assays showed that CXCL9 promoter activity was decreased in Ctrl + sh-RGS19 compared with the control group (Fig. 4L). In addition, immunoblotting indicated increased NF-κB activity in Ctrl + sh-RGS19, which was reduced in Ctrl + sh-RGS19 + CXCL9 (Fig. 4M).

These results indicate that silencing RGS19 promotes autophagy, thereby inhibiting ECM production in TEC and reducing CD8+ T cell proliferation.

Knockdown of RGS19 inhibits TGF-β1-Induced fibroblast activation and proliferation

Fibroblasts play a crucial role as effector cells in the process of renal fibrosis. Therefore, we further evaluated whether knockdown of RGS19 could suppress fibroblast activation (Fig. 5A). To this end, L929 fibroblasts were co-incubated with or without RGS19 knockdown and TGF-β1. The results showed that TGF-β1 stimulation significantly increased the expression of α-SMA, Col I, and vimentin in L929 fibroblasts. However, RGS19 knockdown markedly inhibited the TGF-β1-induced expression of these markers (Fig. 5B). Immunofluorescence staining further confirmed that RGS19 knockdown significantly reduced TGF-β1-induced α-SMA expression (Fig. 5C). These findings suggest that RGS19 knockdown can suppress renal fibroblast activation.

To investigate the impact of RGS19 knockdown on TGF-β1-induced fibroblast proliferation, we measured the percentage of 5-Ethynyl-2’-Deoxyuridine (EdU)-positive L929 cells using an EdU assay. The results revealed that TGF-β1 stimulation significantly increased the number of EdU-positive cells, while RGS19 knockdown markedly attenuated this effect (Fig. 5D, F). Flow cytometry analysis showed that the proportion of proliferating L929 cells was significantly increased upon TGF-β1 stimulation, but this proliferative effect was substantially reduced in RGS19 knockdown-treated cells (Fig. 5E).

In summary, RGS19 knockdown inhibits both the proliferation and activation of fibroblasts.

Knockdown of RGS19 enhances autophagy and reduces CD8+ T cell Infiltration, thus slowing the progression of renal fibrosis in mice

Next, we further explored the molecular mechanisms by which RGS19 knockdown regulates the progression of renal fibrosis in UUO or UIRI mouse models (Fig. 6A). Initially, we assessed the protein expression of RGS19 in the kidneys of RGS19-knockdown mice and found that its expression was significantly reduced (Fig. 6B).

Masson’s trichrome and Picrosirius red staining results revealed no significant morphological differences between the Sham + sh-NC and Sham + sh-RGS19 groups. However, compared to the Sham + sh-NC group, the UUO + sh-NC group exhibited significant collagen deposition in the renal tubular interstitium. Notably, collagen deposition was markedly reduced in the UUO + sh-RGS19 group compared to the UUO + sh-NC group (Fig. 6C & Figure S7A-B). Western blot analysis further confirmed that RGS19 knockdown significantly alleviated the expression of FN, Col I, and α-SMA in the kidneys of UUO-induced mice (Fig. 6D). Similar results were observed in the UIRI mouse model (Figure S7C).

Subsequently, immunohistochemistry and fluorescence staining revealed that RGS19 knockdown significantly decreased the expression of p62 and p-mTOR proteins while increasing the expression of LC3B in the kidney tissues of UUO and UIRI model mice, indicating that RGS19 knockdown promoted autophagy in fibrotic kidneys (Fig. 6E & Figure S7D). Flow cytometry analysis of CD8+ T cell infiltration and proliferation showed a marked reduction in both CD8+ T cell infiltration and proliferation in the kidney tissues of UUO and UIRI model mice following RGS19 knockdown (Fig. 6F-G & Figure S7E-F). Additionally, the levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, and chemokines CXCL9 and CXCL10 were measured, with results showing that RGS19 knockdown significantly reduced the expression of IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α, as well as the levels of CXCL9 and CXCL10 in the kidney tissues of the UUO and UIRI model mice (Fig. 6H-I & Figure S7G-H). In the UUO model, compared with the UUO + sh-NC group, the UUO + sh-RGS19 group showed attenuated fibrosis, as indicated by reduced Masson- and Picrosirius red–positive areas, accompanied by decreased expression of fibrotic markers (FN, Col I, and α-SMA). Compared with UUO + sh-RGS19, the UUO + sh-RGS19 + CXCR3 group exhibited increased fibrotic staining and higher levels of these fibrotic proteins (Fig. 6J-K), indicating that CXCR3 modulation influences the antifibrotic phenotype observed after RGS19 silencing.

These findings suggest that RGS19 knockdown promotes autophagy in fibrotic kidney tissue and reduces CD8+ T cell infiltration, thereby slowing the progression of renal fibrosis in mice.

RDYH58-NPs@si-RGS19 effectively inhibits the production of ECM in TEC

We next investigated whether RGS19 has the potential as a therapeutic target for renal fibrosis. Nanomedicine holds great promise for clinical applications due to its advantages, such as prolonged drug half-life, targeted delivery, controlled release, and ability to cross biological barriers (e.g., the BBB) [15]. Previous studies have reported that PDA, a major component of the natural pigment melanin, is the precursor of nanomaterials derived from PDA, which exhibit excellent stability, biodegradability, biocompatibility, and photothermal conversion properties [15]. Therefore, we developed PDA NPs loaded with RGS19 and synthesized RDYH58-NPs@si-RGS19, incorporating the fibrosis-targeting peptide RDYH58 (Fig. 7A).

Preparation, Characterization, and In Vitro Targeting Efficacy of RDYH58-NPs@si-RGS19. (A) Schematic representation of the synthesis process of RDYH58-NPs@si-RGS19, illustrating the binding of PDA-modified NPs with the fibrosis-targeting peptide RDYH58; (B) TEM images showing the typical spherical morphology of both RDYH58-NPs@si-RGS19 and NPs@si-RGS19 (Scale bar = 100 nm); (C) DLS analysis of the average particle size of RDYH58-NPs@si-RGS19 and NPs@si-RGS19; (D) ζ-potential measurements of RDYH58-NPs@si-RGS19 and NPs@si-RGS19; (E) SiRNA release profiles of RDYH58-NPs@si-RGS19 in PBS at pH 5.0 and 7.4; (F) siRNA release kinetics of the indicated formulations; (G) Fluorescence microscopy images of NP uptake in TKPTS cells (Scale bar = 25 μm); (H) RT-qPCR analysis of RGS19 mRNA expression in TKPTS cells across different groups; (I) Western blot analysis of RGS19 protein expression in TKPTS cells across different groups; (J) Immunofluorescence staining showing the expression of fibrosis markers FN, vimentin, α-SMA, and E-cadherin (Scale bar = 25 μm); (K) Zeta potential measurements of RGS19, NPs@si-RGS19, and RDYH58-NPs@si-RGS19. *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ns, not significant

TEM analysis revealed that both RDYH58-NPs@si-RGS19 and NPs@si-RGS19 exhibited a typical spherical morphology (Fig. 7B). DLS measurements showed that the average particle size of RDYH58-NPs@si-RGS19 and NPs@si-RGS19 was approximately 200 nm, and their ζ-potential was about 28.0 mV, indicating that RDYH58 modification did not significantly affect the NP surface charge (Fig. 7C-D) [15]. The siRNA encapsulation efficiency of the synthesized NPs@si-RGS19 and RDYH58-NPs@si-RGS19 was approximately 78% (Figure S8A). To simulate the physiological extracellular and acidic endosomal environments, we measured the siRNA release profiles of RDYH58-NPs@si-RGS19 in PBS at pH 7.4 and 5.0. At pH 5.0, RDYH58-NPs@si-RGS19 exhibited a faster release of siRNA compared to pH 7.4 (after 48 h, ~ 80.4% release at pH 5.0 versus ~45.0% release at pH 7.4) (Fig. 7E). To evaluate whether RDYH58 modification affects release kinetics, siRNA release from RDYH58-NPs@si-RGS19 and NPs@si-RGS19 was measured at pH 5.0. RDYH58 modification slightly reduced the siRNA release rate (Fig. 7F).

To evaluate the protective effect of RDYH58-NPs@si-RGS19 on siRNA, free si-RGS19 and RDYH58-NPs@si-RGS19 were incubated with RNase for different durations. The results demonstrated that free si-RGS19 was rapidly degraded, whereas si-RGS19 extracted from RDYH58-NPs@si-RGS19 retained its structural integrity even after 4 h of exposure to RNase (Figure S8B). Additionally, we investigated the impact of pH on the stability of RDYH58-NPs@si-RGS19. DLS analysis showed that RDYH58-NPs@si-RGS19 incubated in PBS containing 10% FBS at pH 7.4 or 6.8 exhibited no significant changes in particle size over 48 h (Figure S8C), indicating that RDYH58-NPs@si-RGS19 maintains structural stability in mildly acidic extracellular pH conditions. To assess the in vitro biocompatibility of RDYH58-NPs@si-RGS19, TKPTS cells were treated with varying concentrations of RDYH58-NPs@si-RGS19 for 48 h. We found that even at a concentration of 200 µg/mL, RDYH58-NPs@si-RGS19 did not significantly reduce cell viability (Figure S8D). Hemolysis tests further revealed that RDYH58-NPs@si-RGS19 at 200 µg/mL did not induce significant hemolysis (Figure S8E), suggesting that RDYH58-NPs@si-RGS19 exhibits good in vitro biocompatibility. In addition, analysis of particle size and polydispersity index (PDI) demonstrated no significant differences between NPs@si-RGS19 and RDYH58-NPs@si-RGS19 (Figure S8F–G). Following terminal sterilization of RDYH58-NPs@si-RGS19 by sterile filtration, neither particle size nor PDI showed noticeable changes compared with the pre-sterilization state, and cellular uptake signals remained comparable (Pre-sterilization vs. Post-sterilization) (Figure S8H–J).

Assessment of release and uptake stability revealed that RDYH58-NPs@si-RGS19 exhibited similar cumulative siRNA release profiles under Free FBS and 10% FBS conditions (Figure S8K). Moreover, efficient cellular uptake was observed under both 10% FBS and PBS-free conditions (Figure S8L).

Competitive inhibition experiments further demonstrated that, compared with the PBS condition, the addition of an RDYH58 inhibitor significantly reduced the cellular uptake of RDYH58-NPs@si-RGS19 (Figure S8M), indicating that RDYH58-mediated binding contributes to the cellular internalization of this nanocarrier system.

We next investigated the targeting ability of RDYH58-NPs@si-RGS19 to fibrotic TEC. The results showed that after 6 h of incubation with RDYH58-NPs@si-RGS19, the fluorescence intensity in TGF-β1-induced TKPTS cells was significantly higher compared to the Ctrl group. In contrast, there was no significant difference in fluorescence intensity between the TGF-β1 and Ctrl groups when treated with NPs@si-RGS19 without RDYH58 peptide modification (Fig. 7G). RT-qPCR and Western blot analyses revealed that, compared to the TGF-β1 group, the expression of RGS19 was markedly reduced in the TGF-β1 + NPs@si-RGS19 group, with an even more significant decrease in the TGF-β1 + RDYH58-NPs@si-RGS19 group (Fig. 7H-I). Additionally, immunofluorescence staining for fibrotic markers showed that, compared to the TGF-β1 group, the TGF-β1 + NPs@si-RGS19 group exhibited significantly lower expression of FN, vimentin, and α-SMA, and a marked increase in E-cadherin expression. Furthermore, the TGF-β1 + RDYH58-NPs@si-RGS19 group showed even more pronounced reductions in FN, vimentin, and α-SMA, along with a further increase in E-cadherin expression (Fig. 7J). Zeta potential measurements showed that, compared with the RGS19 group, both the NPs@si-RGS19 and RDYH58-NPs@si-RGS19 groups exhibited higher positive surface charge, consistent with increased cationic character after PEI coating. No significant difference in zeta potential was observed between the NPs@si-RGS19 and RDYH58-NPs@si-RGS19 groups, indicating that RDYH58 modification did not appreciably alter the surface charge properties of the nanoparticles (Fig. 7K).

These results demonstrate that we successfully constructed RDYH58-NPs@si-RGS19, a fibrosis-targeting NP for the kidney. RDYH58-NPs@si-RGS19 effectively inhibits ECM production in TEC and exhibits good in vitro biocompatibility and safety.

RDYH58-NPs@si-RGS19 delays the progression of renal fibrosis in mice

We next investigated the in vivo distribution, biocompatibility, and therapeutic effects of RDYH58-NPs@si-RGS19 in alleviating renal fibrosis in mice (Fig. 8A). After establishing the UUO model, mice were intravenously injected with RDYH58-NPs@si-RGS19 or NPs@si-RGS19. Fluorescent imaging of major organs was performed 4 and 24 h post-injection. In the Sham group, RDYH58-NPs@si-RGS19 predominantly localized in the liver, whereas in the UUO group, RDYH58-NPs@si-RGS19 mainly accumulated in the injured kidneys. In contrast, NPs@si-RGS19 did not show a significant increase in kidney accumulation (Fig. 8B-D). Additionally, immunofluorescence staining revealed that RDYH58-NPs@si-RGS19 co-localized prominently with the fibrosis marker α-SMA in the kidneys of UUO mice, whereas co-localization with α-SMA was rare for NPs@si-RGS19 (Fig. 8E). These results suggest that RDYH58-NPs@si-RGS19 specifically targets fibrotic kidneys.

Pharmacokinetic analysis was performed to assess blood circulation half-life, showing an approximately 10-h plasma half-life for RDYH58-NPs@si-RGS19 (Figure S9A). Quantitative biodistribution analysis indicated higher renal fluorescence in the RDYH58-NPs@si-RGS19 group than in the NPs@si-RGS19 and si-RGS19 groups (Figure S9B). H&E staining was used to evaluate histopathological changes in major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney, and brain) at 14 days after dosing, and no overt differences were observed between the Saline and RDYH58-NPs@si-RGS19 groups (Figure S9C). Hematology and serum biochemistry indices (including ALT, AST, and BUN) remained comparable across groups during a 28-day repeat-dosing follow-up (Figure S9D–E).

The therapeutic efficacy of RDYH58-NPs@si-RGS19 was evaluated in the UUO model. Kidney analyses showed that, compared with the UUO group, UUO + si-RGS19, UUO + NPs@si-RGS19, and UUO+RDYH58-NPs@si-RGS19 exhibited reduced RGS19 mRNA and protein levels, with lower RGS19 expression in UUO+RDYH58-NPs@si-RGS19 than in UUO + NPs@si-RGS19 (Fig. 8F–G). Masson’s trichrome and Picrosirius red staining demonstrated marked collagen deposition in the tubulointerstitium of the UUO group, whereas collagen deposition was reduced in UUO + NPs@si-RGS19 and UUO+RDYH58-NPs@si-RGS19, with a greater reduction in the latter (Fig. 8H). Immunoblotting showed decreased renal expression of FN, Col I, and α-SMA in UUO + si-RGS19 and UUO+RDYH58-NPs@si-RGS19 compared with the UUO group (Fig. 8I). In parallel, renal IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α expression was reduced in UUO + si-RGS19, UUO + NPs@si-RGS19, and UUO+RDYH58-NPs@si-RGS19 compared with the UUO group, and was lower in UUO+RDYH58-NPs@si-RGS19 than in UUO + NPs@si-RGS19 (Fig. 8J).

Immunohistochemistry showed that, compared with the UUO group, the UUO + si-RGS19 group exhibited reduced p62 and p-mTOR signals and increased LC3B signal; relative to UUO + si-RGS19, the UUO+RDYH58-NPs@si-RGS19 group showed a further decrease in p62 and p-mTOR and a further increase in LC3B (Fig. 8K). Flow cytometry showed reduced renal CD8⁺ T-cell infiltration in UUO + si-RGS19 compared with UUO, with a greater reduction in UUO+RDYH58-NPs@si-RGS19 (Fig. 8L). Renal function indices indicated elevated BUN/Cr and urine protein/Cr ratio in the UUO group, which were reduced in UUO + NPs@si-RGS19 and UUO+RDYH58-NPs@si-RGS19, with lower values in UUO+RDYH58-NPs@si-RGS19 than in UUO + NPs@si-RGS19 (Fig. 8M). In addition, immunofluorescence quantification based on CD31/EMCN showed reduced fluorescence density in UUO + NPs@si-RGS19 and UUO+RDYH58-NPs@si-RGS19 compared with UUO, with lower levels in UUO+RDYH58-NPs@si-RGS19 than in UUO + NPs@si-RGS19 (Fig. 8N).

These results demonstrate that RDYH58-NPs@si-RGS19 has favorable in vivo biocompatibility and safety, effectively targets fibrotic renal tissue, and delays the progression of renal fibrosis in mice.

Discussion

Renal fibrosis represents a common end-stage pathological feature of multiple forms of CKD, characterized by excessive extracellular matrix (ECM) deposition accompanied by progressive loss of renal function [2]. The present study integrates bioinformatics analysis, validation in animal models, and development of a targeted delivery system to establish an evidence chain centered on RGS19, spanning clinical relevance, mechanistic interrogation, and in vivo intervention.

In human datasets, transcriptomic analyses showed that RGS19 was significantly upregulated in renal fibrosis and exhibited discriminatory performance (training AUC = 0.88; testing AUC = 0.66), supporting its potential as a CKD-associated fibrotic biomarker. However, heterogeneity in CKD etiologies (e.g., diabetic kidney disease, IgA nephropathy, and chronic tubulointerstitial lesions) and immune states may influence RGS19 expression and downstream pathway activity. Accordingly, its diagnostic and prognostic utility across subtypes and immunophenotypes, as well as its value in predicting treatment response, remains to be systematically evaluated in cohorts with biopsy-defined stratification. Future validation should be conducted in multicenter cohorts incorporating standardized clinical endpoints (e.g., estimated glomerular filtration rate slope and kidney failure events) together with immune lineage/immune infiltration profiling to test the robustness of risk stratification and the capacity to identify potentially responsive patient subsets.

Mechanistic experiments showed that TGF-β1 stimulation impaired autophagic flux in tubular epithelial cells, reflected by reduced LC3B-II, accumulation of p62, and increased p-mTOR, consistent with prior reports [5, 7]. Under these conditions, RGS19 knockdown restored autophagic flux and reversed the associated molecular phenotype (decreased p62 and p-mTOR and increased LC3B-II), supporting a role for RGS19 in autophagic flux regulation; prior bioinformatics work has also implicated RGS19 in autophagy [12], consistent with the present findings. Based on established molecular biology, RGS19 is a negative regulator of GPCR signaling that binds activated Gα subunits and promotes GTP hydrolysis to terminate GPCR signaling [25]. In addition, Gαs-coupled GPCR signaling can inhibit mTORC1 via the cAMP–PKA axis (Raptor Ser791 phosphorylation) [26]. On this basis, an RGS19-dependent shift in GPCR signaling balance may indirectly engage the PI3K/AKT and Rag/tuberous sclerosis complex axes to promote mTORC1 activation and thereby suppress autophagic flux. Under conditions of impaired autophagy, upregulation of pro-inflammatory chemokines such as CXCL9/CXCL10 can promote renal inflammation and fibrogenesis [8], leading to the working model hypothesis of an RGS19–mTOR–autophagy–CXCL9/10 axis to explain the coupling between autophagy impairment and inflammatory amplification.

In pharmacological perturbation experiments, 3-MA (a PI3K inhibitor commonly used to inhibit autophagy initiation) was applied to support the functional association of autophagy with the relevant phenotypes [27]. Importantly, 3-MA is not a selective autophagy blocker and can affect multiple classes of PI3K and other PI3K-associated signaling pathways [28]; therefore, this evidence is best interpreted as supporting the consistency of autophagy involvement rather than serving as a standalone basis for strong causal inference that “autophagy blockade” drives the observed phenotypes. Within this mechanistic framework, combination strategies pairing RDYH58-siRGS19 with autophagy enhancers (e.g., rapamycin) or anti-inflammatory agents warrant further evaluation; autophagy enhancers may facilitate clearance of damaged cellular components and improve tubular function, whereas anti-inflammatory drugs can suppress chronic nephritis-associated inflammation [29], thereby enabling multi-pathway coordinated intervention on the background of RGS19 suppression.

Regarding the immune microenvironment, tubulointerstitial inflammation (TII) is considered a key driver of the transition from Acute Kidney Injury (AKI) to CKD and is initiated primarily by innate immune responses, with macrophages and dendritic cells as critical cell types [30, 31]. In contrast to traditional views, T cells can infiltrate the kidney early during AKI and contribute to tissue injury and inflammatory deterioration [32]. Prior work has further shown increased CD8⁺ T-cell infiltration in the unilateral renal ischemia–reperfusion injury model, and depletion of CD8⁺ T cells improved peritubular capillary loss and attenuated renal fibrosis [11]. The present study further observed that RGS19 modulates CD8⁺ T-cell infiltration and proliferative dynamics through regulation of CXCL9/CXCL10 secretion, providing additional evidence for a coordinated “autophagy–chemotaxis–immunity” contribution to fibrotic progression.

In vivo validation showed that conditional RGS19 knockdown attenuated fibrotic pathology in both UUO and UIRI models: Masson and Sirius Red staining demonstrated reduced ECM deposition, fibrotic markers including α-SMA, Col I, and FN were decreased, and these changes were accompanied by reduced inflammatory mediators and enhanced autophagy activity, supporting a multi-layered antifibrotic effect of RGS19 knockdown and providing in vivo evidence for RGS19 as a therapeutic target.

To enable targeted in vivo intervention, RDYH58-modified nanoparticles (RDYH58-NPs@si-RGS19) were constructed to deliver si-RGS19 to fibrotic kidneys. RDYH58 is a myofibroblast-targeting peptide; prior work has shown that coupling RDYH58 to the exosomal membrane protein Lamp2b enhances preferential accumulation in myofibroblasts [33]. For the carrier, polydopamine (PDA) is biodegradable and highly functionalizable, and prior reports indicate that PDA-based nanocarriers exhibit renal enrichment and favorable biocompatibility [34–36]. In the present study, nanoparticle characterization showed a well-defined size distribution and zeta potential together with acid-responsive release properties. In medium containing 10% FBS, release was modestly delayed while overall kinetics remained stable and cellular uptake was not substantially reduced, consistent with reports that adsorption of serum proteins forms a protein corona that can slow diffusion/release [37]. Prior studies also indicate that specific peptide modifications can increase renal accumulation and prolong tissue retention of nanoparticles [38]. Across multiple time points, RDYH58 conferred both enhanced kidney targeting and increased tissue retention; in the UUO model, RDYH58-NPs@si-RGS19 reduced fibrotic markers and improved autophagy-related readouts, and biodistribution analyses supported enhanced renal accumulation.

In vivo fluorescence imaging further showed that unmodified NPs@si-RGS19 displayed liver-dominant signals, whereas RDYH58-NPs@si-RGS19 exhibited injured kidney–dominant signals under UUO conditions. Given that the baseline physicochemical parameters of the two nanoparticle systems were comparable (approximately 200 nm, ζ potential approximately +28 mV, PEI/PDA composite carrying Cy3-siRNA), this difference is more consistent with a distribution pattern shaped by background clearance combined with lesion retention: positively charged, relatively large nanocarriers can form a protein corona in circulation and be taken up by sinusoidal phagocytes, resulting in liver-dominant distribution [37, 39, 40]. By contrast, early UUO-associated interstitial edema and microvascular permeability/barrier alterations can promote extravasation and local retention in the injured kidney [41], together with a tendency toward lesion enrichment driven by fibrosis-associated inflammation and matrix remodeling [42]. In addition, RDYH58-modified particles showed marked co-localization with α-SMA in UUO kidneys, supporting ligand-mediated recognition of fibrosis-associated cells/regions and enhanced tissue retention, consistent with prior reports of “homing peptide/myofibroblast-targeting” strategies for fibrotic kidney delivery [43].

Although this study provides encouraging evidence, several key limitations should be addressed in future work: the in situ spatial localization and expression gradients of RGS19 across distinct CKD etiologic subtypes and immune phenotypes, as well as its co-localization and quantitative correlations with autophagy and fibrosis markers (e.g., LC3B/p62 and α-SMA/Col I), require validation in independent, biopsy-confirmed, multicenter cohorts using standardized IHC/IF and further support from single-cell and/or spatial transcriptomic approaches; moreover, deeper molecular dissection is needed to pinpoint critical nodes and pathway crosstalk within the proposed RGS19–mTOR–autophagy–CXCL9/10 axis, and the long-term safety, pharmacokinetics, and potential immunogenicity of RDYH58-NPs@si-RGS19 should be systematically evaluated to facilitate clinical translation.

Conclusion

Based on the results, we can preliminarily conclude that silencing RGS19 may improve the renal fibrosis microenvironment and delay disease progression by promoting autophagic flux, reducing CD8+ T cell infiltration, and decreasing the secretion of inflammatory cytokines. This study not only reveals the dual role of RGS19 in regulating autophagy and immune function in renal fibrosis but also demonstrates the potential of RGS19 as a therapeutic target through the construction of RDYH58-NPs@si-RGS19. RDYH58-NPs@si-RGS19 exhibited significant anti-fibrotic effects in both in vitro and in vivo experiments, confirming its role in improving the fibrotic microenvironment and reducing the secretion of inflammatory cytokines. This research provides new insights and potential molecular targets for autophagy-based therapeutic strategies against renal fibrosis.
