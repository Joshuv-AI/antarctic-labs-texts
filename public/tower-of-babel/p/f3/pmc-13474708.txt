High-risk cytogenetic abnormalities impact the cytological and clinical behavior of core binding factor acute myeloid leukemia: a case-control study
Authors: Hani El Achkar, Ali Bazarbachi, Zaher Chakhachiro, Rami Mahfouz, Nada Assaf
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDespite its classification as acute myeloid leukemia (AML) with favorable prognosis, core binding factor AML (CBF-AML) with t(8;21)/inv(16) exhibits clinical heterogeneity with varying remission and relapse rates. Newly emerging data suggest a modulatory effect of additional cytogenetic abnormalities on the clinical behavior of CBF-AML, with high-risk anomalies negatively affecting survival.MethodsAiming at validating these observations, we compare the pathological and clinical characteristics of CBF-AML patients with high-risk additional cytogenetic abnormalities (HR-ACAs) to CBF-AML without HR-ACAs.ResultsThe 14-year retrospective review of the laboratory and clinical data of 535 AML patients diagnosed at our institution shows that 37% of CBF-AML patients carry at least one secondary cytogenetic abnormality. HR-ACAs, primarily complex karyotypes, constituted 38% of secondary abnormalities. Interestingly, patients with HR-ACAs more frequently presented with cytopenia, and exhibited more aggressive clinical courses. The detection of HR-ACAs was found to be associated with higher relapse rates (50% vs. 18%;p = 0.006), a greater requirement for stem cell transplantation (89% vs. 27%;p = 0.001), and higher death rates (63% vs. 17%;p = 0.008).ConclusionThese results indicate that the presence of HR-ACAs impacts the cytological and clinical features of CBF-AML. This entity thus deserves distinct considerations for risk stratification and therapeutic options.

Introduction

The Core Binding Factor (CBF) is a DNA heterodimer composed of two subunits, α and β, which are encoded by the RUNX1 and CBFB genes, respectively. This transcription factor plays a role in the regulation of various genes involved in hematopoietic processes and myeloid differentiation [1]. Its natural function is disrupted by various genetic abnormalities redirecting it towards sustaining self-renewal processes and contributing to the progression of hematopoietic malignancies, including myelodysplastic neoplasms and leukemia [2]. It encompasses specific cytogenetic anomalies such as t(8;21)(q22;q22.1) and inv(16)(p13q22)/t(16;16)(p13;q22), which lead to the formation of the abnormal fusion proteins RUNX1::RUNX1T1 and CBFB::MYH11. These abnormal products of transcription result in the development of specific subtypes of Acute Myeloid Leukemia (AML) encompassing CBF-AML.

These entities are defined by their favorable prognosis compared to other AML subtypes [3]. However, clinical heterogeneity is observed among CBF-AML patients, with varying relapse rates and requirements for therapy intensification, including hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT). Although ~ 40% of CBF-AML patients die from their disease, scarce prognostic markers were identified to date. The detection of CD56 expression by immunohistochemistry and/or c-Kit mutations explain the unfavorable outcome in a subset of those patients, particularly AML with t(8;21) [4]. The effect of secondary cytogenetic anomalies on the prognosis of CBF-AML is still unclear [1, 3]. Newly published data suggest that high-risk additional cytogenetic abnormalities (HR-ACAs) might negatively impact the prognosis of CBF-AML [1, 5, 6]. However, no study has collectively grouped high-risk rearrangements to evaluate their impact on AML with t(8;21) and AML with inv(16).

Aiming at validating these observations, we compare the cytological and clinical characteristics of patients with CBF-AML and HR-ACAs to other CBF-AML patients. We conduct a retrospective analysis of all AML with t(8;21)/inv(16) diagnosed at the American University of Beirut Medical Center (AUBMC) over a 14-year period. An examination of the diagnostic complexities, cytological and molecular attributes, clinical characteristics, and behavior is undertaken to determine a possible effect of HR-ACAs on the clinicopathological characteristics of this AML subtype.

Materials and methods

The laboratory results of all AML cases diagnosed at the AUBMC between January 1, 2010 and January 1, 2024 were reviewed. Patients with CBF-AML (defined by the detection of t(8;21)(q22;q22.1) or inv(16)(p13q22)/t(16;16)(p13;q22) at diagnosis by any of RT-PCR, chromosome banding analysis, and/or Fluorescent in Situ Hybridization (FISH)) were selected. Patient information was obtained from electronic health records, covering demographic characteristics (age at diagnosis and gender), hematological parameters (complete blood count, bone marrow aspirate morphology and flow cytometry immunophenotyping), cytogenetic (karyotype, FISH) and molecular findings (RT-PCR and NGS), along with clinical data (past medical and surgical history, received treatment, and clinical progression including remission, relapse, and overall survival). HR-ACAs encompassed genetic lesions characterized as adverse-risk by the European Leukemia Net (ELN) 2022 classification to include 5q deletion, monosomy 7/7q deletion, 17p/TP53 deletion and complex karyotypes, defined as the presence of ≥ 3 cytogenetic abnormalities other than the CBF alteration [7].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS IBM statistics, United Kingdom) version 29 software. Descriptive analysis was done using the calculation of frequencies (%) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). To compare the biological characteristics and clinical outcomes of high-risk CBF, data were analyzed using the Chi-square test for categorical values and One-way Anova for the difference in means. A p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Among the 535 cases of AML diagnosed at the AUBMC during the study period, 71 (71/535 = 13%) were classified as CBF-AML [AML with t(8, 21): 37 cases (7%); AML with inv(16): 34 cases (6.5%)]. Secondary cytogenetic abnormalities were detected in 26 patients with CBF-AML (26/71 = 37%). As seen in Table 1, trisomy 22 constituted the most common abnormality in AML with inv(16), while loss of the sex chromosome most frequently accompanied AML with t(8;21). HR-ACAs were detected in 10 patients, accounting for 38% of patients with secondary anomalies (10/26), and 14% of all CBF-AML cases (10/71). Complex karyotypes, defined by the presence of ≥ 3 cytogenetic abnormalities other than inv(16)/t(8;21), were the most frequent HR-ACAs (Table 1).

Epidemiological characteristics

The male to female ratio was 1.4:1 among all CBF-AMLs, with a median age at diagnosis of 24 years (range: 3 to 76 years). Patients with inv(16) did not differ from those with t(8;21) in their gender distribution (p = 0.093) nor median age at diagnosis (p = 0.2). Similarly, when stratifying for HR-ACAs, no statistically significant difference was found in gender (p = 0.462) or median age (p = 0.89) (Table 2).

Laboratory parameters

The presence of HR-ACAs was found to correlate with complete blood count parameters, evidenced by a higher occurrence of leukopenia (median high-risk: 3000/cu.mm vs. non high-risk: 14150/cu.mm) and thrombocytopenia (median high-risk: 75000/cu.mm vs. non high-risk: 304000/cu.mm) at presentation (p < 0.05). Additionally, it associated with a lack of typical cytomorphology on bone marrow aspirate such as Auer rods and salmon-pink granulation for AML with t(8;21), and monocytic differentiation with abnormal eosinophils for AML with inv(16) (p < 0.001). The blast count at diagnosis was not significantly higher in patients with HR-ACAs. Cryptic (masked) inv(16) rearrangements were similarly not more prevalent in CBF-AML with HR-ACAs (Table 3). CD56 expression was not detected in any of the cases with high-risk cytogenetic abnormalities, suggesting that the observed outcomes were unlikely to be influenced by CD56 expression.

The mutational status of FLT3, NPM1, c-KIT and CEBPA was determined in only a subset of included participants. FLT3 and NPM1 were tested in 21 patients with AML inv(16) (21/34 = 62%), while c-KIT and CEBPA status were determined in 13 (13/34 = 38%). A FLT3 mutation (FLT3-D835) and mutant NPM1 were reported in one patient each. Two patients with AML inv(16) carried a c-KIT mutation, while all had wild-type CEBPA.

Among patients with AML t(8;21), FLT3 status was determined in 26 out of 37 individuals (70%), out of which 3 were positive (3/21 = 11%). NPM1 and CEBPA were tested in 14 (14/37 = 38%) and 9 (9/37 = 24%) patients respectively, none of whom were positive. c-KIT mutation testing was positive in one patient out of 10 tested (10/37 = 27%). This patient was not found to carry any additional cytogenetic abnormality with t(8;21).

Clinical characteristics

AML with t(8;21) versus AML with inv(16)

The mean follow-up period for all study participants was 7 years (range: 1–14 years). Patients with inv(16) had comparable relapse rates (28% vs. 17%; p = 0.9) and median OS (6.5 vs. 4 years; p = 0.593) to those harboring a t(8;21). By multivariate analysis, the presence of leukopenia at presentation was found to be associated with a lower overall survival (p < 0.001).

Non-high risk additional cytogenetic abnormalities

The presence of non-HR secondary cytogenetic abnormalities was not found to affect the clinical behavior of CBF-AML. As seen in Table 4, the rates of CR, relapse, use of HSCT and death was similar among patients with and without secondary abnormalities.

High-risk additional cytogenetic abnormalities

Pediatric patients were treated based on the AAML1031 protocol (Children oncology group) with ADE induction regimen (cytarabine, daunorubicin and etoposide). Among adult patients, induction therapy included cytarabine combined with an anthracycline, most commonly idarubicin (7 + 3 based regimens). Some patients received FLAG-based regimens, either at induction or relapse.

Although HR-ACAs were not found to impact CR (75% vs. 88%; p = 0.318), their detection negatively affected relapse rates (50% vs. 18%; p = 0.006), the requirement for HSCT (89% vs. 27%; p = 0.001), median overall survival (1.2 years vs. not reached), and death (63% vs. 17%; p = 0.008) (Table 3). Figure 1 depicts the survival curves analysis indicating a significant difference in leukemia-free survival between patients with and without HR-ACAs (p < 0.05). Multivariate analysis could not be performed due to the low number of patients.

Discussion

According to the World Health Organization (WHO), AML with inv(16) accounts for 5–8% of all AML diagnoses [3]. Of those, 45% harbor secondary cytogenetic abnormalities, including trisomy 22, trisomy 8, trisomy 21, and del(7q), in decreasing order of frequency [1, 8]. Concordantly, AML with inv(16) constituted 6% of our AML cohort, of which 33% exhibited secondary cytogenetic abnormalities. Trisomy 22, known to represent a cytogenetic clue for inv(16) [9], was also found to be the most common accompanying genetic anomaly. NPM1 mutations are generally regarded as mutually exclusive with inv(16). However, rare exceptions have been reported. Zidan et al. described a single case of concurrent NPM1 mutation in a cohort of 55 AML patients [10], and isolated case reports have documented the coexistence of NPM1 mutation with inv(16) [11]. In contrast, Falini et al. reported mutual exclusivity between these alterations, noting that the single NPM1-mutated case became negative upon repeat testing. In our study, limited by its retrospective design, repeat molecular testing was not feasible. The patient achieved complete remission, and subsequent molecular evaluations were negative, precluding definitive determination of whether the initial NPM1 result represented a true finding. These observations highlight the rarity of this coexistence and underscore the importance of cautious interpretation of molecular findings in retrospective cohorts.

Conversely, AML with t(8;21) is less frequent, accounting for 1–5% of AML cases [3]. Secondary cytogenetic abnormalities are found in up to 66%, most commonly involving loss of the sex chromosome and del(9q) [1]. Among our AML patients, the fraction of AML with t(8;21) was slightly higher (7%). The prevalence of t(8;21) in AML patients varies significantly across ethnicities, with Africans reported to have a frequency twice higher than White populations [12, 13]. The elevated prevalence has also been observed in Middle Eastern and Asian patients, with reported rates ranging between 11% and 12% in Omani, Saudi, and Moroccans, 17 to 20% in Japanese and Indianians, and reaching 27% in the Iranian cohort [14, 15].

Secondary cytogenetic rearrangements were described in 41% of our AML with t(8;21) cases, and the most frequent accompanying abnormality was -Y, even after adjustment for patient age.

In our cohort, HR-ACAs were detected in 1.8% of all AML diagnoses and 14% of CBF-AML. CBF-AML with HR-ACAs is reported to account for < 1% of AMLs [5, 6] and 6 to 23% of the CBF subgroup [1, 16, 17]. Because AUBMC is a tertiary care facility specializing in the treatment of hematopoietic neoplasms and serving as a referral center for HSCT in Lebanon, one can hypothesize that such patients are more frequently and selectively referred for specialized care.

Complex karyotypes appear to comprise the majority of HR-ACAs in CBF-AML, particularly in AML with inv(16) [1, 6, 16, 17]. The frequent cryptic nature of inv(16), combined with the presence of multiple chromosomal abnormalities, may result in the oversight of the CBFB::MYH11 fusion on karyotype. Furthermore, our data shows an association of HR-ACAs with atypical cytomorphology, further complicating the suspicion for AML with inv(16). To avoid misclassifying those cases, routine FISH testing using CBFB break-apart/dual fusion CBFB-MYH11 probes is recommended for every new AML diagnosis.

In a case series of AML with inv(16) and HR-ACAs, all described patients presented with leukopenia and thrombocytopenia [5]. Our study aligns with this observation extending to patients with t(8;21), suggesting a potential unique biology of this CBF-AML subgroup.

Adult patients with inv(16) and c-KIT mutations are found to have a higher risk of relapse and lower survival rates. This effect is more significant in AML with t(8;21) [18, 19]. The limited sample size and the retrospective nature of our study preclude making a robust analysis of the prevalence and consequences of point mutations in CBF AML with HR-ACAs.

The clinical significance of secondary cytogenetic abnormalities in CBF-AML is not yet clearly defined, resulting in suboptimal disease risk stratification and management [1, 4–6, 8, 16, 17]. HR-ACAs, such as monosomy 7/del(7q) and complex karyotypes, have been reported to adversely impact prognosis. Appelbaum et al. describe lower OS and CR rates in CBF cases with chromosome 7 abnormalities or complex karyotypes [17]. Although survival was also found to be affected by the detection of del(7q) in patients with t(8;21), it appeared to be independent from the presence of complex karyotypes (≥ 3 anomalies) in the multicentric cohort of Young Han et al. [1]. The varying definitions of a “complex karyotype” in CBF AML might account for these conflicting observations. In classical AML, the minimum number of cytogenetic abnormalities required to assign complexity ranges between 3 and 5 chromosomal aberrations [20]. Reports describing a negative impact of complex cytogenetics on the clinical behavior of CBF AML define complexity as the presence of ≥ 4 anomalies (CBF fusion + 3 aberrations) [16, 17]. Using this definition, we noted a trend toward poorer survival in our patient group, which will require confirmation in larger studies.

CBF-AML with HR-ACAs is rarely addressed as a separate entity in the literature. Particularly, HR-ACAs other than 7q abnormalities and complex karyotypes were not evaluated, possibly due to the limited patient number. A recent case series of AML with inv(16) and HR-ACAs highlighted the distinct laboratory and clinical characteristics of this group [5]. In agreement with newly emerging data, our results demonstrate an association between the presence of HR-ACAs and unfavorable clinical courses, with shorter leukemia-free survival.

The latest guidelines for AML management do not recommend HSCT as the first intention treatment of CBF-AML [7, 21]. The French and German AML intergroups do not show an advantage for transplantation as compared to chemotherapy, especially if repeated cycles of high-dose cytarabine are administered after complete remission (CR) [22–24]. Interestingly, our participants with HR-ACAs more frequently required allogeneic transplant, mainly as second line therapy after relapse. Several studies report consistent findings, with the increased number of chromosomal abnormalities [6], complex karyotypes [16], and TP53 deletion [25, 26] constituting a risk factor to HSCT implementation. Therefore, indications for therapy escalation in CBF-AML with HR-ACAs might need to be reevaluated.

The retrospective design of our study, resulting in incomplete/missing data, represents a limitation of the conducted analysis. In addition, the co-occurrence of inv(16)/t(8;21) and HR-ACAs is rare, explaining the small cohort size. Our long follow-up period, however, modulates these restrictions by providing a more comprehensive understanding of the outcomes, especially for patients who underwent transplantation.

Conclusion

In conclusion, CBF-AML with adverse cytogenetics appears to represent a biologically and clinically distinct subgroup with less favorable outcomes compared to classical CBF-AML. Further evaluation in larger, independent cohorts is warranted to determine if this subgroup merits separate classification, including risk stratification and management.
