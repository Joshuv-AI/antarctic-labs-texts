DEPACE-seq enables high-fidelity, single-nucleotide-resolution mapping of abasic sites and characterization of dynamic and long-lived AP landscapes in mammalian genomes
Authors: Hangrui Wu, Jiayu Wang, Chenxu Zhu, Xinyi Li, Tengyuan Zhang, Qifei Zeng, Meiping Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHigh-fidelity single-nucleotide-resolution mapping of abasic (AP) sites in mammalian genomes remains technically challenging due to low abundance and high background. Here we present DEPACE-seq (Dual-End PAB-Conjugated Endo IV-Cleaved Sequencing), a robust and easy-to-implement method for precise genome-wide profiling of AP sites. DEPACE-seq employs an N-pyrrolyl-alanine-2,2'-(ethylenedioxy)bis(ethylamine)-biotin (PAB) probe that selectively conjugates AP sites via a mild Pictet–Spengler reaction. The resulting PAB-AP adducts are efficiently and specifically cleaved by endonuclease IV, while remaining inert to other aldehyde-containing DNA bases. Independent library construction from both cleavage ends and the intersection of the resulting signals enable high-confidence identification of AP sites at single-nucleotide resolution. Application of DEPACE-seq across different cell types and damage conditions enabled high-confidence characterization of two AP-site populations: long-lived sites enriched in satellite and intergenic regions, and repair-intermediate sites induced by transient damage and enriched in transcriptionally active regions, both exhibiting cell-type-dependent features. Molecular dynamics simulations further elucidate how endonuclease IV accommodates bulky PAB adducts. Together, DEPACE-seq provides a robust platform for high-confidence, single-nucleotide-resolution mapping of AP sites in mammalian genomes, enabling systematic investigations of genome instability, DNA repair, aging, and cancer.

Introduction

Genomic DNA is constantly challenged by a wide range of endogenous and exogenous damaging agents [1, 2]. The accumulation of DNA lesions has been causally linked to aging [3, 4], cancer [5, 6], cardiovascular diseases [7], and neurodegenerative disorders [8]. Among diverse forms of DNA damage, abasic (apurinic/apyrimidinic, AP) sites are among the most abundant lesions [9–11]. They arise from spontaneous hydrolysis of the N-glycosidic bond, a process estimated to generate up to ∼10 000 AP sites per mammalian cell per day [12, 13]. AP sites are further induced by reactive oxygen species, alkylating agents, ultraviolet irradiation, and the action of DNA glycosylases during base excision repair (BER) [14–16]. As central intermediates of BER [17–21], AP sites are intrinsically mutagenic due to loss of base information, causing replication and transcriptional stalling and error-prone bypass by translesion synthesis polymerases [22–25]. Emerging evidence further suggests that AP sites may participate in transcriptional regulation, underscoring their multifaceted biological roles [26, 27].

Biophysical studies have shown that >99% of natural AP sites exist in a cyclic hemiacetal form, with only a minor fraction adopting the reactive acyclic aldehyde conformation [28–31]. These sites can form covalent DNA–protein crosslinks [32] and DNA–DNA crosslinks [33–36], with profound biological consequences. In particular, the 5-hydroxymethylcytosine binding, ES cell specific (HMCES) protein forms stable thiazolidine adducts with AP sites via its SRAP domain, shielding them from mutagenic polymerases [37–40]. In contrast, AP-derived interstrand crosslinks are highly cytotoxic, inducing replication stress, double-strand breaks, and genome instability [33–36, 41–44]. Persistent or misprocessed AP sites therefore represent a major threat to genome integrity and are implicated in disease progression [45, 46].

Given their abundance and functional importance, mapping AP sites across the genome is essential for understanding DNA damage landscapes in aging and disease [47–49]. However, genome-wide, single-nucleotide-resolution mapping of AP sites remains technically challenging [50–52]. The aldehyde reactive probe (ARP) approach labels AP sites through oxime formation but is limited to ∼250 bp resolution and suffers from interference by endogenous aldehyde-containing bases such as 5-formylcytosine (5fC) and 5-formyluracil (5fU), which are more abundant than AP sites [53–55]. Hydrazine-based probes yield labile hydrazone adducts and often require acidic or catalytic conditions that can destabilize DNA or introduce artificial lesions.

Several sequencing-based strategies have sought to overcome these limitations. snAP-seq achieved single-nucleotide resolution while reducing 5fC interference, but requires complex probe synthesis, harsh cleavage conditions, additional processing to eliminate 5fU signals, and shows low recovery (∼10%) [51]. Nick-seq improves positional assignment through complementary end-mapping strategies but does not enrich AP-containing fragments, requiring extensive computational filtering to distinguish true lesions from background signals. To date, its applications have mainly been demonstrated in prokaryotic genomes [52]. SSiNGLe-AP employs enzymatic blocking of endogenous breaks followed by APE1 cleavage of AP sites, but may fail to capture lesions associated with obstructive 3′ termini and remains susceptible to background signals arising from endogenous DNA breaks [56]. A dual chemical labeling-assisted sequencing (DCL-seq) strategy improved enrichment specificity but relies on base-conversion chemistry, which may complicate the identification of AP sites originating from cytosine [57]. Very recently, click-code-seq enabled single-nucleotide-resolution mapping of oxidative lesions and depurination in the human genome. However, its reliance on copper-catalyzed click chemistry during library preparation may introduce background DNA damage, and the efficiency of lesion labeling may be influenced by the accessibility of alkyne-tagged lesions within duplex DNA [58].

Indeed, AP sites occur at extremely low frequency in genomic DNA (∼0.9 per 10⁶ bases) [53–55], such that their signals are easily overwhelmed by intact background DNA. Achieving high-confidence localization therefore requires two essential elements: (i) highly specific recognition or cleavage of AP sites, and (ii) efficient removal of non-AP-containing DNA prior to sequencing. Failure to address either challenge at early stages propagates background noise into downstream analyses, analogous to the challenges encountered in ultra-rare mutation detection.

In preliminary studies, we observed that Escherichia coli Endonuclease IV (Endo IV), retains high specificity toward AP sites even after chemical labeling with flexible tags. Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations revealed that Endo IV’s spacious β-barrel architecture accommodates bulky adducts while preserving the zinc-dependent catalytic geometry, enabling efficient cleavage through a structurally adaptive, noncanonical mechanism. In contrast, human APE1 exhibited strong steric sensitivity toward labeled substrates. This unique property of Endo IV suggested a strategy that combines chemical enrichment with precise enzymatic cleavage to meet both requirements for high-fidelity AP mapping.

Building on these insights, we synthesized an N-pyrrolyl-alanine-2,2'-(ethylenedioxy)bis(ethylamine)-biotin (PAB) probe that rapidly and selectively conjugates AP sites via a mild Pictet–Spengler reaction, enabling efficient separation of AP-containing DNA from complex genomic backgrounds. Importantly, PAB-modified AP sites remain excellent substrates for Endo IV, generating clean 3′-OH termini suitable for sequencing. By constructing libraries from both cleavage ends and intersecting complementary signals, we achieve single-nucleotide-resolved, high-confidence AP maps. This method, termed DEPACE-seq (Dual-End PAB-Conjugated Endo IV-Cleaved Sequencing), enables robust profiling of genomic AP sites in mammalian cells with a streamlined workflow, high recovery, and minimal background. Together, our biochemical, computational, and genomic analyses establish DEPACE-seq as a broadly applicable platform for decoding abasic damage and its repair dynamics in complex genomes.

Materials and methods

Synthesis of N-pyrrolyl-alanine--2,2'-(ethylenedioxy)bis(ethylamine)-biotin (PAB, Compound 5)

To synthesize Compound 8, Fmoc-Dap-OH (Compound 6, 0.51 g, 1.56 mmol) was dissolved in 30 ml of ice-cold acetic acid. 2,5-Dimethoxytetrahydrofuran (Compound 7, 0.24 g, 1.82 mmol) was then added, and the solution was heated to reflux (110°C) under nitrogen. After 6–10 min, the reaction mixture turned dark green and was quenched on ice. The mixture was extracted with ethyl acetate (120 ml) and washed with saturated sodium bicarbonate solution (4 × 60 ml). The organic phase was dried over anhydrous sodium sulfate, filtered, and concentrated under reduced pressure to yield a yellow oil. Purification by silica gel chromatography (dichloromethane:methanol = 10:1) gave Compound 8 as a light yellow solid (150 mg, 26% yield).

To synthesize Compound 9, Compound 8 (50 mg, 0.134 mmol) was dissolved in 2 ml of dry N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF) at 0°C under nitrogen. N,N-diisopropylethylamine (DIPEA) (60 μl, 0.345 mmol) was added, and after 10 min of stirring, trifluoroacetic anhydride pentafluorophenyl ester (50 μl, 0.292 mmol) was introduced. The reaction was warmed to room temperature and stirred for 1.5 h. The reaction was quenched with saturated brine (15 ml) and extracted with ethyl acetate (3 × 30 ml). The organic phase was dried, filtered, and concentrated. The crude product was purified by column chromatography (hexane:ethyl acetate = 4:1) to give Compound 9 as a white solid (30 mg, 41% yield).

To synthesize Compound 5 (PAB), Compound 9 (30 mg, 0.056 mmol) was dissolved in 0.5 ml of dry DMF. Biotin-PEG2-NH2 (Compound 10, 20 mg, 0.054 mmol) and DIPEA (24 μl, 0.134 mmol) were added, and the mixture was stirred at room temperature for 1 h. Diethylamine (150 μl) was then added, and the reaction continued for another 15 min. The reaction mixture was diluted with deionized water, filtered, and concentrated under reduced pressure. The resulting yellow oil was purified by reverse-phase high performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) using a C18 column (Supplementary Table S3), and the target product was detected at 215 nm with a retention time of 9–10 min. The eluent was collected and concentrated to obtain Compound 5 (PAB probe) as a colorless oil.

Chemical labeling of AP sites in DNA strands with different small molecules

For the preparation of DNA strands containing AP sites, 5 μl of 10 × UDG buffer (Supplementary Table S2) was added to a 200 μl polymerase chain reaction (PCR) tube, along with single-stranded DNA containing uracil or hybridized DNA double-strands (up to 2.5 μg per tube) and 5 U of uracil-DNA glycosylase (UDG). The volume was then adjusted to 50 μl using DNase/RNase-free deionized water and mixed thoroughly. The mixture was incubated at 37°C for 1 h, followed by purification using an oligonucleotide purification kit (ZYMO) to obtain DNA strands containing AP sites in DNase/RNase-free deionized water or 1 × Tris–EDTA (TE) buffer. The concentration of purified AP site-containing DNA was measured using NanoDrop. The purified DNA strands containing AP sites can be used directly for subsequent experiments or stored at −20°C.

To perform the labeling reaction, 4 μl of 10 × phosphate buffered saline (PBS) buffer (pH 7.4) was added to a 200 μl PCR tube, along with the appropriate amount of DNA strands containing AP sites and different concentrations of small molecules. The volume was adjusted to 20 μl using DNase/RNase-free deionized water and mixed thoroughly. The reaction was incubated at room temperature for at least 2 h, followed by purification using an oligonucleotide purification kit (ZYMO) to obtain the labeled DNA strands in DNase/RNase-free deionized water or 1 × TE buffer. The concentration of purified DNA was measured using NanoDrop. The purified DNA can be used directly for subsequent experiments or stored at −20°C.

Capture and enrichment of the PAB-labeled DNA strands

In a 200 μl PCR tube, add 4 μl of 10 × PBS buffer (pH 7.4), an appropriate amount of double-stranded DNA, and 2 μl of PAB probe (100 mM). Bring the volume up to 20 μl using DNase/RNase-free deionized water and mix well. Allow the reaction to proceed at room temperature for 8 h or overnight. Then, purify the reaction products using an oligonucleotide purification kit (ZYMO) to obtain DNA in DNase/RNase-free deionized water or 10 mM Tris–HCl buffer (pH 7.5). In a 200 μl PCR tube, add 5 μl of 10 × Thermo Endonuclease IV buffer (see Supplementary Table S2 for composition), the purified double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) after labeling with the small molecule probe, and 2 U of Thermo Endonuclease IV. Mix well and incubate at 37°C for 30 min. For comparison, 10 μl of ARP probe (20 mM) was utilized to replace the 2 μl of PAB probe (100 mM) used in the above-mentioned steps.

Vortex the streptavidin-coated magnetic beads from MedChemExpress (MCE) for 15 s on a vortex mixer. In a 200 μl PCR tube, add 20 μl of the well-mixed magnetic bead suspension, 50 μl of 2 × Buffer I, vortex for 15 s, and separate the beads using a magnetic separator. Add 50 μl of 2 × Buffer I and repeat the previous step once. Then, add 50 μl of 2 × Buffer I and 2 μg of poly dI:dC (Thermo) to the PCR tube, vortex for 15 s to resuspend the magnetic beads, followed by the addition of the previous Endonuclease IV digestion mixture. Vortex for 15 s to mix well, place the magnetic bead suspension on a vertical mixer, and incubate at room temperature for 30 min. Separate the beads using a magnetic separator to remove the supernatant. In the PCR tube, add 100 μl of 1 × Buffer I to wash the magnetic beads six times. Add 50 μl of 0.1 M NaOH solution and incubate at room temperature for 10 min. Collect the supernatant by magnetic separation, add 5 μl of 1 M HCl to neutralize, and then purify the supernatant using an oligonucleotide purification kit (ZYMO) to obtain DNA in 20 μl of 10 mM Tris–HCl buffer (pH 7.5). The purified DNA can be directly used for subsequent experiments or stored at −20°C.

To explore the labeling and enrichment efficiency of AP sites within specific secondary structures, we designed a G-quadruplex DNA sequence containing AP sites (AP-G4, Supplementary Table S1). This sequence was annealed in a solution containing 100 mM potassium ions to form the G-quadruplex structure. AP sites were generated by treating the DNA with UDG, followed by an 8-h reaction with 10 mM PAB probe. The reaction product was then purified and incubated with streptavidin-coated magnetic beads. Elution was performed at 65°C on a shaker set to 1500 revolutions per minute, using 98% formamide and 1 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) as the elution buffer. Finally, 2.5% agarose gel electrophoresis was used to analyze both the magnetic bead supernatant and the elution.

Experimental workflow of DEPACE-Seq

Fragmentation of genomic DNA and PAB-treatment. Genomic DNA was sheared to 200–500 bp using a Qsonica Q800R3 sonicator. Sonication was performed using a 0.5 ml probe at 50% amplitude, with a pulse mode of 20 s on and 40 s off, for a total duration of 16 min. The fragmented DNA (50 μl total volume) was incubated with PAB (2 mM final) in 4 × PBS (pH 7.2–7.4) overnight at room temperature (20–25°C). The reaction was purified with a Zymo-Spin IC column (ZYMO) and eluted in 0.1 × TE buffer.

Bioinformatics analysis

Precise localization of AP sites

Next-generation sequencing (NGS) reads were used to localize apurinic/apyrimidinic (AP) sites in spike-in controls and genomic DNA, respectively, using raw reads for spike-ins and quality-filtered reads for genomic samples. Paired-end FASTQ files were trimmed with Cutadapt (v4.1). Trimmed reads were aligned to the hg19/GRCh37 reference genome with BWA-MEM (v0.7.17), and the resulting alignments were coordinate-sorted and indexed with SAMtools (v1.6). Unless otherwise noted, default parameters were used.

Coordinate-sorted BAM files were further processed using SAMtools (v1.6). Reads were first separated by read number, corresponding to read 1 (SAM flag 64) and read 2 (SAM flag 128), and then stratified by the mapping strand. Forward-strand reads were defined as alignments without SAM flag 16, whereas reverse-strand reads were defined as alignments carrying SAM flag 16. To minimize positional ambiguity introduced by soft clipping at the cleavage-proximal end, alignments were filtered based on their CIGAR strings, with reads on the forward strand excluded if the CIGAR string started with soft clipping ($6 !∼ /^[0–9]+S/) and reads on the reverse strand excluded if the CIGAR string ended with soft clipping ($6 !∼ /S$/).

Filtered read 1 alignments were used to infer AP-site positions, and candidate AP-site coordinates were generated using custom scripts. Assuming 0-based half-open coordinates, candidate AP-site positions were inferred from the cleavage-proximal ends of filtered read 1 alignments. For the Left library, the start coordinate of forward-strand alignments was extracted; the AP site was assigned to the nucleotide immediately upstream of this coordinate and placed on the negative strand of the reference genome. For Left-library reverse-strand alignments, the end −1 coordinate (the last aligned nucleotide) was used; the AP site was assigned to the nucleotide immediately downstream of this coordinate and placed on the positive strand. For the Right library, the start coordinate of forward-strand alignments was used to assign the AP site to the nucleotide immediately upstream on the positive strand, whereas the end −1 coordinate of reverse-strand alignments was used to assign the AP site to the nucleotide immediately downstream on the negative strand. Finally, single-nucleotide-resolved AP sites were obtained by intersecting candidate coordinates from complementary library/strand combinations: The intersection of Left-forward and Right-reverse candidates yielded AP sites on the negative strand, whereas the intersection of Left-reverse and Right-forward candidates yielded AP sites on the positive strand.

When only the Left library was available, single-nucleotide-resolved putative AP sites were obtained as follows. Trimmed reads were aligned to the hg19/GRCh37 reference genome with BWA-MEM (v0.7.17). PCR duplicates were marked with Picard MarkDuplicates (v3.0.0) and removed with SAMtools (v1.6). Coordinate-sorted BAM files were partitioned by read (read 1 versus read 2) and by strand (forward versus reverse) using SAMtools and filtered on the CIGAR string as above; read 1 was retained for AP-site localization. Candidate coordinates were generated with custom scripts by extracting, from the Left library, (i) the start coordinate of forward-strand alignments—assigning the AP site to the immediately upstream nucleotide on the negative strand, and (ii) the end −1 coordinate of reverse-strand alignments—assigning the AP site to the immediately downstream nucleotide on the positive strand. Sites supported by ≥2 reads were retained and designated putative AP sites. These calls were used to complement intersection-derived sites from paired Left/Right libraries and were also analyzed separately, as they capture base-composition preferences and genomic distribution of AP sites.

All-atom MD simulations

The initial atomic structure of the complex, consisting of Endonuclease IV, a 9-bp-dsDNA containing an abasic (AP) site and three Zn²⁺ ions at the active site, was obtained through AlphaFold3. The predicted structure was aligned with the high-resolution x-ray crystal structure (PDB ID: 2NQ9) to validate its accuracy. The AP site was manually edited into different labeled structures, and force field parameters for the labeled groups were calculated using Gaussian 16 and Multiwfn 3.8 [59].

All-atom MD simulations were performed using the GROMACS 2018.8 package [60], employing the Amberff14SB force field for proteins [61] and parmbsc1 for DNA molecules [62]. Each protein–DNA complex was placed in an octahedral box and solvated with TIP3P water model [63], ensuring a minimum distance of 10 Å from the protein to the edge of the periodic box. The system was neutralized by adding potassium cations, and additional K+Cl− ion pairs were introduced to achieve a physiological salt concentration of 0.15 M. Long-range electrostatic interactions were treated using the particle mesh Ewald method [64] with a real-space cutoff of 10 Å, and the hydrogen bond lengths were constrained using the P-LINCS algorithm [65]. Periodic boundary conditions were applied from this step onward.

Before the MD production, energy minimization and equilibration were conducted. The system was first energy-minimized using the steepest descent algorithm to relax high-energy contacts. After minimization, the system underwent equilibration under the canonical (NVT) ensemble for 10 ps, followed by the isothermal–isobaric (NPT) ensemble for 100 ps, using Bussi velocity-rescaling thermostat [66] (τT = 0.1 ps) for temperature coupling and the Parrinello–Rahman barostat [67] (τP = 2.0 ps) for pressure regulation, respectively. During minimization and heating, heavy atoms and the protein backbone were restrained using positional restraints, which were then released for the production MD. Each production run lasted 200 ns and was repeated three times independently, maintaining a temperature of 300 K and a pressure of 1 bar. A total of 60 000 conformational snapshots (stored every 10 ps) were collected for each system and were used for analysis. The system’s dynamics and stability, including root-mean-square deviation, root-mean-square-fluctuations, hydrogen bond and atom distances were analyzed using built-in GROMACS tools.

Channel cross-sectional area analysis

The pore dimensions of the two protein channels were analyzed using the HOLE software suite (version 2.2.005). To define the axis of each channel, a starting point (cpoint) within the pore and a directional vector (cvect) along the channel axis were specified based on structural inspection of the β-barrel region. Van der Waals radii were assigned using the provided simple.rad file. The sampling step size was set to 0.1 Å to ensure high-resolution profiling along the pore axis. Solvent molecules were excluded from the calculations using the ignore command. The resulting radius profiles were plotted to visualize the variation of the channel cross-sectional area along the pore axis, allowing direct comparison between the two proteins.

Results

Endo IV-enabled selective and quantitative conversion of chemically labeled abasic sites

To compare how different AP endonucleases process chemically modified abasic sites, we first constructed a 49-mer hairpin DNA containing a single synthetic AP site (AP-hp1) (Fig. 1A) and labeled it with three representative small molecules: O-2-propynylhydroxylamine (2-PHA), O-benzylhydroxylamine (Bz-HA), and N-aminoethylpyrrole (PEA) (Fig. 1B and Supplementary Figs S1–S3). We then evaluated the cleavage activities of human APE1, Endo IV, and T4 pyrimidine dimer DNA glycosylase (T4-PDG) on these modified substrates.

APE1 efficiently cleaved the unmodified AP site but showed only weak activity toward the 2-PHA-labeled AP site and virtually no activity toward Bz-HA- or PEA-labeled AP sites (Fig. 1C). T4 PDG activity was strongly inhibited by hydroxylamine-modified AP sites, consistent with steric interference with its β-elimination mechanism. In striking contrast, Endo IV retained robust cleavage activity toward all hydroxylamine-labeled AP sites and showed substantial activity toward the PEA-labeled AP site. The identities of Endo IV cleavage products were confirmed by liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC–MS) (Supplementary Fig. S4). Notably, the complete resistance of PEA-labeled AP sites to APE1 cleavage indirectly confirms the high conversion efficiency of the Pictet–Spengler labeling reaction, a feature later exploited for distinguishing labeled from unlabeled AP sites.

We next examined cleavage kinetics using a fluorescently labeled AP-containing substrate (AP-hp2) (Fig. 1D). Consistent with the gel-based results, Endo IV rapidly cleaved both unlabeled AP-hp2 and hydroxylamine-labeled AP sites, while cleavage of the PEA-labeled substrate proceeded more slowly but still reached a substantial extent (Fig. 1E and Supplementary Fig. S5). In contrast, APE1 efficiently cleaved only unlabeled AP-hp2 and exhibited minimal or undetectable activity toward chemically labeled AP sites, particularly those modified with Bz-HA or PEA (Supplementary Fig. S6). Together, these results indicate that PEA-based Pictet–Spengler modification produces AP adducts that are highly stable and selectively resistant to APE1 while remaining competent substrates for Endo IV.

Guided by these observations, we synthesized a biotinylated pyrrolyl alanine derivative, PAB (Fig. 1F), to enable selective labeling and enrichment of AP-containing DNA fragments from complex genomic backgrounds. Structural integrity and purity of PAB were validated by LC–MS and 1H nuclear magnetic resonance (1H NMR) and 13C NMR (Supplementary Table S4 and Supplementary Figs S7–S9). We monitored PAB labeling efficiency using an APE1-based fluorescence assay, which reports residual unlabeled AP sites. PAB labeling proceeded most efficiently under near-neutral conditions, achieving near-quantitative conversion at optimized concentrations, and outperformed the commonly used ARP under identical conditions (Fig. 1G and Supplementary Figs S10 and S11). Importantly, PAB labeling was also highly efficient for AP sites embedded within G-quadruplex DNA structures. Using an AP-containing G4 substrate (Supplementary Table S1), we observed nearly complete capture of labeled DNA by streptavidin magnetic beads, with negligible AP-containing DNA remaining in the supernatant (Fig. 1H). These results demonstrate that PAB enables efficient, stable, and structure-tolerant labeling of AP sites, providing a robust foundation for genome-wide AP site enrichment and high-fidelity mapping.

To further confirm the quantitative cleavage efficiency of Endo IV toward PAB-labeled AP sites and to assess whether this activity is influenced by sequence context—particularly by the identity of the base immediately 5′ to an AP site [68]—we prepared a panel of 25-nt dsDNAs containing a single AP site flanked by different 5′ bases (Supplementary Fig. S12). Following PAB labeling, all substrates were efficiently and completely cleaved by Endo IV within 30 min, irrespective of the 5′ base identity. These results indicate that PAB conjugation eliminates sequence-dependent cleavage bias and enables quantitative conversion of AP sites (Fig. 2A and Supplementary Fig. S12).

We next assessed the specificity of PAB labeling and Endo IV cleavage relative to other endogenous aldehyde-containing lesions. While 5fU showed partial reactivity with PAB and 5fC was essentially inert, neither lesion nor their PAB-labeled forms were cleaved by Endo IV (Fig. 2B and Supplementary Fig. S13). These results demonstrate that Endo IV retains strict specificity for AP sites following PAB tagging, ensuring single-nucleotide fidelity.

Based on these properties, we established DEPACE-seq for high-resolution AP site mapping (Fig. 2C). In this workflow, genomic DNA is fragmented, AP sites are selectively labeled with PAB, and all pre-existing 3′ ends are blocked prior to Endo IV cleavage. Endo IV selectively generates strand breaks at labeled AP sites, producing free 3′-OH termini that enable adaptor ligation and enrichment of AP-containing fragments. Dual-end library construction yields complementary Left and Right libraries, whose intersecting read termini define AP positions at single-nucleotide resolution.

To evaluate the performance of DEPACE-seq, we synthesized four model double-stranded DNA substrates containing either an AP site, 5fU, 5fC, or no DNA lesion, designated AP-99-ds, 5fU-99-ds, 5fC-99-ds, and GATC-99-ds (undamaged control), respectively. Sequencing of the AP-containing model substrate (AP-99-ds) confirmed precise localization of the AP site, with sequencing reads terminating immediately adjacent to the lesion (Fig. 2D). Equal amounts of AP-99-ds, 5fC-99-ds, 5fU-99-ds, and GATC-99-ds were then mixed to generate spike-in model substrates and subjected to the DEPACE-seq workflow. DEPACE-seq recovered approximately 90% of the AP-containing DNA while exhibiting strong discrimination against the 5fC-, 5fU-, and undamaged control substrates (Supplementary Fig. S14), demonstrating near-quantitative recovery of AP-containing sequences (Fig. 2E).

To assess the ability of DEPACE-seq to recover low-abundance AP sites from DNA samples with a high background, we prepared a model sample by spiking AP-99-ds into GATC-99-ds at an approximate AP-site abundance of 1:104. As shown in Supplementary Fig. S15, quantitative PCR analysis of the two sequential eluates used to generate the Left-Part and Right-Part libraries showed nearly identical DNA amounts, with each eluate containing approximately 50% of the input AP-containing DNA. These results indicate that, even in the presence of an overwhelming excess of undamaged DNA, a single AP site can generate balanced Left-Part and Right-Part libraries.

To further validate the quantitative recovery of AP-containing DNA by the Left-Part and Right-Part libraries in a complex genomic DNA background, two newly synthesized AP-containing duplex DNA substrates, ds-HCoV-HKU1-AP-79 and ds-IB-AP-82, were designed to mimic the Left-Part and Right-Part libraries, respectively. Equal amounts of the two substrates (70 ng each) were spiked into 1.3 μg of human genomic DNA and processed through the DEPACE-seq workflow. Using ds-HCoV-HKU1-AP-79 and ds-IB-AP-82 as quantitative reference standards for the Left-Part and Right-Part libraries, respectively, we determined recovery efficiencies of ∼80% and ∼85% (Fig. 2F). These results demonstrate that both library construction procedures efficiently recover AP-containing DNA even in the presence of excess genomic DNA, indicating that the dual-end intersection strategy does not substantially compromise the sensitivity of DEPACE-seq.

To examine the fidelity of DEPACE-seq in detecting AP-site signals, we performed a stringent blocking control experiment. Genomic DNA extracted from HeLa cells under three conditions—normal culture, methyl methanesulfonate (MMS) treatment for 1 h (MMS-1h), and 2 h recovery after MMS exposure (MMS-1h-repair-2h)—was split into two aliquots. One aliquot was pretreated with N-benzylhydroxylamine and (1H-benzo[d]imidazol-2-yl)methanamine to block all AP sites, while the other was left untreated. Both samples were processed in parallel using the DEPACE-seq workflow. As shown in Fig. 2G, AP-blocked samples exhibited nearly abolished signals compared with untreated controls, confirming the high specificity and low background of DEPACE-seq.

We further validated the specific contribution of the dual-end library construction strategy to AP-site detection fidelity by directly comparing single-end and dual-end library construction workflows. As shown in Supplementary Fig. S16A, when AP sites were present at very low abundance in the presence of an overwhelming excess of undamaged DNA, the single-end sequencing strategy generated substantial background signals. Furthermore, analysis of AP-blocked genomic DNA samples demonstrated that the single-end workflow still produced numerous nonspecific AP-site signals, whereas the dual-end intersection strategy implemented in DEPACE-seq almost eliminated these nonspecific signals (Supplementary Fig. S16B). Together, these results demonstrate that the dual-end intersection strategy is the key determinant of the high specificity and fidelity of DEPACE-seq, establishing it as a robust and reliable platform for single-nucleotide-resolution mapping of AP sites in complex genomic DNA samples.

High-fidelity, condition-resolved mapping of genomic AP sites

We applied DEPACE-seq to map endogenous AP sites in HeLa cells under normal culture conditions at single-nucleotide resolution. Genome-wide profiling revealed widespread AP-site distribution on both DNA strands (Fig. 3A). The comparable global densities on forward and reverse strands indicate that endogenous AP formation is largely strand-symmetric, supporting the robustness of the dual-end intersection strategy. Visualization of base-resolved AP sites across a representative region of chromosome 1 (47–53 Mb) demonstrated heterogeneous and nonrandom lesion patterns at single-nucleotide resolution, with each AP site unambiguously assigned to its originating base (Fig. 3B).

To investigate damage-induced AP formation, HeLa cells were treated with MMS or tert-butyl hydroperoxide (t-BHP), followed by DEPACE-seq analysis. Both treatments substantially increased genome-wide AP-site density (Supplementary Figs S17 and S18). Analysis of base-of-origin composition revealed that, under normal conditions, guanine-derived AP sites predominated, followed by adenine, with thymine and cytosine contributing minimally (Fig. 3C and Supplementary Fig. S19). MMS treatment preserved guanine dominance (Fig. 3D and E), whereas t-BHP treatment markedly increased the contribution of adenine-derived AP sites (Fig. 3G and Supplementary Fig. S19). Sequence-context analysis showed that guanine-derived AP sites were strongly enriched for guanine at the 5′ flanking position, whereas adenine-derived AP sites displayed no obvious neighboring-base preference (Fig. 3C–F). These patterns likely reflect the intrinsic susceptibility of guanine to oxidative and alkylation damage and highlight damage-specific sequence signatures associated with AP-site formation and repair.

Genome-wide annotation of AP sites demonstrated a nonrandom distribution across genomic features in HeLa cells, consistent with previous AP-site mapping studies [56]. Under normal culture conditions, AP-site signals were relatively enriched in coding sequences (CDSs), with pronounced accumulation in exons, promoters, and regions flanking TSSs and transcription end sites (TESs) (Fig. 2G). In contrast, AP sites were comparatively depleted in 5′ and 3′ untranslated regions (UTRs), introns, gene bodies, and intergenic regions. These patterns indicate a preferential localization of endogenous AP sites within regulatory and transcription-associated chromatin.

Exposure to MMS caused a marked global increase in AP-site abundance (Supplementary Fig. S17), yet the relative distribution across genomic elements remained largely unchanged (Fig. 3G). After 1 h MMS treatment followed by 2 h recovery, AP sites in CDS increased further, accompanied by strong enrichment in exons, promoters, and TSS-proximal regions, whereas the odds ratio of AP sites in intergenic regions decreased during recovery, indicating a reduced relative enrichment compared with other genomic features. Single-nucleotide-resolution profiling of AP-site densities within ±6 kb of TSSs further resolved these trends (Fig. 3H and I, and Supplementary Fig. S20). AP sites exhibited a striking positional bias, with density increasing sharply toward the TSS under both normal and MMS-treated conditions.

In comparison, tert-butyl hydroperoxide (tBHP) treatment produced a distinct AP-site landscape (Supplementary Figs S18 and S21). Under oxidative stress, AP sites were relatively enriched in intergenic regions, whereas the relative enrichment in transcription-associated regions was reduced. This pattern persisted throughout the 2 h recovery period. This redistribution is likely attributable to oxidative stress–induced upregulation of APE1, which promotes more efficient repair of lesions in transcriptionally active regions, while intergenic regions—repaired less efficiently—retain higher AP-site burdens.

AP-site landscapes also differed strikingly across repetitive genomic elements. Under basal conditions, AP sites were strongly enriched in satellite repeats, relatively sparse in long interspersed nuclear elements (LINEs) and short interspersed nuclear elements (SINEs), and depleted in low-complexity regions (Fig. 3J). MMS treatment markedly attenuated the enrichment of AP sites in satellite regions, accompanied by a modest increase in long terminal repeats (LTRs). Following recovery, satellite-associated AP-site enrichment decreased further, whereas enrichment in LTRs increased further. In contrast, tBHP treatment induced pronounced accumulation of AP sites in satellite DNA together with a concomitant reduction in LTRs, with minimal redistribution during the recovery phase. Across all conditions, satellite repeats consistently exhibited the highest degree of AP-site enrichment, whereas LINEs and SINEs remained comparatively underrepresented.

Correlation analysis with chromatin states further supported these findings (Fig. 3K). Under normal conditions, AP-site distributions exhibited stronger correlations with active histone marks H3K9ac, H3K27ac, and H3K4me3. These correlations slightly decreased after MMS treatment but rebounded during recovery. In contrast, repressive marks H3K9me3 and H3K27me3 consistently showed weak correlations with AP sites across all conditions. These results indicate that AP sites are more likely to occur in open, transcriptionally active chromatin regions rather than in dense, transcriptionally silent regions. MMS-induced alkylation damage, which is likely more randomly distributed, may obscure this intrinsic association, while repair during recovery preferentially restores the coupling with active chromatin.

Resolving stable AP sites from transient repair intermediates across cell types

APE1 is the principal enzyme responsible for repairing AP sites in human cells. To investigate how AP sites are distributed across the genome when APE1-dependent repair is blocked, we performed a comparative analysis using APE1-knockout (APE1-KO) TE-1 cells. Under normal culture conditions, the base-origin distribution of AP sites in both wild-type (WT) and APE1-KO TE-1 cells differed from that in HeLa cells (Fig. 4A and D versus Fig. 3C), with adenine being the dominant source of AP sites, followed by guanine—a pattern resembling tBHP-treated HeLa cells (Fig. 3F). This preference was largely maintained after tBHP treatment (Fig. 4C), whereas MMS exposure markedly increased guanine-derived AP sites (Fig. 4B). Flanking-sequence analysis revealed similar nucleotide preferences in WT and APE1-KO cells, characterized by guanine enrichment immediately upstream of AP sites and thymine enrichment at the second upstream position, consistent with HeLa cells.

In WT TE-1 cells, AP sites were relatively enriched across multiple genomic features, including 3′ UTRs, exons, CDSs, promoters, and regions flanking TSSs and TESs (Fig. 4E). In contrast, APE1-KO cells exhibited an almost random distribution of AP sites across most genomic elements, with the notable exception of a pronounced depletion in 5′ UTRs. As 5′ UTRs are among the guanine–cytosine (GC)-richest regions of the genome, guanine bases in these regions are particularly susceptible to oxidative damage. Because OGG1-mediated excision of oxidized guanine and subsequent AP-site generation critically depend on downstream APE1 activity, we speculate that loss of APE1 slows OGG1 turnover, thereby reducing the rate of AP-site formation in these regions.

In both WT and APE1-KO TE-1 cells, AP sites were enriched in satellite regions and depleted in low-complexity regions, LINEs, and SINEs (Fig. 4G), with APE1-KO cells showing substantially stronger enrichment in satellite DNA than WT cells. Following MMS or tBHP treatment (Fig. 4H), AP-site enrichment in satellite regions increased in WT TE-1 cells under both conditions. During the recovery phase, satellite-associated AP enrichment declined markedly after MMS treatment, whereas little change was observed after tBHP exposure. AP-site enrichment in the vicinity of TSSs was observed in both cell lines (Supplementary Fig. S22).

Comparison of WT TE-1 and HeLa cells revealed a substantial set of shared AP sites that remained stable across the two cell types, supporting the presence of long-lived AP-site populations in the genome. More than 95% of these shared AP sites were located in intergenic regions (Fig. 4I), with a small fraction in introns and only a negligible proportion in promoters, exons, or regions proximal to TSSs. Within repetitive elements, over 99% of these stable AP sites resided in satellite regions, with only trace levels detected in LINEs and simple repeats (Fig. 4J).

Mechanistic insights into Endo IV’s interaction with chemically modified AP sites

Our observations indicate that Endo IV retains substantial recognition and cleavage activity toward chemically modified AP sites, in stark contrast to the behavior of APE1. To elucidate the mechanistic basis underlying this substrate tolerance, we employed all-atom MD simulations to examine how Endo IV conformationally adapts to structurally diverse AP lesions. Endo IV is a zinc-dependent endonuclease that utilizes three Zn²⁺ ions during catalysis (Fig. 5A). Proton transfer from a bridging water molecule to Glu261 generates a nucleophilic hydroxide that initiates an SN2 attack on the scissile phosphate. All three Zn²⁺ ions contribute to stabilizing the pentacoordinated trigonal bipyramidal transition state, though their coordination partners vary across reaction stages [69, 70]. For clarity, Zn2 and Zn3 directly coordinate the nonbridging oxygens of the scissile phosphate, whereas Zn1 mediates proton transfer and nucleophile activation (Fig. 5B).

MD simulations were performed on Endo IV bound to unmodified and chemically modified AP substrates (Fig. 5C and Supplementary Fig. S23A–C). Although the PEA adduct appears sterically less bulky than ARP, it inhibits Endo IV cleavage more strongly in vitro (Fig. 1E). To disentangle steric and electronic effects, PEA-labeled AP sites were used as a key probe. Distance analyses over 200 ns revealed that Zn2 and Zn3 maintained stable coordination across all substrates, while Zn1 exhibited significantly reduced distances to PEA- and PAB-labeled AP sites relative to the unmodified substrate (Fig. 5C).

In the native system, the nonbridging phosphate oxygen (O1P) is the primary coordination partner of Zn1, supported by polarization of nearby deoxyribose hydrogens through the bridging water molecule (Fig. 5D and Supplementary Fig. S23D). This coordination network remains stable and positions Zn1 to simultaneously interact with O1P and the nucleophilic water, forming a catalytically competent transition state (Fig. 5B). Upon PEA labeling, the core metal–phosphate interactions are preserved, but Zn1 undergoes slight displacement (Fig. 5E), consistent with reduced yet measurable catalytic activity. Trajectory analysis revealed frequent transient interactions between Zn1 and hydrogens on the PEA aromatic ring (Supplementary Fig. S23E). Electrostatic potential mapping showed a diffuse negative π–electron cloud on the aromatic system, suggesting metal–π interactions that partially compete with phosphate coordination (Fig. 5C). Nevertheless, the stronger negative charge on the phosphate oxygen maintains Zn–O distances within a catalytically permissive range.

The higher cleavage efficiency of ARP-modified AP sites, despite their greater steric bulk (Fig. 1E), is explained by their reduced electronic interference. In ARP-labeled substrates, O1P remains the dominant coordinating partner of Zn1 throughout the simulation (Supplementary Fig. S23F). The flexible ARP linker can extend into the β-barrel cavity, minimizing unfavorable interactions with the metal center (Fig. 5F), in contrast to the rigid Pictet–Spengler adduct of PEA.

To sum up, both rigid and flexible AP-site modifications can be accommodated within Endo IV’s active site without severely disrupting metal–phosphate coordination (Fig. 5G). By contrast, APE1 displays lower substrate tolerance due to its narrower central channel (Fig. 5H). HOLE analysis reveals pronounced constrictions in the APE1 cavity near the substrate-binding region (Fig. 5I and J), leading to severe steric clashes with bulky adducts such as PAB. Endo IV’s wider and more open β-barrel architecture (Fig. 5K) allows modified substrates to relax into stable conformations within 10 ns, preserving catalytic efficiency.

Together, these results highlight the critical roles of metal–ion coordination and active-site architecture in determining Endo IV’s exceptional tolerance toward chemically modified AP sites, providing a mechanistic framework for designing molecular probes and modulators of DNA damage repair.

Discussion

A central challenge in achieving single-nucleotide-resolution mapping of AP sites in large genomes is the overwhelming background from intact DNA lacking lesions. Without effective enrichment, most sequencing reads derive from undamaged DNA, severely limiting sensitivity toward low-abundance AP sites. In our strategy, AP sites are gently but efficiently labeled, enabling selective enrichment of AP-containing DNA fragments from the genomic background. This is followed by site-specific cleavage mediated by the unique catalytic activity of Endo IV, which markedly enhances both the sensitivity and accuracy of AP-site detection.

We employed the N-pyrrolyl alanine Pictet–Spengler reaction to label AP sites in genomic DNA [71]. This reaction is operationally simple, proceeds with high efficiency, and yields chemically stable products that do not require low-temperature storage. Importantly, the labeling reaction exhibits excellent selectivity toward aldehyde-containing DNA lesions. Any off-target aldehyde adducts are completely removed by subsequent Endo IV treatment, ensuring high specificity for AP-site mapping.

Sequence-context analysis revealed that guanine-derived AP sites were strongly enriched for guanine at the 5′ flanking position, whereas adenine-derived AP sites showed no obvious neighboring-base preference (Fig. 3C–F). This feature not only reflects the intrinsic susceptibility of guanine to oxidative and alkylation damage, but also suggests that sequence neighborhoods surrounding AP sites retain damage-origin–specific imprints, enabling inference of the predominant base of origin and underlying damage–repair pathways at the population level.

Notably, APE1 knockout did not lead to a dramatic accumulation of AP sites in TE-1 cells. We speculate that APE1 may represent a bottleneck in the canonical BER pathway, and that efficient turnover of DNA glycosylases—particularly OGG1—critically depends on the presence of downstream APE1 at or near the lesion site. In the absence of APE1, impaired glycosylase turnover limits the formation of AP-site intermediates while exacerbating the accumulation of unrepaired oxidative DNA lesions.

Under normal culture conditions, AP sites were abundant and dynamically enriched in regulatory and transcription-associated regions, consistent with active damage–repair turnover. In addition, we identified a large population of stable AP sites that persist in vivo. These lesions were predominantly localized to intergenic and satellite regions, with a base-of-origin composition following A > G ≈ T > C. While guanine-derived AP sites were more prominent in HeLa cells, adenine-derived lesions dominated in TE-1 cells, a difference that likely reflects cell-type-specific damage–repair equilibria rather than intrinsic base susceptibility alone. Adenine-derived AP sites are likely attributable to spontaneous depurination arising from the intrinsic instability of the N-glycosidic bond. Under oxidative stress, guanine-derived AP sites increased markedly; however, their steady-state levels remained constrained by rapid processing through the BER pathway. Notably, these sites were highly conserved across cell types, culture conditions, and genotoxic treatments, and were largely insensitive to APE1 knockout, indicating that they do not represent transient intermediates of canonical BER.

Together, these observations indicate that not all AP sites behave as short-lived intermediates of canonical BER. Instead, a subset of AP sites persists stably in the genome and preferentially localizes to specific chromatin environments that are less accessible or of lower repair priority. Satellite DNA, in particular, emerges as a major contributor to this persistent AP-site population and exhibits distinct, damage-type-dependent behavior, with relative depletion under alkylation stress but pronounced enrichment under oxidative stress. These findings point to a spatially organized landscape of AP-site formation and repair shaped by genomic context, chromatin accessibility, and damage chemistry. By enabling sensitive and quantitative mapping of AP sites at single-nucleotide resolution, DEPACE-seq resolves both dynamic repair-associated lesions and a previously underappreciated population of persistent AP sites embedded within defined chromatin contexts, providing a framework for future studies of their origin, regulation, and potential biological relevance.

Compared with existing approaches for AP-site mapping, DEPACE-seq combines high recovery efficiency with a dual-library design that enables confident genome-wide identification of AP sites at single-nucleotide resolution. Unlike Nick-seq [52], DEPACE-seq enriches AP-containing DNA fragments before library construction, thereby substantially reducing the overwhelming background from undamaged genomic DNA and facilitating the detection of low-abundance lesions. While Nick-seq relies on the integration of two asymmetric datasets followed by computational filtering to resolve false-positive calls, DEPACE-seq determines AP-site coordinates through the experimental intersection of two complementary libraries. To date, Nick-seq has been applied primarily to bacterial genomes, whereas DEPACE-seq is demonstrated here for genome-wide AP-site mapping in human cells. Compared with single-end sequencing approaches such as snAP-seq [51] and SSiNGLe-AP [56], the dual-library architecture of DEPACE-seq provides an intrinsic validation mechanism in which AP-site coordinates are defined only when complementary signals are independently detected in both libraries. This strategy markedly suppresses nonspecific background while avoiding unnecessary sequencing burden (Fig. 2G and Supplementary Fig. S16). DCL-seq [57], introduces a conceptually attractive strategy by detecting AP sites through altered base-pairing behavior rather than converting AP lesions into sequencing-library termini. However, the current implementation remains incomplete, as AP sites originating from cytosine cannot yet be identified. In addition, DNA polymerases exhibit substantially lower bypass efficiency across AP sites than across undamaged bases, limiting amplification of authentic AP-containing DNA fragments and causing sequencing reads to be dominated by undamaged genomic DNA.

From the perspective of AP-site chemical labeling, DCL-seq [57], and click-code-seq [58] employ hydrazide chemistry and copper-catalyzed azide–alkyne cycloaddition (CuAAC), respectively. In our hands, hydrazide-containing AP-labeling probes bearing nucleobase-like structures produced Schiff-base adducts with limited stability. Click-code-seq converts AP sites into alkyne-containing intermediates and subsequently ligates AP-specific adaptors through CuAAC chemistry. However, when the alkyne group is embedded within a DNA strand, CuAAC coupling may be incomplete. Moreover, the resulting linkage at the AP site differs structurally from a native phosphodiester bond, and whether high-fidelity DNA polymerases can efficiently replicate across this artificial junction remains to be fully established. By comparison, the PAB-labeling chemistry used in DEPACE-seq proceeds under mild reaction conditions, generates chemically stable labeling products, and is straightforward to implement.

Despite the substantial improvements in specificity and fidelity afforded by PAB labeling, Endo IV cleavage, and the dual-library intersection strategy, the overall performance of DEPACE-seq, like that of other sequencing-based methods, remains influenced by the efficiencies of DNA end blocking, adaptor ligation, and library construction. These steps therefore represent opportunities for further optimization. In addition, samples containing extremely low-frequency AP lesions still require sufficient sequencing depth to ensure reliable genome-wide detection. Future improvements in library preparation efficiency and sequencing throughput should further enhance the sensitivity of DEPACE-seq, extending its applicability to rare DNA damage events in biological and clinical samples.

Overall, we demonstrate that DEPACE-seq is a highly specific, efficient, and readily implementable strategy for AP-site sequencing, offering a robust and scalable framework for high-resolution mapping of abasic sites in complex genomes. Beyond resolving endogenous AP-site landscapes, this approach is readily extensible to other chemically defined DNA lesions. The methodological and conceptual insights presented here lay a foundation for the rational development of next-generation molecular tools to interrogate and ultimately modulate nucleic acid damage and repair processes.
