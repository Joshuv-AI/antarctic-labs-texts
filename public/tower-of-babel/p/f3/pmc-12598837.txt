Prevalence and associated factors of comorbidities among hypertensive patients in Northwest Ethiopia, 2024
Authors: Baye Ashenef, Bayu Wondmneh Yimenu, Enatnesh Essa Osman, Derese Sinamaw, Gelagey Baye, Zigale Hibstu Teffera, Adane Adugna, Enyew Fenta Mengistu, Temesgen Baylie, Mohammed Jemal
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHypertension frequently coexists with chronic conditions such as diabetes, dyslipidemia, chronic kidney disease, and obesity. This study aimed to assess the prevalence and associated factors of comorbidities among hypertensive patients in northwest Ethiopia.MethodsA multi-centered, institution-based cross-sectional study was conducted in 2024 among 423 randomly selected hypertensive patients. Data was collected through structured interviews and physical assessments. Bivariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were employed to identify significant associations, with p-values < 0.05 considered statistically significant.ResultsThe prevalence of comorbidities among hypertensive patients was 30.5% (95% CI: 26.2–34.8%). The most common comorbidities were chronic kidney disease (10.9%), diabetes mellitus (9%), dyslipidemia (7.5%), and obesity (3.1%). Factors significantly associated with comorbidity included age ≥ 65 years (AOR = 1.77), hypertension duration ≥ 5 years (AOR = 1.99), alcohol consumption (AOR = 1.93), and physical inactivity (AOR = 1.9).ConclusionNearly one-third of hypertensive patients had at least one comorbid condition. The findings underscore the importance of early detection, integrated care, and targeted interventions, which are supported by enhanced diagnostic services, health worker training, and public awareness. Further longitudinal and community-based studies are recommended to explore causality and broader impacts.Keywords: Hypertension, Comorbidities, Prevalence, Risk factors, Chronic kidney diseases, Diabetes mellitus, Dyslipidemia, Obesity, Ethiopia

Introduction

Hypertension (HTN), or high blood pressure, is one of the most common non-communicable diseases (NCDs) and remains a major global public health challenge [1, 2]. It is defined as a sustained elevation of systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg on repeated measurements [3, 4]. Often termed the “silent killer,” HTN is frequently asymptomatic until it causes damage to vital organs such as the heart, brain, kidneys, and eyes [5, 6].

Globally, an estimated 1.28 billion adults aged 30–79 years are living with hypertension, with the highest age-specific prevalence reported in Africa [7, 8]. The burden is especially high in low- and middle-income countries, where poor awareness, limited healthcare access, and inadequate treatment contribute to ineffective control [9]. Alarmingly, more than 46% of hypertensive individuals remain undiagnosed, and fewer than half of those diagnosed achieve optimal blood pressure control, making HTN a leading cause of cardiovascular disease, stroke, chronic kidney disease, retinopathy, heart failure, disability, and premature mortality [10–12].

The development of hypertension is influenced by both non-modifiable factors (age, sex, family history, race) and modifiable factors (obesity, excess salt intake, unhealthy diet, inactivity, alcohol consumption, smoking, stress, poor sleep, diabetes, and dyslipidemia) [2, 13–16]. Its pathophysiology is multifactorial, involving genetic, environmental, and physiological mechanisms [17]. Increased cardiac output and systemic vascular resistance drive the condition with key contributors including sympathetic nervous system overactivation, stimulation of the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system (RAAS), and endothelial dysfunction [18, 19]. Impaired renal sodium excretion, obesity, insulin resistance, and chronic inflammation further exacerbate vascular tone and blood volume, while vascular remodelling and arterial stiffness sustain persistent hypertension [20, 21]. Hypertension is classified as primary (90–95% of cases, with no single identifiable cause) or secondary (due to kidney disease, endocrine disorders, or certain drugs) [22]. Other clinically relevant forms include isolated systolic, white-coat, masked, and resistant hypertension [23, 24].

Beyond its direct complications, hypertension is of clinical concern because it often coexists with other chronic conditions. Diabetes mellitus, chronic kidney disease, obesity, dyslipidemia, and mental health disorders are among the most frequent comorbidities [25]. These comorbidities complicate management, impair blood pressure control, raise hospitalization and mortality rates, and reduce quality of life, underscoring the need for integrated care [26–29]. Epidemiological studies indicate that more than half of hypertensive patients worldwide live with at least one comorbidity, and in some populations, this figure exceeds 70% [27]. For instance, studies from China [30, 31], Korea [32], and Bangladesh [33] reported comorbidity rates ranging from 50% to 70%, while in Ethiopia, smaller-scale studies have shown that 19.3% and 21% of hypertensive patients also suffer from diabetes mellitus and chronic kidney disease, respectively [34].

Despite strong global evidence, data on comorbidities among hypertensive patients in Ethiopia are scarce, with most studies focusing on hypertension alone or a single comorbidity. This gap limits context-specific prevention and management strategies. Therefore, this study aimed to assess the prevalence and associated factors of comorbidities in hypertensive patients to generate evidence for integrated, patient-centered care.

Methodology

Study design and population

An institution-based cross-sectional study was conducted from September to December 2024 in three referral hospitals of the Amhara Region, Ethiopia (Felege Hiwot, Debre Markos, and Debre Tabor), which serve both urban and rural populations. The study population included hypertensive patients attending follow-up visits during the study period. Adults aged ≥ 18 years with a diagnosis of hypertension for at least one year who provided written informed consent were eligible, while pregnant women, patients with severe cognitive impairment, and those with secondary hypertension were excluded.

Sample size and sampling technique

The sample size was determined using a single population proportion formula.

Sampling procedure

Simple and systematic random sampling techniques were employed to select study participants. Three referral hospitals, such as Felege Hiwot, Debre Markos, and Debre Tabor, were randomly selected using the lottery method. A sampling frame was developed from hypertension clinic records. Systematic random sampling was then conducted, selecting every 8th patient (K = 8), calculated by dividing the total number of hypertensive patients during the study period (3,500) by the desired sample size (423). The starting point, patient number 4, was determined using the lottery method. A proportionate number of participants were drawn from each hospital to ensure representativeness.

Data collection procedure and tools

Data collection was conducted between September and December 2024, using a structured, interviewer-administered questionnaire adapted from various validated sources [30, 32, 33]. The questionnaire gathered information on medical assessments, substance use, and sociodemographic characteristics. Comorbidities were identified using standard clinical and laboratory criteria and confirmed through objective test results/documented medical records. For patients with hypertension, blood pressure and body mass index (BMI) measurements were also recorded. At each hospital, two BSc-level clinical nurses were recruited to carry out data collection and documentation.

Anthropometric and blood pressure measurements

Weight and Height: Participants’ weight was measured using a calibrated balance scale, and height was recorded using a stadiometer. Body Mass Index (BMI) was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by the square of height in meters (kg/m²).

Blood Pressure: Blood pressure was assessed using a calibrated sphygmomanometer, following standard measurement protocols.

Data analysis procedure

The collected data were first checked for completeness and then entered into EpiData version 4.6. Subsequently, the dataset was exported to SPSS version 26 for statistical analysis. To assess associations between study variables, both crude and adjusted odds ratios were calculated, along with 95% confidence intervals. Descriptive statistics, including the median, interquartile range, and frequencies, were computed. Multicollinearity among the selected independent variables was assessed, with the variance inflation factor (VIF) found to be acceptable (VIF < 2). Model fitness was evaluated using the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test, yielding a p-value of 0.752, indicating good model fit (p > 0.05).

Both bivariable and multivariate logistic regression analyses were employed to identify factors associated with comorbidities of hypertension. Variables with a p-value less than 0.2 in the bivariable analysis were included in the multivariable logistic regression model. In the final model, variables with a p-value of 0.05 or less were considered statistically significant. The results were summarized and presented using frequency tables, graphs, and charts.

Data quality management

To ensure data quality, significant attention was given to the design of the data collection instrument. The questionnaire was pre-tested with 20 participants at Bichena Primary Hospital, a setting with similar socio-cultural characteristics to the study area, before the main data collection. This pre-test helped identify and address issues related to wording, language barriers, and contextual differences. A one-day training session was provided to both data collectors and supervisors, covering the study objectives, ethical considerations, data collection procedures, interview and measurement techniques, and methods to maintain data quality and consistency. During the data collection period, supervisors closely monitored the data collectors.

Results

Socio-demographic characteristics

The study included 423 hypertensive patients and achieved a 100% response rate. Participants ranged in age from 25 to 90 years, with a median age of 59 years (± 17) and an interquartile range (IQR) of 49–69 years. The majority (55.2%) were aged 65 years and older. Of the total participants, 233 (55.1%) were female, and 67.4% were married. More than half resided in rural areas, and 63.6% were unable to read or write. Regarding occupation, 31% were farmers, and 30% were housewives. Additionally, more than half of the participants reported a monthly income of less than 2,000 Ethiopian Birr (ETB) (Table 1).

Clinical and behavioral characteristics of hypertensive patients

Among the participants, 235 (55.6%) had lived with hypertension for over five years. More than half (60.3%) reported an average sleep duration of 6 to 8 h each night. Most respondents, 247 (58.4%), identified as vegetarians. While 333 participants (78.7%) had consumed alcohol at some point in their lives, only 65 (15.4%) reported alcohol consumption in the past 30 days, primarily traditional beverages such as tela or tej. Elevated systolic blood pressure was noted in 58.4% of the participants, and 44.7% had elevated diastolic blood pressure. Prevalence of comorbidities of hypertension

The overall prevalence of comorbidities among hypertensive patients was 30.5% (95% CI: 26.2–34.8). Specifically, 10.9% of the respondents had chronic kidney disease (CKD), 7.5% had dyslipidemia, 3.1% had obesity, and 9% had diabetes mellitus (DM) as a comorbid condition (Fig. 1).

Risk factors of comorbidities among hypertensive patients

Bivariate analysis indicated that age, residence, educational status, duration of hypertension, dietary habits, khat chewing, alcohol consumption, BMI, and physical activity were all associated with complications of hypertension.

In the multivariate analysis, only four factors remained significantly correlated with hypertension comorbidities. These included being aged 65 years or older, having hypertension for five or more years, a history of alcohol consumption, and physical inactivity.

Specifically, participants aged 65 years or older were 1.77 times more likely to have comorbid conditions compared to those under 65 (AOR = 1.77; 95% CI: 0.52–4.99). A duration of hypertension of five years or more increased the odds of comorbidities by 1.99 times (AOR = 1.99; 95% CI: 1.25–3.2). Alcohol drinkers were 1.93 times more likely to have comorbidities (AOR = 1.93; 95% CI: 1.06–3.5). Physical inactivity nearly doubled the risk of comorbidities (AOR = 1.9; 95% CI: 1.1–3.3) in hypertensive patients (see Table 3).

Discussion

This study aimed to assess the prevalence and risk factors of comorbidities among hypertensive patients in Ethiopia in 2024. The findings revealed a comorbidity prevalence of 30.5% (95% CI: 26.2–34.8%), which is significantly higher than the 17% reported in a Korean population-based study [32]. This discrepancy may be from differences in healthcare access, socioeconomic conditions, study methodologies, and population characteristics. In Ethiopia, limited access to timely diagnosis and treatment can delay hypertension management, increasing the likelihood of developing comorbidities. Variations in how comorbidities are defined and identified, along with lifestyle factors, environmental exposures, and the burden of infectious diseases, may also play a role. Furthermore, delayed diagnosis and low awareness of hypertension in Ethiopia could result in a higher rate of complications compared to the Korean context.

The lower prevalence of comorbidities observed in this study compared to findings from China (68.24%) [30] and in Bangladesh (65%) [33] is likely attributable to several contextual differences, including disparities in healthcare infrastructure, diagnostic capacity, routine screening practices, and accessibility. Ethiopia’s limited healthcare infrastructure, reduced access to diagnostic services, and fewer routine health screenings may lead to underdiagnosis and underreporting of comorbid conditions. In contrast, the more advanced healthcare systems in China and Bangladesh support higher detection rates. Demographic differences, including younger populations and more rural residents in Ethiopia, may also contribute to lower observed prevalence. Additionally, the smaller sample size in this study reduces the estimate’s precision compared to the much larger cohorts in China (more than 2 million) and Bangladesh (1,912). Variations in lifestyle, with lower rates of sedentary behaviour and more normal BMI levels in the Ethiopian sample, may also play a role. Finally, differences in genetics, environment, culture, health-seeking behaviour, and disease awareness further explain the observed disparity in comorbidity prevalence across these countries.

In this study, comorbidities among hypertensive patients included chronic kidney disease (10.9%), diabetes mellitus (9%), dyslipidemia (7.5%), and obesity (3.1%). These conditions are interlinked with hypertension through shared physiological mechanisms. CKD and hypertension mutually worsen each other, high blood pressure damages kidney structures, while impaired kidneys exacerbate hypertension by fluid retention and hormonal activation [44–46]. Dyslipidemia often coexists with hypertension due to endothelial dysfunction, atherosclerosis, and insulin resistance [47–49]. Similarly, obesity contributes to hypertension by increasing blood volume and activating the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system, while hypertension may promote weight gain through metabolic changes and certain medications [50–53]. Diabetes and hypertension frequently co-occur due to common risk factors such as poor diet, inactivity, and insulin resistance, with each condition intensifying vascular complications and cardiovascular risk [54, 55].

Older adults (aged 65 and above) were 1.77 times more likely to develop comorbidities alongside hypertension, which is supported by studies done in China [30, 56], USA [57] and Turkey [26]. Ageing brings physiological changes like arterial stiffness, impaired glucose metabolism, and immune decline, increasing susceptibility to diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and kidney dysfunction [58–60]. Furthermore, age-related issues such as polypharmacy, diminished physical capacity, and poor medication adherence contribute to the higher burden of comorbid conditions in this age group [61–63].

Participants with hypertension for five years or more had nearly twice the risk of developing comorbidities, as supported by studies from the United Kingdom [28], China [64]. This increased risk is attributed to long-term vascular damage, organ dysfunction, and chronic inflammation [65]. Sustained high blood pressure leads to endothelial dysfunction, arterial stiffness, and atherosclerosis, raising the risk of cardiovascular complications [21, 66]. Additionally, prolonged activation of the hormonal and nervous systems further strains the heart and kidneys, while chronic inflammation and oxidative stress impair insulin sensitivity, increasing vulnerability to conditions like diabetes [67].

Ever Alcohol consumption was associated with a 1.93-fold increased risk of developing comorbidities, supported by findings from studies in Italy [68] and the United Kingdom [69]. Alcohol raises blood pressure through vasoconstriction, stimulation of the sympathetic nervous system, and activation of the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system [70]. Chronic intake also contributes to insulin resistance, dyslipidemia, and cardiovascular complications, while potentially damaging the heart, disrupting heart rhythms, impairing kidney function, and increasing inflammation and oxidative stress, factors that collectively exacerbate hypertension [70, 71].

Physical inactivity was found to nearly double the risk of developing comorbid conditions, supported by studies done in Turkey [26] and the USA [72]. As a modifiable risk factor, lack of exercise contributes to weight gain, poor cardiovascular health, insulin resistance, and lipid imbalances, all of which exacerbate hypertension and accelerate the development of comorbidities [73].

Limitations of the study

This study had limitations, including reliance on participants’ recall, which may introduce recall bias. As a cross-sectional design, it also restricts the ability to establish causal relationships. Furthermore, since the research was conducted in a hospital setting, the findings, particularly regarding the prevalence of comorbidities among hypertension, may not be generalizable to the wider community.

Strength of the study

This study provides context-specific evidence on the prevalence and risk factors of comorbidities among hypertensive patients in Northwest Ethiopia, addressing a regional knowledge gap. Examining multiple comorbidities highlights the broader burden of multimorbidity. The findings can inform integrated, patient-centred care and guide targeted interventions, while inclusion of patients from multiple facilities enhances generalizability.

Conclusion and recommendations

This study found that nearly one-third of hypertensive patients in Ethiopia had comorbid conditions, with chronic kidney disease (10.9%), diabetes (9%), dyslipidemia (7.5%), and obesity (3.1%) being the most common. Risk factors included age, long hypertension duration, alcohol use, and physical inactivity. These findings underscore the need for early detection, integrated care, health promotion, and targeted interventions, as well as improved diagnostic services, healthcare worker training, and public awareness. Further longitudinal and community-based studies are needed to explore the causes and impact of comorbidities in hypertensive patients.
