Maximum water stress is decoupled from climate, traits, and growth in a xeric oak
Authors: Leander D L Anderegg, Robert P Skelton, Jessica Diaz, Prahlad Papper, Piper Lovegreen, Anna T Trugman, David D Ackerly, Todd E Dawson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Summary Our ability to explain and predict the spatial patterns of forest mortality remains limited. We tested the predictability of water stress across populations and explored how water availability and plant allocation mediate spatial variation in tree growth in a widespread oak species (Quercus douglasii Hook & Arn.).We conducted a range‐wide survey of drought stress, xylem water isotopes, growth, and allocation traits across 15 sites spanning almost 1000 m difference in annual precipitation.We found little relationship between end‐of‐season water availability (predawn leaf water potentials) or maximum stress (midday water potentials) and climate or soils. Instead, stem water stable isotopes indicated that water availability was predicted by access to deep water resources derived from large winter storms. We also found a three‐way decoupling of water stress, prior growth, and allocation to leaf tissue, such that simulated hydraulic risk from a mechanistic model was uncorrelated with observed performance.Our results reveal that deeply rooted trees can be hydrologically decoupled from aboveground climate, and that the seasonality of growth, trait development, and hydraulic risk are phenologically disconnected. The complicated relationship between carbon gain and hydraulic risk in seasonal environments and limited data on critical zone hydrology are key challenges to predicting drought vulnerability. Keywords: critical zone, deuterium, Huber value, leaf area to sapwood area ratio, oxygen isotope, trait–environment relationships, trait–growth relationships

Introduction

Anthropogenic climate change drives more frequent and severe droughts, with widespread ecological consequences such as regional scale forest mortality (Allen et al., 2010; Brodribb et al., 2020; Hammond et al., 2022). Two decades of ecophysiological research have revealed that failure of the hydraulic system, interacting with disruptions to carbon metabolism–particularly for defense against biotic agents–drives tree mortality during drought (Adams et al., 2017; Brodribb et al., 2020). This insight has sparked a focus on plant hydraulics as the key to understanding and predicting spatial patterns of forest mortality (Tai et al., 2016). However, our ability to hindcast which trees will die (in space and time) remains extremely limited (Anderegg et al., 2016; Benito Garzón et al., 2018; Venturas et al., 2020; Trugman et al., 2021).

Our inability to predict mortality may be driven by: a poor understanding of water supply (i.e. how meteorological conditions translate into soil moisture limitation); by unappreciated variation in the biological factors mediating how supply limitation and atmospheric demand result in physiological stress; and/or an incomplete understanding of how hydraulic risk during periods of maximum water stress relates to performance at other timescales (e.g. growth, which can be offset by months or years from water stress in arid environments). The first issue is the persistent challenge of translating meteorological inputs into soil moisture supply over a tree's functional rooting depth (Anderegg et al., 2013). Even as our gridded climate products improve and multiply, our understanding of tree‐level critical zone hydrology (from the top of unweathered bedrock to the top of the vegetation) remains quite limited (Rempe & Dietrich, 2018; Fan et al., 2019; Dawson et al., 2020; Callahan et al., 2022; Hahm et al., 2022). As a consequence, translating meteorological drought into plant available water (PAW) remains challenging, particularly in geologically complex landscapes. If this edaphic uncertainty dwarfs climate uncertainty, improving the accuracy and resolution of climate products will not yield improved predictions of plant stress or mortality.

Climatic or meteorological predictors of drought stress often involve water balance metrics such as ‘climatic water deficit’ (CWD, seasonally integrated potential evapotranspiration minus modeled actual evapotranspiration), which estimate the mismatch between water supply and demand (Stephenson, 1990, 1998; Mitchell et al., 2016; Berner et al., 2017; Ledo et al., 2018). These metrics in theory capture the critical interactions between precipitation, temperature, and humidity (Stephenson, 1998). However, water balance approaches require substantial assumptions about soil water holding capacity that drastically simplify complex critical zone hydrology. In the simplest water balance metrics such as Aridity Index (ratio of precipitation to potential evapotranspiration), all precipitation is considered to be plant available. More sophisticated metrics such as CWD involve a simplified soil bucket model based on large‐scale gridded soils data of 1–3 m depth, and a seasonal model of actual evapotranspiration constrained by this soil bucket (Flint et al., 2013; Abatzoglou et al., 2018). These soils data are often quite coarse and inaccurate outside of agricultural areas (Terribile et al., 2011) and typically do not address deeper soil, regolith, or fractured bedrock that can supply substantial water for many forests globally (McCormick et al., 2021). It thus remains an open question whether water balance metrics capture supply limitations in deep‐rooted species growing in geomorphically complex systems.

Testing this question requires on‐the‐ground observations, such as measurements of plant water potentials and plant stable water isotopes across large spatial extents. Predawn water potential (ΨPD), when a tree has theoretically equilibrated with the soil (assuming no nighttime transpiration and that the plant is still hydraulically coupled with the soil), provides an empirical measurement of PAW and is the gold standard for measurement of plant water supply limitation. Meanwhile, xylem stable water isotopes can indicate the hydrological source of transpired water, with deuterium (D or 2H) and 18O being more depleted in precipitation falling in cold months and large storms (Ehleringer et al., 1993). In addition, the relative proportion of DHO to H2 18O can reveal functional rooting depth (if soil water isotopic composition is known), seasonal water sourcing (isotopically depleted winter or enriched summer precipitation) or the extent to which plants use shallow soil water sources that are influenced by evaporation. Evaporation at less than 100% relative humidity enriches D faster than 18O, causing evaporatively enriched soil water to have a D ‘excess’ relative to the ‘Meteoric Water Line’ of D and 18O in precipitation. If site‐specific or even individual‐specific isotopic data can explain spatial variation in ΨPD but climatic water balance metrics cannot, that suggests that our climate or (more likely) soils data are insufficient representations of the critical zone to predict water supply limitation, much less mortality.

The second major issue for predicting drought mortality is how spatial variation in plant physiology complicates the translation of water limitation into maximum plant water stress. A tree's exposure to lethal hydraulic damage is determined by its xylem vulnerability to embolism and its minimum midday plant water potential (ΨMD). Within species (including our study species), xylem vulnerability has often proven fairly fixed (e.g. Pinus sylvestris (Lamy et al., 2013) or Quercus douglasii (Skelton et al., 2019)). However, plant hydraulic efficiency determines how a given level of soil water supply (ΨPD) and evaporative demand (vapor pressure deficit or VPD) translate into ΨMD, and hydraulic efficiency can vary substantially within species (Martinez‐Vilalta et al., 2009; Anderegg & HilleRisLambers, 2016).

In particular, decreasing allocation to evaporative leaf area relative to conductive stem area (decreasing the A l : A s ratio, sometimes referred to by its inverse the ‘Huber value’) helps plants avoid extremely negative ΨMD by increasing leaf area‐specific hydraulic efficiency (Mencuccini & Grace, 1995; Martinez‐Vilalta et al., 2009; Trugman et al., 2019; Mencuccini et al., 2019b; Sanchez Martinez et al., 2020). While many within‐species trait–climate relationships are idiosyncratic (Anderegg, 2023), aridity‐driven intraspecific variation in A l : A s has emerged as a consistent pattern across studies and systems (Mencuccini & Bonosi, 2001; Martinez‐Vilalta et al., 2009; Anderegg & HilleRisLambers, 2016; Rosas et al., 2019; Anderegg et al., 2021). Thus, variable allocation to leaf area may be a key hydraulic acclimatory mechanism driving within‐species variation in mortality risk (Trugman et al., 2019; Mencuccini et al., 2019a).

From a carbon economy perspective, biomass‐based traits such as the mass of leaves relative to the mass of stems (M l : M s) are an alternative basis for quantifying leaf : stem allocation, and also show relationships with water availability (Poorter et al., 2012; Ledo et al., 2018), though they have been less studied from a water stress perspective than A l : A s. Variation in allocation to leaf area should both moderate plant exposure to drought and strongly impact plant growth (Lambers & Poorter, 1992).

Finally, there is a critical question of timescale. Damaging ΨMD and even extreme soil moisture limitation (ΨPD) are short‐term states, lasting hours (for extreme, VPD‐driven ΨMD) to months. Meanwhile, particularly in Mediterranean‐type climates, the majority of carbon assimilation, growth, and trait adjustments occur months to years before the conditions that drive mortality risk. This may fundamentally decouple the things we can more easily survey across space (growth, allocation to leaf area) from the acute risk of mortality during drought. Within a species, mean growth (Klesse et al., 2020), growth sensitivity to drought (Canham et al., 2018; Camarero et al., 2021), drought‐related traits (Martinez‐Vilalta et al., 2009; Anderegg et al., 2021), and drought‐induced mortality (Anderegg et al., 2019) often correlate with site aridity or proximity to the dry range edge. Thus, we often assume that these processes are correlated across space despite the very different timescales they represent (e.g. short‐term ΨMD vs traits such as A l : A s that are developed every spring vs multi‐year growth). In studies of how traits influence growth and mortality risk, and particularly in biogeographic studies of how traits vary across a species' range, the implicit assumption is that a tree or site's relative condition (e.g. how its ΨPD or ΨMD at the time of sampling compares to other trees/sites) is highly correlated across seasons and years. Dry sites are usually dry compared to wet sites, within and across years. Yet this assumption is rarely tested. If, due to mismatches in timescale, maximum water stress (and risk of mortality) does not correlate spatially with drought‐related traits or the water stress constraint on growth, the task of predicting spatial patterns of drought mortality becomes substantially more complicated.

We explored these challenges in blue oak (Quercus douglasii Hook & Arn.), an ecologically and culturally important species that dominates low elevation woodlands and savannas across California, US. This deciduous, deeply rooted species is valued for dendroclimate reconstructions because of its high growth sensitivity to precipitation (Stahle et al., 2013). Blue oak also experienced substantial mortality during the California 2012–2016 drought (Das et al., 2019), though only c. 30% of the spatial variation in canopy dieback could be explained by climate, drought severity, or groundwater depletion (Brown et al., 2018). Variation among populations in hydraulic damage thresholds (leaf and stem P50 or the xylem water potential that causes 50% embolism) appears limited in blue oaks (Skelton et al., 2019), which suggests that spatial variation in hydrology or plant hydraulics (e.g. variation in trait such as A l : A s) are likely key to predicting blue oak mortality (McLaughlin et al., 2020).

We tested how predictable soil moisture limitation is across a c. 1000 mm precipitation gradient, how trait variation moderates drought exposure, and how linked peak drought stress, drought‐related traits, and growth are. To do this, we measured end of dry season soil moisture availability (ΨPD) and maximum midday water stress (ΨMD) during a drought, stable water isotope tracers of functional rooting depth, leaf and allocation traits, and growth rates across the geographic range of blue oak. We asked: (1) can we explain the spatial variation in water availability and maximum drought stress across the landscape using climate, soil, or stable water isotopes? (2) How do allocation traits, particularly A l : A s, vary as a function of climate or soil moisture, and do they feed back to mediate maximum water stress? (3) Do 1 or 5‐yr individual tree growth rates correspond to observed end‐of‐season soil moisture limitation or maximum water stress, site climate/meteorology, or variation in allocation traits? And (4) does mechanistically simulated end‐of‐season hydraulic risk or carbon gain correlate with season‐integrated or multi‐year tree growth?

Materials and Methods

Study System

We sampled 15 sites across the geographic and climatic distribution of blue oak (Quercus douglasii Hook & Arn., Fig. 1a), selecting a subset of the most climatically diverse yet logistically accessible/feasible from the 26 sites of McBride et al. (1997), Papper & Ackerly (2021). Blue oak inhabits a wide range of precipitation and potential evapotranspiration (Fig. 1b), typified by Mediterranean‐type hot, dry growing seasons (April to September) with most precipitation falling December to March.

Water stress, traits and growth were sampled at 15 site across the range of blue oak (Quercus douglasii) during a drought year. Black points show locations of herbarium specimens (Baldwin et al., 2017), sampled locations are shown as orange points. (b) Sampled locations span the vast majority of the MAP and potential evapotranspiration (PET) space inhabited by blue oak. The 2018 water year (wy, crosses) when samples were collected was substantially hotter and drier than the 1981–2010 historical period (closed circles).

At the end of the 2018 growing season from 17 September to 3 October, when trees were experiencing the annual minimum soil moisture before leaf senescence, we sampled predawn and midday leaf water potentials, allocation traits, leaf gas exchange, and tree cores for water stable isotope and growth analysis. We prioritized visiting all sites as quickly as possible for comparability of water potentials, at the cost of ancillary measurements at some sites. We measured water potentials at all 15 sites, but allocation traits at only 12 sites, growth and isotopes at 11 sites, and leaf gas exchange on a subset of trees at 7 sites. Very little precipitation fell (< 15 mm or < 2% of total precipitation) at any site during the 4 months preceding our sampling. Thus, we achieved consistent antecedent drought conditions for all study sites. Finally, we used a mechanistic tree hydraulics model to synthesize multiple interacting factors that cannot be parsed statistically, namely interactions between meteorology and plant water use (e.g. VPD and the effects on stomatal closure), our observations of supply limitation and the effects of leaf area on the plant hydraulic balance.

Leaf water potential

We quantified moisture availability with predawn leaf water potential (ΨPD) sampled between 3 am and first light (local time). We measured 3–8 leaves from each of 5–7 trees per site (mean 5.5), severing the petiole with a sharp razor blade (for short trees) or collecting a branch > 50 cm long using pole pruners and immediately cutting 2–3 leaves using a razor blade. Leaves were immediately wrapped in tinfoil and a lightly damp paper towel to limit evaporation without rehydrating the leaf and measured in a Scholander‐type pressure chamber (PMS Instruments, Corvallis, OR, USA) within 1 min of cutting. A ‘modified petiole’ (Rodriguez‐Dominguez et al., 2022) was cut using a razor blade for leaves with short petioles.

We then measured leaf water potential at midday (ΨMD) between 12:30 h and 14:00 h to estimate maximum water stress. The sampling year (2018) was drier and hotter than average (Fig. 1b), so end‐of‐season ΨMD was a reasonable estimate of long‐term Ψmin values. We calculated ΔΨ (the water potential gradient driven by transpiration from soil to leaf) as ΨPD–ΨMD.

For a subset of 2–4 trees at 6 sites, we also measured midday stem xylem potential of terminal stems (ΨMD_xy, for woody stems immediately adjacent to leaves, typically < 5 cm from branch meristem) by wrapping 4–6 leaves on a terminal stem in tinfoil, damp paper towels, and plastic bags to stop transpiration > 20 min before leaf collection (e.g. Naor, 1998; Ortuño et al., 2006). ΨMD_xy was on average 7% (SE ±1.5%) less negative than ΨMD. We calculated ΨMD_xy for all trees assuming an 7% offset from ΨMD (i.e. that 7% of total resistance was between the branch xylem and leaf lamina). We used the species average P50 values for leaves (−3.88 SE ±0.09 MPa) and stems (−4.47 ± 0.1 MPa) from Skelton et al. (2019), who found < 3.5% CV in P50 among seven populations, to calculate Hydraulic Safety Margins (HSMleaf = ΨMD–P50leaf, HSMstem = ΨMD_xy–P50stem). Because HSMleaf and HSMstem are linear transforms of ΨMD we report statistical relationships only for ΨMD.

Trait measurements

At 12 of the 15 sample sites, we collected three terminal twigs from the sun exposed mid‐ to upper‐canopy of five trees using pole pruners. Terminal twigs, cut at the previous year bud scar, included current year (2018) stem growth and all subtending leaves, thus represent 1 yr's investment in branch and leaf tissue. Branches were rehydrated using the ‘partial rehydration’ method (Pérez‐Harguindeguy et al., 2013) and stored in a cooler or refrigerator for at least 12 h before processing. In the lab, leaves were removed (with petiole), weighed for wet mass (Ml_wet), and scanned on a flatbed scanner. Images were analyzed with ImageJ (Schneider et al., 2012) to calculate total twig leaf area (A l), and total leaf area was divided by leaf number for average leaf size. Stem basal diameter underneath the bark was measured at 2–5 orientations using digital calipers and used to calculate stem cross‐sectional area (A s). Total twig A l was divided by stem A s to calculate branch A l : A s ratio. Stem length was measured with calipers or a ruler. Leaves and stems were oven dried at 60°C for 72 h, and their dry mass measured (M l, M s). Leaf mass per area (LMA) was calculated as M l/A l, leaf mass to stem mass ratio as M l/M s, and leaf dry matter content (LDMC) as leaf dry mass by leaf wet mass (M l/M l_wet). Because samples were not processed until after the water potential campaign, we excluded LDMC values from samples that were stored longer than 1.5 wk from analysis, resulting in exclusion of LDMC values from four sites (n = 8 sites rather than n = 12 for LDMC).

Stable isotope analyses of water

Xylem water oxygen (δ18O) and hydrogen (δ2H or δD) stable isotope composition was measured at 11 of the study sites on 5 trees (7 trees at two sites, 3 trees at one site). Tree cores c. 4 cm long were extracted at breast height, rapidly placed in pop top scintillation vials (after removing bark and cambium), wrapped in parafilm, and stored in a cooler until frozen in the lab. Water was cryogenically extracted (Ehleringer et al., 2000). The oxygen isotope composition of extracted water was determined through mass spectrometry by CO2 headspace equilibration using a Gas Bench II (GB; ThermoFinnigan, Bremen, Germany) connected to a Delta Plus XL mass spectrometer (ThermoFinnigan) at the Center for Stable Isotope Biogeochemistry (CSIB), University of California, Berkeley, CA. The hydrogen isotope composition of samples was determined via injection into an H/Device (HDEV; ThermoFinnigan) coupled to a Delta Plus mass spectrometer (ThermoFinnigan), also at CSIB. All isotopic compositions are reported in delta (δ) notation in parts per thousand (‰) relative to the V‐SMOW standard.

where R = 18O/16O or D/H.

Monthly meteoric water isotope values were estimated for each site using the Online Isotopes in Precipitation Calculator, version OIPC3.2 (Bowen et al., 2005; Bowen, 2024). The evaporative deuterium enrichment of each sample relative to each site's local meteoric water line was used to calculate ‘line conditioned’ excess (lc excess, Landwher & Coplen, 2004). Average monthly precipitation‐weighted meteoric water isotopic values for the sampling year (δDprecip, δ18Oprecip) were calculated for each site. Raw xylem δD/δ18O were interpreted as reflecting the hydrological source of water (more depleted indicating water deriving from colder and larger storms) and lc excess was interpreted as indicative of deep vs shallow soil moisture (higher lc excess indicating shallow, evaporatively enriched water sources) (Ehleringer & Dawson, 1992; Landwher & Coplen, 2004). Because xylem δD shows a bias with tissue gravimetric water content (Sobota et al., 2024), we primarily present results using δ18O in the main text.

Growth

We measured 1 yr branch growth as the branch length of terminal branches collected for trait measurements (12 sites). We also measured 5 yr radial growth on the tree cores collected for water isotopes after cryogenic extraction by sanding them and measuring the most recent 5 yr of ring widths (2014–2018) using calipers and a dissecting scope. From radial growth increment, we calculated basal area increment (BAI) from the outside of the tree inward. Because both mean and variance of BAI were strong functions of tree diameter at breast height (DBH), we corrected for size‐related BAI trends by calculating each tree's growth relative to the maximum observed growth at that DBH across all sites (Supporting Information Fig. S1). We fitted a quantile regression to the 90th percentile BAI–DBH relationship, and then calculated each tree's size‐standardized growth as the ‘percent of maximum BAI’ (% max BAI) or the observed BAI divided by the 90th quantile BAI for the tree's DBH.

Climate and meteorological data

A priori, we expected the water balance of the sampling year to be the most important predictor of water stress, traits and growth, however, we included a range of meteorological (2018 water year or ‘2018 wy’) and climatological (30 yr climate norms, 1981–2010) as potential predictors. Climate normals and 2018 wy (1 October 2017 to 30 September 2018) data were extracted for each site from TerraClimate (Abatzoglou et al., 2018). TerraClimate has c. 4 km resolution monthly climate and water balance values for the global terrestrial surface from 1958 to 2020 (updated periodically). We included precipitation (PPT), evaporative demand (potential evapotranspiration, PET), and water balance metrics (actual evapotranspiration, AET; climate water deficit, CWD = PET–AET), plus maximum and minimum temperature for the historical period (T max, T min), plus 2018 wy annual and seasonal variables. See Methods S1 for full description of variables and selection philosophy. To avoid model overfitting, we only fit one climate/meteorological predictor in any model. When no climate variable was found to predict a quantity, we plotted it against 2018 wy CWD.

Soils data

To test whether site edaphic characteristics can predict water availability, traits, or growth, we extracted PAW, available water holding capacity (AWC), and minimum bedrock depth for each site from the Soil Survey Geographic Database (SSURGO). The data were collected at scales ranging from 1 : 20 000 to 1 : 24 000. Both AWC (0–100 cm) and minimum bedrock depth were taken from the soil map unit composition. In two sites, where minimum bedrock depth was not available, we used average data from the dominant soil series. PAW (cm) was pulled from the dominant soil series in the soil map unit.

Leaf gas exchange

For 1–4 trees at 7 sites (when time allowed while collecting water potentials), 16 total trees, we measured stomatal conductance (g s) and photosynthesis (A s) as a function of leaf water potential at midday. Using a LI‐6800 gas exchange system (LI‐COR Biosciences, Lincoln, NE, USA), we collected spot measurements of g s and A s, setting the chamber to ambient temperature, CO2 concentration and relative humidity, and light to 1500 μmol m−2 s−1. Measurements were made after the conditions in the chamber stabilized (c. 1 min) but before stomata began to react to the chamber environment (typically 3–5 min) and corrected for the leaf area in the cuvette. Water potential was then measured on a leaf immediately adjacent to the measured leaf. At one site, repeated gas exchange and water potential measurements were conducted from 07:00 h to 12:00 h on one tree to determine how the temporal relationship between stomatal conductance and leaf water potential compared to the spatial relationship across sites.

Mechanistic model simulations

We used the HOTTER model (the Hydraulic Optimization Theory for Tree and Ecosystem Resilience model) (Trugman et al., 2018; Quetin et al., 2023), a physiologically based single tree model run at a daily timestep, with a realistic representation of gas exchange (Eller et al., 2018) and a detailed representation of plant hydraulics (Trugman et al., 2018) to capture both the effects of supply limitation and VPD on Ψleaf, carbon balance, and hydraulic function (percent loss of conductivity, PLC). The goal was to integrate the nonlinear interactions between allocation, water availability and VPD to test whether daily tree performance at the time of sampling related to tree growth at longer timescales. We simulated each tree with tree‐specific allocation (A l : A s scaled to the whole tree) and water availability (ΨPD) and site‐specific meteorology for the sampling period (temperature, VPD from 17 to 27 September 2018) and assessed simulated ΨMD against observed ΨMD to determine model skill. See Methods S2 for specifics of model forcing and parameterization. We then compared simulated end‐of‐season hydraulic risk (PLC) and carbon gain (gross primary productivity, GPP, and net primary productivity, NPP = GPP – respiration) to observed growth to see whether performance under extreme stress (end of a very dry growing season) was correlated with performance at longer timescales.

Analysis

We averaged all measurements to individual for analysis (87 trees for water Ψ, 60 traits, 40 for LDMC, 57 for water isotopes, and 59 for growth). We used ANOVAs to calculate the among‐population effect size (η 2) and unbiased among‐population effect size (ω 2) for each trait. We then sought to explain spatial variation in plant water potentials (ΨPD, ΨMD, ΔΨ), plant traits (A l : A s, M l : M s, leaf size, LMA, LDMC), xylem stable water isotope composition (δ18O, δD, and lc excess), and growth (branch length, tree height, % max BAI) with climate or soils using Akaike's Information Criterion corrected (AICc for small sample sizes)‐based model selection in the R statistical environment (v.4.3.1, Team, 2016). We fit linear mixed models relating each response variable to each of the 24 climate, meteorological or soil predictors individually (described above) with a random intercept for site, using the lmer() function in the lme4 package (Bates et al., 2015). We selected the most parsimonious model of the 25 possible models (24 models with individual predictors plus a null model, Table S1) with AICc, ranking among predictors based on AICc score of their univariate models and considering predictors in single predictor models that reduced AICc by 2 or more units over the null to be significant (Zuur et al., 2009). We used t‐tests based on Satterthwaite's approximate degrees of freedom to determine the P‐value for the AICc‐selected predictors, using the R package (Kuznetsova et al., 2016). We report marginal R 2 (R 2 marg) values for attribute–climate relationships from these linear mixed models, which reflect the proportion of site‐to‐site variation explained. We then tested whether including A l : A s and an interaction between A l : A s and climate improved linear models for growth and ΨMD (see Methods S3).

We also used linear mixed effect models with a site random intercept to test whether: (1) xylem water stable isotopes explained tree‐to‐tree variation in plant water potentials; (2) water potential (either ΨPD, ΨMD, or ΔΨ) explained growth (either 5 yr BAI or 1 yr stem length); (3) traits predicted growth; (4) HOTTER output (PLC, GPP, or NPP) predicted growth. For these models, we report R 2 marg values as the proportion of tree‐to‐tree variation within site explained.

Functionally, mixed models with tree‐level predictors (1–4 above) tested whether, on average, there was a significant relationship among trees within a site. We also tested for significant relationships among sites rather than among trees using linear models of site‐level means, and report R 2 values from these linear models as the proportion of among‐site variation explained.

Finally, we used linear mixed effect models (site random intercept) and AICc to test whether adding A l : A s as an interaction with climate improved the best climate models of ΨMD or growth.

Results

Predicting drought stress

Despite sampling across large geographic differences in precipitation and potential evapotranspiration (Fig. 1), and despite massive differences between sites in leaf water potential (−0.82 to −3.78 MPa for ΨPD, −2.02 to −4.45 MPa for ΨMD), neither ΨPD nor ΨMD could be predicted by long‐term site climate, 2018 water year meteorology, or soil characteristics (Fig. 2). There was also no evidence that leaf area allocation (A l : A s) mediated the relationship between ΨMD and climate (null linear mixed model had lower AICc than all nested models of climate, A l : A s and their interaction, Fig. S2). The tree‐to‐tree variation within site was substantial (mean within site range of −1.3 MPa for both ΨPD and ΨMD) and was partially explained by tree size, with larger DBH trees having less negative ΨPD (Fig. S3). However, the between‐site variation still constituted 71% (ΨPD) and 51% (ΨMD) of the total water potential variation (Table S2). The ΔΨ (ΨPD–ΨMD, potential gradient caused by transpiration) increased with growing season VPD (linear mixed model P = 0.011). The average leaf hydraulic safety margin (HSM, ΨMD–P50leaf) was 0 or even negative for a number of both climatically wet and dry sites, and some individual trees from both wet and dry sites had negative stem hydraulic safety margins (ΨMD_xy–P50stem) (Fig. 2d). Despite these negative safety margins, no trees in four sites with smallest safety margins (HSM < 0.1 MPa) died following this study, based on high resolution satellite imagery from 2021 to 2025 (Google Earth Pro, v.7.3.6.10441).

Meanwhile, xylem stable isotope composition, but not evaporative enrichment, predicted leaf water potentials, both among sites and among trees within a site. Xylem water more enriched in δD and δ18O was marginally or significantly associated with more negative ΨPD, more negative ΨMD, and a smaller ΔΨ both among sites and among trees (Figs 3, S4; Table S3). Excluding one site (Pepperwood Preserve, ‘PWD’, gray in Fig. 3), which showed anomalously hydrated water potentials given its water isotopic signature, possibly due to a moderate severity wildfire 1 yr before sample collection, these relationships were even stronger. Excluding PWD, the among tree R 2 marg of δ18O was 0.2 (ΨPD) and 0.27 (ΨMD) and among site R 2 was 0.57 for ΨPD, 0.35 for ΨMD (Table S3). Precipitation‐weighted average meteoric δ18Oprecip/δDprecip of the site also predicted ΨPD (Fig. S5). However, xylem δD (though not δ18O) was still a significant predictor of ΨPD when meteoric composition was included as a covariate in the linear mixed model (P < 0.001 excluding PWD), and tree‐level residuals from the δDxylem~δDprecip relationship were also significantly related to ΨPD within site (Fig. S5). The lc excess, which is designed to isolate the effect of evaporative enrichment on ecosystem water, was unrelated to plant water potentials, at either the site or individual level.

Plant water potential also had profound effects on end of season leaf gas exchange. Across sites and trees within sites, midday stomatal conductance and assimilation steadily declined as ΨMD decreased toward −4 MPa (Fig. 4). The point of stomatal closure coincided quite closely with the previously reported P50 of leaves of −3.88 MPa, a threshold that was consistent across populations of blue oak (Skelton et al., 2019). This point of stomatal closure was also apparent in the morning time course of a single tree as its leaf water potential approached −3.88 near midday (Fig. 4). Thus, the consequences of observed water potential variation across the landscape for late season carbon gain and water loss were substantial. At two of the sampled sites, mean ΨPD was similar to or more negative than leaf P50 (Fig. 2), implying sustained stomatal closure and limited carbon gain at these sites for the entirety of the late growing season.

Predicting traits

All leaf and stem traits demonstrated significant variation among sites, ranging from 17% (M L : M S) to 58% (LDMC) of total trait variation (Table S2). Site climate or meteorology was a significant predictor of only two traits (M L : M S and leaf size), while soil water holding capacity predicted A L : A S and LMA (Fig. 5). M L : M S decreased with increasing minimum temperature of the sampling year (−0.48 ± 0.16 standardized effect size), and leaf size increased with increasing historical precipitation (0.43 ± 0.17 standardized effect size). Meanwhile, A L : A S increased and LMA decreased with soil water holding capacity (0.48 ± 0.17 and −0.46 ± 0.16 standardized effect sizes, respectively) and were not predicted by any climate variables when excluding soil predictors. LDMC was not predicted by any environmental variable, but had a smaller sample size.

End‐of‐season soil moisture and maximum water stress were almost completely unrelated to leaf and allocation traits. ΨPD was not correlated with any trait, and ΨMD was only correlated with LDMC (Fig. S6), with less negative ΨMD/larger HSMs being associated with higher LDMC (linear mixed effect models, P = 0.004). Moreover, traits were largely uncorrelated with each other. A L : A S was positively correlated with M L : M S, as well as leaf size (Fig. S6), but no other traits were significantly correlated.

Drought and trait relationships with prior growth

One year branch elongation and 5 yr basal area growth were significantly positively correlated, but the relationship was relatively weak (marginal R 2 = 0.12, P = 0.01). Both basal area growth from the prior 5 yr and (log10‐transformed) branch length of the current year's growth were almost completely unrelated to end of growing season soil moisture (ΨPD), maximum water stress (ΨMD) or ΔΨ, with the exception of current year branch length marginally significantly increasing with increasing ΔΨ (Fig. 6a,c). Moreover, no site water‐related climate variables outperformed the null model to explain either growth metric. Indeed, the only climate variables that had lower AICc than the null model were winter/spring temperature‐related, and the best climate variable for both 5 yr radial and 1 yr stem growth was 30 yr T min (Fig. 6b,d), with sites with warmer winter temperatures growing faster.

Mechanistic model synthesis

Daily HOTTER simulations of ΨMD, parameterized with observed, tree‐specific A L : A S and forced with tree‐specific end‐of‐season water availability (ΨPD) and site meteorology, were strongly correlated with observations (Fig. 8a). This was true both within‐site (R 2 marg = 0.52, RMSE = 0.33 MPa) and among sites (R 2 = 0.73, RMSE = 0.3 MPa). Variation in VPD did not strongly influence simulated ΨMD for most trees, though it did strongly control carbon uptake (Fig. S7). Simulated 2018 end‐of‐season PLC was completely unrelated to either observed 5 yr basal area growth (Fig. 8b) or 1 yr branch growth (Fig. S8). Meanwhile, simulated GPP at the time of sampling was marginally negatively correlated with observed 5 yr growth and unrelated to 1 yr growth (Figs 8c, S8c). At the same time, daily NPP at mean VPD, or photosynthesis minus temperature‐ and allocation‐dependent respiration, was negative for about half of trees and unrelated to observed 5 yr or 1 yr growth (Figs 8d, S8d).

Discussion

Our range‐wide survey of water availability, water stress, traits and growth in blue oak revealed substantial site‐to‐site variation in all quantities but remarkable disconnects between them. While isotopic proxies for site hydrology were strongly related to end‐of‐season soil water availability (ΨPD), site water balance and soil characteristics rarely predicted plant‐available soil water, water stress, traits, or growth, suggesting that existing water balance products do not capture the hydrological reality of these trees. Moreover, end‐of‐season water stress, leaf and allocation traits, and growth were largely unrelated to each other, and statistically incorporating climate interactions with leaf allocation did not improve our ability to explain spatial variation in either ΨMD or growth. A mechanistic model that integrated soil moisture limitation, sampling period VPD and tree‐specific allocation did skillfully capture variation in ΨMD within and among sites; however, the resulting hydraulic risk and daily carbon gain during the sampled drought simulated by a plant hydraulics model was unrelated to observed prior growth (either 1 or 5 yr). These results raise questions for both how we predict supply limitation and its effects in deeply rooted tree species in geologically and geomorphically complex landscapes, and how we conceptually integrate hydraulic risk and carbon gain in a highly seasonal environment.

Deep water access decouples water availability and stress from aboveground climate, making drought stress hard to predict

We found a remarkable decoupling of plant water status from aboveground climate. No single metric of water supply, demand, or balance (nor their interaction with A l : A s) could explain spatial variation in root‐zone soil moisture (ΨPD) or maximum water stress (ΨMD). This could be partly driven by topographic variation not captured by 4 km climate data, though most sites were on shallow slopes and results were very similar using climate normals from higher resolution products (e.g. 290 m BCM, Flint et al., 2013, data not shown). Moreover, a previous exhaustive study of blue oak ΨPD across a local elevation gradient found no relationship between topo‐edaphic factors (slope, aspect, elevation) (Knops & Koenig, 1994) even while year‐to‐year variation in precipitation drove interannual variation in ΨPD in the same trees (Knops & Koenig, 2000). Thus, we believe the issue is probably not climatic uncertainty, but rather the difficulty of capturing subsurface hydrology with water balance products.

The overriding importance of the subsurface hydrology, or the ‘weather underground’ (McLaughlin et al., 2020), is highlighted by the strong relationship between enriched xylem water stable isotope composition and negative plant water potential (both ΨPD and ΨMD). This impact of hydrology may be somewhat independent of rooting depth, as larger trees had less negative ΨPD presumably due to deeper roots yet did not have more depleted xylem isotopes indicative of stable water sources (Fig. S3). The relationship between ΨPD and xylem water isotopes partly reflects the influence of meteoric water (Fig. S5b), with precipitation isotopes proving a more successful predictor of Ψ than water balance metrics (Figs 2, 3, Table S2). In this system, spatial variation in precipitation‐weighted δD and δ18O is driven by the timing of precipitation and storm size, with more isotopically depleted sites receiving a larger fraction of rain as large, cold, winter storms. Coincidentally, these are the same types of storms likely to recharge shallow aquifers and fractured bedrock, providing deep, isotopically depleted water for blue oaks to use far into the growing season. Moisture balance metrics evidently did not capture the ecohydrological importance of recharge rate and timing in the same way.

Interestingly, meteoric water (δ18Oprecip) was a better predictor of ΨPD than was the evaporative enrichment (lc excess) of xylem water. We could not characterize isotopic end members for each of 15 sites and the absolute functional rooting depth implied by the isotopic values we and others have obtained (see Weitz, 2018) may vary based on site hydrology. However, water limitation was related to isotopic enrichment but not evaporative enrichment (e.g. lc excess), and all trees were similarly offset from the meteoric water line (Fig. S9). Most trees showed a similar amount of evaporative enrichment but the trees sourcing more depleted meteoric sources, presumably from large, winter storms held in deep, stable water reservoirs, remained the most hydrated by the end of the growing season. Collectively, these results highlight that improved representation of Critical Zone hydrology is key to improving spatial predictions of drought stress. Current soils data (either SSURGO or the coarser gridded soils used in water balance products) are insufficient to describe water supply limitation for deeply rooted species in geologically complex environments. However, the relationship between ΨPD and interpolated δ18Oprecip, which are empirically derived from only latitude, altitude, and a spatial interpolation of observations, suggests that the oak‐relevant variation in Critical Zone hydrology is regionally coherent/predictable even without site‐specific edaphic information.

Growth and hydraulic risk are decoupled in a Mediterranean‐type climate

Water stress clearly limits assimilation in blue oak across much of its range by the end of the growing season as leaf water potentials approach the point of substantial leaf embolism (leaf P50, Skelton et al., 2019). The decline in leaf gas exchange approaching leaf P50 was consistent in midday gas exchange rates across sites, within a single tree over the course of a morning (Fig. 4), and previously at a sight over the course of a growing season (see fig. 4 in Xu & Baldocchi, 2003). Such behavior is consistent with optimal stomatal behavior based on hydraulic cost (Anderegg et al., 2019; Sperry et al., 2016). Many sampled trees appeared quite close to the hydraulic edge by the end of 2018, with a number of trees surpassing leaf and stem P50 at midday and even predawn (Fig. 2), suggesting substantial stomatal limitation on carbon gain at the time of sampling.

If end‐of‐season water availability (and limitation to carbon gain) is correlated with conditions earlier in the growing season and growth is primarily water‐limited, 1 yr or even 5 yr tree growth should decline across sites with increasingly negative end‐of‐season ΨPD (and inferred carbon limitation). This was not the case (Fig. 6). Indeed, the simulated end‐of‐season hydraulic damage and gross photosynthesis from a plant hydraulics model, which captures our best understanding of the interacting effects of leaf allocation's impact on whole‐plant hydraulics, and stomatal balancing of carbon gain and hydraulic risk given VPD and supply limitation (ΨPD) at the time of sampling, had no or even a negative relationship with 5 yr observed radial growth and 1 yr branch growth (Figs 8, S8).

One explanation for this disconnect is that, across a c. 1000 mm gradient in annual precipitation, a site's relative end‐of‐season dryness is uncorrelated with its early season dryness. In Mediterranean‐type climates, this could be mediated by complex interactions between precipitation supply, transpiration rates, and critical zone water holding capacity, particularly in the poorly understood bedrock vadose zone (Dralle et al., 2020; Hahm et al., 2022). Related to this possibility, a phenological mismatch could decouple the times of year most critical for carbon gain/growth and those that determine hydraulic risk from drought stress. Due to California's Mediterranean‐type climate (cool wet winters and hot dry summers), photosynthesis in blue oak woodlands peaks in the spring and early summer, up to 50 d earlier than peak solar radiation and then declines precipitously (Ma et al., 2011; Xu & Baldocchi, 2003). Thus, the most productive growing season for blue oaks is a small fraction of their total leaf‐on period. Our results suggest that the water status of the later growing season, which determines blue oak's proximity to hydraulic damage thresholds, may be fundamentally disconnected from the early growing season factors that drive growth, even though growth itself is highly sensitive to water availability (Stahle et al., 2013, 2020).

A second temporal disconnect that may decouple growth from end‐of‐season water stress is the use of prior year(s) carbon to fuel current year growth. Lagged growth effects, memory effects, or legacy effects are ubiquitous in tree rings (Ogle et al., 2014; Anderegg et al., 2015; Klesse et al., 2023). In blue oak at dry sites and during dry years, turgor‐driven limits to radial growth likely manifest early in the growing season (when predawn water potentials drop below −0.8 MPa; Körner, 2015). Consequently, the carbon fixed in much of the leaf‐on period will support respiration and future growth rather than current growth. Thus, the end‐of‐season water limitation we document most likely influences future growth.

Finally, our HOTTER simulations may suggest a nontrivial role for respiration and possibly structural overshoot in determining late growing season carbon balance. At the maximum and mean daily VPDs during the sampling period, trees with a negative carbon balance (respiration exceeded GPP) showed a strong negative relationship between NPP and total tree leaf area (trees with more leaves had more negative carbon balance, Fig. S7). While HOTTER's respiration has not been as rigorously validated as its hydraulics, observations of ΨPD at or beyond the point of stomatal closure (Fig. 2a) suggest that a substantial number of trees never opened stomata during the day, supporting the importance of end‐of‐season respiration for tree carbon balance.

Our results also raise a subsidiary question: Why do the spatial controls on blue oak growth not mirror the interannual controls on growth? Blue oak is a canonical dendroclimatology species (Stahle et al., 2013), often considered ‘better than rain gauges’ for reconstructing precipitation. Yet spatial variation in growth was not related to either tree‐level end‐of‐season water availability, water‐related climate/meteorological variables, or their interaction with A l : A s. Instead, spatial variation in growth was correlated with T min, suggesting that processes such as the onset of leaf out may control site‐to‐site variation. Recently, contrasting spatial and temporal growth sensitivities to climate have been documented in multiple tree species and attributed to local adaptation or acclimation (Canham et al., 2018; Perret et al., 2024). This appears to strongly be the case for blue oaks and is supported by the large among‐site variation in leaf and allocation traits (Table S1), hydraulic traits other than P50 (Anderegg et al., 2024), and ecotypic variation in seedling performance (McLaughlin et al., 2022; Poudel et al., 2025).

Traits have complicated cause‐vs‐effect relationships with growth and hydraulic risk

End of season leaf traits, with the exception of leaf size, were generally unrelated to end of season plant water potentials (Fig. S6), water balance‐related climate variables (Fig. 5), and 5 or 1 yr growth rates (Fig. 7). Leaf to stem allocation, either expressed as A l : A s or M l : M s, was strongly negatively related to growth rates. This directly contrasts with theory (Lambers & Poorter, 1992) and observations in herbs (Poorter et al., 2012) and may suggest that branch‐level A l : A s is the consequence rather than cause of growth variation. At the level of individual branches, A l : A s independently increases with branch leaf size and decreases with branch length, potentially suggesting a joint allocation (leaf area) and structural (branch length) control on branch‐level A l : A s, with longer branches requiring more branch tissue (Fig. S10, Table S4). Alternatively, it could suggest that A l : A s negatively impacted growth through a structural overshoot, which HOTTER simulations suggest to occur under high VPDs (Fig. S7). Full simulations for the entire growing period, or better yet 5 yr growth period, would be needed to actually test for overshoot (hydraulic or carbon costs exceeding carbon returns). However, the 5 yr basal growth period included the end of massive 2012–2016 drought (Williams et al., 2022), and the 1 yr branch growth captured a dry year as well (Fig. 1b). It is possible that high allocation to leaf area was maladaptive during both of these periods.

Ultimately, the large site‐to‐site variation in all traits suggests that traits are responding to a complex combination of environmental cues and endogenous factors (e.g. growth rate) that we still do not fully understand. Functional ecology often focuses on the consequences of traits for fitness components such as growth or survival (Violle et al., 2007). However, our results highlight that, particularly within species, trait variation can be challenging to interpret, as it represents (presumably) adaptive responses to maximize sometimes orthogonal fitness components. For instance, in blue oak, leaf morphology and allocation to leaves vs stems may be simultaneously maximizing early growing season carbon gain and minimizing end of growing season mortality risk due to hydraulic damage, which we found to be largely unrelated. Add in the feedback effects of growth rate on traits themselves and the difficulty of disentangling cause vs effect becomes extremely challenging. The mechanistic modeling application presented here was a first attempt to synthesize the interactions between supply limitation, evaporative demand, and allocation traits and could provide a useful approach for exploring the cause of the disconnection between traits, growth, and end‐of‐season drought stress.

Conclusion

We conducted a range‐wide survey of blue oak supply limitation and drought stress at the end of a substantial drought year, visiting 15 sites over a mean annual precipitation gradient from 280 mm to 1242 mm. We found a remarkable disconnect between site water balance, end‐of‐season drought stress, growth, and drought avoidance‐related allocation traits. These results highlight our profound uncertainty about the edaphic environment of deeply rooted tree species and the importance of illuminating Critical Zone hydrology to understand drought‐induced mortality. Our results also illustrate how, particularly in Mediterranean‐type climates, the temporal mismatch between water stress and the early season conditions that determine growth and allocation traits challenges our simple assumptions about maximizing carbon gain and organismal performance under water limitation.
