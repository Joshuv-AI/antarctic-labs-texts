Evaluation of a Novel Multielectrode Surgical Pulsed Field Ablation Clamp: An Ex-Vivo Vegetal Study
Authors: Sanjai Pattu Valappil, Mohamed Abbas, Jonathan Prashanth Ariyaratnam, Chris Robbins, Mohanaraj Jayakumar, Alexis Puvrez, Melanie Anuscha Gunawardene, Michael Kroon, Ian Fong, Ashutosh Hardikar, Prashanthan Sanders, Jason Varzaly
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesAt present, there is no commercially available surgical device to perform cardiac ablation utilizing pulsed field ablation (PFA). We sought to explore the efficacy and requirements of a novel PFA clamp to achieve transmural lesions in a vegetal model of Tuberosum Solanum (TS).MethodsWe tested the PFA clamp at different voltages (1400 V, 2000 V, 2800 V) and numbers of applications (2 or 4) on slabs of TS having different thicknesses (10 mm, 20 mm, 30 mm). Each slab was sliced transversally and the halves put together or kept whole. Every possible combination was tested twice. After 72 hours, we observed the resulting necrotic tissue and calculated its volume. We assessed the determinants of %volume of necrotic tissue, defined as observed volume relative to the ideal volume of necrotic tissue.ResultsA transmural lesion was noted in 68 (94%) of 72 specimens. Using 4 applications, there was a strong linear relationship between voltage and %volume regardless of specimen thickness (R2 = 0.94, R2 = 0.96, R2 = 0.97, all P < .001) but not when using 2 applications (R2 = 0.37, R2 = 0.87, R2 = 0.69 for 10 mm, 20 mm, and 30 mm, respectively, all P < .05). The voltages at which 100% volume was observed using 4 applications—corresponding to the curve intersections—are 958 V, 928 V, and 1044 V for the 10 mm, 20 mm, and 30 mm specimens, respectively.ConclusionsThe epicardial PFA clamp reliably produced transmural lesions in a vegetal model of TS using 2 or 4 applications. With 4 applications, lesion volume demonstrated a predictable, linear relationship with increasing voltage, supporting the device’s potential for controlled and consistent ablation.

INTRODUCTION

Pulsed field ablation (PFA) is a non-thermal ablation modality which works by the concept of irreversible electroporation. The ultra-short duration of high-intensity direct current (DC) electric fields applied results in transmembrane pores causing myocardial cell death.1 The remarkable ability of PFA to preferentially target cardiac tissue, sparing adjacent tissues, has neutralized the risks associated with thermal injury.1 The first in man trials with single-shot PFA catheters have consistently showed excellent safety profile and acute procedural success.2,3 The remapping studies and real-world data have demonstrated equivalent efficacy in terms of durable pulmonary vein isolation (PVI) and clinical outcomes.4 Currently, there is a paucity of ablation tools for surgical application. The availability of an epicardial clamp, which could deliver PFA energy during cardiac surgery, would be a welcome addition to the surgical arsenal. At present, 2 principal categories of surgical PFA clamps are undergoing assessment in pre-clinical models, differentiated primarily by the temporal characteristics of their pulse delivery, namely within the nanosecond and microsecond domains.5,6

Our group developed a novel multielectrode microsecond epicardial clamp to perform PFA during cardiac surgery. We sought to investigate the efficacy of this PFA clamp and explore the requirements to achieve a transmural lesion in a vegetal model using Tuberosum Solanum (TS). The TS has been shown to be a reliable and reproducible platform for assessment of PFA effectiveness.7

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Ethical statement

The biophysical effects on the TS specimens were analysed after application of energy using the PFA clamp. As a vegetal model was studied, no Institutional Review Board (IRB) approval was required.

Description of the PFA system

The epicardial PFA system consists of a generator and a parallel closing clamp. The generator produces a DC pulse delivered to the tissue through the electrodes on the jaws of the clamp. Each side of the clamp has 5 electrodes, spaced 5 mm apart, facing each other. Each electrode is “pill” shaped, measuring 6.63 mm in length with rounded edges. The length and width of each arm of the clamp housing the electrodes are 55 mm and 10 mm, respectively. The delivery of the PFA energy occurs by the creation of a localized DC electric field between the 2 opposing pairs of electrodes. The device is equipped with 2 arms, which allow the operator to precisely control the distance between the 2 jaws of the clamp. During use, the jaws are carefully positioned around the target tissue specimen and then tightened, ensuring stable contact for effective energy delivery (Figure 1).

PFA Application

Every PFA delivery consists of 7 pulse trains, comprising of 10-15 biphasic bipolar pulses.8 During each application, a cumulative pulse duration of 100-150 microseconds is delivered via a sequence of microsecond-scale pulses. The total duration of a single application, defined from the onset of the first pulse to the conclusion of the final pulse, was 7 seconds.

We tested the PFA clamp at 3 different voltages (2800 V/2000V/1400V) and number of applications (2 or 4) on the slabs of TS. Every possible combination of voltage, number of applications, slab thickness, and slicing was tested twice. A total of 72 specimens of TS were used for the study. The specimens were sliced into slabs with a thickness of 10 mm, 20 mm, or 30 mm. Each slab was either sliced transversally, and the halves put together to mimic the clinical situation with a virtual lumen or kept whole. The slabs were then placed in a saline bath (0.9% NaCl) for 2 hours.

Analysis of lesion morphology

Post PFA application, the specimens were placed in a room maintained at a targeted temperature of 24 °C. The specimens were under continuous observation, and lesion completion was assumed when a definite black border demarcating the edges of the ablation lesion was noted due to melanin generation, a reliable marker of irreversible electroporation.9

After completion of lesion evolution, we calculated the volume of necrotic tissue assuming a cuboid, trapezoid prism, or 2 opposing triangular prisms (Figure 2). The ablation lesion measurements were made with a ruler by 2 independent investigators (SPV/MA). We then assessed the relationship between thickness, slicing, number of applications, and voltage to the %volume of necrotic tissue. The %volume was defined as the observed volume relative to the ideal volume. The ideal volume was assumed to be a transmural ablation lesion spanning the length and breadth of the PFA clamp. The ideal lesion is thus a cuboid with the length and breadth determined by the dimensions of the clamp, and the height given by the slab thickness. A %volume <100% indicated non-transmural lesion. The transmurality and the morphology of the ablation lesions were additionally assessed by sectioning the TS specimens in their long axis in all cases. We also analysed the shapes and the surface characteristics of the necrotic lesions with respect to the voltage and the number of applications delivered.

Statistical analysis

Lesion shapes were categorized into 5 mutually exclusive groups (apple-core, hourglass, narrow cuboid, spool, wide cuboid). To examine whether the distribution of shapes differed across experimental factors, we constructed contingency tables of shape counts by factor level and applied Pearson’s chi-square test of independence. Where expected cell counts were small, we verified results with Fisher’s exact test.

Multiple linear regression was used to evaluate the association between specimen thickness, slicing method, number of applications, voltage and the %volume of necrotic tissue. The model was fit using ordinary least squares. The %volume of necrotic tissue was treated as a continuous outcome. Number of applications, thickness, and slicing method were included as fixed categorical predictors, voltage as a fixed continuous predictor. There was no collinearity (Variance Inflation Factor <5), and normality of residuals was confirmed using the Shapiro-Wilk and Kolmogorov-Smirnov tests.

To further explore these relationships, analyses were repeated after stratification according to the number of applications and specimen thickness. The resulting functions were used to determine the predicted voltage at which %volume = 100% for each specimen thickness. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 10.0.0 for Windows (GraphPad Software). A P-value of ≤.05 was considered statistically significant.

PFA applications causing distinct shapes of lesion

There were 5 characteristic shapes of lesions observed: apple-core (non-transmural lesion), hourglass (transmural lesion with waist at the centre), narrow cuboid (ideal lesion), spool-shaped (narrow cuboid lesion with only surface extension), and wide cuboid (increased length and breadth) (Figure 4).

Non-transmural apple-core lesions were seen only with the lowest energy (1400 V/2 applications) on the thickest tissue specimens (30 mm). There is limited extension of ablation lesions to the depth of the tissue, resulting in the formation of tapering lesions characterized as opposing triangular prisms (Figure 5). The ideal lesion is a transmural lesion presenting as a narrow cuboid. Narrow cuboid lesions were more commonly seen with 2 applications compared with 4 applications (66.7% vs 11.1% P < 0.001). In two instances (1400 V/2 applications/20 mm and 2000 V/2 applications/30 mm), the lesions had funnelling at the depth of the tissue, which were farthest away from the ablation electrodes, resulting in an hourglass morphology.

Determinants of ideal lesion formation

A transmural lesion was noted in 68/72 (94%) slabs. All specimens treated with 4 applications had a transmural lesion extension. Transmural lesion was obtained with 2 applications in all specimens except when using 1400 V on the 30 mm slabs. Multiple linear regression showed that the specimen thickness (β = –0.44, 95% CI, –0.82 to –0.05, P = .028), voltage (β = 0.03, 95% CI, 0.03 to 0.03, P < .001) and number of applications (β = 23.70, 95% CI, 20.50 to 26.80, P < .001) significantly influenced the resulting %volume. Slicing methodology did not affect %volume (β = –2.06, 95% CI, –8.37 to 4.25, P = 0.517).

After stratification, we found there was a strong linear relationship between voltage and %volume using 4 applications regardless of specimen thickness (R2 = 0.94, P < .001; R2 = 0.96, P < .001; and R2 = 0.97, P < .001 for 10 mm, 20 mm, and 30 mm, respectively) but not when using 2 applications (R2 = 0.37, P = .038; R2 = 0.87, P < .001; and R2 = 0.69, P < 0.001 for 10 mm, 20 mm, and 30 mm, respectively). Increasing specimen thickness affected %volume negatively only when using 2 applications (β = –1.01, 95% CI, –1.56 to –0.45, P < .001), but not when using 4 applications (β = 1.36, 95% CI, –0.09 to 0.36, P = .232).

Extrapolating the strong linear relationship noted with 4 applications across all specimen thicknesses, the possible ideal voltages at which observed %volume = 100% are shown where the curves intersect: 958 V for 10 mm specimens, 928 V for 20 mm specimens, and 1044 V for the 30 mm specimens, respectively. Though the 2 applications did not result in a strong linear relationship, it was noted that, with 2 applications at all tested voltages, the observed volumes on the 10 mm and 20 mm specimens were in the range of the ideal volume (Figure 6).

DISCUSSION

This study demonstrates that it is possible to achieve a transmural lesion using the novel PFA clamp. The ablative efficacy of the clamp was seen over varying levels of PFA energy applications. We found that the resulting necrotic tissue volume has a predictable and linear relationship with voltage when using 4 applications. However, significantly more surface lesion extension was also noted with 4 applications when compared to 2 applications. The uniformity of the PFA lesions with the clamp is an emboldening observation as it potentially paves the way for creation of a quick, efficient and reliable means of surgical ablation.

Barriers to performing concomitant surgical ablation of AF during cardiac surgical procedures

It has been observed that less than 40% of patients undergoing mitral valve surgery who have AF receive concomitant surgical AF ablation, despite a class I recommendation.10,11,12 The reasons for the low rate of adoption of concomitant surgical AF ablation are multifactorial.

The Cox-Maze IV is technically challenging as the procedure relies on the use of three different modalities for creating ablation lesions: radiofrequency (RF) ablation, cryoablation, and the conventional surgical cut and sew technique.13 The use of the bipolar RF clamp has the potential to cause tissue heating and thermal injury in the form of PV stenosis, phrenic nerve injury and the life-threatening atrio-oesophageal fistula (AEF).13 A systematic review of observational cases of AEF due to RF energy-mediated thermal injury identified 12 out of 53 cases (22.6%) of AEF to result from surgical/epicardial RF ablation and 41 out of 53 cases (77.4%) from catheter-based ablation.14 Another meta-analysis from 2017 reported 120 cases of AEF from the various thermal energy sources {catheter-based RF ablation 92 patients (76.6%), surgical epicardial ablation 14 patients (11.6%), percutaneous cryo-balloon ablation 12 patients (10%) and high intensity focused ultrasound 2 patients (1.6%)}.15 It is pertinent to note that there were numerically higher number of patients with surgical ablation than cryo-balloon ablation who developed AEF, which highlights the importance of surgeons to be diligent to mitigate this complication in all ways possible.

The novelty and merits of a surgical PFA clamp

The advent of PFA has the ability to reduce the barriers to surgical ablation of AF and expand the number of patients having arrhythmia treatment, and improve survival for patients.16 In PFA, the principle of ablation occurs by the concept of irreversible electroporation and not by thermal injury. The epicardial PFA system consists of a generator and a parallel closing clamp. The generator produces a DC pulse that is delivered to the tissue through the electrodes on the jaws of the clamp. The ablation lesions would theoretically be transmural as the PFA energy would be delivered by creation of a bipolar bidirectional DC electric field across the entire tissue thickness. Currently, the delivery of PFA by the use of single-shot PFA ablation catheters relies on the creation of DC electric fields between the electrodes of the catheter, which need to be in sufficient contact with the endocardial tissue. Indeed, computational modelling analysis and animal studies have shown that ablation lesion efficacy in terms of transmurality is closely related to the adequacy of electrode contact to the myocardial tissue.17,18 Though these ablation catheters have demonstrated equivalent levels of efficacy, there have been issues with haemolysis and potential for coronary artery spasm.19 The strength of the DC electric field created during the application of the surgical epicardial clamp would plausibly have minimal effect on the circulating blood pool owing to its design and extrinsic application, theoretically minimizing hemolysis.20

Electrode design configuration to minimize arcing and improve lesion uniformity

To ensure uniform ablation lesions with the PFA clamp, we conducted extensive pre-clinical research focused on optimizing electrode configuration. A key consideration in the design was minimizing the risk of electrical arcing between electrodes during PFA application. Arcing and heat generation are more likely when DC is applied across larger, linear electrodes with sharper edges. To address this, the clamp was engineered with 5 smaller, rounded, pill-shaped electrodes on each jaw. This segmented electrode layout enables the application of ultrashort durations of discrete DC electric fields across opposing electrode pairs, effectively reducing the potential for arcing while enhancing lesion consistency (Figure 1).

Additional experiments with a high-resolution infrared thermal imaging camera, FLIR E54 (Teledyne FLIR LLC), were also done to record the thermal signature of the clamp. The mean temperature rise during PFA energy delivery in the vegetal model was 1.11 ± 0.11 °C at the highest voltage (2800 V and 4 applications). This temperature rise seen during PFA application is due to “Joule heating” of the electrodes and is too minimal to be associated with ablation lesion formation or arcing21(Figure S1).

The novel multielectrode configuration enables selective deactivation of electrodes. This functionality is particularly relevant in scenarios where the epicardial clamp extends beyond the margins of smaller pulmonary vein (PV) ostia. In such cases, electrode overhang may result in direct contact between opposing electrodes outside the anatomical confines of the PV, potentially leading to electrical arcing. Another important consideration in the design of multielectrode configuration was the limitation associated with delivering a single, large DC electric field using a solitary electrode. Due to spatial variations in tissue impedance, such an approach can result in non-uniform electric field distribution and heterogeneous ablation zones. To address this, the use of multiple smaller electric fields was incorporated, allowing for more consistent ablation.

Microsecond versus nanosecond PFA

A key discriminator between PFA technologies lies in the duration of the delivered electrical pulses, which have significant biophysical implications. Standard PFA systems typically employ microsecond pulses, with durations ranging from 1 to 100 microseconds (µs). There has been extensive research into µs -PFA technology, and almost all of the commercially approved single-shot PFA catheters use µs -PFA. In contrast, nanosecond pulsed-field ablation (ns-PFA) technologies utilize ultrashort pulses, generally in the range of 10 to 1000 nanoseconds (ns), representing a pulse duration approximately 10 to 100 times shorter than that of conventional PFA.22 Pre-clinical animal studies have shown that ns-PFA can mitigate the risk of muscle stimulation seen with µs-PFA and the need for general anaesthesia (GA) or deep sedation during PFA delivery.23 Though there are no commercially available PFA clamps for surgical application, Zemlin et al. have extensively evaluated an ns-PFA clamp (Pulse Biosciences) and have demonstrated excellent lesion durability in acute and chronic porcine models.24,25 However, the shorter pulse widths (300 nanoseconds) necessitated higher voltages for generation of the ns-PFA electric fields, to the tune of 10-12 kV. Indeed, in the preclinical studies with the ns-PFA clamp, the operators had to dial down on the energy from 12 kV to 10 kV due to arcing between the electrodes.26

Our group chose to incorporate µs -PFA based on the strength of its evidence with single-shot catheter-based PFA technology. We performed additional experiments to test the thermal signatures of our epicardial clamp and reliably demonstrated no evidence of arcing with our system.

Translational outlook

We attempted to check the efficacy of the PFA clamp with the specimens sliced to mimic a virtual lumen. There was no difference in the ablation lesion characteristics if the specimens were sliced or whole. The average thickness of the atrial tissue is around 5 mm, which is well within the range of the tested specimens. Our study is notable for the fact that the effectiveness of the clamp was tested in a wide span of specimen thickness, and the PFA clamp was consistently able to achieve transmural lesions with 4 applications, even in the 30 mm samples. We believe that 2-3 applications in the range 2000 V would suffice for transmural ablation in the atrial tissue, and the 4 applications would be the choice in thicker myocardial tissue like the ventricles.

We aim to follow-up this study with an animal model looking at the effectiveness and safety of the PFA clamp in the atrium. The vegetal model study offers significant insight into the ablative range of the clamp and will help us titrate the energy accordingly during the animal study.

Limitations

This study used vegetal model to analyze the efficacy of a novel epicardial PFA clamp. While the linear regression analysis is an oversimplification of complex bioelectric interactions, it provided a practical framework for determining a theoretical voltage threshold for lesion transmurality to guide further research. The authors appreciate that the efficacy and clinical applicability of the clamp need to be corroborated by subsequent animal and human studies. Based on our results, no conclusion regarding possible safety benefits can be drawn.

CONCLUSION

The novel epicardial PFA clamp reliably produced transmural lesions in a vegetal model of TS using both 2 and 4 applications. When using 4 applications, lesion volume demonstrated a predictable, linear relationship with increasing voltage, supporting the device’s potential for controlled and consistent ablation.
