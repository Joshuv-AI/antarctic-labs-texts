Multifractal Characteristics of Pore Structure in Deep Coal Reservoirs of the Suide Block, Ordos Basin
Authors: Yunhe Shi, Chang Xu, Kangle Wang, Hui Zhang, Chen Liang, Huaichang Wang, Jingjing Cao, Xueyuan Jing, Yi Du
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Exploration and development of deep coalbed methane represents a critical strategy for ensuring national energy security. This research concentrates on the No. 8 coal seam of the Benxi Formation in the Suide Block. A comprehensive analytical suiteincluding field emission scanning electron microscopy, low-temperature CO2 adsorption, low-temperature N2 adsorption, and high-pressure mercury intrusion porosimetrywas integrated with multifractal theory to quantitatively characterize the pore structure and heterogeneity of deep coal reservoirs and to decipher their geological controlling factors. Results indicate that the No. 8 coal is dominated by organic pores, with micropores accounting for an average of 86.26% of the total pore system. Micropores, mesopores, and macropores all exhibit distinct multifractal characteristics. Their generalized dimension spectra [D(q)–q] display an inverted “S” shape, while the multifractal singularity spectra [f(α)−α] follow a convex parabolic profile. Compared to micropores and mesopores, macropores possess the highest heterogeneity and the poorest connectivity. Analysis of controlling factors reveals that ash yield is negatively correlated with pore volume but positively correlated with fixed carbon content and thermal maturity. Furthermore, high ash yield significantly impairs the connectivity of mesopores and macropores, whereas its impact on micropores is primarily limited to the reduction of pore volume. This study provides a quantitative basis for evaluating the pore structure and heterogeneity of deep coal reservoirs.

Introduction

Coalbed methane (CBM), as a significant unconventional oil and gas resource, holds dual strategic importance for both environmental protection and disaster mitigation. − Since 2021, persistent advancements in deep CBM exploration and production have been recorded. ,, Breakthrough progress in CBM exploration at depths exceeding 2000 m has been reported across multiple regions, including the Linxing, Daning–Jixian Block, and Shenfu area in the Ordos Basin, as well as the southeastern Sichuan Basin and the thrust belt on the southeastern margin of the Junggar Basin. − Consequently, deep CBM has emerged as a frontier field for exploration and development. ,− According to the latest CBM resource assessments by the Ministry of Natural Resources of China, mid-deep CBM resources at depths of 1000–2000 m are estimated at 18.72 × 1012 m3. ,,− Furthermore, CBM resources at depths over 2000 m are projected to exceed 40 × 1012 m3, with approximately 18–20 × 1012 m3 situated between 2000 and 3000 m. −

Compared to shallow-to-medium-depth counterparts, deep CBM reservoirs are situated in complex geological environments characterized by “three highs”: high geotemperature, high reservoir pressure, and high in situ stress. These conditions lead to significant differences in the characteristics of coal rock, coal quality, and pore structure. Crucially, the microscopic pore structure largely dictates the gas-bearing capacity and recovery efficiency of coal reservoirs. ,, As the primary space for gas storage and transport, the study of pore structures is fundamental to understanding gas adsorption and flow dynamics. In recent years, an increasing array of advanced techniques has been deployed for pore structure characterization, which can be broadly categorized into two groups: fluid invasion methods and radiation-based methods. The former encompasses mercury intrusion porosimetry (MIP), LT-N2GA, LT-CO2GA, thermoporometry, and helium pycnometry. The latter comprises field emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM), transmission electron microscopy (TEM), small-angle X-ray scattering (SAXS), small-angle neutron scattering (SANS), and nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR). − Coal pores typically exhibit pronounced heterogeneity. To better describe such structural complexity, fractal theoryoriginally proposed by Mandelbrothas been widely adopted. Furthermore, multifractal theory, by extending a single fractal dimension into a continuous singularity spectrum function, enables a more precise and detailed characterization of the heterogeneous pore structures in porous media. ,,−

Previous research has primarily focused on the eastern Ordos Basin, establishing comprehensive evaluation frameworks for the Daning–Jixian, Shenmu, and Linxing blocks. In contrast, deep CBM exploration in the Suide Block remains in its nascent stage. Progress in this area has been hindered by significant burial depths and pronounced reservoir heterogeneity. , This study targets the deep No. 8 coal seam in the Suide Block. Following an analysis of basic petrophysical parameters, a multimethod analytical approachcomprising FE-SEM, LT-CO2GA, LT-N2GA, and HPMIwas employed to achieve high-precision quantitative characterization of the full-range pore structure. We apply multifractal theory to quantify the pore heterogeneity and connectivity of these high-rank coals. Additionally, we discuss how proximate analysis, macerals, and thermal maturity influence the pore structure. The objective of this investigation is to establish a scientific foundation for the accelerated exploration of deep CBM in the Suide Block and the characterization of pore structure.

Geological Setting

The Ordos Basin is a vast sedimentary domain bounded by multiple tectonic belts. It is characterized as a large, asymmetrical syncline of Mesozoic age developed upon the Paleozoic North China Craton (Figure a). As the second-largest sedimentary basin in China, it contains a comprehensive stratigraphic record spanning the Proterozoic, Lower Paleozoic, Upper Paleozoic, Mesozoic, and Cenozoic eras. ,,,,

(a) Regional location of the Suide Block; (b) generalized stratigraphic column.

The Suide Block is situated in the transition zone between the Yishan slope and the West Shanxi flexural belt. This area is characterized by relatively gentle structural gradients and a sparse fault development. Three sets of coal-bearing strata are developed within the study area: the Lower Permian Taiyuan and Shanxi Formations and the Upper Carboniferous Benxi Formation. The no. 8 coal seam, situated at the summit of the Benxi Formation, is the primary objective of CBM exploration and development (Figure B). The thickness of this coal seam varies from around 1 to 11 m, exhibiting a gradually thickening trend toward the northeast.

Samples and Methods

The coal samples analyzed in this study were obtained from the No. 8 coal seam, retrieved from four wells (W1, W2, W3, and W4) in the Suide Block, Ordos Basin (Figure a), with sampling depths ranging from approximately 2250 to 2550 m. In accordance with the national standards GB/T 6948–2008 “Microscopic Determination of Vitrinite Reflectance in Coal,” GB/T 30732–2014 “Instrumental Methods for the Industrial Analysis of Coal,” and GB/T 8899–1998 “Methods for the Determination of Microscopic Fractions and Minerals in Coal,” the samples were tested for vitrinite reflectance (R o), microscopic composition and industrial analysis, respectively.

In order to qualitatively observe the pore morphology and genetic types of coal samples, FE-SEM analysis was carried out on the samples. Representative samples were cut into small pieces and polished using an argon ion milling system to form a flat and smooth surface. To ensure conductivity, a thin layer of gold was sprayed on the polished surface. A field emission scanning electron microscope of model QUANTA FEG 450 was used for observation. Coal samples were pulverized and sieved to a diameter of 60–80 mesh in order to characterize their pore structure. They were then subjected to vacuum drying and degassing pretreatment. The specific conditions for pretreatment are as follows: first, dry at 60 °C–80 °C until a constant weight is achieved, and then degas at 120 °C for 6–8 h. Subsequently, LT-CO2GA for micropore analysis was executed at 273.15 K with an approximate pressure (P/P 0) spanning 0.0001–0.0300, while LT-N2GA for mesopore analysis was conducted at 77 K with P/P 0 spanning 0.005 to 0.998 in line with GB/T 21650.3–2011. The Density Functional Theory (DFT) model was employed to analyze the CO2 adsorption data, focusing on the volume and surface area of micropores (<2 nm). The Barrett–Joyner–Halenda (BJH) model was applied to the N2 adsorption/desorption isotherms, while HPMI porosimetry was conducted on cubic specimens to determine macropore features utilizing the Washburn equation.

Result and Discussion

Coal Petrological and Geochemical Characteristics

Based on lithological observations, the macroscopic coal types of the No. 8 coal seam in the research region are predominantly semibright to bright coal. The coal body structure primarily exhibits original textures, with localized development of fractures (Figure ). The results of proximate analysis, maceral composition, and vitrinite reflectance for nine samples from four wells (W1, W2, W3, and W4) are summarized in Table . The No. 8 coal is characterized by low moisture content (0.43%–1.81%, average. 0.96%), low ash yield (2.31%–14.99%, average. 7.53%), and low volatile matter (5.48%–9.03%, average. 6.94%). Conversely, the fixed carbon content is relatively high, ranging from 75.15% to 89.11%, with a mean of 81.24%. Regarding maceral composition, vitrinite is the dominant group (average. 77.62%), followed by inertinite (average. 16.50%), while liptinite content is extremely low (average. 0.40%). The R o,max values fluctuate between 2.06% to 3.01%, signifying a high-to-overmature thermal evolution stage. Consequently, the coal samples are classified as anthracite.

Microscopic Pore Structure Characteristics

Pore Characteristics under Scanning Electron Microscope

FE-SEM observations (Figure ) reveal that the coal macerals are predominantly composed of vitrinite, which is characterized by a moderate degree of internal pore development. The pore system is primarily dominated by organic matter pores, encompassing both primary residual cellular pores (Figure b,c,f) and secondary gas pores (Figure e), and some of them are filled with minerals. The latter originates from the generation and expulsion of hydrocarbons during coalification as thermal maturity advances. These pores, typically ellipsoidal or irregular in morphology, exhibit restricted interpore connectivity; however, their high spatial aggregation makes them the principal contributors to the specific surface area (SSA) of the micropore and mesopore systems. Additionally, the coal matrix hosts a series of exogenous fractures, including shear fractures induced by tectonic stress (Figure B) and tensile fractures (Figure D). These fractures generally display limited connectivity and high degrees of closure with localized occlusion by secondary calcite mineralization, further restricting their potential as fluid pathways.

Scanning electron microscope (SEM) images of the No.8 coal seam (a–f).

LT-CO2GA Isotherms and Pore-Size Distribution

LT-CO2GA results indicate that the adsorption isotherms of the nine coal samples from the research area display similar morphological properties. According to the IUPAC classification standards, , all nine isotherms are identified as Type I isotherms (Figure a). These isotherms are characterized by a sharp, nearly vertical increase in CO2 adsorption capacity at the extremely low relative pressure range (P/P 0 < 0.01). This behavior reflects the primary micropore filling mechanism, where the strong overlapping potential from opposite pore walls enhances adsorption in pores of molecular dimensions. As the pressure increases, the adsorption curve gradually reaches a plateau, signifying the completion of micropore filling. Notably, sample Q8 exhibits a maximum adsorption capacity significantly lower than that of the other samples, indicating a relatively impoverished micropore system and a lower degree of micropore development. The coal samples’ cumulative pore volume curves are generally consistent in shape (Figure b), characterized by a slight increase in adsorption capacity between 0.3–0.35 nm, followed by a rapid escalation within the 0.45–0.6 nm range. The PSD curves (Figure ) reveal a distinct bimodal distribution pattern. The first peak is narrow and ranges from 0.34 to 0.39 nm. The second peak is broader and spans from 0.45 to 0.7 nm. This second peak represents the dominant pore size range for micropores. Furthermore, the peak positions for SSA and PV exhibit high spatial consistency. In terms of volumetric contribution, the micropores within the first and second peak intervals contribute similarly to the total pore volume (Figure a). However, regarding their contribution to the total SSA, the first peak range exerts a slightly greater influence than the second peak range (Figure b).

LT-N2GA Isotherms and Pore-Size Distribution

The results of LT-N2GA (77 K) are presented in Figure and Table . Significant variations in N2 adsorption capacity are observed among various samples, with overall adsorption volumes spanning from 1.7452 to 8.4076 cm3/g. The N2 adsorption isotherms exhibit an inverted “S” shape, classified as Type IV isotherms. At extremely low relative pressures (P/P 0 < 0.01), micropores are filled sequentially, leading to a rapid initial increase in adsorption. Monolayer adsorption occurs within the P/P 0 range of 0.01–0.3, which is also the interval used to calculate the monolayer saturation adsorption capacity. Multilayer adsorption (0.30 < P/P 0 < 0.95) begins after the monolayer stage is complete. During this phase, the adsorption volume increases continuously and at an accelerating rate. This results in a linear rise of the isotherm. Finally, capillary condensation occurs at higher relative pressures, with adsorption reaching saturation as P/P 0 approaches 1. In accordance with the IUPAC classification of hysteresis loops, , most samples exhibit features characteristic of Type H3 and H4 loops. This suggests that slit-like and parallel-plate pores dominate the pore geometries, with a minor presence of ink-bottle pores. In contrast, the hysteresis loops for samples Q2 and Q5 are nearly absent, aligning with Type H1, which suggests the prevalence of cylindrical pores with uniform diameters and open ends. The PSD curves obtained from the BJH model (Figure b) show pore volumes between 0.003 and 0.011 cm3/g. Notably, the PV is almost entirely contributed by pores smaller than 10 nm. The cumulative PV curves demonstrate a trend of rapid initial growth followed by a subsequent deceleration (Figure a).

HPMI Test

The HPMI analysis of the nine coal samples indicates that the mercury intrusion curves exhibit similar morphologies, characterized by a gentle initial slope followed by a sharp terminal increase, which reflects a distinct three-stage evolutionary pattern (Figure ). In the low-pressure stage (Pc < 1 MPa), the intrusion increases in minor incremental steps or remains near-horizontal, suggesting limited development of macropores or primary microfractures, whereas in the intermediate-pressure stage (Pc = 1–40 MPa), the curves show a gradual rise with a slight increase in mercury intake, indicating restricted development of mesopores within this range. As the pressure exceeds 40 MPa, the intrusion volume increases drastically and becomes nearly vertical at approximately 100 MPa, signifying that the relative development of smaller mesopores is most pronounced.

HPMI curves for coal samples (a–i).

The hysteresis effect is evident in all samples, as made evident by the hysteresis between the intrusion and extrusion curves. The high residual mercury saturation resulted from the extrusion curves’ failure to return to the origin at low pressures, which suggests the presence of a significant number of semiclosed pores. On the basis of their hysteresis loops, the samples are categorized into two groups. The first group (Q3, Q5, Q7, and Q9) exhibits narrow hysteresis loops with closely spaced curves, indicating better pore connectivity. The second group (Q1, Q2, Q4, Q6, and Q8) displays larger loop openings; in particular, sample Q6 reflects an extremely heterogeneous pore-size distribution and poor connectivity. Notably, sample Q7 reaches a maximum mercury saturation of only 35%, indicating a minimal effective intrusion space and a lower degree of actual pore development compared to the other samples.

The HPMI data were derived using the Washburn equation, with the results summarized in Table . Due to the fundamentally distinct physical mechanisms underlying HPMI and LT-N2GA, significant discrepancies often arise within overlapping pore size intervals. Specifically, the relatively low mechanical strength of the coal matrix renders it susceptible to compressive deformation and structural damage under elevated intrusion pressures. Such matrix compressibility can induce significant artifacts and distort the characterization of finer pore structures. To ensure data reliability, a complementary characterization strategy was adopted: LT-N2GA data were utilized to characterize mesopores (2–50 nm), while HPMI was reserved for the analysis of macropores (>50 nm). Within the effective HPMI measurement range (50 nm to 100 μm), the coal samples exhibit pore volumes ranging from 0.0022 to 0.096 cm3/g, with a mean of 0.0060 cm3/g. The resulting pore-size distribution (PSD) highlights a characteristic monotonic decline across the macropore scale (Figure ).

Full Pore Size Characteristics of Coal Rocks

Owing to the intrinsic structural heterogeneity and complexity of coal, no single analytical technique can provide a comprehensive and accurate depiction of its pore-size distribution PSD. To overcome this, an integrated approach combining LT-CO2GA, LT-N2GA, and HPMI was employed to obtain a holistic profile of the pore structure. Based on the applicable ranges of different methods, the data of the warm carbon dioxide adsorption method (<2 nm), the low-temperature nitrogen adsorption method (2–50 nm), and the high-pressure mercury intrusion method (>50 nm) are directly connected. No weighted fitting is performed in the overlapping intervals. The pore volume is normalized uniformly in the unit of cm3/g. Finally, the full pore-size distribution curve from 0.3 nm to 100 μm is obtained, realizing the full pore size characterization at a fine scale (Table , and Figure ). The results demonstrate that micropores are the most prevalent, accounting for 84.53%–88.09% of the total pore volume, with dominant pore diameters concentrated at 0.34–0.39 nm and 0.45–0.7 nm. In contrast, mesopores and macropores are relatively underdeveloped. Mesopores contribute 2.50%–9.51% (averaging 5.77%) to the total volume, with a peak distribution between 2 and 7 nm. Macropores account for 2.64%–9.66% (averaging 7.97%), slightly exceeding the mesopore fraction, with dominant diameters primarily below 10 μm (Figure ). This high degree of micropore development underscores the robust adsorption capacity of the coal samples, confirming them as the primary sites for coalbed methane storage. ,

Pore Multifractal Characteristics

Multifractal theory represents an advanced evolution and deepening of monofractal theory. By employing a continuous spectrum of fractal dimensions, the multifractal approach decomposes a complex pore system into multiple subsets characterized by distinct singularity exponents (α). This enables a sophisticated characterization of the local heterogeneity and evolutionary patterns of pore distributions in both spatial and size domains. , Such an approach effectively addresses the limitations of single fractal dimensions, which can only describe the global average statistical features of an object and often fail to capture localized extreme variances within the internal structure. In the context of coal pore research, multifractal theory is particularly effective for processing pore-size distribution data that spans several orders of magnitudeintegrating CO2 adsorption, N2 adsorption, and HPMI. Consequently, it provides a robust framework for quantifying the multiscale heterogeneity of coal reservoir pore architectures.

The LTGA-CO2 Multifractal Characteristics

Quantitative characterization of micropores via LT-CO2GA reveals that the generalized dimension spectrum (Dq–q) exhibits a consistent monotonic decline, adopting a characteristic inverse “S” shape with a diminishing slope as the moment order q increases (Figure a). The capacity dimension (D 0) remains invariably at 1.0 (Table ), a value that fundamentally confirms the spatial continuity of the experimental data set within a one-dimensional box-counting partitioning framework. Pore connectivity is a critical attribute, typically indexed by the Hurst exponent (H). In the nine coal samples, H values range from 0.963 to 0.972. These high values suggest a well-integrated internal micropore network. The degree of pore dispersion and heterogeneity is further elucidated by the D 0–D 1 differential; values exceeding 1.94 point toward a significant agglomeration effect and a relatively low uniformity in pore-size distribution. This structural complexity is complemented by the spectral width ΔD(D –10–D 10), which fluctuates between 0.622 and 0.799. Such a range reflects a moderate degree of localized variation, indicating that while the micropore system is complex, its structural variability remains constrained within a stable multifractal regime.

Key Characteristic Parameters of the Dq–q for Micropores in Coal Samples

The mass scaling function τ­(q) exhibits a pronounced nonlinear evolution with q, characterized by a distinct upward convexity (Figure b). The divergent slopes observed on either side of q = 0 underscore the intrinsic heterogeneity of the micropore system. This complexity is further reflected in the multifractal singularity spectrum, which adopts an upward-convex parabolic profile (Figure c), with key characteristic parameters detailed in Table . The singularity exponent α0 fluctuates between 1.064 and 1.086, while the subsets represented by α10 and α–10 equate to the low and high-probability density regions of the pore-size distribution, respectively. The spectral width Δα­(α10−α–10) is the primary indicator of heterogeneity, with higher values signifying greater complexity. All samples except Q3 yield Δα values exceeding 0.7. This suggests that Q3 possesses a relatively more uniform micropore structure. The internal distribution of pore volumes is further partitioned into concentrated and dispersed regions, indexed by WL (α0−α10) and WR (α–10−α0), respectively. The symmetry ratio WL/WR is consistently less than unity across the micropore suites, indicating that the pore network is predominantly characterized by a dispersed distribution. Analysis of Pore Structure and Control Factors of Heterogeneity

The pore structure of coal is fundamentally governed by the synergistic effects of thermal maturity and maceral composition, which collectively dictate the multifractal characteristics of the internal void space. As illustrated in Figure , the total pore volume (TPV) is primarily sensitive to R o, max, ash content, and fixed carbon content. Given that micropores contribute over 85% of the TPV (Table ), variations in these geological factors predominantly influence the total porosity by modulating the development of the micropore network. All samples in this study exceed a thermal maturity (R o, max) of 2.0%, placing them within the anthracite stage. Within this high-maturity regime, the continued thermodynamic evolution triggers the defunctionalization, dealkylation, and dehydrogenation of organic matter. The subsequent expulsion of volatile phases (e.g., CH4, CO2) results in mass attenuation and volumetric contraction of the solid matrix, thereby generating abundant nanoscale secondary pores (gas pores) and increasing both micropore and mesopore volumes. Conversely, ash content exhibits a significant negative correlation with TPV. This trend arises because the micropore and mesopore systems are primarily hosted within the organic matter substrate; thus, elevated ash content implies a diminished organic fraction. Furthermore, secondary mineralsthe primary constituents of ashfrequently occlude pre-existing pores and fractures, further reducing the effective pore volume. In contrast, fixed carbon serves as the structural foundation for both major pores (e.g., plant tissue and intercellular pores) and secondary pores generated during hydrocarbon evolution. Consequently, the micropore volume demonstrates a positive scaling relationship with increasing fixed carbon content.

Pearson correlation matrix between coal pore parameters, material composition, and thermal maturity.

A comparative analysis of the Δα and H across micropores, mesopores, and macropores provides critical insights into the scale-dependent heterogeneity and connectivity of the coal samples (Figure ). With the exception of the sample Q5, the mesopore systems consistently exhibit the minimum Δα values, while the macropore Δα significantly exceeds those of both micropores and mesopores. This disparity indicates that mesopores possess the weakest structural heterogeneity, whereas the macropore structure is the most complex. Furthermore, excluding samples Q7 and Q8, the H values are highest in the mesopores and lowest in the macropores, suggesting that mesopore networks generally maintain superior connectivity, while macropore connectivity is the most restricted. These observations collectively reveal an inverse relationship: increased structural heterogeneity in the pore system tends to correspond with diminished connectivity. Correlation analysis between material composition, pore structural parameters, and multifractal indices (Figure ) further elucidates the controls on pore evolution. Overall, Δα and ΔD exhibit significant positive correlations across all three pore scales; this synergy suggests that enhanced variability in pore distribution leads to greater internal divergence and, consequently, intensified heterogeneity. Additionally, the positive correlation between α0 and ΔD implies that a higher degree of pore aggregation directly contributes to structural nonuniformity. Regarding the influence of ash content, its accumulation appears to selectively impair connectivity within mesopores and macropores. This is because authigenic minerals have blocked the larger pore spaces and microfractures (Figure b). In contrast, its impact on the micropore system is primarily manifested as a morphological modification of the internal pore geometry, showing a negligible correlation with connectivity.

Conclusions

(1)The No. 8 coal in the study area is characterized as a high-rank anthracite, with a mean R o, max of 2.57%. The geochemical profile is defined by low moisture (0.96%), low ash (7.53%), and low volatile matter (6.83%) alongside a high fixed carbon content (81.24%). Maceral composition is predominantly vitrinite-rich (77.51%), followed by inertinite (16.50%), with a negligible liptinite fraction (0.38%), reflecting a highly evolved organic framework.(2)Micropores constitute the primary storage space, contributing an average of 87.78% to the total pore volume. Their PSD exhibits a distinct bimodal character, with a primary peak at 0.45–0.7 nm and a secondary peak at 0.34–0.39 nm. In contrast, mesopores and macropores provide limited volumetric contributions, with dominant pore diameters concentrated in the 2–10 nm and 0.05–8 μm ranges, respectively.(3)The distribution of micropores, mesopores, and macropores across all samples exhibits pronounced multifractal characteristics. A clear scale effect is observed: mesopores possess the minimum spectral width (Δα) and the maximum Hurst exponent (H), indicating the lowest structural heterogeneity and superior connectivity. Conversely, the macropore network displays the highest degree of heterogeneity and the most restricted connectivity, suggesting a fragmented pore structure at larger scales.(4)Pore evolution is synergistically controlled by the material composition and thermal maturity. The ash content is the main controlling factor for micropore development, which affects the micropore volume but has a weak effect on its pore connectivity. Furthermore, a fundamental inverse relationship exists between structural complexity and integration: enhanced heterogeneity consistently corresponds to diminished connectivity. Notably, an increased ash yield further impairs the connectivity of mesopore and macropore systems by occluding critical flow paths.
