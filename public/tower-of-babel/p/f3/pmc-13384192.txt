Limb salvage versus primary amputation in severe lower extremity trauma: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Javad Salimi, Saeed Soltani asl heris, Amir Shokri, Mahfam Nikzamir, Mohammad Amin Noroullahi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe optimal management of severe lower extremity trauma remains controversial, with debate between limb salvage and primary amputation. Introduction

Severe lower extremity trauma poses one of the most complex challenges in orthopedic and vascular surgery. Deciding between limb salvage and primary amputation involves careful evaluation of several factors, such as injury severity, vascular health, soft-tissue damage, contamination, shock/exsanguination status, hemodynamic stability, and individual patient characteristics.13 Although advancements have been made in surgical techniques, reconstruction options, and rehabilitation over the past 20 years, the optimal management approach remains debated.

Limb salvage has long been favored when feasible, on the premise that preserving the natural limb yields better functional outcomes and quality of life than amputation.3 4 Nonetheless, it often requires multiple operations, prolonged rehabilitation, and carries risks like infection, non-union, and chronic pain.5 6 Conversely, primary amputation, despite removing the injured limb, can enable quicker recovery by allowing earlier prosthetic fitting and rehabilitation. However, it can also lead to issues such as phantom limb pain and prosthetic difficulties.7

The landmark Lower Extremity Assessment Project (LEAP) study found no significant difference in functional outcomes between limb salvage and amputation at 2 years post-injury.7 However, subsequent studies have yielded mixed results, with substantial advances in microsurgery, fixation techniques, and prosthetic technologies.8 Moreover, inconsistent outcome measures have limited earlier systematic reviews, follow-up durations, and the inclusion of older studies that may not reflect current practices.2 9 10

Recent guidelines from the European Society for Vascular Surgery emphasize multidisciplinary decision-making for limb-threatening injuries, but specific evidence-based recommendations remain limited.11 The lack of recent, comprehensive reviews that include modern surgical techniques and patient-reported outcomes highlights a gap in existing evidence.

This systematic review and meta-analysis aim to offer an up-to-date, thorough comparison of limb salvage versus primary amputation in severe lower extremity trauma. It will assess functional outcomes, quality of life, pain, complications, psychological health, and economic factors, employing rigorous methodologies, including Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) certainty assessment.

Methods

Protocol and registration

This systematic review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 statement.12 The protocol was registered prospectively with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO). The registration number is CRD420251236066.

Eligibility criteria

Population: Adult patients (≥18 years) with severe lower limb trauma requiring either limb salvage or amputation. Severe trauma was defined as Gustilo-Anderson IIIB/IIIC open fractures,13 Mangled Extremity Severity Score (MESS) ≥7, or equivalent injury severity scoring systems.14 15

Population: Adult patients (≥18 years) with severe lower limb trauma requiring either limb salvage or amputation. Severe trauma was defined as Gustilo-Anderson IIIB/IIIC open fractures,13 Mangled Extremity Severity Score (MESS) ≥7, or equivalent injury severity scoring systems.14 15

Intervention: Limb salvage procedures including vascular repair or reconstruction, skeletal stabilization, and soft tissue coverage.16 17

Intervention: Limb salvage procedures including vascular repair or reconstruction, skeletal stabilization, and soft tissue coverage.16 17

Comparator: Primary amputation (transtibial or transfemoral) performed within 7 days of initial injury.

This review compares outcomes such as quality of life, function, and pain to emphasize the relevance of evidence in guiding clinical decisions for trauma care professionals.18

Study design: Randomized controlled trials, prospective observational studies, and retrospective cohort studies with control groups. Studies with at least a 12-month follow-up were included.

Study design: Randomized controlled trials, prospective observational studies, and retrospective cohort studies with control groups. Studies with at least a 12-month follow-up were included.

Information sources and search strategy

We systematically searched PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials, Scopus, and Web of Science from 1 January 2000 to 31 January 2025. The search strategy combined controlled vocabulary with text words for concepts related to lower extremity trauma, limb salvage, and amputation. Reference lists of included studies and relevant systematic reviews were hand-searched. Complete search strategies for all databases are provided in online supplemental appendix S1.

Study selection

All identified records were imported into Covidence systematic review software.19 After duplicate removal, two independent reviewers screened titles and abstracts using predefined eligibility criteria. Full-text articles of potentially eligible studies were retrieved and independently assessed. Disagreements were resolved through discussion with a third reviewer when consensus could not be reached.20

Data collection and extraction

Data extraction was performed independently by two reviewers using a standardized, piloted data extraction form. Extracted information included study characteristics, population characteristics, intervention details, outcomes at all reported time points, and follow-up duration. For studies with incomplete data, corresponding authors were contacted via email.

Risk of bias assessment

Risk of bias was assessed independently by two reviewers using the Risk Of Bias In Non-randomized Studies – of Interventions (ROBINS-I) tool for observational studies.21 Detailed risk-of-bias assessments for all included studies are provided in online supplemental table S1. Each domain was rated as low risk, moderate risk, serious risk, or critical risk of bias. Disagreements were resolved through discussion. Detailed ROBINS-I assessments for all studies are provided in table 1.

Given the predominance of observational studies in this review, several sources of bias warrant acknowledgment. Confounding by indication represents a fundamental challenge, as treatment allocation was influenced by injury severity, patient physiology, and surgeon judgment rather than randomization. Patients selected for limb salvage may systematically differ from those undergoing primary amputation in terms of injury characteristics, pre-existing health status, and access to specialized care. We addressed this limitation through several approaches: conducting subgroup analyses stratified by injury mechanism (blunt vs penetrating), population type (military vs civilian), and injury severity scoring systems; performing sensitivity analyses excluding studies at high or critical risk of bias; and evaluating dose-response relationships where sufficient data existed. Additionally, we applied the GRADE framework to transparently assess certainty of evidence, downgrading for risk of bias where appropriate.22 However, residual confounding cannot be excluded, and causal inference from our pooled estimates should be made with appropriate caution.

Data synthesis and statistical analysis

We performed random-effects meta-analyses using the DerSimonian-Laird method.21 For continuous outcomes, we calculated standardized mean differences (SMDs) with 95% CIs. For dichotomous outcomes, we calculated risk ratios (RRs) with 95% CIs. Statistical heterogeneity was assessed using the I2 statistic, with values of 25%, 50%, and 75% representing low, moderate, and high heterogeneity, respectively. Publication bias was evaluated through visual inspection of funnel plots (online supplemental figure S1) and Egger’s regression test.

Subgroup analyses were conducted based on study design, population type (military vs civilian), injury mechanism (blunt vs penetrating), and follow-up duration (<24 months vs follow-up duration ≥24 months). Sensitivity analyses excluded studies at high or critical risk of bias. Results of subgroup and sensitivity analyses are provided in onlinesupplemental tables S3 S4. All meta-analyses were performed using Review Manager V.5.4 (The Cochrane Collaboration, Copenhagen, Denmark) and R statistical software V.4.2.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), with the meta and Metafor packages.

Certainty of evidence assessment

The certainty of evidence for each outcome was assessed using the GRADE approach.22 Evidence was rated as high, moderate, low, or very low certainty based on consideration of risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias. Summary of findings tables were created using GRADEpro GDT software (McMaster University, Hamilton, Canada).

Results

Study selection

The systematic search identified 5350 records across all databases (PubMed 1847; Embase 1523; Cochrane CENTRAL 856; Scopus 678; Web of Science 446). An additional 20 records were identified through hand-searching of reference lists. After removing 1847 duplicates, 3523 titles and abstracts were screened, of which 3311 were excluded. Full-text articles were retrieved for 212 studies, of which 165 were excluded for the following reasons: follow-up <12 months (n=47), no relevant outcomes reported (n=38), mixed trauma populations without separate analysis (n=29), case series without a comparison group (n=22), duplicate publication (n=15), and non-English language (n=14).

Ultimately, 47 studies involving 8742 patients (4318 limb salvage, 4424 primary amputation) met the inclusion criteria and were included in qualitative synthesis. Of these, 38 studies with sufficient quantitative data were included in the meta-analysis (figure 1). A list of excluded studies with reasons is provided in online supplemental table S2.

Study characteristics

Study characteristics are summarized in table 1. The included studies were published between 2002 and 2024, with the majority (72%, n=34) published after 2010, reflecting contemporary surgical practice and outcomes. Study designs comprised prospective cohort studies (38%, n=18), retrospective cohort studies (55%, n=26), and randomized controlled trials (6%, n=3). Most studies were conducted in high-income countries, primarily the USA (n=23), the UK (n=6), and other European countries (n=8). Twenty studies (43%) focused on military populations, predominantly combat injuries from Iraq and Afghanistan conflicts,23 whereas 27 studies (57%) examined civilian trauma populations. Mean follow-up duration ranged from 12 to 84 months, with a median of 36 months (IQR 24–60 months).

Risk of bias assessments are presented in figure 2 and table 1. For the 43 observational studies assessed using ROBINS-I, 12 studies (28%) were judged to be at moderate risk of bias, 20 (47%) at serious risk, and 11 (26%) at critical risk of bias. The most common sources of bias were confounding by injury severity and patient selection factors (24% serious or critical risk), selection of participants into the study (21% serious risk), and missing outcome data (31% serious or critical risk). Classification of interventions and measurement of outcomes were generally at low to moderate risk of bias. The three RCTs were assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 tool and showed some concerns related to deviations from intended interventions and missing outcome data.

Quality of life outcomes

Quality of life outcomes were assessed in 28 studies using validated instruments (table 2). Meta-analysis of 15 studies (n=2847 patients) reporting SF-36 Physical Component Summary24 scores at 2 years revealed a small but statistically significant difference favoring limb salvage (SMD 0.16, 95% CI 0.08 to 0.24, p=0.0001, I2=45.7%; figure 3A). However, this difference was below the minimal clinically significant difference for the SF-36 (typically five points on a 0–10 scale). SF-36 Mental Component Summary scores showed no significant difference between groups (SMD 0.09, 95% CI −0.03 to 0.21, p=0.14, I2=58.4%). Meta-analysis of 11 studies (n=2143 patients) reporting EQ-5D index25 scores similarly showed no significant difference (SMD 0.07, 95% CI −0.06 to 0.20, p=0.31, I2=55.2%). Subgroup analysis by follow-up duration revealed that differences remained non-significant at extended follow-up (≥5 years, n=6 studies), suggesting durable equivalence in quality-of-life outcomes.

Functional outcomes

Functional outcomes were reported in 32 studies, using various instruments, including the Sickness Impact Profile, the Short Musculoskeletal Function Assessment, and the Functional Independence Measure. Meta-analysis showed no significant difference in functional scores between limb salvage and amputation at 2-year follow-up (SMD 0.12, 95% CI −0.02 to 0.26, p=0.09, I2=62.3%).

Return-to-work outcomes were reported in 18 studies (n=4127 patients). Meta-analysis demonstrated modestly higher return-to-work rates following limb salvage (RR 1.08, 95% CI 1.01 to 1.16, p=0.03, I2=0.0%; figure 4), indicating an 8% relative increase translating to approximately one additional worker returning to employment per 12–13 patients undergoing limb salvage rather than amputation. However, time-to-return-to-work was significantly longer in the limb salvage group (mean difference 4.7 months, 95% CI 3.8 to 5.6, p<0.001; 14 studies, n=3542). Walking ability without assistive devices was similar between groups (RR 1.04, 95% CI 0.96 to 1.12, p=0.33; 13 studies, n=3189). Military-specific return-to-duty rates showed no significant difference (RR 1.12, 95% CI 0.98 to 1.28, p=0.10; six studies, n=1453).

Forest plot for return-to-work rates (18 studies, n=4127). Meta-analysis demonstrated modestly higher return-to-work rates following limb salvage (risk ratio 1.08, 95% CI 1.01 to 1.16, p=0.03, I²=0.0%). Values greater than one favor limb salvage.

Pain outcomes

Nineteen studies (n=4892 patients) assessed the prevalence of chronic pain at final follow-up. Meta-analysis revealed significantly higher rates of chronic pain following limb salvage (RR 1.34, 95% CI 1.24 to 1.44, p<0.001, I2=9.2%; figure 5). The pooled chronic pain prevalence was 42.7% (95% CI 38.9 to 46.6%) in the limb salvage group vs 31.8% (95% CI 28.7 to 35.1%) in the amputation group, representing a 34% relative increase and an absolute risk increase of 10.9%. Pain severity scores (VAS) were also higher in limb salvage patients (mean difference 0.8 points on 0–10 scale, 95% CI 0.6 to 1.0, p<0.001; 11 studies, n=2734).

Complications

Infection: Thirty-one studies (n=6128 patients) reported infectious complications. Meta-analysis demonstrated markedly elevated infection rates following limb salvage (RR 2.54, 95% CI 2.34 to 2.75, p<0.001, I2=13.3%; figure 6). The pooled infection prevalence was 38.6% (95% CI 36.2% to 41.1%) in limb salvage vs 15.2% (95% CI 13.8% to 16.7%) in amputation, representing an absolute risk increase of 23.4%.

Psychological outcomes

Psychological outcomes were documented in 18 studies. Meta-analysis indicated no significant difference in depression rates between groups (RR 1.12, 95% CI 0.94 to 1.34, p=0.21, I2=42.7%). The prevalence of post-traumatic stress disorder was similarly comparable (RR 1.08, 95% CI 0.87 to 1.34, p=0.48, I2=38.2%). However, body image dissatisfaction27 28 was more common among amputation patients (RR 0.73, 95% CI 0.58 to 0.92, p=0.008, I2=31.5%), particularly affecting younger patients and those with transfemoral amputations.

Economic outcomes

Economic data were reported in 12 studies. Total direct medical costs during the first 2 years were significantly higher for limb salvage compared with amputation29 (mean difference $28 600, 95% CI $18 200 to $39 000, p<0.001, I2=64.2%). However, lifetime cost projections (n=4 studies with >10-year follow-up) showed convergence between groups due to ongoing prosthetic costs in amputees, with no significant difference in total costs beyond 15 years (mean difference $12 300, 95% CI −$8500 to $33 100, p=0.25, I2=71.8%). Indirect costs including lost productivity showed no significant difference between groups (mean difference $5200, 95% CI −$11 400 to $21 800, p=0.54, I2=78.3%).

Certainty of evidence

GRADE certainty of evidence assessments are presented in table 3. Evidence certainty was moderate for quality-of-life outcomes (downgraded for risk of bias), moderate for complications including infection and re-hospitalization (downgraded for risk of bias), low for pain outcomes (downgraded for risk of bias and inconsistency), and low for economic outcomes (downgraded for risk of bias, inconsistency, and indirectness due to variation in healthcare systems). Psychological outcomes were rated as moderate certainty (downgraded for risk of bias). No outcomes were upgraded despite large effect sizes for complications, as residual confounding could not be excluded.

Discussion

Principal findings

This comprehensive systematic review and meta-analysis of 47 studies involving 8742 patients demonstrates that long-term functional outcomes and quality of life are comparable between limb salvage and primary amputation for severe lower extremity trauma. This finding challenges the traditional assumption that salvage universally provides superior outcomes and supports a paradigm of individualized decision-making. Although quality of life metrics showed no clinically meaningful differences, limb salvage was associated with significantly higher rates of complications. The 2.5-fold increased infection risk, 87% higher re-hospitalization rate, and 34% increased chronic pain prevalence represent substantial morbidity burdens that must be weighed against potential benefits. The secondary amputation rate of 22.7% following attempted salvage represents a considerable failure rate that exposes patients to the risks of both salvage attempts and eventual amputation.

Implications for practice guidelines

Our findings have direct implications for current clinical guidelines. The ESVS 2020 guidelines emphasize timely revascularization and limb preservation when technically feasible.11 However, our synthesis demonstrates that technical feasibility does not guarantee superior patient outcomes. The substantial complication burdens—2.5-fold increased infections and 34% higher chronic pain—suggest guidelines should incorporate explicit recommendations for shared decision-making with realistic outcome expectations. Current injury severity scores (MESS, LSI)14 30 provide limited guidance on patient-centered outcomes. Future guidelines should emphasize individualized approaches integrating patient values alongside anatomical criteria, supporting evidence-based counseling rather than defaulting to limb preservation.

Contemporary wound care and treatment evolution

The time period covered by this review (2000–2025) has witnessed substantial evolution in wound care technologies and limb salvage techniques. Modern approaches including negative pressure wound therapy, bioengineered skin substitutes, advanced flap techniques, and improved antibiotic stewardship have transformed the landscape of complex extremity reconstruction. The majority of studies in our analysis (72%) were published after 2010, thus capturing outcomes that reflect these contemporary wound management strategies. Although these advancements have improved local wound healing and reduced early infectious complications, our meta-analysis demonstrates that even with modern techniques, infection rates remain significantly elevated in the salvage group (38.6% vs 15.2%), and secondary amputation continues to occur in nearly one-quarter of salvage attempts.

This finding suggests that despite technological progress, fundamental biologic and anatomic limitations persist in severely traumatized extremities. Future research should specifically evaluate whether emerging technologies—such as biofilm-targeted therapies, stem cell applications, or novel biosynthetic materials—can meaningfully reduce the complication burden while preserving the comparable quality-of-life outcomes we observed.

Comparison with existing literature

Our findings are consistent with the seminal LEAP study, which found no significant differences in functional outcomes between salvage and amputation at 2 years, and extend these findings with 25 years of additional evidence.7 Recent systematic reviews similarly concluded that both approaches yield comparable outcomes. However, these reviews included fewer studies and did not perform comprehensive meta-analyses across all outcome domains. A recent meta-analysis by Serlis et al2 corroborated our core findings, reporting similar long-term functional outcomes despite elevated complication rates in limb salvage cohorts.2 Our analysis extends these findings by demonstrating that despite higher complication rates, ultimate quality of life outcomes remain similar, suggesting that patients who complete salvage achieve outcomes comparable to amputation, whereas those whose salvage fails undergo secondary amputation.

The higher complication burden in salvage patients aligns with findings from extensive cohort studies of military trauma, where infection rates after complex lower extremity reconstruction frequently exceed 40%.23 31 32 The economic data showing higher initial costs for salvage but convergence over time challenge simplistic cost-effectiveness analyses29 33 and highlight the importance of lifetime cost perspectives. The finding that chronic pain is more prevalent in salvage patients, whereas phantom limb pain affects the majority of amputees,5 34 35 emphasizes that pain is an inevitable consequence of severe lower extremity trauma, regardless of treatment approach, though its character differs between groups.

Clinical implications

These findings have several important clinical implications. First, they support a shift from the traditional salvage-at-all-costs mentality towards a more balanced approach that acknowledges both treatment modalities as potentially reasonable options. The decision should be individualized based on injury characteristics (Gustilo-Anderson classification, MESS score, vascular status), patient factors (age, comorbidities, occupation, social support), available resources (surgical expertise, microsurgical capabilities, rehabilitation facilities), and critically, patient preferences after informed discussion of realistic expectations.

Second, the comparable long-term outcomes support the ethical acceptability of primary amputation when salvage is extremely complex, uncertain, or would require prolonged reconstruction with multiple procedures and high complication risk. This is particularly relevant for patients with severe concomitant injuries, marginal tissue viability, or limited physiological reserve.36 Third, the high complication and secondary amputation rates emphasize the critical importance of setting realistic expectations during patient counseling, including discussing the likelihood of multiple procedures, prolonged recovery, and possibility of ultimate amputation despite salvage attempts.

Finally, these findings underscore the importance of multidisciplinary decision-making involving orthopedic surgeons, vascular surgeons, plastic surgeons, rehabilitation specialists, psychologists, and the patient and family. Early involvement of physiatry for rehabilitation planning and psychology for adjustment support may improve outcomes in both groups. Shared decision-making tools that present outcomes data in accessible formats may facilitate informed patient participation in treatment selection.

Strengths and limitations

This review has several strengths. We employed rigorous systematic review methodology following PRISMA 2020 guidelines, conducted comprehensive searches across five databases, and used validated tools for bias assessment (ROBINS-I). The GRADE framework provides transparent certainty assessments for each outcome. Our inclusion of studies from 2000 to 2025 ensures contemporary relevance reflecting modern surgical techniques and prosthetic technology. The comprehensive evaluation of multiple outcome domains provides a holistic assessment of both treatment approaches. Low heterogeneity for several key outcomes (infection, pain, re-hospitalization) suggests consistent findings across diverse populations and settings.

An additional methodological consideration relates to our definition of primary amputation (performed within 7 days of injury). Although this 7-day threshold represents an important early decision point and is commonly used in trauma literature, a 30-day amputation rate would provide complementary information about salvage failures occurring during the acute perioperative period. Unfortunately, inconsistent reporting of amputation timing across studies precluded systematic analysis of 30-day outcomes. Only 8 of 47 included studies explicitly reported amputation rates stratified by time intervals, with varying definitions (7 days, 14 days, 30 days, or hospital discharge).

Future prospective studies should adopt standardized temporal reporting—including rates at 7 days (primary amputation), 30 days (acute failure), 6 months, 1 year, and annually thereafter—to better characterize the natural history of salvage attempts and identify optimal windows for intervention or conversion to amputation. Such data would enhance prognostic modeling and facilitate more precise counseling of patients regarding the likelihood of secondary amputation at various time points.

However, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the predominance of observational studies (91%) limits causal inference, as treatment allocation was not randomized and likely influenced by injury severity, surgeon preference, and patient characteristics. Residual confounding cannot be excluded despite adjustment in individual studies. Second, substantial heterogeneity in outcome measurement (different quality of life instruments, varying pain scales, inconsistent complication definitions) limits precise comparability. Third, publication bias may overestimate complication rates if studies with higher complication rates are more likely to be published, though our funnel plot analysis did not suggest major bias. Fourth, most studies came from high-income countries with well-resourced healthcare systems, limiting generalizability to resource-limited settings where surgical expertise, microsurgical capabilities, and intensive rehabilitation may be less available.37 Fifth, loss to follow-up ranged from 15–40% across studies, potentially biasing results if patients with poor outcomes were more likely to be lost.38 Finally, military trauma populations (43% of studies) may not be directly comparable to civilian trauma regarding injury mechanisms, patient characteristics, and available resources.

Research implications

Several research priorities emerge from this review. First, high-quality prospective registries with robust confounder adjustment, standardized outcome measurement, and long-term follow-up are needed to better understand which patient subgroups benefit most from each approach. International collaboration could facilitate adequately powered studies. Second, standardization of outcome measurement is essential for future meta-analyses. Development and validation of limb-trauma-specific instruments such as the LIMB-Q represent progress toward this goal.17 These instruments should capture domains most important to patients including pain, function, body image, and treatment burden.

Third, improved prognostic models incorporating injury characteristics, patient factors, and institutional capabilities would facilitate individualized risk prediction and shared decision-making. Machine learning approaches may enhance predictive accuracy by identifying complex interactions. Fourth, economic analyses should adopt lifetime perspectives accounting for all direct and indirect costs and consider quality-adjusted life-years to capture both quantity and quality of life.39 Fifth, psychological interventions warrant investigation, as both groups experience substantial psychological morbidity.40 Trials evaluating cognitive–behavioral therapy, acceptance and commitment therapy, or peer support programs could inform optimal psychosocial care. Finally, research into the prevention of secondary amputation after salvage attempts,41 42 including optimal infection control strategies and criteria for early conversion to amputation, may reduce failed salvage attempts and associated morbidity.

Conclusions

Long-term functional outcomes and quality of life are comparable between limb salvage and primary amputation in severe lower extremity trauma, although salvage is associated with significantly higher complication rates, chronic pain, and re-hospitalization. These findings support a paradigm shift toward shared decision-making that acknowledges both treatment approaches as potentially reasonable options depending on injury characteristics, patient factors, and preferences. Clinicians should engage patients in informed discussions about realistic expectations, complication risks, and alternative pathways to functional recovery, rather than defaulting to salvage whenever technically feasible. The decision between salvage and amputation represents a preference-sensitive choice that should be made collaboratively by multidisciplinary teams and well-informed patients.
