Cairo pentagon tessellated covalent organic frameworks with mcm topology for near-infrared phototherapy
Authors: Yong Liu, Liangchao Yuan, Wenwen Chi, Wang-Kang Han, Jinfang Zhang, Huan Pang, Zhongchang Wang, Zhi-Guo Gu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractDespite the prevalent of hexagonal, tetragonal, and triangular pore structures in two-dimensional covalent organic frameworks (2D COFs), the pentagonal pores remain conspicuously absent. We herein present the Cairo pentagonal tessellated COFs, achieved through precisely chosen geometry and metrics of the linkers, resulting in unprecedented mcm topology. In each pentagonal structure, porphyrin units create four uniform sides around 15.5 Å with 90° angles, while tetrabiphenyl unit establish a bottom edge about 11.6 Å with 120° angles, aligning precisely with the criteria of Cairo Pentagon. According to the narrow bandgap and strong near-infrared (NIR) absorbance, as-synthesized COFs exhibit the efficient singlet oxygen (1O2) generation and photothermal conversion, resulting in NIR photothermal combined photodynamic therapy to guide cancer cell apoptosis. Mechanistic studies reveal that the good 1O2 production capability upregulates intracellular lipid peroxidation, leading to glutathione depletion, low expression of glutathione peroxidase 4, and induction of ferroptosis. The implementation of pentagonal Cairo tessellations in this work provides a promising strategy for diversifying COFs with new topologies, along with multimodal NIR phototherapy.Subject terms: Polymers, Organic molecules in materials science, Polymers

Introduction

Covalent organic frameworks (COFs) are an attractive class of porous crystalline polymer that enables the precise generation of extended networks through the principle of reticular chemistry1–4. By far, most of reported COFs are 2D structures based on hexagonal, tetragonal, and triangular pores, thus restricted to form the several regular topologies, such as hcb, hxl, kgm, sql, tth, bex, kgd, mtf, and cem5–19. While there have been great efforts to increase the structural diversity of 2D COFs, by decreasing symmetry of framework, reticulating multiple types of linkers or changing the edge length and vertex angle, the underlying pore structures and topologies remain limited to the above types20–25. The most fundamental reason lies in the lack of other polygonal pores as tessellation models to express novel 2D topologies.

Pentagons, which possess extraordinary tessellation patterns26–30, thus hold the great potential for constructing new topologies. Notably, the regular pentagons cannot tile the surface in a gapless way, whereas Cairo pentagon could31–33. The geometry of this ideal Cairo tiling requires the angle to be 3 × 120° and 2 × 90°, and should have four equal longer edges and one shorter edge with the length ratio of 1: 0.732 (Fig. 1a). Two distinct vertices of such a pentagon must be four- and three-linked in the ratio of 1:2, which is defined as mcm topology according to the RCSR database (Fig. 1b)34. When considering two adjacent three-linked vertices as wholes, a pseudo-mcm topology can be deconstructed into four-branched C2- and C4-symmetryic knot modules in the ratio of 1:1 (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Fig. 1).

Thus, to construct the Cairo pentagonal tessellated COFs with pseudo-mcm net, the selection of those two symmetric tetratopic linkers with geometrically satisfying angles and lengths is imperative. Porphyrin-based tetraamine, well-recognized for their typical 90° angle and four equal sides, serves as a potential C4-symmetryic building block (Supplementary Fig. 2a). In addition, polyphenylene tetraaldehyde, featuring a satisfactory 120° angle attributed to the aldehyde in the meta-position of the terminal benzene, is selected as another C2-symmetryic tetratopic linker, forming the bottom edge of pentagon. Notably, the lengths of the porphyrin- and polyphenylene-derived units can be meticulously finetuned to approach a ratio of 1:0.732. This precision is achieved by manipulating the count of benzene units within the monomer structure. Accordingly, porphyrin tetraamine with one or two benzenes and polyphenylene tetraaldehyde with three or four benzenes were chosen for further analyses of length ratios (Supplementary Fig. 2b). After precisely measuring the lengths of these two types of modules (from the points of extension to the intersection), the length ratio (1:0.74) between porphyrin-based tetraamine with two benzenes (M-5,10,15,20-tetrakis(4-aminobiphenyl)porphyrin, M-TBPP, M = Zn or H) and polyphenylene-based tetraaldehyde with four benzenes (quaterphenyl-3,3″′,5,5″′-tetracarbaldehyde (QPTA) and 5,5′-(2,2′-bipyridine)−5,5′-diyl-diisophthalaldehyde (BPY)) is closest to the theory value of Cairo pentagon (Fig. 1d).

In this context, four 2D COFs (mcm-1, mcm-2, mcm-3, and mcm-4) with pseudo-mcm topology were constructed by geometrically equivalent linkers of Zn-TBPP and H-TBPP, with BPY and QPTA, which represent examples of pentagonal Cairo tessellations in COFs. Furthermore, due to the formation of 2D extended porphyrin-based pentagonal networks with lower symmetry, all the four COFs showed strong near-infrared absorbance (absorb edge near 1000 nm) and narrow bandgap (1.7–1.8 eV), realizing the efficient photothermal conversion for photothermal therapy (PTT) and 1O2 generation for photodynamic therapy (PDT). Thus triggering mcm-COFs mediated PTT synergized PDT-induced apoptosis and ferroptosis of tumor cell.

Results

Designed synthesis and characterizations

Four crystalline frameworks were synthesized by utilizing BPY/QPTA and Zn-TBPP/H-TBPP in a mixture of o-dichlorobenzene and n-butanol at 120 °C for 3 d, in which 6 M acetic acid and aniline/ benzaldehyde served as the catalyst and modulator, respectively (Fig. 2a). The FT-IR spectroscopy of mcm-COFs all exhibited the appearance of the C = N stretching vibration at 1620–1630 cm−1, revealing the formation of imine linkages (Supplementary Fig. 8). Furthermore, all of their 13C NMR spectroscopy showed the characteristic C = N imine resonances at ~160 ppm, confirming again the formation of imine bonds (Supplementary Fig. 9). All COFs were stable in 6 M HCl (aq), 6 M NaOH (aq) and common organic solvents for 3 days (Supplementary Figs. 10 and 11). In addition, the thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) showed these frameworks were thermally stable up to 500 °C under N2 atmosphere (Supplementary Fig. 12).

The crystallinity of mcm-COFs was confirmed by powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) (Fig. 2b–e). The pattern displays three main diffraction peaks at around 3.4°, 4.6°, and 5.2°, corresponding to the (110), (020), and (120) reflection planes, respectively, followed by a series of less intense peaks. Considering its geometric and connective matching of the linkers, possible nets only including sql and mcm which were built in Materials Studio software. The experimental diffraction patterns of mcm-COFs matched well with the simulated pattern obtained from the mcm topology with the AA stacking mode in Pbam space group, while other stacking mode and sql topology were mismatched (Supplementary Figs. 13–16). A full profile Pawley refinement was performed to obtain the unit cell parameters, which resulted to satisfactorily acceptable profile differences and residual values. In addition, the high crystallinity of four COFs with mcm topology were further demonstrated by small-angle X-ray scattering (SAXS) (Fig. 2f–i).

As the above COFs have the same topology, mcm-1 is selected as an example to illustrate their unique structures (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Figs. 17–19). In ab plane, BPY, serving as the bottom edge with length of 11.62 Å, connected two Zn-TBPP at an angle approaching 120° through C = N linkages (Fig. 3a). Each Zn-TBPP contributed two longer edges about 15.52 and 15.55 Å with an angle of 90°, resulting in a ratio of 1:0.749 and 1:0.747 between the long and bottom sides. At last, the m-phthalaldehyde group from another BPY served as vertex connecting two Zn-TBPP at 120° to form a closed Cairo pentagonal ring, with the theoretical pore size of 14 Å. Interestingly, each BPY in mcm-1 not only connected two side-symmetric pentagons by acting as the shared bottom edge, but also extended two other mirror-symmetric pentagons through its ends serving as vertices. The four differently oriented pentagons thus formed the simplest repeating unit of mcm-1 with BPY as bridges. Along the a axis (Fig. 3b), the repeating units were linked via the vertices of pentagons to form a 1D chain, taking BPY as the connecting rod. Moreover, a 1D ribbon structure was formed along the b axis by extending the repeating cells, in which BPY was the shared bottom edge (Fig. 3c). Eventually, the Cairo pentagonal tessellated mcm-1 was collaged around the BPY, to form an unprecedented mcm topology in ab plane, and the 2D layers are AA stacked with a layer distance of 3.4 Å (Fig. 3d).

Scanning electron microscope (SEM) showed the granular morphology of mcm-COFs (Supplementary Fig. 20). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) revealed the elemental C, N and O in all mcm-COFs, with the additional Zn(II) present in mcm-1 and mcm-2 (Supplementary Figs. 21–22). The transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images displayed visualized parallel lines with a distance ~0.3 nm, attributed to the dominant (001) plane of the ordered frameworks (Fig. 4a and Supplementary Fig. 23). Interestingly, the reticular structure with approximately pentagonal pores with a size of ~1.3 nm was also observed from all mcm-COFs (Fig. 4b and Supplementary Fig. 24). The dislocation diagram obtained by inverse Fourier transform showed clear reticular structure, which also matched well with the mcm topology.

The porosity of mcm-COFs was further studied using nitrogen adsorption/desorption isotherms at 77 K. The typical type I isotherm suggested the microporous character (Fig. 4c–f). The BET surface areas of mcm-1, mcm-2, mcm-3 and mcm-4 were 1098, 1136, 995, and 907 m g−1, respectively. Notably, the pore size distribution curves of four COFs all revealed only one type of pore ~1.3–1.4 nm, which further proved that mcm-COFs adopts mcm topology with AA stacking rather than sql topology (Supplementary Fig. 25).

UV-vis diffuse reflection absorption spectra presented that the absorption edge of four COFs all extended to NIR region near 1000 nm with the narrow band gaps (1.73-1.82 eV), demonstrating the excellent coefficient absorption (Fig. 5a, b and Supplementary Fig. 26)35–39. The pentagonal structure with low symmetry imparts a higher anisotropy, which may contribute to a decreased bandgap and thus increased the efficiency of photon utilization. The photocurrent response under both NIR and visible-light irradiation clearly manifest the generation of electron-hole, while also suggest that the pentagonal structure with low symmetry could mitigate the coulombic attraction and consequently promotes the separation of electron-hole pairs (Fig. 5c and Supplementary Fig. 27)27. Moreover, the bipyridine in those frameworks further improves the carrier separation, as can be observed the higher photocurrent from mcm-1 and mcm-3. This argument was also supported by the lower fluorescence intensity, charge-transfer resistance and the increased charge carrier lifetimes form photoluminescence spectroscopy (PL), electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS), and the time-resolved PL decay measurements (Supplementary Figs. 28–30). Subsequently, the photocatalytic H2 evolution from water by mcm-COFs were further investigated, the high H2 evolution rate under 700 ≤ λ ≤ 780 nm demonstrating the efficient light absorption and photoactivity in NIR irradiation (Supplementary Figs. 31–37 and Supplementary Table 1).

In vitro investigation of the photothermal and photodynamic therapy

Near-infrared (NIR) of 650-900 nm is known as the optical therapeutic window, which can penetrate human tissue reaching ~10 cm. Encouraged by the above results, the synthesized mcm-COFs exhibited strong NIR absorbance and narrow bandgap, which satisfy the fundamental requirements of NIR phototherapy. Therefore, the potential of mcm-COFs for photodynamic therapy and photothermal therapy were then studied (Fig. 5d). All mcm-COFs showed the excellent photothermal conversion under 660 nm laser irradiation, and mcm-1 displayed better photothermal conversion capability and recyclability (Fig. 5e, f and Supplementary Figs. 38 and 39). In addition, the photothermal conversion efficiency (PCE) of mcm-1 was calculated to be η = 43.3% (Supplementary Fig. 40), which was comparable with those reported COF-based phototherapy systems (Supplementary Tab 2). Subsequently, utilizing the 2,7-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) fluorescent probe as an assay tool, all COFs were able to detect ROS at 525 nm and mcm-1 had the best photoactivity for photodynamic therapy (Fig. 5g and Supplementary Fig. 41). In addition, the production of ROS was detected as singlet oxygen when 9,10-dimethylanthracene (DMA) was chosen as the label (Fig. 5h and Supplementary Fig. 42), which was further confirmed by electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectroscopy (Fig. 5i and Supplementary Fig. 43). Moreover, the singlet oxygen quantum yield (ΦΔ) of mcm-1 was calculated at 0.54, which was comparable with those reported porphyrin-based phototherapy systems (Supplementary Fig. 44 and Supplementary Table 3). According to the density functional theory (DFT) calculation, bipyridine constructed in framework will facilitate the separation of holes from porphyrin centers and reduce the recombination of charges40,41, thus providing the higher photoactivity and singlet oxygen generation ability for mcm-1 and mcm-3 (Fig. 5j).

The sample size after sonication were determined by dynamic light scattering (DLS) to be ~200 nm (Supplementary Fig. 45), which was suitable for in vitro studies. After 12 h of coculture, 4T1 cells showed a clear red fluorescence signal, indicating successful internalization of these nanoscale COFs into 4T1 cells, and more than 67% of cells internalized COF particles within 24 h (Fig. 6a–c). The cytotoxicity assay of mcm-COFs was evaluated by MTT method. The results showed that more than 80% of 4T1 cells and 90% of normal cells survived after treatment with different concentrations of mcm-COFs solutions, indicating that mcm-COFs have good biocompatibility and biosafety (Supplementary Figs. 46 and 47). In contrast, treatment with mcm-1 and laser irradiation resulted in <25% of 4T1 cells surviving, demonstrating high photocytotoxicity (Fig. 6d). And the next flow cytometry analysis of Annexin V and PI staining further supported this, with an observed apoptosis rate of 60.7% for mcm-1 (Fig. 6e and Supplementary Figs. 48 and 49).

Mitochondrial membrane potential imaging and flow cytometric analysis revealed that mcm-COFs effectively diminished the mitochondrial membrane potential (a key feature of iron apoptosis) (Fig. 6f, g and Supplementary Figs. 50–52), resulting in mitochondrial membrane damage. Typically, ferroptosis arises from the incapacitation of the glutathione (GSH)-based antioxidant system, culminating in the intracellular buildup of lipid peroxides (LPO)42–44. A pivotal orchestrator of ferroptosis is the GSH-dependent enzyme glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4), whose activation hinges significantly upon intracellular GSH levels. Furthermore, heat stress can accelerate GSH depletion, thereby fostering ferroptosis. The remarkable ROS generation and photothermal conversion efficacy exhibited by mcm-COFs may instigate ferroptosis. Consequently, biomarkers associated with ferroptosis were assessed in vitro, encompassing intracellular GSH levels, GPX4, and LPO. The results revealed that laser irradiation led to increased Fe2+ content (Fig. 6h) and a reduced GSH/GSSG ratio (Fig. 6i) in 4T1 cells treated with mcm-COFs, indicating robust ferroptosis occurrence. GPX4, serving as a pivotal suppressor of ferroptosis, is regulated by GSH levels. Therefore, the assessment of GPX4 expression was extended through western blot (WB) analysis and immunofluorescence staining (Fig. 6j, k and Supplementary Fig. 53). The results indicated that mcm-1 coupled with NIR irradiation substantially suppressed GPX4 expression, correlating with the reduction in intracellular GSH levels42. Likewise, transferrin receptor (TFR1), another marker of ferroptosis, exhibited conspicuous overexpression, aligning with the observed increase in intracellular Fe2+ content. Compared to the control group, 4T1 cells treated with mcm-COFs under 660 nm light showed markedly increased green fluorescence with DCFH-DA staining (Fig. 6l and Supplementary Fig. 54), indicative of elevated intracellular ROS. The elevated levels of lipid peroxidation content further substantiate the efficacy of mcm-COFs in potently inducing ferroptosis (Fig. 6m, Supplementary Fig. 55). Additionally, TEM results underscored that, in contrast to normal mitochondria, those treated with mcm-1 exhibited pronounced shrinkage (Fig. 6n), characteristic of ferroptosis-induced mitochondrial dysfunction45. These results unequivocally demonstrated the successful generations of sufficient ROS within lipids via mcm-COFs-mediated PDT, leading to intracellular lipid peroxidation, GSH depletion, and diminished GPX4 expression, thereby instigating ferroptosis. Moreover, PTT serves to augment the efficacy of ferroptosis.

In vivo therapeutic effect

Encouraged by the promising in vitro results, the in vivo phototherapy of mcm-COFs was assessed utilizing a 4T1 tumor-bearing mouse model, with mcm-1 selected as the representative due to its superior phototherapy efficacy (Fig. 7a). Under 660 nm laser irradiation, in vivo photothermal imaging revealed that tumor-site temperatures reached 35.3 °C for PBS-injected mice and 50.0 °C for mcm-1-injected mice, indicating a notably enhanced PTT efficiency conferred by mcm-1 (Fig. 7b). In vivo imaging at different time points demonstrated that mcm-1 was capable of accumulating at the tumor site within 1 h after tail vein injection, reaching its highest level after 4 h (Fig. 7c and Supplementary Fig. 56). Furthermore, the fluorescence of mcm-1 completely overlapped with the bioluminescence, indicating that mcm-1 has excellent tumor-targeting properties, attributable primarily to the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect46. The tumor size and body weight of mice were monitored throughout the entire experiment. As depicted in Fig. 7d, treatment with mcm-1 + 660 nm effectively suppressed the growth rate of 4T1 tumors, indicating remarkable in vivo therapeutic efficacy (Fig. 7e, f). Furthermore, no notable fluctuations in mouse body weight were observed over the course of experiment, indicative of the excellent biocompatibility of mcm-1 (Fig. 7g). Subsequently, histopathological analysis was conducted to assess cellular apoptosis and ferroptosis in tumor tissues (Fig. 7h). Hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) staining of tumor tissues from mice treated with mcm-1 + 660 nm revealed tumor damage, characterized by enlarged intercellular spaces and nuclear shrinkage47. Immunohistochemical staining results demonstrated a marked reduction in the proliferation marker Ki67 following treatment with mcm-1 + 660 nm, indicating successful inhibition of tumor growth. Furthermore, Tunel staining revealed extensive apoptosis in the tumors induced by mcm-1 under 660 nm irradiation. And the immunofluorescence staining images revealed an obvious downregulation in the expression of GPX4 in the mcm-1 + 660 nm group, coupled with a notable upregulation in the expression of TFR1, suggesting that mcm-1 could inherently induce ferroptosis in vivo and confer antitumor benefits. Notably, apart from the antitumor effect, no signs of injury or inflammatory lesions were detected in the major organs of tumor-bearing mice treated with mcm-1, as determined by H&E staining, further affirming the excellent biocompatibility of mcm-1 (Supplementary Fig. 57). These results unveil the successful induction of apoptosis and ferroptosis mediated by the combination of PDT and PTT within 4T1 tumor tissues following treatment with mcm-1 + 660 nm, coupled with good biocompatibility.

Discussion

In summary, we have designed and synthesized the examples of pentagonal Cairo tessellations in 2D COFs, which was achieved with the linkers of precise geometry and metrics, resulted in unprecedented mcm topology. Due to the excellent NIR absorption, narrow bandgap and rapid carrier transport which facilitate photothermal conversion and 1O2 generation. Thus triggering mcm-COFs mediated PTT combined with PDT-induced apoptosis to achieve robust tumor repression. Furthermore, owing to its exceptional ROS production capacity, mcm-1 also upregulates intracellular lipid peroxidation, leading to glutathione depletion, low expression of glutathione peroxidase 4, and induction of a GPX4-related ferroptosis. This work not only expand the types of pores in 2D COFs and provide a strategy for increasing topological diversity of COFs, but also but also benefit the further advancement in the field of cancer phototherapy.

Methods

Materials

All commercially available starting compounds and solvents used in the research, except additionally stated, were purchased from Adamas-beta without further purification.

Synthesis of mcm-1

A 20 × 60 mm Pyrex tube, loaded with 0.02 mmol BPY (8.40 mg), Zn-TBPP (20.80 mg), 0.8 mL n-Butanol, 0.8 mL 1,2-Dichlorobenzene, 30 μL aniline and 30 μL benzaldehyde, underwent 20 min of sonication, followed by the addition of 0.4 mL 6 M acetic acid. Subsequently, the tube was flash frozen at 77 K, evacuated to 50 mTorr, flame sealed, and heated at 120 °C for 72 h. The resulting powder was filtered, DMF washed until the filtrate was colorless, then washed with 5 × 5 mL THF, and finally purified in a Soxhlet with acetone for 12 h. The dark green product was vacuum dried at room temperature overnight. Yield: 85%. Elemental analysis of mcm-1: Calcd. for C188N20Zn2H108: C, 81.39%; N, 10.10%; Zn, 4.62%; H, 3.89%.

Synthesis of mcm-2

The synthesis was carried out by utilizing the same protocol with a mixture of 0.02 mmol QPTA (8.36 mg), 0.02 mmol Zn-TBPP (20.80 mg), 0.5 mL n-Butanol, 0.5 mL 1,2-Dichlorobenzene, 30 μL aniline, 30 μL benzaldehyde and 0.2 mL aqueous acetic acid (6 M). Finally, the dark green product was evacuated at room temperature under vacuum overnight. Yield: 78%. Elemental analysis of mcm-2: Calcd. for C192N16Zn2H112: C, 83.24%; N, 8.09%; Zn, 4.62%; H, 4.05%.

Synthesis of mcm-3

The synthesis was carried out by utilizing the same protocol with a mixture of 0.02 mmol BPY (8.40 mg), 0.02 mmol H-TBPP (19.56 mg), 0.5 mL n-Butanol, 0.5 mL 1,2-Dichlorobenzene, 30 μL aniline, 30 μL benzaldehyde and 0.2 mL aqueous acetic acid (6 M). Finally, the maroon product was evacuated at room temperature under vacuum overnight. Yield: 75%. Elemental analysis of mcm-3: Calcd. for C188N20H112: C, 85.20%; N, 10.57%; H, 4.23%.

Synthesis of mcm-4

The synthesis was carried out by utilizing the same protocol with a mixture of 0.02 mmol QPTA (8.36 mg), 0.02 mmol H-TBPP (19.56 mg), 0.8 mL n-Butanol, 0.8 mL 1,2-Dichlorobenzene, 30 μL aniline, 30 μL benzaldehyde and 0.4 mL aqueous acetic acid (6 M). Finally, the maroon product was evacuated at room temperature under vacuum overnight. Yield: 79%. Elemental analysis of mcm-4: Calcd. for C192N16H116: C, 87.14%; N, 8.47%; H, 4.39%.

Photocatalytic H2production

5 mg of photocatalyst was carefully dispersed in 25 mL of H2O with 0.1 M ascorbic acid acting as a sacrificial agent. This solution was then placed into a sealed quartz tube setup. A Pt co-catalyst (3 wt%) was photo-deposited onto the photocatalyst using H2PtCl6. Before the photocatalytic evaluation, the system was purged with Ar to remove air. A 300 W Xe lamp (Beijing Perfectlight Technology Co., Ltd., Microsolar300) served as the light source, and a 420 nm or 700 nm cutoff filter facilitated visible light irradiation (780 nm > λ > 420 nm or 780 nm > λ > 700 nm). Hydrogen production was measured by gas chromatography. Post-collection and water rinsing, the cyclic photocatalytic tests were repeated, adhering to the same procedure.

DFT calculation

Geometry optimizations and frequency calculations utilized the hybrid B3LYP functional with the 6-31 G(d) basis set for C, H, and N, and the LANL2DZ basis set for Zn atoms, all performed with the Gaussian 03 software. The model, derived from a simulated COFs structure, had its dangling bonds capped with hydrogens. Post full optimization, frequency calculations were executed at the same computational level. The electronic energies were refined by single point calculations using the expanded 6-31++ G(d,p) basis set for nonmetal atoms. Visualization of the HOMO and LUMO molecular orbitals was achieved with Multiwfn and VMD software48.

Photothermal properties

The ROS generation efficiency of four COF samples was evaluated using DCFH-DA as an indicator. The COF samples were suspended in a PBS solution at a concentration of 100 μg mL−1, followed by the addition of DCFH-DA to a final concentration of 10 μM. Upon exposure to a 660 nm laser at an intensity of 1 W cm−2, the resulting fluorescence was monitored at various intervals with an F7000 spectrofluorometer from Hitachi, capturing the emission spectra.

Detection of ROS in solvents

200 μL of COF samples in varying concentrations were irradiated using a 660 nm near-infrared laser sourced from Beijing Laserwave Optoelectronics Technology Co., Ltd. The temperature variations were captured in real time using the FOTRIC 225 s infrared camera.

Cell and animals

The 4T1 mouse breast cancer cell line was sourced from the National Collection of Authenticated Cell Cultures in Shanghai, China, and maintained in culture medium comprising RPMI-1640 supplemented with 1% penicillin-streptomycin and 10% certified FBS, all from Gibco. The 4T1 cell line, cataloged as CRL-2539, was additionally procured from Wuhan Procell Life Science & Technology, while the 4T1-Luc cell line was acquired from Xiamen Immocell Biotechnology Co., Ltd., with its original source being ATCC. Healthy female BALB/c nude mice, 4–6 weeks old and ~15 g in weight, were selected for the study and obtained from the Model Animal Research Center (MARC) of Nanjing University (Nanjing, China), due to the predominance of mammary cancers in females. These mice were kept in an SPF-grade facility with a 12-hour light/dark cycle, ambient temperature ranging from 20 to 26 °C, and humidity levels stabilized between 40% and 70%. Conducting all animal experiments in accordance with the guidelines approved by the Animal Ethical and Welfare Committee of Nanjing University, the research was carried out under the oversight of protocol number IACUC-2405005.

Cytotoxicity assay

4T1 cells (3 × 103) were initially cultured in RPMI-1640 medium overnight. Subsequently, the medium was replaced with COF materials at varying concentrations (0, 25, 50, 75 and 100 μg mL−1), and subjected to 660 nm laser at an intensity of 1 W cm−2 for 5 min. After 24 h incubation, the viability of the 4T1 cells was evaluated using the MTT assay.

In vitro cellular uptake

4T1 cells were meticulously seeded onto round coverslips at the base of a 12-well plate and incubated at 37 °C for 12 h. Subsequently, they were subjected to COF samples at a concentration of 100 μg mL−1 for durations between 0 to 24 h, after which the cells were assessed using flow cytometry. In preparation for cell imaging, the 4T1 cells were first treated with mcm-1, followed by staining with Mito Tracker Green at 0.5 μM and Hoechst 33342 at 1 μM for 30 min. The cells were then extensively washed with PBS, a minimum of three times, prior to fixation with a 4% paraformaldehyde solution for 20 min. The prepared samples were examined under CLSM, utilizing excitation wavelengths of 405 nm with an emission bandpass of 410–500 nm, 488 nm with an emission bandpass of 550–650 nm, and 633 nm with an emission bandpass of 650–750 nm.

Detection of ROS in cells

The 4T1 cells were treated with COF samples at 100 μg mL−1 for 6 h. Specific samples were treated with NAC at 100 μM for 30 min, followed by irradiation with a 660 nm laser at 1 W cm−2. Subsequently, the cells were stained with DCFH-DA at 10 μM for 30 min and then observed using CLSM.

Analysis of mitochondrial membrane potential

4T1 cells were evenly spread on round coverslips in 12-well plates and incubated at 37 °C for 12 h. After 6 h treatment with 100 μg mL−1 COF samples, some were exposed to 660 nm laser irradiation at 1 W cm−2 for 5 min. The JC-1 kit was employed to evaluate mitochondrial membrane potential, with samples analyzed by flow cytometry and CLSM. JC-1 monomers were identified with excitation at 514 nm and emission at 529 nm, whereas aggregates were detected with excitation at 585 nm and emission at 590 nm.

Mitochondrial morphology assays

4T1 cells were treated with mcm-1 at a concentration of 100 μg mL−1 for 24 h. After irradiation with a 660 nm laser at 1 W cm−2 for 5 min, the cells were incubated for an additional 6 h, then washed three times with PBS and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 1 h. The mitochondrial morphology was examined using TEM. The cells were initially fixed in 0.1 M sodium cacodylate buffer (pH 7.4) containing 2.5% glutaraldehyde, followed by post-fixation in 2% aqueous osmium tetroxide and dehydration in an ethanol/propylene oxide mixture. Subsequently, the samples were embedded in Epon and incubated at 60 °C for 24 h (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany). Prior to TEM analysis, ultrathin sections were prepared and stained with 0.5% uranyl acetate for 30 min, followed by 3% lead citrate for 7 min.

Intracellular detection of GPX4 and TFR1

4T1 cells were treated overnight with different samples. Following treatment, 2 × 103 cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and infiltrated with 0.5% Triton X-100 (BS084, Biosharp). They were then incubated in 3% BSA (Biosharp BS114) at room temperature with (a) an anti-GPX4 antibody diluted 1:200 (CY6959, Abways) followed by goat anti-rabbit Cy3 diluted 1:300 (AB0133, Abways), or (b) an anti-TFR1 antibody diluted 1:200 (CY6618, Abways) followed by goat anti-rabbit IgG Alexa Fluor 488 diluted 1:400 (AB0141, Abways), both incubations lasting 1 h. The cells were finally counterstained with DAPI (P0131, Beyotime Biotechnology) and examined using an SP8 confocal microscope from Leica.

Western blot

Proteins were lysed with 30 min in buffer provided by Beyotime and rinsed with PBS. Protein concentrations were assessed using the BCA assay kit from the same manufacturer, loading a standard volume of 30 μg per well. Separation of proteins was achieved through polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis with Epizyme reagents, and the resolved proteins were transferred onto a PVDF membrane from Beyotime. Non-specific antigens were blocked with 5% skimmed milk for 2 h, after which immunoblotting was performed with different antibodies, including glutathione peroxidase 4 (1:1000 dilution, CY6959, Abways), transferrin receptor (1:1000 dilution, CY6618, Abways), and GAPDH (1:5000 dilution, 60004-1-Ig, Proteintech).

Measurement of lipid ROS

Cells from each experimental group were rinsed and immersed in 1 mL of PBS with 0.5 μM C11-BODIPY (581/591) dye (D3861, Invitrogen) at 37 °C for 60 min. Subsequently, the alterations in fluorescence signals were evaluated using confocal microscopy.

Animal experiments

Healthy female BALB/c nude mice, 4 to 6 weeks old and approximately 15 g in weight, were procured from Model Animal Research Center (MARC) of Nanjing University (Nanjing, China). The experimental procedures were strictly in line with the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC-2405005) guidelines at Nanjing University.

In vivo treatment

To establish a 4T1 hormonal mouse model, healthy female BALB/c nude mice aged 4–6 weeks were subcutaneously injected into 4T1 cells (1 × 106 cells in PBS) in the right hind leg. Once tumors reached a volume of 100 mm3, the mice were randomly divided into four groups (n = 5): (1) PBS, (2) PBS + Laser, (3) mcm-1, and (4) mcm-1 + Laser. mcm-1 was dosed at 10 mg kg−1. Treatments were applied three times. Body weight and tumor volume (calculated as volume[V] = length × width2/2) were monitored every two days for 12 days of treatment. At the end of treatment, mice were euthanized, and tumors along with major organs were harvested from each group for analysis via H&E staining. To observe apoptosis after different treatments, TUNEL and Ki67 staining were additionally applied. Immunohistochemical staining for GPX4 and TFR1 was performed to evaluate the expression levels of GPX4 versus TFR1 in the tumor tissues among the different groups.
