Generative artificial intelligence models outperform students on divergent and convergent thinking assessments
Authors: Vikram Arora, Alex Thabane, Sameer Parpia, Goran Calic, Mohit Bhandari
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) has garnered significant attention for its remarkable capabilities and is now widely used in many creative domains, sparking scientific interest in its creative capacities. While previous studies have assessed GenAI on creativity tasks, there is no evidence comparing the latest GenAI models to the same sample of humans on both divergent and convergent thinking assessments. In this study, we compared the creative ability of human participants (n = 46) against three state-of-the-art GenAI chatbots—ChatGPT-4o, DeepSeek-V3, and Gemini 2.0—using the Alternate Uses Task (AUT) and the Remote Associates Test (RAT). For divergent thinking, we compared the median and maximum originality scores on the AUT, representing the level of originality of the ‘average’ and ‘best’ idea. We then compared performance on 57 RAT items as a measure of convergent thinking. All GenAI models outperformed human participants in both tasks: the ‘average’ and ‘best’ GenAI ideas were significantly more original than human-generated ideas. All GenAI models demonstrated superior RAT performance vs. humans. Among GenAI models, ChatGPT-4o consistently demonstrated the best scores on both tasks. These findings illustrate the immense creative potential of GenAI, but call into question the appropriateness of current creativity assessment methods in the study of GenAI creativity.

Introduction

Artificial intelligence and creativity

Artificial intelligence (AI) technologies are now capable of performing complex tasks, many of which were previously considered the exclusive purview of humans1–3. This rise in capability has been largely fueled by the advent and evolution of generative AI (GenAI) via large language models (LLMs) like OpenAI’s Generative Pre-Trained Transformers (GPT) series. The capability and accessibility of recent LLMs have led to the widespread utilization of GenAI in virtually every field, including in creative and artistic domains. GenAI’s apparent capacity for creativity – AI-generated art products are often indistinguishable from human-created products4,5 – has challenged our traditional notions of creativity and what is required to generate creative products6.

The increasing use of GenAI has stimulated scientific research on its creative abilities. Human-AI co-creativity, defined as the collaboration of AI systems and humans in an interactive and shared creative process7–9, has been another common area of inquiry, with some evidence suggesting its ability to enhance the creativity of humans7,10. At the same time, AI has been explored as a creative assessment tool, with studies showing high agreement between AI- and human-generated creativity ratings11,12. On narrative tasks, such as writing stories, studies have found that GenAI exhibits similar or poorer creativity compared to humans, but higher fluency13–15.

Despite these advances, the independent creative abilities of AI remain under active debate. Many scholars argue that genuine creativity involves more than just generating novel and useful outputs, but also requires additional criteria like intentionality, consciousness, and imagination, which AI systems currently lack6. Under this view, while an AI may produce something original through the statistical recombination of its training data, it should not be deemed truly creative since it does not originate from an autonomous inventive intent or personal experience. On the other hand, some theorists take a more inclusive view of creativity, focusing on outcomes rather than the creator’s nature. Under the “standard” criteria of creativity16, they believe AI outputs can qualify as creative. Additionally, just as human creativity builds on past knowledge, some argue that the recombination of existing ideas in new ways that AI undertakes is not fundamentally different from human creativity, just faster and vaster in scope17.

Ultimately, understanding whether AI can be truly creative is not just a philosophical question but a practical one. Fortunately, the language-based nature of LLMs makes them well-suited to the current psychometric approaches to measuring individual creative ability. The longest-standing and most widely utilized tests of creativity are assessments of divergent thinking (DT), a psychological operation first described by J.P. Guilford’s Structure of the Intellect Model as the ability to generate novel and useful ideas18. In the creative problem-solving process19, DT is related to and typically followed by convergent thinking (CT), which is defined as the ability to connect different thoughts to identify correct answers or problem solutions8.

Current research

Unsurprisingly, GenAI can generate vastly larger volumes of ideas than humans in divergent thinking tasks. But more interestingly, scientific evidence has emerged suggesting GenAI has a comparable, and even superior, ability to generate original ideas on divergent thinking tests compared to humans20–24. This is an important finding given the relationship between divergent thinking tests and creative achievement25, as well as the value and utility of creative ideas to society26,27. While a growing literature base exists exploring GenAI performance on divergent thinking tasks, the evaluation of GenAI on convergent thinking tasks has been less frequently explored. We found only two studies exploring GenAI performance on convergent thinking tasks, which reported similar or slightly stronger performance compared to humans28,29. This outcome is expected given the nature of convergent thinking tasks, which typically require arriving at a single correct answer—AI models are trained on vast amounts of data, which likely includes the task data. However, convergent thinking is widely regarded as a crucial component of the creative problem-solving process19, particularly in high-risk domains where novel but ineffective ideas can be harmful30. Thus, despite the anticipated advantages of AI on such tests, its convergent thinking abilities warrant further exploration to understand how it can contribute to creativity and the creative problem-solving process.

Despite significant progress in AI-creativity research, several gaps remain in the existing literature. We are unaware of any research that has directly compared the performance of the same population on both divergent and convergent thinking tasks to GenAI models. This is essential for building a nuanced understanding of GenAI’s creative profile and how its capabilities compare with those of humans. Additionally, given the rapid evolution of GenAI technologies over the last few years and the resulting improvements in creative performance20, continual research is required to update our understanding of the creative capabilities of GenAI. With the recent releases of OpenAI’s 4o models, Google’s Gemini 2.0, and the Chinese DeepSeek V3 model, a new study comparing GenAI and humans on creativity assessments was warranted and of great interest.

The present study

This study aimed to compare the divergent and convergent thinking abilities of humans and three different GenAI chatbots. Specifically, our primary objectives were:

As a secondary objective, we aimed to compare divergent and convergent thinking performance across different GenAI models.

We hypothesized that GenAI would match or outperform humans on the divergent thinking task, in terms of ‘average’ and ‘best’ ideas, consistent with previous findings using similar GenAI models20–24. We also hypothesize that GenAI performance will match the performance of humans on the convergent thinking task, which is similar to the findings in a previous study28.

By conducting a side-by-side comparison of humans and GenAI on both divergent and convergent thinking tasks, this study aimed to provide foundational insight into the independent creative abilities of current GenAI models. This is a necessary step toward designing future systems that support collaborative, rather than competitive, human-AI creative processes.

Methods

This study was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the experimental protocol was approved by the Hamilton Integrated Research Ethics Board (Project #17960). All participants provided informed consent prior to the start of the study.

Participants

Human participants

To calculate our required sample size, we utilized data from existing research that employed the same scoring methodology to assess AUT scores as we planned in our study20. Specifically, we calculated our sample size for a Wilcoxon signed-rank test using the mean originality scores and standard deviation in humans and AI for the Alternative Uses Task (AUT) item ‘tire’, which we also planned to use in our study. At an α of 0.05 and power of 0.80, we estimated that we would require 16 participants per group to detect differences in AUT scores between humans and AI. We conducted the sample size calculation using G*Power (version 3.1)31. For the RAT, with 57 items with a 30–80% accuracy rate, we estimated the population variance to be 50.77 using the range rule of thumb32. To detect a mean difference of 5 points between groups at an α of 0.05 and power of 0.80, we estimated that we would require 32 participants. To be conservative, we took the maximum sample size from both tests and aimed to recruit at least 32 participants for this study.

Human participants (n = 46) were recruited via convenience snowball sampling, which exceeds the number required based on the prior calculation. We recruited undergraduate or graduate students aged 18 years or older at McMaster University in any faculty or department. Those who could not understand English, who were unable to understand or perform the creativity tasks, or those who did not consent were not eligible to participate. The average age of participants was 23.35 (SD = 3.47) years, ranging from 18 to 31 years. Complete demographic information of participants is shown in Table 1.

GenAI participants

We tested three GenAI chatbots: OpenAI’s ChatGPT-4o, DeepSeek-V3, and Google’s Gemini 2.0. Introduced in 2024, ChatGPT-4o is a proprietary autoregressive transformer-based LLM developed by OpenAI, based in San Francisco, California, USA. The chatbot aims to simulate a natural human-computer interaction, processing both images and text33. DeepSeek-V3 utilizes multi-head latent attention and DeepSeek mixture-of-experts language model architectures and is trained on 14.8 trillion diverse tokens, followed by supervised fine-tuning and reinforcement learning stages34. Last, Gemini 2.0 is a multimodal model that is optimized for large-scale language modelling. Trained on data from web documents, books, and code, Gemini 2.0 also incorporates image, audio, and video data35. The default parameters, such as temperature, were used to best simulate the most common interaction with these chatbots.

Creativity assessments

Alternate uses task

The Alternate Uses Task (AUT) measures divergent thinking by presenting a common object (i.e., ‘shoe,’ ‘tire,’ and ‘pants’) and asking participants to generate as many creative uses as possible for these objects36. The AUT measures fluency (i.e., number of responses) and originality (i.e., uniqueness of responses).

Remote associates task

The Remote Associates Task (RAT) is a measure of convergent thinking that presents a group of three words and requires participants to provide a single extra word that links all the others together37 (e.g., spot/throat/loser; correct answer: sore). Performance on this task is expressed as the number of correctly solved items within a time limit.

Procedure

The responses from human participants were collected online via REDCap electronic data capture tools hosted at McMaster University38,39. REDCap (Research Electronic Data Capture) is a secure, web-based software platform designed to support data capture for research studies. Before starting the creativity tasks, we collected demographic information (i.e., age, level of education, faculty of their current program, and whether English was their first language). After completing the initial form, participants read the instructions for the creativity tasks and had the opportunity to ask questions to the first author before starting the task. The order of the creativity tasks presented to participants was randomized.

Similar to prior studies22,40, the instructions for the AUT for human respondents were: “For this task, you’ll be asked to come up with as many original and creative uses for a [item] as you can. The goal is to come up with creative ideas, which are ideas that strike people as clever, unusual, interesting, uncommon, humorous, innovative, or different. Your ideas don’t have to be practical or realistic; they can be silly or strange, even, so long as they are CREATIVE uses rather than ordinary uses. You can enter as many ideas as you like. The task will take 3 min. You can type in as many ideas as you like until then, but creative quality is more important than quantity. It’s better to have a few really good ideas than a lot of uncreative ones. List as many ORIGINAL and CREATIVE uses for a [item]. Type each idea on a new line."

For GenAI chatbots, responses were generated by the first author using the above prompts, who administered each creativity task in the same test and item order as the respective human participant. ChatGPT-4o was tested on Feb/16/2025 and Feb/17/2025; DeepSeek was tested on Feb/17/2025, Feb/19/2025; and Feb/23/2025, and Gemini was tested on Feb/17/2025.

An equal number of GenAI sessions were conducted for each chatbot, corresponding to the number of human participants. For the AUT, the fluency of human resources was calculated after incomplete responses were removed and was used to match the fluency of GenAI chatbots. For the RAT, we ran the same number of sessions for each GenAI chatbot as the number of human participants. Each GenAI session was run separately, ensuring that the number of tests for the GenAI corresponded to the number of human participants in each group.

Scoring

To automate the scoring of AUT responses, we used the Open Creativity Scoring with AI (Ocsai) tool, which calculates the originality of ideas by assigning unique scores to each response41. Specifically, we used the ocsai1-4o model, an LLM-based scoring system that has demonstrated improved reliability over semantic distance scoring42. While human scoring has many potential confounds for scoring a large number of responses at a time (e.g., subjectivity, time, cost, fatigue43–45, automated scoring tools, such as Ocsai, have a high correlation with validated and reliable human ratings42. This model scored each response on a scale from 1 to 5, where 1 is minimally original and 5 is maximally original41.

Before conducting the automated AUT scoring, we checked responses for spelling and task compliance and removed any invalid or incomplete entries. Across all GenAI models, the ideas were presented as a core idea, followed by an extra explanation of the idea (e.g. “Portable Zen Garden: Fill a shoe with fine sand, add a few tiny pebbles and a rake made from toothpicks. Instant relaxation for the foot-weary soul on the go.”). To address elaboration bias, which refers to the tendency of AI scoring models to overvalue responses that are longer or more detailed46, only the core idea or “title” of each use was extracted and scored. For example, from the GenAI-generated sentence “Portable Zen Garden: Fill a shoe with fine sand, add a few tiny pebbles and a rake made from toothpicks. Instant relaxation for the foot-weary soul on the go.”, we only submitted “Portable Zen Garden” for scoring. This resulted in AI responses that more closely resembled the structure of the human responses.

For the RAT, the responses were automatically scored using the answer key from the scientific article that presented the normative data for the chosen questions32. Answers were reviewed to account for spelling errors and scores were adjusted accordingly.

Statistical analysis

For the first co-primary objective, in which we sought to compare the level of creativity of the ‘average’ idea generated by humans and GenAI, we compared the median scores between human and GenAI AUT originality scores. We used the median AUT score as a measure of the ‘average’ idea since originality scores in a set of AI responses may not be normally distributed. As each human AUT test had a corresponding GenAI test matched for fluency (number of responses), we compared the difference in median originality score of each participant and the corresponding GenAI result. A Wilcoxon signed-rank test was then used to assess whether the median differences across all pairs were significantly different from zero.

For the second co-primary objective, which aimed to compare the originality of the ‘best,’ or most creative, ideas between humans and GenAI, the differences in maximum AUT scores were calculated for each human-GenAI pair. We used the maximum score for each participant to reflect their ‘best’ idea. As with the first objective, a Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used to assess whether the median difference in maximum scores was significantly different from zero.

For both the first and second objectives, we reported the raw AUT scores in each group in the form of means, standard deviations, and ranges. The results from the statistical tests were presented as median differences with 95% confidence intervals and the corresponding p-value. We estimated a minimally important difference (MID) of 0.35 by using half of the standard deviation, which is a common standard across disciplines47–49; to be conservative in our estimate, we used the largest standard deviation reported in a previous experimental study that used an identical AUT item (‘tire’) and AI-based scoring program20.

For the final co-primary objective, which aimed to compare the convergent thinking ability of humans and GenAI, we reported the mean RAT scores, with standard deviations and ranges for the four experimental groups (humans, ChatGPT, DeepSeek, and Gemini). We conducted pairwise Student’s t-test comparisons between humans and each GenAI model (i.e., human vs. ChatGPT, human vs. DeepSeek, and human vs. Gemini), presenting the results in tables and reporting the associated p-values.

Lastly, for the secondary objective comparing the performance of GenAI models on divergent and convergent thinking ability, we conducted ANOVA analyses to assess whether significant differences existed within the GenAI groups (ChatGPT, DeepSeek, and Gemini) for both the AUT and RAT. For each AUT item, two separate ANOVAs were performed comparing the performance of the three GenAI models: one comparing the difference in the ‘average’ idea originality and ‘best’ idea originality across GenAI groups. Thus, six separate ANOVAs were conducted: two for each AUT item, comparing either the ‘average’ or ‘best’ originality scores. For the RAT, a single ANOVA was conducted to compare the average performance scores across the three GenAI groups. If any ANOVA yielded a p-value < 0.05, post-hoc Bonferroni-corrected t-tests were performed to further investigate the pairwise differences between the GenAI groups.

We conducted all analyses using R (Version 4.3.2) in RStudio50. All statistical tests were performed at an α level of 0.05 unless otherwise stated.

Discussion

This study aimed to compare the performance of humans and GenAI using three different models (ChatGPT-4o, DeepSeek V3, and Gemini 2.0) on divergent and convergent thinking assessments. We found that all GenAI models outperformed humans on both the divergent and convergent thinking tasks, demonstrating a higher level of originality in both their ‘average’ and ‘best’ ideas, and achieved near-perfect scores on the RAT. We also found significant differences between GenAI models, with ChatGPT-4o performing the best among the three models tested on both divergent and convergent thinking assessments.

Human participants scored significantly lower on the AUT than GenAI models, which aligns with the results of prior studies20–23. One explanation for the significant outcomes across all comparisons is that our sample was composed of undergraduate and graduate students, a slightly younger cohort than those used in previous AUT studies. Life experience and expertise have been associated with increased originality51, which may have influenced human performance and led to lower-than-expected originality scores. Second, GenAI models likely excel at generating the type of unique responses that would score highly on the AUT due to their training on vast amounts of text data, which allows them to recombine concepts in ways that appear creative. Third, from a cognitive perspective, human participants are often limited by cognitive constraints such as social norms and functional fixedness, which limit the exploration of unconventional ideas52,53. GenAI models lack such constraints and are theoretically capable of freely generating novel responses16, which explains the higher originality scores. However, it is possible that the cognitive constraints in humans, which limit the originality of ideas, may actually be necessary to ensure its usefulness, which is an essential criterion for creativity16. Research suggesting that LLM-generated ideas exhibit greater novelty but lower feasibility relative to human ideas may support this notion54. Given that the AUT only assesses originality, valid inferences on the creative potential GenAI relative to humans cannot be made based on originality scores alone.

While our results indicated the high originality of GenAI, it is important to note that current AI systems are not fully autonomous in their ability to set goals and intentions, which is critical to the real-world application of creative thinking55,56. Thus, GenAI is most likely to be practically utilized as a collaborative partner for humans in creative domains. The effects of GenAI assistance on creative tasks appear to be mixed, with some studies reporting an enhancement in creativity with GenAI use3, whereas others find no benefit and even negative effects55,57. Moreover, the use of GenAI has been found to homogenize creative products and diminish the level of intrinsic motivation and personal agency humans subsequently experience in their work3,58,59. As it currently stands, major scientific and creative discoveries will still require human ingenuity and creativity60; it is therefore critical that future studies continue to monitor the short and long-term effects of GenAI use to adequately balance the immediate productivity and creativity gains with any possible negative implications on human creativity in the long term.

GenAI also outperformed humans on the RAT, suggesting that GenAI models have greater convergent thinking ability than humans. It was interesting to note that the human sample results performed worse than expected, falling below established normative data32. Given that convergent thinking is closely related to working memory and intelligence61–63, this underperformance may have been due to the younger sample of students tested. We also suspect that our results may have been influenced by the large proportion (28.3%) of non-native English speakers in our sample. Non-native English speakers often struggle with word associations commonly found in Western-centric psychometric assessments64, which has led to reduced performance in the RAT in other studies65. Nevertheless, GenAI models demonstrated exceptionally high accuracy, with ChatGPT-4o achieving near-perfect performance; these findings differ from similar tests on older models29. GenAI LLMs are trained on increasingly large volumes of data from the internet, which likely includes the answers to the RAT items tested; evidence of this can be seen in the restricted variance of scores on the RAT from GenAI models and evidence of a ceiling effect. Based on this data, we believe that the RAT and other measures of creativity with online-accessible answers are now likely inappropriate for assessing the creativity of GenAI models.

Our secondary objective was to determine whether there were meaningful differences in creative ability between GenAI models. We found that ChatGPT-4o produced the most original responses for the AUT, while DeepSeek consistently generated the least original ideas. ChatGPT-4o has a larger and more diverse training dataset, which may have allowed it to generate responses that are more varied and novel compared to models with smaller datasets66. Furthermore, ChatGPT-4o employs a dense transformer architecture, which means all its parameters are activated during use67. This allows for a more comprehensive integration of information across various contexts, potentially leading to more original and varied responses. In contrast, DeepSeek uses a mixture-of-experts architecture, where only a subset of the model’s parameters are activated for a given input67. While this approach offers computational efficiency, it might limit the model’s capacity to combine disparate concepts creatively. These architectural and performance differences suggest that not all GenAI systems are interchangeable, and highlight the importance of model choice in co-creative systems. In the RAT, ChatGPT-4o outperformed the other GenAI models, scoring significantly higher than Gemini and DeepSeek. We similarly suspect that this stems from differences in training data size and model architecture, as some GenAI models may have been fine-tuned more explicitly for natural language processing tasks that involve associative reasoning68.

While our study provides valuable insights, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the potential for test fatigue in human participants may have influenced their performance, as human participants were required to complete multiple creativity tasks within a limited timeframe69,70. Second, our sample was limited to university students, which may not be representative of the broader population. Future research should examine whether older individuals with more crystallized knowledge and life experience perform differently compared to a general student population against GenAI. Third, while fluency control for the AUT ensured consistent comparisons between humans and GenAI, we may have missed unique ideational patterns in GenAI that are not seen in humans. For example, continuous prompting or later output may exhibit stronger or weaker results over time. Future research should investigate the effect of response order, such as the serial order effect71, in GenAI models. Fourth, participants were aware that their performance would be compared to AI models, which may have affected their performance. However, prior studies on the effect of competition on cognitive tests show mixed results72,73, so we do not believe this had a significant impact on the results. Last, we did not compare creativity task performance in grouped conditions, such as a human-human group or a human-GenAI group. Such data is of interest to explore differences in creativity between humans and GenAI in a co-creative arrangement, which is more akin to how creative ideas manifest in the real world.

Conclusions

We found that GenAI models outperformed humans on both divergent and convergent thinking tasks. Differences in divergent and convergent thinking ability also exist between different GenAI systems, with ChatGPT-4o displaying the highest levels of divergent and convergent thinking abilities. While these results suggest that GenAI LLMs are now highly creative beyond the level of humans, they also call into question the appropriateness of current psychometric approaches in assessing the creativity of GenAI. Future research on valid approaches to GenAI creativity assessment, as well as the long-term effects of GenAI use on human creativity, is of interest.
