Developing a DNA Methylation Signature to Differentiate High-Grade Serous Ovarian Carcinomas from Benign Ovarian Tumors
Authors: Douglas V. N. P. Oliveira, Edyta Biskup, Colm J. O’Rourke, Julie L. Hentze, Jesper B. Andersen, Claus Høgdall, Estrid V. Høgdall
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
IntroductionEpithelial ovarian cancer (EOC) represents a significant health challenge, with high-grade serous ovarian cancer (HGSOC) being the most common subtype. Early detection is hindered by nonspecific symptoms, leading to late-stage diagnoses and poor survival rates. Introduction

Epithelial ovarian cancer (EOC) is the second leading cause of death from gynecological malignancies and has a 5-year survival rate of approximately 52%. High-grade serous ovarian cancer (HGSOC) is the most prevalent histological subtype, ranging from 69 to 75% of all EOC [1–3]. The major reason for the high mortality among EOC patients is a lack of, or unspecific, early symptoms, resulting in more than 60% of patients being diagnosed in late stages of the disease, where the tumor has spread beyond the pelvic region [2–4]. The etiology of EOC remains elusive, further highlighting the need for efficient biomarkers to support diagnostics and personalized treatment, thus improving the overall survival and outcome for these patients.

Epigenetics is an emerging field in cancer diagnostics, from which one of the most well-studied processes is DNA methylation characterized by the addition of methyl groups to cytosines predominantly in CpG dinucleotide sites. Its biological function is associated with DNA stability [5] and programmed cellular functions, such as embryogenesis and inactivation of the X sex chromosome [6, 7]. In human cancer, epigenetic alterations are emerging as common hallmarks of disease initiation and progression, providing promising evidence as prognostic biomarkers [8–10]. Indeed, changes in the methylation landscape have been indicated to be precursors of tumor initiation [11, 12]. Moreover, recent epigenomic studies have shown encouraging results on the identification of molecular subtypes of EOC, assisting its further clinical characterization [13–15]. In contrast to gene expression-based biomarkers, DNA methylation is chemically and biologically stable, underscoring its potential as a cancer biomarker [16–19]. Among the technologies available, the Infinium® Methylation BeadChip assay from Illumina is widely used to investigate global methylation patterns. The latest array format (EPIC v2) allows for the investigation of approximately 935,000 known CpG sites for each individual sample, encompassing regulatory regions, such as enhancers and promoters, and gene bodies, providing the ability to reveal functional sites never investigated before in EOC research. For instance, altered methylation patterns have been detected in early stages of different cancer types, even at preneoplastic stages in developing tumors [12, 20, 21]. Specifically, in a cohort of patients with EOC, the methylation profiling of 27,000 sites suggested that the risk of neoplastic transformation could be predicated in normal cells by collecting cell samples from healthy individuals 3 years prior to the first signs of morphological transformations in those individuals that developed EOC [20]. In a more comprehensive study, 5852 gene-associated methylated targets were identified comparing EOC with normal fallopian tubes [22]. Nonetheless, either due to the coverage of only 0.1% of potential methylation targets in the genome, or the comparison between malignant and normal tissue, investigations do not fully address the potential initial changes on the methylation landscape of patients with ovarian cancer (OC).

Surgical removal of the pelvic mass is the current standard procedure for suspicious EOC cases, based on preoperative risk assessment criteria for malignancy. These criteria may be imprecise, and thus identifying new biomarkers that would enhance diagnostic accuracy would be beneficial [23]. Of note, although cells of the fallopian tube with a specific cancer-associated p53 signature are considered precursors of HGSOC [24, 25], detecting early-stage disease is challenging as the presence of a pelvic mass is usually asymptomatic. Hence, methylation profiling of pelvic mass samples might have significant clinical relevance, and as yet has not been investigated. In the current study, we aimed to investigate genome-wide differential DNA methylation alterations between HGSOC and non-malignant ovarian pelvic mass tissue samples, to observe whether that difference is associated with early development of the disease. Herein, we further described a predictive model based on our samples with further validation in two independent cohorts.

Materials and Methods

Patients and Tissue Samples

In total, we collected 49 samples from the Danish Pelvic mass/GOVEC cohort, which includes the registration of all women admitted to the Gynecologic Clinic at Rigshospitalet for surgery due to a potentially malignant mass in the pelvic region. Clinical data were retrieved from the Danish Gynecologic Cancer Database (DGCD, www.dgcg.dk/), a national clinical database, which has information on all Danish women with gynecological cancers with regular updates. The study was carried out according to the guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki, including written informed consent from all participants, and it was approved by the Danish National Committee for Research Ethics, Capital Region (approval codes KF01-227/03 and KF01-143/04). All patients were followed until death of any cause, emigration, or January 17th, 2015.

Fresh frozen tissue samples, verified by pathologists to reflect the content of tumor cells and diagnosis, were collected from each individual, and further submitted to analysis and classification for histological subtype, grade, and tumor stage. The classification was performed by specialized gynecological pathologists from the Department of Pathology at Herlev Hospital, following the guidelines from FIGO. All tumor samples were from the serous adenocarcinoma histological subtype. Samples were registered also in the Danish CancerBiobank (Bio- and GenomeBank Denmark) and handled according to their guidelines (www.rbgb.dk).

Methylation Analysis

Fresh frozen tissue was cut with a scalpel in a clean environment and homogenized in TE buffer, before DNA extraction using the Maxwell® RSC Tissue DNA Kit (Promega), followed by a purification step performed using the QIAquick PCR Purification system (Qiagen). Samples were quantitated on a Qubit fluorometer (Thermo Scientific) and diluted to a concentration of 500 ng/µL. Prior to methylation protocol, 500 ng of each sample was bisulfite-converted using the EZ DNA Methylation Kit (Zymo Research). Global DNA methylation was profiled with the Infinium® Methylation EPIC BeadChip Kit (manual protocol, Illumina), comprising approximately 850,000 cancer-related methylation sites. Sampling was performed blindly and randomly before loading onto the BeadChip arrays. Stained BeadChips were scanned using the iScan system (Illumina).

Bioinformatics

Array quality control (QC) assessment, filtering, normalization, and differential methylation analysis were performed in the R environment, with IlluminaHumanMethylationEPICanno.ilm10b4.hg19, Illumina Human Methylation EPIC manifest, minfi, missMethyl, and DMRcate packages [26–29]. The QC step included (1) removal of samples with an average detection p value > 0.05 across all probes (one benign sample removed from the study cohort) and (2) removal of individual probes with a detection p value > 0.01 in at least one of the samples. Data were further subjected to functional normalization using quantile normalization, by adjusting for known covariates measuring unwanted variation, specifically designed for Illumina’s platform [30]. Further filtering included removal of probes known to be affected by common single nucleotide variants (SNPs) and removal of cross-reactive probes and polymorphic CpGs [31]. β values were calculated as the ratio between the intensities of methylated alleles and total allele intensity. M values were obtained from the logit of β values. Given that M values were reported to be more statistically valid, with a better performance in terms of the ratio between detection rate and true positive rate, they were used in the differential methylation and statistical analysis [32].

Statistical Analysis

Following background correction and normalization, the data were initially submitted to a microarray-implemented univariate linear regression with an empirical Bayes (eB) approach [33], identifying 12,304 probes (adjusted p value < 0.05; log fold-change [lfc] > 2). We proceeded by submitting those probes to a second step of the general linear model, resulting in 1457 targets. The large number of predictors were further submitted to a least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO)-penalized model for multivariate linear regression, where the resulting targets were submitted to cross-validation (fivefold). Targets with p < 0.01 were considered associated with EOC, and a penalty α = 1.0 was applied for the LASSO method, resulting in 21 targets (Supplementary [Suppl.] Table S1; see the electronic supplementary material). Those final targets were able to distinguish two subgroups: serous OC and benign/borderline samples. From such candidates, we devised a predictive model based on the centroids of the sample subgroups, modified from a previous model [34]. Briefly, for a given sample S (from an external origin) to be assigned to either subgroup, and X the total set of probes measured (~ 850,000) from S, we proceeded as follows: (1) identify the 21 probe sets common to X; (2) compute the centroids of the two subgroups on the reduced dataset of 21 probe sets, using the 48 from our own cohort; (3) compute the distance of the new input S to those two centroids; (4) assign sample S to the subgroup with the closest centroid value, defined by Pearson’s correlation.

For batch correction, we performed two different approaches designated for large-scale expression datasets [35–37]. In both cases, they were applied post-data normalization and prior to use in our trained model. For the first, we used an eB-supervised method, namely comBat, where the biological information is added to the batch correction. Briefly, the method consists of three steps: (1) data standardization, (2) estimation of eB batch effect, and (3) data adjustment for batch effect [35]. For the second approach, given the proposition of our model, we applied a non-supervised method developed specifically for prediction, namely frozen surrogate variable analysis (fSVA) [37]. Here, we pooled the data of all cohorts, randomly assigned a training and a test, composed of 60 and 126 samples, respectively. For the samples in the test set, neither the outcome/class nor the latent sources of variability are known. We further performed post hoc tests to evaluate potential batch correction bias. Tukey honestly significant difference (HSD) test was employed to determine whether the differences of means were maintained for each cohort, pre- and post-batch correction treatment [38]. Similarly, Levene’s test was applied individually to each cohort to assess equality of variances [39]. All statistical analyses were performed in the R environment.

External Dataset Validation

Methylation data from normal ovarian epithelium and OC samples from two publicly available cohorts were assessed in order to test the efficiency of the predictive model developed on our study samples. The first cohort was composed of 91 primary HGSOC and 12 normal fallopian tube epithelia (FTE) samples [22]. The second cohort included 12 matched precursor serous tubal intraepithelial carcinoma (STIC) and adjacent normal FTE samples [11], one non-matched FTE sample, and two HGSOC samples. Both cohorts were retrieved from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) data repository, with their corresponding identification GSE133556 and GSE155761, respectively. Raw data were background corrected, normalized, and transformed by applying the same methods described above. Noteworthy, both cohorts also employed the Illumina Methylation EPIC 850K array platform, so to minimize data loss or incompatibility for the validation study.

For comparison of performances, we used the targets found in an early study and tested them against the 21 targets from our current investigation, by clustering analysis and employment on our predictive model. Their raw data were submitted to their analysis approach, following all the steps described [22]. We next evaluated how those targets clustered samples in our own cohort and the GSE155761 study, and finally employed them to train the predictive algorithm to examine its performance in those cohorts.

Results

Identification of Differentially Methylated Sites in HGSOC Samples

We examined differentially methylated patterns between benign ovarian pelvic mass and HGSOC samples by probing over 850,000 targets, to (1) identify candidate biomarkers specific to the disease and (2) devise a training-based predictive algorithm for sample classification. For the former goal, we performed methylation array in 48 samples of our own cohort: 29 HGSOC, 14 benign ovarian pelvic mass, and five BOTs. We included BOT samples as they share histopathological similarities with benign pelvic mass but are known to harbor low malignant potential. The clinicopathological characteristics of patients are shown in Table 1. We investigated a total of 865,859 loci across the genome from all samples. On the first round of quality check, 778,954 probes (90.0%) passed the detection p value threshold (p < 0.01), while 86,905 (10.0%) were removed. In the following filtering steps, we performed removal of (1) probes known to be affected by common SNPs and (2) probes with cross-reactivity in multiple loci in the genome, resulting in 728,363 probes subjected to further analysis. Prior to differential methylation analysis, the overall methylation levels among samples were assessed in an unsupervised manner, where a trend towards a clustering for HGSOC is already observed (Suppl. Figure 1a; see the electronic supplementary material). We next identified differentially methylated probes (DMPs) between benign/BOT tissue samples and EOC. Provided that BOTs are of low malignancy potential, with very distinct features compare to EOC, we included five samples from patients diagnosed with BOT together with benign samples to determine a clear unique methylation pattern of EOC alone. In total, 12,304 probes were found differentially methylated (5329 hypermethylated and 6975 hypomethylated), using an adjusted p value < 0.05 and lfc above 2. The selected targets underwent a second step of linear modeling, resulting in 1457 targets (627 hypermethylated and 830 hypomethylated). All analysis steps are depicted in Fig. 1. Here, the topmost DMPs showed similar methylation profiles for benign and BOT samples, whilst markedly different profiles for EOC samples (Fig. 2a; Suppl. Figure 1b). Given the large number of targets to build a predictive model, we next applied a LASSO-penalty regularization followed by cross-validation, resulting in 21 DMPs (Suppl. Table 1; Suppl. Figure 1b). From those, ten were in either gene bodies or transcription start sites (TSSs), such as for RAD52, ABL1, RP4, CCL28, KCNIP1, VOPP1, RUNX1T1, CKLF, BCAR1, and ZBTB4, whilst the remainder spanned untranslated regions (UTRs). We observed that EOC presented a considerably unique methylation pattern (Fig. 2b), whereas the benign and BOT pelvic mass samples showed very similar methylation patterns between them. Heatmap cluster analysis further showed the distinction between the EOC and non-EOC groups, with a strong association among the final candidates (Fig. 2c, d). The performance assessment of all targets measured by receiver operating characteristics (ROCs) showed an average area under the curve (AUC) of 0.924 ± 0.04 and 0.933 ± 0.03 for the hypomethylated and hypermethylated candidates, respectively (Fig. 2e; Suppl. Figure 2). Noteworthy, despite showing very similar profiles for these DMPs (R2 = 0.82; p value = 5.69 × 10−6), when analyzed separately, benign pelvic mass and borderline samples did present four DMPs between their methylation landscapes that did not overlap with those in our 21-DMP classifier, indicating the latter are specific to HGSOC (Suppl. Figure 3). Predictive Model and Performance Assessment on External Cohorts

To investigate whether our findings were reproducible, we assessed the performance of the 21 DMPs in two independent external cohorts, namely GSE133556 and GSE155761. First, we tested the classification efficiency of those targets by ROC curve, where it achieved an average AUC of 0.812 ± 0.069 (confidence interval [CI] = 95%) and 0.807 ± 0.061 (CI = 95%), respectively (Suppl. Figure 4; see the electronic supplementary material). Notably, neither of the cohorts were composed of ovarian pelvic mass as controls. The study GSE133556 was composed of HGSOC and FTE samples. When comparing to our findings, 17 out of 21 DMPs (81.0%) were significantly different in cohort GSE133556, with a clear distinction between the FTE samples and HGSOC groups. Similarly, the GSE155761 cohort, composed of 27 samples divided into at least one matching pair of malignant and benign biospecimens, showed 15 out of 21 significantly different DMPs (71.4%) (Suppl. Figure 5). In both cohorts, we observed a few malignant samples clustering within the benign group, 13.1% (13/99) and 14.3% (2/14), respectively (Suppl. Figure 6a and b). Overall, in both cohorts, all targets showed a similar directional trend (hyper- or hypomethylation) to those in our cohort (Suppl. Figure 5).

We next sought to devise a predictive model able to perform the classification of independent external samples. The core model was built based on a distance-to-centroid approach, i.e., new data points were classified as benign/borderline or EOC based on their distance from a cluster central point (centroid). Here, we used the previously identified EOC-specific 21 DMPs and employed our own cohort as the training set. To refine the prediction efficiency, we applied different approaches, as shown in Fig. 3a, b. On the first approach, we trained the model using our own cohort and tested it on the two other external cohorts (Fig. 3c). We observed a 53.2% and 77.8% classification concordance in cohorts GSE133556 and GSE155761, respectively (Table 2). By concordance in this case we understand the fraction (percentage) of samples where the output of our predictive model agrees with their real phenotype. Considering that training predictive models is heavily dependent on the number of samples, we speculated that the efficiency of our algorithm could benefit if trained in a larger cohort. Hence, we next employed the samples from GSE133556 (n = 111) as the training set. We further considered performing batch correction of both external cohorts to our own cohort prior to submitting them to the predictive model. The rationale behind it was to minimize any technical variance produced within different cohorts, while preserving the biological differences. Nonetheless, to control for any potential bias introduced by batch correction, we performed post hoc analysis to each external cohort, pre- and post-batch correction treatment. Results showed that the correlation of those 21 candidate DMPs between our own samples and either cohort GSE133556 or GSE155761 was overall concordant prior to batch correction (R2 = 0.97 and 0.79, respectively), and stronger for the latter post-batch correction (R2 = 0.94 and 0.93, respectively) (Fig. 4a). Moreover, batch correction maintained the differences of means between normal and malignant samples in each cohort, whilst preserving the equality of variance between groups (Fig. 4b; Suppl. Tables S2 and S3). Following batch correction, our results showed a 74.8% classification concordance for GSE133556 and 74.1% for GSE155761. Moreover, when trained on a larger cohort (GSE133556), that classification efficiency increased further to 92.6% and 100% for GSE155761 and our own cohort, respectively (Table 2). Altogether, these results indicate that the predictive performance of our model is sensitive to the size of the training set, an inherited limitation of this type of analysis. Comparison with Previous External Molecular Classifier

To further assess the efficiency of our centroid-based model, we evaluated the performance of previously published results by Reyes and colleagues [22], where they identified 5852 DMPs between FTE and primary HGSOC. Where those targets were not available, as an alternative, we proceeded by performing the same analysis pipeline described in their publication [22]. In contrast to their report, we observed 7344 DMPs (3418 hypermethylated and 3926 hypomethylated) between these two groups. Of note, three out of the 7344 DMPs (namely cg12162450, cg17518215, and cg01142407) overlapped with our classifier. Despite the discrepancy in the number of targets from the original publication, those targets were able to clearly differentiate FTE from primary HGSOC in that cohort (Suppl. Figure 7a and b; see the electronic supplementary material). The same 7433 targets performed similarly in GSE155761 cohort and to lesser extent in our own data. Interestingly, GSE155761 was also composed of FTE and serous ovarian carcinoma samples, whilst our study made use of ovarian pelvic mass samples, which may partially explain the differences observed. We proceeded by exploring the prediction performance of those targets by applying the same criteria in our model. The only exception is the use of the 7344 targets in contrast to those 21 from our own study. Here, we observed that the model was able to predict 75.0% samples of our own study and 92.6% of GSE155761 (Table 3). When applying batch correction, there was no improvement of the predictive model. Instead, there was a marked decrease for GSE155761 to 55.6%. Moreover, as observed previously when training was performed using a smaller cohort (e.g., our own samples), the efficiency decreased to 85.2% for GSE155761 and 78.6% for GSE133556. Discussion

Research into cancer etiology and the discovery of new biomarkers are important steps towards earlier diagnostics, personalized medicine, and prolonged survival for OC patients. EOC represents the majority of OC diagnosed, with approximately 90% of the cases [40]. A critical clinical feature of EOC is that it has the highest fatality rate among female reproductive cancers, primarily due to over 70% of cases being diagnosed at advanced stages [1, 3, 40]. On the other hand, improving prevention or early detection of the disease can result in an extensive impact on patient life quality. When diagnosed at a local, early stage, it can have a 5-year relative survival rate of 88–93% [3, 40]. A large challenge is an inconclusive understanding of the early events of ovarian carcinogenesis, which is reflected in there being no effective screening strategy in clinical practice.

Epigenetic alterations have long been suggested to be associated with the development and progression of human cancers [41], with studies initially centered on investigating single targets, due to methodological limitations. Within the past decade, epigenetic research has evolved to genome-wide analyses, confirming those markers as a common hallmark of human cancers [42–44]. DNA methylation is a key epigenetic regulator of gene expression associated with different cancer types [45]. Because typical features, such as global DNA methylation disruption, can be commonly observed in non-malignant neoplasia and early-stage tumors, evidence indicates that epigenetic dysregulation likely precedes the canonical DNA transforming events, such as mutations in tumor suppressors, activation of oncogenes, and genomic instability [42, 46]. In the current study, we sought to investigate the genome-wide methylation landscape of EOC. To our knowledge, this is the first large-scale methylation comparative study with ovarian pelvic masses. In our study, we also included BOT samples together with those from benign pelvic masses as they are more similar in behavior to samples from patients diagnosed with benign ovarian tumors as compared to those from OC patients. The reason for doing so was to identify a specific methylation pattern for serous OC. On one hand, the borderline histological type represents a separate entity within the EOC group, presenting unique features of its own—non-invasive, low malignant potential, atypical proliferation, and favorable prognosis [47]. On the other hand, borderline and benign samples seem to share some common molecular and clinical characteristics when compared to EOC, such as comparatively similar levels of CA-125 and HE-4 and Risk of Malignancy Algorithm (ROMA) and Copenhagen Index (CPH-I) scores [48].

Given the nature of the method providing information on over 850,000 target sites, it favored the use of more stringent quality cutoffs and statistical analysis. In that manner, after performing the standard practice for DMP analysis, we proceeded by submitting the found candidates to a round of multivariate analysis with a LASSO-penalty regression to find those with a closer association and to achieve smaller variance within the samples [49, 50]. This method is reported to perform best in clinical studies such as ours, where the sample size is comparatively small in relation to the number of regression coefficients [50]. Then, to validate our findings, we set out to identify external cohorts with a sample composition comparable to ours using the same technological platform. Noteworthy, despite other OC methylation data being publicly available, most studies did not investigate either benign or BOT cases, and nor were they performed on the same platform. Thus, only two studies fit the criteria, GSE133556 and GSE155761. In accordance with our findings, the 21 DMPs were able to distinguish benign cases from tumor patients. For cohort GSE133556, there were a few cancer samples that clustered closer to the benign sample group. However, due to the lack of clinical data, we were not able to investigate whether that was associated with any common feature among patients. Moreover, in cohort GSE155761, we observed two tumor samples grouped within the benign group. Interestingly, both clustered together with their matched benign counterpart, possibly indicating a methodological cross-contamination during the laser capture microdissection, or an early onset of the disease, where the methylation changes are transitioning to a malignant onset [11].

We sought to devise a predictive algorithm capable of identifying serous OC from benign and BOT cases. To that end, we employed the 21 candidate targets and used a centroid-based prediction approach. Furthermore, we executed the algorithm in two different forms. On the first case, we employed our own samples as a training set, while using the GSE133556 and GSE155761 individually as testing sets. On an initial assessment, the algorithm reached a classification concordance of 53.2% and 77.8%, respectively. Considering that non-biological variations are inherited, but not restricted, to high-throughput platforms, batch effect corrections are essential when developing or validating molecular signatures for diagnostics and prognostics [51]. Hence, when correcting our model for potential batch effects, we observed an improvement of 74.8% for GSE133556, whilst seeing a similar performance for GSE155761 and the training set, 74.1% and 97.9%, respectively, underlying the importance of non-biological minimization when considering results validation even within the same platform [51, 52]. Indeed, even though the latest methylation platform employed in this study has drastically advanced in comparison to its predecessors, improvement of batch effect reduction has continually been considered and developed, including in OC research [52, 53]. Noteworthy, we opted to use a logistic regression-based machine-learning approach for our predictive model, as this method tends to perform better with reduced sample size when compared to other methods [54, 55]. Nonetheless, if overly reduced, the sample size may also be a limitation for the predictive efficiency [56]. Hence, we further assessed the performance of our model by increasing the sample size of our training set with the GSE133556 cohort (n = 111). In corroboration with our initial findings, there was a noticeable improvement of our model, and further accuracy increase when batch correction was employed. In regard to the feasibility of our method of choice, a recent study systematically investigated the performance of a logistic regression-based predictive model in 12 different datasets, reporting an AUC as high as 0.71 in a study comprising 131 individuals and 47 predictors [55, 57].

We also attempted to perform a benchmark comparison between our findings and the previously reported DMPs. Thus, we employed the same analysis pipeline described by Reyes and colleagues and found as many as 7344 DMPs [22]. Albeit prediction accuracy was comparable for both signatures (our 21 DMPs and the 7344-DMP set), it is worth noting that it would be challenging to design a diagnostic panel based on so many features, and in this aspect, our approach may be preferred.

To date, there are no other reported markers with a public dataset available for EOC performed on the platform used in our study. Therefore, apart from indicating the novelty of the current findings, it will be interesting to perform further benchmark comparisons with future findings. In summary, both the predictive model we developed (the 21-DMP-based centroid model) and the strategy to develop it have the potential to impact future diagnostic applications.

Limitations

One significant limitation in implementing our findings is the platform itself. Methylation arrays are time-consuming and technically demanding. Therefore, a possible solution would be to develop a sequencing panel for diagnostic purposes that includes only the CpG sites of interest, enabling a faster, streamlined procedure.

Another consideration is the applicability of our signature. It was built using OC tissue collected during surgical intervention, which does not facilitate early cancer diagnosis. Therefore, our next step will be to validate whether the same signature can be applied to other types of samples, such as blood or cervical swabs. We must also consider a scenario where the developed signature cannot be applied to other diagnostic materials. In that case, we intend to use the robust statistical procedure described in this study to identify and benchmark DMPs in other types of diagnostic materials.

Conclusion

This study serves as a cornerstone for future validation studies, as it presents both the signature differentiating benign ovarian samples from OC samples and a description of the procedure to develop a similar signature in other biological media.
