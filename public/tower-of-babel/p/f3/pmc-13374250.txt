Available data sources to estimate adolescent birthrates in Mexico: a users’ guide
Authors: Blair G Darney, Evelyn Fuentes-Rivera, Raffaela Schiavon, Gabriela Mejia-Pailles, Fernando Riosmena
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundResearch, policymakers, and advocacy groups rely on valid estimates of adolescent births to inform their work. Estimates of births among very young adolescents (10–14 years old) are especially lacking.MethodsWe compared adolescent fertility rates in Mexico across the census, population-based surveys, civil registration, and birth certificates. We calculate live birth rates among very young adolescents (VYA; 10–14 and 12–14) and older adolescents (15–19) in Mexico, at national and state levels, and by year (2008–2019). We compare estimates with official reconciled data from the Mexican government. For context, we also calculate all other age-specific fertility rates (ASFRs) and total fertility rates (TFRs).FindingsOverall, all national-level data sources in the period studied show declines in TFR and ASFRs for adolescents. Official government estimates for older adolescents (15–19) declined from 78·3 to 65·1 births per 1,000 adolescents 15–19 years old between 2008 and 2019. Rates for VYAs showed a decline in hospital registration (from 3·5 to 2·8 births per 1,000 adolescents aged 12 to 14 years old), and vital statistics (from 2·6 to 1·9) compared with the census. At the state level, VYA birth rates calculated using SINAC are higher than those estimated with vital statistics; the census estimates, at only three timepoints, are variable by state. InterpretationFour different publicly available data sources in Mexico produce adolescent birthrate estimates that are largely consistent with official reconciled data, validating the use of each of these data sources individually. For VYA estimates, which the government does not include in population projections, hospital registration (birth certificates) may be the best source.Keywords: Fertility rate, Adolescent, Mexico, Data sources

Introduction

Adolescent fertility is recognized as an indicator of social vulnerability, and is linked to poor health, educational, and economic outcomes that can span generations [1]. Research, policies, programs, and advocacy groups from a variety of disciplines and sectors (e.g. health, education, economic) rely on valid estimates of adolescent births to inform their work. Globally, data on adolescent fertility comes from vital statistics of birth certificates or other civil registration systems, or from national surveys [2, 3]. The majority of data focuses on adolescents 15–19 years old and more limited data exists on very young adolescents (VYA), 10–14 years old [4], despite the fact that they are more vulnerable; VYAs have worse outcomes [5, 6] and experience high rates of sexual violence both compared with older adolescents and with women over 20 [7, 8].

Mexico is an important case study for adolescent fertility measurement given recent trends. Increases in the 15–19 adolescent birthrate revealed by the 2014 National Survey of Population Dynamics [9] (known as ENADID, its Spanish-language acronym) prompted a renewed focus on adolescent fertility in Mexico, via the National Adolescent Pregnancy Prevention Strategy (known as ENAPEA, its Spanish-language acronym) [10]. ENAPEA has two goals, 1) reduce adolescent fertility by half (among 15–19) and 2) eradicate births among very young adolescents (VYA), 10–14 years-old [10]. Official statistics from CONAPO show sharp declines in adolescent fertility since 2014, with births per 1,000 15–19 year-olds declining from 70·6 in 2016 to 45·2 in 2021 [11, 12], a 36% reduction that brings Mexico closer to the ENAPEA’s 50% reduction goal. However, as overall fertility falls in Mexico the proportion of all births to Mexican women under 20 years old has remained stable since 2009 at just under 20% [5, 13]. More importantly, the estimation of fertility rates for VYAs is challenging. Indeed, VYAs are not included in official government population projections, making assessment of the second ENAPEA goal difficult.

Mexico is also a relevant case study because of the availability of various data sources to estimate fertility, including those that do allow for the estimation of rates for VYAs. The country has relatively robust vital statistics civil registration (VSCR) and, especially, health and survey data systems. Indeed, birth data can come from four major sources: (1) the civil registration system (feeding into vital statistics system); (2) a national hospital-based birth certificate system (SINAC, its Spanish-language acronym); (3) decennial censuses and related decennial/intercensal enumeration surveys; and (4) demographic surveys, particularly the ENADID, which was designed specifically to produce Total Fertility Rate estimates at the national and state level. The national population council, known as CONAPO, its Spanish-language acronym, is a government agency that produces official estimates and projections of total and age-specific fertility rates based on these data sources [14]. However, as in the international arena, the majority of data are focused on 15–19-year-olds, with more limited data available for 10–14-year-olds (VYA).

Furthermore, each data source has its limitations. The census may have errors in the report of births as the main informant can be any person in the household, and its infrequent collection schedule (i.e., every 5 years) also makes it difficult to track trends. The ENADID is fielded every four to six years and permits only estimates averaged for the three-year period before the year of the survey, only covers women ages 15–54 (excluding VYAs), and may not produce robust estimates of age stratified fertility rates (ASFR) at the state level. The civil registration data require a long lag to fully capture all births given that many children (e.g., in remote rural areas with more difficult access to a civil registration office) may not be registered until they are required to present a birth certificate to enter school, often at age six. Finally, the SINAC data are publicly available, contain rich sociodemographic and clinical data on all births that occurred in a health facility, and are produced in real time. Given that nearly 95% of births occur in a health facility in Mexico [15], these features make SINAC a key data source to estimate fertility, including that of older adolescents and VYAs. However, there has been concern that births are underreported in SINAC, and that it therefore should not be used to calculate birthrates [16].

Ultimately, these are empirical questions. An empirical comparison of estimates across these sources could improve our understanding of the strengths, limitations, and potential biases of each data source, assist on the interpretation of adolescent fertility data in Mexico and help researchers, policymakers, and program implementers better measure changes over time and across geographic areas. The objective of this study is to calculate and compare live birth rates among very young adolescents (VYA) and (older) adolescents with four different data sources at the national and state levels and by year (2008–2019) in Mexico. We compare these four data sources with the official reconciled data for older adolescents produced by the government population agency, CONAPO (Consejo Nacional de Población), in order to provide context for end-users of these data who use or must interpret the individual data sources.

Methods

Data sources

In this descriptive study, we used four different data sources (Table 1) that are publicly available, have different strengths and limitations, and can be used to estimate fertility levels at national and sometimes state levels. For this analysis, we used SINAC from 2008 to 2019; although SINAC began in 2007, 2008 is the first year with complete data from all geographic regions in Mexico. We end our study period in 2019 to have several years for our reconstruction methods with vital statistics (see below) and to avoid the pandemic, which caused disruptions to birth registration. We also use ENADID 2009, 2014 and 2018 and vital statistics data from 2008–2023 to estimate fertility rates for the period 2008–2019 (see Methods section below for details). We also used the 2010 and 2020 census, and the Intercensal Survey of 2015 for this analysis. Finally, we used CONAPO reconciliated estimates from 2008 to 2019 to compare with estimates from the individual data sources.

Births and fertility rates

We derived births by maternal age at national and state levels from all sources. We utilized these data to estimate age-specific fertility rates (ASFRs) for eight age groups (10–14, 15–19, 20–24, 25–29, 30–34, 35–39, 40–44 and 45–49) from 2008 to 2019. From these national ASFRs, we then computed the total fertility rate (TFR) for each period and data source. Fertility estimates for VYAs are challenging; first, births are rarer among VYAs than among adolescents 15–19 years old, so cell sizes, especially across geography or other factors can be small, and the calculation of the rate is challenging. The numerator should logically be births to VYAs 10–14 years old, but the denominator poses problems. Age at menarche is not routinely reported and thus risk of pregnancy is unknown; a denominator of per 1,000 VYAs 10–14 years old will include some VYAs who are not yet capable of pregnancy (have not reached menarche) [23] and thus not at risk for the numerator. For our calculations, we used two approaches: the ratio of births to VYAs 10–14/1,0000 VYAs 10–14 and rate of births to VYAs 12–14/1,000 VYAs 12–14. We present both: the 12–14 rate is our main analysis because menarche usually does not occur until age 12, and because our previous work has shown that 95% of births in the VYA age group are to 12–14 years old [13]; we show the 10–14 ratios in the Appendix.

Data sources

For SINAC, we extracted births (numerator) and organized it by maternal age group. We used mid-year female population data for the denominators, as published by CONAPO [22]. For the ENADID analysis, we utilized data from the 2009, 2014 and 2018 survey waves. Initially, we identified births (numerator) occurring within the three-year timeframe preceding each survey, drawing from the comprehensive births data available in the mother and child health module. Then we identified women falling within each age bracket during the same three-year period (denominator), using the women’s module. Finally, we computed rates for the respective three-year periods.

A similar process was performed for the census and intercensal analyses: this time, births that occurred in the previous year and women in each age bracket during the previous year were identified. After the identification of numerators (births) and denominators (women) from the same data and period, we estimated fertility rates for the previous year of each census and the intercensal survey.

For vital statistics, our calculations were different, to account for the lag in registration of births; many births may be registered only when the child enters pre-school or primary school. Calculations incorporating reconstruction methods to account for a lag is sometimes made using 4 years (entrance to kindergarten) or 7 years (entrance to elementary school after a child turns 6 years-old). We used a 7-year reconstruction; however, vital statistics data is only available up to 2023 at the time of analysis and we therefore have a gap in data (our data only permit a seven-year reconstruction from 2016 and before). To complete the 7-year reconstruction for last years (2017–2019), we performed an imputation using the means of proportions of births registered in the previous years (2008–2016). As a check on this estimation method, we performed two additional sensitivity analyses using other methods to impute and complete the 7-year reconstruction. First, we performed the imputation with a method that utilizes ENADID to fill in the gaps [14]; we also applied a regression-based method used in previous literature [24] (see Appendix 1 for details on these methods).

For the comparison with CONAPO official reconciliated data, we extracted the original data from CONAPO’s web page [22]. Within these data, ASFRs (with the exception of the 10–14 and 12–14 years old age group) are already estimated at national and state level for every year since 1950 up to 2070. CONAPO’s projections are the construction and evaluation of future scenarios of demographic trends if a series of specific conditions were to occur [22]. For the period from 1950 up to 2019 the estimations are already reconciled, which means that previous projections were contrasted with retrospective data of occurred events and retrospective corrections to previous projections weremade when needed to match observed reality. This CONAPO reconciliation process uses surveys, census, SINAC, and vital statistics as we do here; however, the reconciliation process also includes the previously produced projections. Documentation of the reconciliation is publicly available, but quite technical and the methodological details of final smoothing are not easily and completely described in order to permit replication or a full understanding of the behavior of resulting estimates [22]. In this article, we share our detailed methodology, identify each individual data sources strengths and limitations, and compare them with CONAPO projections.

Analysis

Once we calculated ASFRs for all age groups and years at the national and state levels, we first computed the total fertility rate (TFR) for each period and data source and created a graphic to compare differences visually. We next graphed ASFRs separately for all age groups included in the study (12–14, 15–19, 20–24, 25–29, 30–34, 35–39, 40–44 and 45–49) and for each data source at national level. As we stated, the focus in this analysis is on providing detailed information on adolescent births; we therefore estimated ASFRs for the two adolescent subgroups (VYA/10–14 and 15–19) at the state level. All data used in this analysis are publicly available (see Table 1).

For the VYA rates, we rely on only three data sources—SINAC, vital statistics, and the census; we are unable to calculate ASFR for VYAs using ENADID and CONAPO does not publish official reconciled estimates for the VYA group within the projections project (Table 1). Again, all available sources show a decline; but in this age group, SINAC shows a meaningfully higher birth rate across the study period, compared with vital statistics (Fig. 2; see Appendix Figure 2 for 10–14 estimates). The rate calculated using SINAC declines from 3·5 in 2008 to 2·8 births per 1,000 adolescents aged 12 to 14 years old in 2019, while vital statistics declines from 2·6 to 1·9 children per 1,000 adolescents. This behavior may result from a significant under-registration of births in the vital registries specifically for this maternal age group. Discussion

We show that four different publicly available data sources in Mexico produce adolescent (15–19) birthrate estimates that are largely consistent with official government statistics, validating the use of each of these data sources individually, depending on question and needs, and with attention to the strengths and limitations of each. Despite some differences in levels, estimates from all sources also show similar declines in TFRs and in ASFRs at national and state levels for the studied period. However, there are important differences that researchers, policymakers, and program implementers should consider when using or interpreting birthrate data from these different sources.

First, when interpreting estimates from diverse data sources, it is essential to understand data provenance. ENADID and the Census are population surveys (ENADID a sample, the Census a population census), SINAC is registry at birth in a heath facility, and vital statistics are registry at a civil registration office. Overall, official CONAPO projections tend to be higher for all age groups at the national level, compared to the four data sources we assess (ENADID, SINAC, vital statistics, Census), and despite the fact that CONAPO uses these same data sources to generate their official projections and reconciled estimates. We are unable to assess the underlying cause of this discrepancy. One hypothesis is the COVID19 pandemic, which disrupted the 2020 Census and thus potentially the retrospective adjustments made by CONAPO, particularly for the last years of the studied period. CONAPO projections still remain the gold standard for fertility rates in Mexico in the sense that they are used to develop public policy and budgeting. However, it is important to note that CONAPO does not produce estimates for VYAs as part of the projection and reconciliation process which produce the official demographic estimates in Mexico. This could hinder research, policy evaluation, and evidence-based advocacy for this important and vulnerable age group.

For VYA estimates specifically, SINAC, the birth certificate system implemented in 2008 that registers births within health facilities, may be the best data source. Relying on vital statistics would risk a gross underestimation of birthrates in this age group. The gap between vital statistics and SINAC for VYA is most likely mainly explained by an under-registration of births to VYAs in civil registration procedures. It is unlikely that SINAC misestimated the age of mothers and thus overestimates VYA rates, although we cannot empirically test this. We speculate that, to avoid the stigma associated with giving birth as a very early age, relatives of VYAs (most likely the parents) are registering the child to their name, [25] also providing other forms of economic and social support. It is perhaps much less likely that relatives are able to do this when hospital births are registered.

Despite the clear advantages of using hospital registration to estimate adolescent rates, note that SINAC does likely undercount births, particularly in places where access to medical facilities is more difficult. For example, in Chiapas, the gap in rates for older teens between SINAC and other sources is large, suggesting under-registration in SINAC, which may be explained by higher rates of out of facility births in Chiapas compared with other Mexican states [26–28]. In addition, to Chiapas, the states of Guerrero and Oaxaca have traditionally also had larger under-registration in SINAC; these are also the states with the greatest levels of marginalization in Mexico.

Despite some strengths of SINAC data, the selection of which data source to use to estimate fertility rates, and the interpretation of those rates, also depends on the study goal. As we discussed, official estimates from CONAPO do not include VYAs; additionally, they do not contain individual-level sociodemographic information. Individual data sources may thus be desirable for multilevel or more complex analyses. For example, while SINAC may slightly underestimate birth rates at the national level, it is the best source for the VYA (10–14 or 12–14 years old) group. Additionally, it provides rich sociodemographic and clinical data and is the only data source available almost in real time. ENADID and the census also provide rich individual and household-level information but only produce three-years averages or estimates every five years, respectively, which may not be helpful when year-to-year birth-rate comparisons are needed to evaluate programs or policies, such as ENAPEA, for example. Rates derived from vital statistics also require that delays in registration must be accounted for, which may make them less attractive for some data users or make them less useful for current policy evaluation.

Previous researchers have compared some of the data sources we present here at the national level [29] and conclude that vital statistics are the best data source to estimate adolescent fertility. This is perhaps due to the fact that the study pertained to a period in which the coverage of SINAC data was relatively poor. Our updated analyses, which include all age groups and state-level figures, show that SINAC better captures births to VYAs in particular.

Our analysis has limitations beyond those inherent in the data, such as the under-reporting of SINAC births in the earlier years of our study period. We do not include the COVID-19 pandemic years, when each of these information sources may have suffered from different levels of incompleteness. However, we acknowledge that all data sources could be affected by the pandemic disruption, in particular civil and, perhaps, hospital registration. All population estimates used as denominators are taken from CONAPO projections, which used the 2020 census, notoriously affected by the pandemic. Also, for vital statistics reconstruction of births, we use birth information up to 2023, which again included the pandemic period. We are not able to fully explain the reasons behind observed discrepancies between data sources; information and interpretation about under- or over-reporting in each data source is largely anecdotal, and there is no “absolute truth” – which is why CONAPO produces the reconciled estimates.

Our analysis can help end users of fertility data in Mexico understand the strengths and limitations of each available data source. Improvements in data collection and availability can also be advocated for, for example, the need for including sufficient samples by age groups – especially VYAs—and smaller geographic regions, and the importance of guaranteeing universal access to birth registry within the health system, independently of the birth attendant and of the place of birth. Reporting adolescent fertility rates, with a focus on VYAs, is a policy priority for Mexico and many countries in Latin America and globally. A better understanding of the individual data sources available for such estimates provides useful context to researchers, policymakers and advocates.
