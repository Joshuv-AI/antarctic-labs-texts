What Is Going on? The Purpose, Organisation and Supervision of Australian First‐Year Nursing Students on Clinical Placements in Nursing Homes
Authors: Deirdre Fetherstonhaugh, Jo‐Anne Rayner, Anne‐Marie Mahoney, Samantha Clune
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimExploration of factors associated with clinical placements in nursing homes for first‐year nursing students which may impact their career choices.DesignThis paper reports on Stage I of a larger study which comprised a 2‐stage sequential mixed‐methods design.MethodsStage I was a national survey conducted as a telephone interview with nurse academics involved in arranging clinical placements for first‐year student nurses; and Stage II which included: interviews with student nurses before and after their first clinical placement in a nursing home, and interviews with clinical supervisors. The Stage I national survey was conducted as a telephone interview and was audio recorded. Transcripts of the interviews were analysed using descriptive statistics, content analysis, and reflexive thematic analysis.ResultsAll Australian nursing schools that offered a Bachelor of Nursing in 2023 were invited to participate. Twenty‐nine (81%) participated. The first clinical placement for most (79%) Australian student nurses is in a nursing home. Four themes were identified from the data analysis: (1) clinical placements are increasingly organised by centralised teams (48%); (2) the supervision model and the clinical supervisor are determined by placement agreements where students can be supervised by someone ill equipped for the role; (3) the purpose of this first‐year clinical placement is difficult to articulate, other than to practise fundamental skills; and (4) nursing homes can provide positive learning experiences for student nurses, if they are prepared for the setting and supervised appropriately.ConclusionWhile many of these findings have been documented for decades, clinical placements in nursing homes may provide student nurses with valuable experiences. Schools of nursing need to better prepare students for clinical placements in nursing homes (especially if it is their first clinical placement) and ensure they are appropriately supervised. If not, graduate nurses will not consider aged care nursing as a career option.Implications for PracticeThese findings should encourage nursing schools to reconsider their curriculum design, especially the timing of clinical placements in nursing homes, and their employment of clinical supervisors. The Australian Nursing and Midwifery Accreditation Council (ANMAC) should mandate a care of older persons subject in all undergraduate nursing curricula to be undertaken before students undertake a clinical placement in a nursing home.Public ContributionThere was no public contribution to this research. All participants were nurse academics from participating Australian schools of nursing.Keywords: clinical placements, clinical supervision, nurse education, nursing homes, nursing students

Introduction

Australia's rapidly ageing population presents a challenge for the aged care workforce in relation to supply and demand (Australian Government, Department of Treasury 2023). Older people use healthcare services more often than other age cohorts (Australian Institute of Health and Welfare [AIHW] 2024a) and require more care because of disabilities associated with complex chronic illnesses (AIHW 2024b). Demand for aged care services is rapidly increasing across all sectors including nursing home care (Residential aged care is referred to as nursing home care in this manuscript but is also called long‐term care elsewhere.) (AIHW 2025) at a faster rate than the workforce is growing (Parliament of Australia, Department of Parliamentary Services 2026).

In response to findings from the Australian Royal Commission into Aged Care Quality and Safety (the Commission) (Pagone and Briggs 2021), nursing homes are mandated to have a registered nurse (RN) on site 24 h, 7 days a week, providing a minimum of 40 min of care daily to each resident (Australian Government, Department of Health and Aged Care 2021) (Nursing homes in small rural towns and remote communities with fewer than 31 places may apply for an exemption from the responsibility until 1 July 2024 if they have appropriate alternative clinical care arrangements in place). Therefore, it is essential that graduating RNs have gerontological knowledge and skills, and the capabilities and confidence to care for older people in nursing homes, especially as there is no medical leadership (Pagone and Briggs 2021; Turnock et al. 2024). Recent Australian research has identified that the implementation of core gerontological nursing competencies including dementia care, communication and palliative care, that are essential for safe, high‐quality practice (Turnock et al. 2024), not only improves the capability and confidence of RNs to work in nursing homes but also increases workforce retention (Traynor et al. 2025).

However, evidence shows that undergraduate nursing programs have limited gerontological nursing content which is inconsistently integrated across curricula, and curricula remain predominantly acute care focused (Fetherstonhaugh et al. 2022). Consequently, many graduate nurses report they feel underprepared to manage the clinical and relational demands of caring for older people (Piper et al. 2024), highlighting a gap between educational preparation and workforce expectations (Traynor et al. 2024; Sun et al. 2023). In addition, student nurses (SNs) are often first exposed to care of older people in nursing home clinical placements (Clinical placements are also referred to as work‐integrated learning (WIL), work‐based learning, professional experience placement (PEP), professional placement, professional experience, work placement, clinical experience, clinical attachments, practice placements, clinical internship, clinical rotation, clinical observation or experiential learning.), which frequently occur in the first year of their undergraduate degree, and are not reinforced in later clinical placement learning experiences. This restricts the development of confidence and competence in working with older people and SNs often conflate the care of older people to nursing home care (Fetherstonhaugh et al. 2022; Rayner et al. 2022; Alsaeed et al. 2025), despite statistics that indicate older people utilise more health and care services across the health sector (AIHW 2024a, 2024b).

The first clinical placement induces feelings of anxiety, as SNs have never worked with ‘real’ people, and they are worried about making mistakes and not fitting in (Keeping‐Burke et al. 2020). SNs also face challenges related to their own and others' attitudes and perceptions of ageing and aged care (Rayner et al. 2023), where gerontological nursing is often undervalued compared to acute care, contributing to low interest in gerontological nursing careers and reduced engagement with learning in this area (Holt et al. 2023). This is compounded by SNs' early clinical placement in a nursing home, a complex setting due to the number of older people with dementia, which can be challenging without sufficient educational preparation and placement support. SNs undertaking their first clinical placement in a nursing homes report feeling unprepared, unsupported and bullied, and witness poor care (Levett‐Jones et al. 2015; Budden et al. 2017).

SNs consistently demonstrate low interest in pursuing careers in aged care, with a strong preference for hospital‐based roles (Anyango et al. 2024). Australian evidence shows that few SNs actively choose careers focused on older people, despite growing workforce demand (Rayner et al. 2023), and systematic review findings that indicate working with older adults is often perceived as unattractive (Hebditch et al. 2020). The research suggests career decisions are highly dependent on experience, with clinical placements playing a central role. Supportive supervision, quality learning environments and positive mentorship increase the likelihood of considering aged care careers, whereas poor placements reinforce reluctance (Anyango et al. 2024). Initial perceptions of aged care careers are often shaped by low status, limited career progression and negative stereotypes, reinforced by acute‐focused curricula and limited exposure to the complexity of gerontological nursing (Rayner et al. 2023; Holt et al. 2023).

This manuscript reports findings from Stage I of a larger sequential mixed‐methods study that explored factors associated with first‐year clinical placements in nursing homes which may impact career choices. Stage I was a survey exploring nurse academic perspectives on the purpose, organisation and supervision of SNs on clinical placements in nursing homes. The impact of early clinical placements in nursing homes on SNs' career choice requires further exploration considering the continuing challenge of RN recruitment and retention in the Australian nursing home workforce (Australian Government, Department of Health and Aged Care 2022).

Methods

Design

The larger study incorporated a two‐stage, sequential mixed‐methods design. Stage I (reported here) was a national survey of nurse academics; and Stage II included: two interviews with SNs, one interview before and then another interview after their first clinical placement, and single interviews with clinical supervisors.

The survey (Stage I) was conducted as a telephone interview and collected data on: the organisation of SNs' clinical placements in nursing homes, including: the year level, semester, and length (weeks); the purpose and intended learning outcomes of the clinical placement; the model(s) of supervision and selection of clinical supervisors for clinical placement.

In line with the study's multistage sequential mixed‐methods design, in which the survey and interviews with different participant groups addressed distinct research questions, findings from each component have been reported in separate, transparently cross‐referenced manuscripts to ensure analytic coherence (Mahoney et al. 2026a, 2026b; Rayner et al. 2026a, 2026b, 2026c).

Participants and Recruitment

The Heads of School at all 36 Australian nursing schools that offered a Bachelor of Nursing in 2023 were invited to participate in the research and asked to nominate a nurse academic involved in the design or scheduling of clinical placements who the research team could approach. The research team emailed the nominated academics inviting them to participate in the survey and followed them up with a telephone call.

Data Collection

The national survey was conducted as a telephone interview. The research team had used this method successfully in previous nursing curriculum research (Fetherstonhaugh et al. 2022; Rayner et al. 2022, 2023); it enables systematic data collection while simultaneously allowing probing and exploration in predetermined areas (Patton 2002).

Nurse academics who agreed to participate nominated the telephone interview dates and times. The researchers audio‐recorded all telephone interviews, and an independent transcription service transcribed the interviews. Transcripts were cross‐checked against the audio recordings for accuracy, and researchers deleted all potentially identifying details of individuals, nursing schools, or organisations from the transcripts to maintain confidentiality and anonymity.

Data Analysis

Pre‐coded survey responses were analysed using descriptive statistics, including frequencies, proportions and medians. Inductive content analysis was initially used to analyse the open‐ended responses. This is a systematic method for examining open‐ended responses by identifying patterns in participants' words and meanings. It is a particularly useful method for analysing written materials as it reduces data into manageable representations, bridges the gap between descriptive accounts by drawing attention to the context of the research, and allows researchers to make valid and replicable inferences (Krippendorff 2013).

Data analysis proceeded with codes identified in segments of the open‐ended responses to each question. Two researchers (J.‐A.R. and D.F.) independently coded the data, which were then grouped through consensus into the broader categories. These categories were then developed into overarching themes that reflected ideas relevant to the research questions, through consensus, using Braun and Clarke's (2022) reflexive thematic analysis approach. This method of analysis identifies, analyses, organises, describes and reports themes found within data and produces trustworthy and insightful findings, and is a useful method for examining the perspectives of different research participants.

Ongoing checks for coding consistency were undertaken throughout the analysis; coding reliability was ensured using Braun and Clarke's (2022) systematic approach, and researcher consensus enhanced the credibility and trustworthiness of the findings. Methodological rigour was maintained by using the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Data (Tong et al. 2007); describing the study aims and design, sampling and recruitment of participants, and data collection and analysis; independent analysis before consensus on coding; and discussion and refinement of overarching themes (Nowell et al. 2017).

Ethical Considerations

La Trobe University Human Research Ethics Committee (HEC23110, 15/5/2023) gave ethical approval to conduct the research and participants provided written informed consent before undertaking the telephone interview.

Results

Nurse academics from 29 Australian nursing schools participated in the study, a response rate of 81%. Two‐thirds of these academics were involved in designing or co‐ordinating clinical placements, reflecting the gendered nature of nursing; 90% were female.

Four themes were identified from the data analysis: (1) the difficulties associated with organisation and scheduling of the clinical placements in nursing homes; (2) the complexities of student nurse supervision in nursing homes; (3) struggling to articulate the purpose of clinical placements in nursing homes; and (4) nursing homes can provide positive learning experiences. Each is described below with subthemes highlighted and direct quotes illustrative of the findings were identified by participant unique identifier (ID).

The Difficulties Associated With the Organisation and Scheduling of the Clinical Placements in Nursing Homes

Centralising Clinical Placements

There was a trend towards clinical placements being organised outside the school of nursing. Thirteen (45%) nurse academics reported that clinical placements were organised centrally, by a college, faculty or university placement team. Five academics reported the school of nursing had some involvement in organising clinical placement; however, the school was not routinely consulted about the suitability of placement sites, and two were unsure who organised the clinical placements in their school:

We have a clinical placements team that's faculty based as opposed to school based, where we have placement officers that focus on nursing placements, but even they still don't necessarily understand the nature of the placements. (ID04)

We moved into a central work integrated learning team. We used to have our own clinical placement team within nursing and I loved it, it ran beautifully. (ID29)

There is a team which is university wide work integrated learning team, they're all professional staff and they organise all our placements. There's quite a bit of consultation and input by the academic team, but it's usually if it's a complaint. (ID21)

Clinical placements organised within the nursing school were perceived by nurse academics to be more likely to meet both the SNs' needs and the Australian Nursing and Midwifery Accreditation Council [ANMAC] (2019) requirements, that is, appropriate supervision by a RN and a positive learning environment:

We have a school clinical placement team that we work extremely closely with We will look at the placements, looking at student requirements for the placement, location that the student lives at in relation to where the placement site is, any sort of challenges the student has had in previous placements. We spend lots of time making sure that the placement is correct for the student. (ID19)

The Uncertainty of Securing Clinical Placements

Nurse academics reported that all placement opportunities were quite difficult to source, largely dependent on the number of sites available annually, and the number of SNs each site could accommodate. As such, the scheduling of nursing home clinical placements was generally limited to first‐year students only. Twenty‐three (79%) nurse academics reported that SNs could have a clinical placement in a nursing home during their undergraduate degree and 16 (55%) reported that this always occurred in the first year. One academic reported that SNs no longer have a nursing home placement due to the difficulty in providing the required RN supervision (After the survey, another school reported that they had suspended clinical placements in nursing homes for the same reason).

First‐year SNs spent an average of 90 h (range 40–160 h) on their first clinical placement. At the time of the survey, SNs from these nursing schools undertook an average of 804 h (range 800–1040 h) of clinical placement during their degree, as 10 (35%) nursing schools had increased clinical placement hours above the mandated 800 h.

The Complexities of Student Nurse Supervision in Nursing Homes

Placement Agreements Dictate the Supervision Model

Most nurse academics reported that the type of clinical supervision model used was governed by placement service agreements with each site, which, as reported above, often did not involve nurse academics. There was a strong sense in the quotes from nurse academics that this was not always a good thing.

Its industry driven. It's driven by the clinical sites more than the university because we're going into their site so we must agree. (ID12)

Our supervision models are what the industry wants. They direct it. (ID02)

The clinical placement agreement with an industry partner is across disciplines. It goes beyond the school, up to the college to be negotiated. (ID16)

It [the supervision model used] comes from industry; they tell us how they're going to do it. So, we [school]don't have much say in it. (ID01)

The most common supervision model used for first‐year clinical placements in a nursing home was the clinical teacher/facilitator model (n = 24, 83%) where an RN supervises and assesses a group of six to eight students but does not engage in clinical work. Second and third year SNs on clinical placement are supervised using the preceptor model, where they are buddied with a RN.

The clinical supervisors of SNs on placement in a nursing home may be casual nurse academics employed by the school of nursing (n = 15, 51%) or an RN employed by the nursing home or an agency RN.

Casual Nurse Academics as Clinical Supervisors

Among the nursing schools participating, there were no mandatory academic requirements, such as gerontological nursing qualifications for the selection and suitability of RNs to work as clinical supervisors in nursing homes. The criteria for selecting clinical supervisors included proof of registration, current clinical practice, 5 years' post‐graduation work experience, and a police check. Two nurse academics reported a mandatory requirement of work experience in a nursing home, but generally RNs were employed as clinical supervisors if they were able to supervise in multiple clinical settings across all year levels.

We don't make that specifically the criteria of being a clinical supervisor of students looking after older people. They must be suitable to supervise and facilitate learning for all students in any context. (ID06)

We ask if someone has an interest or background in aged care, but if they don't, they're not excluded. We don't require them to have anything specific. (ID01)

We orient clinical supervisors to our curriculum. We don't use the term scope of practice; we talk about supervision and appropriate supervision. (ID09)

Interestingly, one academic reported that there was a mandatory requirement for employment as a clinical supervisor for Diploma of Nursing students on clinical placement in a nursing home:

For the diploma of nursing students, the clinical supervisor must have a training and assessment certificate. For the SNs, they don't need this qualification. They must be an RN with five years of clinical experience; that's our requirement for the role. (ID36)

Non‐Supernumerary Nursing Home RNs as Clinical Supervisors

The supervision role in nursing homes can be allocated to an RN, who may or may not be supernumerary, or the nursing homes may have their own clinical educator/supervisor. However, understanding the educational suitability of this person to supervise SNs is often fraught, and due to the service agreements with clinical sites, many nurse academics reported that economics drives the decisions about SN supervision:

From an education perspective they should have some level of qualification. But in nursing homes, I would say 99.9% of the time, they don't have that. Now, I'm not talking about the nurse unit manager, because that's not their responsibility. I'm talking about whoever oversees education within the organisation. (ID12)

We have a service agreement, and we have less control. What that often means is that the nominated supervisor can change fortnightly, or monthly, and that is problematic because even though we work closely with these environments, they have such a high turnover of staff; they have a high burnout rate. These staff are senior, so they're not only supporting students they're also onboarding staff, so their focus is on orientating new staff to the environment and supporting new staff. We've put some strategies in place to provide education to support them and provide supervisors. However, from an industry perspective, supervision is a remuneration pathway, we are paying this environment to supervise students, so they see it as an income stream. (ID07)

In addition, nurse academics acknowledged the low numbers of RNs working in nursing homes, and their heavy workload, and reported that SNs were in some instances poorly supervised and unable to interact with, or observe, the role of RN in this setting. SNs were most likely buddied with staff such as enrolled nurses (ENs), assistants in nursing (AINs), or personal care workers (PCWs):

There are not enough RNs with the time for education and support. Even if the RN is the dedicated educator, they are constantly being pulled out of their role to do something else. (ID08)

I find there is enthusiasm in the aged care sector to have more students, but they do not have the resources to do it. They must free up staff to act in that supervising role, and they don't have the staff to do this. The students are not having a good time because the staff just can't provide a quality experience. They don't have the capacity to put a supervisor on and therefore we must reduce the number of students going to be able to make the experience better. (ID33)

The students are often without a supervisor as there will only be one RN for 60 residents and all the other staff. (ID10)

Nurse academics reported that the use of clinical placement site clinical educators/supervisors could make it difficult for SNs to report examples of bad practice if they occur:

If they [site] have in‐house supervisor, the feedback from students is that they don't feel that they can raise issues because they're talking to someone who works there. There are power differentials. (ID05)

Agency RNs as Clinical Supervisors of Student Nurses in Nursing Homes

Fourteen (48%) nurse academics reported that agency RNs were used to supervise first‐year students on clinical placement in nursing homes. As nursing schools may not be involved in the clinical placement negotiations, some nurse academics reported that they had little control of the suitability of the clinical supervisor to supervise SNs. However, 11 (79%) of the 14 academics that reported the use of agency RNs to supervise students also reported that the agency nurses were employed by their school of nursing. This high use was reported to be associated with limited resourcing, clinical placement agreements or a lack of suitable clinical supervisors.

We are challenged with getting clinical teachers. We must get approval from our head of school to use agency staff, but we have done it a few times. (ID15)

We have had to use agency staff for first‐year students if the venue doesn't have an educator. (ID30)

Suddenly we're having to contact an agency to say we need some clinical teachers. I can see it becoming more frequent. (ID26)

Despite this, nurse academics expressed unease at the use of agency RNs as clinical supervisors as they may have limited experience in supervision, and little understanding of the curriculum, the placement learning outcomes, or the SN's scope of practice:

I think it's very easy for an agency to do the shift and not support the students at all. (ID34)

Struggling to Articulate the Purpose of a Clinical Placement in a Nursing Home

The purpose of a nursing home clinical placement for first‐year SNs was difficult for many nurse academics to articulate. Most referred to the first clinical placement as the opportunity to practise as a nurse for the first time, or to practise basic or fundamental nursing skills:

For first years, they're trying to learn how to shower someone and communicate and feed someone – do all those sorts of things. (ID35)

It's around the fundamentals of nursing, sort of assessment skills. (ID12)

In first year, there to learn those fundamental skills. It's more about those activities of daily living (ADLs), those sorts of activities. (ID02)

Learning Outcomes for Nursing Home Placements

Only one nurse academic reported that their school included care of older people and healthy ageing in the learning outcomes for the nursing home clinical placement; another reported that the SNs must develop their own learning outcomes; and another reported that learning outcomes were not attached to the nursing home clinical placement. The most frequently reported learning outcomes for SNs attached to the clinical placement in a nursing home were: therapeutic communication and adhering to nursing standards of practice (Nursing and Midwifery Board Australia. Registered Nurse Standards for Practice. 1 June 2016. https://www.nursingmidwiferyboard.gov.au/Codes‐Guidelines‐Statements/Professional‐standards/registered‐nurse‐standards‐for‐practice.aspx) (n = 11, 39% each); demonstrating professional behaviour (n = 10, 36%); critical thinking (n = 8, 29%); reflection/reflective practice and basic or foundational care (n = 8, 29% each); clinical reasoning/decision making (n = 7, 25%); and the ability to complete assessments such as vital signs (n = 6, 21%):

We have three learning outcomes. We focus on demonstrating safe, reflective, evidence‐based and person‐centred nursing care at a beginning level, including assistance with activities of daily living, which is consistent with the NMBA standards for practise. (ID02)

Nursing Homes Can Provide Positive Clinical Learning Experiences

Promoting Aged Care in Nursing Curricula

Despite acknowledging the challenges associated with a first‐clinical placement in nursing homes, especially the lack of RNs, nurse academics reported that SNs can learn from the experience and that many do enjoy the placement. Some nurse academics reported their school was trying to promote aged care placements to dispel many negative beliefs SNs had about the sector:

I guess in that essence what we're trying to do is shift some thinking from students around those negative connotations of aged care nursing. (ID02)

We really try here to promote nursing homes as a good place to work. I think it's a bit of a vicious cycle in the sense that aged care facilities' staffing is not that good and, that really does impact the student's experience of placements. If you're not having a good experience, why would you want to go back there? (ID01)

Part of this promotion of the aged care sector involved some nursing schools actively changing their curricula to include a second nursing home clinical placement in third year for a small number of SNs who express a desire to pursue a career in the sector. The third‐year clinical placement was thought to provide a more meaningful clinical experience about the role of the RN in this setting:

Several students are interested in pursuing a career in gerontology, so in the new curriculum we'll give them the opportunity to do their capstone placement at the end of their course working with a RN in aged care and looking at the management and the opportunities there. (ID06)

We've just started offering it [clinical placement in a nursing home] in our third year as a specialty gerontological placement for students. (ID29)

Sometimes a very small number, a handful of students, get a third‐year aged care leadership placements, which would be a three‐to‐four‐week placement. Say, 10 students or 15 students would do that type of placement in third year. We were just trying to promote the aged care placements, that's why we started the aged care leadership placements in third year. (ID24)

However, some logistical problems with providing third‐year SNs with a nursing home clinical placement was raised:

The current model is limited as to being able to send students of different year levels to their facilities. One thing that we're exploring, is having second or third years on clinical placement in nursing homes. A boutique program where you have the third‐year student buddied up with the nurse in charge. But that's bespoke, only one or two students would be able to go, and you'd have to have so many nursing homes involved. Also, in graduate years the health services may not look favourably on a non‐acute placement in the third year. (ID08)

Industry Is Leading the Way

As part of this promotion of aged care as a potential career, five academics (17%) reported that industry was leading the way in their curricular redesign. To build the nursing home workforce, some nursing homes offered a small number of clinical placements for third‐year SNs which focused on nursing management and leadership, and which had scholarships or free board attached to the placement:

We do quite a few aged care placements in third year, we have two eight‐week clinical placement blocks in third year, and we have 30 or 40 nursing home placements, specific placements. We've also got some aged care venues who are looking at options for pathways which we're starting to investigate now with them. So that may change as well. (ID31)

We've got three third‐year students who are in [town] at a nursing home. They'll be there for eight weeks and that's their final placement. Those students have been supported by a scholarship from the nursing home trying to get people more interested in working in aged care. (ID01)

Discussion

This study found that most Australian SNs undertake their first clinical experience in a nursing home, despite over two decades of evidence showing many report these experiences negatively when they witness poor care practices, are buddied with care workers, and feel unprepared and unsupported (Rayner et al. 2023, 2022; Keeping‐Burke et al. 2020; Levett‐Jones et al. 2015). SNs undertaken their first clinical placement as a full‐time block of 2 or 3 weeks (Nyoni et al. 2021; Cooper et al. 2021), which is based on the traditional apprenticeship learning style, a model that accommodates large numbers of SNs and aligns with the placement sites' capacity (Cooper et al. 2021). However, there is a lack of evidence for the model's efficacy (Jayasekara et al. 2018) and it has been argued that short blocks reduce the SN's ability to establish a sense of belonging and understand the clinical setting, and that staff are less likely to be invested in them (Levett‐Jones et al. 2008). A settling‐in period or orientation to the setting before the clinical placement, has been suggested to help SNs and staff relationships, which is known to be important to student learning (Nyoni et al. 2021; Rowland and Trueman 2024).

Consistent with the literature, nurse academics in this study highlighted the highly competitive nature of securing clinical placement sites (Cooper et al. 2021). The literature consistently identifies clinical placements within Australian nursing education as both a limited (Luders et al. 2021) and highly contested resource (Neville et al. 2009). Scarcity is driven by constrained placement capacity within healthcare settings and increasing demand associated with growing student numbers, alongside decentralised allocation systems (Van de Mortel et al. 2020). As demand now exceeds available supply (Taylor et al. 2017; Rudland, Ash, Barclay, et al. 2025), nursing schools are forced into competitive practices, including targeting the same placement sites and navigating complex procurement processes shaped by relationship management and organisational ‘gatekeeping’ (Taylor et al. 2017, 3099). These pressures are further exacerbated by rising student enrolments linked to anticipated nursing workforce shortages (Australian Government, Department of Health, and Aged Care 2024), which continue to intensify strain on the already limited pool of suitable clinical placement opportunities (Universities Australia 2023).

This study found that the organisation of SNs' clinical placements is often undertaken by staff with limited understanding of ANMAC requirements and curriculum expectations, while placement agreements with clinical providers shape the supervision models employed. Although ANMAC (2025) mandates that SNs be supervised by a RN with the capacity to support learning, these arrangements result in considerable variability, with supervision provided by casual academics, non‐supernumerary RNs, or agency nurses employed by either universities or clinical providers. Such variability reflects broader workforce and resourcing constraints. Australian nursing programs rely heavily on casual staff to deliver clinical education, driven by budget limitations and the need for workforce flexibility (Richardson et al. 2021; Ryan and McAllister 2020; Ryan et al. 2018; Oprescu et al. 2017).

The use of agency RNs further indicates ongoing difficulties in the recruitment and retention of suitably qualified clinical supervisors (Needham et al. 2016). Moreover, selection processes for clinical supervisors appear less rigorous than those for ongoing academic staff, raising concerns about consistency and quality (Richardson et al. 2021). Evidence suggests that supervisors who are educationally underprepared may struggle with the role, particularly in relation to contemporary pedagogy, assessment and curriculum alignment (Needham et al. 2016; Ryan and McAllister 2020; Ryan et al. 2018). Inconsistencies in qualification requirements further compound these issues. For instance, while clinical supervisors of Diploma of Nursing students may be required to hold training and assessment qualifications, equivalent standards are not always applied to those supervising Bachelor of Nursing students. There remains a lack of a comprehensive competency framework to guide clinical supervisors' practice (Cutcliffe et al. 2018), highlighting an ongoing gap in the standardisation of supervision within clinical education. Although recent guidance stipulates that clinical supervisors should undertake professional development in clinical teaching to enhance student learning during placements (Australian Government, Department of Health and Aged Care 2025), notably in this study, nurse academics reported an absence of ongoing professional development opportunities for clinical supervisors employed by nursing schools, suggesting a disconnect between policy expectations and practice. This discrepancy highlights a lack of standardisation in clinical supervision and raises concerns regarding the preparedness of those supporting undergraduate learning in clinical settings.

While national policy (Australian Government, Department of Health and Aged Care 2025) supports expanding clinical placement opportunities in nursing homes, the workforce profile within this sector presents significant challenges. Nursing homes are characterised by relatively low numbers of RNs alongside a growing proportion of less qualified care workers (AIHW 2024c; Australian Government, Department of Health and Aged Care 2024). This imbalance can constrain the capacity for effective clinical supervision, as the limited RN workforce must juggle supervisory responsibilities with their existing clinical, administrative, and leadership duties (Anderson et al. 2020; McKenna et al. 2010). Supporting SNs in this setting therefore requires RNs to integrate students into often complex and resource‐constrained environments, supervise and assess their performance, and simultaneously manage junior staff and resident care demands (Courtney‐Pratt et al. 2015). The effectiveness of supervision is further influenced by factors such as limited organisational support at placement sites and variability in RNs' preparedness for the supervisory role. Evidence suggests that targeted strategies including ongoing education, structured support and adjustments to workload can enhance RNs' capacity to supervise students and strengthen their supervisory competence (Jayasekara et al. 2018; Tuomikoski et al. 2020). These challenges are compounded by increasing reliance on agency RNs to meet mandated staffing requirements (AIHW 2024c), which may further disrupt continuity of supervision and the overall quality of student learning experiences in nursing home settings.

Australian Health Practitioner Regulation Agency Board, Accreditation Committee (2025) highlights the need for regular review and evaluation of clinical placement programs to ensure they remain current and support high‐quality student learning. Within this context, nursing education requires more comprehensive and innovative approaches to enhance SNs' understanding of caring for older people, including reconsideration of early placement models and diversification of placement settings. Persistent disinterest among SNs in working in nursing homes, documented both nationally and internationally (Neville et al. 2014; Rababa et al. 2020), continues to pose a significant challenge. International evidence further suggests that the timing and structure of early clinical experiences play a critical role in shaping students' perceptions of aged care and their ability to provide care to older people. In particular, allocating first clinical placements to nursing homes when students have limited understanding of the complexities of older people's care may inadvertently contribute to reduced interest in pursuing careers in this sector (McCloskey et al. 2022).

To address these challenges, there is increasing emphasis on the development of well‐designed practice academic partnerships between nursing schools and nursing homes. Such partnerships should be grounded in shared goals and mutual expectations, fostering supportive and high‐quality learning environments that enhance SN engagement with aged care practice. Evidence also highlights that strong partnerships between education providers and nursing homes can improve placement experiences when supported by structured SN orientation, effective clinical supervision and targeted support for nursing home staff (Splitgerber et al. 2021). These elements can strengthen students' understanding of the RN role in aged care and potentially may influence future career intentions. Further innovations, including longitudinal clinical placement models, offer an alternative to traditional block placements. Widely used in medical education (Goers et al. 2022), these models promote continuity of supervision, the development of sustained professional relationships and a deeper understanding of systems of care over time.

Within the Australian context, recent collaborative models that incorporate supported third‐year placements in nursing homes have demonstrated that, with adequate resourcing and support, these environments can provide valuable and positive learning experiences for SNs which may increase career interest. The earlier Teaching Research Aged Care Services program (Loffler et al. 2018) was based on the ‘teaching nursing home’ concept, positioning nursing homes as integrated sites for education, research and clinical practice, like teaching hospitals. It emphasised organisational transformation, multidisciplinary learning and embedding students within a culture of ongoing staff–student collaboration and is widely seen as a precursor to more recent supported placement programs. The gerontology clinical learning experience model, a collaboration between one school of nursing and nursing homes, involved a structured clinical learning experience for third‐year SNs, supported by both university academics and registered nurses within the facilities. Students reported positive learning experiences, and the model was well regarded by nursing home staff (Ryan et al. 2018). The most recent Clinical Placements with Older People initiative represents two key stages in the evolution of supported clinical education models in Australian nursing homes. This is a nationally funded program designed to scale high‐quality clinical placements by providing structured support to third‐year SNs. It focuses on improving learning experiences through dedicated clinical facilitators, mentorship by gerontological nurses, and targeted engagement with the RN role in aged care. It represents a pragmatic, workforce‐oriented adaptation that prioritises consistent supervision and student support within existing service structures and is a promising strategic intervention at the career‐decision point in undergraduate nurse education (University of Canberra, Faculty of Health 2024).

More research is needed to see if these alternative clinical placement models increase SNs' willingness to consider a career in aged care. While third‐year placements can improve attitudes and appreciation of aged care nursing (Narayan 2025), these changes do not consistently translate into career choice, as interventions tend to influence attitudes more than actual workforce intentions (Dahlke et al. 2024; Tao et al. 2024).

Limitations

The generalisability of the findings is limited by two types of bias. Sampling bias, as participating nurse academics may have different knowledge and experiences of organising clinical placements in Australian undergraduate nursing programs than nurse academics who did not participate; and recall bias, as some academics were unsure or could not answer certain questions, and self‐report measures were used in the survey. However, the research design, including the selection of respondents, the high response rate (81%), the study design (sequential mixed methods) and the data analysis, contributed to the reliability of the findings. Data analysis was conducted rigorously, with findings confirmed through consensus. Combining two types of qualitative data analysis with survey responses provides a richer understanding of research findings. These strategies increase the likelihood that the findings accurately represent the information that the respondents provided.

Conclusion

As Australia's population continues to age, it is increasingly important that RNs, regardless of their eventual practice setting, are equipped with the knowledge and skills required to deliver high‐quality care to older people. However, many SNs undertake their first clinical placement in nursing homes where the purpose of the placement is often unclear, and learning outcomes are not well aligned with the specific context of aged care. These placements are frequently organised with limited input from nurse academics, and students may receive insufficient supervision.

There is an urgent need to establish clear criteria to evaluate the suitability of nursing homes as placement settings for first‐year SNs, including their capacity to provide positive learning experiences and the preparedness of clinical supervisors to support student learning. Addressing these issues is critical to ensuring that graduating RNs develop the appropriate attitudes, knowledge, and critical thinking skills necessary to deliver evidence‐based care in nursing homes and other settings that support older people.

Recommendations

Competency of Clinical Supervisors: RNs who supervise SNs in nursing homes possess appropriate levels of competence for the role, including evidence of recent clinical experience in gerontological nursing and/or relevant postgraduate qualifications. It would be in the interests of Schools of Nursing to provide ongoing professional development to support and maintain supervisory capability, to ensure that supervisors are well equipped to facilitate high‐quality student learning.
