Plasma p‐tau markers, vascular factors, and cognitive decline in the CIMA‐Q cohort
Authors: Rosalie J. Cottez, Clémence Peyrot, Hélèna L. Denis, Cyntia Tremblay, Andréanne Loiselle, Caroline Dallaire‐Théroux, Ali Filali‐Mouhim, Sébastien S. Hébert, Nicole Leclerc, Kaj Blennow, Henrik Zetterberg, Consortium for the Early Identification of Alzheimer's Disease – Quebec (CIMA‐Q), Marie‐Jeanne Kergoat, Frédéric Calon, Alexa Pichet Binette
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONWe evaluated associations between phosphorylated au 217 (p‐tau217), p‐tau181, p‐tau231, and vascular risk factors with cognitive outcomes across the Alzheimer's disease (AD) continuum.METHODSBaseline plasma p‐tau concentrations and vascular risk factors were assessed in 277 Consortium for the Early Identification of Alzheimer's Disease–Quebec (CIMA‐Q) participants. Associations between these markers, cognition, and clinical progression over on average 3.10 years were examined.RESULTSHigher plasma p‐tau levels were associated with worse cognition at baseline and over time, with the strongest effect observed with p‐tau217 in cognitively impaired individuals (β = ‐0.49, p < 0.001). Hypertension was further linked to steeper memory and executive function decline (β = −0.10, both p = 0.04) in this group, and it amplified the effect of p‐tau217 on cognitive decline across the whole group. Higher p‐tau217 levels were associated with cognitive decline in cognitively unimpaired individuals, and it also predicted progression from mild cognitive impairment (MCI) to AD dementia (hazard ratio [HR] = 1.22, p = 0.016).DISCUSSIONPlasma p‐tau217 was the most sensitive marker of cognitive decline, with hypertension contributing to longitudinal cognitive changes.

BACKGROUND

Alzheimer's disease (AD) is a multifactorial disease in which pathological changes occur over a long period of time until clinical symptoms appear. 1 , 2 The core neuropathological markers that define AD are beta‐amyloid (Aβ) plaques and tau neurofibrillary tangles. 3 AD pathology can be assessed through positron emission tomography (PET) imaging and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis, 4 , 5 but these methods remain costly and are not widely accessible. Since a few years, given the fast development of blood‐based markers, it is now possible to measure AD pathological biomarkers with high accuracy in plasma. 6 Prominent markers include phosphorylated tau (p‐tau) at different epitopes, such as p‐tau181, p‐tau231, and most recently p‐tau217. 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 Among all markers, p‐tau217 is the one that correlates most strongly with AD pathology in the brain, and that has shown the highest diagnostic accuracy for AD in different head‐to‐head studies. 11 , 13 , 14 , 15 , 16

Along with AD pathology, clinical trajectories are also influenced by multiple factors, 17 among which vascular risk factors (as hypertension and/or high low‐density lipoprotein [LDL] cholesterol) are well established contributors to cognitive decline and increased risk of dementia. 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 Together, midlife vascular risk factors might account for up to 10% of dementia risk, and multidomain intervention that include improving cardiovascular health is a promising approach to reduce the risk of dementia. 22 , 23 Still, there are conflicting results as to whether vascular risk factors have independent or additive effects beyond AD pathology on cognitive decline in older age, particularly when individuals already have cognitive impairment. 24 , 25 , 26 These comorbidities may contribute alongside AD pathological processes, ultimately influencing clinical outcomes and highlighting the importance of considering them when evaluating disease progression.

The objective in this study was to evaluate the predictive value of plasma p‐tau markers and vascular risk factors on both clinical progression and cognitive decline along the AD continuum over on average 3.10 years (range 2–8 years) of follow‐up data. We hypothesized that the importance of different biological factors might differ depending on whether people are still cognitively unimpaired (CU) versus when they have mild cognitive impairment (MCI); thus, we analyzed the two populations separately in the main analyses. While studies have emerged suggesting the ability of p‐tau markers, in particular p‐tau217, to track cognitive decline, 11 , 13 , 14 , 15 , 27 most prior studies have not assessed the combination of plasma AD markers and vascular risk factors. To fill this gap, we used data from the Consortium for the Early Identification of Alzheimer's Disease–Quebec (CIMA‐Q), a longitudinal, ongoing cohort of older adults based in the province of Quebec, Canada covering the AD continuum. 28

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Participants

CIMA‐Q participants were recruited across the province of Québec between 2019 and 2024. 28 , 29 This ongoing consortium aims to facilitate early diagnosis of AD and to identify new therapeutic targets. Participants enrolled span the AD continuum, from CU older adults to patients with early AD dementia. Briefly, participants needed to be at least 65 years old, to understand, read and write French or English, to have an informant available to provide additional information and a Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR, 30 , 31 ) global score of maximum 1. Exclusion criteria included active substance use disorder, presence or history of a central nervous disorder, and regular use of benzodiazepines. Participants were followed up every 2 years and each visit included a neuropsychological evaluation, a blood draw, and questionnaires on psychoaffective measures and comorbidities. At each visit, participants were clinically assessed by expert physicians and underwent a neuropsychological and neuropsychiatric examination with a certified psychometrician. CU participants were recruited from the community. They all had a CDR global score of 0 and a Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA, 32 ) between 26 and 30. 33 , 34 The majority of CU participants had subjective cognitive decline (SCD), defined according to the Jessen criteria corresponding to self‐reported experience of cognitive decline in the absence of objective impairment on standardized testing, based on a positive response to the question “Are you worried that your memory is getting worse?” 35 A smaller group without SCD (33% of the total CU group) was also included. By design, the CU participants without SCD were not followed over time and are included in cross‐sectional analyses only. Patients with MCI and AD dementia were recruited from memory clinics. Participants were diagnosed as MCI according to education‐adjusted delayed recall scores on the Wechsler Logical Memory test, a MoCA between 20 and 25, and a CDR global score of 0.5. All MCI participants also had to meet NIA/AA clinical criteria for MCI due to AD. 34 Diagnostic criteria for patients with early AD dementia were to have a MoCA between 13 and 24, a CDR global score of 1.0, and fulfill NIA/AA clinical criteria for probable AD dementia. 34 , 36 By design, patients with AD dementia only underwent a baseline visit and were not followed over time.

In cross‐sectional analyses, we included all participants who had plasma p‐tau measurements and vascular factors available at baseline (n = 277) and longitudinal analyses were restricted to those who had at least one follow‐up visit (n = 172). A flowchart of individuals included in each set of analyses is shown in Figure S1. All data were acquired between November 2014 and September 2024. Ethics approval was obtained from the ethics committee of the “Institut Universitaire de Gériatrie de Montréal”. All participants provided a written informed consent in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Cognitive measures

Participants underwent a neuropsychological evaluation at baseline and at every follow‐up visit. 28 We focused on four cognitive measures that are commonly used in the preclinical and prodromal stage of AD: (1) the MoCA score as a measure of global cognition (score out of 30); (2) the sum of words remembered from a list over five trials on the Rey Auditory Verbal Learning Test (RAVLT) as a measure of immediate memory (out of 75 as a total score); (3) the number of words remembered from the RAVLT list after a delay as a measure of delayed recall (out of 15); and the score on the Digit Symbol Substitution Test (DSST) as a measure of executive function. 37

Vascular risk factors

The vascular risk factors included in the current study were hypertension, hypercholesterolemia, and diabetes. We derived the presence or absence of each factor from commonly used references based on biological measurements and/or medication from the baseline visit. 21 , 38 Hypertension was defined as a resting systolic blood pressure ≥140 mmHg, a diastolic blood pressure ≥90 mmHg or use of antihypertensive medication. Hypercholesterolemia was defined as total cholesterol ≥5.18 mmol/L. Diabetes was defined as fasting plasma glucose ≥7.0 mmol/L or use of antidiabetic medication.

Plasma p‐tau biomarkers

Plasma samples collected at baseline were analyzed for p‐tau217, p‐tau181, and p‐tau231. Plasma p‐tau217 levels were measured using the S‐PLEX® Human Tau (pT217) kit (MSD, Rockville, MD). Plasma p‐tau181 and p‐tau231 were assessed using an in‐house single molecule array (Simoa) method at the Clinical Neurochemistry Laboratory, University of Gothenburg, Mölndal, Sweden). 8 , 10 A total of 283 participants had baseline measurements available for p‐tau217, p‐tau181, and p‐tau231. We excluded three participants who had extreme values on p‐tau biomarkers (two AD and one CU), with values exceeding six standard deviations from the mean, and three individuals without all vascular risk factors available, resulting in 277 participants retained for analyses. The participants with extreme p‐tau levels also had very high creatinine levels, with two of them having the highest level (above 190 µmol/L), which could have also influenced the p‐tau measurements. 39 , 40

CSF biomarkers

A small subset of participants (n = 58) had CSF available at baseline, in which Aβ42, Aβ40, and p‐tau181 concentrations were measured. CSF was collected by a neurologist following standard procedures and all lumbar punctures were done in the afternoon. Samples were aliquoted and stored at −80°C in the CIMA‐Q biobank at CHUL (Quebec, QC). Enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assays (ELISA) following the manufacturer's instructions were used to measure Aβ42 and Aβ40 (A Peptide Panel 1 Kit (6E10), #K15200E) and p‐tau181 (S‐PLEX Human Tau (pT181) Kit, #K151AGMS).

Statistical analysis

Differences in continuous variables between diagnostic groups at baseline were assessed using the Kruskal–Wallis test, followed by Dunn's post‐hoc test. Categorical variables (sex, APOE ε4 genotype, hypertension, hypercholesterolemia and diabetes) were analyzed using the Fisher test. Bivariate associations evaluated how plasma p‐tau levels correlated with the CSF measures.

The p‐tau levels were compared between diagnostic groups in linear models. For each comparison, estimated means adjusted for covariates (marginal means) were extracted. Pairwise group comparisons were performed, and the resulting p‐values were corrected for multiple testing using Tukey's test. We also evaluated if the vascular risk factors were associated with the different p‐tau levels using linear regression models.

We used Cox proportional hazards models to evaluate whether plasma p‐tau measures and vascular risk factors were related to clinical progression over on average 3.10 years of follow‐up (range 2–8 years), focusing on progression to MCI, and progression to AD dementia in separate models. Time from baseline at which a change in diagnosis occurred was recorded for individuals with clinical progression, and total follow‐up time was used for individuals who remained CU or those who remained MCI.

We also investigated if p‐tau levels and vascular risk factors were associated with cross‐sectional and longitudinal cognitive performance. For cross‐sectional associations, linear models were fitted with baseline measures of p‐tau, hypertension, hypercholesterolemia, and diabetes as predictors and cognition as the outcome. For longitudinal analyses, linear mixed‐effect models with random slope and intercept were used to assess the effect of the same predictors on cognitive decline over time. The time variable was the years from baseline. The analyses were conducted separately in CU, in participants with cognitive impairment, as well as in the whole group. To test the potential synergistic effect of vascular risk factors on p‐tau levels and cognition, we also fitted separate models including the interaction term between p‐tau and each vascular factor. Such analyses were done in the whole group to maximize statistical power.

To ensure consistency across analyses, the same set of covariates (age, sex, and education) was included in all models. Statistical significance for all models was set at p < 0.05 All analyses were performed in R v.4.4.1 (R Core Team, 2024).

RESULTS

Sample demographics

We included 277 participants from the CIMA‐Q cohort with available plasma p‐tau measurements at baseline, consisting of 167 CU older adults, 83 patients with MCI and 27 patients with AD dementia. On average, participants were 74.3 years old, had 15.0 years of education, and 64% were female. A total of 60% of participants were classified as having hypertension, 47% had hypercholesterolemia, and 8.7% had diabetes. Associations between p‐tau markers, vascular risk factors, clinical diagnosis, and progression

Regarding plasma biomarkers, p‐tau217, p‐tau181, and p‐tau231 levels were higher in AD dementia patients compared to MCI or CU participants (all p < 0.0001), particularly for p‐tau217 (marginal mean AD‐CU = 1.85 and AD‐MCI = 1.59) compared to p‐tau181 (AD‐CU = 1.34 and AD‐MCI = 1.09) or p‐tau231 (AD‐CU = 1.12 and AD‐MCI = 0.85). Also, there was a trend toward higher p‐tau217 in MCI compared to CU participants (MCI‐CU = 0.26, p = 0.07) (Figure 1(A)). When considering clinical progression in individuals with longitudinal data, no differences in the three p‐tau markers were observed between CU participants who progressed to MCI (n = 32) and those who remained CU (Figure 1(B), all p > 0.56). In contrast, among individuals with MCI, those who progressed to AD dementia (n = 15) had higher baseline p‐tau217 concentrations compared to those who remained with a diagnosis of MCI (Figure 1(B), p = 0.005). No group differences were observed for p‐tau181 or p‐tau231 (p = 0.31 and 0.23, respectively). Such results were recapitulated using Cox proportional hazard models that combined p‐tau markers and vascular risk factors together to investigate which ones are associated with clinical progression (Figure 2). In the CU group, none of the p‐tau markers or vascular risk factors were associated with progression to MCI (hazard ratio [HR] between 0.94 and 1.06, all p > 0.41, Figure 2(A)). In the MCI group, only higher p‐tau217 levels at baseline were associated with progression to AD (HR = 1.22, p = 0.016 for continuous p‐tau levels, Figure 2(A)), with no additional effect of vascular risk factors. Splitting p‐tau217 levels into tertiles for visualization and to facilitate interpretation of progression risk, participants in the top tertile had higher risk of progressing to AD dementia compared to those in the lower (HR = 8.08, p = 0.01) and the intermediate tertile (HR = 4.27, p = 0.05) (Figure 2(B)). Associations between p‐tau markers, vascular risk factors, and cognition

We next examined associations of p‐tau markers and the three vascular risk factors on cognitive performance at baseline and cognitive decline over time. In the CU group, none of the plasma p‐tau markers or the vascular risk factors were associated with cognitive performance in cross‐sectional analyses (Figure 3(A)). In cognitively impaired individuals (MCI and AD dementia), higher p‐tau levels were associated with worse cognition on all four cognitive tests (Figure 3(A)). The p‐tau217 showed the strongest association with worse cognitive performance, especially on the MoCA score (std β = ‐0.49, p < 0.001), compared to p‐tau181 (std β = −0.29, p < 0.01) and p‐tau231 (std β = −0.19, p = 0.06). Vascular risk factors had no significant effect. In the whole group, higher plasma p‐tau217, p‐tau181, and p‐tau231 were associated with worse performance across cognitive tests, with no effect of the vascular risk factors (Figure S3).

When examining cognitive performance over time, higher baseline levels of p‐tau217 and p‐tau231 in the CU group were associated with greater decline in MoCA scores over time (p‐tau*time std beta = −0.10 and −0.11, p = 0.03 and p = 0.02, respectively). A few additional associations were observed between p‐tau markers and decline in memory performance on the RAVLT (Figure 3(B)). All statistical associations are reported in Table S3. In the MCI group, higher baseline levels of all p‐tau markers were associated with longitudinal decline across most cognitive domains (Figure 3(B–C)). The highest effect was again observed with the MoCA, with a more pronounced association for p‐tau217 (p‐tau217*time = −0.24, p < 0.001). While p‐tau markers had the strongest effect on cognitive decline, hypertension was also associated with steeper decline on the RAVLT total score and DSST (std β for hypertension*time of −0.09 and −0.10, all p = 0.04, Figure 3(B)). All statistical associations are reported in Table S4. In the MCI group, unexpectedly, hypercholesterolemia was associated with better episodic memory performance over time. We performed a sensitivity analysis including use of lipid‐lowering medication in the models, given that 68% of individuals with normal cholesterol levels were taking medication. After this adjustment, the association was no longer observed (Figure S4), an association that we hypothesize was driven by individuals suffering from hypercholesterolemia, but who had cholesterol values normalized by medication.

Results on the whole group are presented in Figure S3, where we only observed that higher p‐tau levels were associated with worse cognition at baseline and over time, with no effects of the vascular risk factors.

Interaction between plasma p‐tau217 and vascular risk factors on cognition

Lastly, we investigated whether vascular risk factors moderated the association between plasma p‐tau217 and cognition in the whole group (see Table S5 and Figure S5). Cross‐sectionally, higher p‐tau217 levels in the presence of hypertension were associated with lower RAVLT total scores (std β = −0.24, p = 0.05). Similarly, higher p‐tau217 levels in individuals with diabetes were associated with lower MoCA scores (std β = −0.30, p < 0.001) and lower RAVLT total scores (std β = −0.15, p = 0.04). Regarding longitudinal analyses, the only significant interaction was between p‐tau217 and hypertension on DSST performance over time (std β = −0.07, p = 0.03), indicating a steeper decline in executive function over time in individuals with hypertension and higher baseline p‐tau217 levels.

DISCUSSION

In this longitudinal investigation across the AD continuum in the CIMA‐Q cohort, we simultaneously evaluated plasma p‑tau biomarkers and vascular risk factors in relation to clinical progression and cognitive trajectories. Plasma p‑tau217 emerged as the most robust and consistent marker of cognitive impairment and decline. Across diagnostic groups, elevated p‑tau217 levels were associated with worse global cognition, memory, and executive function, and predicted progression from MCI to AD dementia. Although p‑tau181 and p‑tau231 were also associated with cognitive outcomes, their effects were generally weaker and less consistent compared with p‑tau217, reinforcing the growing evidence that p‑tau217 may be the most sensitive plasma marker of AD pathology. Vascular risk factors also had an impact on cognition, but they did not predict clinical progression. Hypertension contributed to steeper cognitive decline independently of p‐tau markers in cognitively impaired individuals and potentiated the impact of p‐tau217 on memory in the whole group.

Plasma p‐tau217 levels, and to a lesser extent p‐tau181, were associated with faster cognitive decline, particularly in cognitively impaired individuals, in line with recent findings comparing plasma p‐tau217, p‐tau181, and p‐tau231. 41 In CU participants, none of the p‐tau markers were significantly associated with cognitive scores and clinical progression, which differed from some previous studies. 42 , 43 , 44 Most of the CU participants who progressed to MCI had low levels of p‐tau217 (most below 3.6 pg/mL, corresponding to the middle tertile, Figure 1(B)) and fell below the recently proposed Aβ‐positivity cutoffs between 5.0–6.0 pg/mL using the same assay. 45 This suggests that, overall, a low proportion of them are in the preclinical phase of AD and (an)other etiology(ies) might underly the clinical progression, highlighting the importance of examining other risk factors. Still, CU individuals with the highest p‐tau levels showed faster cognitive decline over time, highlighting the predictive value of those plasma markers before the appearance of symptoms. These individuals might still be years away from clinical progression, and longer follow‐up as well as longitudinal measures of p‐tau levels will help clarify their trajectories.

Among the vascular risk factors, hypertension emerged as the only vascular factor associated with longitudinal decline in memory and executive function in patients with MCI. This finding is consistent with prior evidence showing that hypertension increases risk of cognitive decline, while blood pressure reduction may mitigate this risk, as shown in population‐based cohort studies 46 , 47 and clinical trial. 48 Our results align with prior studies reporting largely additive effects of midlife hypertension 20 or cumulative vascular risk factors 25 and AD biomarkers on cognition, with no interactive effect. Still, in the whole group, we also found that hypertension and diabetes both potentiated the effect of p‐tau217 on cognition. The findings with diabetes should however be interpreted with caution given the small number of affected individuals (n = 24). Taken together, the results highlight the complex associations between vascular risk factors and AD pathology and underscore the importance of evaluating these processes jointly across the AD continuum.

We observed more limited associations of vascular risk factors than have been reported in the literature. 20 , 25 , 49 We believe this might be due in part to the follow‐up period and the timing of vascular risk exposure. Growing evidence, including a meta‐analysis on hypertension, suggests that vascular risk factors in midlife have a stronger effect on risk of dementia and cognitive impairment than those measured in late life. 50 , 51 A recent large study from the Atherosclerosis Risk in Communities also estimated that 22%–44% of incident dementia cases by age 80 were attributable to vascular risk factors accumulated across midlife and late life. 21 The attributable risk of these factors decreased after age 80, pointing to the importance of midlife vascular risk to dementia risk. 21 Evidence from interventional studies also illustrates the complexity of vascular risk factors management in relation to cognitive outcomes. While a recent study reported no significant cognitive benefit from exercise and intensive vascular risk reduction over 24 months in CU older individuals, 52 intensive blood pressure control resulted in lower incidence of mild cognitive impairment 53 or all‐cause dementia 48 over 4 to 5 years in recent trials. Our findings, in the context of prior literature, highlight the importance of longitudinal approaches that integrate vascular exposures alongside AD biomarkers. Late‐life assessments over relatively short follow‐up periods may capture only a fraction of their cumulative impact.

The way vascular risk factors are defined may also contribute to heterogeneity across studies. In particular, the use of biological measures versus clinical diagnoses or medication‐based definitions can lead to different interpretations. 47 , 54 , 55 Continuous measures (e.g., blood pressure or lipid levels) reflect current physiological status but may underestimate vascular risk in individuals whose levels are well controlled by treatment. Conversely, definitions based on diagnosis or medication use capture individuals with established disease but may not reflect current biological exposure. Medication can modify the relationship between vascular risk factors and different outcomes, which is important to consider in interpreting the literature. 56

Our study has both strengths and limitations. The longitudinal design allowed us to capture clinical progression and cognitive change over time, although the duration of follow‐up may have limited our ability to detect certain associations, particularly at earlier disease stages. The simultaneous evaluation of plasma p‐tau species alongside vascular risk factors, along with their interactions, enabled direct comparison of their relative contributions to cognitive decline and clinical progression. In addition, vascular risk factors were defined using clinical measures and standardized thresholds. The CIMA‐Q cohort is not enriched for individuals at high risk of AD (e.g., based on family history or APOE4 genotype), and the proportion of CU individuals likely to be Aβ‐positive, as estimated from p‐tau217 levels, appears lower than in some previous large‐scale studies of preclinical populations. 27 , 43 , 57 This may partly explain the more modest associations observed between p‐tau217 and clinical outcomes. Participants were also highly educated, relatively well managed with respect to vascular risk factors, and had limited ethnic diversity, as the cohort was predominantly White individuals, which may affect generalizability. This is particularly relevant as plasma p‐tau levels 58 , 59 and vascular risk burden 60 , 61 may vary across ethnic groups. In addition, the cohort was relatively older than those included in some large multi‐cohort studies looking at clinical progression in CU. 44 , 62 , 63 These cohort characteristics should be considered when interpreting both effect sizes and rates of clinical progression.

In conclusion, our findings highlight the robust association of plasma p‐tau217 with cognitive decline and clinical progression, while suggesting that hypertension also contribute to cognitive decline, potentially through independent and synergistic effect on p‐tau levels.
