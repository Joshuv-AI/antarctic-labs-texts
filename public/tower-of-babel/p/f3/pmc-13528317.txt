Utilising the digital twin for disease prevention, treatment and in silico research
Authors: Neera Ahuja, Jason Hom, Jonathan H Chen, Olivier Gevaert
Published: 2026

Much of modern healthcare remains reactive: patients present with symptoms and clinicians respond. What if, instead, we could anticipate illnesses long before symptoms emerge using the ‘digital twin’: a comprehensive model that dynamically integrates, analyses and simulates a wide array of data from the electronic medical record (EHR) allowing for pattern recognition and projection, allowing providers to intervene along a clinical trajectory to prevent or treat earlier in the disease course. Unlike standard EHR analytics, which typically produce static population-level predictions from historical clinical data, a medical digital twin is a continuously synchronised, patient-specific, computational model, enabling in silico simulation of disease trajectories and therapeutic interventions for a single patient. A digital twin goes beyond simple ‘analytics’ as it allows ‘n of 1’ studies, simulates future trajectories and asks counterfactuals. Moreover, in the digital twin construct, the virtual and physical twins are bidirectionally updated, converting biological and physiological knowledge into computational models to inform clinical decision-making.1 This offers an immense opportunity to efficiently and accurately predict clinical trends proactively, moving us from a treatment mindset to a preventive paradigm.

To realise this concept of a digital twin, we anticipate that a standard machine learning (ML)/artificial intelligence (AI) approach will not be sufficient, as it cannot be deployed for a single patient; rather it needs a cohort or population. To deploy a model for a single patient, we need stronger assumptions of the system; that is, we need to rely on mechanistic-based modelling. Current ML/AI approaches are not (yet) capable of realising these three main points required to develop a digital twin. A mechanistic component is also likely required (a hybrid approach where both mechanistic and data-driven modelling are combined) and will likely be the most successful approach to building a medical digital twin.

A recent example of the potential of medical digital twins is Epic’s Cosmos AI project which illustrates the scale and trajectory of this vision. Using deidentified records from over 300 million patients, the Cosmos models can depict a clinical trajectory. Early results reveal how such generative medical event models can evolve into real-time engines for prediction and prevention, offering a path to move beyond descriptive analytics and towards precision medicine. Additionally, the digital twin offers the opportunity for in silico human research resulting in large-scale treatment algorithms for myriad disease conditions.2 The in silico approach, while not an alternative to a rigorous, double-blinded, randomised, placebo controlled clinical trial, offers a robust, rapid, lower cost counterpart to assess treatment safety and efficacy with virtually no risk to the human subject.

In order to fully realise the promise of digital twins, several key issues need to be addressed. The first is a long-standing challenge with the accuracy and timely completion of data entered into the EHR. If important factors are missing, predictions could be misleading and unsafe.1 Just as a mosaic is only as good as its individual tiles, a model is only as good as its assumptions and data.

To address this, many hope that advances in generative AI will increase the accuracy of documentation. Indeed, many providers are transitioning to ambient AI scribes, and evidence suggests generative AI may improve efficiency of documentation.3 4 Yet there is also a risk it could worsen long-standing EHR problems. A recent NEJM perspective cautions that large language model–generated notes may increase chart bloat, entrench billing-oriented documentation, and embed confabulations into the record.5 If so, digital twins trained on such data may amplify errors, rather than reduce them.

The next key issue is a need to ensure that raw data from wearable devices are continuously integrated for computation and simulation for digital twins to be effective. As digital twins are dynamic systems requiring frequent updates to be meaningful, wearables may provide a cost-effective continuous stream of data. Even when integration is feasible, there are concerns about provider medical-legal responsibilities for monitoring and responding to 24/7 data streams.6 This leads to the important consideration of ethical and regulatory oversight. Current medical approval systems are built around population-level trials, not individual ‘virtual trials’, so integrating digital-twin–based treatments will require deeper regulatory oversight and ethical integration into medical practice.1

From a health equity perspective, socioeconomic disparities that limit EMR access for certain patient populations may also limit the digital twin’s potential for diagnostic and treatment opportunities to further disadvantage populations who already have limited access to healthcare. Ensuring techquity (‘technological equity’) to balance the integration between the virtual and physical twin will be key to ensure accuracy and access from a social and philosophical viewpoint. The optimal solutions will combine multidisciplinary expertise from clinicians, engineers, health policy experts, bioethicists and scientists.

From a treatment perspective, pharmacogenetic-based prescriptions leveraging the digital twin model will likely become the standard of care for drug treatment. In silico human research will be a cost-effective, expeditious complement to clinical trials with rapid and exponential impact. Digital literacy will be important to ensure safe and value-based interventions in an ethical practice.

The ability to prospectively predict clinical trends and intervene sooner with appropriate screening can significantly reduce the burden of disease (including cost, polypharmacy, the emotional toil of medical illness and mortality rates) from a population health perspective. This phenomenon can dramatically shift the landscape of medicine from one that is currently more reactive and treatment-oriented towards a broader paradigm of prevention.
