Aging-rewired metabolic cues promote autophagy and senescence via DRAM1
Authors: Jinghong Yang, Haobin Sun, Keqing Xu, Xiaomei Zhang, Mudan Huang, Guanghui Jin, Yasong Liu, Weizhao Chen, Shunan Lin, Juan Shen, Chuan-Qi Zhong, Yan Xu, Qi Zhang, Wei Liu, Yang Yang, Jingxing Ou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBeing a major contributor to cell senescence and aging, DNA damage activates macroautophagy/autophagy, but how this process is affected by aging-rewired metabolism in normal biological systems remains to be explored. Here in cultured human umbilical cord-derived mesenchymal stem cells (HsMSCs) and the mouse liver that accumulate DNA damage during aging, we found an elevation of DRAM1 (DNA damage regulated autophagy modulator 1) and DRAM1-mediated pro-senescent autophagy (DMPA). Confirming that DRAM1 activated AMPK, we sought DMPA-associated metabolic features and noted substantial enrichment of N-acetylhistamine (N-AcHA) and phosphatidylethanolamine (PE) products in the aging HsMSCs and mouse liver. Elevating DNA damage and senescence, N-AcHA supplements were sufficient to upregulate DRAM1 and DMPA in primary hepatocytes from young mice but not even in pre-senescent HsMSCs, hence reflecting the differential tolerance of these cell models toward cytotoxic metabolic cues. The effects of N-AcHA were further verified in mouse aging and post-hepatectomy liver regeneration models. In contrast, accumulating cellular PE contents via ethanolamine supplements augmented autophagy but not DNA damage and senescence despite tending to induce DRAM1. Combined treatments with N-AcHA and ethanolamine were sufficient to trigger DMPA in HsMSCs. Despite their differential cellular responses toward N-AcHA and ethanolamine supplements, in primary HsMSCs and mouse hepatocytes DMPA did not notably downregulate SQSTM1/p62 proteins, which differed from general macroautophagy and may constitutively support the fusion of SQSTM1-modified cargo-containing autophagosomes with lysosomes. Overall, this study reveals DMPA-promoting metabolic and molecular features. Introduction

DNA damage occurs from exposure to genotoxic environmental cues, mutations, transcription blocking, telomere erosion and metabolic stress. Failure in DNA repair and persistent DNA damage response (DDR) impair metabolic homeostasis, imposing risks of senescence, apoptosis, degenerative diseases or tumorigenesis [1–4]. Metabolic by-products such as aldehydes and alkylating agents, and free radicals generated from oxidative phosphorylation can directly damage DNA. While the pentose phosphate pathway can be activated to support nucleotide synthesis and redox homeostasis, certain metabolites such as 2-hydroxyglutarate, fumarate and succinate have been proposed to impair histone demethylation and DNA repair [5–8]. As the senescent/aging process gradually but substantially rewires cellular metabolism, knowledge on whether the rewired metabolic fluxes or accumulation of certain metabolites may contribute to DNA damage, DDR and even senescence/aging in normal biological systems remains largely lacking.

Intriguingly, perhaps depending on the mechanistic context of DDR and autophagy [9–13], variable outcomes that promote DNA repair, senescence or apoptosis occur. To support DNA repair, DDR kinase Ataxia-Telangiectasia Mutated (ATM) and DNA-dependent protein kinase can interact with 5’AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) to boost ATP production and transcriptional stress response [14–16]. As AMPK activation is the classic signal for cellular energy deficiency and macroautophagic/autophagic induction, DDR-activated TP53/p53 has been proposed to restrain glucose uptake and glycolysis, limiting cell growth and elevating autophagy via AMPK repression of MTORC1, thus triggering apoptosis in various models [17–19]. Also induced by TP53, DRAM1 has been suggested to promote autophagic flux and apoptosis [20,21], and involve in tumor suppression, myocardial protection, and autophagic defense against pathogens [22–25]. Alternatively, ATG5 participates in basal autophagy that suppresses TP53-induced apoptosis [26], and also has been implicated in potentiating DNA repair upon genotoxic stress [27]. Except for the DRAM1-dependent pathway, many of these key factors function beyond DDR and respond to other stress cues. Thus, the context of cellular metabolism, DDR and its downstream DRAM1-dependent autophagic activities in normal biological systems remain to be investigated and elucidated.

Understanding the DDR-specific metabolic and autophagic signatures has great implication to the field of regenerative medicine. For instance, human mesenchymal stem cells (HsMSCs) derived from various donor tissues have shown promising potential in transfusion therapies, but ATM-dependent DDR is one of the major processes that lead to HsMSC early senescence during expansion and passaging [28,29], thus limiting the therapeutic values of HsMSCs. Another example is post-partial hepatectomy (PHx) liver regeneration, which is compromised by aging-associated accumulation of DNA lesions and defective DNA repair and replication [30–32]. Intriguingly, both pro- and anti-proliferative effects of autophagy have been described in the senescent process of HsMSCs [33] and liver regeneration in aged rodent models [34,35], which likely result from different interventions or distinct components of the autophagic machinery being recruited [36,37]. It is unclear whether DDR-mediated autophagy may play a significant role in the senescent/aging process of HsMSCs and the mouse liver.

Here in cultured umbilical cord-derived HsMSCs, we reveal an increase of DRAM1, which activates AMPK and mediates pro-senescent autophagy (DMPA). We find that DMPA is connected with aging-rewired cell metabolism. Particularly, how DMPA is facilitated by the cellular accumulation of N-acetylhistamine (N-AcHA) and phosphatidylethanolamine (PE) is investigated. Despite minor model specificities, verifications in mouse primary hepatocyte cultures, natural aging and PHx liver regeneration models have led to similar conclusions, suggesting that DMPA is unlikely an HsMSC-specific feature.

Results

DNA damage enhances DMPA in cultured HsMSCs and mouse hepatocytes

Telomere shortening in primary cultures of human fetal cells results in cell senescence after 40–60 times of division, known as the “Hayflick limit” [38]. However, revealed by senescence-associated GLB1/β-galactosidase (SA-GLB1/β-gal) staining and CDKN1A/p21 immunofluorescent staining, primary HsMSC strains derived from multiple donors developed senescent phenotypes without losing their MSC characteristics (Fig. S1A-E) much earlier than the designated Hayflick limit. To search for factors driving HsMSC early senescence, we first looked into the transcriptomic changes in these cultured HsMSCs (Figure 1A and Data S1). Pathway enrichment analyses indicated that in the aging HsMSCs, genes related to the TNF-NFKB signaling, cytokine signaling and inflammatory response were upregulated, while genes responding to stress, chromatin organization and DNA repair and various metabolic activities were downregulated (Figure 1(B,C) and Fig. S2A, B). At the proteomic level (Data S2), proteins functioning in metabolism, cellular functions related to phagosomes and lysosomes, and DNA damage and repair tended to be altered in the aging HsMSCs (Figure 1(D,E)). Western blotting confirmed upregulation of pro-inflammatory IL1B/IL-1β (interleukin 1 beta), diminished expression of NFE2L2/NRF2, a versatile transcription factor for anti-oxidative damage and detoxification [39,40], and more severe lipid peroxidation indicated by the elevated levels of 4-hydroxynonenal (4-HNE)-modified proteins in the aging HsMSCs (Fig. S2C). Thus, both the transcriptomic and proteomic analyses suggested profoundly rewired metabolic activities, downregulated DNA repair machinery, and possibly upregulated autophagic turnover in the senescent HsMSCs. Among the differentially upregulated genes associated with the senescent/aging process of the cultured HsMSCs, augmented expression of DRAM1 and SQSTM1 was observed (Figure 1F,G). Consistent with the transcriptomic data, the elevated expression senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP)-related genes and the expanded lysosomal population, DRAM1 and SQSTM1 expression was augmented in senescent HsMSCs (Fig. S2D, E). Meanwhile at the protein levels, DRAM1 and MAP1LC3/LC3-II (microtubule associated protein 1 light chain 3)-II proteins were upregulated, while no overt changes in ATG5 (autophagy related 5), SQSTM1, TFEB (transcription factor EB) and p-MTOR (Ser2448) were noted (Figure 2C and Fig. S2C). To investigate whether DDR deploys the DRAM1 pathway to regulate autophagic activities in the senescent/aging process, we first focused on DRAM1 functions. Overexpressing DRAM1 in young or pre-senescent HsMSCs, and DRAM1-knockdown by shRNA interference in pre-senescent or senescent HsMSCs affected the level of LC3-II and stem cell colony formation as expected (Figure 2(D) and Fig. S2F). Next, autophagic flux in live HsMSCs was assessed by the classical method of expressing the mCherry-GFP-LC3 fusion proteins and measurement of GFP and mCherry fluorescent puncta [41]. As expected, overexpressing DRAM1 elevated the population of autolysosomes, while diminishing DRAM1 functions decreased the autophagosome and autolysosome populations (Figure 2(E)). Further, treating HsMSCs with the DNA-damaging drug etoposide (ETO) (Fig. S2G) augmented TP53, DRAM1, LC3-II and CDKN1A, while DRAM1-overexpression and -knockdown affected LC3-II and CDKN1A but not TP53 as expected; intriguingly, though DRAM1-knockdown (lowered autophagic turnovers) resulted in increased SQSTM1 levels as expected, DRAM1-overexpression (heightened autophagic turnovers) did not notably downregulate SQSTM1 (Figure 2(D,F)), which might be compensated as DRAM1-overexpression or the ETO treatment elevated SQSTM1 expression (Fig. S2H).

Indeed, further evaluations on the expression of SASP-related genes and SA-GLB1/β-gal staining (Figure 3(A,B) and Fig. S2H), the stem cell proliferative potential revealed by MKI67 immunostaining (Figure 3(C)), and DNA damage adjacent to the telomeres visualized by telomere-associated foci (TAF) Immuno-FISH imaging [42–44] (Figure 3(D)) confirmed that either ETO-exacerbated DNA damage or DRAM1-overexpression promoted senescent phenotypes in cultured HsMSCs, while DRAM1-knockdown effectively reversed them. Further, though manipulating DRAM1 expression did not notably affect the p-MTOR (Ser2448) level as reported previously [45], it apparently mediated PRKAA/AMPK and RPS6KB/p70S6K downstream of MTORC1, as increasing DRAM1 resulted in enhanced PRKAA Thr172 phosphorylation and simultaneously declined RPS6KB T389 phosphorylation, and vice versa (Figure 3(E)). Accumulation of N-acetylhistamine (N-AcHA) induces DNA damage and DMPA in mouse hepatocytes but not in HsMSCs

How DNA damage is accumulated in cultured HsMSCs and the mouse liver was little known. As our HsMSC transcriptomic and proteomic analyses suggested profoundly rewired metabolic activities in the aging HsMSCs (Figure 1(B–D) and Fig. S2A, B), we sought the metabolomic alterations in the HsMSC strains we had. Interestingly, among the differentially presented metabolic products (DPMP) in aging HsMSCs, cellular accumulation of N-acetylhistamine (N-AcHA) and Methanesulfonic acid (MSA) was noted, and was also found in the liver samples from aged mice (Figure 5(A,B), Fig. S3, Data S3 and S4). To test their possible effects on HsMSCs, N-AcHA or MSA was supplemented into the culture medium. Although high doses of N-AcHA and MSA both apparently inhibited HsMSC expansion following 5-day incubation, no pro-senescence effect was seen in MSA treatment (Fig. S4A, B). Cell viability assay and evaluating mitochondrial oxidative stress levels by MitoNeoD [46–49] in cultured HsMSCs and mouse primary hepatocytes following 1-day incubation with N-AcHA found no apparent cytotoxic effects (Fig. S4C-E). No apparent cell apoptosis was induced by N-AcHA supplements (Fig. S4F). In contrast, various doses of N-AcHA could accelerate senescence in cultured HsMSCs and mouse hepatocytes (Figure 5(C)). Subsequent assays revealed that N-AcHA elevated DNA damage and the expression of SASP genes in both HsMSCs and mouse hepatocytes (Figure 5(D–F)).

Intriguingly however, though high-dose N-AcHA supplements downregulated DNA repair proteins such as PARP and XRCC1 in HsMSCs, it did not overtly augment TP53, DRAM1, and hence did not notably alter the PRKAA/AMPK and MTORC1-RPS6KB signaling, LC3-II and SQSTM1 protein levels and the autophagic flux; in comparison, N-AcHA supplements were sufficient to elevate Dram1 expression, TP53, DRAM1 and LC3-II proteins and the autophagic flux, and mediate PRKAA/AMPK, MTORC1-RPS6KB signaling activities in cultured mouse hepatocytes (Figure 6(A,B) and Fig. S5A-F). Further, Dram1-knockdown enhanced proliferative potential and downregulated SA-GLB1/β-gal and TAF signals in primary mouse hepatocytes, antagonizing the DMPA-promoting effects of N-AcHA (Figure 6(C–G)). Taken together, cellular accumulation of some metabolites may elicit cytotoxic effects, and N-AcHA is likely to deteriorate DNA damage and exacerbate DDR. While primary mouse hepatocytes were more sensitive to N-AcHA and elicited DMPA phenotypes, it is plausible that under our experimental conditions, the DNA repair machinery in HsMSCs was sufficient to prevent N-AcHA-induced DMPA. N-AcHA hinders post-PHx liver regeneration and accelerates aging in mice

To test the effects of N-AcHA in vivo, we first tried with the mouse post-PHx liver regeneration model (Figure 7(A)). Supplementing N-AcHA to eight-week-old mice elevated their hepatic N-AcHA levels within the physiological range (Fig. S6A), apparently resulted in slower liver regeneration, fewer proliferating cells, and lower expression of a few marker genes for bile acid synthesis and xenobiotic metabolism [50–52] post-PHx (Figure 7(A,B) and Fig. S6B). Then, in experiments testing the aging process in mice (Figure 7(C)), though N-AcHA did not overtly affect the average respiratory exchange ratio (RER), heat production, food/water intake and locomotor activities of middle-aged mice as measured by an indirect calorimetry system (Fig. S6C), these mice had lower levels of oxygen consumption (VO2), grip strength, body weight, blood glucose concentration, hepatic glutathione reduced form to oxidized form ratio (GSH:GSSG), and hepatic expression of marker genes for bile acid synthesis, xenobiotic and lipoprotein metabolism [53]; meanwhile, an increased population of SA-GLB1/β-gal+ cells in the liver, and cases of death were noted in these N-AcHA-treated mice (Figure 7(C–F)). Consistently, N-AcHA treatments also elevated γ-H2AX, DRAM1 and LC3-II protein levels in the mouse liver (Figure 7(G)). As others and we found that N-AcHA normally would not be maintained at high levels for an extended period of time in mice [54] (Fig. S6D), these results were unlikely to result from our N-AcHA regimen-induced organ injuries. Phosphatidylethanolamine (PE) enhanced autophagic flux and together with N-AcHA facilitated DMPA in cultured HsMSCs and mouse hepatocytes

Because N-AcHA supplements were insufficient to trigger DMPA in cultured HsMSCs, we postulate that other metabolic products also contribute to the occurrence of DMPA and early senescence in HsMSCs. we noted from combined transcriptomic and metabolomic analysis [55] that the metabolism of glycerolipids (GL) and glycerophospholipids (GP) was enriched in the aging HsMSCs (Fig. S7A and Data S5). Indeed, metabolic products in the categories of PE, lysophosphatidylethanolamine (LPE), phosphatidylcholine (PC), lysophosphatidylcholine (LPC), phosphatidylglycerol (PG), triglyceride (TG) and ceramide were found accumulated in the aging HsMSCs (Figure 8A and Data S3). Similarly, the majority of PE, LPE, LPC and PG products showed aging-related accumulation in the liver of aged mice (Fig. S7B and Data S4). Thus, an aging-rewired lipid metabolic flux may occur in these models that resulted in the accumulation of these metabolic products. Because the formation of LC3-II by covalent attachment of PE onto LC3-Ι is critical for efficient autophagosome biogenesis and autophagosome-lysosome fusion [56–58], while ethanolamine (Eth) is the precursor of PE and has been used to enrich PE metabolism [59–61], we wondered how an enhanced Eth-PE metabolism may impact DMPA in cultured HsMSCs. As expected, short-term supplementing high-dose Eth increased cellular PE and other lipid products, and DPMPs from the Eth treatment were enriched in pathways such as GP metabolism and autophagy (Figure 8(B,C) and Data S6). Autophagic flux assessments in cultured HsMSCs and mouse hepatocytes supported that the Eth treatment increased autophagosome biogenesis and facilitated autophagy in a dose-dependent manner (Figure 8(D,E)). To further prove that this was achieved via Eth-PE metabolism (Fig. S7C, D), we opted to knock down the expression of a key enzyme of the pathway, PCYT2, which indeed downregulated LC3-II and upregulated SQSTM1 in Eth-treated HsMSCs (Figure 8(F)). Unexpectedly, SQSTM1 expression at the transcript level was elevated by the Eth treatment (Fig. S7D, E), consistent with the result that Eth did not decrease SQSTM1 protein levels, and also in line with our observation that despite an active DMPA, SQSTM1 proteins were not downregulated in senescent HsMSCs (Figure 2(C)).

Interestingly, low doses of Eth showed the trend of DRAM1 upregulation in cultured HsMSCs and mouse hepatocytes without exacerbating DNA damage or senescence (Figure 9(A-C) and Fig. S7F). Unlike HsMSCs, primary mouse hepatocytes could not elevate SQSTM1 expression following Eth treatments, so that SQSTM1 proteins were downregulated as the supplemented Eth increased (Figure 9B and Fig. S7E). Finally, combined treatments with N-AcHA and Eth were sufficient to induce DMPA in young HsMSCs and further exacerbated the DMPA phenotypes in mouse hepatocytes than that with N-AcHA treatment alone (Figure 9(C–G)). Discussion

Metabolic rewiring and persistent DDR accompany the senescent/aging process and profoundly impact cellular activities such as proliferation, autophagy and apoptosis. Compared to the well-appreciated TP53-AMPK-MTORC1-autophagy axis [10,11,62], we reckon the more DDR-specific TP53-DRAM1 pathway has remained understudied. Here in the in vitro HsMSC and mouse hepatocyte models and in vivo mouse aging model, we show a consistent DRAM1 induction in the presence of DNA-damaging cues. Additional to demonstrating that the level of DRAM1 functions is connected with AMPK activation, the cellular autophagic flux and senescent process that we term as “DMPA” in this study, by investigating the cellular effects of N-AcHA and Eth supplements, we show that a rewired cellular metabolism and hence accumulation of certain metabolites may exacerbate DNA damage and/or facilitate DMPA (Figure 9(G)). As DNA-damaging cues such as ETO and N-AcHA (Figure S2H, S5D), and enriched Eth-PE metabolism (Figure S7D) may enhance SQSTM1 expression (observed in cultured HsMSCs in this study), another feature distinguishing the DMPA scenario from conventional autophagy is its often-undiminished SQSTM1 protein level. This work may pave the way for future in-depth studies on the interconnection among DDR, cellular metabolism and DMPA, in which SQSTM1 functions may be key to support a constitutively active state of DMPA in pre-senescent cells.

In humans, N-AcHA has been reported to arise from intestinal absorption and acetylation of histamine and be excreted into the urine and feces [63]. N-AcHA has been shown to induce lipid peroxidation, which compromises algal cell membrane integrity and subcellular structure and inhibits cell growth [64,65], but its role in mammalian cells remains little known. How N-AcHA is processed in HsMSCs and the mouse liver has not been reported and not yet addressed in this study. As supplementing N-AcHA only resulted in its accumulation for a short period of time in vital organs of normal healthy mice (Fig. S6D), we speculate that aging-compromised cellular excretory function or histidine-histamine metabolism in the models we studied could cause a gradual intracellular accumulation of N-AcHA. Other than contributing to the normal autophagic flux (Figure 8D–F), we note that an extendedly enriched Eth-PE metabolism may lever toward DRAM1 activation (Figure 9(A,B)) and elevated SQSTM1 expression in cultured HsMSCs (Fig. S7D). How SQSTM1 functions may be differentially regulated by DNA-damaging cues and the Eth-PE metabolism in the senescent/aging process of different biological systems remains to be elucidated. The accumulation of other metabolites such as MSA demonstrates an adversary effect on cell proliferation (Fig. S4B). As these metabolic products were found similarly accumulated in the senescent/aging process of unrelated biological systems (HsMSCs and mouse liver), they might represent conserved metabolic mechanisms associated with aging and warrant further investigations.

With our efforts to verify findings regarding DMPA in different models, this study is limited by resources and tools. Metabolic flux assays may help shed light on how N-AcHA, PE and MSA are differentially processed in the aging HsMSCs and hepatocytes, but it remains challenging to trace their upstream pathways. Although some molecular context of DMPA has been described in this work, we are not yet able to pinpoint whether it might contribute to later cell apoptosis in the senescent HsMSC population (Figure 2A,B). Apoptotic mechanisms may be easily triggered in cultures of senescent primary cells but not so in vivo. Particularly, mammalian primary hepatocytes can only be maintained in cultures for 3–4 days. Thus, the development of in vivo models that allow real-time tracing of DMPA and cell fate, such as Dram1-overexpression or -knockout reporter mouse models would be ideal for future in-depth evaluations.

Materials and methods

Data reporting

The sample sizes used in this study have been calculated by GPower software [66] and ensured to meet the desired numbers. Additionally, similar group sizes are used in recent publications: n = 3–5 samples to determine the mRNA levels of a specific gene [67–69]; n = 3–7 samples to determine the expression levels and phosphorylation levels of a specific protein [69–73]; n = 4 experiments to determine the apoptotic (TUNEL+) cells [74]. n = 5–6 experiments to determine the colocalization of mCherry and GFP signals in cells transfected with plasmid coding for mCherry-GFP-LC3B [75]. n = 3–5 experiments to determine SA-GLB1/β-gal (senescence-associated galactosidase beta 1) assays [73,76,77]. n = 3–6 samples to determine the proliferation marker MKI67 in cells and tissues [78,79]; n = 3–4 independent experiments to quantification the mean number of TAF [44]; n = 6 mice for 2/3 PHx [78,80]; n = 18–20 mice, male or female, to determine survivorship [81–83]; n = 15 mice for the oxygen consumption rates (VO2) [84]; n = 15 mice for respiratory exchange ratio (RER) [84,85]; n = 13–15 mice for body weight analyses [81,82]; n = 21 mice for grip strength analyses [81,82]; and n = 13–15 mice for blood glucose analyses [70,83]; All findings were reproducibly confirmed, including independent replication attempts. For animal experiments, mice were housed under the same condition or place. For cell experiments, cells were cultured in the same incubator and were seeded in parallel. All experiments included appropriate controls, with comparative samples collected and analyzed under identical conditions. Randomization was applied wherever possible. During MS analyses (for metabolites and proteins), samples processing and analysis orders were randomized. For animal studies, sex- and age-matched littermates were randomly allocated to N-AcHA or vehicle treatment groups. For cell experiments, parallel-seeded cells were randomized to treatments, except where impractical (e.g., western blot sample loading followed a defined order for figure assembly. Blinding was implemented whenever feasible. All samples and cages were coded during collection and processing, with identifiers later revealed by an independent investigator not involved in these procedures or outcome assessment. For microscopy and statistical analyses, fields of view were randomly selected and typically evaluated by different operators to minimize selection bias.

Cell culture

Human umbilical cord-derived MSCs (HsMSCs) used in this study were approved ([2020]14) by the Research Ethics Committee of the Third Affiliated Hospital at Sun Yat-Sen University, Guangzhou, China. Donor information is listed in Table S1. Serum-free defined medium (yocon, NC0103 + NC0105.S) was used for HsMSCs culture, the medium was changed every 2–3 days. When cultures reached approximately 90% confluence, the cells would be dissociated by accutase (Thermo Fisher Scientific, A1110501) and passaged with a split ratio of 1:3. Typically, the proliferation ability of HsMSCs decreased gradually after passage 5 (p5) and showed an enlarged volume and flattened shape beyond passage 9 (p9). Thus, we defined “young” HsMSCs as cells between p3 and 5, and “old” HsMSCs as cells from p9 and beyond in this study. All cultures were incubated at 37°C with a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2.

Animals

Young (8–14 weeks) and old (more than 18-months old) male and female C57BL6/J mice were used in the present study, respectively purchased from Zhuhai BesTest Biotech Co.,Ltd (Guangdong, China) and Shanghai Yitong Biotech Co.,Ltd (Shanghai, China). All animals were housed at a 12-h light/12-h dark cycle in a specific-pathogen-free environment, and had free access to standard laboratory chow (Xietong Shengwu, 1,010,001) and water ad libitum. The animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Jennio Biotech Co., Ltd (JENNIO-IACUC-2023-A006).

Isolation of primary mouse hepatocytes

Primary hepatocyte isolation was performed using male wild-type C57BL6/J mice (8–12 weeks old) as previously described [80]. Briefly, mice underwent portal vein perfusion first with 0.5 mM EGTA (Sigma, E4378) at 6 mL/min for 3–4 min, then with 0.4 mg/mL collagenase IV (Sigma, C5138) at 4 mL/min for 7–8 min. All buffers were maintained at 37°C using a water bath. Following liver excision, tissues were gently dissociated by tearing and shaking to release cells. After centrifugation at 50 ×g for 3 min at 4°C 3 times and removal of the supernatant, the cells were suspended in DMEM/F12 medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific, C11330500BT), mixed gently with equal volume of 90% Percoll solution (Cytiva, 17–0891-09), and centrifuged at 200 g for 10 min at 4°C to remove non-viable cells. Subsequently, hepatocytes were resuspended in DMEM/F12 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibico, 10,099–141) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco, 15,140,122). Cells were cultured in a humidified incubator at 37°C and 5% CO2. The medium was replaced with serum-free medium 4–6 h later, and the cells were cultured overnight before use.

Lentivirus generation and infection

For time-lapse imaging of live cell expressing mCherry-GFP-LC3B (HsMSCs and mouse hepatocytes) and TP53BP1 reporters (HsMSCs), lentiviruses were generated by co-transfection of HEK293T cells with pCDH-CMV-mC-G-LC3B-P or Apple-53BP1trunc and the packaging vectors pMD2.G and psPAX2 (Table 2). To overexpress DRAM1, full-length DRAM1 cDNA was inserted into pLenti CMV GFP Puro vector (Table 2) linearized by BamHl and Sall digestion. For DRAM1 and PCYT2 knockdown, shRNA plasmids (Table S2) were purchased from IGE Biotechnology Co., Ltd or Tsingke Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Plasmids were transfected into HEK293T cells together with lentiviral packing vectors including pMD2.G and psPAX2. Culture media was changed 6–8 h post-transfection. Then, the cell culture medium was collected at 48–72 h post-transfection by centrifugation at 800 × g for 10 min at room temperature. Carefully transfer the supernatant into a new 15-mL tube, add 1 volume of 4 × Lentivirus Concentrator Solution (Dissolve 80 g PEG-8000 [Solarbio, P8260], 14.0 g NaCl [Macklin, S875253] in 80 mL MillQ water and 20 mL of 10 × PBS [Servicebio, G4207-500], adjust pH to 7.0 ~ 7.2 and the final volume to 200 mL) to 3 volumes of supernatant, mix well by shaking for 60 s and stand overnight at 4°C. Spin down at 1600 × g for 60 min at 4°C and the pellets (lentiviral particles) were gently resuspended in Opti-MEM medium (Gibco, 31,985,070), aliquot and store at −80°C before use. Transduction is typically performed in 6-well plates, for other plate formats, the lentivirus volume is adjusted proportionally based on the well surface area. Using the 6-well plate as a reference, 100 μL of 1 × 108 TU/mL lentivirus stock and 1 mL of Opti-MEM medium along with 10 μg/mL polybrene (MCE, HY-112735) were added to cells. 0.5 μg/mL puromycin (InvivoGen, ant-pr-1) was used for up to 3 days for post-transduction screening in this study. Longer selections with higher concentrations of puromycin were not possible due to the biological nature of the primary HsMSCs and mouse hepatocyte cultures. About 50–80% cells were estimated to be successfully transduced, depending on the specific vectors used.

Quantitative RT-PCR

The procedure of RNA extraction with Trizol (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 15,596–026) was conducted according to the manufacturer’s instructions. A NanoDrop OneC spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) was used to evaluate the purity and concentration of RNA. RNA was reverse transcribed using Prescript IIIRT ProMix (Vazyme, R323-01), and RT-qPCR was performed by using Ultra SYBR Green qPCR Mix (Vazyme, Q712-03) on a Light Cycler 480 Detection System (Roche, USA). The amount of mRNA was evaluated using 2−ΔΔCt method. qPCR primers listed in Table S2.

RNA sequencing

Cells and mouse liver tissues were collected and lysed in TRIzol (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 15,596–026), total RNAs were purified with RNeasy® Mini kit (Qiagen, 74,104), and further processed for mRNA purification using poly-T oligo-attached magnetic beads, and RNA-sequencing (Novogene, Beijing, China).

Clean data were obtained for downstream analysis by removing reads containing adapter and poly-N as well as low quality reads derived from raw data. Reference genome and gene model annotation files were downloaded from ensemble website directly. Using Hisat2 v2.0.5 [86], index of the reference genome was built and paired-end clean reads were aligned to the reference genome.

Gene expression levels were quantified separately for each sample. Only the reads aligned to exons with a mapping quality score larger than 10 were counted using the featureCounts v2.0.1 [87] package.

Differential expression analysis was performed by DESeq2 v1.26.0 [88] package using a model based on the negative binomial distribution. The resulting p-values were adjusted using the Benjamini and Hochberg’s approach for controlling the false discovery rate. Heatmap of genes were plotted using R, and pathway analysis were performed using the Metascape web interface [89].

Proteomics analysis

Sample preparation by SCASP lysis buffer [90]: Protein samples were lysed with SCASP buffer containing 1% sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS; Sigma, 71,725), 10 mM Tris(2-carboxyethyl)phosphine hydrochloride (TCEP; Sigma, C4706), 40 mM 2-chloroacetamide (CAA; Sigma, 22,790), and 100 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.5). Followed by 95°C for 10 min. The protein concentration was assayed with a BCA kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 23,250). (2-hydroxypropyl)-beta-cyclodextrin (HP-β-CD; Sangon, A600388) was added into the samples to trap SDS. About 30 μL 250 mM HP-β-CD were added into 100 μL SDS samples. Subsequently trypsin was added at protein:trypsin (Signalchem, T575-31N-1000) of 50:1. Digestion was performed at 37°C overnight.

Peptide desalting: Peptide desalting was performed using StageTips (CDS, LBRK-98–0604–0226-4EA) packed with SDB-RPS (poly[styrenedivinylbenzene] reverse phase sulfonate) material. The SDB-RPS StageTips were conditioned with 100 μL 100% acetonitrile (ACN; Sigma, 3485). The peptides were loaded into StageTips, followed by centrifugation at 4000 × g for 5 min. StageTips were washed once with 100 μL 1% trifluoroacetic acid (TFA; Sigma, T6508) in isopropyl alcohol (Sigma, I9030), then washed with 100 μL 0.2% TFA. Elution of peptides was performed using 80% ACN in 5% Ammonium hydroxide (NH3.H2O; Merck, 5,330,030,050). All eluted materials were collected in glass vials (CNW technologies, A3511040) and dried at 45°C using a SpeedVac centrifuge (Concentrator plus; Eppendorf, 5305). The dried peptides were dissolved in 1% TFA followed by SDB-RPS StageTip desalting again.

Orbitrap Fusion Lumos: The peptide samples were resolved in 2% acetonitrile, 0.1% formic acid (Sigma, F0507) for LC-MS analysis. The LC used was an EASY-nLC 1200 system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, San Jose, CA) Ultisil UHPLC Column XB-C18 (1.8 µm, 2.1 × 50 mm, H00201-11010). A 135-min LC separation was configured on the basis of the mix of buffer (0.1% formic acid in H2O) and buffer B (80% acetonitrile containing 0.1% formic acid). Buffer B was made to increase from 4 to 34% in 120 min, then to surge to 100% in 3 min, and then kept at 100% for 8 min. The LC-MS flow rate was kept at 300 nL/min. The Orbitrap Fusion Lumos Tribrid mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) instrument coupled to a nanoelectrospray ion source (NanoFlex, Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used. In brief, following MS settings were applied: 1 MS1 scan at 60 k resolution with an AGC target of 4 × 105 and max injection time of 50 ms in the range of 350 to 1200 Th, followed by 20 MS2 scans. The scan resolution in the Orbitrap was set to 30k with an AGC target of 1 × 106 and max injection time of 54 ms. The HCD collision energy was set to 32%.

Informatics protocol: DDA files including raw were searched with MSFragger (v4.1) [91] search engine which was integrated into FragPipe software (v22). DIA files (.raw) from phosphoproteomics in HeLa-S3 dataset were searched with MSFragger-DIA tool [92] and diaPASEF files from SCASP-Biotin dataset were analyzed with diaTracer tool [93]. Protein sequence databases Homo.sapiens (UP000005640) from Uniprot (reviewed sequences only; downloaded on 29 April 2024), including common contaminants and decoys, contained 40,958. Precursor and fragment mass tolerances were set to 10 ppm and 20 ppm for Orbitrap and 15 ppm and 0.05 Da for TimsTOF Pro. Enzyme specificity was set to “semi,” and up to two missed trypsin cleavages were allowed. Oxidation of methionine and acetylation of protein N-termini were set as variable modifications. Carbamidomethylation of cysteine was set as a fixed modification.

MSFragger output files (pepXML) were processed by MSBooster [94] or PeptideProphet [95] (for PNGase F based glycoproteomics) which was integrated into the Philosopher [96] toolkit. The resulting output files from MSBooster or PeptideProphet were processed using ProteinProphet [97] to assemble peptides into proteins. The protein groups assembled by ProteinProphet were filtered to 1% protein-level FDR using the picked FDR strategy, allowing unique and razor peptides.

Metabolomics analysis

The sample extracts were analyzed using an LC-ESI-MS/MS system (UPLC, ExionLC AD, Sciex, USA; MS, QTRAP, Sciex).

For hydrophilic compounds, the analytical conditions were as follows, UPLC: column, Waters ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 C18 (1.8 μm, 2.1 mm × 100 mm; 176,003,996); column temperature, 40°C; flow rate, 0.4 mL/min; injection volume, 2 μL; solvent system, water (0.1% formic acid): acetonitrile (0.1% formic acid); gradient program, 95:5 v:v at 0 min, 10:90 v:v at 11.0 min,10:90 v:v at 12.0 min, 95:5 v:v at 12.1 min, 95:5 v:v at 14.0 min. The ESI source operation parameters were as follows: source temperature 500°C; ion spray voltage (IS) 5500 V (positive), −4500 V (negative); ion source gas I (GSI), gas II (GSII), curtain gas (CUR) were set at 55, 60, and 25.0 psi, respectively; the collision gas (CAD) was high. Instrument tuning and mass calibration were conducted with 10 and 100 μmol/L polypropylene glycol solutions in QQQ and LIT modes, respectively. A specific set of MRM transitions were monitored for each period according to the metabolites eluted within this period.

For hydrophobic compounds, the analytical conditions included, UPLC: column, Thermo Accucore™ C30 (2.6 μm, 2.1 mm × 100 mm; 27,826-102130); solvent system, A: acetonitrile:water (60:40, v:v, 0.1% formic acid, 10 mmol/L ammonium formate), B: acetonitrile:isopropanol (10:90 v:v, 0.1% formic acid, 10 mmol/L ammonium formate); gradient program, A:B (80: 20, v:v) at 0 min, 70:30 v:v at 2.0 min, 40: 60 v:v at 4 min, 15: 85 v:v at 9 min, 10: 90 v:v at 14 min, 5: 95 v:v at 15.5 min, 5: 95 v:v at 17.3 min, 80: 20 v:v at 17.5 min, 80: 20 v:v at 20 min; flow rate, 0.35 mL/min; temperature, 45°C; Injection volume: 2 μL. The ESI source operation parameters were as follows: ion source, turbo spray; source temperature 500°C; ion spray voltage (IS) 5500 V (Positive), −4500 V (Negative); ion source gas 1 (GS1), gas 2 (GS2), curtain gas (CUR) were set at 45, 55, and 35 psi, respectively; the collision gas (CAD) was medium. Instrument tuning and mass calibration were performed with 10 and 100 μmol/L polypropylene glycol solutions in QQQ and LIT modes, respectively. QQQ scans were acquired as MRM experiments with collision gas (nitrogen) set to 5 psi. DP and CE for individual MRM transitions was done with further DP and CE optimization. A specific set of MRM transitions were monitored for each period according to the metabolites eluted within this period.

Based on the self-built target standard metware database (MWDB) (Metware Biotechnology Co., ltd., Wuhan, China), metabolites were identified by retention time and secondary spectrum data according to the standards hosted on the database curated by Metware Biotechnology Co., ltd.

Immunofluorescence

Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde/PFA (Biosharp, BL539A), 15–20 min at room temp, 1 × PBS (Servicebio, G4207-500) wash 2 times for 5 min each. Permeated with 0.5% Triton X-100 (Servicebio, GC204003) made in 1 × PBS for 5 min at room temp. 1 × PBS wash 3 times for 5 min each. Cells were incubated with primary antibodies (Table 1) overnight at 4°C, 0.05% Triton X-100 wash 3 times for 5 min each. Incubate with secondary antibodies (diluted in 1 × PBS; Table 1) for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. 1 × PBS wash 3 times for 5 min each, mount and seal the sample, then visualize the target antigen using a fluorescence microscope. Images were acquired with LSM880 confocal microscope (Zeiss, Germany) or Leica Stellaris confocal microscope (Leica Microsystems, Germany). Some images of stained cells were also visualized using the Operetta CLSTM High-Content Analysis System (PerkinElmer) in the confocal with two peaks of autofocus mode and a water objective lens (63 x), Primary antibodies are listed in Table 1.

Immunohistochemistry

For IHC, after antigen retrieval in EDTA (ZSGB, ZLI-9072), the slides were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C, followed by secondary antibodies (Dako, K5007) incubation at 37°C for 1 h. Sections were stained using diaminobenzidine (Dako, K5007) and hematoxylin (Servicebio, G1005-1), then viewed and imaged using a light microscope (Nikon, E100).

Immuno-FISH

Immuno-FISH was performed as previously described [43] with some modifications. Cells seeded in 8-well chamber slides were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Biosharp, BL539A) and incubated with γ-H2AX antibody (Table 1) overnight at 4°C as described above. After application of the secondary antibody (Table 1), cells were washed with PBS and FISH was performed. According to the manufacturer’s protocol, the samples were pretreated with 100 μg/mL RNase A solution (Solarbio, R1030) and 0.005% Pepsin (MCE, HY-P1635), and dehydration through a graded ethanol series. 20 µL hybridization buffer (60% formamide [Macklin, F809511]; 20 mM Na2HPO4, pH 7.4 [Sigma, V900061]; 20 mM Tris, pH 7.4 [Sigma, V900483]; 2 × SSC buffer [Promega, V4261]; 0.1 µg/mL salmon sperm DNA [Coolaber, CS9671]) containing 200 nM Cy3-labeled telomere specific (C3TA2)3 peptide nucleic acid (PNA) probe (Panagene, F1002) was applied to the cells followed by denaturation at 85°C 10 min, and hybridization for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. Cells were then washed twice with wash solution (2 × SSC buffer with 0.1% Tween-20 [Solarbio, T8220]) at 55–60°C 10 min, once at room temperature 1 min. Cells were incubated with DAPI, mounted and imaged using LSM880 confocal microscope with Ariyscan (Zeiss, Germany).

TUNEL assay

The apoptosis degree of HsMSCs was detected using a commercial detection kit (Promega, G3250) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, HsMSCs were permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100 (Servicebio, GC204003) for 10 min, followed by incubation in TUNEL reaction mixture in the dark for 60 min at 37°C, then stained with DAPI (Sigma, D9542) and observed under an LSM880 confocal microscope (Zeiss, Germany).

Quantification of N-acetylhistamine (N-AcHA)

For HsMSCs, 500 μL of 80% methanol was used to as extraction solution. After lysed and centrifugated, the supernatant of HsMSCs was lyophilized, the lyophilized samples were reconstituted in 80% methanol before LC-MS/MS analysis. For liver, heart, kidney, muscle and spleen, approximately 50 mg of tissue was homogenized in 1 mL of 70% methanol using cold steel balls at 60 Hz for 3 min. The homogenate was centrifuged at 15,000 × g at 4°C for 10 min. Subsequently, 400 μL of supernatant was collected and stored at −20°C overnight. After centrifugation at 15,000 × g at 4°C for 3 min, 100 μL of supernatant was subjected to LC-MS/MS analysis. For plasma samples, 100 μL of plasma were mixed with 300 μL of 100% methanol and incubated on ice for 15 min. Following centrifugation at 20,000 × g at 4°C for 3 min, 100 μL of supernatant was collected for N-AcHA quantification. N-AcHA standard (Sigma, 858,897) was prepared as 1 mg/mL stock solution in 100% methanol. Calibration curves were measured from 0.1 to 250 ng/mL. The sample extracts were analyzed using an LC-ESI-MS/MS system (LC: Agilent, 1290; MS: Agilent, 6470). The analytical conditions were as follows, UPLC: column, Waters ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 C18 (1.8 µm, 2.1 mm × 100 mm, 176,003,996); column temperature, 40°C; flow rate, 0.3 mL/min; injection volume, 2 μL; solvent system, water (0.1% formic acid): acetonitrile (0.1% formic acid); gradient program, 100:0 v:v at 0.50 min, 2:98 v:v at 7.50 min, 2:98 v:v at 10.00 min, 100:0 v:v at 10.01 min, post time 3 min. The ESI source operation parameters were as follows: sheath gas heater 350°C, sheath gas flow 12 L/min, capillary 3000 V in positive ion mode; The ion transitions were m/z 154.11→136.1, 95 for N-AcHA (collision Energy = 10, 20 V). The raw data from the mass spectrometer were analyzed by QQQ Quantitative Analysis (Agilent).

Determination of reduced glutathione (GSH) and oxidized glutathione (GSSG)

GSH and GSSG levels were determined using the GSH and GSSG Assay Kit (Beyotime, S0053) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, mouse liver tissues with or without N-AcHA treatment were homogenized, protein removal reagent M was used as lysis buffer. After diluted, different samples were added into two groups. One was for original GSSG determination, and the other was for total GSSG determination (original GSSG + GSSG after GSH oxidation). Total GSH = (Total GSSG – original GSSG) x 2. Standard solution of GSSG was prepared to generate standard curves. Colorimetric determination was conducted using a Multifunction microplate reader (Tecan, Switzerland).

Establishment of 2/3 partial hepatectomy model (2/3PHx)

2/3PHx in mice was performed as previously described [98]. In brief, mice were anesthetized by inhalation of 2% isoflurane (RWD, R510-22) before conducting a midline abdominal incision. Then, using 4–0 silk (Ethicon, SA83G), left lateral lobe and median lobe removed separately by ligaturing related vessels and bile ducts. According to the experimental outline, mice received intraperitoneally injection of designated drug or vehicle control, and liver samples and plasma were collected at different timepoints after PHx.

Grip strength test in mice

The mouse grip strength was measured at the beginning of the experiment (day 0), and on day14 (2 weeks) after N-AcHA (Sigma, 858,897) supplementation. A Grip Strength Meter (San Liang, SF-20) was used to measure forelimbs and hind limbs grip strength. The mouse was placed on the top of the grip strength meter. As a mouse grasped the grid, the peak pull force was recorded on a digital force transducer. The mouse was pulled along the direction of the grid at a constant rate until the grip strength meter was released by the mouse. This process was repeated for 10 times with 1 min interval between each time. The mean of the values of the trials excluding the maximum and minimum ones were recorded as the grip strength of each mouse.

Mouse indirect calorimetry

A standard 12-h light/12-h dark cycle was maintained throughout the calorimetry measurement. Mice were placed in metabolic cages (Columbus, CLAMS-HC) located in the Third Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-Sen University in a temperature- and humidity-controlled housing room. After 24 h adaptation, data were recorded at 8:00 a.m. the next day, with the ambient temperature set at 25°C and humidity set at 50%. N-AcHA (Sigma, 858,897) treatment were conducted according to the schematic diagram presented in the related figures. After long-term N-AcHA treatment, mice underwent 24 h indirect calorimetry .

In vitro and in vivo N-AcHA treatment

For N-AcHA treatment on HsMSCs or mouse primary hepatocytes were cultured in medium supplemented with 20, 100, 500 μM and 3 mM N-AcHA (MCE, HY-112175 or Sigma, 858,897) during the entire experiments. The cells of the control group were cultured in medium without N-AcHA. SA-GLB1/β-gal staining assay were conducted after 48–120 h with N-AcHA treatment on HsMSCs or mouse hepatocytes.

For 2/3 partial hepatectomy model with N-AcHA (Sigma, 858,897), mice were treated with N-AcHA (40 or 200 mg/kg i.p.) every day for 5 days, blood and tissues were harvested 48 h after hepatectomy following N-AcHA administration at 2 h pre-PHx and 24 h post-PHx. For mice long-term N-AcHA treatment, 14–16-month-old mice were treated with N-AcHA every day (40 mg/kg i.p.) for 7 weeks.

In vitro treatments with metabolites or drugs

For etoposide (ETO; MCE, HY-13629), methanesulfonic acid (Macklin, M813392), ethanolamine (Eth; Aladdin, E103809), BafA1 (MCE, HY-100558) treatments, drugs were used following manufacturer’s instruction, working concentration and duration were annotated on the related figures and legends.

SA-GLB1//β-gal (senescence-associated galactosidase beta 1) staining assay

SA-GLB1/β-gal activity of HsMSCs and mouse livers was measured using Senescence β-galactosidase Staining Kit (Beyotime, C0602) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.
