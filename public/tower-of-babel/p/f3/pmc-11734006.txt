Cardiac Surgery, Interventional Cardiology, and the Sustainable Development Agenda
Authors: Dominique Vervoort
Published: 2024

Background

Cardiovascular diseases constitute the leading cause of morbidity and mortality in high- and low- and middle-income countries. Globally, over 20 million people die from cardiovascular disease each year, with over 80% of deaths taking place in low- and middle-income countries.1 An estimated one-third to one-half of all cardiovascular pathologies will require surgical or interventional care at least once in their lifetime; nevertheless, 6 billion people globally—over 90% of the population in low- and middle-income countries—cannot access procedural care when needed.2 The reasons underlying global gaps in cardiovascular care capacities are multifold but largely driven by a lack of political prioritization and policy inclusion at the national and international level.3 Without political buy-in and integration into budding or existing health policies, the implementation of cardiovascular care capacity, if any, will remain fragmented. To ensure sustainable and effective health systems, cardiovascular care cannot be omitted.4

In May 2024, the 77th World Health Assembly (WHA) took place in Geneva, Switzerland, with the theme of “All for Health, Health for All.” Despite a large presence of the global surgical and cardiology communities, representation of global cardiac surgery was lacking, instead resulting in “Some for Health, Health for Some.”

National and international health policy

Health policymaking occurs at the national and international level. Nationally, 8 countries have developed National Surgical, Obstetric, and Anesthesia Plans (NSOAPs) to date, which serve as longitudinal policy plans to strengthen surgical systems.3 These NSOAPs are generally embedded within national health plans to ensure integration within broader health policies and avoid siloing of individual diseases or health care sections. Only 1 NSOAP (Zambia) specifically mentioned cardiovascular health and cardiac surgical care needs, despite clear benefits of cardiac surgical care on all aspects of health systems.5 Without explicit inclusion—and thus health planning and budgeting—implemented plans will strengthen surgical systems without cardiovascular pillars, eventually leaving a gap that requires many years to be bridged considering workforce training needs.

Ministries of Health (and other national agencies) generally follow recommendations at the international level, whether through bilateral and multilateral agreements between nations or aligned with intergovernmental organizations, such as the World Health Organization and United Nations. In May each year, the World Health Organization convenes all affiliated Ministries of Health and partner organizations and institutions at the WHA in Geneva, Switzerland, to discuss pressing global health concerns. In the past years, important global surgical (WHA68.15 on surgical care and anesthesia in 2015 and WHA76.2 on integrated emergency, critical, and operative care in 2023) and cardiovascular (WHA71.14 on rheumatic heart disease in 2018) resolutions were adopted.4 However, none mentioned cardiac surgical or interventional care. During the coming years, the noncommunicable disease agenda is placed more at the forefront as noncommunicable disease burdens continue to grow across the globe.4 More broadly, each September, the United Nations hosts the United Nations General Assembly in New York, United States, to discuss health and non-health matters with state leaders, delegations, and other organizations. Through this forum, the Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) Agenda for 2030 was developed. Cardiac surgical and interventional care is closely linked to 15 of the 17 SDGs, yet generally left out of the SDG discourse.6

Policy engagement for heart teams

There is a clear continuum between research, clinical practice, advocacy, and health policy (Figure 1). At each level, individuals may engage to ultimately promote inclusion of global cardiac surgery and interventional cardiology in national and international health policy.

Policy Engagement for Heart Teams

Strengthening the role of cardiac surgery and interventional cardiology as part of the Sustainable Development Goal agenda requires integration within current cardiovascular and global surgical policy movements. This, in turn, requires recognition of and engagement with different levels of the continuum between research, clinical practice, advocacy, and health policy by Heart Team members.

Global health and research funding drives academia and resulting health policy decision-making. For example, approximately half of all global health funding is allocated to HIV/AIDS, malaria, and tuberculosis, compared to <2% to noncommunicable diseases, <1% to global surgery, and a very small fraction to rheumatic heart disease.7 Research output, based on PubMed, matches these trends, whereas disease burdens do not (ie, a disproportionate focus on the first 3 conditions). Professional societies and funders may expand research funds to study health care disparities, whereas cardiovascular health care professionals and researchers may hold countries, institutions, and grant-issuing bodies accountable for research funding proportionate to global and local needs.

Contextual research on clinical practice may further optimize practice while benefitting practice elsewhere. Current trials are rooted in Caucasian/White male populations, whereas guidelines predominantly stem from and are developed based on evidence from high-income countries.8 Ensuring more representative research originating from around the globe, clinical practice can be optimized for all patients. In addition, health systems research involves an assessment of the current capacities and met and unmet needs in cardiovascular care delivery. Nationally, an understanding of the cardiovascular care capacity can inform health planning in terms of workforce, infrastructure, and budgeting. For example, Forcillo et al developed a country-level needs assessment tool for cardiac surgery that has been piloted in Namibia, Uganda, and Zambia.9 Regionally, needs assessments and budget impact analyses may empower governments to explore collaboration between countries in developing higher-volume centers. Regionalization of care results in lower perprocedure costs due to higher volumes (purchasing at economies of scale), improved outcomes, and greater efficiency. Initial models of regionalization have included transborder collaboration, such as the Salam Center in Khartoum, Sudan, providing free surgical care for patients across the sub-Saharan African subcontinent and supporting training of non-Sudanese African cardiac surgeons. Similarly, the Tenwek Hospital in Bomet County, Kenya, has become a cardiac surgical training hub in East Africa, including the training of nonsurgical Heart Team members. Globally, quantifying the met and unmet cardiac surgical and interventional needs, including the necessary workforce, infrastructure, and costs, can help guide policymakers to determine the prioritization and potential socioeconomic benefit of investing in cardiovascular capacity. For example, economic modeling has shown that the African Union can benefit USD $2.8 billion by 2030 by scaling secondary and tertiary care for rheumatic heart disease.3 Targets have recently been developed to guide integration of cardiac surgical planning in policy plans and may be iteratively updated or expanded as further data become available.10 In particular, gaps in knowledge still remain with regard to current procedural volumes and training capacities by country, the cost-effectiveness and cost-utility of different cardiac surgical and interventional procedures across settings, and cardiovascular supply chains and patient-centered care and outcomes in low- and middle-income countries, among other topics.

Advocacy, drawing upon research as evidence-based messaging, is an often overlooked but nonetheless critical pillar to changing global cardiovascular realities. Grabbing a seat at the table and ensuring inclusion of global cardiac surgery terminology in the global health discourse ensures both consideration during policymaking and policy implementation as well as priming of policymakers, funders, and other actors when not directly engaging with cardiovascular stakeholders.4 Various organizations are active in the space of advocacy and health policy for global surgical care and global cardiovascular health. Surgically, the Global Surgery Foundation (GSF) is an independent nongovernmental organization based in Geneva and collaborating with governments, intergovernmental, and nongovernmental organizations, academic centers, professional societies, and funders and industry. The GSF focuses on equipment and infrastruture, education and training, policy development, and research with clinical foci on maternal health, pediatric surgical care, injury and trauma surgery, and cancer surgery. Here, care for pregnant women with cardiac surgical conditions (eg, living with rheumatic heart disease), congenital heart surgery, and cardio-aortic and cardio-oncology care have immediate strategic entrypoints. In recent years, the GSF has established the Value-Based Healthcare Hub, Global Surgery Learning Hub (SURGhub), and SURGfund in collaboration with the United Nations Institute for Training and Research to, respectively, prioritize value in global health, centralize open-access educational resources for surgical care delivery, and pool funds to strengthen surgical systems. The cardiovascular community may support these efforts through ensuring inclusion of specialty-specific educational materials as well as emphasizing the importance of value-based health care in global cardiovascular care. Furthermore, the Global Alliance for Surgery, Obstetric, Trauma, and Anaesthesia Care (The G4 Alliance) represents over 70 organizations in the field of global surgery in order to create one common voice for surgical advocacy at the global scale. Currently, none of the member organizations specifically represent cardiovascular surgery, providing an opportunity for increased engagement. In terms of cardiovascular health, the World Heart Federation is the leading body acknowledged by and partnering with the World Health Organization, and represents over 200 organizations in over 100 countries. The World Heart Federation’s engagement in the space of global (cardiac) surgery has been limited to the support of societal efforts but may be extended to better integrate and strengthen the continuum of cardiovascular care. Similarly, the Global Alliance for Rheumatic and Congenital Hearts (Global ARCH) convenes nearly 100 patient-family organizations for congenital heart disease and rheumatic heart disease worldwide, with approximately half located in low- and middle-income countries. Accordingly, Global ARCH provides a growing platform for engagement of patients and families in the global cardiovascular discourse.

Conclusions

Gaps in global cardiac surgical and interventional care remain vast despite growing cardiovascular disease burdens around the world. The lack of integration of cardiovascular care, particularly surgical and interventional care, in national and international health policy is partially a result of limited advocacy by the cardiac surgical and interventional community at the health system and global health level. Various opportunities arise to better embed cardiovascular surgical and interventional care in the global surgical and global health agenda, thereby accelerating work toward the SDGs.
