Transcriptomic validation of a 7,12 Dimethylbenz(a)anthracene (DMBA)‐induced leukemia rat model: Parallels with human leukemogenesis
Authors: Khaldoon Alsamman, Omar S El‐Masry
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundExperimental animal models are essential for understanding the molecular mechanisms of human leukemia and testing potential therapies. Chemical induction using 7,12‐dimethylbenz[a]anthracene (DMBA) has shown promise in recapitulating features of leukemogenesis in rodents, but its molecular fidelity to human disease remains underexplored. The aim of this study was to evaluate the transcriptomic landscape of a DMBA‐induced leukemia rat model and assess its alignment with known gene expression signatures and oncogenic pathways in human leukemia.MethodsMale Wistar rats were treated with DMBA to induce leukemia. RNA was extracted from whole blood and subjected to RNA sequencing. Differentially expressed genes (DEG) were identified using RNA‐seq by expectation–maximization and NOISeq. Functional enrichment analysis, Gene Ontology mapping, and protein interaction networks were analyzed via STRING. Prognostic relevance of key DEGs was explored using data from the Human Protein Atlas.ResultsThe DMBA‐induced model showed significant upregulation of leukemia‐associated genes, including FLT3, NRAS, KRAS, ABL1, BCR, and NPM1. The top 10 up‐ and downregulated genes were enriched in pathways related to cell fate, proliferation, apoptosis, and immune regulation. Particularly, genes such as MEIS2, CXCL9, and WIF1 were linked to hematopoietic dysregulation and poor prognosis in various cancers. Comparative analysis revealed strong transcriptomic overlap with human leukemia/carcinogenesis gene signatures, supporting the model's translational validity.ConclusionIn conclusion, the DMBA‐induced leukemia rat model exhibits a transcriptomic profile that closely mirrors key oncogenic and prognostic features of human leukemia. This model holds significant promise for preclinical studies targeting leukemogenesis and therapeutic intervention strategies.Keywords: 7,12‐dimethylbenz[a]anthracene (DMBA); gene expression; leukemia; RNA‐seq; transcriptomics

INTRODUCTION

The development of experimental animal models for human diseases is important in preclinical research for understanding the pathophysiological changes in these diseases and investigating the therapies‐to‐be agents. Rat models of human neoplasms are more suitable than smaller murine due to their genomic similarities to humans and their larger surface area, which enables easier intervention, sampling, and detection of small neoplastic lesions. 1 The molecular basis, treatment response, and validation and fate of leukemia has been studied in rat models that were induced via viral infections, irradiation, or chemicals (reviewed in Arató et al. 1 ). We have recently demonstrated chemical induction of acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) in male Wistar rats using 7,12‐dimethylbenz[a]‐anthracene (DMBA). 2 Another rat model of acute myeloid leukemia (AML) was also described in an article by Steinskog and colleagues. 3 Moreover, rat models of leukemia, including DMBA induced, were also used to investigate the efficacy of therapeutic interventions. 4 , 5 Additionally, a recent study demonstrated a DMBA‐induced leukemia model in which the expression of selected genes was altered, including KRAS and p53. 6 In this context, N‐ras mutations were reported as a key contributor to leukemogenesis induced by DMBA. These N‐ras mutations are thought to be involved in the initiation phase of leukemogenesis as they are detected in the premalignant stage. Trisomy of chromosome 2 was also evident in this DMBA‐induced model of leukemia. 7 Whole transcriptome profiling provides a single‐step approach to explore gene expression signatures in chemically induced animal models. Using this data, the aim of this study was to analyze the whole transcriptome of the DMBA‐induced leukemic rat model 2 to evaluate differential gene expression. This was performed to assess the validity of this induced model to mimic human leukemogenesis.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This cross‐sectional, laboratory‐based study was reviewed and approved by the institutional review board (lRB‐2017‐03‐147) and the Animal Care and Use Committee of Imam Abdulrahman Bin Faisal University. DMBA was used to induce leukemia in male Wistar rats as described in our previous publication. 2 Briefly, DMBA was mixed with sesame oil and administered through oral intubation (1.5 mg twice a week). Sesame oil was administered to the corresponding control rats at the same frequency. Rats were observed by the appointed veterinarian to identify any physical changes. Dose‐limiting toxicities included lesions, masses or unusual physical signs, petechiae, or onset of leukemia. The definition of dose‐limiting toxicities meant to exclude unnecessary overdosing of DMBA.

Blood collection and RNA extraction

Controlled inhalation of oxygen/sevoflurane (SEVOrane, 4456, AbbVie, IL, USA) mixture was used to anesthetize rats, and whole blood was collected from the tail vein in PAXgene blood RNA tubes (762165, PreAnalytiX, Hombrechtikon, Switzerland) to preserve RNA until extraction. RNA was then extracted using the PAXgene Blood RNA Kit (762 164, PreAnalytiX).

RNA sequencing

RNA integrity, concentration, and purity were assessed using the Agilent Bioanalyzer system (Agilent Technologies, Waldbronn, Germany); then RNA was sequenced as previously described by our team. 8 Gene expression levels were estimated using RNA‐seq by expectation–maximization. 9 Differentially expressed genes (DEG) were screened using NOIseq algorithms, 10 with log2 fold change values ≥1 and a probability of 80% or greater indicating gene upregulation, and log2 fold change values ≤ −1 and a probability of 80% or greater indicating downregulation compared to the corresponding control (expression level was considered statistically significant at p‐ and Q‐values ≤ 0.05). Figure 1 shows the steps involved in RNA sequencing from sample extraction to bioinformatics.

Experimental procedures and bioinformatics road map. The figure shows a stepwise presentation of methodological approaches starting with blood sample collection from DMBA (7,12‐dimethylbenz[a]anthracene)–treated animals using RNA sequencing through identification of candidate genes. RSEM (RNA‐seq by expectation–maximization) 9 was used for gene quantification, and NOIseq algorithms 10 were used to define the differentially expressed genes (upregulation: Log2 fold change ≥1 and a probability ≥80%, downregulation: Log2 fold change ≤ −1 and a probability ≥80%). Figure was created using Napkin AI platform.

Gene ontology and prognostic value of candidate genes in human cancers

Gene names and symbols were validated using Rat Genome Database (https://rgd.mcw.edu). 11 Gene Ontology (GO) terms for DEGs were retrieved from the STRING database portal (https://string‐db.org) by searching using gene symbols and Rattus norvegicus as organism. GO terms, including “biological process,” “molecular function,” and “cellular component,” along with protein–protein interaction network are summarized; co‐expression and gene correlation analysis are presented with significance level and confidence scores.

To investigate the prognostic values of candidate genes in human cancers and the validity of the induced model to resemble human malignancies, the Human Protein Atlas database (https://www.proteinatlas.org) was used to retrieve survival analysis data available for the candidate genes.

Transcriptomic validation of candidate genes in human leukemia datasets

To validate the expression of the candidate genes in human leukemia datasets, the Tumor, Normal and Metastatic (TNM) portal 12 was explored to investigate the differential gene expression between normal and malignant specimens. Additionally, the Gene Expression Profiling Interactive Analysis platform 13 was used to construct survival analysis plots of the candidate genes in human acute leukemia dataset.

RESULTS

DMBA induced overexpression of BCR, ABL1, and ABL2. Also, PML, RARA, FLT3, NPM1, CEBPA, KRAS, and NRAS were among the upregulated genes, in addition to other reported leukemic gene signatures. This gene expression profile that was induced by DMBA suggests the potential of this chemical model to mimic the expression profile of human leukemia.

GO studies

Analysis of GO data indicated that several DMBA‐regulated genes in the induced model have functions and interaction networks thar are directly related to the control of the cell fate, differentiation, and cell proliferation and growth. For example, the biological processes in which GPC3 is involved include cell fate commitment and the canonical Wnt signaling pathway (also known as Wnt/β‐catenin signaling pathway). β‐Catenin is stabilized in the canonical Wnt signaling pathway and promotes cell proliferation, survival, and migration, processes that are directly associated with carcinogenesis. Also, the molecular functions of PDX1 involve interaction with AMP‐activated protein kinase and cAMP‐activated protein kinase (protein kinase A), thereby exerting direct influence on cellular metabolism, cell cycle, gene expression, and cell differentiation. In addition, CXCL9, another DMBA‐upregulated gene, regulates cell proliferation and migration of leukocytes, regulates the Mitogen‐Activated protein Kinase (MAPK) pathway, and negatively regulates apoptosis of leukocytes and macrophages. This suggests a direct effect of DMBA on bone marrow and leukocyte production and migration. Moreover, IMPAD1 is a reported risk factor in breast cancer, whose ontology terms are not available at STRING portal. Additionally, WIF1 is involved in several biological processes that are related to cell fate determination, differentiation, and survival signaling pathways, including bone morphogenic protein signaling and Wnt signaling and regulation of growth. In the context of the downregulated genes, APH1B is involved in many biological processes associated with survival and proliferation of cells, including regulation of neuron apoptosis, cell fate specification, cell fate commitment, epithelial cell proliferation, leukocyte activation, and apoptotic process. HMGN1 functions in positive regulation of megakaryocyte differentiation and DNA binding. Prognostic values of the candidate genes in human cancer in human protein atlas

RAB1B, a DMBA‐induced upregulated gene, has been reported as an unfavorable marker for lung adenocarcinoma and a favorable marker for renal clear cell carcinoma according to Human Protein Atlas survival studies. Similarly, GPC3 and M6PR have been reported as unfavorable markers for stomach adenocarcinoma and hepatocellular carcinoma, respectively. Moreover, CXCL9 has been reported as a favorable prognostic marker in breast invasive carcinoma. Additionally, IMPAD1 (also known as BPNT2) has been reported as an unfavorable prognostic marker in breast invasive carcinoma and chromophobe renal cell carcinoma. WIF1, on the contrary, is a favorable marker in uterine corpus endometrial carcinoma. In addition, MEIS2 has been reported as a prognostic marker in several types of cancers. For example, MEIS2 has been reported as a favorable marker in cervical squamous cell carcinoma, renal clear cell carcinoma, and bladder urothelial carcinoma, whereas it has been reported as an unfavorable marker in renal papillary cell carcinoma and thyroid carcinoma. Human Protein Atlas data showed that CTSG is a favorable marker in lung adenocarcinoma and renal clear cell carcinoma. Last on the list of the upregulated genes, RETREG1 has been reported as a favorable marker in renal clear cell carcinoma and renal papillary cell carcinoma. All survival analyses and prognostic values of candidate genes can be found at “cancer” tab of each reported gene that can be retrieved from the Human Protein Atlas 14 by searching using the gene symbol.

With respect to the DMBA‐downregulated genes, APH1B has been reported as a favorable marker in renal clear cell carcinoma and renal papillary cell carcinoma, whereas DALRD3 has been reported as a favorable marker in renal cell papillary carcinoma. HMGN1 is a favorable marker in uterine corpus endometrial carcinoma but an unfavorable marker in hepatocellular carcinoma, lung adenocarcinoma, and chromophobe renal cell carcinoma. Additionally, HSPA1A has been reported in the Human Protein Atlas database as an unfavorable marker in ovary serous cystadenocarcinoma and pancreatic adenocarcinoma. CPNE1 has also been reported as an unfavorable marker in pancreatic adenocarcinoma. GUCY2C and RTKN have been reported as unfavorable markers in hepatocellular carcinoma and head and neck cancer, respectively.

Kaplan–Meier curves for survival analyses data are provided in the supplementary file and were retrieved from the individual gene pages of the Human Protein Atlas 14 (Figures S1–S9 for the upregulated genes and Figures S10–S16 for the downregulated genes).

These results revealed several DMBA‐regulated genes that could mediate its carcinogenic potential. The roles and functions of these genes that are reported in the scientific literature will be discussed in the following section to highlight scientific evidence on the validity of the proposed DMBA‐induced model.

Transcriptomic validation of candidate genes in human leukemia genomic datasets

For further validation of the study findings and to ensure the potential imitation of the animal model to human leukemogenesis, datasets from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) genomics portal were analyzed for differential gene expression between patients with leukemia and healthy individuals. Selected datasets of leukemia were selected based on their availability in the used analysis portals. The TNM portal 12 was used to analyze DEGs in acute lymphoblastic leukemia patients (the tumor that was induced in our model) compared to healthy individuals. ALL gene expression data were obtained from the TCGA's ALL3 TARGET dataset. The results revealed that M6PR (mean fold change value = 1.54), PDX1 (mean fold change value = 170.14), IMPAD1 (BPNT2, mean fold change value = 4.14), MEIS2 (mean fold change value = 21.99), and RETREG1 (mean fold change value = 3.74) were significantly upregulated as shown in the box plots in Figure 4 for each gene. On the contrary, RAB1B, GPC3, CXCL9, WIF1, and CTSG were significantly downregulated.

Tumor, Normal and Metastatic (TNM) platform box plots comparing gene expression status in acute lymphoblastic leukemia and healthy subjects. The figure shows the expression of the upregulated genes reported in the present study in ALL patients (red) compared to healthy subjects, including adults and children. M6PR, PDX1, IMPAD1 (BPNT2), MEIS2, and RETREG1 were significantly upregulated in tumor samples, whereas RAB1B, GPC3, CXCL9, WIF1, and CTSG were significantly downregulated.

In addition, analysis of RNA sequencing data of AML patients in the “TARGET” dataset showed that the upregulated candidate genes that we reported in the proposed rat model were differentially expressed between AML and normal subjects. In AML patients, CTSG, CXCL9, GPC3, IMPAD1 (BPTN2), MEIS2, and PDX1 were significantly upregulated compared to the normal cohort analyzed (mean fold change values = 4.27, 2.49, 8.08, 2.9, 15.48, and 1.52, respectively). Meanwhile, M6PR, RAB1B, RETREG1, and WIF1 were significantly downregulated in AML patients (mean fold change values = 0.98, 0.54, 0.43, and 0.93, respectively) (Figure 5).

Moreover, the Gene Expression Profiling Interactive Analysis platform 13 was used to construct survival plots of the upregulated genes of leukemia datasets from TCGA and GTEx projects. Analysis of the available acute myeloid leukemia cohort data (LAML dataset) revealed that CXCL9, GPC3, IMPAD1 (BPTN2), M6PR, RAB1B, and RETREG1 (shown as FAM134B) are unfavorable prognostic markers in AML patients, where higher expression of these genes is associated with lower survival probabilities as indicated by hazard ratios >1; however, this finding was not statistically significant. On the contrary, CTSG and MEIS2 were found to be favorable markers in AML as their high expression is associated with better survival probabilities and lower hazard ratios; however, the finding was also not statistically significant (survival plots for PDX1 and WIF1 genes could not be extracted due to sample size limitations) (Figure 6).

DISCUSSION

This study aimed to evaluate the transcriptomic fidelity of a 7,12‐dimethylbenz[a]anthracene (DMBA)‐induced leukemia rat model compared to established genomic signatures in human leukemogenesis. In relation to this report, we have shown previously that DMBA induced acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) and other tumors. 2 The comprehensive gene expression profiling and GO analyses supported the hypothesis that the DMBA model closely mimics key features of acute leukemias, particularly in relation to the gene expression profile.

Transcriptomic data revealed significant upregulation of canonical leukemia‐related genes, including FLT3, NRAS, KRAS, ABL1, BCR, NPM1, and CEBPA, which are widely associated with human AML and acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL). 15 The expression of FLT3, a tyrosine kinase receptor mutated or overexpressed in approximately 30% of AML cases, has been consistently linked to poor prognosis and increased proliferation. 16 , 17 In our model, FLT3 expression was significantly elevated, suggesting an analogous leukemogenic mechanism. Supporting this, Issa et al. and Gentles et al. 18 , 19 identified FLT3 among key genes in leukemic stem cell (LSC) signatures predictive of minimal residual disease and clinical outcomes.

The RAS pathway was tentatively altered in the DMBA model, with upregulation of NRAS and KRAS, which are implicated in leukemogenic transformation through enhancement of uncontrolled proliferation and suppression of apoptosis. 20 , 21 , 22 RAS mutations, such as NRAS^G12D and KRAS^G13D, were shown to accelerate AML progression and impair therapeutic response in vivo. 23 , 24 In addition, mutations of this oncogene have been linked to acute lymphoblastic leukemia. 25 This aligns with our findings of robust RAS overexpression, positioning the DMBA‐induced model as a valuable system for studying targeted inhibitors, such as Mitogen‐Activated Protein Kinase Kinase (MEK) inhibitors.

Among the upregulated genes, MEIS2 and CXCL9 also surfaced as significant. MEIS family genes, particularly MEIS1/2, are regulators of hematopoietic differentiation and LSC self‐renewal. 26 , 27 Their overexpression has been associated with unfavorable outcomes in certain AML subtypes and ALL. 28 , 29 , 30 The chemokine CXCL9, although known for its role in immune cell recruitment, may also influence leukemic niche dynamics, consistent with studies highlighting the microenvironment's role in AML and ALL pathophysiology. 31 , 32

Furthermore, ontology mapping demonstrated perturbation of multiple cancer‐associated pathways, including Wnt/β‐catenin signaling, MAPK cascade, and apoptotic regulation. Genes like WIF1, a Wnt inhibitor, and PDX1, a homeobox gene involved in transcriptional regulation, were upregulated. These findings suggest possible secondary mechanisms of transformation involving stem cell plasticity and immune evasion, which have been reported in acute leukemia biology. 33 , 34 , 35

The downregulated genes in our model APH1B, HSPA1A, and HMGN1, among others, are also notable. HMGN1 is a nucleosome‐binding protein with roles in chromatin remodeling, and its loss may contribute to leukemic plasticity or epigenetic deregulation. 36 HSPA1A, a heat shock protein, has been linked to apoptotic resistance, and its suppression in our model may reflect compensatory cellular stress mechanisms.

Importantly, many of the DEGs identified in our study have established prognostic value in human AML cohorts, as highlighted in large‐scale profiling studies. 37 , 38 Integration of these genes into predictive models has improved stratification and informed treatment decisions, particularly for high‐risk subtypes. 17 , 39

Despite the compelling evidence of relevance, limitations exist. The model is chemical and systemic, unlike genetic or patient‐derived xenografts, which may more precisely reflect mutational landscapes. Furthermore, transcriptomic data alone do not provide insights into protein‐level regulation or posttranscriptional modifications, which are critical in leukemia pathogenesis. 40 , 41 Functional validation through proteomic assays, lineage tracing, and drug‐response profiling is needed to consolidate our findings.

In conclusion, the DMBA‐induced leukemia rat model faithfully recapitulates several transcriptomic, ontological, and prognostic features of human acute leukemia. This includes dysregulation of key oncogenes, pathways implicated in leukemic stemness and proliferation, and biomarker profiles associated with clinical outcomes. These insights position the model as a viable preclinical tool for studying leukemogenesis and testing novel therapeutics in acute leukemia and perhaps other malignancies.

LIMITATIONS

The results presented in this article were the conclusions of a small study sample. Additional gene expression validation using quantitative polymerase chain reaction and translational data at the protein level might be required in future studies involving a larger sample size to validate transcriptome data.
