Active protein-capturing hydrogel for broad-spectrum pathogen defense
Authors: Jiaming Liu, Shiqi Hu, Mengrui Liu, Savannah Weihang Zhang, Na Yan, Xuan Mei, Dashuai Zhu, Ming Shen, Ke Cheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Proteinaceous agents, including viral particles and allergenic proteins, play central roles in infection, inflammation, and immune dysregulation, yet few materials can broadly neutralize them through direct protein capture. Here, we present an inhalable protein trap (IPT), an active hydrogel that rapidly immobilizes diverse amine-containing biomolecules via NHS-amine chemistry. Structural optimization enables IPT to stably incorporate a high density of reactive NHS esters that remain functional under physiological conditions, allowing efficient protein capture at mucosal surfaces. Upon hydration, powdery IPT rapidly forms an active barrier that covalently traps proteins and proteinaceous particles within minutes. This nonselective capture mechanism allows IPT to bind viral particles, allergens released from pollen or fungi, and soluble immune mediators such as histamine. Across multiple animal models, including allergic rhinitis, pollen-induced allergy, and viral infection, IPT consistently outperformed commercial barrier sprays in blocking pathogenic proteins and mitigating disease progression. These results establish IPT as a versatile platform for broad-spectrum protection at biological interfaces.

INTRODUCTION

Proteins and protein-containing agents, such as viruses, allergens, inflammatory cytokines, and bacterial toxins, are key mediators of host-pathogen and host-environment interactions, and often act as triggers in the initiation and progression of a wide range of human diseases, including infection, inflammation, and immune dysregulation (1–4). These agents often act at biological interfaces such as mucosal, dermal, or vascular barriers, where they engage in direct protein-protein interactions to either activate host responses or penetrate the barrier, thereby initiating disease processes (5–7). For example, respiratory viruses exploit envelope proteins to bind epithelial receptors and initiate infection (8–10), while pollen and fungal spores release allergenic proteins that stimulate type 2 immune cascades through epithelial alarmin signaling (11–13). These disease mediators often act before adaptive immune responses are fully activated, making early-stage neutralization at the site of exposure an attractive strategy for intervention (14, 15). While vaccination or immunosuppressants can address downstream inflammation, few strategies directly target the initiating protein agents in a broad and durable manner.

Many existing materials have been designed to block proteinaceous pathogens. Affinity-based approaches, including monoclonal antibodies, aptamers, and engineered protein scaffolds, provide molecular specificity but require prior knowledge of target structure, are prone to loss of efficacy due to mutation or polymorphism, and are often limited by high cost and complex manufacturing procedures (16–21). Molecularly imprinted polymers offer synthetic alternatives but typically suffer from low binding kinetics and limited in vivo compatibility (22, 23). Passive barrier materials such as cellulose filters, hydrogels, and nasal sprays provide a physical blockade against particulates, but they lack biochemical reactivity and cannot neutralize soluble proteins that diffuse through the matrix (24–26). Moreover, interactions in many of these systems are reversible or reliant on electrostatics, which may result in pathogen release under variable physiological conditions, including shifts in pH or ionic strength (27). These limitations highlight the need for broadly reactive materials that enable irreversible protein capture with low production cost and ease of fabrication.

Here, we introduce a covalently reactive hydrogel platform, termed the inhalable protein trap (IPT), designed to nonselectively bind amine-containing biomolecules via N-hydroxysuccinimide (NHS)–amine chemistry (28, 29). Upon hydration at mucosal surfaces, IPT rapidly forms an adhesive hydrogel layer that captures a broad spectrum of proteinaceous agents within 5 min under physiological conditions, including multiple types of viral particles, fungal and pollen-derived allergens, and soluble immune mediators such as histamine (Fig. 1). This covalent capture mechanism enables both prophylactic and postexposure intervention, offering advantages over traditional barriers by irreversibly neutralizing pathogenic proteins at the site of exposure. IPT demonstrated superior efficacy across various in vivo models, reducing inflammation, blocking pathogenic proteins, and relieving disease symptoms more effectively than commercially available products. Unlike previous studies that used NHS chemistry primarily to facilitate tissue adhesion and prolong material residence (30, 31), IPT was structurally engineered to embed a high density of bioactive NHS esters in a stable and accessible manner through rational material design and structural optimization. These embedded NHS groups remain reactive under physiological conditions, enabling efficient and sustained capture of free proteins and proteinaceous pathogens at mucosal surfaces.

RESULTS

Fabrication and characterization of IPT

IPT microspheres were fabricated using a modified water-in-oil emulsification and polymerization process (fig. S1). Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) revealed that the resulting microspheres were uniformly spherical with smooth surfaces (Fig. 2, A and B). Elemental mapping confirmed the homogeneous distribution of carbon, oxygen, and nitrogen atoms (Fig. 2C and fig. S2), corresponding to acrylic acid– and gelatin-derived components. The IPT microspheres have a highly hydrophilic and porous network formed by poly(acrylic acid) and gelatin, which facilitates rapid water absorption through hydrogen bonding (32). Quantitative analysis showed a swelling ratio of nearly 30-fold in volume (v/v), with similar swelling behaviors observed in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and bovine serum albumin (BSA) solution (Fig. 2, D and E, and fig. S3). To visualize the hydrogel formation process, we monitored the morphological transition of IPT using both SEM and confocal fluorescence microscopy (Fig. 2, F to H). Upon hydration, discrete microspheres rapidly expanded and fused together to form a cohesive hydrogel layer. Rheological analysis showed that the formed hydrogel exhibited solid-like behavior with a dominant storage modulus (G′) over loss modulus (G″), indicating its elastic and mechanically stable gel network (fig. S4).

Interception of microscale particles by IPT hydrogel

To evaluate the ability of IPT hydrogel to physically intercept microsized matter, a filtration model was established in which dry IPT powder was sprayed onto a wetted 100-μm pore-size filter, allowing the formation of a hydrogel layer. Upon exposure to microsized particulates, including pollen (27 μm), dust (2 to 7 μm), and 2 μm-diameter microbeads (fig. S5), the IPT hydrogel effectively prevented particle penetration across all tested size ranges (Fig. 3B). Quantitative analysis confirmed that IPT-treated surfaces retained >98% of all particles. To assess particle-trapping under airflow, pollen-laden air was directed onto a carbon tape partially coated with IPT hydrogel or water. SEM imaging showed substantially more pollen adhered to the IPT-coated region, attributed to the adhesive nature of the hydrogel (fig. S6).

To further visualize the spatial distribution of trapped particles within the IPT hydrogel, confocal imaging was performed using rhodamine B–labeled pollen deposited on Cy5-labeled IPT hydrogel. Three-dimensional reconstruction and cross-sectional views revealed that pollen particles localized exclusively at the hydrogel surface, forming a distinct “surface trapping layer” atop an underlying “particle-free zone” (Fig. 3C). Notably, the hydrogel appeared to partially encapsulate the pollen, suggesting a tight hydrogel-particle interface that may limit the release of allergenic proteins from the trapped particles. SEM provided additional confirmation of this surface retention phenomenon. While pristine pollen particles exhibited well-defined morphology (Fig. 3D, top), those deposited onto IPT hydrogel appeared embedded and clustered within the hydrogel surface (Fig. 3D, bottom, and fig. S6), consistent with strong physical entrapment.

In addition to passive entrapment, IPT hydrogel also demonstrated chemical reactivity towards amine-functionalized particles. When amine-modified microbeads were incubated with either individual IPT microspheres or preformed IPT hydrogel in PBS, the beads remained stably associated with the IPT after washing (Fig. 3E). The adsorption ability of IPT hydrogel for amine-modified microbeads was calculated as 1.17 × 105 particles mm−2. Together, these results indicate that IPT not only forms an efficient physical barrier to intercept microsized particulates but also has chemical reactivity to immobilize amine-modified particles.

Covalent immobilization of free proteins by IPT hydrogel

In addition to intercepting microparticles, IPT hydrogel was designed to actively immobilize soluble proteins through covalent conjugation. This is achieved via active NHS esters embedded in the hydrogel network, which readily reacts with primary amine groups. Notably, the ε-amino groups of lysine residues are abundant on protein surfaces (Fig. 4, A and B). This spontaneous amide bond formation enables stable and irreversible protein entrapment within the hydrogel matrix. To directly visualize this protein-binding capacity, red fluorescent protein (RFP) was applied onto Cy5-labeled IPT hydrogel and incubated briefly before thorough washing. Confocal imaging revealed strong retention of red fluorescence along the NHS-containing IPT hydrogel, indicating effective RFP binding via covalent immobilization, whereas negligible signal was observed in the NHS-free control, confirming minimal nonspecific adsorption in the absence of reactive groups (Fig. 4C). The spatial distribution of the fluorescence suggested uniform adsorption at the interface, further supporting the presence of efficient protein capture via covalent linkage. RFP remained stably retained within IPT hydrogel over 8 hours in PBS, indicating durable covalent immobilization without detectable release (fig. S7). We next evaluated whether IPT could prevent the transmission of allergenic proteins released from pollen particles. Using a transwell system with hydrogel-coated 100-μm membranes, pollen particles were applied to either thick (>2 mm) or thin (~1 mm) hydrogel layers (Fig. 4D). Western blot analysis of the underlying PBS revealed that both IPT and a commercially available hydroxypropyl methylcellulose (HPMC) nasal spray blocked protein diffusion in the thick-layer condition. However, under thinner coating or direct mixing conditions, only IPT maintained effective protein retention, while HPMC failed to prevent allergen leakage. IPT also effectively binds Asp f1, a representative fungal allergen, highlighting its broad applicability in capturing diverse proteinaceous triggers (fig. S8). The areal protein loading capacity of IPT was approximately 31.4 μg mm−2 at equilibrium (fig. S9). To assess the availability and stability of IPT and its NHS esters in a complex physiological protein environment, we quantified the residual active NHS esters and corresponding protein-binding capacity of IPT over time in artificial mucus and pH 6.8 PBS (fig. S10). IPT retained a substantial fraction of active NHS esters in artificial mucus, exhibiting a gradual decay with a half-life of approximately 2.1 hours due to hydrolysis, while maintaining measurable BSA-binding capacity over the same period. These results demonstrate that IPT remains chemically reactive and functionally available under mucus-like conditions, supporting its effectiveness in complex protein-rich airway environments.

To further mimic allergen release from particulate carriers such as pollen, we designed a model system in which RFP was encapsulated within alginate-based hydrogel microspheres using a modified gelation method (33) (Fig. 4E). Upon exposure to ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), calcium ions within the alginate network are chelated, resulting in microsphere collapse and subsequent RFP release, mimicking the way allergenic proteins are released from pollen when exposed to moisture. RFP-loaded alginate microspheres were incubated in IPT or HPMC hydrogels with 1 mM EDTA. In the presence of NHS-containing IPT hydrogel, RFP released from alginate microspheres remained concentrated around the microsphere periphery with minimal diffusion into the surrounding matrix, indicating that the released RFP was rapidly captured and immobilized in the interface (Fig. 4F). In contrast, NHS-free IPT (Fig. 4G) and commercial HPMC (Fig. 4H) showed widespread RFP dispersion. Further quantification showed that IPT(NHS+) maintained stable full width at half maximum (FWHM) and average fluorescence intensity within 120% of the initial microsphere region over time (Fig. 4I), whereas both NHS-free IPT and HPMC exhibited greater diffusion and reduced fluorescence intensity (Fig. 4, J and K). These results highlight the essential role of NHS-mediated covalent capture in effectively immobilizing released proteins.

Pollen allergy prevention by IPT in vivo

We evaluated the biocompatibility of IPT at both cellular and in vivo levels. In bronchial epithelial cells, IPT exhibited negligible cytotoxicity across a broad concentration range (up to 20 mg ml−1), as confirmed by CCK-8 assays and live/dead staining under both direct and indirect exposure conditions (fig. S11). For in vivo assessment, mice received intranasal IPT administration every other day for 30 days. Histological examination of major organs revealed no detectable tissue abnormalities compared to untreated controls (fig. S12), and no notable increases were observed across a panel of key inflammatory cytokines compared with normal mice (fig. S13), indicating that long-term IPT administration does not induce detectable nasal inflammatory responses.

The in vivo formation and function of the IPT hydrogel barrier were evaluated using a murine nasal delivery model. Cy5-labeled IPT microspheres were administered intranasally via a custom-designed inhalation apparatus (Fig. 5A). Confocal imaging of the nasal cavity revealed that the IPT rapidly hydrated and formed a continuous hydrogel film adhering to the nasal mucosa (Fig. 5B). To assess in situ particle interception, RhB-labeled pollen was delivered intranasally following IPT inhalation. Pollen particles were observed to colocalize with the hydrogel layer and remain physically separated from the underlying mucosa, indicating successful entrapment by IPT (Fig. 5C). To further evaluate the residence time and clearance of IPT and trapped allergens, Cy7-labeled IPT and Cy5-labeled pollen were tracked via in vivo imaging system (IVIS). Fluorescence signals confirmed persistent localization of IPT within the nasal cavity for up to 8 hours, with pollen particles accumulating within the hydrogel layer (Fig. 5D and fig. S14). Over time, fluorescence signals in the nasal cavity progressively decreased and were transiently detected along the gastrointestinal tract, consistent with physiological mucociliary transport driven by coordinated ciliary beating that moves the mucus-hydrogel complex toward the nasopharynx, followed by swallowing (34–36). Once in the gastrointestinal tract, the IPT hydrogel and captured particles are expected to undergo degradation under digestive conditions and be eliminated through fecal excretion. By 24 hours postadministration, signals from both IPT and pollen were largely diminished, with no evidence of persistent accumulation in major organs, indicating elimination via physiological clearance rather than systemic absorption or metabolic processing.

To assess the prevention efficacy of IPT, a murine model of allergic rhinitis was established using intraperitoneal injections of pollen extract with alum adjuvant over a 2-week period. One week after sensitization, mice received IPT inhalation 30 min before daily pollen exposure for five consecutive days (Fig. 5E). Compared to untreated allergic mice, those pretreated with IPT exhibited a significant reduction in sneeze frequency following the final challenge (Fig. 5F), indicating effective symptom suppression. Histological analysis of the nasal mucosa revealed characteristic features of allergic inflammation in the control group, including epithelial thickening and immune cell infiltration (Fig. 5, G and H). These inflammatory changes were mostly not observed in IPT-treated mice, whose nasal tissues appeared similar to those of healthy mice. In addition, blood samples were analyzed to quantify systemic markers of allergic response (Fig. 5I). Total serum immunoglobulin E (IgE) level markedly increased after pollen exposure, while mice pretreated with IPT showed normal IgE level. Key cytokines that are highly associated with inflammatory and allergic response, including interleukin-4 (IL-4), IL-5, IL-10, IL-13, and tumor necrosis factor–α, markedly increased in the control group but remained normal in the IPT protection group. These results collectively demonstrate that IPT is able to form a protective hydrogel film in the nasal cavity and prevent allergic reactions induced by inhaled pollen particles.

Nonselective immobilization of diverse viruses by IPT hydrogel

The active NHS esters in IPT hydrogel enable it to react with primary amine groups on viral surface proteins, forming stable amide bonds and thereby immobilizing the virus within the hydrogel network (Fig. 6A). To directly visualize virus binding, DiO-labeled inactive influenza A particles were incubated with individual IPT microspheres containing or lacking NHS groups. Fluorescence microscopy revealed extensive viral accumulation on the surface of IPT(NHS+) microspheres, whereas NHS-free IPT showed negligible binding (Fig. 6B). The virus-capturing efficiency was quantified by incubating DiO-labeled inactive influenza A particles with IPT(NHS+), IPT(NHS−), or a commercial HPMC formulation in PBS for 10 min. The residual viral concentration in buffer was measured using nanoparticle tracking analysis. Virus concentration remained unchanged in the presence of IPT(NHS−) or HPMC, while IPT(NHS+) achieved over 96% inhibition at 2 mg/ml (Fig. 6C). To determine whether this viral depletion translated into reduced infectivity, green fluorescent protein (GFP)–expressing influenza A pseudoviruses were pretreated with increasing concentrations of IPT, and the supernatants were subsequently applied to human embryonic kidney (HEK) 293T cells. After 72 hours, GFP fluorescence intensity decreased with increasing IPT concentration, indicating a dose-dependent reduction in pseudovirus infectivity (Fig. 6D and fig. S15). To assess the versatility of IPT-mediated viral inhibition, we extended the pseudovirus neutralization assay to multiple virus types. GFP-expressing pseudoviruses representing influenza A, influenza B, and SARS-CoV-2 were each preincubated with IPT(NHS+), IPT(NHS−), or a commercial HPMC formulation before application to HEK293T cells or HEK293T-hsACE2 cells. Fluorescent imaging (Fig. 6E) and relative intensity (Fig. 6F) revealed widespread GFP expression in HPMC-treated or IPT(NHS−)-treated groups, whereas cells exposed to IPT(NHS+)-treated pseudoviruses showed markedly reduced GFP signals across all virus types. Flow cytometry further confirmed that IPT(NHS+) markedly reduced the percentage of GFP-positive (infected) cells compared to other treatment groups across all virus types (Fig. 6, G and H), including various subtypes of SARS-CoV-2 (fig. S16).

To assess the in vivo protective efficacy of IPT, CD-1 mice received intranasal IPT administration, or with two commercially available nasal sprays, followed by two intranasal administrations of influenza A pseudovirus at 1 and 4 hours post-treatment. Lung tissues were collected 24 hours later for histological analysis. Fluorescence imaging revealed detectable but reduced GFP signals in the lungs of mice treated with either of the two commercially available nasal sprays, indicating that both provided partial protection against viral entry (Fig. 6I). In contrast, IPT-treated mice exhibited markedly reduced GFP fluorescence, suggesting more effective blockade of viral penetration. Quantitative analysis confirmed that all three formulations offered airway protection, but IPT achieved the most substantial reduction in pulmonary viral burden (Fig. 6J).

Dual prophylactic and therapeutic effects of IPT in a rhinitis model

Given IPT’s ability to covalently bind a broad range of amine-containing proteins, it holds promise for capturing various allergenic proteins at mucosal surfaces. In addition, the reactive NHS esters in IPT may also covalently bind to small-molecule mediators of allergic inflammation, such as histamine, thereby further mitigating allergic symptoms. We hypothesized that this protein-trapping mechanism would not only allow for prophylactic protection, by intercepting allergens before epithelial contact, but also enable therapeutic intervention shortly after allergen exposure by limiting further diffusion and immune activation (Fig. 7A). To test this, we used ovalbumin (OVA), a well-characterized model allergen, in a mouse model of allergic rhinitis.

To confirm the ability of IPT to bind key allergy-related molecules, we incubated IPT with histamine or OVA in PBS and quantified the residual concentration in the supernatant by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay. IPT markedly reduced the levels of both histamine and OVA, indicating strong amine-binding capability under physiological conditions (Fig. 7, B and C, and fig. S17).We then evaluated the therapeutic efficacy of IPT in a mouse model of OVA-induced allergic rhinitis (Fig. 7D). BALB/c mice were sensitized through intraperitoneal injections of OVA with alum adjuvant and challenged intranasally with OVA in the presence or absence of protective formulations. In the prophylactic protocol, mice received nasal administration of IPT or two commercially available nasal sprays 4 hours before each OVA challenge. In a separate therapeutic protocol, mice were first challenged intranasally with OVA, followed by IPT inhalation 5 min later (“IPT-5min” group).

To assess the protein- and histamine-binding capacity of IPT in nasal airway lavage fluid (NALF), we collected NALF from allergic mice 30 min after the final OVA challenge and incubated it with IPT in vitro. After 10 min of incubation, the levels of both OVA and histamine in the supernatant were markedly reduced (Fig. 7, E and F, and fig. S18), indicating that IPT can effectively capture allergenic proteins and reduce the local availability of small-molecule mediators from inflamed airway secretions. Notably, the reduction of histamine reflects covalent capture of histamine within the mucus phase, leading to a decrease in the local free concentration and effective lifetime of histamine available for downstream signaling. Serum levels of OVA-specific IgE, a key indicator of systemic allergic sensitization, were significantly reduced in both prophylactic and therapeutic IPT-treated groups compared to untreated allergic controls and other treatment groups (Fig. 7G). Consistently, flow cytometry analysis showed a marked reduction in eosinophil infiltration in peripheral blood following IPT treatment, with comparable effects observed in both administration modes (Fig. 7, H and I). Furthermore, IPT treatment suppressed the elevation of multiple inflammatory and type 2 cytokines in plasma, including IL-4, IL-5, IL-10, IL-13, IL-17A, and granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF; Fig. 7, J to O). Notably, postexposure administration of IPT remained effective in reducing allergic responses, whereas commercially available nasal sprays provided only partial cytokine suppression. These results demonstrate that IPT can effectively neutralize allergenic proteins in vivo, offering both prophylactic and therapeutic benefit in allergic rhinitis through covalent protein capture.

DISCUSSION

The effectiveness of NHS ester–based chemistry has been demonstrated in tissue adhesion and wound repair, where rapid amide bond formation with surface-exposed amines enables strong and stable coupling under physiological conditions (37–39). However, applications of NHS-based systems beyond tissue adhesion remain largely unexplored. Here, we extend this chemical strategy to address a broader biomedical challenge: The interception and neutralization of a broad spectrum of pathogenic proteinaceous agents at biological interfaces. By embedding NHS-functionalized components into a mucoadhesive hydrogel matrix, we developed a reactive materials platform capable of nonselectively capturing amine-containing biomolecules through covalent linkage. Unlike conventional barrier materials that rely on passive entrapment or reversible electrostatic interactions, IPT provides irreversible chemical modes of protection. It not only intercepts proteinaceous particles such as diverse virus particles, but also neutralizes a wide range of soluble proteins by covalent immobilization. This nonselective nature of NHS-amine chemistry is a deliberate design feature, as IPT is intended for broad-spectrum interception of diverse and unpredictable proteinaceous threats at the mucus interface rather than selective pathogen recognition. Potential unintended interactions are mitigated by both spatial and temporal constraints: IPT remains confined to the mucus layer without penetrating epithelial tissues, and NHS reactivity persists only for a few hours under physiological conditions, limiting prolonged or systemic effects. To further minimize nonspecific risks, future designs may incorporate strategies such as tuning NHS ester density to balance capture efficiency and reactivity, or introducing steric components to restrict accessibility to large biomolecules.

Although nasal mucus is highly proteinaceous, our results indicate that IPT retains sufficient functional NHS reactivity under mucus-like conditions. Partial consumption of NHS esters by endogenous proteins reduces, but does not abolish, protein-capturing capacity, owing to the intentionally high initial NHS density and the stabilized polymer network. Moreover, larger exogenous particulates such as viruses or pollen-derived allergens benefit from kinetic and spatial advantages in mucus, promoting efficient multivalent interactions with the IPT hydrogel. These features enable effective pathogen interception despite substantial biological background proteins.

While pseudoviruses provide a safe and widely adopted model for evaluating mucosal entry, live viruses additionally involve complex shedding and replication cycles. Our study primarily captures the initial interception step whereby IPT prevents luminal access to the mucosal surface. For replicating viruses, intracellular replication and subsequent shedding introduce additional rounds of viral exposure to the mucus layer. Although IPT does not interfere with intracellular replication, newly released virions may again be immobilized at the mucus-hydrogel interface by residual NHS reactivity, thereby reducing effective viral load and limiting early lateral spread. As a prophylactic extracellular barrier, IPT is designed to reduce effective viral exposure, infection probability, and early disease severity, rather than to treat established infection. Postentry replication, shedding dynamics, and lateral release and spread are not represented by the pseudovirus model and will require future studies with live replicating viruses to fully delineate IPT’s protective scope (40–42).

A limitation of IPT is that its efficacy is restricted to an early window after allergen exposure, as allergens rapidly interact with immune cells and initiate downstream immune cascades within minutes. Accordingly, IPT functions as an early interception barrier that prevents allergen-immune engagement, rather than a treatment capable of reversing established immune activation. Another important consideration for airway-applied materials is their potential impact on mucociliary clearance. IVIS biodistribution studies showed that IPT and IPT-bound particulates are cleared synchronously from the nasal cavity and subsequently detected along the gastrointestinal tract, consistent with intact physiological mucus transport (36). No persistent retention in the airway or accumulation in distal lung regions was observed, and long-term administration did not induce nasal inflammation. These findings suggest that IPT does not measurably impair the mucociliary escalator under the conditions tested.

The protein-trapping capability of IPT, driven by rapid and irreversible NHS-amine chemistry, offers broad translational potential beyond the nasal mucosa, such as the gastrointestinal tract and skin. At other biological interfaces where pathogenic or pro-inflammatory proteins initiate disease, IPT-like materials could serve as versatile protective barriers or therapeutic adjuncts. For example, at dermal or wound surfaces, covalent capture of bacterial toxins or inflammatory cytokines may help prevent infection or modulate chronic inflammation. In addition, IPT chemistry may be adapted to coat surgical implants or tissue interfaces to reduce local immune activation by sequestering damage-associated proteins or secreted inflammatory factors (43). Beyond therapeutic use, IPT-inspired platforms could also contribute to biointerface design in regenerative medicine or cell therapies, where controlling protein composition at the material surface is critical for host-material interactions (44–46). Beyond NHS-amine chemistry, click chemistry may also be included in the biomaterial interface interaction (47). Collectively, these applications highlight the broader utility of covalent protein-trapping materials in managing protein-driven pathologies across diverse tissue environments.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Preparation of IPT

IPT was prepared using an emulsification-based method. In brief, gelatin (0.5 g; G1890, Sigma-Aldrich) and AAc-NHS ester (200 mg; A8060, Sigma-Aldrich) were dissolved in 10 ml of ultrapure water at room temperature and stirred for 30 min. Potassium persulfate (4 mg; 202010250, Thermo Scientific Chemicals) and acrylic acid (1.5 ml; 147230, Sigma-Aldrich) were then added to the gelatin solution, followed by stirring at room temperature for an additional 10 min. The resulting mixture was added dropwise into 250 ml of corn oil containing 0.2 weight % Tween 80 and emulsified at 500 rpm using an overhead stirrer at 65°C for 1 hour. The emulsion was subsequently cooled in an ice-water bath and stirred for an additional 2 hours at 500 rpm To obtain IPT, the resulting microparticles were collected by filtration, washed thoroughly with acetone and ethanol to remove unreacted components, air-dried in a chemical hood, and stored in a desiccator until further use. To maintain a near neutral environment in the nasal cavity during animal procedures, 0.2% calcium carbonate was incorporated into the IPT powder. Cy5 or Cy7 labeling of IPT was performed using Cy5-NHS ester or Cy7-NHS ester as previously described (48).

SEM imaging of IPT

A piece of conductive carbon tape was attached to the SEM specimen holder. Dry IPT was sprayed onto the carbon tape to form a thin microsphere layer, followed by air blowing using a bulb syringe to remove the unattached samples. Samples were sputter-coated with Au plasma for 1 min before imaging. SEM images were acquired by a JEOL (JCM-7000) under 15 kV acceleration voltage in low vacuum mode.

Swelling behavior of IPT

IPT powder was dispersed on a clear glass slide and visualized by an optical microscope. Ten microliters of ultrapure water, PBS, or BSA solution was dropped directly on the IPT, and the swelling process was recorded by an optical microscope and digital camera. The swelling ratio of IPT was calculated by volume expansion ratio according to the photographs captured during swellingVr=(DtD0)3

Vr represents swelling volume ratio, D0 represents initial IPT diameter, and Dt represents IPT diameter at the specific time point.

Microparticle interception assay

Five milligrams of IPT powder was uniformly sprayed onto a PBS-moistened 100-μm cell strainer (Corning), allowing it to swell and form a continuous hydrogel barrier. The filter was then placed into a 12-well plate, with 1 ml of PBS added to the lower compartment of each well. Three types of microparticles, including pollen grains, dust, and microbeads, were sprayed onto the surface of the hydrogel layer. After 30 min of incubation at room temperature, the PBS solution from the lower chamber was collected, and the number of particles that penetrated through the hydrogel was quantified using optical microscopy.

Amine group and protein binding by IPT

To measure amine-group binding ability, IPT was incubated with RhB-labeled NH2-silica microspheres (0.1 mg ml−1; 24757-1, Polysciences) suspension for 5 min at room temperature. The unbonded NH2-silica microspheres were removed by differential centrifugation. The IPT-RhB-beads were imaged by a confocal microscope (FluoView, Olympus).

To evaluate free protein-binding performance, IPT microspheres with or without NHS esters were labeled with Cy5 and dispersed in PBS to form hydrogels. RFP (1 mg ml−1 in PBS) was added onto the hydrogel surface and incubated at room temperature for 10 min. The samples were gently washed three times with PBS to remove unbound RFP. Confocal fluorescence imaging was performed to visualize RFP binding on hydrogel.

To assess binding of pollen-released proteins, 2 mg of IPT or commercial nasal spray (Nasaleze Allergy Blocker, purchased from Amazon) were sprayed in the transwell (Corning 3397; pore size, 0.4 μm) with 100 μl of PBS to form a thick or thin layer of hydrogel, followed by spraying of 2-mg pollen powder. For direct mixing groups, pollen and IPT or commercial nasal spray powder were directly mixed followed by spraying in the transwell with PBS solution. The transwells were placed in a 12-well plate containing 500 μl of PBS per well and incubated at room temperature for 30 min. PBS solution in the 12-well plate was collected and the proteins were measured by Western blotting using a stain-free SDS–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis gel.

RFP release and capture assay

RFP-loaded alginate microspheres were prepared by ionotropic gelation. In brief, 100 μl of 1% (w/v) sodium alginate solution was mixed with 10 μg of RFP and gently vortexed. The mixture was then loaded into a microsyringe and slowly dropped into 100 mM calcium chloride (CaCl2) solution under vigorous stirring at room temperature. After 10 min of cross-linking, the resulting microspheres were collected by centrifugation at 500g for 3 min, washed twice with PBS to remove excess CaCl2 and unencapsulated RFP.

To simulate the release of protein allergens, RFP-loaded alginate microspheres were introduced into an EDTA-triggered collapse system. Specifically, the RFP-loaded alginate microspheres were dropped into 100 mM EDTA solution, followed immediately by the addition of IPT or HPMC-based nasal spray powder. The mixture was then imaged using a fluorescence microscope.

Cytotoxicity of IPT in vitro

Primary human bronchial/tracheal epithelial (HBE) cells [PCS-300-010, American Type Culture Collection (ATCC)] were obtained from ATCC and cultured with airway epithelial cell basal medium. HBE cells (1 × 104) were seeded in a 96-well plate and left to adhere overnight. IPT was added to the wells at the concentration of 5, 10, or 20 mg ml−1 and further incubated for 24 or 48 hours. Cell viability was measured with Cell Counting Kit-8 (CK04, Dojindo) as previously described (49).

For indirect contact cytotoxicity measurement, 2 × 104 HBE cells were seeded and cultured in a four-well Millicell EZ slide (PEZGS0416, Millipore) per well (50). Ten milligrams of IPT was incubated with cell-free culture medium at 37°C for 24 hours to prepare the conditioned medium. After centrifugation at 1000g for 10 min, the supernatant of conditioned medium was collected to replace the culture medium of HBE cells and further incubated for 24 hours. The live and dead cells were stained with annexin V/propidium iodide (PI) and imaged by a fluorescent microscope (Revolve, ECHO).

For direct contact cytotoxicity measurement, 2 × 104 HBE cells were seeded in the transwell (Corning 3401; pore size, 0.4 μm) containing 500 μl of culture medium per well and placed in a 12-well plate. Ten milligrams of IPT was added to the upper well and incubated for 24 hours. The live and dead cells were stained with annexin V/PI and imaged by a fluorescent microscope.

Virus neutralization assay

To evaluate the virus-trapping and neutralization capacity of IPT, a pseudovirus-based infection model was used. Pseudovirus particles encoding a GFP reporter were incubated with IPT (with or without NHS ester) or HPMC-based nasal spray in PBS for 10 min under gentle shaking at room temperature. After incubation, the suspensions were centrifuged and filtered with 0.45-μm filter to pellet and remove the hydrogel. The resulting virus-containing supernatants were then added to HEK293T cells or HEK293T-hsACE2 cells seeded in a 24-well plate and cultured under standard conditions (37°C, 5% CO2) for 72 hours. GFP fluorescence was observed using a fluorescence microscope, and fluorescence intensity was quantified to assess infection efficiency. For single-cell resolution analysis, flow cytometry was performed to determine the percentage of GFP-positive cells.

Animal procedures

All studies and protocols were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of North Carolina State University (22-391, 22-431) and Columbia University (AABY-3665).

For measurement of airway coverage and biodistribution of IPT, mice were anesthetized with isoflurane before IPT inhalation. Cy5- or Cy7-labeled IPT was administered via a customized inhaler at a dose of 1 mg per mouse. All major organs were collected at different time points postinhalation and imaged by IVIS (AMI, Spectral). The cryosectioned lung tissues were stained with DAPI and imaged by a confocal microscope.

The pollen-induced allergic rhinitis model was established in BALB/c mice. In brief, the sensitizing solution was prepared by mixing Imject Alum (2.5 mg ml−1; 77161, Thermo Fisher Scientific), aspen tree pollen (2.5 mg ml−1; P7770, Sigma-Aldrich), and pollen extract in PBS solution (0.25 mg ml−1). Animals were intraperitoneally injected with the sensitizing mixture weekly for 2 weeks at a dose of 200 μl per mouse. On day 21, the mice in the IPT group were administered with IPT by inhalation at a dose of 1 mg per mouse. After 30 min, all animals received 0.5 mg of pollen powder inhalation to trigger allergy. Eyes and mouths were protected in case of unexpected allergic reaction. The protection and challenge were performed every day for five consecutive days. On day 25, after the last pollen exposure, the sneezing number of each mouse was recorded for 10 min. All mice were euthanized on day 26. Bloods were collected, and serums were sent to Eve Technology for Luminex analysis of cytokines. Mouse heads were fixed with 10% neutral buffered formalin for 3 days, followed by decalcification with 10% EDTA for 21 days. The decalcified nasal cavities were embedded with paraffin and sectioned for histological analysis.

To evaluate the in vivo virus-blocking efficacy of the hydrogel barrier, a murine nasal delivery model was established using influenza A pseudotyped lentivirus expressing GFP. CD-1 mice (6 to 8 weeks old) were anesthetized with isoflurane and administered IPT, HPMC powder, or PCANS nasal spray intranasally. After 1 and 4 hours, mice were challenged with influenza A GFP pseudovirus. At 24 hours postinfection, mice were euthanized, and the lungs were harvested. GFP expression was evaluated using a fluorescence microscope.

For OVA-induced allergic rhinitis model, female BALB/c mice (6 to 8 weeks old) were sensitized and challenged with OVA following a modified protocol based on previous reports. During the sensitization phase, mice received intraperitoneal injections of 50 μg of OVA (08950512, MP Biomedicals) emulsified in 1 mg of aluminum hydroxide adjuvant in a total volume of 200 μl of PBS on days 0, 7, and 14. On day 21, 10 μl of OVA (10 mg/ml) in PBS was administered into the nasal cavity once daily for five consecutive days (days 21 to 25). In preexposure groups, IPT or commercially available nasal sprays were administered intranasally 4 hours before each OVA challenge. In the postexposure group, OVA was administered first, followed by IPT inhalation 5 min later (IPT-5min group). Mice were euthanized on day 26, and samples including blood, NALF, and nasal mucosa were collected for further histological and immunological analyses.

Biosafety evaluation in vivo

The mice were administered with IPT by inhalation every other day at a dose of 1 mg per mouse for 30 days. All mice were euthanized on day 31. The major tissues (nasal cavity, trachea, lung, heart, liver, spleen, and kidney) were collected for histological analysis following standard hematoxylin and eosin staining protocols, as previously described (51). The nasal lavage fluid was collected for cytokine analysis.

Statistical analysis

Animals were randomized to treatment groups. All quantitative experiments were presented as means ± SD. A two-tailed unpaired Student’s t test was used to analyze differences between two groups. Differences were analyzed using GraphPad PRISM software and exact P values were documented in the figures or figure captions.
