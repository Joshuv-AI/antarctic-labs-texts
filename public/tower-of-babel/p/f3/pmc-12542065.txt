Anencephaly in Brazil: an ecological study on sociodemographic factors and prenatal care
Authors: Helana Augusta Leal Dias Andrade, Gustavo Cipullo Nesteruk Moreira, José Ribamar Leal Dias, Deizyane dos Reis Galhardo, Eric Renato Lima Figueiredo, João Simão de Melo Neto, Fabiana de Campos Gomes
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAnencephaly is a serious and fatal congenital anomaly. It can be detected in the first trimester of pregnancy through an ultrasound performed during prenatal care. This study aimed to identify maternal and prenatal care factors that predict anencephaly in Brazil.MethodsThis was an ecological observational study using secondary data on live births in Brazil between 2017 and 2022. The study variables were grouped into maternal sociodemographic factors (Brazilian regions, maternal age, marital status, schooling and race/color) and prenatal care (consultations performed, gestational week (time) and month of onset of care). A multivariate binary logistic regression model was used.ResultsThe results obtained through the multivariate analysis of maternal sociodemographic factors showed that the southern region (p = 0.005) had a lower association with maternal sociodemographic factors than did the northern region; married (p = 0.002) and widowed (p = 0.007) marital statuses had a greater association with maternal sociodemographic factors than did single marital status; and any education of less than 11 years or no schooling had a greater chance (p < 0.001) of neonates being born with anencephaly. For the prenatal care variables, the number of visits was less than 6 (p < 0.004) and was related to a higher prediction, and gestation time > 22 weeks (p < 0.001) was related to a lower association with anencephaly.ConclusionsWe concluded that the predictors of anencephaly in Brazil for sociodemographic and prenatal factors were being in the Northeast Region, being married or widowed, having a low level of schooling, having a low number of consultations, and having premature births.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12887-025-06099-1.Keywords: Anencephaly, Sociodemographic factors, Prenatal care

Background

Anencephaly is a neural tube defect (NTD) anomaly that occurs around the eighth week of pregnancy due to a failure to close the upper portion of the neural tube, resulting in total or partial absence of the brain [1, 2]. Although its etiology is not well defined, environmental, genetic, and nutritional factors are among the main risk factors.

Neural tube defects (NTDs) affect approximately 300,000 children globally each year and are among the most deadly congenital anomalies in the world. Anencephaly is one of the most serious conditions due to fetal death occurring inside the womb or shortly after delivery [3]. Countries such as Mexico, China, and Turkey have a high prevalence of anencephaly [4]. In the United States, it is estimated that approximately 1 in 5,250 live births have anencephaly [3]. In Brazil, there are approximately 7 cases of anencephaly for every 10,000 births [1]. Most studies on anencephaly discuss the topic in light of its etiopathogenesis, surveillance, and monitoring networks or with an ethical approach. In Brazil, abortion is considered a crime, and anencephaly is one of the three causes within the criminal code that guarantees women the right to interrupt pregnancy; however, it is up to the pregnant woman the right to decide to continue with pregnancy, having the right to assist with specialized support [5, 6].

Although there are several studies that address abortion as a public health problem, information on assistance and maternal mortality due to abortion is still dispersed, requiring a detailed investigation in several public information systems for data collection [7]. Additionally, it is important to consider geographical heterogeneity, the territorial extent of each region, and the sociodemographic factors that may influence the maternal profile of pregnant women with anencephalic fetuses [8] To our knowledge, there are few studies on the number of cases of anencephaly in the epidemiological context, and such information can contribute to a better understanding of prenatal care for cases of anencephaly, including preventive actions and care directed toward clinical management [3, 9].

In view of the existing gaps in the profile of anencephaly cases and previous findings, it is necessary to know the maternal sociodemographic and healthcare factors to identify possible predictors of anencephaly that can assist in surveillance and control measures of the occurrence of this serious congenital anomaly that leads to the mortality of thousands of children in Brazil and worldwide. This study hypothesized that regions of Brazil with greater social inequality, such as the North and Northeast Regions, Black race/skin color, low schooling, advanced age, premature births, and poor prenatal care, increase the risk of live births with anencephaly. Here, in this study, we propose to evaluate which maternal and healthcare factors are predictors of anencephaly in Brazil.

Methods

Study design and period

This was an ecological observational study of the Brazilian population database using secondary data on live births with anencephaly collected between 2017 and 2022. Clinical trial number: Not applicable.

Ethical aspects

By the resolutions of the National Health Council n. 510 [10] and n. 738 [11], the database used in this research contains consolidated information without identifying individuals, respecting the commitment to values, ethics, and principles of good practice in conducting research, dispensing with the need for evaluation by the Research Ethics Committee.

Population and area of study

The study population included a control group (labor births without anencephaly) and a study group (labor births with anencephaly) corresponding to the International Classification of Disease 10 (ICD10) - Q000.

The units of analysis were Brazil’s five macroregions, according to the Brazilian Institute of Geography and Statistics (IBGE): North, Northeast, Southeast, South and Midwest. The layout of the regions on regional political maps includes state and international boundaries and the seats of all the municipalities in each state, among other elements [12].

Sources and data collection

The data were collected from The Information System of Live Births (SINASC), which is integrated into the platform of the Department of Informatics of the Unified Health System (DATASUS) of the public domain linked to the Unified Health System (SUS)/Ministry of Health.

According to the Ministry of Health, SINASC has a national data coverage of approximately 98.2% and a data quality of over 95.0% for some variables such as type of pregnancy, duration of pregnancy, birth weight, sex, ethnicity/color, Apgar score in the first minute of life and detection of congenital anomalies [13]. These socioeconomic variables and others such as place of residence and occurrence, childbirth, and prenatal care from the Declaration of Live Births, a standard instrument used throughout Brazil since 1990 [14]. The reliability of the data is enhanced by the process of systematic and periodic collection of information, which follows strict data management protocols of the federal government [13].

The data were obtained using the microdatasus package available in RStudio (RStudio: Integrated Development Environment for R), which allows the acquisition and preprocessing of information of interest to the research problem, available in the declarations of live births registered in SINASC, managed by the open access platform DATASUS [15].

Variables of study

First, data were collected from the control group during the period of interest of the study. Second, the filter tool was used to select the code of interest of the anomaly; subsequently, the maternal variables were extracted, referring to maternal age, maternal marital status, education, date of birth, sex race/color, age of the father, gestational week, number of prenatal consultations, month of beginning prenatal care, code of the municipality and Federative Unit. After evaluating the frequency of gaps in the tabulated data and the relevance to the study, the variable age of the father was excluded from the analysis. For the variables collected from the newborn, data on date of birth, sex, and race/color were collected.

After data collection, the variables were organized and evaluated in two categories: maternal sociodemographic factors and prenatal care. Maternal sociodemographic factors included region of Brazil (North, Northeast, South, Southeast, Midwest), age in years, marital status (single, married, widowed, separated/divorced, common-law marriage), years of schooling (none, 1 to 3, 4 to 7, 8 to 11, over 11), and race/skin color (white, black, yellow, brown, indigenous).

Prenatal care was categorized by the frequency of prenatal visits (none, 1 to 3, 4 to 6, more than 6 and not reported), number of visits, gestational week (less than 22, 22 to 27, 28 to 31, 32 to 36, 37 to 41, more than 41, not reported) and gestational month at the beginning of care.

Statistical analysis

For statistical analysis, the study groups were evaluated according to the dependent variable “anencephaly”. This variable was coded as “0” = absence and “1” = presence of anencephaly. As this is a dichotomous dependent variable, the model chosen was binary logistic regression. To determine whether maternal sociodemographic factors and prenatal care factors are predictors of anencephaly in the regions of Brazil, univariate and multivariate binary logistic regression analyses were carried out (considering p values of ˂0.25 and ˂0.05, respectively). Tolerance values ˃ 0.1 and variance inflation factor (VIF) values ˂ 10 were considered to verify the absence of multicollinearity for inclusion in the multivariate analysis to be included in the final model. For independent samples, the Mann‒Whitney normality test (nonparametric) was performed. For the chi-squared test, a 95% confidence interval and a p value < 0.05 were considered. All analyzes were performed using the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS for Windows, version 21.0; IBM, Armonk, NY, USA), with p-values of less than 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Discussion

Anencephaly is a severe malformation of the central nervous system caused by abnormal neurulation, with multifactorial etiologies that include genetic and environmental factors [4, 16, 17]. Due to the scarcity of data on the causes and number of live births with anencephaly, a survey of epidemiological aspects can help track associated factors and populations that are more vulnerable to congenital malformation. In this study, we identified regional, maternal, and prenatal care factors predictive of anencephaly in Brazil.

This study revealed a difference in the incidence of anencephaly between Brazilian regions. Compared with the North Region, the South Region of Brazil had a lower prediction of anencephaly and a lower association with the occurrence of anencephaly. In contrast, the Northeast Region had the highest proportion of cases per 10,000 live births, which has been identified as a predictor of increased risk and association for anencephaly. Recent data on the incidence of malformations in the southern region have shown that osteomuscular anomalies are most common in this region of Brazil [18, 19]. In addition, this regional difference can be attributed to better socioeconomic indicators and greater availability of and access to health services in the South.

In contrast to the Northeast, a region historically marked by inequalities in human development and healthcare coverage [20, 21]. Previous studies have shown that regions with worse human development indicators, lower prenatal care coverage, and greater exposure to environmental risk factors tend to have a greater incidence of neural tube defects [9, 22]. Therefore, the findings of this study reinforce the need for a regional approach to understanding and addressing anencephaly in Brazil to identify the social determinants that may influence its occurrence. Nevertheless, for the Northeast Region, it is important to consider improvements in the notification system in recent years due to the high number of microcephaly cases between 2015 and 2016 [23]. In addition, it should be noted that environmental and/or genetic factors not evaluated in this study may have direct implications for the differences in the number of patients with anencephaly.

Although the northern region has the lowest population density in the country, with 4.5 inhabitants/km2 [24], our results show it to be the second region with the most cases of anencephaly in Brazil. This is quite interesting because the entire territory of this region is part of the Legal Amazon, which is characterized by mineral and plant extractivism, agriculture, cattle ranching and heavy deforestation [25]. Many studies have shown that these activities can lead to the contamination of water, soil and food by heavy metals such as lead, mercury and cadmium, which are considered teratogenic agents [26]. It should also be noted that high mercury concentrations in maternal blood are more likely to be associated with NTDs [27], as is mercury [28]. In this sense, in contrast to the findings of the present study and the differences in the number of cases of the anomaly in question by administrative regions of Brazil, there is a need to investigate possible relationships between work activities to establish relationships with the profile of the population group with the highest incidence for the occurrence of anencephaly. Thus, the number of anencephaly cases in certain regions, such as the northern region, could be related to the effects of mercury contamination from illegal gold mining in the Amazon, as reported in Ellwanger & Chies, 2023 [29]. Although the authors report findings such as neurotoxicity associated with mercury exposure, there is still a lack of studies on these effects in juvenile or adult populations and their potential impact on gamete viability, as well as monitoring of heavy metal exposure and its respective effects during specific life stages, such as the gestation period. Such information is necessary for the implementation of public policies that assess the impact of mercury exposure on the most vulnerable populations, and a possible relationship with cases of anencephaly mainly in the Northern region of Brazil.

Still, compared to the global average, congenital anomalies such as anencephaly are estimated to be more prevalent in countries in East Asia due to socioeconomic conditions related to health care and preventive measures such as the regular use of folic acid [30]. Among South American countries, Brazil had a prevalence of 4.57 cases per 10,000 live births between 2010 and 2019 [1]. In our study, a progressive decrease in cases of anencephaly was observed, especially in 2020, possibly as a result of the COVID-19 pandemic, which included pregnant women in the risk group for complications of the virus, generating a decrease in the birth rate [31]. In addition, underreporting and inadequate records in the Declaration of Live Births, recognized by the Ministry of Health, may also contribute to this low rate, reflecting the difficulties health professionals have in recording and identifying anomalies [32].

According to the analysis of maternal sociodemographic characteristics, pregnant women with low education levels had a greater proportion of cases of anencephaly per 10,000 live births. In others study also was observed that low level educational maternal are associated with neural tube defects [33, 34]. This finding highlights the importance of considering the woman’s fertility period in studies on this subject due to greater exposure to possible teratogenic agents, such as alcohol, drugs and heavy metals, which can harm fetal development [35]. Therefore, the promotion of measures such as health education can help to disseminate information about congenital malformations.

Additionally, in the analysis of maternal sociodemographic characteristics, a greater proportion of cases of anencephaly were found among pregnant women who were widowed, had no schooling and were indigenous. In another study conducted by our research group, we revealed that ethnic factors and years of schooling influence the risk of mortality among children with Down’s syndrome in the administrative regions of Brazil [36]. Indigenous women, due to sociocultural factors, are more likely to have children earlier, and due to difficult access to education, health services and nutritional deficiencies, they are more prone to premature birth and birth defects than are nonindigenous women [37]. Social inequalities such as difficult access to services, inadequate nutrition and low levels of schooling, which are characteristic of low-income countries, are closely linked to race/skin color and are reflected in the worse health indicators of the population, which is referred to as brown/black, as well as the vulnerability of indigenous people [9, 37].

In terms of marital status, the study revealed that married and widowed pregnant women were more likely to have a newborn with this congenital malformation than single pregnant women were, in line with the findings of Reis and Ferrari [38] in their cross-sectional study on congenital anomalies, contrasting with other studies in which there was a predominance of single women [38]. Genetic inheritance should be emphasized in married women due to the family history of congenital anomalies and the risk of recurrence in each subsequent pregnancy of the anencephalic fetus [33, 39, 40].

Another finding was that any schooling of fewer than 11 years or the absence of maternal schooling were identified as risk factors for the occurrence of anencephaly, possibly due to their association with less access and adherence to prenatal care and lack of access to health information. Recently, a study conducted in southern Brazil showed that maternal education can influence the occurrence of congenital anomalies, considering that a low number of studies can lead to a low frequency of health services and limited access to information and monitoring in health care [41]. In this regard, it is suggested that health education can be a preventive strategy to reduce the number of cases of anencephaly due to preventable causes.

Access to prenatal health services and their quality can influence the prevalence of anencephaly, given the greater likelihood of prenatal diagnosis [42]. The results indicated that pregnant women with newborns affected by this condition had fewer prenatal visits than mothers of babies without anencephaly. This finding was also corroborated in our regression model as a predictor for the occurrence of anencephaly. This suggests that the lower frequency of prenatal consultations may decrease the early intake of folic acid, in addition to the early detection of anencephaly through important tests such as ultrasound, which is considered the gold standard for diagnosing this anomaly before delivery [43]. Another important prenatal test is maternal serum screening, such as serum alpha-fetoprotein levels, which are often high in pregnancies affected by anencephaly [38, 39, 44]. On the other hand, we find that a later onset of prenatal care was associated with a lower probability of anencephaly cases. These results lead us to hypothesize the following: (1) Some women have a higher or lower genetic probability of having a child with anencephaly, regardless of initial prenatal care. (2) The information on the late start of prenatal care does not allow us to assess and infer the quality of monitoring at the beginning of pregnancy, regardless of the date of official registration at the health facility. These hypotheses may be explained by the etiopathogenesis of anencephaly, which indicates that genetic and nutritional factors may have a direct influence on the occurrence of cases of anencephaly [4, 16]. Such assumptions and findings may clearly indicate that, although the importance of prenatal care in early pregnancy is well established in the literature, there are factors such as genetics that may be decisive in the occurrence of anencephaly. At this point, we believe that referral to the genetic counseling services can help identify the causes of the differences in the number of consultations and the period of initiation of prenatal care with the number of lives born with anencephaly.

As the alteration of the fetus’s central nervous system occurs in the first few weeks, many women start prenatal care after this stage, either because of the delay in identifying the pregnancy or because of late access to the health system. This delay means that pregnant women do not receive adequate folic acid as a measure to prevent neural tube defects, in accordance with the recommendation for the prevention of this type of defect [9, 45]. Another serious consequence is period of gestacional, as our results show. Without adequate follow-up, pregnant women are not initially referred to a specialized unit, which can lead to premature birth and maternal-fetal complications that can be controlled if prenatal care is carried out early [46].

In general, the variations in the number of live births with anencephaly in the administrative regions may be linked to cultural factors, since in Brazil, the law supports the interruption of a fetus with anencephaly; it is up to the woman the ability to choose whether to proceed with pregnancy [47]. Considering the profile of the civil state most affected and the variations in the number of prenatal consultations, we can inquire about the aspect of the reception of these women when receiving the diagnosis of a child with anencephaly because, much more than family support, data show the need for multidisciplinary reception [48]. This discussion is important because it identifies risk groups and can guide public policies and health programs to prevent congenital anomalies, especially disabling and fatal anomalies such as anencephaly, which place a heavy burden on health services due to the need for specialized professionals, highly complex services and lengthy hospital stays [1, 49]. Thus, better planning of the availability of health resources in areas that require greater health care may allow better prenatal care, screening, and monitoring of pregnancies with anencephaly.

Limitation

Although in 2020 SINASC presented national data coverage of approximately 98.2% and data quality with completeness above 95.0% for certain variables, including type of pregnancy, gestation length, birth weight, sex, race/color, Apgar score at 1 min of life, and detection of congenital anomalies. These indicators ensure data quality and relevance for public health decision-making.

Although this research is extremely necessary and relevant, we need to describe some limitations. One of the limitations is the type of study, which may result in ecological bias. Thus, the observations should be applied to the data set and not to individual cases. Another point corresponds to an atypical period in Brazil: (1) after the Zika virus infection epidemic and (2) after the COVID-19 pandemic. It is also important to note that the data reflect the number of live births with anencephaly and not necessarily the number of occurrences by geographic region of Brazil since we did not analyze data on abortion, which may reflect answers to questions about which geographical areas with the highest number of gestational diagnoses for anencephaly. Therefore, the results should be critically evaluated by reflecting on the question and timing of the investigation.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we identified that the predictors of live births with anencephaly in Brazil are related to certain sociodemographic factors, including a greater number of cases in the northeast region, a profile of individuals composed of young women, married or widowed marital status, a low level of schooling, a low number of consultations and an association with premature births. Therefore, identifying these factors is highly relevant for developing public policies aimed at reducing the number of cases through preventive measures. Finally, we emphasize that such strategies can be achieved with the exposure of epidemiological data that evidence the health situation to enable the creation and development of health strategies directed to the most vulnerable locations and populations.
