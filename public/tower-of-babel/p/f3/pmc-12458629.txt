Tuberculosis at the crossroads: urgent actions for migrant and refugee health in a turbulent era
Authors: Tereza Kasaeva, Kerri Viney, Hannah Monica Dias, Martin van den Boom, Santino Severoni, Josette Najjar-Pellet, Diana Abou Ismail, Sanaa T Al-Harahsheh, Allen Gidraf Kahindo Maina, Poonam Dhavan, Farai Mavhunga
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTuberculosis (TB) remains the world’s deadliest infectious disease kiler, affecting the most vulnerable, including refugees and migrants. Their vulnerability is intensified by structural and social barriers that hinder diagnosis and treatment and restrict healthcare access. To put a spotlight on this issue, the WHO in collaboration with the Qatar Foundation launched a technical report on innovative solutions for TB elimination among refugees and migrants at the Seventh World Innovation Summit for Health (WISH) in November 2024. The report proposes 10 policy options and includes seven illustrative case studies to address the issue of TB among refugees and migrants. The global public health landscape has shifted dramatically since the report’s release. Widespread funding cuts for health and development coupled with escalating geopolitical tensions now threaten hard-won public health gains. On the back of an already chronically underfunded TB response, where only 26% of the needed funds were available, both global and local responses to TB are faltering—putting lives, equity and elimination goals at serious risk. While the 2024 WISH report outlined policy actions to address TB among refugees and migrants, shrinking funding for health and development now threatens implementation. Therefore, in this analysis piece, we examine the current and urgent challenge of addressing TB among migrants and refugees framed in the context of three policy actions in the WISH report—namely, political commitment, adequate resourcing and equitable access to healthcare. We argue that sustaining and scaling up efforts to end TB is not optional—it is imperative.

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB) remains the world’s deadliest infectious killer.1 In 2023 alone, 10.8 million people became ill with TB and 1.25 million lives were lost—despite the fact that TB is both preventable and curable.1 Yet alarmingly, only 26% of the global TB response was funded. TB is the leading cause of death among people with HIV and is a major driver of mortality related to antimicrobial resistance.1 Though it is most prevalent in 30 high TB-burden countries, TB is more likely to affect the most vulnerable population groups in any setting.2 This includes refugees and migrants who are at higher risk of developing TB due to overlapping social, structural or biomedical determinants and risk factors, including limited access to healthcare and poor living and working conditions.35

There have been unprecedented levels of human migration and displacement in recent years. Today, about 1 billion globally (or one in eight) people are migrants (including international migrants) or have been forcibly displaced.5 6 By June 2024, more than 122 million people were forcibly displaced worldwide as a result of persecution, conflict, violence or human rights violations and other disruptive events (mid-year estimate).7 This includes 37.9 million refugees, 72.1 million internally displaced people, 8 million asylum seekers and 5.8 million other people in need of international protection.7 8

To address this critical issue, the WHO in collaboration with the Qatar Foundation developed and launched a seminal technical report on innovative solutions for TB elimination among refugees and migrants at the Seventh World Innovation Summit for Health (WISH) in November 2024.9 10 The report proposes 10 key policy options and includes seven illustrative case studies to address the issue of TB among refugees and migrants.9

In the past few months since the report’s release, the global public health and aid landscape has shifted dramatically. Widespread cuts to funding for public health and development, and for vital work on refugees and migration, along with escalating geopolitical tensions and conflicts now threaten hard-won public health gains.11 12 On the back of an already underfunded TB response, both global and local responses to TB are faltering, putting lives, equity and elimination goals at serious risk.

In this analysis piece, we examine the current and urgent challenge of addressing TB among migrants and refugees, and we argue that in today’s crisis-driven world, sustaining and scaling up efforts to end TB is not optional—it is imperative. While the 2024 WISH report outlined key policy actions, solutions and innovations to address TB among refugees and migrants, austerity measures now threaten implementation. By drawing on the policy options and case studies outlined in the WISH report, this paper analyses how to sustain TB services for refugees and migrants amid fiscal constraints, highlighting actions under three policy levers: mobilising high-level political leadership, ensuring the TB response is fully resourced and ensuring access to people-centred TB services through primary healthcare.

The heightened risk of TB among migrants and refugees

Migrants and refugees are at higher risk of developing TB than host populations.4 While migration or displacement does not cause TB, it is the conditions surrounding mobility—including poor living conditions, overcrowding, inadequate housing, poor working conditions and the absence of occupational health and safety protections, which significantly increase vulnerability. These risks are especially acute for individuals without health insurance or access to social protection (figure 1).13 14 Crucially, migrants and refugees are not a homogenous group, and their legal status carries implications for healthcare access and protection. Refugees, protected under international law through instruments such as the 1951 Refugee Convention, are entitled to specific rights, including access to health services.15 Migrants, by contrast, encompass a more diverse population, including labour migrants, undocumented individuals, international migrants and others, whose legal protections and entitlements vary widely. These legal and administrative differences often lead to stark disparities in access to TB services. Cultural and linguistic barriers, stigma, misinformation and restrictive laws and policies can further limit healthcare access.16 Additionally, the presence of comorbidities or health-related risk factors such as HIV, diabetes, undernutrition and mental health conditions further exacerbates TB risk.1

The escalating impact of funding cuts on efforts to combat TB among refugees and migrants

The global fight against TB, the broader public health agenda and humanitarian support for refugees and migrants are all reaching a breaking point. Hard-won progress is being rapidly eroded as deep and widespread funding cuts take hold. Health systems already strained by conflict, climate-related disasters, mass displacement and economic instability are now being pushed to the brink by a dramatic reduction in international investment.11 17 18 The withdrawal of critical financial support for public health, development and TB-specific services is dismantling infrastructure, weakening frontline responses and pushing the world’s most vulnerable—particularly migrants and refugees—into deeper crisis.11 Overall, if left unaddressed, the fallout of decreased funding may mirror the setbacks seen during the COVID-19 pandemic—when TB case notifications plummeted by 18%, over 7 00 000 additional preventable deaths occurred, and there is evidence to suggest that some at-risk populations suffered disproportionately.19

In this context, leading global agencies such as the WHO, the International Organization for Migration (IOM) and the United Nations High Commissioner for Refugees (UNHCR) have sounded the alarm and are working with countries, partners and communities on the ground to mitigate the impact of aid cuts and ensure access to essential services.

Disruption to TB services and research

On the fight against TB, early reports to WHO confirm widespread disruptions in 27 high TB-burden countries, with the African Region hardest hit, followed by South-East Asia and the Western Pacific regions—many of which also face high levels of displacement and migration.20 The cuts are dismantling core functions of TB and health systems alike. Ministries of health, non-governmental organisations, international agencies and academic institutions are reporting breakdowns in essential services. Key pillars of the TB response are under significant strain including frontline health workforce capacity, drug procurement and supply chains, access to and supply of laboratory diagnostics, surveillance and data systems and vital community-based efforts such as case finding and contact tracing. TB prevention and treatment services—especially for people living with HIV or other comorbidities—are being severely compromised, potentially delaying access to care, increasing TB transmission and amplifying drug resistance.2123

TB research is also suffering: just 20% of the US$5 billion required was funded prior to these funding disruptions and many studies are now halted or delayed which could set back innovation and progress for years.1 24 Noting the disruptions to research implementation, experts warn that critical advancements across the entire TB care continuum—from prevention to diagnosis and to treatment, as well as innovative models of care—will be delayed, setting back progress by years and leaving millions without access to future innovations.23 25

Impact on refugees and migrants

Regarding refugees and migrants, in a world facing historic levels of displacement, driven by war, environmental catastrophe and fragile economies, mobility is central to global security, resilience and sustainable development. Yet paradoxically, the resources required to address these mounting challenges are being scaled back. The IOM warns that these reductions are already deepening humanitarian crises and are stripping vulnerable migrant communities of critical health and support systems. Given the current context, UNHCR estimates that, without urgent financial intervention, 12.8 million refugees—including 6.3 million children—could be left without access to life-saving health services in 2025.26 These cuts come on top of long-standing shortfalls in humanitarian aid, exacerbating system-wide disruptions. For instance, in Mozambique’s Maratane refugee settlement, a 50% funding cut has crippled health, nutrition and TB services for over 8000 people. Globally, water supply systems, sanitation and waste management are also being affected, creating the conditions for deadly outbreaks of cholera, hepatitis, malaria and dysentery.26

Innovative TB solutions for refugees and migrants in a challenging funding landscape

Despite the increasingly challenging global landscape, a clear path forward exists—grounded in the commitments that world leaders have already made to end TB and address the health needs of refugees and migrants.2730 The WHO’s End TB Strategy30 aims to end the global TB epidemic by 2030, aligning with the Sustainable Development Goals target 3.3.31 The political declaration from the 2023 United Nations high-level meeting on the fight against TB,29 WHO’s global action plan on promoting the health of refugees and migrants32 and the global compacts on refugees,27 and for safe, orderly and regular migration,28 emphasise the need for comprehensive healthcare, particularly for at-risk groups such as refugees and migrants. They also aim to safeguard essential TB services during humanitarian crises and health emergencies.29

The WISH technical report on innovative solutions for TB elimination among refugees and migrants builds on these global commitments, emphasising actionable policy options needed in times of crisis (figure 2). In today’s volatile and underfunded landscape, several of these policy options emerge as particularly urgent and impactful—offering clear, strategic pathways to safeguard refugees and migrants and prevent the erosion of hard-won gains in the fight against TB. In this analysis piece, we focus on three key policy options, which concern the mobilisation of high-level political leadership, ensuring the TB response is fully resourced and ensuring access to people-centred TB services through primary healthcare.

Mobilising high-level political leadership

First, mobilising high-level political leadership and commitment is essential to drive the coordinated, sustained action needed to address TB among refugees and migrants and vulnerable host communities—particularly in the face of dual crises of conflict and funding cuts. National health and emergency strategies must fully integrate TB interventions for these populations, backed by domestic resources wherever possible. This requires embedding refugee- and migrant-sensitive measures into TB strategic and operational plans to ensure universal access to high-quality care.33 Strengthening national capacity to meet the unique health needs of mobile and displaced populations is vital. A robust multisectoral approach is critical to tackling underlying drivers of TB—especially poverty and undernutrition—which disproportionately affect these communities and fuel ongoing transmission.34 35 Furthermore, global coordination by United Nations agencies such as WHO, UNHCR and IOM must be preserved and adequately funded, taking into account the critical role these institutions have in setting standards, supporting surveillance and research providing technical assistance and catalysing funding support to governments. Without bold political commitment and a whole-of-government approach, countries risk setbacks, including the spread of drug-resistant TB strains.

Poland has exemplified strong political leadership and commitment in addressing TB among refugees and migrants, particularly in response to the influx of Ukrainian refugees. By implementing the European Union Temporary Protection Directive, Poland ensured full medical access for displaced populations. In 2022, the Ministry of Health—supported by WHO Regional Office for Europe and Médecins Sans Frontières—launched the Emergency People-Centered Multidrug-Resistant-TB Response Project.36 This initiative introduced WHO-aligned, patient-focused care models, including video-supported ambulatory treatment, shorter drug-resistant TB regimens and upgraded diagnostics. Key activities and outcomes included reduced treatment costs through home-based care, WHO-donated TB medications benefiting primarily non-Polish patients, health worker training and a strengthened laboratory network for rapid diagnosis. The establishment of Poland’s National TB Consilium further enhanced care for people with complex needs. This coordinated, multisectoral response demonstrates how decisive political action, international collaboration and adaptable health systems can drive an effective TB response—even amid crisis—and underscores the need for sustained efforts to combat drug-resistant TB.

Ensuring the TB response is fully resourced

Second, the 2025 funding cuts threaten to exacerbate an already underfunded global TB response. In 2023, only 26% of the $22 billion annually needed for TB prevention and care was available, leaving a massive shortfall.1 To ensure a well-resourced TB response for refugees and migrants, a two-pronged approach is needed. In countries experiencing acute health system breakdowns, international partners must rapidly mobilise emergency stabilisation funds specifically targeted to sustain TB services for displaced and mobile populations. This support must be tailored to the unique vulnerabilities of low-income settings, where even minor disruptions can leave refugees and migrants without life-saving care. Higher-burden countries with stronger fiscal capacity can significantly increase domestic investments in TB services for these groups. Reliance on external aid may no longer be viable in all contexts and may fall short of addressing the scale and complexity of TB among migrants and refugees. Governments must embed TB financing for refugees and migrants into core national budgets and development strategies, recognising it as a vital investment in health security and social cohesion. A whole-of-government approach—engaging sectors such as housing, labour, social protection and justice—is essential to leverage resources from these sectors, ensure broader protections for workers and to build a comprehensive, inclusive and sustainable TB response.

For instance, the Global Fund has been instrumental in supporting regional TB responses, especially for displaced populations.37 A special initiative in the Mekong region, covering Thailand, Vietnam, Cambodia, Myanmar and Lao People’s Democratic Republic, supported by the Global Fund and coordinated by IOM, played a major role in reducing TB among refugees and migrants. It focused on reaching underserved mobile populations—including cross-border migrants, refugees and other at-risk groups—through systematic TB screening, peer outreach and community-based care. By linking over 9000 diagnosed individuals to treatment and training nearly 1000 community volunteers, the initiative demonstrated the power of cross-border collaboration and targeted interventions in closing TB care gaps among migrants, with international donor support.9 38 Sustaining the same level of funding for these kinds of complex responses to TB is imperative; however, in reality, this may prove challenging, given grant reprioritisation for the current Global Fund grant cycle.

Ensuring access to people-centred TB services through primary healthcare

Third, countries must act urgently to ensure uninterrupted, equitable TB care for refugees and migrants, especially amid growing fiscal pressures and shrinking health budgets. These populations must be fully included in universal health coverage, with access to comprehensive, people-centred TB services delivered through primary healthcare.33 Without this, delays in diagnosis and treatment are inevitable, as illustrated during the COVID-19 pandemic, which saw increased TB incidence and mortality due to reduced access.39 With funding constraints now intensifying, some countries are scaling back essential WHO-recommended TB interventions—such as screening and preventive treatment—putting at-risk populations in even greater danger. In times of economic strain, there is a heightened risk that the health of migrants and refugees will be deprioritised due to political pressures and resource competition. However, many of the interventions needed to prevent, detect and treat TB are cost-effective and particularly impactful for these at-risk groups.40 In addition to mobilising transitional financing, governments must institutionalise equitable access through stronger policy and legal frameworks that guarantee care for all, regardless of legal status or nationality. Addressing stigma and discrimination is equally vital. This includes training health workers in culturally competent, rights-based care; engaging communities to dispel TB-related myths; and establishing confidential mechanisms to report discriminatory practices.

Qatar offers a compelling example of a comprehensive national approach to TB prevention and care that ensures universal access for all, including refugees and migrants. Anchored by the Communicable Disease Center under the Ministry of Public Health,41 the country’s TB programme integrates a multidisciplinary care model, free diagnosis and treatment and holistic support services. Systematic pre-arrival and post-arrival screenings for migrants from high TB-burden countries, annual health checks for workers at high-risk and targeted interventions for vulnerable groups such as the immunocompromised and incarcerated contribute to early detection and effective management. The provision of effective treatment, including newer TB drugs and video-assisted treatment support, is employed alongside strict infection control measures and contact tracing. Public awareness campaigns, strategically involving community leaders, have effectively reduced stigma. Qatar’s efforts have led to a significant decline in TB incidence—from 41 per 1 00 000 in 2002 to 23 per 1 00 000 in 2024—while maintaining high treatment success rates and low levels of drug resistance. These efforts underscore Qatar’s leadership in TB prevention and care and its contribution to the global response.

Another example is from the Intergovernmental Authority on Development (IGAD) in the African region.42 In collaboration with UNHCR and partners, IGAD implemented a regional project to combat TB among refugees in six East African countries, resulting in an increase in TB case notification rates from 87 to 131 per 1 00 000 (2017–2022), with community referrals increasing from 20.7% to 37.5% (2019–2021). Treatment success exceeded 90% in Kenya, South Sudan and Uganda. These results underscore the value of integrated, community-driven and cross-border approaches to TB care in refugee settings.

The path forward

Amid a challenging global context, WHO, UNHCR and IOM, in close collaboration with governments and key partners, are providing critical support to countries in prioritising and implementing the innovative solutions and policy options set out in the WISH report. Drawing on its comprehensive TB data systems across all six regions and a suite of cutting-edge tools—including the digital TB data hub, the integrated health costing tool and updated TB guidelines—WHO equips countries to make bold, evidence-based decisions tailored to the needs of refugees and migrants. As part of its mandate, WHO is also responsible for monitoring progress on addressing TB among refugees and migrants, including the implementation of key policy options. Findings are expected to be presented at the 2026 WISH.

Conclusion

Urgent, coordinated action is imperative to confront the devastating impact of TB on refugees and migrants and to address the structural drivers that drive the epidemic. Yet, these efforts are increasingly threatened by shrinking investments in health and development, making sustained political leadership, reliable resourcing and uninterrupted access to high-quality TB services, even more critical than ever. Maintaining the same level of attention, political commitment and financing for health, TB and migration will require sustained advocacy and innovative approaches, considering the current geopolitical pressures and tensions. Strong global leadership and sustained advocacy are needed. The policy solutions serve as a blueprint, are evidence-based and are ready for implementation. What is urgently required is the political will to act. By embedding inclusive, migrant- and refugee-sensitive TB responses into national health strategies and leveraging multisectoral partnerships, countries can transform ambition into impact. The goal of ending TB is within reach—and ensuring the health and dignity of refugees and migrants must remain central to that vision.
