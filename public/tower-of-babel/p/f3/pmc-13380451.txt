Laser-based mid-IR spectroscopy as a rapid analytical technique for the quantification of human milk proteins
Authors: Isabel Ten-Doménech, Shilpa Vijayakumar, Andreas Schwaighofer, Georg Ramer, Sabrina P Van den Oever, Alba Moreno-Giménez, Anna Parra-Llorca, María J Vaya, María Gormaz, Helmut K Mayer, Guillermo Quintás, Julia Kuligowski, Bernhard Lendl
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHuman milk (HM) provides essential nutrients for infant growth and development. When own mother's milk is unavailable, donor human milk (DHM) is used and typically pasteurized, which may alter protein integrity. This study presents the use of external cavity-quantum cascade laser (EC-QCL) mid-infrared spectroscopy, as a rapid, non-destructive technique, to quantify both individual and total proteins in 276 HM and DHM samples. The method showed high accuracy (≥84% explained variance) and strong correlation with reference techniques. Pasteurization significantly reduced α-lactalbumin (24%) and lactoferrin (14%), while casein and total protein declined slightly (3%). Protein levels decreased notably during early lactation, then stabilized. In the cohort of the present study, no statistically significant differences in protein content were observed between preterm and term milk. EC-QCL spectroscopy also differentiated raw and pasteurized DHM. These results support EC-QCL as a reliable tool for clinical use and quality control, with implications for improving processing methods to preserve the nutritional and immunological integrity of DHM.Keywords: Infrared spectroscopy, External cavity quantum cascade laser (EC-QCL), Pasteurization, Protein, Casein, Lactoferrin, Alpha-lactalbumin

Introduction

Human milk (HM) is considered the gold standard for infant nutrition as it provides a unique balance of both macronutrients and micronutrients as well as bioactive compounds and immunological factors required for optimal development (Masi and Stewart, 2024). Among its components, proteins in HM play a pivotal role in supporting infant growth and development contributing especially to immune defense (Zhu and Dingess, 2019). In the framework of prematurity, when own mother's milk is insufficient, donor human milk (DHM) becomes the preferred alternative. However, to safeguard receptors, DHM is often subjected to Holder pasteurization (62.5 °C for 30 min followed by rapid cooling to 4 °C) to inactivate potential pathogens present in the milk. This thermal treatment is known to affect protein structure and functionality (Piemontese et al., 2019), although the magnitude and protein-specificity of these changes in HM remain variable across studies (Paulaviciene et al., 2020a).

Accurate determination of protein content in HM and DHM is essential for understanding its nutritional profile and ensuring optimal feeding practices and personalized nutrition, especially for preterm infants (PIs). In clinical practice, quantitation of milk protein content and other macronutrients relies usually on the use of commercial devices such as the Miris HMA® – Human Milk Analyzer (HMA) (Uppsala, Sweden) (Kwan et al., 2020), which employs mid-infrared (MIR) transmission spectroscopy. Alternative spectroscopic techniques, such as attenuated total reflectance-Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (ATR-FTIR) (Bērziņš et al., 2021), FTIR in transmission mode (Navarro-Tapia et al., 2024), near-infrared (NIR) spectroscopy (Jo et al., 2020; Jorge dos Santos et al., 2021), Raman spectroscopy (Bērziņš et al., 2021), and ultrasound-based (Ruan et al., 2022) methods, have also been explored and some integrated into commercially available HM analyzers. These techniques offer rapid and non-destructive analysis with minimal sample preparation and the potential for accurate, real-time analysis of HM composition. Notable examples include the MilkoScan™ from Foss (Hillerød, Denmark) or SpectraStar from Unity Scientific (Brookfield, United States) based on FTIR in transmission and NIR spectroscopy, respectively. The accuracy of these devices for protein determination has often been compared to one another (Fusch et al., 2015), and with different settings (Kwan et al., 2020), as well as evaluated against well-established reference methods for protein determination, such as the Kjeldahl method and the Bradford assay (Navarro-Tapia et al., 2024; Giuffrida et al., 2019; Zhu et al., 2017). While the Kjeldahl method remains a reference technique for total protein determination, it is labor-intensive, requires extensive sample preparation, and provides only a bulk estimate of protein content. On the other hand, the Bradford assay offers a faster and simpler alternative for total protein determination but is susceptible to matrix interferences. In addition, both methods are primarily intended for total protein determination and do not provide information on individual protein fractions. This represents an important limitation, as there is growing interest in the determination of individual proteins in HM, as they exert distinct biological functions and variability during lactation (Lönnerdal, 2016). This can be easily accomplished through enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISA) (Paulaviciene et al., 2020a), although it might be susceptible to matrix effects, which can interfere with antibody binding and lead to inaccurate quantification. Additionally, ELISA's dynamic range is relatively narrow, meaning it may require multiple dilutions for accurate quantification of proteins present at very high or very low concentrations, increasing the time and complexity of the assay. On the other hand, the advent of proteomics resulted in the quantitative analysis of a plethora of individual HM proteins (Dayon et al., 2021). However, its associated complexity, high cost, and requirements for specialized facilities and expertise make proteomics unsuitable for routine clinical or field applications. For this reason, simpler, targeted, and robust analytical methods for the individual protein quantitation are still of interest for clinical research. Manso et al. (2007) developed a capillary zone electrophoresis method for the separation of the major HM proteins and their follow-up throughout lactation. However, the developed methodology underestimated the content of the most electropositive whey proteins, such as lactoferrin and lysozyme (Manso et al., 2007). On the other hand, even though liquid-chromatography (LC) coupled with UV detection has been applied to the quantitation on individual milk proteins in bovine milk (Boitz et al., 2015) and other mammals, its application to HM analysis is scarce. In addition, it requires extensive sample preparation (i.e., casein precipitation and denaturation of whey proteins) and relatively long analysis time. More recently, Montemurro et al. developed an external cavity-quantum cascade laser (EC-QCL) setup for IR transmission measurements that allowed the quantitative analysis of casein, alpha-lactalbumin, and beta-lactoglobulin in commercial bovine milk with minimal sample preparation (Montemurro et al., 2019; Kuligowski et al., 2017; Schwaighofer et al., 2018). The present study aims to: (i) develop and validate EC-QCL mid-infrared spectroscopy-based PLS regression models for the simultaneous quantification of α-lactalbumin, lactoferrin, casein, and total protein in HM with minimal sample preparation; (ii) evaluate the performance of the developed models against established reference methods (LC and Kjeldahl analysis) using an independent external set; and (iii) apply the developed methodology to a large cohort of HM and DHM samples collected within the frame of the Spanish-Mediterranean NUTRISHIELD clinical study (Ramos-Garcia et al., 2023) to investigate the influence of lactation stage, prematurity, and Holder pasteurization on HM protein composition. To the best of our knowledge, this represents the first application of EC-QCL spectroscopy for the quantification of individual proteins in HM and its evaluation in a large clinical cohort.

Materials and methods

Standards and reagents

α-Lactalbumin from HM (≥95%), lactoferrin from HM (≥85%), casein sodium salt from bovine milk, and sodium dodecyl sulfate (≥98.5%) were acquired from Sigma-Aldrich Química SL (Madrid, Spain).

All chemical analyses for Kjeldahl and HPLC reference methods were performed in quadruplicate. Chemicals used for sample preparation (analytical grade) and solvents for chromatographic analysis (HPLC grade) were purchased from Roth (Karlsruhe, Germany), Sigma (St. Louis, MO, USA), Fluka (Buchs, Switzerland) or Fischer Chemicals (Geel, Belgium). Ultrapure water prepared using a SG Ultra Clear UV system (Siemens Water Technologies, Warrendale, PA, USA) was used throughout all experiments.

Preparation of solutions

Individual stock solutions of α-lactalbumin, lactoferrin, and casein at concentrations of 10.95, 12.45, and 9.94 g L−1, respectively, were prepared by direct weighing of the corresponding amount of lyophilized protein powder in a volumetric flask and dissolving in water.

Matrix-containing calibration solutions were prepared as follows. A HM sample with known concentrations of individual proteins determined by the reference methods (see below) was employed. Three levels of HM content (i.e., 80, 50 and 20%) were considered to which different amounts of aqueous standard solutions of α-lactalbumin, lactoferrin and casein were added. The final concentrations of each protein were calculated considering the exact concentration determined by the corresponding reference method and the amount of spiking solution added (see Table S1).

HM samples

23 raw and 26 pasteurized DHM samples were provided by 49 donors admitted after the routine screening at the HM bank (Banco de Leche Materna de la Comunidad Valenciana, Valencia, Spain). In addition, 199 HM samples and 77 raw/pasteurized DHM samples, of which 37 were paired, collected within the NUTRISHIELD study, were employed. Own mother's milk was collected in the morning (between 7 and 10 a.m.) from one breast after full expression, whereas DHM was collected following the standard operating procedure routinely employed at the hospital and the HM bank. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The Ethics Committee for Biomedical Research of the Health Research Institute La Fe (Valencia, Spain) approved the study protocol (approval number 2019-289-1), and mothers gave their written consent to participate.

EC-QCL set-up and HM analysis

Laser-based IR spectra were obtained using a ChemDetect Analyzer (Daylight Solutions Inc., San Diego, USA), featuring a custom-made 25 μm transmission flow cell with calcium fluoride windows, and an EC-QCL that covers the spectral range of 1350 to 1770 cm−1 (Schwaighofer et al., 2021). The laser head was externally cooled to 17 °C. For analysis of HM/DHM samples, 91 scans were recorded and averaged over a 45-s measurement period. The spectrometer was purged with dry air to minimize atmospheric water vapor interference. The spectra were processed using Savitzky-Golay smoothing (2nd order, 15-point window). Data acquisition was performed with the ChemDetect software, and subsequent data processing and analysis were carried out using custom code developed in Matlab R2021b (MathWorks, Inc., Natick, MA, 2014).

1.5 mL aliquots of collected HM/DHM samples were thawed at room temperature, inverted two to three times and then warmed up in a water bath (40 °C for 5 min). Then, samples were homogenized for 2 s with an ultrasonic processor (130 W, 20 Hz, amplitude 75%). For spectra acquisition, 0.5 mL of liquid sample were injected into the transmission cell of the laser-based IR spectrometer with a suitable syringe. An H2O background spectrum was recorded before each measurement. The transmission cell was cleaned by flushing with 1 mL of H2O twice, once with 1 mL 1% sodium dodecyl sulfate, and three times with 1 mL H2O after each measurement.

Reference methods

Kjeldahl analysis

Total nitrogen (TN), non-casein N (NCN), and non-protein N (NPN) were determined by the Kjeldahl method according to the corresponding IDF/ISO standard (ISO 17997, 2004; ISO 8968, 2014; ISO 8968-4:2016, 2016). Whey protein N and casein N were calculated from the difference between NCN and NPN, and between TN and NCN, respectively. Protein equivalents were calculated from nitrogen data using the factor 6.38 (ISO 8968, 2014).

Protein quantitation

Sample preparation was conducted according to (Bobe et al., 1998). Frozen aliquots of DHM samples were thawed and incubated in a reducing buffer solution (also containing 6.1 M guanidine hydrochloride, 5.37 mM sodium citrate, and 20 mM dithiothreitol, pH = 6.8) to fully dissolve caseins and whey proteins and hence enable the quantitation of individual HM proteins (e.g., α-lactalbumin, lactoferrin). Samples were filtered through 0.20 μm Minisart RC4 filters (Sartorius, Goettingen, Germany) and reversed-phase high-performance liquid chromatography (RP-HPLC) was applied to determine HM proteins. A Waters Alliance e2695 HPLC system (Waters Corporation, Milford, MA, USA) equipped with a C8-column (Zorbax 300SB-C8; 3.5 μm, 4.6 × 150 mm; Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) was used for separation. Gradient elution was performed according to (Van Den Oever and Mayer, 2026). Monitoring of column eluates was carried out at 205 nm with a Waters 2489 UV/Vis detector interfaced with a PC running Waters Empower™ 3 software for data acquisition and management. Method performance was assessed following the EURACHEM guide (Magnusson, 2014), including evaluation of analytical parameters such as linearity, precision, accuracy, and limits of detection and quantification (LOD and LOQ, respectively) (Van Den Oever and Mayer, 2026).

Results and discussion

EC-QCL spectroscopy of HM and HM proteins

Fig. 1 shows laser-based IR spectra of individual protein solutions as well as a spiked HM sample at varying dilution prepared for calibration measured against a background of 100% distilled H2O. The position and shape of the amide I band changes depending on the secondary structure of the protein. The irregular and extended α-helical secondary structures of casein lead to a broad IR absorbance band with a maximum at 1655 cm−1 (Malin et al., 2001; Farrel et al., 2001). α-Lactalbumin is predominantly composed of α-helical structure elements and shows a narrow amide I band with a maximum at 1653 cm−1 (Dabrowska et al., 2022). Lactoferrin features both α-helix and β-sheet structures resulting in a amide I band maximum at 1654 cm−1 with a shoulder at 1631 cm−1 (Keiderling and Xu, 2002).

(A) Laser-based IR spectra of 2 g L−1 α-lactalbumin, lactoferrin, and casein. (B) Laser-based IR spectra of HM at dilution levels (i.e., 20, 50, and 80% of HM) spiked with α-lactalbumin (0.9 g L−1), lactoferrin (1.0 g L−1) and casein (1.0 g L−1).

In the HM spectra measured against an H2O background (see Fig. 1), negative absorbance values varying with the dilution factor applied to the HM sample (20, 50 or 80% of HM) were observed. These negative bands are observed because of the overlap of the amide I band from HM proteins with the strong deformation vibration of H2O located at 1639 cm−1, and due to the fact that HM samples also contain other molecules, such as carbohydrates and fat, resulting in their reduced water content compared with the reference solution (distilled water). Fig. 1 clearly illustrates spectral differences between individual protein standards and HM samples spiked with different contents of these proteins. These differences in the position, shape, and relative intensity of the amide I and amide II bands reflect differences in protein structure and concentration, providing the chemical information required for discrimination and quantification of the studied proteins. However, the strong overlap between protein absorption bands in the studied IR region highlights the need for a multivariate approach for the quantification of individual proteins.

EC-QCL based IR spectroscopic quantitation of individual milk proteins

Direct protein quantitation by EC-QCL based IR spectroscopy in HM samples was achieved by building Partial Least Square (PLS) regression models for each individual protein as well as for the total protein content. PLS regression was selected as the multivariate modeling strategy because of its well-established suitability for spectroscopic datasets characterized by a large number of highly collinear variables and overlapping spectral features. In the amide I region (1600-1700 cm−1), the peptide bond absorptions of α-lactalbumin, lactoferrin, caseins, and other proteins overlap extensively (see Fig. 1) and water absorption and matrix effects further increase spectral complexity, precluding univariate quantification approaches. PLS addresses this challenge by extracting latent variables (LVs) that capture the maximum covariance between the spectral data and the reference concentration values, enabling simultaneous quantification of multiple analytes from a single spectrum. Thus, for PLS modeling, a previously characterized HM sample using chromatographic reference methods spiked with protein standard solutions was used as shown in Table S1. Table 1 shows the parameters applied for each model that include details on the selected spectral range, employed data pre-processing to enhance the model's ability to differentiate between overlapping spectral features and baseline correction, number of LVs, root mean square error of cross validation (RMSECV) estimated by leave-one-out CV, and % explained variance. While quantitation of total proteins and casein was achieved by incorporating a broader spectral range to capture the overall protein content, for α-lactalbumin and lactoferrin, the amide I region (i.e., 1700-1600 cm−1) was selected. The high degree of explained variance (i.e., ≥84%) jointly with the low RMSECV (i.e., ≤0.25 g L−1) achieved for all PLS models indicate their capability for quantifying these targeted proteins in HM samples.

Method validation and comparison with reference values

For external validation of PLS models, 49 non-paired raw and pasteurized DHM samples with known concentrations of proteins (i.e., α-lactalbumin, lactoferrin, and total protein) determined by reference methods were employed. For casein, only non-pasteurized samples (N = 23) were employed, as the casein nitrogen estimated through Kjeldahl analysis of heat-treated milk is artificially high because of whey-protein denaturation (ISO 17997, 2004). Clinical descriptors of the population used for method validation are presented in Table S2.

Fig. 2 shows regression analysis and Bland-Altman plot of reference and PLS predicted concentrations for each protein and the total protein content. As can be observed, confidence intervals (CIs) of the slope and intercept of regression curves included 1 and 0, respectively, with correlation coefficients (r) ≥0.6 and p-values <0.001, confirming that the results obtained with EC-QCL spectroscopy do not provide biased results when compared to the corresponding reference method. Bland-Altman plots showed mean (bias) and standard deviation of differences between reference and predicted concentrations. Mean and standard deviation of differences were 0.21 (±0.68), −0.07 (±0.42), −0.66 (±0.96), and 0.20 (±0.57) g L−1 for α-lactalbumin, lactoferrin, casein, and total protein, respectively; with upper and lower limits of agreement ranging between +1.58 and −2.57 g L−1. While PLS prediction for α-lactalbumin, lactoferrin, and total protein demonstrate good agreement with small bias and narrow interval for limits of agreement, PLS prediction for casein was more limited. This could be attributed on one hand to the small sample size employed for casein content comparison since only raw DHM samples were considered. On the other hand, the present casein quantification approach is based on the use of bovine sodium caseinate as calibration standard instead of HM casein, which differs from its bovine counterpart in subunit composition (Liao et al., 2017), as well as in post-translational modifications and supramolecular organization (Dingess et al., 2021). These structural differences are reflected in the amide I and amide II spectral profiles and may therefore affect the accuracy of the PLS model. While the matrix-matched calibration strategy employed here partially compensates for matrix-related spectral contributions, the limited availability of commercial HM casein standards represents a practical constraint shared by most analytical studies in this field.

Furthermore, it should be noted that the correlation coefficients reported here were obtained from an independent external validation set comprising highly heterogeneous HM samples collected across different lactation stages and donor characteristics. Such biological variability is expected to affect correlation-based metrics. Therefore, model applicability was not evaluated solely on the basis of the correlation coefficient, but also through regression analysis, including confidence intervals for slope and intercept, and Bland-Altman assessment of agreement with the reference methods.

The EC-QCL spectroscopic method was applied to the complete set of HM samples (N = 276), providing concentrations of α-lactalbumin, lactoferrin, casein, and total protein (Table S3). Models for α-lactalbumin and lactoferrin retrieved a negative prediction for 5 (i.e., 1.8%) and 6 (i.e., 2.2%) samples, respectively, which were not considered for further analysis. Fig. 3 shows concentrations of α-lactalbumin, lactoferrin, casein, and total protein throughout the first six months of lactation in HM of mothers of PIs and TIs. Total protein ranged from 5.2 to 21.1 g L−1 with a median (interquartile) of 9.9 (3.5) g L−1, with casein having the highest average concentration among the three induvial proteins quantified. This is in agreement with findings reported in literature, where individual proteins, whey proteins and casein subunits were quantified through LC-MS/MS (Liao et al., 2017). HM of mothers from PIs generally presented higher protein concentrations compared to HM samples from TIs, although no significant differences between groups were observed (p-value >0.05, Wilcoxon ranksum test). On the other hand, protein concentrations tended to decrease significantly during lactation, particularly when comparing the time point of achieving complete enteral nutrition (CEN; >150 mL/kg/day) in PIs or recovery of birth weight (RBW) in TIs to one month and later time points, after which levels stabilized. This decline is thought to reflect the dynamic adaptation of HM composition to the changing nutritional requirements of the growing infant. Higher protein concentrations during early lactation support the rapid growth and development that characterize the neonatal period, whereas the subsequent decrease accompanies the transition toward mature milk and the progressively lower protein requirements of the infant. The higher protein content of preterm milk as compared to term milk and its rapid decrease in the early postnatal period has been consistently reported in literature (Boyce et al., 2016). However, significant differences between the groups are not always achieved due to small sample size and the high inter- and intra-individual variability of milk composition (Hsu et al., 2014; Fischer Fumeaux et al., 2019). Nevertheless, the observed trend toward higher protein concentrations in preterm milk is biologically plausible and has been interpreted as an adaptive response aimed at supporting the increased nutritional demands associated with premature birth and accelerated postnatal growth. Consistent with this concept, supplementation of both OMM and DHM is commonly required in neonatal practice to achieve the protein intake recommended for preterm infants.

Influence of pasteurization on HM protein composition

The EC-QCL spectroscopic method was also employed to gain insight into changes in HM protein composition induced by Holder pasteurization. Fig. 4 shows EC-QCL spectra of non-pasteurized and pasteurized DHM samples illustrating distinctive changes in the amide I region of the IR spectrum, with pasteurized HM samples showing lower protein contents. Employing PLS-discriminant analysis (PLS-DA), a significant separation between samples collected before vs. after pasteurization was observed, indicating that the variance in the collected IR spectra induced by pasteurization can be exploited to differentiate pasteurized and non-pasteurized HM samples. Finally, concentrations of all individual as well as total proteins as determined by PLS regression models decreased upon pasteurization, in agreement with other literature reports (Piemontese et al., 2019; Adhisivam et al., 2019; Paulaviciene et al., 2020b). The mean decreases for α-lactalbumin, lactoferrin, casein, and total protein were 0.63, 0.17, 0.18, and 0.24 g L−1, respectively, representing percentage losses of 24%, 14%, 3%, and 3%. PIs require higher protein intake (3.5-4.5 g/kg/day) to support growth and development, and protein fortification of HM is a common practice in neonatal services (Arslanoglu et al., 2019). Given this, the 3% protein loss in total protein and casein is unlikely to be clinically relevant. Moreover, previous studies did not observe a significant decrease in the total protein content of HM upon pasteurization (Binder et al., 2023; Kotrri et al., 2016). However, the greater losses of α-lactalbumin (24%) and lactoferrin (14%) might have biological significance, particularly regarding the digestibility, absorption, antimicrobial, and immunomodulatory properties of HM, beyond what is reflected by changes in total protein content alone.

It is important to emphasize that this study specifically examines the effect of Holder pasteurization on HM and does not account for the potential impact of freezing and storage, which is known to influence protein content of HM (Chang et al., 2013). On the other hand, an additional limitation of this method is that it quantifies the total casein content rather than distinguishing the individual casein subunits present in HM (i.e., α-, β-, and k-caseins). Furthermore, the modeling was conducted using bovine-derived casein, which does not fully represent the compositional variability of caseins present in HM.

Conclusions

This study represents the first application of EC-QCL spectroscopy for the quantification of individual proteins in HM in a large cohort study, demonstrating its potential as a rapid, accurate, and non-destructive analytical technique. The developed PLS models provided robust quantitation of α-lactalbumin, lactoferrin, casein, and total protein, showing strong agreement with conventional reference methods. In the cohort of the present study, no statistically significant differences in protein content were observed between preterm and term milk. A clear decline in protein concentration over the course of lactation was evident. On the other hand, the findings highlight the selective impact of Holder pasteurization on HM protein composition. While total protein and casein exhibited minimal losses (3%), α-lactalbumin and lactoferrin were significantly reduced (24% and 14%, respectively). Given the essential role of these bioactive proteins in digestion, absorption, and immune defense, their reduction upon pasteurization may have clinical implications, particularly for those PIs who rely on DHM as their primary source of nutrition. Additionally, this study focused exclusively on the effects of Holder pasteurization and did not assess the potential impact of freezing and storage, which are known to affect HM protein content. Future research should incorporate these variables to provide a more comprehensive understanding of HM processing and its nutritional implications. Furthermore, the use of HM-derived casein fractions as calibration materials should be explored to further improve the specificity and accuracy of casein quantification models. If the cost of EC-QCL spectrometers can be substantially reduced, the developed methods could be implemented for quality control of HM samples collected, processed, and stored in milk banks for administration to PIs. With more detailed analyses of milk composition in relation to infant health and development planned for future research, EC-QCL spectroscopy may also support the selection and preparation of milk tailored to the specific nutritional needs of individual infants.
