In Silico Exploration of the Endocrine-Disrupting Potential of Phthalate Esters through Structural Interaction with the Human Androgen Receptor
Authors: Ariana Alarco-Cusi, Andrea Apaza-Chaña, Margot Paco-Chipana, Angela Emperatriz Centeno Lopez, Fabrizio Johnson-Corrales, Karel Mena-Ulecia, Miguel Chavez-Fumagalli, Haruna Luz Barazorda-Ccahuana
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Phthalate esters are ubiquitous environmental pollutants with a well-documented potential to disrupt steroid hormone signaling, particularly through interactions with the human androgen receptor (hAR). This study used a structure-based virtual screening approach to evaluate phthalates using testosterone as the reference ligand. Three independent molecular docking platforms were used to assess binding affinity and pose consistency. The top-ranked compound underwent further investigation, including the analysis of quantum chemical descriptors, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, binding free energy estimation, per-residue energy decomposition, and noncovalent interaction profiling. Biphenyl phthalate emerged as the leading candidate, exhibiting electrophilicity and electron-accepting capacity greater than that of testosterone, suggesting an enhanced potential for receptor engagement. MD simulations demonstrated that the biphenyl phthalate–hAR complex maintained structural stability comparable to that of testosterone-bound complex. Although MM/PBSA analysis indicated a stronger binding affinity for testosterone, primarily due to electrostatic and hydrogen-bond interactions, biphenyl phthalate binding was predominantly stabilized by van der Waals forces. These contrasting interaction patterns were further supported by energy decomposition and noncovalent interaction analyses. In conclusion, biphenyl phthalate mimics key structural and electronic features of testosterone, supporting its ability to engage hAR and reinforcing its potential classification as an endocrine-disrupting compound.

Introduction

Phthalate esters (PAEs) are synthetic chemicals widely employed as plasticizers in the production of polyvinyl chloride (PVC) and other polymeric materials to enhance flexibility, durability, and processability. − These compounds are ubiquitous in consumer products such as food packaging, cosmetics, medical devices, and toys, leading to widespread human exposure via ingestion, inhalation, dermal absorption, and even medical procedures.

PAEs are a prominent class of endocrine-disrupting chemicals (EDCs), recognized for their ability to interfere with hormonal systems in both humans and wildlife. Due to their structural similarity to endogenous hormones, PAEs can mimic or antagonize natural hormones, particularly by binding with nuclear receptors such as androgen and estrogen receptors. This disruption of endocrine signaling pathways has been associated with developmental, reproductive, and metabolic disorders, positioning PAEs as contaminants of significant environmental concern. −

Among the primary endocrine targets, the human androgen receptor (hAR) plays a crucial role in regulating male sexual development, reproductive function, and the maintenance of secondary sexual characteristics. − The hAR is a nuclear receptor that functions as a ligand-activated transcription factor, modulating the expression of androgen-responsive genes upon binding to endogenous ligands such as testosterone and dihydrotestosterone. Interference with this signaling pathway by exogenous compounds like PAEs has been linked to adverse effects, including testicular dysgenesis, reduced sperm quality, and hormonal imbalances. ,

Emerging evidence suggests that certain PAEs can bind to the hAR and modulate its activity by either mimicking or antagonizing endogenous androgens. − However, the experimental elucidation of these molecular mechanisms is often limited by the low environmental concentrations of PAEs, the short-lived nature of reactive intermediates, and the complexity of environmental matrices. Laboratory conditions often fail to replicate the physicochemical variability present in real-world settings, and analytical limitations may compromise the detection of specific reaction (interactions or degradation) intermediates. In this context, in silico approaches have become indispensable tools for predicting the molecular behavior, biological interactions, and environmental fate of such contaminants. Computational techniques such as virtual screening, density functional theory (DFT) and molecular dynamics (MD) simulations enable the exploration of degradation pathways, binding affinities, and conformational dynamics under well-defined theoretical conditions. − These methods not only reduce experimental costs but also provide mechanistic insights that support hypothesis generation, experimental design, and environmental risk assessment. −

The present study aims to investigate the potential endocrine-disrupting activity of a curated set of phthalate esters through in silico interaction analysis with the hAR. Virtual screening techniques were employed to assess the structural affinity of each PAE for the hAR ligand-binding domain. The top-ranked complexes identified through molecular docking were further analyzed via MD simulations using GROMACS to evaluate the stability and conformational behavior of receptor–ligand interactions over time. These simulations enabled the characterization of structural fluctuations and refinement of binding poses under physiologically relevant conditions. In parallel, binding free energy calculations were performed using the Molecular Mechanics Generalized Born Surface Area (MM/GBSA) method to quantitatively estimate the thermodynamic favorability of each interaction. Following the MD simulations, key noncovalent interactions responsible for stabilizing the ligand–receptor complexes were identified and visualized, providing atomistic insights into binding specificity. Overall, the findings contribute to comprehensive understanding of structure–activity relationships (SARs) among PAEs and emphasize the utility of in silico methodologies for early stage hazard identification and risk assessment of potential EDCs.

Computational Details

Structural Preparation of the Human Androgen Receptor Model

The crystal structure of the ligand-binding domain of the human androgen receptor (hAR) in complex with testosterone (PDB ID: 2AM9) was obtained from the RCSB-PDB database (https://www.rcsb.org) in November 2023. The selected crystal model was chosen based on its superior structural resolution of 1.64 Å, ensuring high accuracy for subsequent analyses. The model was prepared by removing nonstandard amino acid residues and water molecules. Polar hydrogen atoms were added, and partial charges were assigned. Then, it was energetically minimized for use in molecular docking protocols.

Obtaining and Identifying Phthalate Compounds

The PAEs were selected based on their environmental prevalence, toxicological significance, and structural relevance, reflecting their high representativeness as emerging contaminants with potential EDC activity. The selection and design of the compound list were guided by their percentage of structural similarity to testosterone, considering key molecular criteria such as molecular weight, logP, and polar surface area. All compounds were retrieved from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov) in November 2023 to ensure consistency and accuracy in structural information for subsequent in silico analyses. The chemical structures were subsequently standardized and optimized: basic geometric refinements were applied, conformational redundancies were removed, ionization states were adjusted to physiological pH, and the orientation and rotation of each compound were normalized to ensure accurate evaluation during the virtual screening process.

Virtual Screening

To identify compounds with the highest affinity for the hAR, exhaustive virtual screening was performed using three independent platforms: DockThor, iGEMDOCK v2.1, and PyRx v0.9.8 (AutoDock Vina), thereby enhancing the reliability of the analysis. Each software program employs unique search algorithms and energy evaluation functions, facilitating a more robust perspective and reducing the likelihood of false positives. Each of PAEs was docked into the hAR active site using the same binding pocket and spatial orientation as the cocrystallized testosterone in the original crystal structure. Binding energy values, key molecular interactions, and the consistency of ligand orientation within the receptor site were systematically compared. The compound exhibiting the highest binding affinity across all three tools was identified as the top candidate. The top-ranked compounds were subsequently subjected to molecular dynamics simulations and binding free energy calculations to evaluate their stability and interaction profiles in a dynamic environment.

Additionally, to assess the environmental toxicity profile of the top-ranked compoundidentified by its consistently high binding affinity across all three docking platformsthe ECOSAR (Ecological Structure–Activity Relationships) program was employed. This predictive tool estimates both acute (short-term) and chronic (long-term or delayed) toxicity in key aquatic species, including fish, invertebrates, and algae, based on structure–activity relationship (SAR) models. By leveraging the molecular structure of the compound, ECOSAR infers ecotoxicological end points relevant to regulatory and environmental safety assessments. These results were then integrated with physicochemical parameters and bioaccumulation indicators to support a comprehensive hazard evaluation.

Molecular Geometry Optimization

In this study, the top-ranked compound from the virtual screeningidentified based on its strongest zscore toward the hAR was selected alongside testosterone for further analysis. The molecular geometries were fully optimized using the B3LYP functional with the 6-311++G­(d,p) basis set for all atoms, as implemented in ORCA software version 5.0.1.1. The ground-state nature of each structure was confirmed by the absence of imaginary frequencies in the vibrational analysis. These optimized geometries were then employed to calculate the global chemical reactivity descriptors, as detailed in the following section.

Global reactivity descriptors, derived from conceptual Density Functional Theory (DFT), have been widely employed to predict reactive sites within molecules, as well as to clarify reaction mechanisms and regioselectivity patterns. Fukui Function Calculations

The calculations for electrophilic, nucleophilic, and radical Fukui functions were performed using the optimized geometries of all compounds. Single-point energy calculations were carried out on the neutral, cationic (N – 1), and anionic (N + 1) states using the B3LYP/6-311++G­(d,p) basis set, as implemented in ORCA software version 5.0.11. The Fukui functions , were derived from Mulliken atomic charges of the three charge states, according to the following equations: f+(r)=ρN+1(r)−ρN(r)(nucleophilicattack) 1 f−(r)=ρN(r)−ρN−1(r)(electrophilicattack) 2 f0(r)=12[ρN+1(r)−ρN−1(r)](radicalattack) 3 Δf(r)=f+(r)−f−(r)(dualdescriptor) 4

Here, ρ N (r) represents the electron density at a point r in space for the neutral molecule with N electrons. Where N corresponds to the number of electrons in the molecule. ρ N+1(r) and ρ N–1(r) correspond to the electron densities of the anionic and cationic species, respectivelyobtained by adding an electron to the LUMO or removing one from the HOMO of the neutral molecule.

The dual descriptor Δf(r) provides insight into both nucleophilic and electrophilic reactivity. Its sign is particularly informative: if Δf(r) > 0, the site is more susceptible to nucleophilic attack, identifying it as an electrophilic center. Conversely, if Δf(r) < 0, the site is more prone to electrophilic attack, indicating nucleophilic character.

Molecular Dynamics Simulation

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were conducted using GROMACS v2023 in conjunction with the CHARMM36 force field. Input files for each system were prepared via the CHARMM-GUI web server (https://www.charmm-gui.org/). , Each protein–ligand complex was centered in a cubic simulation box with a 1.5 nm margin between the protein surface and the box boundaries, and solvated using explicit TIP3P water molecules.

Energy minimization was performed using the steepest descent algorithm for 500,000 steps. Subsequently, the systems underwent equilibration in two phases: 100 ps under the canonical ensemble (NVT) followed by 100 ps under the isothermal–isobaric ensemble (NPT), with positional restraints applied throughout. Production MD simulations were then carried out for 500 ns under the NPT ensemble, employing the V-rescale thermostat (maintained at 309.65 K) and the Parrinello–Rahman barostat (set to 1 bar) to ensure thermodynamic stability.

Postsimulation analyses were performed using standard GROMACS utilities. The structural stability and conformational behavior of the ligand–receptor complexes were assessed through root-mean-square deviation (RMSD), root-mean-square fluctuation (RMSF), B-factor analysis, radius of gyration (R g), and solvent-accessible surface area (SASA) profiles. These parameters provided insights into the dynamic stability, flexibility, compactness, and solvent exposure of the systems throughout the simulation trajectories. Protein–ligand interactions were visualized using UCSF ChimeraX and Schrödinger Release 2024-1. All graphical representations were generated with the Matplotlib library. In addition, noncovalent interactions between the androgen receptor and the selected ligands were characterized using the Protein–Ligand Interaction Profiler (PLIP) server (https://plip-tool.biotec.tu-dresden.de/plip-web/plip/index).

Principal Component Analysis (PCA)

Another postsimulation analysis was the principal component analysis (PCA) using the gmx covar utility, which calculates and diagonalizes the mass-weighted covariance matrix of atomic positional fluctuations. Prior to the analysis, the trajectory was preprocessed using GROMACS tools to remove periodic boundary conditions (PBC), center the protein within the simulation box, and align the structures to a common reference frame to eliminate translational and rotational artifacts. The conformational dynamics were subsequently projected onto the first two principal components using gmx anaeig, producing a two-dimensional projection file (2d.xvg). The projection file 2d.xvg was processed in Python, where a two-dimensional histogram of PC1 vs PC2 was generated using NumPy and Pandas to estimate the probability distribution of sampled conformational states.

Free Energy Landscape (FEL) Analysis

The free energy landscape (FEL) was constructed from the molecular dynamics trajectory by analyzing the essential motions captured through PCA. The free energy G(x, y) was then calculated according to the Boltzmann relation using eq : G(x,y)=−kBTlnP(x,y) 5 where G(x, y) is the free energy at the coordinate pair (x, y), k B is the Boltzmann constant, T is the temperature (in Kelvin), and P(x, y) is the normalized probability density. A Gaussian filter (SciPy) was applied to smooth the energy surface, and the FEL was visualized using Matplotlib as a contour plot, with color gradients gnuplot indicating energy minima (stable states) and energy barriers (transition regions).

Binding Free Energy Calculation

Binding free energy analyses were performed on the last 20 ns of the MD trajectories using the MM/PBSA (Molecular Mechanics/Poisson–Boltzmann Surface Area) approach with the entropy-corrected binding free energy and the energy contribution per-residue to determine the contribution of individual residues to binding. The analysis was conducted using gmx_MMPBSA, which interfaces with the MMPBSA.py module from AmberTools.

The binding free energy (ΔG bind) of the protein–ligand complex was calculated using the following eqs and : ΔGbind=Gcomplex−(Gprotein+Gligand) 6 ΔGbind=ΔEMM+ΔGSOLV−TΔS 7 where ΔE MM includes the bonded (ΔE bond, ΔE angle and ΔE dihedral) and nonbonded (ΔE ele and ΔE vdW) energy terms, ΔG SOLV comprises polar (Poisson–Boltzmann) and nonpolar (SASA-based) solvation energies, TΔS represents the entropic contribution.

Results and Discussion

Template Selection and Identification of Promising Hits from Virtual Screening

The crystal structure of the human androgen receptor (hAR) ligand-binding domain in complex with testosterone (PDB ID: 2AM9) exhibits high structural quality. This structure was resolved by X-ray diffraction at a resolution of 1.64 Å, providing excellent atomic detail. Geometric validation revealed no bond length or angle outliers, and no chirality or planarity issues were detected. The Ramachandran analysis showed that 100% of residues fall within favored or allowed regions, with 0% outliers, placing this model in the 100th percentile relative to all X-ray entries of comparable resolution. This information is available at the following link https://files.rcsb.org/pub/pdb/validation_reports/am/2am9/2am9_full_validation.pdf. Based on these quality metrics, this structural model was selected for all subsequent analyses and designated as the Wild-Type (WT) reference.

In the PubChem database, 88 phthalate compounds (PAEs) with high structural similarity to testosterone were identified based on key molecular criteria such as molecular weight, logP, and polar surface area. Therefore, using the hAR model (PDB ID: 2AM9), virtual screening was conducted across three different docking platforms, with each screening performed in triplicate to ensure reproducibility. In this context, the Z-score was applied as a statistical parameter to evaluate the relative significance of docking results by standardizing each ligand’s binding affinity against the mean and standard deviation of the reference distribution.

Figure a displays a heatmap representing the Z-scores of binding affinities for each ligand across three molecular coupling platforms: DockThor, AutoDock Vina, and iGEMDOCK. Each row corresponds to a ligand and each column to a docking method, with the color intensity reflecting the standardized binding strength (red: weaker binding, blue: stronger binding). Notably, ligand 41, identified as biphenyl phthalate, is highlighted due to its consistently low Z-scores across all docking platforms, indicating strong binding affinities independent of the algorithm used. This pattern underscores its robustness as a potential candidate. Furthermore, the heatmap reveals variations in binding profiles among other ligands, emphasizing the relevance of employing multiple docking strategies to mitigate algorithm-specific biases in compound selection.

Figure c shows the structural comparison between testosterone and the best compound, which was biphenyl phthalate. These compounds exhibit distinct structural and physicochemical characteristics that influence their interactions with biological targets. Testosterone is a rigid, naturally occurring steroid composed of four fused rings with well-defined stereochemistry, featuring a hydroxyl group at position 17β and a carbonyl ketone group at position 3. These functional groups contribute to its moderate polarity and enable a specific hydrogen bond with the androgen receptor. In contrast, biphenyl phthalate is a synthetic, more flexible compound composed of two aromatic rings connected by a single bond and substituted with phthalate ester groups. Its structure lacks defined stereochemistry and polar functional groups capable of hydrogen bonding, resulting in a predominantly hydrophobic profile. Although testosterone’s conformational rigidity supports a precise and stable interaction with its receptor, the flexibility and hydrophobicity of biphenyl phthalate may facilitate nonspecific binding through van der Waals and π–π stacking interactions. These differences underscore the structural divergence between a bioactive natural hormone and a potential endocrine disruptor compound.

Together, these characteristics explain why testosterone exhibits stronger binding energies, yet biphenyl phthalate can still mimic its hydrophobic interactions and act as an endocrine disruptor.

Structural Stability Assessment by Molecular Dynamics Simulations

To evaluate the structural impact of the selected compounds on the target protein, 500 ns all-atom molecular dynamics simulations were performed. Four key parameters were analyzed: root-mean-square deviation (RMSD), root-mean-square fluctuation (RMSF), radius of gyration (R g), and solvent-accessible surface area (SASA).

The RMSD profiles over a 500 ns molecular dynamics simulation reveal insights into the structural stability of the WT protein and its complexes with biphenyl phthalate and testosterone (see Figure a). The WT system exhibits consistently low RMSD values, suggesting a stable native conformation throughout the simulation. In comparison, the biphenyl phthalate-bound complex demonstrates a similarly stable trajectory, maintaining lower RMSD fluctuations than the testosterone-bound system, particularly during the first 300 ns. This indicates that biphenyl phthalate binding does not significantly destabilize the protein structure and may confer a stabilizing effect. In contrast, the testosterone-bound complex shows slightly elevated RMSD values and more pronounced fluctuations, especially during the initial 150 ns, suggesting increased conformational flexibility. Overall, RMSD analysis supports the notion that biphenyl phthalate engages the protein in a manner comparable to or more structurally stabilizing than the endogenous ligand testosterone.

Structural dynamics analysis of the human androgen receptor in wild-type (WT), biphenyl phthalate-bound, and testosterone-bound states across 500 ns of molecular dynamics simulations.

The RMSF analysis quantifies the per-residue flexibility of the WT protein and its complexes with biphenyl phthalate and testosterone over the simulation trajectory (see Figure b). As shown in the plot, all systems exhibit similar fluctuation patterns, with most residues displaying low to moderate flexibility (0.05–0.20 nm), indicating overall structural rigidity across the protein backbone. Notably, residues near the termini show elevated RMSF values, a common feature due to terminal disorder and solvent exposure. The complex with biphenyl phthalate (red line) generally shows reduced fluctuations relative to the testosterone-bound form (blue line), particularly in the central regions between residues 700 to 850. This suggests that biphenyl phthalate may contribute to greater stabilization of specific protein regions. In contrast, the testosterone complex shows slightly higher fluctuations in several regions, indicating potentially increased local flexibility. These findings are consistent with the RMSD results and support the hypothesis that biphenyl phthalate binding confers structural stability comparable to or exceeding that of the native ligand.

The R g analysis provides insights into the overall compactness of the WT protein and its complexes with biphenyl phthalate and testosterone throughout the 500 ns molecular dynamics simulation. As shown in the Figure c, the WT structure (black) maintains the lowest and most stable R g values, indicating a consistently compact conformation. In contrast, both ligand-bound systems exhibit slightly increased R g values. The biphenyl phthalate complex (red) shows a modest but progressive increase in R g over time, suggesting a slight loosening of structural compactness upon ligand binding. The testosterone-bound protein (blue) follows a similar trend with moderate fluctuations, maintaining a slightly expanded radius compared to the WT. These results indicate that while both ligands influence the global conformation of the protein, the degree of expansion is minimal, and the overall structure remains relatively stable. The observed differences in R g are consistent with ligand-induced subtle conformational adjustments rather than major unfolding events.

In Figure d, the SASA analysis illustrates the extent of protein surface exposure to solvent over the course of a 500 ns. The WT system (black) maintains the lowest SASA values, fluctuating around approximately 125 nm2, which is indicative of a compact and relatively solvent-shielded conformation. In contrast, the testosterone-bound (blue) and biphenyl phthalate-bound (red) complexes exhibit slightly elevated SASA values, suggesting increased surface exposure likely resulting from subtle conformational rearrangements upon ligand binding. Notably, the biphenyl phthalate complex demonstrates more pronounced SASA fluctuations after 300 ns, reflecting localized dynamics at the protein–solvent interface. Overall, the SASA profiles indicate that ligand binding induces modest structural adjustments rather than significant unfolding or destabilization. These results support the interpretation that both ligands, particularly biphenyl phthalate, modulate surface accessibility in a manner consistent with their binding behavior while preserving the overall structural integrity of the protein.

Principal Component and Free Energy Landscape Analysis

Principal Component Analysis (PCA) combined with Free Energy Landscape (FEL) calculations was performed to evaluate the conformational dynamics of the hAR in three conditions: WT in complex with testosterone and biphenyl phthalate (see Figure ).

Principal component analysis (PCA) and free energy landscapes (FEL) of hAR in three conditions: (a,b) wild-type (WT), (c,d) hARbiphenyl phthalate, and (e,f) hARtestosterone. The panels on the left show the 2D projections of MD trajectories onto the first two principal components (PC1 and PC2), with colors representing simulation time progression. The panels on the right display the corresponding three-dimensional FEL surfaces derived from the PCA space, where energy minima are indicated by a gradient from purple (low energy) to yellow (high energy).

The PCA plot for the WT receptor (Figure a) shows a wide dispersion across PC1 and PC2 with two main clusters, suggesting the exploration of multiple conformations during simulation. The corresponding FEL map (Figure b) reveals a relatively shallow and dispersed energy landscape, suggesting the presence of multiple low stability conformational states. The lack of well-defined deep minima indicates greater structural flexibility and conformational diversity, but lower thermodynamic stability.

For the biphenyl phthalate-bound complex (Figure c,d), an intermediate behavior was observed. The PCA projection exhibited at least two distinct conformational clusters, suggesting the existence of multiple macrostates. The FEL exhibits several distinct and moderately deep energy minima, which is indicative of multiple metastable conformations with intermediate stability. This pattern suggests a dynamic system that explores diverse conformational states with moderate energy barriers that separate them.

In the testosterone-bound complex, the distribution in the PCA space (Figure e) was considerably narrower. The trajectories clustered within a well-defined region, reflecting reduced conformational diversity upon ligand binding. The corresponding FEL (Figure f) shows one or two wells with deep energy wells, pointing to highly stable conformational states. The steep energy gradients and confined minima suggest a structurally stable system dominated by one or a few energetically favorable configurations.

Taken together, the conformational flexibility of the hAR decreases in the order WT > biphenyl phthalate > testosterone, while its energetic stability follows the opposite trend: testosterone > biphenyl phthalate > WT. Testosterone, as the natural ligand, confines the receptor to a highly stable conformational state, whereas biphenyl phthalate partially mimics this effect by inducing conformational restriction, albeit with greater structural variability.

Additionally, the PCA projection in Figure a reveals distinct conformational sampling patterns for the hAR under different conditions. The wild-type (WT) protein (black) displays a broad distribution across PC1 and PC2, suggesting extensive flexibility and dynamic conformational transitions throughout the simulation. In contrast, the hAR bound to biphenyl phthalate (red) occupies a more confined region in the PCA space, indicating a moderate restriction in structural variability. The testosterone-bound hAR (blue) exhibits the most compact distribution, highlighting a strong conformational constraint and stabilization induced by the natural ligand.

Principal component analysis of protein dynamics across systems. (a) Projection of molecular dynamics trajectories onto the first two principal components (PC1 vs PC2) for the WT, hARbiphenyl phthalate, and hARtestosterone, illustrating differences in conformational space sampling. The right panel displays the eigenvalue spectra for each system, which represent the amplitude of atomic fluctuations explained by each principal component. The scree plot in Figure b presents the eigenvalue distribution for the first 20 principal components, reflecting the contribution of each mode to the overall motion. The testosterone and biphenyl phthalate-bound systems show higher eigenvalues in the first few components, particularly the first and second, indicating that a greater portion of the collective motions is captured by fewer dominant modes. In contrast, the WT system shows lower eigenvalues spread more evenly across components, suggesting more complex and distributed dynamic behavior in the absence of a ligand.

Together, these results demonstrate that ligand binding significantly influences the global dynamics of the hAR. Testosterone induces the most pronounced conformational restriction and concentrates motion into fewer dominant modes, consistent with its role as the endogenous ligand. Biphenyl phthalate partially mimics this behavior, reinforcing its potential to interfere with hAR function, while the WT receptor remains more structurally flexible and dynamically diverse.

Binding Free Energy Estimation Using MM/PBSA

The Molecular Mechanics Poisson–Boltzmann Surface Area (MM/PBSA), complemented by entropy correction via the interaction entropy approximation (TΔS), reveals key differences in the binding profiles of biphenyl phthalate and testosterone to the androgen receptor (see Table ). Biphenyl phthalate (−46.12 kcal/mol) exhibits more favorable van der Waals interactions (ΔE VDW) than testosterone (−42.09 kcal/mol), suggesting effective hydrophobic engagement. However, testosterone shows a marked advantage in electrostatic contributions (ΔE ELE) with −16.14 kcal/mol and −2.25 kcal/mol, respectively indicating a greater involvement of polar interactions and hydrogen bonding. Despite comparable solvation penalties, the total binding free energies (ΔG bind) consistently favor testosterone (−32.04 kcal/mol) over biphenyl phthalate (−22.43 kcal/mol). These findings suggest that although biphenyl phthalate can partially mimic testosterone’s binding behavior, the endogenous ligand maintains superior affinity when both enthalpic and entropic effects are considered.

Noncovalent Interaction Analysis

Noncovalent interactions are weak, reversible forces that play a central role in stabilizing biomolecular structures and facilitating specific molecular recognition processes. These interactions include hydrogen bonds, van der Waals forces, ionic interactions, π–π stacking, cation−π interactions, and hydrophobic effects. , While individually weaker than covalent bonds, their cumulative effect is essential for protein folding, ligand binding, and the dynamic behavior of biological systems. Each type of interaction contributes uniquelyhydrogen bonds offer directionality, ionic forces provide strong electrostatics, and hydrophobic effects drive molecular organization in aqueous environments. Together, they underpin the specificity, stability, and functionality of macromolecular complexes.

Based on the results of this study, the binding of the hAR with both biphenyl phthalate and testosterone was mediated by distinct noncovalent interactions. As summarized in Table , biphenyl phthalate engages in extensive hydrophobic contacts with residues such as LEU704, GLN711, MET745, VAL746, MET749, PHE764, MET780, LEU873, PHE876, and LEU880, in addition to π–π stacking with PHE764. Notably, it does not form hydrogen bonds, suggesting that its stabilization within the binding site is primarily driven by hydrophobic and aromatic interactions. In contrast, testosterone exhibits fewer hydrophobic contactsmainly with LEU704, LEU707, PHE764, and LEU768 but uniquely forms hydrogen bonds with LEU707 and GLY708. This indicates a distinct binding mode, in which testosterone relies more on directional hydrogen bonding to achieve specificity and affinity, while biphenyl phthalate depends on shape complementarity and nonpolar interactions. These observations underscore how chemically diverse ligands can utilize different noncovalent strategies to achieve stable and specific binding within the same receptor cavity.

Conclusions

This study used an integrative computational approach to characterize the molecular interactions between phthalate compounds and the human androgen receptor (hAR), using testosterone as the endogenous reference ligand. The crystallographic structure of hAR (PDB ID: 2AM9) was served as the foundation for all coupling and simulation analyzes. A multiplatform virtual screening strategy consistently identified biphenyl phthalate as a top-ranked candidate, exhibiting robust binding affinities across the DockThor, AutoDock Vina, and iGEMDOCK platforms. Notably, despite its structural dissimilarity to testosterone, biphenyl phthalate adopted a similar orientation within the ligand-binding domain and interacted with key recognition residues. Complementing these molecular findings, ECOSAR-based ecotoxicity predictions indicated that biphenyl phthalate poses a considerable environmental risk.

Furthermore, quantum chemical descriptors obtained from the Finite Difference approximation and Koopmans’ theorem provided insight into the electronic behavior of biphenyl phthalate. The compound exhibited electrophilicity, chemical reactivity, and electron-accepting capacity greater than those of testosterone, indicating a dual functional character that may enhance its ability to emulate the electronic profile of natural ligands. These properties likely contribute to its strong receptor binding and its ability to modulate hAR activity.

Otherwise, molecular dynamics simulations confirmed that the biphenyl phthalate–hAR complex preserved structural stability throughout the simulation period, displaying RMSD, RMSF, radius of gyration, and solvent-accessible surface area profiles closely resembling those of the testosterone-bound system. MM/PBSA free energy analysis revealed that testosterone exhibited a higher overall binding affinity, primarily driven by electrostatic and hydrogen-bonding interactions. In contrast, the binding of biphenyl phthalate was dominated by van der Waals and hydrophobic forces, suggesting distinct interaction mechanisms. Noncovalent interaction profiling supported this observation.

In general, the combined analyzes reveal a complementary perspective: while MM/PBSA calculations predict testosterone to have stronger binding free energy (consistent with its well-defined hydrophobic core and polar hydroxyl anchor), the Finite Difference, Koopmans’ theorem, and reactivity descriptor analyses indicate that biphenyl phthalate exhibits higher chemical reactivity, increased electron-accepting capacity, and a more polarized electrostatic profile. This dual view suggests that, despite testosterone’s thermodynamic advantage, biphenyl phthalate can still mimic its binding behavior through versatile electronic interactions, providing a mechanistic explanation for its potential role as an endocrine disruptor.
