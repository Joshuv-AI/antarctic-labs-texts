Idiopathic intracranial hypertension initially diagnosed in ophthalmology: A case series
Authors: Xianliang Zhang, Hongpei Cui, Hongling Chen, Ke Fan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Idiopathic intracranial hypertension (IIH), presenting with blurred vision and papilledema in ophthalmology clinics due to elevated intracranial pressure, faces misdiagnoses like optic neuritis. This study explored clinical and radiological characteristics of IIH patients initially diagnosed in ophthalmology. This study included patients diagnosed with IIH between December 2016 and January 2021 at Henan Eye Hospital’s ophthalmology department. Data on best corrected visual acuity, cranial magnetic resonance imaging, magnetic resonance venography, computed tomography venography, and digital subtraction angiography were collected. The study included 41 patients (82 eyes, 34 females, median age: 38 years, and range 19–62 years). Among them, 13 had intracranial pressure above 400 mmH2O, 20 between 300 and 400 mmH2O, and 8 between 250 and 300 mmH2O. Blurred vision, temporary vision loss, and dizziness or headache occurred in 97.6%, 70.7%, and 65.9% patients. All patients showed bilateral optic disc edema, with 13 cases showing positive relative afferent pupillary defect and 28 negative. Visual acuity was ≥ 1.0 in 31 eyes (37.8%) and 0.6 to 1.0 in 26 eyes (31.7%), while only 5 eyes had normal visual fields. Magnetic resonance imaging showed that 17 patients had empty sella signs; 30 cases (73.2%) showed intracranial venous sinus stenosis on magnetic resonance venography or computed tomography venography. Twelve patients underwent digital subtraction angiography, all showing intracranial venous sinus stenosis. IIH patients initially diagnosed in ophthalmology show varied clinical manifestations lacking specificity. Diagnosis may require multiple disciplines to avoid misdiagnosis.

1. Introduction

Idiopathic intracranial hypertension (IIH), also known as pseudotumor cerebri, is a clinical syndrome characterized by increased intracranial pressure (ICP) without identifiable cause, including mass lesion or hydrocephalus.[1,2] The condition predominantly affects obese women of childbearing age, although it can occur in individuals of any age or gender.[3] IIH is often associated with symptoms such as headaches, temporary vision loss, pulsatile tinnitus, and, in severe cases, permanent vision loss due to optic nerve damage.[4] Currently, the pathophysiology of IIH remains poorly understood, but it is believed to involve dysregulation of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) dynamics, impaired venous sinus drainage, or metabolic factors related to obesity.[5,6]

However, because patients with IIH commonly present with ocular manifestations, they usually seek initial consultation in ophthalmology departments rather than neurology. This often results in delays in diagnosis and fragmented management, and when ophthalmologists have limited familiarity with the condition, it may also lead to misdiagnosis or missed diagnosis.[7] Misdiagnoses such as optic neuritis or ischemic optic neuropathy are common, leading to delayed treatment and poorer outcomes.[8] Therefore, imaging techniques, such as magnetic resonance venography (MRV) and computed tomography venography (CTV), are used more often, and many studies have highlighted the role of imaging presentation in the pathogenesis of IIH.[9–11] Additionally, research has emphasized the importance of multidisciplinary approaches involving neurology, ophthalmology, and radiology for accurate diagnosis and management.[12]

Given its potential to cause significant visual morbidity, early diagnosis and management are critical to preventing irreversible vision loss. Therefore, this study aimed to explore the clinical and radiological characteristics of IIH patients initially diagnosed in ophthalmology.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Study design and participants

This retrospective study included patients diagnosed with IIH between December 2016 and January 2021 at the ophthalmology department of Henan Eye Hospital. The inclusion criteria were: patients conformed the diagnostic criteria[13] for IIH (including the presence of papilledema; normal neurological examination findings; imaging studies revealing normal brain parenchyma without meningeal enhancement; normal CSF; and an opening pressure >250 mmH2O on lumbar puncture) and age > 18 years. The exclusion criteria were: patients without complete clinical data and patients with prior ophthalmic surgery. This study was approved by the ethics committee of Henan Eye Hospital, and informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of this study.

2.2. Data collection and definition

Data were obtained from medical records, including age, gender, body mass index (BMI), prior medical history (including misdiagnosis and clinical presentations), Best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) and radiological examination results. Each patient underwent a comprehensive diagnostic evaluation, and BCVA was conducted using the international standard logarithmic visual acuity chart, cranial magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and either MRV or CTV. To measure CSF pressure, a lumbar puncture was also performed in all patients at the lateral decubitus position; meanwhile, the CSF sample was collected. The laboratory analyses of both CSF and blood were also performed. Digital subtraction angiography (DSA) examinations were conducted.

2.3. Statistical analysis

SPSS 25.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago) was used for statistical analysis. Continuous data were expressed by median (inter-quartile range), and categorical data were presented as n (%). As this was a retrospective descriptive study, only descriptive statistics were applied, and no inferential or comparative analyses were performed.

3. Results

3.1. Basic characteristics

A total of 41 patients (82 eyes) were included for analysis. Among the patients, there were 34 females and 7 males. The median age was 38 years, with a range from 19 to 62 years. All patients had bilateral involvement; 6 cases (14.6%) had a normal BMI (18.5–23.9), 17 cases (41.4%) were overweight (24.0–27.9), and 18 cases (43.9%) were obese (≥28). Among the 41 patients, 13 cases (31.7%) had ICP >400 mmH2O, 20 cases (48.8%) had pressures between 300 and 400 mmH2O, and 8 cases (19.5%) had pressures between 250 and 300 mmH2O. Misdiagnosis is also common in these patients, 17 and 10, and 9 cases were previously misdiagnosed as “optic neuritis” and “ischemic optic neuropathy,” while 9 cases did not receive a clear diagnosis. Additionally, 5 cases were individually misdiagnosed as “sinusitis,” “cervical spondylosis,” “migraine,” “nerve palsy,” and “central retinal vein occlusion” (Table 1).

4. Discussion

This study revealed that females and populations with higher BMI are more prone to develop IIH, with optic neuritis and ischemic optic neuropathy as the most common misdiagnoses. Blurred vision, temporary vision loss, and dizziness or headache were the most common clinical presentations. The MRI revealed that about one-third of patients presented with empty sella, while intracranial venous sinus stenosis was observed in most patients using MRV or CTV. Their diagnosis may require the involvement of multiple disciplines to avoid misdiagnosis.

The exact mechanism of IIH is currently unclear. In our study, females accounted for 82.9% of all participants, and 85.4% of patients were overweight or obese. The most common clinical presentations included blurred vision, temporary vision loss, dizziness or headache, and tinnitus. These findings align with previous reports that IIH predominantly affects overweight women of childbearing age and is less common in men.[14–16] McCluskey also reported that more than 80% of IIH patients are overweight,[17] and other studies have highlighted similar symptom profiles.[18,19] The predominance in obese women may result from the combined effects of altered ICP dynamics, venous outflow impairment, and endocrine–metabolic dysregulation. Central adiposity increases intra-abdominal and thoracic pressure, which elevates central venous pressure and reduces CSF absorption at the arachnoid granulations.[20] In addition, obesity has been linked to venous sinus stenosis, in which both intrinsic factors (such as arachnoid granulations) and extrinsic compression from raised ICP create a feedback loop that sustains venous outflow obstruction.[21] Hormonal and adipokine influences also play an important role. Obese women with IIH exhibit androgen excess and upregulated glucocorticoid metabolism, including increased activity of 11β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase type 1, which promotes CSF secretion.[22,23] In addition, IIH is associated with leptin hypersecretion from adipose tissue, resulting in elevated circulating leptin levels independent of obesity. Leptin may act on central receptors to increase CSF secretion and disturb ICP regulation.[24] Together, these mechanisms provide a plausible link between female sex, obesity, and the pathogenesis of IIH.

The resulting intracranial hypertension manifests clinically as papilledema, headache, and transient visual obscurations. Understanding these mechanisms is important for distinguishing IIH from other optic nerve disorders such as optic neuritis or ischemic optic neuropathy. Previous studies have reported headaches in 75% to 94% of patients and blurred vision in about 32%.[25] In IIH, headaches may arise from raised ICP stretching pain-sensitive meninges and compressing dural venous sinuses,[26] and blurred vision is mainly attributable to papilledema, which distorts the retinal architecture and interferes with optic nerve conduction.[27] In our cohort, 65.9% of patients reported dizziness or headaches, while a significantly higher proportion (97.6%) experienced blurred vision. This discrepancy in data may be related to the fact that the study sample was sourced from an ophthalmology department, where patients primarily present with eye-related symptoms. Patients with more prominent dizziness or headaches might be more likely to seek care in emergency departments or neurology clinics. Another symptom that requires special attention is temporary vision loss, resulting from papilledema-induced axoplasmic stasis and reduced optic disc perfusion during postural changes.[28] In our study, it accounted for 70.7%, which is close to the 68% reported by Michael et al.[29] Given its high incidence, it can serve as an important reference for the diagnosis of this condition. These mechanisms contrast with optic neuritis, in which vision loss results from inflammatory demyelination often accompanied by pain on eye movement,[30] and with ischemic optic neuropathy, where inadequate perfusion causes sudden, irreversible vision loss.[31] Recognizing the ICP – related origin of symptoms in IIH is therefore important for accurate differential diagnosis.

In patients with IIH, papilledema is often bilateral and represents a typical manifestation of increased ICP.[32,33] The associated papilledema is characterized by marked swelling with dome-shaped elevation, and may be accompanied by peripapillary subretinal fluid.[34] However, early visual acuity and visual field symptoms are relatively mild. This discrepancy between symptoms and signs is a hallmark clinical feature of IIH. In this study, all 41 patients exhibited bilateral papilledema. However, some reports indicate that certain patients may present with unilateral papilledema.[35,36] This phenomenon suggests that the bilateral optic nerve damage caused by IIH may not be symmetrical. Additionally, 31.7% of the cases in this study showed positive relative afferent pupillary defect, which, to some extent, also supports the notion of asymmetrical optic nerve damage in IIH.

Diagnosis of IIH is relatively complex and requires first ruling out pseudopapilledema. Pseudopapilledema is caused by congenital developmental abnormalities of the optic disc, such as crowded or tilted discs and optic disc drusen, which make the appearance of the optic disc resemble edema.[37] Once pseudopapilledema is ruled out, the next step is to confirm ICP. Lumbar puncture and intraventricular catheter are the most direct methods to confirm intracranial hypertension.[38] But before these invasive procedures, a cranial CT or MRI scan is required to rule out intracranial space-occupying lesions. Finally, secondary causes such as hydrocephalus, intracranial space-occupying lesions, intracranial infections, impaired intracranial venous return, arteriovenous fistula, and dural arteriovenous fistula should be systematically ruled out to confirm IIH.[39] These procedures make the diagnosis of IIH complicated and easy to diagnose, while in the present study, optic neuritis, ischemic optic neuropathy, and unclear diagnosis were most common, optic disc edema is another common misdiagnosis, due to its potential ophthalmological and neurological sequelae, it can be life-threatening and constitutes an ocular emergency, therefore, once optic disc edema is diagnosed, it is essential to perform neuroimaging to rule out brain lesions and cerebral venous sinus thrombosis.[40,41] Moreover, these procedures also require some time. Ebraheim et al reported that early diagnosis and proper monitoring of IIH could reduce mortality,[8] therefore developing accurate early-detection methods, providing timely treatment, and shortening diagnosis time are of ultimate importance.

Visual acuity and visual field examinations are simple and practical methods to promptly detect changes in visual function, making them commonly used tools for assessing visual function in patients with IIH.[42] This study found that most patients experienced mild visual impairment, with 69.5% having a good BCVA. Moreover, common visual field defects in these patients included enlarged physiological blind spots, reduced sensitivity, paracentral scotomas, nasal staircase, arcuate scotomas, and ring scotomas. Visual field changes are quite varied, and a single eye can exhibit 2 or more types of abnormalities simultaneously, such as enlarged physiological blind spots and arcuate scotomas occurring in the same eye.[43,44] Additionally, visual field changes between the 2 eyes of a patient do not necessarily match exactly. For instance, 1 eye may have developed tunnel vision while the other eye only shows an enlarged physiological blind spot. Therefore, patients can present with symptoms affecting only 1 eye.

Imaging studies are crucial for the diagnosis and differential diagnosis of this condition. Venous sinus stenosis is considered one of the most important components in the potential pathogenesis of IIH, and the total cerebral blood flow is significantly increased in IIH.[45,46] When IIH is suspected, the 1st-choice examinations are cranial MRI or CT. These radiological techniques can not only examine the brain vasculature, but also provide evidence for the differentiation of IIH and secondary intracranial hypertension, such as intracranial mass or hydrocephalus.[47] Currently, multiple research studies support the idea that intracranial venous sinus stenosis can lead to increased ICP.[48,49] Elder et al[50] reported that the incidence of transverse sinus stenosis in IIH patients ranges from 10% to 90%, which was higher than in the general population (approximately 6.8%). In this study, MRV or CTV showed a high proportion of venous sinus stenosis, reaching 72.7%. Although some patients refused DSA examination, all of the 12 patients who received DSA presented intracranial venous sinus stenosis. Since DSA requires contrast injection, if patients resist, MRI or MRI quantitative hemodynamic studies can also be considered.[51]

In future research, we plan to utilize the collected dataset to perform additional statistical analyses, which may allow exploration of potential associations between clinical characteristics, imaging findings, and visual outcomes. Comparative analyses between subgroups could further clarify risk factors and patterns of disease manifestation, thereby strengthening the interpretability of the present findings.

This study still has certain limitations. First, this retrospective single-center study only included 41 patients, the findings of this study should be further evaluated in a large-scale cohort. Second, this study did not include the treatment protocols and prognostic outcomes, and the comparisons between clinical presentations and examination results were also limited by the sample size. Third, as this was a purely descriptive study, it lacked inferential statistical analyses, appropriate controls, and formal comparisons with other groups, which limits the strength of causal or generalizable conclusions. These aspects will be addressed and improved in future research.

5. Conclusion

In summary, the ocular clinical manifestations of IIH patients are highly variable and lack specificity, while intracranial venous sinus stenosis was observed in most IIH patients. There is an urgent need to increase awareness among ophthalmologists about this disease. By summarizing and analyzing the clinical characteristics of these cases, we hope to provide valuable references for ophthalmologists to achieve early, accurate diagnosis and provide appropriate treatment for this condition.
