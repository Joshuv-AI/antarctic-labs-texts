Current and Future Perspectives of LDL-C Lowering Therapies 2026
Authors: Hayato Tada, Kenji Sakata, Soichiro Usui, Masayuki Takamura
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
LDL cholesterol (LDL-C) is the central causal factor for atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD), and its reduction is a cornerstone of both primary and secondary prevention. Since the introduction of statins more than three decades ago, LDL-C-lowering therapy has expanded substantially, now encompassing ezetimibe, proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9 (PCSK9)-targeting agents, bempedoic acid, and other emerging modalities. This expanding therapeutic landscape has improved the feasibility of achieving guideline-recommended LDL-C targets, but it has also increased the complexity of clinical decision making. This review provides a contemporary and practical overview of the LDL-C-lowering strategies, beginning with the initial evaluation of patients with elevated LDL-C, including differentiation between primary and secondary causes and the identification of familial hypercholesterolemia (FH). We summarize the current treatment targets for primary and secondary prevention, highlight the optimal selection and use of statins, and discuss the assessment and management of statin intolerance, including the role of the nocebo effect. Non-statin therapies, including ezetimibe, bile acid sequestrants, PCSK9 inhibitors, inclisiran, and bempedoic acid, are reviewed with an emphasis on their mechanisms, efficacy, and clinical positioning. Advanced therapies for severe dyslipidemia, such as lipoprotein apheresis, lomitapide, and evinacumab, are also discussed in this review. Finally, we outline the future directions, including oral PCSK9 inhibitors, next-generation cholesteryl ester transfer protein (CETP) inhibitors, lipoprotein(a)-lowering agents, and genome-editing approaches. Collectively, these developments offer new opportunities to address unmet clinical needs, particularly in patients with FH, statin intolerance, and residual cardiovascular risk. A comprehensive understanding of these therapies is essential for further reducing the burden of ASCVD in the coming decades.

1. Introduction

Dyslipidemia is one of the most frequently encountered conditions in routine clinical practice and it can be regarded as a quintessential disease. Among its various forms, elevated low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) is the most important risk factor for coronary atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease and remains the primary therapeutic target 1 , 2) . Since the introduction of pravastatin in 1989, LDL-C-lowering therapy has evolved substantially, progressing to the so-called “strong statins” and, more recently, to a range of non-statin agents such as ezetimibe, proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9 (PCSK9) inhibitors, and bempedoic acid 3 , 4) . These advances have made it increasingly feasible to achieve an adequate LDL-C reduction in many patients with CAD. At the same time, however, the expanding therapeutic landscape has raised new clinical questions: Which agents should be selected for which patients, and to what extent should they be used? In this era of abundant LDL-C-lowering options, clinicians are often challenged to determine the most appropriate therapeutic strategy for individual patients. This review aims to provide a practical and contemporary overview of LDL-C-lowering therapy ( Table 1 ) and serve as a guide for navigating this rapidly evolving field.

2. Initial Evaluation of Patients with Elevated LDL-C

2.1. Distinguishing Primary from Secondary Hyper-LDL Cholesterolemia

When encountering a patient with elevated LDL-C, the first essential step—consistent with the evaluation of any clinical condition—is to differentiate primary from secondary causes. Among primary disorders, familial hypercholesterolemia (FH) is of particular importance. FH is relatively common, with an estimated prevalence of 1 in 300 individuals 5) , and untreated patients are at a markedly increased risk of premature coronary artery disease. Therefore, early recognition is critical. Clinicians should suspect FH when LDL-C ≥ 180 mg/dL, and the likelihood becomes very high when LDL-C ≥ 250 mg/dL 6 , 7) . Most cases encountered in daily practice represent heterozygous FH, typically caused by a single pathogenic variant in the LDL receptor (LDLR), apolipoprotein B (APOB), or PCSK9 genes. The diagnosis relies on the LDL-C levels, family history, and physical findings such as tendon xanthomas. Genetic testing, although not mandatory, is highly informative for both diagnosis and risk stratification and should be considered when available. Despite its clinical importance, the diagnosis rate of FH in Japan remains extremely low—namely, only 2.6% according to a claims database study—suggesting that the majority of cases remain undiagnosed or misdiagnosed 8) . Patients with two pathogenic variants (homozygous FH) present with severe hypercholesterolemia from early childhood and often exhibit characteristic cutaneous xanthomas. These cases require prompt referral to specialists. Even when genetic testing does not confirm biallelic pathogenic variants, patients with LDL-C ≥ 370 mg/dL, childhood-onset xanthomas, or marked treatment resistance should be considered probable homozygous FH ( Table 2 ) . In patients with extremely high LDL-C levels (typically ≥ 400 mg/dL), sitosterolemia—an autosomal recessive sterol metabolism disorder—should also be considered 9 , 10) ( Table 3 ) . Although sitosterolemia can mimic homozygous FH, several features aid differentiation: LDL-C reduction with dietary sterol restriction, a recessive inheritance pattern, and a marked response to ezetimibe. Ultimately, genetic testing provides a definitive diagnosis 11) .

2.2. Establishing Baseline LDL-C in Patients Already on Therapy

In real-world practice, many patients are already receiving statins or other lipid-lowering therapies, make it difficult to determine the baseline LDL-C levels. When possible, clinicians should obtain prior laboratory data by contacting previous healthcare providers or requesting reissued results from health checkup organizations. If the baseline values remain unavailable, then estimating pretreatment LDL-C by dividing the on-treatment LDL-C by 0.7 is widely accepted in research settings 3) . For example, an LDL-C of 126 mg/dL while taking atorvastatin 10 mg suggests a baseline level of approximately 180 mg/dL, which may support a diagnosis of FH when combined with the clinical findings. However, adherence and individual drug responses must be considered, and genetic testing is particularly valuable in such cases.

2.3. Secondary Causes of Elevated LDL-C

Important secondary causes include Hypothyroidism, Nephrotic syndrome, and Diabetes mellitus. Therefore, appropriate management of these underlying conditions is essential. Because primary and secondary factors may coexist, a thorough evaluation is required for all patients with elevated LDL-C levels.

2.4. LDL-C Treatment Targets in Primary and Secondary Prevention

2.4.1. Treatment Targets in Primary Prevention

In primary prevention, LDL-C targets should be determined according to the patient’s overall atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) risk. Consider, for example, a 60-year-old man with normal blood pressure and no glucose intolerance, but who smokes and presents with an LDL-C level of 160 mg/dL. As he has no history of cardiovascular or cerebrovascular disease, he is classified as a primary prevention case. According to the 2022 Japan Atherosclerosis Society (JAS) Guidelines, the LDL-C target for this patient is ＜120 mg/dL 12) . Although he appears to have only a modest number of risk factors, risk prediction using the Hisayama risk score indicates a 10-year ASCVD incidence exceeding 10%, placing him in the high-risk category. In addition to lifestyle modification—particularly smoking cessation—pharmacologic therapy is warranted. Statins are recommended as a first-line therapy in nearly all primary prevention cases. To achieve the target of ＜120 mg/dL from a baseline of 160 mg/dL, a reduction of ≥ 25% is required. In Japan, several statins are available, but achieving a ≥ 25% reduction generally necessitates the use of a strong statin, such as atorvastatin, rosuvastatin, or pitavastatin.

2.4.2. Treatment Targets in Secondary Prevention

In secondary prevention, LDL-C targets are more stringent. Consider a 65-year-old woman with diabetes who experienced a myocardial infarction three years earlier and underwent percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI). Her current LDL-C level is 140 mg/dL. Because she has established coronary artery disease, she is classified as a secondary prevention case. The general LDL-C target for secondary prevention is ＜100 mg/dL. However, among secondary prevention patients, those with acute coronary syndrome, familial hypercholesterolemia, diabetes mellitus, or atherothrombotic stroke are known to have particularly high recurrence risk. For such patients, the recommended LDL-C target is ＜70 mg/dL 13) . This patient meets two of these criteria—acute coronary syndrome and diabetes—so her LDL-C target is ＜70 mg/dL. Achieving this from a baseline of 140 mg/dL requires a reduction of ≥ 50%. Even at maximal doses, strong statins typically reduce LDL-C by only 40–45%, making monotherapy insufficient. In such cases, adding ezetimibe, an inhibitor of intestinal cholesterol absorption, is recommended. Because ezetimibe acts through a mechanism distinct from that of statins, combination therapy produces an additional ~25% LDL-C reduction, making it highly likely that the patient will reach the guideline-recommended target. Furthermore, clinical trial data support the use of ezetimibe in patients with diabetes. In the IMPROVE-IT trial, ezetimibe added to statin therapy resulted in greater reductions in cardiovascular events among patients with diabetes compared with those without diabetes, thus underscoring its particular suitability for this population 14) .

2.5. Clinical Selection and Use of Statins

Since the introduction of pravastatin in 1989, six statins have become available in Japan: pravastatin, simvastatin, fluvastatin, atorvastatin, pitavastatin, and rosuvastatin. Based on their LDL-C-lowering potency, these agents are broadly categorized as follows:

✓Standard statins: pravastatin, simvastatin, and fluvastatin

✓Strong statins: atorvastatin, pitavastatin, and rosuvastatin

Strong statins provide substantially greater LDL-C reduction and are therefore preferred in most clinical scenarios. As discussed earlier, LDL-C lowering follows the principle of “the lower, the better”, even in primary prevention 15) . Thus, unless specific contraindications exist, a strong statin should be initiated as the first-line therapy. However, a subset of patients experience what is termed statin intolerance, which may manifest as muscle-related symptoms or laboratory abnormalities 16) . In such cases, switching from a strong statin to a standard statin is a reasonable strategy. Indeed, many patients who appear intolerant to strong statins can tolerate standard statins without difficulty. Among the strong statins, the author frequently selects rosuvastatin, particularly in the management of FH. Rosuvastatin offers a wide dosing range—from 2.5 mg/day to a maximum of 20 mg/day, representing an eightfold titration capacity. When used at maximal doses, rosuvastatin provides the most potent LDL-C–lowering effect among oral statins, making it especially valuable in patients requiring intensive lipid reduction.

2.6. Management of Patients With Statin Intolerance

Statins remain the cornerstone of pharmacological therapy for elevated LDL-C. It is particularly noteworthy that a drug class originally developed in Japan is now used worldwide and has made a major contribution to the prevention of atherosclerotic cardiovascular diseases. Nevertheless, statin intolerance is observed in approximately 1–several percent of patients 16) . Statin intolerance can present in several forms: some patients develop symptoms such as myalgia, whereas others remain asymptomatic, but exhibit laboratory abnormalities, including elevated liver enzymes or creatine kinase. In patients with confirmed statin intolerance, additional therapies are required to achieve the LDL-C target levels. The options include ezetimibe and/or PCSK9-targeting agents, such as evolocumab or inclisiran. Although PCSK9 inhibitors are generally used in combination with maximally tolerated statin therapy, they can be administered without statins in patients who are truly statin-intolerant. However, great caution is required when diagnosing statin intolerance in patients with DM. While the placebo effect refers to symptom improvement induced by an inactive treatment, the converse phenomenon, whereby adverse events occur in response to a placebo, is known as the nocebo effect 17) . Nocebo responses are thought to be driven by negative emotions, such as stress, fear, and anxiety, related to treatment. In a study of patients who had discontinued statins because of adverse effects, participants were randomly assigned in monthly blocks to receive a statin, placebo, or no treatment. The severity of adverse symptoms during placebo administration was twice as high as during periods without any medication, and there was no difference in symptom severity between the statin and placebo periods. These findings suggest that anxiety induced by information about potential adverse effects, such as myalgia or fatigue, may trigger such symptoms. In the author’s clinical practice, when prescribing statins, explanations of potential adverse reactions are accompanied by a discussion of these clinical trial results and the concept of the nocebo effect. Using this approach, patients presenting with true statin intolerance are exceedingly rare. Therefore, when evaluating suspected statin intolerance, clinicians should carefully consider the possibility of a nocebo effect and assess each case with particular attention to this effect.

3. Non-Statin Therapies

3.1. Ezetimibe

Ezetimibe is classified as an inhibitor of intestinal cholesterol absorption and is a key non-statin option for the management of dyslipidemia. It selectively blocks the cholesterol transporter NPC1L1 (Niemann-Pick C1-Like 1) located on the brush border membrane of enterocytes. Through this mechanism, ezetimibe exerts several pharmacological effects:

✓Inhibition of dietary cholesterol absorption.

✓Inhibition of the reabsorption of biliary cholesterol

✓Reduction in hepatic cholesterol delivery.

✓Upregulation of hepatic LDL receptor expression.

✓Resulting reduction in the circulating LDL-C levels

In contrast to statins, which primarily inhibit hepatic cholesterol synthesis, ezetimibe suppresses intestinal absorption. Consequently, combination therapy with a statin produces additive LDL-C-lowering effects, making it particularly valuable in patients requiring intensive lipid reduction, such as those with familial hypercholesterolemia (FH). As a monotherapy, ezetimibe lowers LDL-C by approximately 15–20%. Substantially greater reductions can be achieved when combined with statins. Ezetimibe is also supported by robust clinical outcome evidence 18 - 20) .

✓IMPROVE-IT: demonstrated reduced cardiovascular events when ezetimibe was added to statin therapy after acute coronary syndrome.

✓SHARP: showed a reduction in atherosclerotic events in patients with chronic kidney disease

✓EWTOPIA 75: demonstrated primary prevention benefits in Japanese adults aged ≥ 75 years using ezetimibe monotherapy

Taken together, these findings establish ezetimibe as a well-validated agent for ASCVD risk reduction and an essential component of contemporary LDL-C-lowering strategies.

3.2. Bile Acid Sequestrants (Resins)

Bile acid sequestrants, such as cholestyramine and colestimide, represent one of the oldest classes of LDL-C-lowering agents. Although classical in nature, they have a well-defined mechanism of action and an excellent safety profile. These agents bind to bile acids within the intestinal lumen, thus preventing their reabsorption and promoting fecal excretion. Consequently, hepatic bile acid stores decrease, leading to upregulated bile acid synthesis in the liver. This process increases hepatic LDL receptor expression, ultimately lowering the circulating LDL-C levels. Before the advent of statins, resins were widely used as primary LDL‑C–lowering therapy. Their LDL-C-lowering efficacy is modest (typically 10–20%). However, they remain valuable in specific clinical scenarios, particularly when other agents are difficult to use 21) . Resins are administered orally, and although the dosing schedules vary by formulation, they are generally more effective when taken with meals. Common adverse effects include constipation (the most frequent), abdominal bloating, flatulence, and reduced absorption of fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, and K). As resins can interfere with the absorption of other medications, they should be taken 1–4 h apart from other concomitant drugs. In clinical practice, bile acid sequestrants are often used for:

✓Statin‑intolerant patients

✓Pregnant women with FH (due to lack of systemic absorption)

✓Pediatric FH: Safety is a priority

✓Patients inadequately controlled on statin + ezetimibe, particularly when PCSK9 inhibitors are not feasible.

Although no longer a first-line therapy, resins continue to play an important role in selected patient populations where safety, tolerability, or treatment constraints limit the use of other LDL-C-lowering agents.

3.3. PCSK9-Targeting Therapies

3.3.1. Evolocumab

Evolocumab is a fully human monoclonal antibody that targets PCSK9 and is a potent LDL-Clowering agent. By binding to circulating PCSK9 and preventing PCSK9-mediated degradation of LDL receptors, evolocumab increases the number of LDL receptors available on hepatocytes, thereby markedly reducing the plasma LDL-C levels. This mechanism is particularly valuable in patients with FH or in high-risk individuals whose LDL-C levels remain above the target despite statin therapy. Statins increase LDL receptor expression, but also upregulate PCSK9, which limits the maximal LDL-C reduction achievable with statin therapy. The introduction of PCSK9 inhibitors has therefore enabled substantially greater LDL-C lowering when used in combination with statins. Indeed, evolocumab added to statin therapy typically produces an additional 50–60% reduction in LDL-C levels.

Large-scale outcome trials have demonstrated the clinical benefits of evolocumab 22 , 23) :

✓FOURIER: In patients with established ASCVD, evolocumab reduced LDL-C by approximately 60% and lowered the risk of major cardiovascular events, including cardiovascular death, myocardial infarction, stroke, hospitalization for unstable angina, and coronary revascularization, by 15%.

✓VESALIUS-CV: In high-risk patients without either prior myocardial infarction or stroke, evolocumab reduced LDL-C by approximately 55% and decreased major cardiovascular events by 25%.

Although typically used in combination with maximally tolerated statins, evolocumab can be used in patients with true statin intolerance.

3.3.2. Inclisiran

Inclisiran is a novel lipid-lowering therapy that uses small interfering RNA (siRNA) technology. It targets the hepatic production of PCSK9 by promoting the degradation of PCSK9 mRNA, thereby reducing circulating PCSK9 levels. Unlike monoclonal antibodies that neutralize the PCSK9 protein, inclisiran acts upstream, preventing PCSK9 synthesis. The most distinctive feature of inclisiran is its twice-yearly dosing schedule, achieved through subcutaneous administration every six months. This provides a sustained LDL-C reduction of approximately 50%, with even greater reductions when combined with statins. Inclisiran became available in Japan in late 2023, thus expanding the therapeutic options for patients with FH and other high-risk conditions. Large cardiovascular outcome trials, including ORION-4, are ongoing 24) .

Choosing Between Evolocumab and Inclisiran:

Although both therapies target the PCSK9 pathway, their mechanisms and clinical characteristics differ.

✓Evolocumab is a monoclonal antibody that neutralizes the circulating PCSK9.

✓Inclisiran is an siRNA agent that suppresses the hepatic PCSK9 production.

Because PCSK9 is primarily produced in the liver, both agents achieve similar LDL-C reductions in most patients. However, PCSK9 is also expressed in extrahepatic tissues (e.g., the lung), and in such cases, monoclonal antibodies may provide slightly stronger LDL-C lowering. Some clinical data suggest that evolocumab may achieve marginally greater LDL-C reductions, and importantly, its cardiovascular outcome benefits are already established, making it the preferred option in more severe or very high-risk cases. Dosing convenience also differs; for example, evolocumab requires self-injection every two weeks. Inclisiran is administered by healthcare professionals once every six months. Therefore, inclisiran may be particularly advantageous for patients with concerns about adherence. The concept of LDL-C exposure over time—the cumulative burden of elevated LDL-C contributing to atherosclerosis—further supports the use of inclisiran in patients with uncertain adherence, as its long-acting profile ensures a sustained reduction in LDL-C. In real-world practice, additional considerations, such as cost, insurance coverage, and patient preference, also influence the choice between these agents.

3.4. Bempedoic Acid

Bempedoic acid is a novel oral lipid-lowering agent that inhibits ATP citrate lyase (ACL), an enzyme upstream of HMG-CoA reductase in the hepatic cholesterol synthesis pathway. By blocking ACL, bempedoic acid reduces intrahepatic cholesterol levels, upregulates LDL receptor expression, and consequently lowers circulating LDL-C. The drug was approved in Japan in 2025 and it is expected to be increasingly utilized, including in Kanazawa, as the “third oral option” following statins and ezetimibe. A key distinguishing feature of bempedoic acid is that it is a prodrug activated only in the liver and not in the skeletal muscle. This characteristic makes it particularly useful for patients with statin intolerance, as it does not provoke muscle-related adverse effects.

LDL‑C–Lowering Efficacy of bempedoic acid:

In a Japanese phase 3 trial (n = 96), bempedoic acid reduced LDL-C by −25.25% at 12 weeks, compared with −3.46% in the placebo group (p＜0.001) 25) . International data show LDL-C reductions of ~17–20% with monotherapy. When combined with ezetimibe, reductions of approximately 35–40% have been reported, demonstrating a synergistic effect. Although its LDL-C-lowering potency is less than that of strong statins (typically ~20–25% as monotherapy), its oral administration and favorable tolerability profile make it an attractive option, especially in statin-intolerant patients.

Clinical Positioning of bempedoic acid: In real‑world practice, bempedoic acid is positioned as follows:

✓The next step after statins and ezetimibe, when additional LDL‑C lowering is required

✓A first-line option for statin-intolerant patients

✓A useful combination partner with ezetimibe in patients who cannot take statins

✓An alternative to PCSK9 inhibitors when cost, access, or injection burden is a concern.

Cardiovascular Outcome Evidence of bempedoic acid:

The CLEAR Outcomes trial demonstrated that in high-risk patients, either statin-intolerant or with established cardiovascular disease, bempedoic acid 26)

✓Reduced LDL‑C by ~21%

✓Lowered major cardiovascular events (cardiovascular death, nonfatal myocardial infarction, nonfatal stroke, or coronary revascularization) by 13%.

✓A prespecified subgroup analysis of high-risk primary prevention patients also showed significant reductions in cardiovascular events, highlighting the drug’s utility in populations for whom evidence was previously limited.

Given these findings, bempedoic acid should be actively considered in high-risk patients, particularly those with statin intolerance, who require additional LDL-C lowering to reach guideline-recommended targets.

3.5. Lipoprotein Apheresis

Lipoprotein apheresis is an extracorporeal therapy that physically removes LDL-C from the circulation. It is primarily used in patients with severe dyslipidemia whose LDL-C levels cannot be adequately controlled with pharmacologic therapy, including statins, ezetimibe, and PCSK9‑targeting agents. The procedure involves withdrawing whole blood or plasma, selectively removing LDL particles using an adsorption or filtration system, and returning the treated blood to the patient’s body. Several techniques are available.

✓Double-filtration plasmapheresis (DFPP): Separates plasma and removes the LDL-rich fraction

✓Direct adsorption of lipoproteins (DALI): whole blood is passed through an LDL‑adsorbing column

✓Dextran sulfate cellulose adsorption (DSA): the most widely used method in Japan; LDL binds selectively to dextran sulfate.

A single session typically reduces the following:

✓LDL‑C by 40–70%

✓Lipoprotein(a) by 30–50%

✓Fibrinogen and inflammatory markers may also decrease.

Although the evidence is largely observational, LDL apheresis has been associated with slowed progression of coronary artery disease, reduced cardiovascular events, and improved survival in patients with FH. In patients with homozygous FH, where pharmacologic therapy alone is often insufficient, LDL apheresis can be life-saving.

Indications (Japan)

In Japan, LDL apheresis is covered by insurance for the following indications:

✓Homozygous FH (virtually all cases qualify because of a severely impaired LDL receptor function)

✓Heterozygous FH unresponsive to maximal pharmacological therapy

✓Selected other severe dyslipidemic conditions (as outlined in Table 4 ).

With the advent of potent pharmacologic therapies, including PCSK9 inhibitors, inclisiran, and bempedoic acid, the indications for LDL apheresis have narrowed. Nevertheless, it remains an essential therapy for homozygous FH and selected refractory heterozygous FH cases, where drug therapy alone cannot achieve an adequate LDL-C reduction 27 - 29) .

3.6. Lomitapide

Lomitapide is an oral microsomal triglyceride transfer protein (MTP) inhibitor approved exclusively for treating homozygous familial hypercholesterolemia (HoFH). By inhibiting MTP in hepatocytes and enterocytes, lomitapide suppresses the assembly of VLDL in the liver and chylomicrons in the intestine, respectively. As a result, the production of apoB-containing lipoproteins is markedly reduced, leading to a substantial decrease in circulating LDL-C levels. A major advantage of lomitapide is that its mechanism of action is independent of LDL receptor function. This makes it particularly effective in HoFH, where LDL receptor activity is severely impaired or nearly absent, and conventional LDL-C-lowering therapies often fail to achieve adequate reductions. Lomitapide therapy must be initiated at a low dose (typically 5 mg/day) and gradually titrated upward. As gastrointestinal side effects increase with a higher dietary fat intake, adherence to a low-fat diet is essential during treatment.

Efficacy of lomitapide:

✓Clinical studies have demonstrated that lomitapide can reduce the LDL-C levels by more than 50%, making it one of the most potent oral therapies available for HoFH.

Safety and Monitoring

✓Despite its efficacy, lomitapide carries a significant risk of hepatic adverse effects, including:

✓Elevated liver enzymes

✓Hepatic steatosis

Therefore, strict and regular monitoring of the liver function and hepatic imaging are required. Additional adverse effects include:

✓Gastrointestinal symptoms (diarrhea and nausea)

✓Weight loss

✓Deficiencies in fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, and K)

Given these considerations, lomitapide is reserved for carefully selected patients with HoFH and it should be administered under the supervision of clinicians experienced in managing severe lipid disorders 30 - 32) .

3.7. Evinacumab

Evinacumab is a fully human monoclonal antibody that targets angiopoietin-like 3 (ANGPTL3) and it is the first approved ANGPTL3 inhibitor capable of lowering LDL‑ levels independently of LDL receptor function. In Japan, evinacumab is approved for the treatment of homozygous familial hypercholesterolemia (HoFH), a condition in which therapeutic options are extremely limited owing to severely impaired or absent LDL receptor activity. ANGPTL3 is a circulating protein that inhibits lipoprotein lipase (LPL) and endothelial lipase (EL). By neutralizing ANGPTL3, evinacumab enhances LPL and EL activity, improves VLDL production and catabolism, and ultimately reduces the LDL-C levels through LDL receptor–independent pathways. This unique mechanism makes evinacumab particularly valuable in HoFH, where conventional LDL receptor–dependent therapies, such as statins, ezetimibe, and PCSK9 inhibitors, often fail to achieve an adequate LDL-C reduction. Clinical studies have demonstrated that evinacumab reduces LDL-C by approximately 50% in adults and adolescents aged ≥ 12 years and children aged ≥ 5 years. Pharmacokinetic modeling suggests that dosing may be feasible in children under 5 years of age when clinically necessary. Evinacumab was administered as an intravenous infusion at 15 mg/kg every four weeks, with each infusion given over at least 60 min. Because very few therapies can lower LDL-C independently of the LDL receptor activity, evinacumab is considered a major breakthrough in the pharmacological management of HoFH. In clinical practice, it is positioned as an adjunctive therapy for patients with HoFH whose LDL-C levels remain above the target despite maximally tolerated LDL receptor–dependent treatments, including statins 33 - 35) .

Efficacy:

✓Demonstrates a ~50% reduction in LDL cholesterol in patients with homozygous familial hypercholesterolemia (HoFH), regardless of residual LDL receptor activity.

✓It lowers multiple atherogenic lipoproteins, including triglycerides, non-HDL cholesterol, and apolipoprotein B.

✓Provides clinically meaningful lipid-lowering effects, even in patients unresponsive to statins, ezetimibe, PCSK9 inhibitors, or lipoprotein apheresis.

✓Acts through an LDL receptor–independent mechanism by neutralizing ANGPTL3, enabling lipid reduction in patients with null LDLR mutations.

✓It improves the overall lipid profile when used as an add-on therapy to maximally tolerated standard treatments.

Safety:

✓Generally well tolerated in clinical trials, with a safety profile consistent with that of other monoclonal antibodies.

✓Common adverse events include nasopharyngitis, dizziness, rhinorrhea, abdominal discomfort, constipation, and back pain.

✓Infusion-related reactions may occur, typically mild to moderate in severity.

✓Rare but serious adverse events, such as anaphylaxis, have been reported.

✓No major hepatic or muscular toxicity signals have been identified to date.

✓Caution is advised during pregnancy due to potential fetal effects observed in animal studies, likely related to maternal lipid lowering.

Practical considerations

✓It is administered as a 15 mg/kg intravenous infusion every 4 weeks for at least 60 min.

4. The Use of Icosapent Ethyl (EPA) in Patients with Controlled LDL-C but Elevated Triglycerides

In patients with established coronary artery disease whose LDL-C levels are adequately controlled with statins, the presence of residual hypertriglyceridemia warrants consideration of an adjunctive therapy with EPA. For example, consider a 60-year-old man with diabetes who developed angina three years earlier. Treatment with rosuvastatin 10 mg successfully reduced his LDL-C to 60 mg/dL, achieving the guideline-recommended target of ＜70 mg/dL for secondary prevention. However, his triglyceride levels remained elevated at 210 mg/dL. In Japan, two major clinical trials, JELIS (2007) and RESPECT-EPA (2024), demonstrated that adding EPA to statin therapy reduces cardiovascular events in patients with prior coronary artery disease with well-controlled LDL-C levels, but continue to exhibit elevated triglycerides. Although EPA has only modest triglyceride-lowering effects, its cardiovascular benefits are thought to arise from multiple pleiotropic mechanisms, including:

✓An anti-inflammatory effect

✓Antiarrhythmic properties

✓Improvement of the endothelial function.

✓Stabilization of atherosclerotic plaque

These mechanisms likely contribute to the reduction in cardiovascular events observed in previous clinical trials. Given this evidence, EPA should be considered in patients with established coronary artery disease who have achieved LDL-C targets with statins, but continue to exhibit elevated triglyceride levels, particularly in the context of diabetes or other high-risk conditions 36 , 37) .

5. Future Directions in Lipid-Lowering Therapy

5.1. Oral PCSK9 Inhibitors

Two PCSK9-targeting agents, monoclonal antibodies and siRNA-based therapies, are currently available, both requiring subcutaneous injection. Although their efficacy and safety in lowering LDL-C and preventing ASCVD are well established, the need for injections remains a barrier for some patients. To address this limitation, several pharmaceutical companies are developing oral PCSK9 inhibitors, each employing distinct mechanisms while sharing the common goal of inhibiting PCSK9 ( Table 5 ) . Although the magnitude of LDL-C reduction varies across agents, the development of oral PCSK9 inhibitors is highly anticipated, particularly because LDL-C management in Japan remains suboptimal. These agents may become important additions to the therapeutic armamentarium 38 , 39) .

5.2. Obicetrapib (CETP Inhibitor)

Since the detailed characterization of CETP deficiency, which is associated with markedly elevated HDL-C levels, was first reported in Japan, CETP inhibitors have been developed with the expectation of raising HDL-C levels 40) . CETP (cholesteryl ester transfer protein) mediates the transfer of cholesteryl esters from HDL to LDL/VLDL. Thus, CETP inhibition was expected to increase HDL-C and reduce LDL-C, offering a novel anti-atherosclerotic strategy. However, three CETP inhibitors—torcetrapib, dalcetrapib, and evacetrapib—failed to demonstrate cardiovascular benefits in clinical trials during the 2000s. Mendelian randomization studies have suggested that LDL-C lowering, rather than HDL-C elevation, is the key determinant of cardiovascular risk reduction. The third-generation CETP inhibitor anacetrapib produced substantial increases in HDL-C and meaningful reductions in LDL-C, and the REVEAL trial (2017) demonstrated a significant reduction in cardiovascular events (CVEs). Nevertheless, concerns regarding its high lipophilicity and long-term tissue accumulation, combined with the emergence of PCSK9 inhibitors, led to the discontinuation of its development. The fourth-generation CETP inhibitor, obicetrapib, has been developed as an improved, highly selective, water-soluble agent with minimal adipose tissue accumulation and a favorable safety profile. Phase 3 trials (BROOKLYN, BROADWAY) have shown 36–41% reductions in LDL-C, with safety comparable to a placebo. Pending further evaluation of cardiovascular outcomes, obicetrapib is expected to become a promising new LDL-C–lowering therapy 41 , 42) .

5.3. Lipoprotein(a)–Lowering Therapies

Lipoprotein(a) [Lp(a)] is an LDL-like particle composed of apolipoprotein B-100 linked via a disulfide bond to apolipoprotein(a). Similar to LDL, Lp(a) is cholesterol-rich and contributes directly to cholesterol deposition in the arterial wall. Its structural similarity to plasminogen may also promote thrombosis by competitively inhibiting plasminogen binding to coagulation factors.

Current evidence indicates that

✓Lp(a) levels are genetically determined and minimally influenced by diet and exercise.

✓Lp(a) is an independent risk factor for ASCVD and represents a major component of residual risk despite LDL-C lowering.

✓Statins do not meaningfully reduce the Lp(a) levels, and nucleic acid–based therapies are currently under development.

In Japan, guidelines acknowledge Lp(a) as a risk factor, but provide limited guidance on when and in whom it should be measured. In contrast, Western guidelines recommend a once-in-a-lifetime measurement, particularly in individuals with premature ASCVD, a family history of early onset disease, or a suboptimal response to statin therapy.

Although some clinicians hesitate to measure the Lp(a) levels because of the perceived lack of treatment options, this view is outdated. PCSK9 inhibitors reduce the Lp(a) levels by approximately 25–30%, and lifestyle interventions—smoking cessation, exercise, dietary modification, and weight reduction—may mitigate some of the associated risks. In patients with ACS and elevated Lp(a) levels, PCSK9 inhibitors and aggressive lifestyle modifications should be considered. Multiple Lp(a)-lowering agents are currently in clinical trials worldwide ( Table 6 ) 43 - 45) , and given their profound Lp(a)-lowering effects, they may soon become the standard therapy for patients with ASCVD with elevated Lp(a). 5.4. Genome Editing for Lipid Disorders

One of the most exciting developments in lipid therapeutics is genome editing, which represents a potential “one-shot therapy.” VERVE-101, developed by Sekar Kathiresan and colleagues at Verve Therapeutics (Boston, USA), uses a CRISPR/Cas9-based system to knock down the PCSK9 gene in hepatocytes, aiming to achieve lifelong LDL-C reduction with a single treatment.

In a first-in-human study presented at the 2023 American Heart Association (AHA) meeting, 10 patients with FH and established coronary artery disease were administered VERVE-101. At 28 days post-treatment 47) .

✓The plasma PCSK9 levels decreased by 59% and 84% at the 0.45 mg/kg dose and 47% at the 0.6 mg/kg dose.

✓The LDL-C levels decreased by 39% and 48% at 0.45 mg/kg and 55% at 0.6 mg/kg.

✓The LDL-C reduction at 0.6 mg/kg persisted for six months.

Although challenges remain, including acute hepatic toxicity and long-term safety, this trial represents a landmark step in genome-based therapy for inherited metabolic diseases.

Importantly, this approach is not limited to the PCSK9. Any liver-expressed gene could theoretically be targeted, and combination (“cocktail”) editing strategies may be feasible for this purpose. Genome editing for lipid disorders and beyond is advancing rapidly, particularly in Western countries.

Conclusion

Atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease remains a leading cause of death in Japan, and its prevention is still incomplete. Although the treatment of dyslipidemia, especially elevated LDL-C levels, has advanced with the development of new therapies, significant unmet needs persist. As highlighted in this review, novel agents are emerging to address the most challenging clinical scenarios, including homozygous familial hypercholesterolemia and statin intolerance. With an appropriate understanding and application of these therapies, further reductions in the ASCVD burden in Japan can be anticipated.
