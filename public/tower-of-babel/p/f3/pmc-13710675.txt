Osteoporosis and post-fracture management in the primary care setting: a systematic review
Authors: Mystica Jude, Mícheál Ó Breasail, Stefanie Bird, Jakub Mesinovic, Paul Jansons, Jackson J. Fyfe, David Scott, Robin M. Daly, Jason Talevski
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
To synthesize evidence on osteoporosis and fragility fracture management within the primary care setting. A systematic review was conducted using Ovid Medline, Embase, Scopus, and PsycINFO to identify studies published between 2005 and 2025. Eligible studies included qualitative, quantitative, or mixed-methods designs that examined any stage of osteoporosis and fragility fracture care (risk assessment, diagnosis, treatment, prevention) in adults ≥ 50 years within primary care. Two reviewers independently screened studies, extracted data, and appraised methodological quality using the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool. Outcomes were categorised into four domains: (1) health provider and patient knowledge, perceptions, and experiences; (2) osteoporosis diagnosis and treatment patterns; (3) interventions to improve care; and (4) barriers and facilitators to optimal care. Eighty studies were included, comprising cross-sectional surveys, cohort studies, randomized controlled trials, and qualitative studies. Clinician knowledge was moderate, with gaps in guideline familiarity, fracture risk assessment, and pharmacotherapy. Osteoporosis and fragility fractures were widely under-identified and undertreated, with minimal screening, minimal diagnostic testing, and substantial undertreatment of guideline-eligible patients. Interventions such as electronic decision-support and fracture liaison services consistently increased BMD testing and appropriate prescribing. System-level barriers to osteoporosis and post-fracture care included limited consultation time, unclear responsibility allocation, and fragmented communication between care settings, while patient-level barriers involved limited understanding of osteoporosis and concerns about medications.Osteoporosis management in primary care remains inadequate. System-integrated, coordinator-driven approaches combined with targeted patient education effectively increase diagnosis and treatment rates, highlighting known strategies to reduce subsequent fractures and improve patient outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00198-026-08157-5.

Introduction

Osteoporosis is characterized by low bone mineral density (BMD) and deterioration of bone microarchitecture, leading to bone weakness and an increased risk of fragility fractures [1, 2]. Globally, osteoporosis affects an estimated 18.3% of the population [3], and approximately one in three women and one in five men over the age of 50 will experience an osteoporosis-related fracture in their lifetime [4]. As populations continue to age, the burden of osteoporosis and fragility fractures is projected to grow, contributing to increased risk of morbidity, mortality, and greater healthcare costs [1, 2, 5]. Despite well-established guidelines recommending screening, diagnosis, and both pharmacological and non-pharmacological interventions, osteoporosis remains widely underdiagnosed and undertreated [6]. This disparity between those at risk and those receiving appropriate care is commonly referred to as the “osteoporosis treatment gap” [7, 8].

Primary care plays a pivotal role in osteoporosis identification and management, as general practitioners (GPs) and other primary care providers are typically the first point of contact for patients at risk [9]. However, osteoporosis is frequently unrecognized in these settings, resulting in suboptimal rates of BMD testing and limited initiation of treatment following fractures [10, 11]. Barriers that hinder optimal care delivery, leading to missed opportunities for fragility fracture prevention, occur at both provider and patient levels. Key provider-level barriers include limited consultation time, competing clinical priorities, uncertainty about guideline recommendations, and challenges with patient adherence to medication [12–15]. Patient-level barriers include fear of medication side effects, concerns about long-term medication safety, cost of treatment, and reduced trust or communication with clinicians [16, 17]. A range of interventions have been introduced to improve post-fracture care and reduce the osteoporosis treatment gap. Fracture liaison services (FLS), a coordinated multidisciplinary program, have been shown to increase rates of investigation and treatment [18, 19]. However, these models of care are typically implemented in tertiary healthcare settings. Enhancing osteoporosis care in primary care settings requires an understanding of current practices, provider and patient barriers, and the effectiveness of existing or novel interventions [20].

This systematic review aims to comprehensively examine osteoporosis and fracture care within the primary care setting. The specific objectives are the following: (1) assess health provider and patient knowledge, perceptions, and experiences of osteoporosis and fracture management; (2) synthesize current patterns of osteoporosis diagnosis and treatment; (3) summarize interventions and strategies designed to improve osteoporosis diagnosis and fracture prevention; and (4) identify key barriers and facilitators influencing the delivery of optimal osteoporosis and fracture care.

Materials and methods

This systematic review was undertaken and reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [21]. The protocol of this systematic review was registered with PROSPERO 2025 (CRD420251160541).

Eligibility criteria

All original research studies published in English utilising qualitative, quantitative, or mixed‐methods study designs and undertaken within the primary care setting were considered for this review. Studies addressing any stage of osteoporosis and fragility fracture care within primary care were eligible, including risk assessment, diagnosis, treatment, and post-fracture secondary prevention. Systematic reviews, meta‐analyses, opinion pieces, letters to the editor, and conference abstracts were excluded. Studies in patients were included if participants were either adults ≥ 50 years of age with a minimal trauma fragility fracture, had a high risk of fragility fracture based on screening risk (e.g., postmenopausal women or men aged 65 years or older), were diagnosed with osteopenia or osteoporosis, and/or were receiving treatment for osteoporosis. Studies in healthcare providers were included if participants were practicing primary care providers (e.g., GPs, family practice physicians, general internists, etc.). Studies that included specialists (e.g., endocrinologists) were excluded unless outcomes were reported separately for clinical groups. Outcome domains of interest included the following: health provider and patient knowledge, perceptions, and experiences of osteoporosis and fracture care; osteoporosis diagnosis and treatment patterns (prevalence and rates); interventions/processes to improve osteoporosis diagnosis and fracture care; and barriers and facilitators to optimal osteoporosis and fracture management. We limited the publication date to articles published in the past 20 years to ensure results are based on the most recent literature [22].

Search strategy and information sources

An electronic database search for published literature was performed on 31 August 2025 using Ovid Medline (biomedical literature), Scopus, Embase and PsycINFO. A sensitive search strategy was developed for Ovid Medline comprising a broad range of terms for ‘osteoporosis’, ‘fractures’ and ‘primary care’. This was modified for each subsequent database search. Backward citation screening of eligible studies and systematic reviews discovered by the search was undertaken for any other relevant studies.

Study selection

Two authors (JT, SB or MJ) independently screened the titles and abstracts of all studies identified from the electronic database searches against the eligibility criteria using Covidence—a web‐based platform for undertaking the steps in the study review process (www.covidence.org). Full‐text articles were obtained for the potentially relevant studies identified from this initial screening and assessed for inclusion independently by two authors. Data extraction

The following data were independently extracted by two authors (MJ, SB, JM, PJ, or JJF) from the full text of each included study: lead author, publication year, country of study, study design, participant characteristics (number, % female, mean age), intervention description (if applicable), outcome measures, and key findings. The senior author (JT) resolved any discrepancies observed between the extracted data.

Critical appraisal of evidence

All studies were assessed for methodological quality independently by two authors using the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (2018 version) [23] designed for the quality appraisal stage of systematic reviews that include qualitative, quantitative, and mixed-methods studies. The tool includes five specific criteria for each study design (qualitative research; randomized controlled trials (RCTs); non-randomized studies; quantitative descriptive studies; mixed-methods studies) with three response options (Yes; No; Unclear). The results of the quality assessment are presented using descriptors such as stars (*) or % indicating the proportion of criteria met for each study (range: 5* or 100% quality criteria met to 1* or 20% quality criteria met). For qualitative studies, quality criteria included appropriateness of the approach used; suitability of data collection and analysis methods; and coherence between data sources, analysis, and interpretation. For quantitative studies, quality criteria included randomization (if appropriate), representativeness of participants, completeness of outcome data, accounting for confounding, and statistical analysis. For mixed-methods studies, criteria were related to both quantitative and qualitative aspects above and additional items relating to the rationale for using a mixed-methods design and integration of qualitative and quantitative phases in the study. The Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool has been evaluated for content and initial construct validity [23].

Synthesis of results

To synthesize the outcomes reported across included studies, we conducted a structured categorization process. The senior author (JT) examined the primary and secondary outcomes extracted for each study and grouped them into the four overarching outcome domains according to their underlying focus: 1) Health provider and patient knowledge, perceptions, and experiences of osteoporosis and fracture care; 2) Osteoporosis diagnosis and treatment patterns (prevalence and rates); 3) Interventions/processes to improve osteoporosis diagnosis and fracture care; and 4) Barriers and facilitators to optimal osteoporosis and fracture management (i.e., diagnosis and treatment). This approach allowed conceptually similar outcomes to be consolidated and ensured consistency in how outcome types were interpreted and summarized. Any discrepancies in categorization were resolved through consensus by one other author. Quantitative data were summarized thematically and analyzed to address the review objectives. Qualitative data were analyzed using a thematic synthesis as described by Thomas and Harden [24]. An inductive approach was used, whereby themes related to osteoporosis and post-fracture care were identified from within the data summaries and interpretation of findings from study authors.

Results

Following the removal of duplicates, 4,982 articles were retrieved from the electronic search. Of these, 4,712 articles were excluded based on the review of the title and abstract, and the full text was obtained for the remaining 270 articles. Based on the authors’ assessment, 80 unique studies [10–14, 16, 17, 25–97] met the eligibility criteria and were included in this review (Fig. 1).

Study and participant characteristics

The 80 studies encompassed a wide range of study designs and geographical locations. The most common study design was cross-sectional (n = 27), followed by retrospective cohort studies/audits (n = 13) and prospective observational/cohort studies (n = 7). Several studies used intervention or evaluative approaches, including RCTs (n = 9), before-and-after/pre-post studies (n = 5), and quasi-experimental designs (n = 3). There were 12 qualitative studies and two mixed-methods studies. The largest proportion of studies originated from Canada (n = 16), the United States (n = 13), and Australia (n = 12), followed by several European countries, including the United Kingdom (n = 7), the Netherlands (n = 5), the Czech Republic (n = 4), Belgium (n = 3), France (n = 2), and Sweden (n = 2).

Across the included studies, 50 included older adults with osteoporosis, fragility fractures, or elevated fracture risk. Sample sizes ranged widely from small qualitative samples to large administrative cohorts exceeding 200,000 patients. Across the studies that reported demographic characteristics, the mean age of patient participants was approximately 71 years (50–104), and samples were predominantly female (mean 85%). Healthcare provider involvement was reported in 42 studies, including GPs, family physicians, primary care nurses or medical officers. Demographic information for clinicians was variably reported. Quality assessment

The methodological quality of the included studies was generally high. Most studies (n = 57; 71.3%) achieved a rating of four stars or higher using the MMAT, reflecting that they met at least 80% of the relevant MMAT criteria. Across study designs, items relating to the clarity of research questions, the appropriateness of data collection methods, and the alignment between study aims and analytic approaches were the most consistently satisfied. In contrast, criteria associated with risk of bias and strategies for addressing confounders in non-randomised studies were the least frequently met (Supplementary File 1).

Knowledge of osteoporosis, fracture prevention and recommended diagnosis

Osteoporosis knowledge among GPs was overall moderate, with specific gaps reported in pharmacology, diagnostic thresholds, and risk factors for fractures. In a large Czech survey of 524 GPs, the mean osteoporosis knowledge score was 12.2 out of 21, with osteoporosis knowledge being negatively correlated with age and experience [29]. A cross-sectional study of 129 Moroccan GPs reported that only 52% of GPs correctly defined osteoporosis as a T-score ≤ − 2.5 SD, and 73% were unfamiliar with the FRAX tool despite its relevance for fracture risk assessment [78]. A Malaysian survey in family medicine practitioners reported a large knowledge gap in the management of osteoporosis, with only 31% of respondents achieving a “satisfactory” osteoporosis knowledge score, and 45% reporting they were confident in advising patients for initiation of osteoporotic medications [90]. Approximately 50% of primary care physicians in a Canary Islands cross-sectional study (n = 332) reported being unaware of when to order imaging investigations for suspected osteoporosis cases [59]. A UK study of 309 primary care health professionals reported high levels of agreement with confidence in osteoporosis care skills, including confidence in explaining osteoporosis in a way that patients understand, identifying patients who require a fracture risk assessment, and making recommendations about starting osteoporosis medications [58]. Patient knowledge of osteoporosis and fractures was generally inadequate across studies. Patients (n = 944) did not think a fragility fracture put them at risk of another fracture, did not know that osteoporosis pharmacotherapy reduced future fracture risk, and had poor knowledge of osteoporosis causes or risk factors [32, 73].

Knowledge and use of fracture risk assessment tools and clinical guidelines

Eight studies reported on the use of fracture risk tools or formal osteoporosis guidelines in GPs. GPs had limited familiarity with fracture risk tools and reported uncertainty about their application in practice [40, 76, 79]. A Belgian study reported that one third of GPs knew of FRAX, and those who did regarded it as complementary for management [12]. A survey of 129 Moroccan GPs found many GPs (73%) had never heard of FRAX [78]. A German survey reported that just over half of GPs had reasonable knowledge of the national osteoporosis guidelines, with less admitting to using them in practice [37]. Similarly, three cross-sectional studies of GPs in Australia, Czech, and Singapore reported poor adherence to implementing osteoporosis guidelines in routine osteoporosis and fracture care [13, 39, 93]. With respect to glucocorticoid-induced osteoporosis, Dutch clinicians reported treating approximately 69% of eligible patients in line with guidelines [49]. Time constraints and lack of integration into their workflow were among the most common reasons for underutilization of fracture risk assessment tools and clinical guidelines [12, 13].

Clinical practice and decision-making around osteoporosis management

Austrian GPs’ decisions to order BMD testing and other investigations were strongly influenced by fracture history, age and risk factor profile [48]. Spanish primary care physicians showed variation in follow-up investigations, with only a minority (10%) applying all recommended laboratory tests before referral [59]. A survey of 129 Moroccan GPs revealed that the practices regarding postmenopausal osteoporosis management often deviate from the recommended standards [78]. GPs often reported using denosumab as first-line osteoporosis therapy, most commonly citing convenience as the main reason [85]. Half envisaged a treatment duration of 3–5 years, and practice around stopping therapy varied, with 21% reporting no concerns about stopping [85].

Osteoporosis diagnosis and treatment patterns (prevalence and rates)

Twenty population-based observational studies examined osteoporosis diagnosis and treatment patterns (Table 2). Of these, 13 studies included data on osteoporosis screening and diagnosis, 13 reported prescription patterns for osteoporosis treatment, two reported treatment adherence or persistence, and six quantified the proportion of eligible patients receiving osteoporosis treatment.

Rates of osteoporosis screening and diagnosis

BMD testing and formal fracture risk assessment were suboptimal in primary care. In a Canadian cohort of fracture patients aged > 50 years, only 40% of patients had a BMD test recorded [28]. In Australian general practice studies of older patients (aged > 50 years), only 23% had undergone a DXA scan [36], and osteoporosis diagnoses were extremely underreported with only 12% of patients having a listed osteoporosis diagnosis [80]. In a community survey of US women, approximately half (49.6%) of those aged ≥ 65 years reported ever having had a BMD test [56]. In a large Taiwanese general practice audit, a formal diagnostic test of osteoporosis was recorded in just 13% of women and 4% of men aged over 60 years [38]. A large guideline-based cohort study in the UK showed that among 158,946 individuals meeting NICE criteria for osteoporosis, only 8% were coded with an osteoporosis diagnosis [60].

Prescription patterns for osteoporosis therapies and supplements

Studies reporting prescription patterns for osteoporosis pharmacotherapy showed substantial variation. In a French cross-sectional study of postmenopausal women with established osteoporosis, 97% of 1019 patients were receiving osteoporosis treatment, the majority using weekly bisphosphonates [30]. In a Taiwanese dataset of 29,000 patients, just 54% with a recorded osteoporosis diagnosis received prescriptions for osteoporosis medications [38]. Similarly, in Australian general practice, relatively few patients with a coded osteoporosis diagnosis were on osteoporosis pharmacotherapy (approximately 30%) [80]. In a UK study of women with osteopenia, only 52% of women were treated appropriately in accordance with clinical guidelines [97]. The US-based NORA study of 200,160 postmenopausal women described temporal trends and prescriber behavior, reporting that the proportion of primary care physicians prescribing bisphosphonates increased fourfold from 15 to 59% between 1998 and 2006 [96]. Many studies reported that calcium supplementation was often not accompanied by guideline-accordant pharmacotherapy recommendations [50, 56, 80].

Treatment adherence and compliance rates

Only two studies explicitly reported adherence or persistence outcomes. In an Italian cohort study of 119 women, 50% discontinued bisphosphonate therapy within 2 years [47]. In Spain, over one quarter (26%) of patients aged > 70 years seen in primary care centres had no follow-up visits after treatment initiation, suggesting limited monitoring and potentially shortened persistence during the 2-year follow-up period [74].

Proportion of eligible patients not receiving osteoporosis therapy

Six studies explicitly quantified the ‘osteoporosis treatment gap’, defined as the proportion of patients who were eligible for therapy but not treated, and reported that 25–50% of eligible patients remain untreated in real-world settings. In a Canadian fragility-fracture cohort, only 29% of patients with an osteoporosis diagnosis were treated appropriately post-fracture [28]. In Taiwan, fewer than 50% of 29,000 patients with a recorded osteoporosis diagnosis received prescriptions for osteoporosis medications [38]. In a UK cohort (n = 28,657), 37% of osteoporosis-coded patients had no history of ever receiving osteoporosis medications despite meeting guideline criteria [60]. In a large US longitudinal cohort of 600 community-based women > 55 years of age, 48% of women meeting osteoporosis treatment criteria were not receiving any pharmacologic therapy [97].

Interventions/processes to improve osteoporosis diagnosis and fracture care

Interventions or care processes aimed at improving osteoporosis diagnosis and fracture care were evaluated in 23 studies (Table 2). These studies assessed changes in diagnostic and treatment practices, as well as clinical and patient-reported outcomes. Interventions included: electronic/decision-support systems; fracture liaison or case-management models; educational and knowledge-translation programmes; and population screening tools. Formal economic evaluations of included intervention studies were absent from this evidence base.

Changes in osteoporosis diagnosis and treatment rates

Overall, the evidence showed consistent improvements in BMD testing and prescribing of anti-fracture therapy and supplements across a variety of intervention types. Feldstein et al. tested an intervention in which primary care providers received EMR messages with patient-specific osteoporosis risk and treatment reminders, while patients received educational letters on osteoporosis management, including information about exercise, vitamin D, and fall prevention [53]. An RCT of 311 older patients who had sustained fractures and had not received medication for osteoporosis showed an increased proportion of patients receiving a DXA scan or osteoporosis medication at 6-months compared with usual care [53]. In an RCT of 270 postmenopausal women with acute wrist fractures, primary care physicians and patients received reminder letters and educational materials on osteoporosis assessment, treatment options, and fracture risk, resulting in increased DXA testing in primary care compared with controls [43]. Screening tools and simplified risk algorithms improved identification of patients with densitometric osteoporosis or high fracture risk, reducing unnecessary DXA in low-risk individuals [55, 72]. A range of educational and quality-improvement programmes targeting primary care physicians [61, 82, 89] consistently increased appropriate prescribing of osteoporosis medications in line with guideline criteria. In the Bowers et al. study of 686 female patients aged > 65 years, a pharmacist-physician collaborative model showed that almost all pharmacist-managed high-risk patients received initiation or continuation of appropriate anti-osteoporosis therapy, compared with roughly half of those under physician-only management [33]. Fracture liaison models consistently increased documentation of osteoporosis diagnoses, coding of fragility fractures, and prescribing of anti-osteoporosis drugs after a sentinel fracture across studies [35, 86, 91]. Population screening trials demonstrated that risk-stratified screening can substantially increase treatment uptake among those identified at high fracture risk. Clark et al. [41] reported higher prescription rates of osteoporosis medications in the screening arm compared with usual care at 6 months, while Merlijn et al. [77] and Shepstone et al. [87] observed particularly high medication uptake and persistence among high-risk women identified through FRAX-based screening pathways. Several interventions also increased initiation of calcium and/or vitamin D supplements in older patients with osteoporosis or osteopenia [43, 82, 89].

Changes in clinical outcomes (fractures, falls, fracture risk, mortality)

Only three studies reported clinical outcomes. In a community screening trial, allocation to a fracture-risk screening intervention increased osteoporosis medication prescribing and was associated with a lower incidence of new fractures at 12-month follow-up [41]. Conversely, in a pragmatic primary care screening trial utilizing the FRAX tool [77], screening and subsequent treatment had no statistically significant effect on the primary outcome fracture (HR = 0.97; 95% CI: 0.87–1.08), nor on the secondary outcomes of osteoporotic fractures, major osteoporotic fractures or hip fractures. In the Canadian Quality Circle project [61], a two-session educational intervention improved appropriate prescribing, but there were no significant differences in new fracture events between groups at final follow-up.

Changes in patient-reported and behavioural outcomes

Behavioral and patient-reported outcomes were heterogeneously assessed across three studies [27, 43, 57]. A multifaceted intervention involving letters and educational materials for physicians and patients did not improve osteoporosis knowledge in 270 postmenopausal Canadian women with wrist fractures [43]. In Beattie et al. [27], patients reported better understanding of their bone health and follow-up plan after receiving a customized FRAX report, using femoral neck BMD and clinical risk factors, which included treatment recommendations. In a prospective observational study in Spain involving 745 postmenopausal women with osteoporosis receiving raloxifene [57], an educational leaflet on osteoporosis and raloxifene showed no significant improvement in medication adherence compared to the control group at 12 months (47.4% vs 52.5%).

Barriers and facilitators to optimal osteoporosis and fracture management

Twenty-one studies examined barriers and facilitators to optimal osteoporosis and fracture management, reporting a range of clinician-, patient-, and system-level factors influencing care delivery (Table 2).

Clinician-related factors

GPs commonly described osteoporosis as a lower clinical priority than more acute or life-threatening conditions, contributing to limited proactive case-finding and treatment initiation [14, 76, 81]. Limited consultation time and competing clinical priorities reduced opportunities to address osteoporosis in routine care [16, 40, 84]. Uncertainty and lack of confidence were recurring themes, particularly regarding which patients to screen, use of fracture risk assessment tools, and interpretation of BMD results [40, 76, 79]. Several qualitative studies identified concerns about prescribing medications including knowledge on long-term medication use, skepticism about benefits, and fear of adverse effects [14, 40, 81]. Despite these concerns, clinicians expressed a strong desire to improve care.

System-related factors

Clinicians frequently reported uncertainty regarding whether GPs, orthopedic surgeons, FLS teams, or other specialists were responsible for treatment initiation and long-term follow-up [14, 16, 75, 95]. Communication gaps between acute, specialist, and primary care services were prominent, including delayed or incomplete discharge information, limited feedback from hospitals and FLS services, and inconsistent communication from orthopedic teams, all of which undermined continuity of care [16, 75, 95]. Time and funding constraints were consistently identified as major barriers. Several facilitators were also identified, including coordinated care protocols that clearly delineate roles and streamlined post-fracture pathways that reduce duplication and patient burden [66, 92, 95].

Patient-related factors

Patients commonly demonstrated incomplete or inaccurate understanding of osteoporosis, BMD testing and the rationale for long-term medication use, which reduced motivation to initiate or adhere to treatment [16, 17, 75, 88, 95].. Quality of the doctor-patient relationship, trust in clinicians and satisfaction with care were consistently associated with greater engagement and adherence [16, 17]. Reasons frequently cited for non-initiation of bisphosphonates included insufficient information, concerns about side effects, and doubts about treatment necessity [17, 88]. From the clinician perspective, patient non-adherence and reluctance to attend specialist appointments were reported as barriers to providing optimal post-fracture care [39, 49, 94].

Information and educational factors

Although osteoporosis guidelines were widely available and many clinicians reported having read them, GPs described difficulty reconciling multiple guideline documents and translating specialist guidance into practical primary care protocols, particularly for vertebral fracture management [39, 49, 94, 95]. Across studies, clinicians consistently identified learning needs related to interpretation of BMD results, follow-up intervals, imaging indications, treatment duration and sequencing, and management of complex patient groups, as well as a need for improved patient-facing educational materials to support shared decision-making [66, 79, 84].

Discussion

This systematic review synthesized 80 studies examining osteoporosis and fracture care in the primary care setting, focusing on health provider and patient experiences, patterns of diagnosis and treatment, improvement strategies, and the barriers affecting care delivery. Overall, the findings indicate substantial and persistent gaps in osteoporosis and post-fracture care within primary care settings globally. Across studies, limited health provider and patient knowledge, misconceptions about osteoporosis risk, and variable experiences of care were commonly reported. In addition, patterns of diagnosis and treatment consistently demonstrated low rates of osteoporosis assessment and pharmacological management following fragility fracture, highlighting a sustained treatment gap.

This review found that clinicians generally recognised osteoporosis as an important condition, but have variable knowledge of risk factors, guideline thresholds and medication safety profiles, and often underuse DXA, fracture risk tools and pharmacotherapy [39, 90]. A Singaporean study of 334 primary care physicians reported that reading osteoporosis guidelines was associated with greater knowledge and better practice (i.e., more frequent screening and use of risk assessment tools) [39]. Similarly, in a German survey of 892 GPs, knowledge of osteoporosis guidelines was positively associated with feeling competent in caring for patients with osteoporosis [37]. Given the availability of clear, evidence-based clinical guidelines for osteoporosis management from organisations such as RACGP and UK National Osteoporosis Guideline Group [98, 99], these findings suggest clinician education and guideline awareness can improve knowledge, confidence, and osteoporosis management in primary care. On the patient side, our findings show that there is often a lack of awareness that fractures are manifestations of osteoporosis, and a lack of understanding of the health implications of osteoporosis [32, 73]. This may indicate that clinical encounters do not consistently translate into improved patient understanding of osteoporosis, potentially through lack of acknowledgement from clinicians due to competing priorities of other health conditions. Improving osteoporosis and fracture care requires addressing multiple domains, including enhancing health literacy and communication between patients and providers [100]. Strengthening clinician competence and integrating clear tools into clinical workflows can ensure that patients understand their risk and treatment options, supporting informed shared decision-making [16].

A clear pattern emerged of persistent under identification and undertreatment of osteoporosis and fragility fractures across primary care. Screening and diagnostic activity was consistently low, formal risk assessment tools were rarely used, and large population datasets showed substantial undertreatment of individuals meeting guideline criteria of an osteoporosis diagnosis [11, 28, 36, 42, 60]. The gaps in diagnosis were reflected in treatment patterns, with substantially lower pharmacotherapy use and varied prescribing behaviours in primary care settings [38, 60, 80]. While adherence and persistence data were limited in our review, available data suggested high rates of early discontinuation and insufficient follow-up [47, 74]. Collectively, these findings highlight major system-level gaps and several key areas for improvement in osteoporosis identification and follow-up that could be remedied through greater implementation of effective interventions.

Across 23 of the included intervention-based studies, the most consistent finding was that system-level and coordinator-driven interventions substantially improved osteoporosis diagnosis and treatment rates, particularly uptake of BMD testing and initiation of anti-fracture therapy [33, 35, 43, 53, 61, 82, 86, 89, 91]. Effective intervention models included FLS, pharmacist-supported reviews, and EMR-prompted case finding tools, all of which produced the most reliable effects. Relatively few studies in our review demonstrated improvements in clinical outcomes such as subsequent fractures or mortality, which study authors attributed to short follow-up durations, limited power to detect fracture endpoints, and dilution of treatment effects due to contamination within usual care [41, 77]. Decision support appeared more useful when it provided an explicit treatment recommendation rather than risk information alone [27], and multifaceted patient-and-clinician interventions showed larger effects with higher-intensity implementation [43, 86]. Conversely, written recommendations to GPs and stand-alone EMR tools produced limited change when no person or pathway was accountable for follow-through [31, 82, 91]. The intervention literature suggests that osteoporosis care is more likely to improve within the primary care setting when identifying fragility fracture patients and initiating investigations/treatment is assigned to a specific person or process, such as a nurse, pharmacist, coordinator or active patient-specific decision-support prompt, rather than relying on education and voluntary follow-up alone [101, 102]. From a practice perspective, the most plausible model for primary care is a coordinator-led, risk-stratified pathway that adapts fracture-liaison principles to primary care. Sustained investment in coordinated, system-integrated models are likely to be essential for achieving scalable and durable improvements in fracture prevention [103, 104]. Further research is needed on GP-based FLS that includes fracture endpoints and cost-effectiveness data. These should also be tested in low socioeconomic groups, who are at a greater risk of osteoporosis and fractures [105].

Lastly, several consistent and intersecting barriers and facilitators to optimal osteoporosis and fracture management were identified, with important implications for frontline practice. Clinician-level factors such as limited confidence in prescribing antiresorptive therapy, uncertainty about treatment thresholds, and variable familiarity with contemporary guidelines remained prominent barriers [14, 40]. These clinician-level challenges seem to also consistently be reinforced by system-level obstacles [106], including unclear responsibility for ongoing osteoporosis care, fragmented referral pathways, and poor continuity between acute, specialist, and primary care services [14, 16, 75, 95]. Organizational and policy-level strategies, such as financial incentives and formalized post-fracture assessment pathways, were suggested as mechanisms to increase the priority of osteoporosis within primary care [14, 16, 94]. Strengthening integrated care models, such as FLS, and deploying digital prompts or shared-care protocols can meaningfully reduce these system inefficiencies. Future research should prioritize implementation-focused studies examining strategies to operationalize care coordination, test scalable models for clinician education, and evaluate patient-facing tools that improve treatment initiation and long-term adherence.

Strengths and limitations

The strengths of this review include a large and diverse evidence base and a rigorous search methodology. By synthesizing findings from 80 studies across multiple countries, this review provides broad international representation, enhancing the generalizability of the findings. The inclusion of qualitative, quantitative, and mixed-methods evidence, as well as a range of study designs including observational, interventional, and qualitative studies, allowed for a comprehensive and balanced synthesis of the existing literature. Several limitations of this review should also be considered. There was substantial heterogeneity in outcome definitions, methodologies, and reporting across included studies, which limited the ability to directly compare findings and precluded quantitative synthesis. In addition, clinician demographics and key patient characteristics were inconsistently reported, restricting the ability to explore how these factors may have influenced outcomes. Finally, the review was limited to studies published in English, introducing potential bias.

Conclusion

This review provides insights into the effectiveness of current primary care management strategies for osteoporosis, highlights key challenges, and identifies opportunities to strengthen osteoporosis and fracture prevention efforts. Osteoporosis management in primary care is characterized by persistent underidentification and undertreatment, despite clear guideline recommendations and effective prevention strategies. The strongest evidence for successful osteoporosis management in primary care supports system-integrated, coordinator-driven approaches that improve identification of high-risk patients and increase investigation and treatment initiation.
