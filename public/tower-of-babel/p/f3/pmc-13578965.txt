Effects of chitin derived from black soldier fly larvae on growth performance, antioxidant capacity, hepatopancreas morphology, intestine microbiota, immune capacity, and lipid metabolism of Pacific white shrimp (Litopenaeusvannamei)
Authors: Xiaomin Li, Yongkang Chen, Xuanqi Chen, Haiqi Pu, Wenrui Zhang, Tao Ye, Xingyu Gu, Zhihong Liao, Wei Zhao, Jin Niu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The use of polysaccharides as feed immunostimulants represents an effective alternative to antibiotics for disease prevention, with chitin emerging as a promising immune modulator. In this study, chitin was systematically extracted from black soldier fly (Hermetia illucens) larvae and incorporated into diets for Pacific white shrimp (Litopenaeus vannamei). Six experimental diets were formulated to contain graded levels of chitin: 0 (control, CD), 0.05%, 0.10%, 0.20%, 0.40%, and 0.80%, respectively. A total of 720 juvenile shrimp (0.32 ± 0.01 g, 6 weeks old) were equally allocated to the six dietary treatments (four replicates per group) and fed the respective diets for 56 d. The final body weight in the group supplemented with 0.20% chitin showed an increasing tendency compared with the control CD group (P = 0.056). Compared with the CD group, the relative mRNA expression level of cat in the hepatopancreas of Pacific white shrimp tended to be up-regulated in the 0.20% and 0.40% chitin-supplemented groups (P = 0.055); the activity of superoxide dismutase (SOD) in the hepatopancreas also significantly increased after the addition of 0.40% and 0.80% chitin (P = 0.006). Immunologically, a significant increase in the relative mRNA expression levels of dorsal and relish in the hepatopancreas was observed following the addition of 0.20% chitin compared with the CD group (P < 0.05). Regarding material metabolism, a decrease in serum glucose levels was observed when chitin was added at levels above 0.20% (P = 0.014); and it was observed a significant upregulation of irs, pi3k, s6k, srebp, and tor mRNA expression levels relative to the CD group (P < 0.05). A chitin-supplemented diet can enhance the antioxidant capacity, immune capability, lipid synthesis, and catabolism in Pacific white shrimp, without exerting negative effects on the morphology of the hepatopancreas and intestine microbiota. In the present study, the recommended inclusion level of the insect chitin to be added to the diets was 0.20%.

Introduction

Pacific white shrimp (Litopenaeus vannamei) is one of the important economic species in the current aquaculture industry. It is characterized by its fast growth rate, strong adaptability, and delicious taste, making it a popular choice among consumers in China (Liao and Chien, 2011; Tian et al., 2024). However, with the increase in aquatic scale and density, the risk of disease outbreaks increases, which can affect the growth and survival of aquatic animals and cause substantial economic losses (Kelly and Renukdas, 2020). The use of antibiotics and chemical drugs is effective in preventing and controlling diseases. However, the long-term application of these substances may lead to the development of antibiotic resistance in environmental microorganisms, posing significant risks to the environment (Kümmerer, 2009; Polianciuc et al., 2020).

The addition of immunostimulants to the diet, substituting for antibiotics, has become an important topic in modern aquaculture (Mohan et al., 2023). It has been reported that natural polysaccharides, which derive from plants, animals, or microorganisms, such as kelp polysaccharide, carrageenan, β-glucans, cyanobacteria polysaccharides, ginseng polysaccharide, and propolis polysaccharides, exhibited effective immune-modulating effects (Fan et al., 2012; Hu et al., 2022; Pi et al., 2024; Shi et al., 2025; Stephanie et al., 2010; Sukhikh et al., 2024). Natural polysaccharides are biodegradable and non-toxic, making them more environmentally friendly compared to antibiotics and chemical drugs (Maiti et al., 2025). Chitin is one such polysaccharide, being a cationic natural biopolymer made up of N-acetyl-D-glucosamine, widely found in crustaceans, fish scales, and insects (Singh et al., 2017). Chitin has multiple bioactivities as a food additive, including enhancing immunity, improving intestine microbiota structure, reducing blood lipids and cholesterol, and enhancing gastrointestinal function (Mu et al., 2024; Santisteban et al., 2024). A previous study has shown that supplementing the diet with 0.75% chitin can increase the phenoloxidase activity in freshwater prawn (Macrobrachium rosenbergii) and improve survival rate after treatment with white tail disease virus (Kumar et al., 2015). While the injection of chitin in Pacific white shrimp could enhance phenoloxidase activity and increase immunity to resist infection by Vibrio alginolyticus (Wang and Chen, 2005). Adding chitosan (a kind of chitin derivative) derived from Daphnia similis in the diet had the potential to enhance the growth and immune response of Pacific white shrimp, enhance the gene expression of phenoloxidase and hsp70 (the full names of the abbreviations of all genes can be found in Table S1), and effectively resist infection by Vibrio spp. (Cheng et al., 2021). Furthermore, incorporating 0.2% chitosan into the diet can boost the growth performance and stress tolerance of Pacific white shrimp (Niu et al., 2011).

The black soldier fly (Hermetia illucens) is a saprophagous insect capable of converting organic waste into its own nutritional components (Nayak et al., 2024). It possesses strong disease and antibacterial resistance (Zhang et al., 2025). Research has indicated that the lipids and proteins derived from black soldier fly larvae (BSFL) can serve as sustainable alternatives in aquafeeds, replacing fish oil, soybean oil, or fish meal (Chang et al., 2025; He et al., 2024). In addition to these components, BSFL also contains a high level of chitin, which has the potential to be used as an aquafeed additive (Yuan et al., 2024). In laboratory's previous study, the defatted BSFL meal was hydrolyzed to obtain the BSFL chitin product (Chen et al., 2025). In the current study, this chitin, after further purification, was used in the feed for Pacific white shrimp, and a detailed comparison was conducted on different properties, activities, and usage doses of the chitin.

Materials and methods

Animal ethics statement

The research, along with its experimental protocols, has received clearance from the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at Sun Yat-sen University (SYSU-IACUC-2024-B0529). Laboratory animals were raised and handled in compliance with institutional regulations, and their welfare was ensured throughout the experiment.

Experimental diets

The chitin used in this experiment was derived from BSFL provided by Qinghai Kunjie Environmental Technology Co., Xining, Qinghai, China. The extraction method of chitin was carried out according to the following steps (Chen et al., 2025). To summarize, the larvae underwent drying at 105 °C for a duration of 24 h. After drying and grinding, the BSFL powder was treated with a combination of alkaline protease (Bacillus subtilis), alkaline protease (Bacillus licheniformis), and keratinase at a weight ratio of 1:1:1 for 1 h at 55 °C with an enzyme-to-substrate (E/S) ratio of 3.0%. The remaining solid residue was heated at 70 °C for 90 min in 2.2 mol/L NaOH to dissolve the melanin and obtain crude chitin. The crude chitin was soaked in 1.0 mol/L HCl for 24 h, then treated with 0.40% sodium hypochlorite (NaClO) at pH 3 and 70 °C for 30 min, followed by thorough washing and drying, yielding relatively pure chitin.

Prior to feed preparation, all raw feed ingredients were ground and sieved through a 100-mesh screen to remove coarse particles. All powdered ingredients were fully blended homogenously, and then processed into cylindrical pellets (2 mm in diameter and 5 mm in length) using a feed extruder. The obtained pellets were subjected to steam sterilization at 85 °C for 90 min, followed by air-drying at room temperature (20 °C). After cooling, all diet samples were sealed and stored at −20 °C until use.

Chitin purity and degree of acetylation (DA)

Chitin was characterized using a fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectrometer (Nicolet iS5, Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA, USA). The chitin sample was measured and compared with shrimp shell chitin (BR, CAS 1398–61-4, Xiya Reagent Co., Ltd., Linyi, Shandong, China) within the wavelength range of 525 to 4000 cm−1. The absorbance A = −log10(T/100) (T: transmittance, %), for shrimp shell chitin samples with a DA greater than 95%, the DA level of BSFL-derived chitin can be roughly estimated using the formula (Fatima, 2020):DA%=A1650/A2920sample/A1650/A2920standard×100.

The purity of chitin was determined following the high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) and liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) method described by D'Hondt et al. (2020). A 50 mg dried powder sample was mixed with 5 mL of 6 mol/L HCl in a heat-resistant and pressure-resistant glass bottle and hydrolyzed at 110 °C for 2 h. The acetate concentration was determined by partially neutralizing 250 μL of the filtered supernatant hydrolysate sample with 750 μL of 400 mmol/L NaOH. A 25 μL diluted sample was injected into a Shimadzu Prominence LC-20AT high-performance liquid chromatography system coupled with an RID-20A differential refractive index detector (Shimadzu Corporation, Kyoto, Japan). Separation was performed on a Dikma CarboPac H+ column (8.0 mm × 300 mm, 6 μm) maintained at 55 °C. The mobile phase was 0.01 mol/L H2SO4 delivered at a flow rate of 0.6 mL/min, and the total running time was 30 min. To determine the contents of glucosamine and N-acetylglucosamine, 0.5 mL of the filtered supernatant was diluted to 10 mL. Then, 40 μL of the diluted sample was added to a mixture prepared from 960 μL of a solution consisting of 50 mL acetonitrile, 45 mL water, and 1 mL 1 mol/L NaOH, and detected using the Shimadzu LCMS-8050 LC-MS/MS system (Shimadzu Corporation, Kyoto, Japan), with a Shim-pack GIST-HP column (2.1 mm × 150 mm, 1.9 μm) at a temperature of 40 °C, and a mobile phase of acetonitrile-0.1% formic acid, gradient elution.

The testing for this project was completed by Beijing Zhongke Institute of Photoanalysis Science and Technology, Beijing, China. The content of chitin was roughly estimated using the formula provided in the literature:Hydrolyzedchitinmg/mL=cGlucosamine×161.15+cAcetate×42.04+cN-acetylglucosamine×203.19,where c represents the concentration. The chitin content was calculated as the formula (D'Hondt et al., 2020):Chitincontent(%)=(Hydrolyzedchitin×Totalvolumeofhydrolysate)/Initialmassofthesample×100.

Experimental design

All the shrimps were provided by Guorong Aquatic Co., Ltd., Yangxi, Yangjiang, China. A total of 720 shrimps (initial body weight 0.32 ± 0.01 g) were evenly distributed into six groups, four replicates per group (total 24 tanks), with 30 shrimps per tank. Among the 24 tanks, four tanks were designated as the CD group, and the remaining tanks were the experimental groups. The shrimps were fed with the corresponding diets three times a day (09:00, 15:00, and 21:00) for eight weeks. The feeding amount was adjusted daily based on the shrimps’ consumption. The breeding facility was located in Guorong Aquatic Co., Ltd., Yangxi, Yangjiang, China. During the breeding period, water quality was monitored once a week: dissolved oxygen 7.7 to 8.1 mg/L, water temperature 27.2 to 29.2 °C, pH 7.9 to 8.2.

At the end of the breeding experiment, all the shrimps were sampled on the day following the last night of feeding. All surviving shrimps in each tank were counted and weighed. Six shrimps per tank were used for proximate composition analysis. Four shrimps were weighed for total body weight and hepatopancreas weight, and their body length was measured as the distance from the post-orbital edge of the eyestalk to the tip of the telson. The hepatopancreas of six shrimps was collected for biochemical analysis or histological section preparation. The hemolymph of six shrimps in each tank was collected for biochemical analysis. The muscle tissues of six shrimps were collected to measure the proximate composition.

Determination of proximate composition

The analysis of moisture (method 925.09), crude protein (method 997.09) and crude lipid (method 960.39) was determined according to the standard methods of the AOAC (2023). The whole shrimps and their muscle tissues were subjected to drying at 105 °C until they reached a stable weight for the determination of moisture content. The feed, dried shrimps, and muscles were subsequently ground into powder. Crude lipid content of the powder was measured using a Soxhlet extractor (Soxtec System HT6, Tecator AB, Höganäs, Sweden). The crude protein (N × 6.25) content of the powder was determined using a Dumas nitrogen analyzer (N pro DT Ar/He Basic, Gerhardt GMBH & Co. KG, Königswinter, Germany). Ash content was determined according to GB/T 6438-2007 (AQSIQ, 2007), and organic matter content was calculated by subtracting ash content from dry matter content.

Hepatopancreas histology

The hepatopancreas tissue samples were stored in a 4% paraformaldehyde solution for 24 h. The tissues were then dehydrated in a graded ethanol series, immersed in a solution of equal parts ethanol and xylene, and left for 30 min, followed by immersion in xylene for another 30 min. Subsequently, the tissues were embedded in paraffin, and sections were prepared and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) (Li et al., 2019). Finally, the sections were observed under a Nikon Eclipse Ni-E upright microscope (Nikon Corporation, Tokyo, Japan). The number of B cells (secretory cells), R cells (absorptive cells), and E cells (embryonic cells) in each hepatopancreatic tubule was counted directly. The area of B and R cells was measured using ImageJ 1.52a (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA) and then analyzed in IBM SPSS Statistics software (version 27.0, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Biochemical assays of hepatopancreas and serum

The hepatopancreas tissues collected were quickly placed in liquid nitrogen and then stored at −80 °C. To prepare the samples, tissues were added to phosphate buffered saline to make a 10% homogenate, followed by centrifugation at 6500 × g, 4 °C for 25 min, and harvesting the supernatant. The shrimp hemolymph was coagulated at 4 °C, then homogenized and centrifuged at 1100 × g for 5 min at 4 °C. The supernatant (serum) was collected for experimental use.

The supernatant of the hepatopancreas and the serum were used for the detection of antioxidant indicators, lipid content, and phosphatase activity. Biochemical detection kits applied in this study included superoxide dismutase (SOD; S0101S), glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px; A005-1-1), total antioxidant capacity (T-AOC; A015-3-1), glucose (Glu; A154-2-1), total protein (Pro; W010-1-1), non-esterified fatty acids (NEFA; A042-2-1), triglycerides (TG; A110-1-1), total cholesterol (T-CHO; A111-1-1), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C; A113-1-1), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C; A112-1-1), alkaline phosphatase (AKP; A059-2-2), and acid phosphatase (ACP; A060-2-2). The SOD assay kit was purchased from Beyotime Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China), whereas all remaining kits were obtained from Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute (Nanjing, Jiangsu, China). All absorbance measurements were performed using an Epoch microplate spectrophotometer (BioTek Instruments, Inc., Winooski, VT, USA).

Real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR)

All the hepatopancreas samples were stored at −80 °C before use. The total RNA was extracted from them with the Plus RNA kit (Beyotime Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) before use. Then total RNA quality and concentration were detected using the Nnanodrop spectrophotometer (NanoDrop 2000, Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.,Waltham, MA, USA). The gDNA removal and reverse transcription of total RNA were performed according to the protocol of the Evo M-MLV Reverse transcription reagent kit (Accurate Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Changsha, Hunan, China). The RT-qPCR system (10 μL) consisted of 2 × 5 μL SYBR (SYBR Green Premix Pro Taq HS qPCR Kit II, Accurate Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Changsha, Hunan, China), 0.5 μL of forward and reverse primers, 1 μL of the reverse transcription product, and 3 μL of diethyl pyrocarbonate (DEPC)-treated water. The reaction program was as follows: denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 5 s, annealing at 60 °C for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 30 s, finally cooling to 4 °C (Chen et al., 2023). The reaction was carried out using Roche LightCycler 480 Instrument II Real Time PCR System (Roche Diagnostics GmbH, Mannheim, Germany) in a 384-well plate. The ef-1α was selected as the reference gene. The data obtained were analyzed for relative expression using the 2-ΔΔCt method (Livak and Schmittgen, 2001). Intestine microbiota

For the CD and CT0.20 groups, four replicates were randomly selected, and the intestines of four shrimps per replicate were pooled for analysis. These samples were sent to Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China, for 16S rRNA gene sequencing. The process involved DNA extraction, followed by amplification of the V3 + V4 regions of the 16S rDNA using barcoded primers (5′-3′): 341F (CCTACGGGGNGGCWGCAG) and 806R (GGACTACHVGGGGTATCTAAT). The PCR products were then purified, ligated to sequencing adapters, and subjected to Illumina sequencing. Raw reads were quality-filtered, and paired-end reads were merged and further refined into clean tags using QIIME (v1.9.1). Operational taxonomic units (OTUs) were identified, and their abundances were determined using Usearch, and amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) were analyzed. Finally, Mothur (v1.45.3) was employed for taxonomic annotation, community composition analysis, linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) analysis, and the calculation of α-diversity indices, including ACE, Chao, Simpson, and Shannon indices.

Statistical analysis

The formulas used to calculate growth performance and morphological indices are as follows:Initialbodyweight(IBW,g)=Totalweight/Initialnumberofshrimps;Finalbodyweight(FBW,g)=Totalweight/Finalnumberofshrimps;Weightgainrate(WGR,%)=100×(FBW-IBW)/IBW;Specificgrowthrate(SGR,%/d)=100×(lnFBW-lnIBW)/Days;Survivalrate(SR,%)=100×Finalnumberofshrimps/Initialnumberofshrimps;Feedconversionrate(FCR)=Feedconsumed/(FBW-IBW);Conditionfactor(CF,100g/cm3)=100×FBW/Bodylength3;Hepatosomaticindex(HI,%)=100×Liverweight/FBW;Feedingrate[FR,g/(kg·d)]=Totalfeedintake/[Days/(Initialtotalweight+Finaltotalweight)/2];Feedintake(FI,g)=Feedconsumed/Survivalnumber.

The experimental data were processed using IBM SPSS Statistics software (version 27.0, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The data were first evaluated for normal distribution using the Shapiro-Wilk test and for homogeneity of variance using Levene's test. Statistical differences were evaluated using one-way ANOVA and Tukey and Duncan's multiple comparisons. The P-values for linear and quadratic predictions were calculated using the "curve prediction" function. The results were expressed as the mean and standard error of the mean (SEM), and the data were considered significant at P < 0.05.

The data calculations refer to the following models for one-way ANOVA:Yij=μ+αi+ϵij,where Yij is the j-th observation in the i-th group; μ is the overall mean; αi is the treatment effect of the i-th group; and ϵij is the random error. Tukey and Duncan's multiple range comparisons:q=Yi−YjSY¯,SY¯=MSWn,where q is the studentized range statistic; Yi is the mean value of the i-th treatment group; Yj is the mean value of the j-th treatment group; SY¯ is the standard error of the mean; M SW is the mean square within groups; and n is the number of observations per group. The Duncan test is conducted based on the one-way ANOVA model.

The purity and properties of chitin

After measurement and calculation, the purity ratio of commercial purified chitin (derived from shrimp shells) to BSFL-derived chitin was determined to be 1:0.8473, with an adjusted ratio of 1:0.8501. Since the purity of the purified chitin was greater than 99%, the predicted purity of the sample in this experiment was between 83.88% and 85.01%. The FTIR calculation results showed that the values of A1650/A2920 were purified chitin 1.7744, BSFL-derived chitin 0.9089. Given that the DA level of the purified chitin was greater than 95%, the estimated DA level for the BSFL-chitin was roughly between 48.66% and 51.23%. The FTIR results and HPLC spectra are shown in Fig. S1.

Discussion

The outbreak of diseases is one of the important reasons for declines in aquaculture productivity (Mo et al., 2017). Although the extensive use of antibiotics can control diseases efficiently, it also causes antibiotics to accumulate, leading to water environmental pollution (Adenaya et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2017). In recent years, prebiotics, synbiotics, and polysaccharides have been added to aquafeed as functional additives to enhance animals' immunity and disease resistance (Amenyogbe et al., 2020; Arun et al., 2023; Mohan et al., 2019). Chitin is a nitrogen-containing polysaccharide composed of N-acetyl-D-glucosamine (GlcNAc) monomers linked by β-1,4-glycosidic bonds, with a chemical structure similar to cellulose (Moussian, 2019). As a dietary additive, chitin is widely available, renewable, and biodegradable, making it an environmentally friendly material (Hasan et al., 2023; Souza et al., 2011). Currently, chitin used as a food additive is primarily extracted from the exoskeletons of crustaceans (such as crab shells and shrimp shells), with relatively fewer studies focusing on extraction from insects (Lv et al., 2023).

During insect development, chitin synthase (CHS) and chitin deacetylase (CDA) act synergistically. Specifically, during the molting process, the deacetylation activity of CDA is required to soften chitin (Lu and Shen, 2024; Yu et al., 2016). In contrast, crustaceans such as shrimp and crabs rely on demineralization for exoskeleton replacement, and their CDA expression levels are much lower (Zhang et al., 2021). Generally, therefore, insect-derived chitin exhibits a lower DA. For instance, the DA level was 59.5% for BSFL, 81.9% for yellow mealworms (Tenebrio molitor), and 83.4% for shrimp shells (Pedrazzani et al., 2025). In another study, the DA level of whole BSFL was determined to be approximately 78.1%, whereas commercially available shrimp shell-derived chitin exceeded 95% (D'Hondt et al., 2020). A similar conclusion was also reached after FTIR was used to roughly estimate the DA level of the chitin used in this experiment. Beyond the lower DA, insect chitin also has a smaller molecular weight and lower crystallinity, enabling it to be degraded more rapidly by chitinases in the shrimp intestine. Further, chitooligosaccharides can exert their functional effects more efficiently (Pedrazzani et al., 2024). During the determination of chitin purity, the harsh conditions of the hydrolysis step may lead to the loss of acetylation, preventing the detection of GlcNAc. Therefore, the purity of chitin could only be roughly estimated based on the concentrations of acetate and glucosamine. In this study, chitin purified from BSFL under laboratory conditions reached a purity of approximately 84%, with potential impurities including residual proteins and minerals. This method enables the separation of various components from BSFL, simultaneously yielding products such as oil, protein sources, and melanin, thereby reducing production costs. In practical applications, BSFL can be mass-reared using food waste, ensuring a more reliable source. This approach is more environmentally friendly and sustainable, thereby endowing it with greater practical application value (Iqbal et al., 2024; Ramzy et al., 2025).

Previous studies have highlighted the strong biological functions of chitin, including immune regulation, enhanced disease resistance, and so on (Mohan et al., 2023). Shanthi Mari et al. (2014) have found that feeding mrigal (Cirrhina mrigala) with a diet supplemented with 1% shrimp-derived chitin could enhance phagocytic and lysozyme activities and increase resistance to Aphanomyces invadans. Harikrishnan et al. (2012) found that feeding Kelp grouper (Epinephelus bruneus) with a diet containing 1% chitin could amplify the activities of lysozyme and peroxidase and increase disease resistance to Philasterides dicentrarchi. Moreover, a previous study has found that freshwater prawns (Macrobrachium rosenbergii) fed with a diet containing 0.75% chitin (with a purity of 63.16%) had a higher survival rate after being attacked by white tail disease (Kumar et al., 2015).

However, research on dietary chitin in Pacific white shrimp remains limited (Mohan et al., 2023). After consuming BSFL-derived chitin, it was more easily degraded with endogenous chitinase compared to traditional commercial chitin. The resulting chitooligosaccharides or monosaccharides act as pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs), which can be recognized by Toll-like receptors (TLRs) on the cell surface, thereby activating the innate immune response of the organism (Da Silva et al., 2008; Govindaraj et al., 2022). In the present study, the increased relative expression levels of dorsal gene indicated the activation of the TLR pathway. The increased expression of another nuclear factor kappa-B (NF-κB) molecule, relish, is associated with the activation of the immune deficiency (IMD) pathway (Nishide et al., 2019). Although chitin cannot directly bind to peptidoglycan recognition protein (PGRP) in the IMD pathway, previous studies have shown that the cross-talk between the TLR and IMD pathway may lead to the activation of the IMD pathway (Horng and Medzhitov, 2001). The elevated expression of these two NF-κB genes promoted the transcription of more immune-related genes (Bhatt and Ghosh, 2014; Mitchell and Carmody, 2018), which was also confirmed by the increased relative expression of pen3a gene. The secretion of these antimicrobial peptides played a positive role in moderately activating the immune system and enhancing antibacterial capacity (Dembic, 2015; Jang et al., 2021; Kucharska et al., 2019; Yang et al., 2023).

Antioxidant enzymes or antioxidants can scavenge reactive oxygen species (ROS) in cells, thereby protecting the body's cells from ROS damage and preventing tissue damage and inflammation caused by oxidative stress (Hasanthi et al., 2024; Liao et al., 2025). In addition, antioxidant enzymes or antioxidants can counteract the excessive inflammatory response caused by inflammatory factors and maintain homeostasis in the body (Bhol et al., 2024). This study infers that reduced serum antioxidant enzyme activity occurs because dietary chitin lowers ROS production, which suppresses the expression of antioxidant enzyme genes and subsequently diminishes enzyme activity (Lennicke and Cochemé, 2021). Similarly, in the hepatopancreas, the SOD converted superoxide anion radicals into oxygen and hydrogen peroxide (Parrilla-Taylor and Zenteno-Savín, 2011). The expression of cat showed an increasing trend, thus promoting the consumption of hydrogen peroxide, which may indirectly lead to the reduced expression of gpx (Ighodaro and Akinloye, 2018). Previous studies have demonstrated that chitin and its derivatives can scavenge free radicals and suppress inflammation and oxidative stress in LPS-induced RAW264.7 macrophages (Chotphruethipong et al., 2023). Similarly, providing 0.4% chitosan in the diet of Pacific white shrimp can enhance the SOD activity in hemolymph (Cheng et al., 2021), and when 0.4 % chitin was added to the diet of giant tiger prawn (Penaeus monodon), SOD activity decreased while GSH-Px activity increased, collectively indicating an enhanced antioxidative status of the hepatopancreas (Niu et al., 2013). This was contrary to the biochemical assays obtained in the present study, which may be related to species differences and sampling time differences. Generally speaking, chitin has lower antioxidant capacity than chitosan and chitooligosaccharides, as it possesses fewer free amino groups, resulting in a reduced ability to bind with oxidative free radicals and chelate metal ions (Yanat et al., 2022). BSFL-derived chitin has a lower DA level, indicating more free amino groups, which may confer greater potential for antioxidant activity than traditional commercial chitin (Izadi et al., 2025).

In addition to immunological effects, chitin and its derivatives also have the potential to promote the growth of shrimps. Previous studies have found that dietary supplementation with 20% chitin was effective in promoting the growth rate of Macrobrachium tenellum juvenile prawns (de Los Santos-Romero R). Cheng et al. (2021) found that adding 0.4% Daphnia similis-derived chitosan could accelerate Pacific white shrimp growth rate, while in another study, it was suggested that adding 4% chitosan in the Pacific white shrimp diet could achieve a higher growth rate compared to adding 0, 1%, 2%, and 3% chitosan (Ekaputri et al., 2020). Due to the varying sources and compositions of chitin or chitosan, the optimal supplementation levels considered in previous studies have been inconsistent (Mohan et al., 2023; Niu et al., 2013). In the present study, the CT0.20 group showed an increasing trend in FBW, which demonstrated a growth-promoting effect consistent with the previous studies. However, the slight and non-significant increase in FCR suggested a potential risk that higher doses of chitin might further elevate FCR, which suggested potential negative effects associated with chitin. Chitin or chitosan (especially chitosan) in the digestive tract may encapsulate and adsorb nutrients, such as proteins, fats, and minerals, thereby reducing feed digestion and absorption efficiency (Koide, 1998; Ngasotter et al., 2022). The properties of BSFL-derived chitin fell between those of traditional chitin and chitosan, addressing the insufficient bioactivity of traditional chitin while avoiding the excessive adsorptivity characteristic of chitosan. The effects of chitin-supplemented diets on hepatopancreas morphology, phosphatase activity, and proximate composition in Pacific white shrimps were further investigated. The chitin-supplemented diet conferred a protective effect on the hepatopancreas, as reflected by increased B cell area and elevated R and E cell counts, which may facilitate absorption, secretion, and repair functions (Cervellione et al., 2017; Vogt, 2019). The increased trend of activities of ACP and AKP in the hepatopancreas indicated the higher levels of energy metabolism and antioxidant capacity, as well as the enhanced immune capacity in the hepatopancreas (Sun et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2024). Regarding the proximate composition, no significant differences in the crude lipid content of the whole body and muscle were observed, which may be an area of future research.

Chitin in the diet exerts a subtle influence on the intestine microbiota. It is considered by some to be a prebiotic, as its intake promotes the growth of beneficial bacterial genera capable of utilizing chitin, such as some species in Bifidobacterium, Lactobacillus, and Bacteroides (Aprilia et al., 2022; Chen et al., 2002; Nurhayati and Yew, 2016). After preliminary digestion, chitooligosaccharides can disrupt the integrity of the cell membranes of harmful bacteria (such as Escherichia coli and Salmonella), thereby inhibiting their growth and proliferation (Kipkoech et al., 2021; Meng et al., 2022). In this regard, as BSFL-derived chitin has a lower DA level and is more easily and effectively digested, it theoretically exhibits stronger activity towards improving the intestine microbiota. In the present study, although the Chao and Simpson indexes of the CT0.20 group showed a slight decrease compared with the CD group, the overall changes in α-diversity and community composition were minor after the addition of 0.2% chitin. β-diversity analysis revealed a slight shift in the microbiota community structure, which was not statistically significant—this may be attributed to the insufficient dosage of chitin added. An increase in the dominant genus Thalassococcus was observed in the CT0.20 group. This genus is associated with the marine environment and may be linked to salt tolerance (Yang et al., 2025). Interestingly, a reduction in the abundance of Chlamydiota and Planctomycetota was observed in the CT0.20 group. Chlamydiota are obligate intracellular parasites, and this phylum includes pathogens that have significant impacts on aquatic organisms (Stride et al., 2014). While Planctomycetota contributes to the decomposition of organic matter in the intestine microbiota, excessive proliferation of certain taxa within this phylum may cause diseases in aquatic organisms (Kaboré et al., 2020). Previous studies have also suggested that the addition of chitin to the diet has a positive impact on the intestine microbiota. Feeding Atlantic salmon (Salmo salar) a diet containing 6.4% chitin increased the abundance of Actinomyces, Bacillus, and Enterococcus (Holen et al., 2025); diets supplemented with 2% and 6% chitin led to a significantly higher relative abundance of Lactobacillus, Weissella, and Neoscardovia in the intestine of Pacific white shrimp (He, 2022); and the addition of 5% dietary chitin was able to regulate the intestine microbiota of Atlantic cod (Gadus morhua L.), reducing populations of E.coli and Anaerorhabdus furcosa (Zhou et al., 2013). In the present study, whether or not supplementation with BSFL-derived chitin significantly affects the structure of the intestine microbiota at higher doses requires further investigation.

Chitin can bind to dietary lipids, affecting the absorption of lipid substances and thereby influencing lipid metabolism (Pascon et al., 2024; Zhen et al., 2022). Since there were no significant differences in crude lipid content, lipid biochemical assays in the hepatopancreas and serum were further investigated. In the present study, the levels of LDL-C in the serum decreased with the increased addition of chitin. It was suggested that higher levels of dietary chitin may inhibit lipid absorption in the intestine, leading to reduced levels of lipids in the serum (Baker et al., 2009; Santisteban et al., 2024; Zhen et al., 2022). In the phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase/protein kinase B/target of rapamycin (PI3K-AKT-TOR) signaling pathway, it was observed that the expression of genes such as irs, pi3k, tor, s6k, and lipid synthesis-related genes like srebp and fas was elevated. It was inferred that lipid and protein synthesis in the hepatopancreas might increase, especially in the CT0.20 group (Guo et al., 2025; Lu et al., 2023; Wei et al., 2023). Reduced lipid absorption in the intestine may have led to compensatory upregulation of srebp and fas expression in the hepatopancreas to maintain stable lipid levels in the body (He Gui-Lun, 2023). Considering the lipid content in the hepatopancreas, no significant changes were observed except for a decrease in NEFA, indicating that chitin supplementation did not cause significant lipid deposition. In addition, the decrease in serum glucose level may relate to the increased expression of irs. Since there were no significant changes in crude lipid content and hepatopancreas morphology, it was considered that the enhanced lipid synthesis and catabolism were within a reasonable range and should not have a substantial impact on the health of shrimps (Chen et al., 2024).

Totally, the increase of the expression of genes of lipid metabolism and antioxidant, corresponding to higher activities of AKP, might contribute to the better growth performance observed in the CT0.20 group. The BSFL-derived chitin used in this experiment, compared to traditional crustacean chitin, achieved improvements in growth, immunity, and antioxidant capacity at a lower dosage, while also exerting certain effects on hepatopancreatic morphology, intestine microbiota and lipid metabolism (Brol et al., 2021; Ekaputri et al., 2020). Unlike traditional chitin extraction processes, this method allows for large-scale farming and production and generates additional BSFL-based products, thereby reducing overall costs. Furthermore, its lower DA level eliminates the necessity for deacetylation to achieve high bioactivity, further lowering production costs and enhancing its application value (Mohan et al., 2024).

Conclusion

Overall, the addition of 0.20% BSFL-derived chitin to the diet was found to promote growth performance, enhance antioxidant capacity, immune capacity, and lipid metabolism. The addition of 0.20% BSFL-derived chitin to the diet was considered the optimal level in this study (Fig. S2). Moreover, the addition of chitin up to 0.80% did not inhibit the growth of Pacific white shrimp and had a positive impact on the morphology of the hepatopancreas. The chitin obtained through a systematic extraction method from BSFL exhibited higher bioactivity than traditional chitin, and as a greener and lower-cost immunomodulator, has more potential for application and promotion in feed formulations.
