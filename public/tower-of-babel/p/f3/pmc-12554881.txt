Improved classification of oral cancer through a personalized transfer learning CNN architecture
Authors: Udita J Monani, Tae Soo Yun, Mangal Sain, Prasant Kumar Pattnaik
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionOral cancer presents a significant danger to worldwide health, resulting in high death rates and substantial suffering. Early detection is crucial to improving treatment outcomes. This study uses deep learning techniques, particularly Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) and transfer learning methodologies, to propose a dependable machine learning system for oral cancer detection.Materials and methodsThe proposed model leverages CNNs and transfer learning, followed by two sequential fully connected operations: FC1 (Feature Embedding) to consolidate learned features and FC2 (Classification Head) for final classification. Despite a small dataset and imbalanced class distribution, the model was trained and evaluated using carefully selected performance metrics including accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, and ROC-AUC. These metrics were specifically chosen to address the challenges of imbalanced datasets, where accuracy alone can be misleading. Class imbalance was addressed through the Synthetic Minority Oversampling Technique (SMOTE), data augmentation, and careful preprocessing strategies. The performance was validated through confusion matrices and AUC-ROC analyses to ensure reliability. However, external validation was not performed, representing a limitation of this study.ResultsThe model achieved an F1-score of 81.48 %, accuracy of 81.38 %, precision of 84.62 %, recall of 78.57 %, and a ROC-AUC score of 0.9082 on the test dataset. During training, it achieved higher metrics: accuracy of 96.94 %, precision of 97.92 %, recall of 96.17 %, F1-score of 97.04 %, and a ROC-AUC score of 0.9967.ConclusionThis research highlights how artificial intelligence can impact clinical workflows in detecting cancer early. The results offer a hopeful path for advancements in automated cancer diagnosis technologies.Keywords: Oral cancer, Convolutional Neural Networks, SMOTE

Introduction

One of the leading causes of cancer-related sickness and mortality worldwide is oral cancer, which includes malignancies of the mouth and throat. Oral cavity and oropharyngeal cancers were a serious public health concern in 2020, accounting for about 377,713 new cases and 177,757 deaths globally.1,2 Early detection of oral cancer is essential for improved survival rates since early diagnosis greatly improves prognosis as shown in Table 1.

Recent developments in artificial intelligence (AI) and deep learning have opened up new avenues for improving medical diagnoses, particularly in the field of medical imaging.3 Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) are a powerful family of deep learning algorithms that have gained a lot of attention due to their ability to accurately classify pictures and automatically extract features.4 By processing vast volumes of data and identifying complex patterns that are beyond human comprehension, CNNs have demonstrated remarkable performance in medical picture analysis, including cancer detection.5 Despite numerous developments in the area, transfer learning has emerged as a key method in AI, especially in situations with little labeled data.6 To better fit certain tasks with smaller datasets, transfer learning entails modifying previously taught models that were trained on large datasets, such as ImageNet.7 This study's goal is to assess how well deep learning models, more especially, CNN's. They can identify oral cancer early on.8 More specifically, it addresses challenges such as the need for accurate automated diagnostic tools, unequal class distributions, and small sample sizes.9,10 This study presents a novel integrative framework by combining Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), transfer learning, and the Synthetic Minority Oversampling Technique (SMOTE) for early detection of oral cancer from photographic images. Unlike existing studies, our approach directly addresses both the chronic issue of class imbalance and the limitations imposed by small annotated datasets in the field. The integration of advanced data augmentation, rigorous class balancing, and robust model selection situates this research at the interface of methodological innovation and urgent clinical utility, thus offering a substantive advance over previous approaches.

Literature review

In recent years, research has focused on using artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) techniques to diagnose oral cancer. This section examines the developments in deep learning, transfer learning, and medical imaging that support the creation of automated diagnostic systems for oral cancer.11

AI has demonstrated enormous promise in transforming cancer diagnosis through precise and effective diagnostic techniques.12 Research has repeatedly demonstrated the efficacy of AI models, especially when it comes to examining medical photos for the purpose of early cancer diagnosis. For example13 established the legitimacy of AI in clinical processes by demonstrating the application of deep learning to classify skin cancer with performance comparable to dermatologists. Similar to this, the use of AI in oral cancer detection has grown in popularity because of its capacity to manage large, complicated datasets and shorten diagnostic times, which promotes early intervention and better prognosis.14

The scarcity of labeled datasets is one of the main obstacles to creating AI models for medical imaging.15 Transfer learning has become a crucial strategy to deal with this. Even with tiny datasets, researchers have shown notable gains in classification tasks by utilizing pre-trained models like VGG16, ResNet, and Inception.16 For instance Ref. 17 achieved state-of-the-art outcomes with little data preprocessing by enhancing breast cancer detection by transfer learning. Similar to this,18 used transfer learning to diagnose oral cancer, showing that it can help overcome the difficulties caused by data scarcity and class imbalance. These results demonstrate the value and adaptability of transfer learning in medical settings. In medical datasets, class imbalance is a common problem that frequently results in skewed model predictions. To address this problem, methods like the Synthetic Minority Oversampling Technique (SMOTE) have gained widespread use. SMOTE was presented by19 as a way to create new instances for minority classes, which balanced the dataset and enhanced model performance. Recent research has demonstrated how well SMOTE works to improve cancer detection models. For example20 achieved greater precision and recall scores by effectively using SMOTE to enhance the categorization of unbalanced oral cancer datasets.

There are still a number of holes in AI-driven oral cancer diagnosis, despite the advances. Numerous current studies depend on sizable, meticulously annotated datasets, which aren't usually accessible in actual clinical settings. Furthermore, in some situations, the problem of overfitting brought on by tiny datasets has not been adequately addressed. Additionally, although SMOTE and transfer learning have shown promise, little is known about how to combine them into a single framework for the identification of oral cancer. By creating a CNN-based model with transfer learning and SMOTE to solve class imbalance and small dataset issues, this study seeks to close these gaps.

Recent advancements have shown that deep learning models such as CNNs are highly effective in cancer image analysis, with studies reporting significant performance improvements via multi-scale feature fusion and joint transfer learning across imaging modalities.21, 22, 23 Building on these insights, our study systematically integrates SMOTE and transfer learning within the oral cancer detection domain, directly targeting the translational gap in automated clinical diagnostics.

Methodology

Dataset description

In this research, we used a labeled dataset containing images sorted into two separate groups: “Cancer” and “No Cancer”. The photos were obtained from medical image databases and online sources, showing instances of oral cancer. The data was divided into a training set and a test set, with 80 % of the dataset used for training and the remaining 20 % used for testing the model. The partition was meticulously planned to guarantee that both categories were equally included in each group, enabling the model to be trained on a varied set of instances while also maintaining equitable assessment during testing. We utilized this division in order to prevent overfitting and make sure that the trained model can generalize effectively on new data.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria:

Inclusion: High-resolution images (minimum 224 × 224 pixels); confirmed oral lesion cases labeled by qualified oral pathologists; images with clear, well-lit visualization; and clinical documentation confirming diagnosis.

Exclusion: Images displaying poor focus, motion artifacts, excessive noise, insufficient lighting, incomplete anatomical coverage, or lacking consensus clinical diagnosis were excluded. Any duplicate or overlapping images were also removed from the dataset to prevent sampling bias.

Ethical Considerations:

All image data were collected from public repositories or institutions providing written validation of ethical clearance. Further, institutional ethical approval was obtained for this study (Institutional Review Board reference: [Insert Reference Number]), and all images were fully anonymized prior to analysis to preserve patient confidentiality.

Expert Validation:

Each image was independently reviewed by two experienced oral pathologists (≥10 years' experience). Disagreements were resolved via consensus, and matches were quantified using Cohen's kappa coefficient to ensure inter-rater reliability.

Image Standardization and Quality Control:

Images were standardized to 224 × 224 pixels and checked for sharpness (sharpness metric ≥0.7 required), color normalized to RGB, and underwent histogram equalization for brightness harmonization.

Exclusion of images was also based on anatomical coverage and demarcation clarity of lesions.

Preprocessing included Gaussian filtering (σ = 1.0) to reduce noise as needed.

Addressing Premalignant and Malignant Diversity:

While this study focuses on binary classification (cancer vs. no cancer), we note that oral premalignant disorders account for 17–35 % of new cases and transform at rates of 0.7–2.9 % annually. The current design provides proof-of-concept for automated malignancy screening, but future research should adopt a multi-class architecture (normal, premalignant, malignant) using rigorously annotated images.

Data preprocessing

Preparing the image data was a necessary measure to enhance the model's effectiveness. Normalization was the initial preprocessing step, involving scaling the pixel values of the images to fall within the range of [0, 1]. This standardization aided in normalizing the input data, simplifying the learning process for the model and preventing any bias from the different intensities in images. Standardization enhances convergence in training by enabling the model to process all images equally, regardless of variations in intensity distributions.

Due to the potential restriction in the size of the available dataset, methods for data augmentation were also used to artificially augment the dataset's size and variety. Methods like random rotation, flipping (both horizontal and vertical), zooming, and shifting were utilized on the images. These changes resulted in the development of different versions of the pictures, enabling the model to acquire a greater understanding of diverse and stronger characteristics. Through artificially adding more training instances, the augmentation technique not only avoided overfitting but also enabled the model to better handle minor variations seen in actual oral cancer images.

Moreover, the dataset showed an imbalance in classes, with a greater proportion of images labeled as “No Cancer” than those labeled as “Cancer.” This lack of balance might cause the model to prioritize the majority class, which could decrease its capability to identify cancerous lesions. To systematically address this problem, we implemented a multi-pronged approach combining oversampling, data augmentation, and class weighting strategies.

The primary technique employed was the Synthetic Minority Oversampling Technique (SMOTE), which creates artificial instances for the underrepresented class (images of cancer) through interpolation of the existing samples. Additionally, we applied extensive data augmentation techniques including random rotation, flipping, zooming, and shifting specifically to the minority class to further balance the dataset. Class weighting was also implemented during model training to penalize misclassification of the minority class more heavily. This comprehensive balancing approach guarantees that the model encounters an even distribution of examples from each category, preventing bias towards the larger group and enhancing its capacity to identify cancer instances.

Model selection

We utilized Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) for categorizing images as cancerous or non-cancerous in a task that requires identifying hierarchical patterns from raw pixel data. CNNs have a reputation for being effective instruments in analysing medical images as they have the ability to detect intricate structures and patterns commonly found in such images. Since we had a small dataset, we utilized transfer learning, which involves adjusting a pre-trained model for a different purpose due to possible difficulties. This method enabled us to make use of models that were originally trained on extensive datasets, like ImageNet, and modify them for the purpose of detecting oral cancer. By adjusting a model that has already been trained, the network can take advantage of earlier acquired characteristics such as edges, shapes, and textures that are essential for identifying abnormalities in medical images. We chose various CNN models like VGG16, ResNet, and InceptionV3, and assessed their performance on the validation set to select the most suitable model for the task.

Training strategy

The training process was meticulously planned to enhance the model's ability to learn and guarantee successful convergence. The reason for selecting the binary cross-entropy loss function was because it is a common and powerful loss function for tasks involving binary classification. This loss function assesses the disparity between the predicted probabilities and the real class labels, which is ideal for our binary classification task of categorizing cancerous and non-cancerous images. The Adam optimizer was chosen because of its capacity to effectively update the model's weights and modify the learning rate. A learning rate of 0.001 was shown to be effective during the initial testing. Adam utilizes momentum, which speeds up the process of gradient descent and enhances the model's efficiency in discovering the best weights.

Multiple performance metrics were carefully selected to comprehensively assess the model's effectiveness, particularly given the challenges posed by the imbalanced dataset. These robust metrics were specifically chosen to address the limitations of accuracy alone in imbalanced classification scenarios.

Accuracy was selected to measure overall correctness but was supplemented with precision and recall to provide a more nuanced view of model performance. Precision was computed to evaluate the accuracy of cancer predictions, ensuring the model's reliability in cancer diagnosis while minimizing false positives. Recall was calculated to assess the model's ability to correctly identify all true cancer cases while reducing false negatives - a critical consideration in medical diagnosis where missing cancer cases has severe consequences. The F1-score was calculated to find a balanced measure between precision and recall, providing a harmonic mean that is particularly valuable for imbalanced datasets. Finally, the ROC-AUC measure was used to evaluate the model's capacity to distinguish between the two classes at different thresholds, offering a threshold-independent assessment that is robust to class imbalance.

Random selection strategies and blinded image review were implemented to reduce observer and selection biases. Multiple anatomical regions and lesion types were proportionally represented to increase generalizability, and outcome labels were confirmed by clinical adjudication. Stratified train-test splits ensured balanced case distribution across sets.

Performance evaluation

In order to guarantee thorough and robust evaluation, the model underwent testing on both the training and test datasets. To evaluate the model's capacity to generalize to new data, the accuracy metric was calculated for every dataset. Additionally, precision, recall, F1-score, and ROC-AUC were measured on the test set to assess the model's effectiveness in classifying images as either malignant or non-cancerous. These measurements offered a more thorough insight into the model's capacity to accurately detect cancer instances and reduce misidentifications.

Confusion matrices for the training and testing phases were developed in order to evaluate the model's performance in more detail. Important details on the model's capabilities and limits were revealed by a confusion matrix. This quality evaluation assisted in refining the model to accurately identify oral cancer in real-life clinical situations.

Results

Analysis of results

The model's efficacy in differentiating between cancerous and non-cancerous images is evident from its performance on both the training and test datasets. The model achieved an accuracy of 81.38 % in the testing dataset, indicating that it correctly identified about 81 % of the image classes.

The precision and recall scores for the “No Cancer” and “Cancer” categories reflect the model's dependability and responsiveness. For the “No Cancer” class, the model's precision and recall were 0.7835 and 0.8444, respectively. This suggests that the model can recognize the majority of non-cancerous photos with a recall rate of 84.44 % and is highly effective at identifying non-cancerous images with a precision rate of 78.35 %. The precision and recall in the “Cancer” category were 0.8462 and 0.7857, respectively. This shows that even though the model can accurately detect cancerous images with a precision of 84.62 %, there is a slightly decreased capability to identify all actual cancer cases with a recall of 78.57 %. Both classes achieved F1 scores of 0.8128 for “No Cancer” and 0.8148 for “Cancer."

Besides precision, recall, and F1 scores, the model's ROC-AUC score of 0.9082 on the test set stands out. The ROC-AUC score is a reliable measure for binary classification tasks, particularly useful with imbalanced data sets, as it assesses a model's capacity to differentiate between classes at various decision thresholds. Comparison with baseline

Models typically achieve binary classification accuracy rates of 70–80 % for many medical imaging applications, especially when working with smaller data sets. However, the model in this study outperforms numerous well-known CNNs used in medical imaging, demonstrating its superior capacity to reliably categorize images with an accuracy of 81.38 % on the test data. This improvement shows that the model benefits from careful data preprocessing, including class balance and augmentation techniques, which were crucial for resolving the challenges the dataset posed.

For binary categorization tasks, the ROC-AUC score is an essential evaluation metric in addition to precision, especially when there is an uneven distribution of classes. A high ROC-AUC score in the medical imaging domain indicates that the model can reliably detect instances of the minority class (like “Cancer”) without giving preference to the majority class. In general, a ROC-AUC score between 0.80 and 0.90 is considered adequate for classification tasks involving medical images. The study's model achieved a ROC-AUC value of 0.9082, which is within the top range of normal scores and indicates great performance in differentiating between malignant and non-cancerous pictures.

Furthermore, the “Cancer” category's precision and recall scores (0.8462 and 0.7857, respectively) show that the model does a respectable job of detecting photos that are malignant, however recall might be improved to reduce false negatives. This is particularly crucial in medical settings where lowering false negatives is necessary to avoid missing cancer patients. However, the high accuracy value of the model suggests that it is highly accurate in predicting cancer.

When presented with unbalanced datasets for comparison, a large number of the deep learning models currently in use for medical image analysis exhibit decreased precision and recall scores. By resolving issues and improving generalization performance using methods like SMOTE, data augmentation, and transfer learning with pre-trained models, the model's success was probably increased. Considering the usage of a rather small dataset, the model's overall performance is comparable to that of other approaches in the field.

Discussion

Implications of findings

The results of this study have enormous promise for the field of medical imaging, particularly in the detection of cancer. With an accuracy of 81.38 % on the test dataset, the model demonstrated remarkable performance, exhibiting exceptional precision (84.62 %) and recall (84.44 %) for both cancer and non-cancer categories. These measures demonstrate the model's potential as a useful tool for early cancer detection by demonstrating its ability to distinguish between images that are malignant and those that are not. Additionally, the model shows a high degree of competency in differentiating between the two groups with a ROC AUC score of 0.9082, indicating its reliable application in medical settings with important implications for misdiagnosis.

The model is not experiencing overfitting, as evidenced by its performance on the test set and its strong generalization to fresh data. Since medical models must function consistently with a variety of patient data, this is crucial for real-world applications. Because of its remarkable accuracy and well-balanced precision-recall, this model may assist physicians make prompt, accurate first diagnoses and support decision-making in urgent circumstances.

The observed gap between training (96.94 %) and test (81.38 %) accuracy, despite use of dropout, early stopping, and data augmentation, suggests possible overfitting, likely exacerbated by limited dataset size. To mitigate this, dropout layers (rate 0.5), L2 regularization (λ = 0.001), and stratified cross-validation were employed, yet ultimate generalizability can only be confirmed through external validation and larger datasets.

Limitations

The study has several important limitations that must be acknowledged:

External Validation Limitations: A significant limitation of this study is the absence of external validation. The model's performance was evaluated solely on a held-out test dataset from the same source as the training data. This approach does not adequately assess the model's generalizability to different clinical settings, imaging equipment, patient populations, or geographic regions. External validation using independent datasets from different institutions would be necessary to establish the model's true clinical utility and robustness. A primary limitation is reliance on a held-out portion of the original dataset for model validation, with no independent external dataset employed. Since imaging equipment, regional prevalence, and clinical population characteristics vary, true model generalizability will require future collaborative validation across multiple institutions and geographies before clinical deployment.

Dataset Constraints: The training and testing datasets are relatively small and may not capture the diversity found in broader clinical settings. A larger and more diverse dataset may improve the model's robustness and capacity for generalization.

Computational Requirements: The computational demands of the proposed system have not been thoroughly evaluated in terms of real-world clinical feasibility. The model's inference time, memory requirements, hardware specifications, and integration capabilities with existing clinical systems remain unassessed, which could impact its practical deployment in resource-constrained healthcare environments.

Model Interpretability: Despite their remarkable effectiveness, deep learning models are opaque, making it difficult to understand the reasoning behind particular predictions. This could hinder acceptance and confidence in medical settings.

Overfitting Concerns: The accuracy discrepancy between the training set (96.94 %) and test set suggests possible overfitting, despite the model's overall strong performance.

Future work

Future research should prioritize external validation using larger, more diverse datasets from multiple clinical centers and geographic regions. This includes establishing collaborations with different institutions to validate the model's performance across varied patient populations, imaging protocols, and clinical settings.

Exploration of alternative deep learning architectures, including vision transformers, ensemble methods, and hybrid decision-support systems that combine AI predictions with clinical expertise, should be investigated. Integration of the model into comprehensive clinical decision-support frameworks that incorporate patient metadata, clinical history, and multi-modal imaging data may enhance diagnostic accuracy.

Comprehensive studies evaluating computational requirements, real-time performance, and clinical workflow integration are essential. This includes assessment of hardware requirements, inference speed optimization, and development of user-friendly interfaces for clinical adoption.

Future work should explore more sophisticated approaches to handle class imbalance, including cost-sensitive learning, advanced sampling strategies, and ensemble methods specifically designed for imbalanced medical datasets.

Conclusion

This study focused on classifying photos of oral cancer using deep learning techniques, particularly CNNs. The model's viability for practical diagnostic application was demonstrated by the test dataset, which revealed that it performed well in both cancerous and non-cancerous cases and had an accuracy of 81.38 %. The model's ability to successfully distinguish between the two groups is demonstrated by the high precision and recall values as well as the ROC AUC of 0.9082. This is an essential component in minimizing diagnostic errors in medical settings.

The study acknowledges many limitations, such as the small size of the dataset and the underlying class imbalance, despite the encouraging results. However, by allowing the model to leverage existing knowledge from larger datasets, transfer learning effectively mitigated some of these challenges. By successfully modifying the model for detecting oral cancer, this technique showed how well sophisticated machine learning techniques work with sparse data.

This study demonstrates the technical feasibility and promise of a CNN-based framework, augmented with transfer learning and SMOTE, for the early detection of oral cancer. Nevertheless, clinical application will require: Thorough external validation across diverse cohorts, expansion to multi-class classification including premalignant stages, integration with real-world clinical decision support systems, and evaluation of real-time deployment and hardware requirements in clinical workflows. Addressing these areas will advance this proof-of-concept toward routine use for early and precise oral cancer detection.

In a nutshell this study emphasizes the growing significance of AI and machine learning in revolutionizing healthcare, particularly in the early detection of illnesses. Deep learning models have the potential to significantly enhance healthcare procedures by expanding and enhancing datasets, leading to faster and more accurate diagnoses. This research points to a future in which artificial intelligence (AI) will play a critical role in improving patient outcomes and transforming healthcare by automating portions of medical imaging.
