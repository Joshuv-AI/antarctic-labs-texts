Effects of surface roughness and Reynolds number on the solute transport through three-dimensional rough-walled rock fractures under different flow regimes
Authors: Na Huang, Shengqun Han, Xuepeng Zhang, Gang Wang, Yujing Jiang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn this study, the effects of surface roughness and Reynolds number (Re) on fluid flow and solute transport are investigated based on a double rough-walled fracture model that precisely represents the natural geometries of rock fractures. The double rough-walled fracture model is composed of two three-dimensional(3D) self-affine fracture surfaces generated using the improved successive random additions (SRA). Simulation of fluid flow and solute transport through the models were conducted by directly solving the Navier-Stokes equation and advection-diffusion equation (ADE), respectively. The results indicate that as the Re increases from 0.1 to 200, the flow regime changes from linear flow to nonlinear flow accompanied with the tortuous streamlines and significant eddies. Those eddies lead to the temporary stagnant zones that delay the solute migration. The increment of Re enhances the transport heterogeneity with the transport mode changing from the diffusion-dominated to the advection-dominated behavior, which is more significant in the fracture with a larger joint roughness coefficient (JRC). All breakthrough curves (BTCs) of rough-walled fractures exhibited typical non-Fickian transport characteristics with “early arrival” and “long tailing” of BTCs. Increasing the JRC and/or Re will enhances the non-Fickian transport characteristics. The ADE model is able to accurately fit the numerical BTCs and residence time distributions (RTDs) at a low Re, but fails to capture the non-Fickian transport characteristics at a large Re. In contrast, the continuous time random walk (CTRW) model provides a better fit to the numerical simulation results over the whole range of Re. Whereas, the fitting error gradually increases with increasing Re.Keywords: Fracture, Surface roughness, Reynolds number, Fluid flow, Solute transportSubject terms: Hydrogeology, Hydrology

Introduction

Geologic discontinuities such as faults, joints and fractures are ubiquitous in the Earth’s crust, dominating the geometrical and hydraulic properties of subsurface rocks. Investigating the fluid flow and solute transport behavior in fractured rock masses is essential for various fields of rock engineering such as petroleum reservoirs, geothermal energy development, nuclear waste disposal, and geological storage of carbon dioxide1–4. However, due to the geometrical heterogeneity of fracture networks, accurate assessment of the flow and transport properties of fractured rock masses becomes notably challenging, and substantial efforts have been devoted to this endeavor5–11.

In the complex fracture networks, the single rock fracture is the basic geometric and hydraulic component. In the early studies, the single fracture was assumed as smooth parallel-plates (PP), based on which the cubic law was proposed to describe the fluid flow12–14. Although the smooth PP does not accurately describe the roughness of real rock fractures, it can still be used as a theoretical basis for modeling the fluid flow. Later, a plane model assigned with two-dimensional(2D) aperture distribution was proposed to account for the influence of aperture anisotropy on fluid flow15–19. This kind of model went on step further to involve the aperture distribution, whereas, the surface roughness is still not considered explicitly. In order to characterize the geometry of rock fractures more realistically, a double-rough-walled model is proposed20,21, in which the fracture surface is generated based on a real rock fracture. This model directly accounts for the void structure of the rough-walled fracture, and has outstanding advantages in helping simulate the flow and transport behavior in fracture more accurately.

The PP model assumes a linear relationship between the flow rate and pressure gradient22, providing a simple and effective method to describe the fluid flow. This simple model also gives an additional advantage that the solute transport follows the Fickian transport law with well-behaved breakthrough curves (BTCs)23. However, many field observations and laboratory tests have confirmed that a more complex nonlinear relationship between the flow rate and pressure gradient exists in the natural rock fractures, which is affected by the surface roughness24,25, aperture26,27, the Reynolds number (Re)28,29, and shear displacement30,31, etc. and can be effectively characterized using the Forchheimer and Izbash Eqs32–35. The nonlinear flow regime as well as the geometric heterogeneity of fracture result in anomalous transport behavior featured by the “early arrival” and “long tailing” of BTCs36–38. Dou et al.39 thoroughly investigated the effects of 2D fracture roughness on fluid flow and solute transport, especially the role of small-scale secondary roughness in non-Fickian transport. Chen et al.40 designed single fractures with different roughness and conducted a series of tracer test experiments, and it was found that rough fractures have a significant effect on the non-Fickian transport, and the nonlinearity of the flow will further enhance the phenomenon of non-Fickian transport. Wang et al.41 analyzed the effects of different contact areas on fluid flow and solute transport at Re ranging from 0.0001 to 1 by solving the Navier-Stokes equation and advection-diffusion equation (ADE). The results show that above moderate Re (Re > 0.1), the BTCs of the fractures clearly exhibit anomalous transport behaviors. Zhang et al.42 used numerical simulations to investigate the effect of shear displacement on solute transport in rough fractures. They proposed that with the increase of shear displacement, larger apertures and major flow channels are formed within the fracture, which accelerates solute transport in the direction of fluid flow and leads to early breakthrough of solutes. Wang et al.43 investigated the critical role of recirculation zones in controlling both fluid flow and solute transport through numerical simulation experiments within three-dimensional(3D) real fractures. However, the effects of flow transitions from linear to nonlinear regimes under a large range of Re on the solute transport have not been thoroughly understood, which need be further studied in terms of high-precision fracture model.

The primary objective of this study is to accurately characterize the nonlinear flow behavior and its effect on solute transport in rock fractures induced by the surface roughness considering a large range of Re. To achieve this goal, a double-rough-walled model consisting of two self-affine surfaces was used to simulate the real geometry of rock fractures. The fluid flow and solute transport processes through the model were simulated using the numerical simulation software COMSOL Multiphysics by directly solving the Navier-Stokes equation and ADE. The effects of surface roughness and Re on the flow regime, concentration field and non-Fickian transport properties were comprehensively evaluated. Two inverse models, namely the classical ADE model and the Continuous Time Random Walk (CTRW) model were examined to fit the non-Fickian transport characteristics for rough-walled fractures under different Re.

As shown in Fig. 2, the upper and lower surfaces of fracture were assumed to be well-mated at the initial state. Then the upper surface was shifted tangentially along the fracture plane direction to simulate the shear process. During shearing, the two fracture walls may touch each other due to the surface roughness. Thus to avoid the contact, the upper surface was further lifted along the direction perpendicular to the fracture plane. In this study, a constant shear displacement of 10 mm was assigned to each model, and the upper surface wall was lifted to a certain height to keep the average aperture of the fracture as 2.5 mm. To ensure a constant analyzed area, an aperture field with a cross-sectional area of 200 × 100 mm was extracted from the center of each original model after shearing. It should be noted that all models established in this study are open fractures with no contact between the upper and lower surfaces, as a result, the contact effect on the solute transport is not considered. This is mainly due to the difficulty of discretization arising from the narrow void spaces around the contacts. In further studies, we will consistently investigate the contact effect on the solute migration when more experimental and/or numerical data are available. Another limitation of this study is that all numerical simulations were conducted on the model with the size of 200 × 100 mm and thus the size effects on the solute transport are not considered. The fluid flow and solute transport properties of fractures are scale dependent and the solute transport behavior at field scales cannot be accurately estimated based on the sample of a relative small model size. Therefore, it is necessary to conduct a detailed study of the size effect on the solute transport properties in the further studies.

Boundary conditions

In this study, the COMSOL Multiphysics package is utilized to numerically solve the Navier-Stokes equation and ADE for simulating fluid flow and solute transport. As shown in Fig. 4(a), the fracture model is discretized into tetrahedral elements, which is encrypted along the aperture direction. The chosen boundary conditions are consistent with the experimental conditions in many laboratorial studies as shown in Fig. 4(b). For fluid flow, a series of flow rates with Re increasing from 0.1 to 200 are imposed at the inflow boundary, and the outflow boundary is set as zero pressure (P = 0). The other fracture walls (i.e. top, bottom, and side walls) in the model are set as non-slip boundary conditions. For solute transport, a Dirichlet condition is applied at the inlet with a constant concentration value of 1.0, representing a continuous injection. The exit boundary condition is defined as an open boundary indicating that water and solute outflow primarily occurs through advection processes. Initially, the entire region within the fracture has a concentration of 0; once steady-state flow is established after introducing fluid into the model, solute injection begins at the inlet. The fracture wall assumes a flux-free boundary condition without considering adsorption, exchange or reaction processes occurring at its surface.

Results

Flow pattern under different flow regimes

Fluid flow through fractures with different σ/b was simulated at the increasing Re of 0.1 ~ 200. Without loss of generality, Fig. 5 shows the distribution of velocity field in fractures of PP, σ/b = 0.117 and σ/b = 0.215 at Re = 0.1 and 200 respectively to interpret the impact of surface roughness and Re on the flow field. For the PP model, the flow pattern is homogeneous and the flow velocity remains constant in the cross section. As the fracture surface becomes rough, the flow pattern is non-uniform and tortuous with a wide variety of flow velocity, in which the faster flow velocity is channelized within the void spaces of larger aperture. This phenomenon of flow channelization is induced by the roughness of fracture surface walls, which become more obvious as σ/b increases by comparing the results of PP, σ/b = 0.117 and σ/b = 0.215 in Fig. 5 regardless of Re. As Re increase, the spectrum of flow velocity is expended nonlinearly. More fast flow channels are concentrated in the straight pathways while the flow velocity in the tortuous ways increase less obviously. This phenomenon is more remarkable in the fracture of σ/b = 0.215 by comparing Fig. 5(c) and (f).

Concentration fields under different flow regimes

Figures 7 and 8 display the evolution of concentration distribution with σ/b and PV under Re = 0.1 and 200, respectively, in which both the surface concentration and internal concentration distribution profiles are plotted to show the concentration field more clearly. When Re = 0.1, the solute is approximately uniformly transport along the cross-sectional direction for the PP model, and the transport is dominated by diffusion. As σ/b increases, the solute distribution becomes increasingly heterogeneous during the transmission process, in which the fast transport pathways are consistent with the fast flow channels in Fig. 5. Comparison between Figs. 7 and 8 indicates that the transport heterogeneity increases with increasing Re, even for the PP model shown in Fig. 8(a) and (k). This is because as Re increases, the dominated transport mode changes from diffusion to advection, and the concentration distribution is increasingly affected by the flow structure. As expected, the heterogeneity of solute distribution is more remarkable in the rougher fractures at a larger Re. It should be noted that the non-slip flow boundary condition is used on the fracture surface in this study, which leads to a parabolic flow distribution with a faster transmission speed inside the rock fracture than that on the fracture surface.

Effect of flow eddies on solute transport

Figure 9 shows the segments of flow streamlines on the cross-section of fracture σ/b = 0.152 and σ/b = 0.215 with Re = 1, 100 and 200. It can be clearly observed that as Re increases, eddy gradually forms and the eddy flow region gradually increases, mainly in the area where the aperture suddenly expands. This phenomenon is more obvious for the fracture with a larger σ/b. For example, when Re = 100, there is no eddy in the cross-section of fracture σ/b = 0.152 as shown in Fig. 9(b), while obvious eddy flow occurs in the cross-section of fracture σ/b = 0.215 as shown in Fig. 9(e). As Re increases to 200, both fracture cross-sections show obvious eddy, in which the eddy flow region is significantly larger in fracture σ/b = 0.215 than that in σ/b = 0.152.

The spatial evolution of the solute distribution on the cross-section of fracture σ/b = 0.215 with Re = 200 at different PV is presented in Fig. 10 to illustrate the influence of fracture aperture and eddy on the solute transport. For all cases at different PV, the spatial distribution of solute is highly correlated with the aperture distribution, in which the solute transport velocity near the fracture wall is significantly slower than that in the center, clearly indicating an effective advection channel within the rock fracture. This is mainly due to the non-slip boundary conditions used in this study, which results in lower flow velocities near the fracture wall than that in the central region of the fracture. Hence, during the process of solute transport, a preferential flow pathway is established, facilitating the rapid movement of solute from the inlet to the outlet along this route, recognized as the phenomenon of “early arrival” of the BTCs. Furthermore, the existence of eddies results in a temporary stagnant area, which will hinder the spreading and mixing of solute. The transport process within the eddy flow region is therefore predominantly governed by the molecular diffusion, resulting in an apparent delay in solute migration.

Effects of surface roughness and Reynolds number on BTCs

The flux-weighted BTCs are calculated based on the numerical simulations and the results are displayed in Fig. 12. The typical non-Fickian transport featured by the ‘’early arrival’’ and ‘’long tailing’’ are observed in all BTCs. For the classical Fickian transport, the concentration should equal to 0.5 when PV = 1, and as PV increases, it is expected to rapidly reach 1.0. While the BTCs estimated in this study has a concentration larger than 0.5 when PV = 1, indicating the plume “early arrival”. As PV increases, the observed BTCs do not rapidly increase to 1.0. Instead, it showed a slow and gradual rise, indicating the presence of a “long tailing”. Its “early arrival” behavior is mainly due to fast migration of solute along the flow channelings induced by the surface roughness, which is more obvious for fractures with a larger Re. Due to the influence of boundary layer and the presence of eddy within the rock fractures, the flow velocity near the surface of rock fractures is significantly lower than that in the middle. As a result, the mass exchange is hindered within the low flow regions, characterized by the “long tailing” of BTCs.

To further illustrate the non-Fickian transport behavior, the residence time distributions (RTDs) are calculated as shown in Fig. 13. Inverse ADE and CTRW models

The ADE and CTRW TPL models are utilized to fit the BTCs and RTDs under different simulation conditions. Figure 16 shows some examples of the fitting results for fractures with σ/b = 0.117 and σ/b = 0.215 at Re = 0.1 and 200. It can be easily seen that when the Re is small, both the ADE and CTRW TPL models can accurately fit the data results. As the Re increases, the ADE model fails to capture the “early arrival” and “long tailing” of BTCs. Whereas, the CTRW TPL model is able to well fit the whole curve over the Re range studied here. Figure 17 summarized the fitting parameters of ADE model and CTRW TPL model for the fractures with different surface roughness and Re. The fitting U and D for both ADE and CTRW TPL models exhibit an increasing trend as the Re increases, indicating that advection and diffusion are significantly enhanced with increasing Re. In the inversion analysis of CTRW TPL model, the fitting parameter β is smaller than 2.0 and decreases as Re increases, which indicates an increasingly obvious non-Fickian transport behavior. However, the parameters t1 and t2 demonstrate relatively low sensitivity and exhibit no discernible intrinsic relationship with rock fracture geometric characteristics and Re, in which t1 is significantly smaller than t2. Overall, compared to the ADE model, the CTRW TPL model is more capable to describe the non-Fickian transport characteristics of BTCs, i.e. “early arrival” and “long tailing”. It should be noted that although the CTRW TPL model can well fit the BTCs over the whole Re range studied here, the fitting error gradually increases with increasing Re.

Conclusions

In this study, the double-rough-walled model that can accurately characterize the heterogeneity of fracture surface was adopted to investigate the fluid flow and solute transport processes in the rock fracture. The fluid flow and solute transport processes were simulated under a large range of Re by solving the Navier-Stokes equation and ADE based on the COMSOL Multiphysics software. The effects of surface roughness and Re on the flow regime, concentration distribution and non-Fickian transport characteristics were investigated. The classical ADE model and CTRW TPL model were examined to fit the non-Fickian transport characteristics for rough-walled fractures under different Re. The following conclusions can be obtained:

The flow field becomes increasingly tortuous with increasing surface roughness, in which the faster flow channels are mainly concentrated in void spaces with larger apertures. Meanwhile, the solute distribution becomes increasingly heterogeneous with the fast transport pathways consistent with the fast flow channels. As the Re increases, the flow eddies gradually from and expend within the region of the large aperture. Those flow eddies lead to the temporary stagnant zones that delay the solute migration. The transport heterogeneity is enhanced with increasing the Re, and the transport mode changes from diffusion-dominated to advection-dominated behavior.

Based on the localized analysis of solute transport on cross-section of fractures, a region with a negative velocity vector is observed, which is due to the formation of eddies along the continuous downward and upward trend of the fracture wall. The negative flow area increases with increasing Re, which will affect the concentration profile deviating from the parabolic-like distribution. Moreover, advection is directly proportional to the flow velocity distribution and dominates the solute transport compared to the diffusion. The diffusion is more pronounced close to the fracture wall, and tends to be slightly enhanced by the variation of the rough surface geometry.

Two inverse models, i.e., ADE and CTRW TPL models were tested to fit the BTCs. The results show that both models demonstrate satisfactory performance at a low Re. As Re increases, the ADE model becomes inappropriate to capture the eddy-induced non-Fickian transport characteristics. However, the CTRW TPL model offered a better fit for capturing the early arrival and long trailing of BTCs at a large Re. The fitting parameter β of the CTRW TPL model decreases as the eddies develop, which indicates an increasingly obvious non-Fickian transport behavior induced by the developing eddies.
