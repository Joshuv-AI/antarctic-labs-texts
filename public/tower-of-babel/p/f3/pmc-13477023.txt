Two atypical resistance proteins confer plant resistance to Melanaphis sorghi in sorghum
Authors: Kang Lei, Dong Tian, Yutao Shao, Faming Wang, Jinhua Chang, Xiaohuan Jin, Si Nian Char, Zhenying Dong, Jianping Zhang, Jiang-Hui Cui, Songmin Zhao, Jingjing Li, Hua Liu, Guo-Qing Liu, Peng Lv, Mingshu Wei, Qisheng Song, Bing Yang, Zheng Qing Fu, Kunpu Zhang, Guangwei Li, Daowen Wang, Di Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAphids are among the most common insect pests that reduce crop production worldwide. However, the breeding of aphid-resistant crops is currently hindered by the scarcity of resistance genes. This problem is particularly significant in sorghum, for which resistant cultivars are urgently needed to control the aphid Melanaphis sorghi (MES). Here, we report map-based cloning of RMES1A and RMES1B, which encode two atypical resistance proteins that confer strong defense against MES. Analysis of knockout mutants and natural variations demonstrated that RMES1A and RMES1B are both required for sorghum resistance against MES. Both genes are induced by MES feeding, specifically in sclerenchyma cells and vascular bundles. RMES1A/RMES1B interact with the sorghum aphid protein MsEF1 to form a functional complex in sorghum cells, leading to key defense responses such as the H2O2 burst and enhanced callose deposition. Accordingly, silencing of MsEF1 expression by RNA interference disrupted RMES1A/RMES1B-mediated resistance, as evidenced by significantly greater growth and fecundity of MES aphids on a resistant cultivar. Structural modeling predicted that RMES1A/RMES1B possess a potential nucleotide-binding domain and two leucine-rich repeat domains but lack the coiled-coil or Toll/interleukin-1 receptor/resistance domains observed in typical nucleotide-binding leucine-rich repeat immune receptors (NLRs). Moreover, RMES1A/RMES1B and their homologs form a distinct cluster in a phylogenetic tree of plant NLRs and likely represent a new type of plant NLR. Our work thus reveals new resistance genes that can be used to investigate and improve immunity against insect pests in sorghum and other crops.Key words: aphid resistance genes, atypical resistance proteins, Melanaphis sorghi, plant immunity, ROS burst, sorghum

Introduction

Rapid and significant progress is being made in identifying plant resistance proteins that can be used to develop crop cultivars resistant to phytopathogens and insect pests. At present, two types of immune responses, pattern-triggered immunity and effector-triggered immunity (ETI), have been documented in plant–pathogen and plant–insect interactions (Snoeck et al., 2022a; Boissot, 2023; Contreras et al., 2023; Jones et al., 2024). Pattern-triggered immunity is activated upon perception of pathogen- or herbivore-associated molecular patterns by pattern recognition receptors on the cell surface, whereas ETI is initiated upon perception of pathogen or herbivore effectors by intracellular nucleotide-binding leucine-rich repeat immune receptors (NLRs). Recent studies have divided NLRs into six main clades based on their N-terminal domains: coiled-coil (CC) NLRs (CC-NLRs), RPW8-like CC domain NLRs (CCR-NLRs), Toll/interleukin-1 receptor/resistance (TIR) protein NLRs (TIR-NLRs), protein kinase domain NLRs, α/β-hydrolase domain NLRs, and DUF676-NLRs (Lee et al., 2021; Kourelis and Adachi, 2022; Contreras et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2023; Jones et al., 2024; Dong et al., 2025). In addition to the N-terminal domain, NLRs typically have a central nucleotide-binding domain (NBD) and a C-terminal LRR domain (Contreras et al., 2023; Jones et al., 2024). The CC and TIR domains are mainly involved in triggering downstream signaling pathways and promoting resistosome formation; the NBD acts as a molecular switch to regulate the activation state, and the LRR domain primarily determines effector binding specificity; some NLRs also contain additional integrated domains (IDs) that act as effector sensors (Contreras et al., 2023; Jones et al., 2024). NLRs can confer resistance as singletons, pairs, or networks after binding effectors directly or indirectly (Kourelis and Adachi, 2022; Contreras et al., 2023; Jones et al., 2024). In addition, there are increasing numbers of reports on the disease-resistance roles of atypical NLRs with disruptions in the CC or NBD domain (Lee et al., 2021; Kourelis and Adachi, 2022; Adachi et al., 2023; Sunil et al., 2024; Guo et al., 2025; Hu et al., 2025; Klymiuk et al., 2025). For example, an atypical NLR (TaNLR2/NL) lacking the CC domain and a canonical CC-NLR (TaNLR1/CNL) are both required for Yr84-mediated immunity against wheat stripe rust disease (Hu et al., 2025; Klymiuk et al., 2025). However, the roles of atypical NLRs in plant defense against insect pests remain to be characterized (Snoeck et al., 2022a; Boissot, 2023).

Among the major-effect insect-resistance proteins characterized to date, only a few have been shown to initiate host-plant resistance after recognition of their cognate effectors (Rossi et al., 1998; Klingler et al., 2009; Dogimont et al., 2014; Guo et al., 2018; 2020; Nicolis and Venter, 2018; Du et al., 2020; Steinbrenner et al., 2020; Kloth et al., 2021; Shi et al., 2021; Snoeck et al., 2022a; 2022b; Boissot, 2023). An leucine-rich repeat receptor-like protein (LRR-RLP) conserved in Phaseoloid plants has been demonstrated to initiate immune signaling and defense responses to caterpillar herbivory after recognition of the effector protein inceptin (Steinbrenner et al., 2020). An EDR1-like protein kinase has been shown to promote tobacco defense against Myzus persicae by interacting with the aphid effector CathB3 (Guo et al., 2020). The Mp10 effector of M. persicae is recognized by the TNL protein RCSP, thereby activating EDS1-mediated immune responses (Bos et al., 2010; Gravino et al., 2025; Rao et al., 2025). The rice brown planthopper (BPH) resistance protein BPH14, a canonical CC-NLR, has been reported to confer ETI to BPH after binding the insect effector protein BISP (Guo et al., 2023). Nonetheless, the mechanisms by which insect-resistance proteins activate downstream responses remain poorly understood.

Sorghum is a highly valuable food, feed, and energy crop, but its global production is seriously threatened by the aphid Melanaphis sorghi (MES hereafter) (Nibouche et al., 2018; 2021; Muleta et al., 2022). MES damages sorghum by sucking nutrient-rich phloem sap and covering infested plants with honeydew, causing nutrient deficiency, leaf chlorosis, and reduced photosynthesis (Singh et al., 2004; Bowling et al., 2016). Heavy infestations of MES typically lead to leaf necrosis, stunted growth, wilting, and even plant death, which can result in sorghum yield losses of 75%–100% (Singh et al., 2004; Colares et al., 2015; Craigie et al., 2024). Consequently, there is increasing interest in identifying genes that function in sorghum resistance to MES for use in the breeding of resistant cultivars (Tetreault et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2021; Muleta et al., 2022; Poosapati et al., 2022; Punnuri et al., 2022; VanGessel et al., 2024; 2025). However, no specific pattern-recognition receptors or NLRs involved in sorghum MES resistance have been characterized through in-depth genetic and molecular research. We previously mapped the RMES1 locus, which confers strong resistance against MES, to a 126-kb genomic region on the short arm of chromosome 6 using genetic populations developed from Henong 16 (HN16; an elite MES-resistant sorghum variety) and BTx623 (susceptible to MES and with an available genome sequence) as parents (Chang et al., 2012; Wang et al., 2013). We proposed two candidate genes (RMES1A and RMES1B) for the RMES1 locus in a subsequent study (Lei et al., 2024). Recently, VanGessel et al. (2025) also suggested the involvement of RMES1A and RMES1B in RMES1-mediated resistance against MES on the basis of transcriptomic and pangenomic analyses. Here, we used complementary genetic and molecular analyses to demonstrate that RMES1A and RMES1B in the RMES1 locus are both required for sorghum resistance to MES. We also identified an MES aphid protein (MsEF1) that binds to RMES1A/RMES1B and strengthens their interaction in the exocyst complex, thereby upregulating defense responses to MES feeding (i.e., the H2O2 burst and callose deposition). Notably, RMES1A/RMES1B differ from canonical NLRs by lacking a CC or TIR domain. Our work thus identifies new resistance genes that can be used to investigate and improve plant defense against insect pests.

Results

Map-based cloning and functional validation of the RMES1 locus

Building on our previous research (Wang et al., 2013), we further mapped RMES1 to a 25-kb region using newly developed DNA markers and 5136 BC5F2 plants segregating for RMES1 (Figure 1A). Surprisingly, although the 25-kb region was flanked by two genes annotated as encoding LRR-containing proteins (LRRP2 and LRRP3), it did not contain any protein-coding genes in the BTx623 genome assembly (Figure 1A), suggesting the presence of chromosomal structural variations among different sorghum genotypes. We therefore constructed a bacterial artificial chromosome (BAC) library for the MES-resistant cultivar HN16 and found that the marker-tagged 25-kb region in BTx623 corresponded to a 169-kb chromosomal fragment in HN16. This fragment harbored two additional LRR genes (i.e., RMES1A and RMES1B) not present in BTx623, as determined by detailed gene annotation and functional prediction of the 169-kb sequence (Figure 1A; GenBank: PP693302). RMES1A and RMES1B were therefore considered candidate genes for RMES1. The deduced RMES1A and RMES1B protein sequences, which were 96.2% identical (Supplemental Figure 1), each contained 1032 amino acids and had a predicted molecular mass of approximately 118 kDa.

We next developed four MES-susceptible mutants (aphid-susceptible mutant 1 [asm1], asm2, asm3, and asm4) using HN16: asm1 and asm2 were prepared by mutagenesis with diepoxybutane or ethyl methanesulfonate, and asm3 and asm4 were identified from two separate fast neutron mutant families. Both asm1 and asm2 carried nucleotide deletions in the RMES1A coding sequence, and the deduced asm1 and asm2 proteins lacked 59 residues or were prematurely truncated (Supplemental Figure 2A). Genome resequencing and genomic PCR analyses showed that asm3 and asm4 lacked the entire RMES1B gene, with RMES1A remaining intact (Supplemental Figures 2B and 2C). When grown in the field, HN16 showed strong resistance to MES, with no growth of adults or nymphs on the leaves, whereas the four mutants were all highly susceptible, supporting numerous aphid colonies (Figure 1B). Although HN16 was highly resistant, no obvious cell death was observed on its leaves (Figure 1B). A double mutant (asm1asm4) lacking RMES1A and RMES1B was prepared by crossing asm1 and asm4. In addition, two near-isogenic lines with or without the RMES1 locus, designated NILR+ and NILR−, were selected from a BC7F2 family developed with BTx623 as the recurrent parent. As anticipated, the double mutant, NILR−, and BTx623, but not HN16, were susceptible to MES and died within 14 days post inoculation (dpi) (Figure 1C). In repeated MES feeding assays with all lines examined in parallel, BTx623, NILR−, and the five mutants, but not HN16 or NILR+, died by 14 dpi (Figure 1D). The number of inoculated aphids increased by more than 2.5-fold on BTx623, NILR−, and the five mutants but increased only 1.1-fold on HN16 and NILR+ by 96 h post inoculation (hpi) (Figure 1E). Accordingly, aphid honeydew was produced in significantly larger amounts in the seven susceptible genotypes than in HN16 and NILR+ at 48 hpi (Figure 1F). We also generated a CRISPR-induced knockout (KO) mutant using the sorghum accession P89002 (PI 561847), which carried both RMES1A and RMES1B and exhibited resistance to MES infestation (Supplemental Figure 3). This mutant (rmes1a1b-#25), which harbored KO mutations in both RMES1A and RMES1B, was highly susceptible to MES feeding (Supplemental Figures 3A–3D), consistent with the phenotypes of the single and double asm mutants (Figure 1).

Natural variations in RMES1A and RMES1B were analyzed using 166 sorghum accessions collected from different countries (Supplemental Data 1 and 2). We designated the RMES1A and RMES1B sequences isolated from HN16 as the wild-type genes; the natural null alleles of RMES1A and RMES1B as rmes1a-1 and rmes1b-1; and variants with amino acid substitutions in RMES1A and RMES1B proteins as rmes1a-2 and rmes1b-2. On the basis of RMES1A and RMES1B status and the results of MES feeding tests, the 166 lines were divided into three types (Supplemental Data 1). Type I lines (n = 36) carried both RMES1A and RMES1B, and all showed resistance to MES, whereas type II lines (n = 115) carried rmes1a-1 and rmes1b-1 and exhibited MES susceptibility. Type III lines (n = 15) carried wild-type RMES1B but had the null allele rmes1a-1; all were susceptible to MES, despite the presence of RMES1B (Supplemental Data 1 and 2). Clearly, 78.31% (130/166) of the analyzed lines did not contain a functional RMES1 locus with both RMES1A and RMES1B. Consistent with this finding, none of the 11 sorghum lines with high-quality genome assemblies reported in a previous pangenome analysis (Tao et al., 2021) carried the RMES1 locus when compared with HN16, and two of these 11 lines were confirmed to be highly susceptible to MES attack (Supplemental Figure 4). Taken together, these genetic data strongly suggest that RMES1A and RMES1B are both required for sorghum resistance to MES.

Structural modeling of RMES1A and RMES1B and phylogenetic analysis of RMES1A/RMES1B and their homologs

Protein database searches revealed that RMES1A and RMES1B had low amino acid sequence identities (< 20%) to typical CC-NLRs, CCG10/GA-NLRs, CCR-NLRs, and TIR-NLRs. However, their identities to BPH6 and BPH30, two previously reported LRR-domain-containing proteins that confer BPH resistance in rice (Guo et al., 2018; Shi et al., 2021), were greater than 30% (Supplemental Data 3). Because functional TIR-NLRs have not been reported in monocots (Liu et al., 2023), we performed protein structure modeling for RMES1A and RMES1B using AlphaFold3 (Abramson et al., 2024) and compared their predicted domains to those of SR35 and ZAR1, which are structurally resolved CC-NLRs that control plant immunity (Wang et al., 2019; Bi et al., 2021; Förderer et al., 2022). BPH14 and BPH9 were also included in this comparison as representative CC-NLRs that control insect resistance (Zhao et al., 2016; Hu et al., 2017). Both RMES1A and RMES1B were modeled as having four domains with relatively high predicted local distance difference test scores (Figures 2A and 2B). In contrast to SR35 and ZAR1, no typical CC domain was detected in the predicted structures of RMES1A and RMES1B (Figures 2A–2E). The putative N terminus (residues M1–P194) of RMES1A features five-stranded parallel β sheets flanked by several α helices, and its middle domain (residues F195–T300) comprises four α helices (Figure 2F); these two predicted domains resemble the NBD and helical domain 1 (HD1), respectively, of SR35 and ZAR1 in overall topology (Figures 2E and 2F; Supplemental Figures 5A and 5B). However, no winged-helix domain was predicted for RMES1A (Figures 2E and 2F). The AutoDockFR engine (Eberhardt et al., 2021) predicted a probable ATP-binding site in RMES1A, located in a groove between the N-terminal and middle domains, with eight residues (D70, R73, K75, K87, H213, K280, Q281, and R284) potentially involved in the binding (Figure 2F; Supplemental Figure 5C). The location of the predicted ATP-binding site in RMES1A resembles that in SR35 and ZAR1 (Figure 2F). Moreover, many of the conserved sequence motifs associated with ATP binding and catalysis in NLRs, such as VG; P-loop; RNBS-A, -B, -C, and -D; Walker-B; and GLPL (Van Ghelder et al., 2019; Hu et al., 2025), were also found in the predicted NBD of RMES1A/RMES1B (Supplemental Figure 5D).

The C-terminal region (residues K301–R1032) of RMES1A was predicted to form two LRR domains (LRR1 and LRR2) that contain 13 and 15 LRR units, respectively (Figure 2G; Supplemental Figures 6A and 6B). LRR1 and LRR2 likely form a wave-shaped structure, in contrast to the single, horseshoe-shaped LRR domain present in SR35 and ZAR1 (Figures 2C and 2D). The LRR1 units (LRR1–LRR13) are each composed of 23–26 residues, whereas the LRR2 units (LRR14–LRR28) each have 26–32 residues (Supplemental Figures 6A and 6B). On the basis of the predicted RMES1A structure, the asm1 mutant protein, which is missing 59 C-terminal residues (Supplemental Figure 1A), lacks two repeat units (LRR24 and LRR25) in LRR2 (Supplemental Figures 6B and 6C). Similar predictions were obtained for RMES1B (Figure 2B; Supplemental Figures 6D and 6E). Thus, RMES1A and RMES1B are atypical resistance proteins that lack the CC or TIR domain; they each carry a potential NBD at the N terminus, an HD1-like domain in the middle region, and two putative LRR domains at the C terminus (Figure 2E). Consistent with these modeling data, two recent studies identified an atypical CC-NLR (TaCNL2/NL) that lacks a CC domain but still functions in wheat immunity against stripe rust disease, in partnership with a typical CC-NLR (Hu et al., 2025; Klymiuk et al., 2025). Moreover, noncanonical NLRs that lack the CC domain (i.e., NLs) have also been found in several comprehensive bioinformatics studies of plant NLRs, with NL-type proteins identified in the CC-NLRs, CCR-NLRs, and CCG10/GA-NLRs (Kourelis et al., 2021; Li et al., 2023; Martin et al., 2023).

Protein database searches (identity ≥ 30% and coverage ≥ 50%) identified RMES1A/RMES1B homologs in other monocot plants, including common wheat, barley, rice, maize, and Brachypodium distachyon (Supplemental Data 4). Despite low sequence identities, the homologs—which included the BPH resistance proteins BPH6 and BPH30 (Guo et al., 2018; Shi et al., 2021)—all lacked a typical CC or TIR domain according to structural modeling with AlphaFold3 (Abramson et al., 2024). Their predicted structures resembled those of RMES1A/RMES1B, although some of the homologs had other types of predicted structural elements (Supplemental Data 4). In a phylogenetic analysis of plant NLRs based on their NBD sequences (Contreras et al., 2023), RMES1A/RMES1B and their homologs formed a separate group related to the CCG10/GA-NLR clade (Figure 2H).

Impairment of key defense responses in asm mutants

We next examined whether the asm mutants were impaired in antixenosis or antibiosis, which deter aphid feeding and inhibit aphid reproduction, respectively (Knolhoff et al., 2014). In free-choice tests, the number of MES adults that settled on the resistant genotypes (HN16 and NILR+) generally decreased and remained low, whereas the opposite pattern was observed for the susceptible lines (asm1-asm4, ams1asm4, NILR−, and BTx623) (Supplemental Figure 7A). In no-choice tests, the number of newly produced nymphs increased dramatically on all seven susceptible lines, but no or very few nymphs were found on the two resistant genotypes (Supplemental Figure 7B). Thus, both antixenosis and antibiosis are involved in RMES1-mediated resistance against MES.

We performed histochemical staining of MES-inoculated sorghum leaves using diaminobenzidine (DAB), which detects localized hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) production in plant cells (Goggin and Fischer, 2021). Increasingly intense H2O2 accumulation in more MES feeding sites was observed from 0 to 48 h in HN16 and NILR+ (Figure 3A), whereas lower levels of H2O2 accumulation were visible in fewer MES feeding sites in the seven susceptible lines (Figure 3A). H2O2 content increased significantly upon MES inoculation in HN16 and NILR+, especially at 48 hpi, but no significant increase in H2O2 content was observed in the seven susceptible genotypes (Figure 3B). This result is consistent with reports that increased H2O2 accumulation is closely associated with the MES resistance exhibited by sorghum RTx2783, which carries the RMES1 locus (Tetreault et al., 2019; Pant and Huang, 2021). No obvious cell-death lesions were observed in the leaves of HN16, despite intense H2O2 accumulation at 48 h (Figure 3A). Callose deposition, a critical component of plant resistance to insect herbivory (Hao et al., 2008; Walker, 2022), also increased in HN16 and NILR+ but not in the seven susceptible lines after MES inoculation, as revealed by histochemical staining with aniline blue and quantitative measurement (Supplemental Figure 8).

Expression, subcellular targeting, and interactions of RMES1A and RMES1B

RMES1A and RMES1B expression was detected in both vegetative (root, stem, and leaf) and reproductive (spike and seed) organs of HN16 by RT–qPCR, and RMES1A showed significantly higher expression than RMES1B, especially in stems and leaves (Figure 4A). RMES1A expression was strongly upregulated in 14-day-old HN16 seedlings upon MES inoculation peaking at 24 hpi; RMES1B was also induced by MES feeding, although its expression was considerably lower than that of RMES1A at most examined time points (Figure 4B). In situ hybridization assays using an antisense probe that recognized both RMES1A and RMES1B transcripts showed that they were present in the sclerenchyma cells and vascular bundles of HN16 and were substantially upregulated at 48 hpi with MES (Figures 4C and 4D).

The subcellular targeting of RMES1A and RMES1B was analyzed by expressing their C-terminal GFP fusion proteins in sorghum protoplasts. Confocal microscopy revealed the presence of discrete green fluorescent structures in the cytoplasm upon expression of RMES1A-GFP or RMES1B-GFP, which overlapped with red fluorescent structures tagged with the OsEXO70H3-RFP fusion protein (Figure 4E). By contrast, no specific overlap was observed between the fluorescence signals of RMES1A-GFP or RMES1B-GFP and those of the mitochondrion, endoplasmic reticulum, Golgi, or peroxisome markers (Supplemental Figure 9A). Because OsEXO70H3 is a subunit of the exocyst complex that regulates exocytosis by tethering secretory vesicles to the plasma membrane (Wu et al., 2022), this result suggests that RMES1A and RMES1B are both located in the exocyst in sorghum cells.

RMES1A and RMES1B interacted with themselves and with one another in yeast two-hybrid (Y2H) assays and in split luciferase complementation (SLC) assays in tobacco leaves (Supplemental Figures 9B–9D). These interactions were confirmed by co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP), Förster resonance energy transfer (FRET), and bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) assays in sorghum protoplasts (Figure 4F; Supplemental Figure 10). BiFC signals resulting from the self- or mutual interaction of RMES1A and RMES1B overlapped with the red fluorescence of OsEXO70H3-RFP (Supplemental Figure 10B), indicating that RMES1A/RMES1B interactions occurred in the exocyst complex. In addition, the asm1 mutant protein—which had an internal deletion of 59 residues in the C terminus of RMES1A and lacked two predicted LRR repeats in the LRR2 domain (Supplemental Figures 2A, 6B, and 6C)—failed to interact with RMES1B in yeast cells or sorghum protoplasts (Figure 4F; Supplemental Figures 9B–9D and 10). Neither the three sequence paralogs (LRRP1–LRRP3; Figure 1A) of RMES1A and RMES1B nor the homolog BPH30 (Figure 2) could interact with RMES1A or RMES1B (Figure 4F; Supplemental Figures 9B–9D and 10). These data suggest that the interaction between RMES1A and RMES1B is highly specific.

The sorghum aphid protein MsEF1 interacts with RMES1A and RMES1B

We next screened a Y2H library constructed with mRNAs extracted from MES aphids, using RMES1A as bait. A homolog of PP5 phosphatases, which are conserved Ser/Thr-specific phosphatases in eukaryotes (Sager et al., 2020), was found to interact with RMES1A. This MES protein (MsEF1 hereafter) consisted of 493 residues, had a predicted molecular mass of 56.4 kDa, and contained two putative tetratricopeptide repeats and a calcineurin-like phosphoresterase domain (Figure 5A). We then prepared an anti-MsEF1 polyclonal antibody, which detected the MsEF1 protein not only in MES aphids but also in MES-inoculated sorghum leaves (Figures 5B and 5C). MsEF1 homologs are present in diverse aphid species (Figure 5D), and although they have not been functionally characterized in aphids, the fruit fly MsEF1 homolog DmPP5 was previously shown to regulate cell death triggered by oxidative stress (Sekine et al., 2012).

MsEF1 interacted with both RMES1A and RMES1B in yeast, but not with the paralogs LRRP1–LRRP3 or the homologous protein BPH30 (Supplemental Figures 11A and 11B). These RMES1A–MsEF1 and RMES1B–MsEF1 interactions were confirmed in sorghum protoplasts and tobacco cells using Co-IP, FRET, and SLC assays (Figure 5E; Supplemental Figures 11C and 11D). In the absence of RMES1A and RMES1B, MsEF1-GFP was present in both the cytoplasm and the nucleus of BTx623 protoplasts (Figure 5F). However, MsEF1-GFP relocated to the exocyst complex when it was co-expressed with RMES1A-GFP or RMES1B-GFP (Figure 5F). This observation was verified by co-expression of MsEF1-GFP and OsEXO70H3-RFP in the protoplasts of BTx623: MsEF1-GFP did not co-localize with OsEXO70H3-RFP, despite being abundantly expressed in the protoplasts (Figure 5F, bottom). Thus, MsEF1 is not associated with the exocyst when expressed alone, but it appears to relocate to the exocyst complex in the presence of RMES1A or RMES1B. This reasoning is consistent with the result of the BiFC assay, which revealed that MsEF1 interacted with RMES1A or RMES1B (but not BPH30) specifically in the exocyst complex of sorghum protoplasts (Figure 5G). Importantly, Co-IP assays demonstrated that RMES1A, RMES1B, and MsEF1 interacted with each other in sorghum cells (Figure 5H). The three proteins also interacted with each other in yeast tri-hybrid (Y3H) assays, and the β-galactosidase activity stimulated by the three-protein interaction (RMES1A–RMES1B–MsEF1) was significantly stronger than that stimulated by the two-protein interaction (RMES1A–RMES1B) (Supplemental Figures 11E and 11F). Moreover, MsEF1 significantly strengthened the interaction between RMES1A and RMES1B in SLC assays performed in tobacco leaves (Supplemental Figure 11G). However, the three-protein interaction did not occur when RMES1A was replaced by the mutant asm1 protein or by BPH30 in Y3H and SLC assays (Supplemental Figures 11E–11G). Lastly, both the predicted NBD and LRR domains of RMES1A and RMES1B, but not their putative HD1 domain, were involved in the interaction with MsEF1 in Y2H and SLC assays (Supplemental Figure 12). Concordantly, blue native polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (BN–PAGE), performed using bacterially expressed recombinant proteins, demonstrated the formation of RMES1A or RMES1B homo-oligomers, as well as RMES1A–RMES1B and RMES1A–RMES1B–MsEF1 hetero-oligomers with molecular masses of ∼800 kDa (in the absence of MsEF1) or > 800 kDa (in the presence of MsEF1) (Supplemental Figure 13). Collectively, these data suggest that MsEF1 interacts with RMES1A and RMES1B and forms a protein complex in the exocyst of sorghum cells. Whereas RMES1A and RMES1B promote the exocyst localization of MsEF1, MsEF1 strengthens the interaction of RMES1A and RMES1B in the exocyst.

MsEF1 is required for RMES1A/RMES1B-mediated resistance against MES

To assess the potential role of MsEF1 in RMES1A/RMES1B-mediated MES resistance, we silenced MsEF1 expression in MES aphids using RNAi, resulting in clear decreases in MsEF1 transcript abundance and MsEF1 protein level (Figures 6A and 6B). The aphids in which MsEF1 had been silenced by treatment with MsEF1-double-stranded RNA (dsRNA) exhibited greater feeding and fecundity in the resistant line HN16 than those treated with GFP-dsRNA or the mock control (Figures 6C–6E). Moreover, the MsEF1-silenced aphids exhibited a significantly lower capacity for inducing H2O2 production in HN16 compared with the controls (Figure 6F).

We next used two approaches to investigate whether MsEF1 was required for the activation of key anti-aphid defense responses by RMES1A/RMES1B. First, RMES1A-FLAG, RMES1B-MYC, and MsEF1-GFP fusions were singly, doubly, or triply expressed in Nicotiana benthamiana leaves, and their effects on H2O2 production were examined at 48 h post transfection. DAB staining and quantitative measurement of H2O2 content revealed that the H2O2 burst occurred only in leaves that simultaneously expressed MsEF1, RMES1A, and RMES1B (Supplemental Figure 14). Second, we examined whether simultaneous expression of RMES1A, RMES1B, and MsEF1 caused an H2O2 burst in sorghum protoplasts. The results showed that H2O2 production was strongly enhanced only in protoplasts simultaneously expressing RMES1A-MYC, RMES1B-HA, and MsEF1-FLAG, whereas no significant differences were detected in H2O2 content among the protoplast samples expressing GFP or those singly or doubly expressing RMES1A-MYC, RMES1B-HA, and MsEF1-FLAG (Figures 6G and 6H). Together, these results support the requirement for MsEF1 in RMES1A/RMES1B-mediated MES resistance in sorghum.

Immune activation of NLRs is often accompanied by the induction of programmed cell death (PCD; Ma et al., 2020; Förderer et al., 2022). We therefore explored whether RMES1A/RMES1B-mediated MES resistance involved PCD by transiently expressing RMES1A-FLAG, RMES1B-MYC, and MsEF1-GFP, singly or in combination, in N. benthamiana leaves. Pm60, a typical CC-NLR that controls wheat powdery mildew resistance and has been shown to trigger PCD in N. benthamiana (Zou et al., 2018), was used as a positive control. From Supplemental Figures 15A and 15B, it is apparent that Pm60 clearly induced PCD, but no obvious cell death was observed upon either individual or combined expression of RMES1A-FLAG, RMES1B-MYC, and MsEF1-GFP. Immunoblotting assays confirmed the expression of RMES1A-FLAG, RMES1B-MYC, and MsEF1-GFP, but not Pm60, in infiltrated leaf tissues (Supplemental Figure 15C). The failure to detect Pm60 accumulation may be due to the strong PCD associated with its expression. A similar phenomenon has been found in the expression of other PCD-inducing R proteins (Bulgarelli et al., 2010; Hu et al., 2017).

Discussion

Mounting damages caused by the rapid expansion of MES urgently necessitate the development of MES-resistant sorghum cultivars (Nibouche et al., 2018, 2021; Muleta et al., 2022). To facilitate more effective use of the RMES1 locus in sorghum breeding, we cloned the causal genes of RMES1 and found that both RMES1A and RMES1B are required for MES resistance, using a combination of fine-mapping, analysis of multiple KO mutants, and examination of natural gene variants in sorghum germplasm.

By integrating the different datasets generated here, we propose a hypothetical working model for the function of RMES1 (Figure 7). In resistant cultivars such as HN16, MES feeding enhances the expression of RMES1A and RMES1B in sclerenchyma and vascular cells. The sorghum aphid protein MsEF1 is recruited to the exocyst complex by interacting with RMES1A and RMES1B, forming a functional protein complex that leads to upregulation of key defense responses such as the ROS burst and enhanced callose deposition. These processes not only deter MES settling (antixenosis) but also inhibit MES reproduction (antibiosis) on the resistant plant (Figure 7A). In the ams1 mutant, disruption of the LRR2 domain in RMES1A results in MES susceptibility by abrogating the interaction among RMES1A, RMES1B, and MsEF1 (Figure 7B). Previous studies have also reported both antixenosis and antibiosis in MES-resistant sorghum genotypes that carry RMES1 (Tetreault et al., 2019; VanGessel et al., 2024), providing direct support for the data and model presented above.

A working model for the mechanism by which RMES1A/RMES1B mediate resistance against MES aphids.

(A) In resistant cultivars (e.g., HN16), the aphid protein MsEF1 interacts with RMES1A and RMES1B in the exocyst complex. The resulting protein complex triggers immune signaling and, in turn, downstream defense responses such as a reactive oxygen burst and transport of more callose to the cell wall, thus giving rise to both antixenotic and antibiotic resistance against MES infestation.

(B) In the asm1 mutant, immune signaling and downstream defense responses are disrupted because asm1, which lacks two LRR units in the putative LRR2 domain, is unable to form a protein complex with RMES1B and MsEF1 in the exocyst. This abolishes antixenosis and antibiosis, causing MES susceptibility and abundant aphid proliferation on the mutant.

Compared with previous studies on insect-resistance proteins, our work is unique in identifying two atypical NLRs that are both required for insect resistance, through detailed genetic analysis involving both single- and double-KO mutants of these R genes (RMES1A/RMES1B). Recently, VanGessel et al. (2025) also demonstrated the involvement of RMES1A/RMES1B in sorghum resistance against MES aphids through transcriptomic and pangenomic analyses. However, unlike previously characterized NLR pairs, in which at least one partner has a CC or TIR domain (Contreras et al., 2023; Jones et al., 2024), neither RMES1A nor RMES1B contains such a domain. Moreover, neither protein has additional IDs. Despite lacking a CC domain, TIR domain, or ID, RMES1A and RMES1B can interact and upregulate key defense responses (e.g., the H2O2 burst) by forming a complex with the aphid protein MsEF1 in sorghum cells. Together, the unusual structural and functional features of RMES1A/RMES1B distinguish them from previously reported NLRs and NLR pairs. Accordingly, RMES1A, RMES1B, and their homologs form a separate cluster in the phylogenetic tree of plant NLRs (Figure 2H). RMES1A and RMES1B may therefore represent a new type of plant NLR. The closer relatedness of atypical RMES1A/RMES1B-type NLRs to CCG10/GA-NLRs (Figure 2H) is consistent with recent findings that CCG10/GA-NLRs tend to have partial or complete deletions of their CC domain (Lee et al., 2021; Kourelis and Adachi, 2022; Sunil et al., 2024). The unusual features in the modeled RMES1A/RMES1B structures discussed above were also noted by VanGessel et al. (2025).

Despite the insights discussed above, the control of MES resistance by RMES1A and RMES1B is likely more complex than heretofore revealed. Previous studies have shown that atypical NLRs may act autonomously or may require other NLRs or an NLR network to function in plant immunity (Lee et al., 2021; Kourelis and Adachi, 2022; Contreras et al., 2023; Jones et al., 2024; Sunil et al., 2024; Guo et al., 2025; Hu et al., 2025; Klymiuk et al., 2025). It will therefore be important to investigate whether RMES1A/RMES1B require partner NLRs to confer MES resistance and to assess whether RMES1A and RMES1B function mainly as sensors for effector perception in RMES1-mediated MES resistance. The mechanism underlying the difference in expression between RMES1A and RMES1B and the potential role of this difference in regulating the dynamics of MES defense also merit further investigation. The co-localization of RMES1A, RMES1B, and MsEF1 with the exocyst complex and their interaction therein probably play a pivotal role in MES resistance, given that exocyst complexes (also termed exocyst-positive organelles), which facilitate the transport of cytosolic proteins and other substances (including defense compounds) to the cell surface, have emerged as a critical battlefield for attack and counterattack in plant biotic interactions (Stegmann et al., 2013; Lin et al., 2015; Brillada et al., 2021; Michalopoulou et al., 2022; De la Concepcion, 2023). Nevertheless, further work is needed to determine whether RMES1A and RMES1B interact with MsEF1 in the exocyst complex specifically in the sorghum phloem cells on which aphids feed. The presence of two putative LRR domains in RMES1A/RMES1B, a feature shared with BPH6 and BPH30, as well as the Yr87/Lr85 protein that confers resistance to wheat stripe and leaf rust diseases (Guo et al., 2018; Shi et al., 2021; Sharma et al., 2024), may represent a novel functional variation of the single LRR domain commonly seen in canonical NLRs. This is supported by the findings that the asm1 mutant protein, disrupted in the second LRR domain of RMES1A, failed to interact with RMES1B and that asm1 mutant plants were susceptible to MES attack. Consequently, it will be interesting to analyze the function of the two predicted LRR domains in MES resistance conferred by RMES1A/RMES1B in greater detail. Finally, although we have demonstrated the requirement for both RMES1A and RMES1B in RMES1-mediated MES resistance by analyzing multiple mutants lacking RMES1A, RMES1B, or both (Figure 1; Supplemental Figure 3), it is still necessary to confirm this key finding by transgenic expression of the two genes singly or together in a sorghum line lacking the RMES1 locus.

Notably, no cell death occurs in the MES resistance controlled by RMES1A/RMES1B. This is supported by the lack of cell death observed on leaves of HN16 in MES-infested fields or in MES-inoculated seedlings (Figures 1B and 1D) and by the failure to induce PCD in N. benthamiana by transient expression of RMES1A, RMES1B, RMES1A/RMES1B, or RMES1A/RMES1B together with the putative effector protein MsEF1 (Supplemental Figure 15). This may not be surprising, given that a lack of cell death has been observed in the resistance mediated by diverse NLRs, including RDG2A and BPH14 (Bulgarelli et al., 2010; Hu et al., 2017). In typical helper/sensor NLR pairs such as RGA4/RGA5 and RRS1/RPS4 (Saucet et al., 2015; Liu et al., 2021), the helper NLRs usually trigger plant cell death upon recognition of avirulent effectors by the sensor NLRs. In this context, we speculate that RMES1A/RMES1B-mediated aphid resistance may not involve a helper NLR that orchestrates cell death. Nevertheless, we demonstrated the involvement of the ROS burst and enhanced callose deposition in RMES1A/RMES1B-controlled MES resistance (Figure 3; Supplemental Figure 8). How RMES1-carrying sorghum plants achieve strong aphid resistance without cell death is an important question for future research.

Finally, this work demonstrated the importance of MsEF1 in RMES1A/RMES1B-mediated MES defense using multiple approaches. In the future, it will be important to investigate whether MES clones carrying mutant MsEF1 proteins exist in the field and can overcome RMES1-mediated MES resistance. This could facilitate more detailed structural and functional studies of effector (MsEF1) and immune receptor (RMES1A/RMES1B) interactions, which would in turn guide further improvement and more rational use of RMES1-mediated resistance for sustainable control of MES infestation in worldwide sorghum production. Overall, our finding that RMES1A and RMES1B are both required for MES aphid resistance expands the spectrum of molecularly characterized insect-resistance proteins. Continued studies of RMES1A/RMES1B and their homologs will not only contribute new information on the functions and mechanisms of plant NLRs but also identify new resistance genes for the development of insect-resistant crops.

Methods

Plant materials and growth conditions

The sorghum materials used here included HN16, BTx623 (GenBank: GCA_000003195.3), five asm mutants, two NILs (NILR+ and NILR−), P89002 (PI 561847), TX430 (PI 655996), and 166 sorghum germplasm accessions. For field planting, sorghum genotypes (populations) were sown in May and harvested in October using standard cultivation practices. Alternatively, sorghum plants were cultivated in a greenhouse at 28°C with 60% relative humidity and a 16-h light/8-h dark photoperiod. To investigate the response of different sorghum materials to MES infestation at the seedling stage, the tested genotypes were grown in a growth chamber with environmental parameters set as above. Tobacco (N. benthamiana) plants were grown in a greenhouse at 25°C with 60% relative humidity and a 16-h light/8-h dark photoperiod.

Insects

The sorghum aphid, previously named Melanaphis sacchari (GenBank: GCA_002803265.2) but now corrected to M. sorghi (Nibouche et al., 2018; 2021), was reared on seedlings of BTx623 at 28°C under a 16-h light/8-h dark photoperiod with 60% relative humidity.

Fine mapping of RMES1

Two previously reported markers flanking RMES1, i.e., Sb6m2650 and Sb6rj2776 (Wang et al., 2013), were used to screen 5136 BC5F2 plants, resulting in 44 recombinants. Additional DNA markers were designed on the basis of the BTx623 genome sequence and used to analyze the recombinants. This narrowed RMES1 to a 25-kb region between the markers Sb6id2705 and Sb6id2730. A BAC library was constructed for the aphid-resistant sorghum cultivar HN16 as described previously (Zhang et al., 2012a; 2012b). The library, containing 57,600 clones and exhibiting approximately 6.25× coverage of the sorghum genome, was screened by PCR as detailed previously (Farrar and Donnison, 2007). Ten positive BAC clones were identified. Four minimum-tiling-path BAC clones covering the RMES1 region were sequenced by MiSeq sequencing system, and their inserts were assembled. This resulted in a 194-kb sequence containing four LRR protein-encoding genes, i.e., LRRP2, LRRP3, RMES1A, and RMES1B, located in a subregion of 169 kb, corresponding to the 25-kb interval bordered by the DNA markers Sb6id2705 and Sb6id2730 (Figure 1A).

Development of asm mutants and NILs

Diepoxybutane was used to mutate 3000 mature seeds of HN16 as described previously (Wu et al., 2005), with 1500 M2 families cultivated in the field and screened for MES-susceptible mutants by visual inspection of MES aphids on the leaves. asm mutants were found in eight different families, from which asm1 was identified by genomic PCR amplification of the RMES1A or RMES1B coding sequence. Subsequently, ethyl methane sulfonate was used to treat 10 000 HN16 seeds (Wu et al., 2005), with 3176 M2 families grown in the field and visually inspected for asm mutants as described above. The resulting aphid-susceptible M2 plants were investigated for potential mutations in the RMES1A or RMES1B coding sequence by genomic PCR, leading to the identification of asm2. In addition, 10 000 HN16 seeds were irradiated with two doses of fast neutrons (10 and 20 Gy, 5000 seeds each) (Roberts et al., 1999), yielding 7800 M1 families. These families were screened in the greenhouse after inoculation with MES, and asm3 and asm4 were identified in two separate families. These two mutants were found to lack RMES1B by genomic PCR as described above. To clarify the precise chromosomal deletions in the RMES1 locus of asm3 and asm4, the two mutants were re-sequenced using the HiSeq 2000 platform (Mace et al., 2013), and 26.81 and 25.06 Gb of clean data were obtained for asm3 and asm4, respectively. After removal of low-quality reads and adaptor sequences, the reads were mapped to the 169-kb HN16 BAC contig covering the RMES1 locus. Read depth was calculated with mosdepth (v0.2.6) using 100-bp non-overlapping sliding windows. This revealed a region with low read coverage that represented the deleted segment containing RMES1B. Lastly, the double mutant asm1asm4 was prepared by artificial crossing between asm1 and asm4. A BC7F1 plant was selfed in the greenhouse. The resulting BC7F2 population was screened for plants with or without the RMES1 locus by amplifying the coding sequences of RMES1A and RMES1B, leading to the development of NILR+ and NILR−, respectively.

Generation of a CRISPR mutant of RMES1A and RMES1B

Genome editing of MES-resistant sorghum was performed using the sorghum line P89002, which was confirmed to carry the RMES1 locus by PCR amplification and DNA sequencing of the coding region for RMES1A and RMES1B. The CRISPR–Cas9 method was used for editing (Char et al., 2020). The sgRNA target sites were 5′-GCACTGATATGGTTGACT-3′ and 5′-GAAATGGGTGGCAGTACCA-3′, which were conserved in both RMES1A and RMES1B. Five sibling lines, derived from a homozygous mutant (P89002 #25) with KO mutations in both RMES1A and RMES1B, were tested for response to MES infestation. The mutations in RMES1A and RMES1B in P89002 #25 and its derivative lines were confirmed by Sanger sequencing of the PCR amplicons obtained using primers specific for RMES1A and RMES1B (Supplemental Data 5).

Assessing the responses of asm and CRISPR mutants, NILs, and 166 germplasm accessions to MES attack

PCR amplification and sequencing were used to analyze natural variations in RMES1A and RMES1B in 166 germplasms. To evaluate resistance, seeds were germinated in a growth chamber at 28°C (see above). At the two-leaf stage, 15 healthy seedlings (5 per pot) per genotype were inoculated with apterous adult aphids (10 per seedling unless otherwise specified). After inoculation, each pot was covered with an insect-proof device and then returned to the growth chamber. The mean number of aphids on each genotype was determined at 24, 48, 72, 96, 120, and/or 168 hpi using the values collected from 15 seedlings (in three separate experiments). In general, MES aphids could not reproduce on the leaves of resistant sorghum, but they reproduced rapidly on susceptible sorghum plants (Wang et al., 2013). Moreover, the susceptible genotypes (e.g., BTx623, TX430, asm mutants, CRISPR KOs, and NILR−) began wilting 2 weeks after inoculation and died by 21 dpi, whereas the resistant materials (e.g., HN16, P89002, and NILR+) continued to grow.

Free-choice and no-choice tests and honeydew assays

These experiments were performed in a growth chamber at 28°C using sorghum seedlings at the two-leaf stage and adult apterous MES aphids. In a free-choice test, seven trials were conducted, each involving two genotypes (one resistant and one susceptible, four seedlings each) arranged evenly in the same pot, allowing the aphids to freely choose between the genotypes. Each seedling was inoculated with 5 apterous adult aphids, and the number of aphids that remained on the inoculated seedlings was recorded 3, 6, 24, 48, and 72 h after inoculation (Guo et al., 2018). In a no-choice test, seven sets of trials were performed, each involving one resistant and one susceptible genotype planted in different pots. For each genotype, 8 seedlings were used, and 10 apterous adult aphids were inoculated on each seedling; the pot was then covered with a transparent plastic device to prevent the aphids from escaping. The number of nymphs produced by the aphids on each seedling was scored 3, 6, 24, 48, and 72 h after inoculation.

The honeydew secretion assay was performed as described previously (Shangguan et al., 2018). Two fresh stem segments (1.5 cm each) of the tested sorghum genotype were placed onto a filter paper in a clean Petri dish; 5 aphids were inoculated, and the lid was replaced. The whole system was transferred to a growth chamber at 28°C with 80% humidity. The aphids and stems were discarded at 36 hpi, and the filter paper was stained with 0.1% (w/v) ninhydrin in acetone solution. The area of the ninhydrin sediment was measured using ImageJ software.

RT–qPCR assays

Total RNA was extracted using the RNAiso Plus reagent (TaKaRa, 9109) and converted to cDNA with Superscript II reverse transcriptase (Invitrogen, 18080400) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The cDNA was diluted and used as a template for qPCR with gene-specific primers on a Light Cycler 480 Real-Time PCR system (Roche, Basel, Switzerland). Gene-specific primers are listed in Supplemental Data 5. Thesorghum actin gene (NCBI: XM_002441128) and aphid actin gene (NCBI: XM_025337117) served asinternal controls for the RT–qPCR assays.

The expression profiles of RMES1A and RMES1B during sorghum development were measured by RT–qPCR using total RNA samples extracted from roots, stems, leaves, flag leaves, spikes, and mature grains of greenhouse-grown HN16 plants at the heading or harvest stage. To investigate changes in RMES1A and RMES1B expression in response to MES feeding, HN16 seedlings (at the two-leaf stage) were inoculated with MES (20 adult aphids per plant), and their leaf sheaths were collected 6, 12, 24, 48, and 72 h after MES inoculation for use in RT–qPCR assays as described above.

In situ hybridization assays

HN16 seedlings were inoculated with MES as described above. Their leaf sheaths, collected 48 h after inoculation, were cut into 1-cm segments and fixed in FAA solution (3.7% formaldehyde, 5% acetic acid, and 50% ethanol). Leaf sheaths were also collected from uninoculated HN16 seedlings, which were similarly fixed to serve as controls. After wax embedding and sectioning, the fixed samples were used for in situ hybridization assays as reported previously (Zhao et al., 2002). The antisense probe used in these assays was prepared from the oligonucleotide 5′-UGCUUCUACGGCUUCAUUGAUUCU-3′, which was specific for the coding sequences of RMES1A and RMES1B. As a control, the complementary sequence of this oligonucleotide was used to prepare a sense probe. All probes were labeled with digoxigenin-dUTP, and hybridization signals were detected using DAB as a substrate (Gao et al., 2021). The signals were examined and recorded using a light microscope (Zeiss, Jena, Germany).

Y2H and Y3H assays

The GAL4 Y2H system was used to investigate protein–protein interactions. The coding sequences of RMES1A, RMES1B, BPH30, LRRP1–LRRP3, MsEF1, and derivative fragments were individually amplified and cloned at the EcoRI and BamHI sites of the pGBKT7 (BD) or pGADT7 (AD) vector. Different pairs of the resulting vectors were co-transformed into the yeast strain Y2HGold and grown on synthetic medium lacking leucine and tryptophan (SD/−Leu/−Trp), and the resulting clones were tested for protein–protein interactions on medium lacking leucine, tryptophan, and histidine (SD/−Leu/−Trp/−His) at 30°C for 3 days.

For Y3H assays, the coding sequences of RMES1A, asm1, and BPH30 were specifically amplified and cloned into the MSC I location of the pBridge vector at the EcoRI–BamHI sites, resulting in bait constructs. The coding sequence of MsEF1 was amplified and cloned into the MCS II location of bait I/bait Ⅲ/bait V (pBridge-RMES1/pBridge-asm1/pBridge-BPH30) at the NotI sites, producing bait II/bait Ⅳ/bait Ⅵ. The pGADT7–RMES1B construct, developed for the Y2H assays, was used as prey. Various pairs of constructs were introduced into the yeast strain AH109, and the cells were grown on SD/−Leu/−Trp medium for 3–5 days. Yeast strains carrying different sets of constructs were then serially diluted and plated onto SD/−Leu/−Trp/−His and SD/−Leu/−Trp/−His/−Met media.

O-nitrophenyl-β-d-galactosidase tests in the Y3H assays were performed as described by Kippert (1995). In brief, yeast cells were resuspended in 800 μl of Z-buffer (60 mM Na2HPO4, 40 mM NaH2PO4, 10 mM KCl, 1 mM MgSO4, and 50 mM β-mercaptoethanol) and placed on ice. The suspension was then incubated at 30°C for 15 min, and 160 μl of 4 mg ml−1 O-nitrophenyl-β-d-galactoside was added. The mixture was briefly shaken and incubated at 30°C for 15 min, and the reaction was stopped by adding 400 μl of 1 M Na2CO3. After centrifugation for 30 s at 16 000 g, the OD420 of the supernatant was determined using a spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, USA).

Identification of MsEF1

MsEF1 was identified by Y2H screening of a cDNA library prepared using mRNA from approximately 6000 adult MES aphids that had been feeding on HN16 seedlings for 2 days. The Y2H library, which contained 2 × 106 recombinant clones, was developed using the SMART cDNA Library Construction Kit (Clontech, 634901) as described by He et al. (2019). The bait vector pGBKT7-RMES1A, in which the full-length coding sequence of RMES1A was fused to that of the GAL4 DNA-binding domain, was used to screen the Y2H library. Co-transformed yeast cells were grown on SD/−Leu/−Trp/−His medium for 3–5 days at 30°C. The resulting positive clone carrying the MsEF1 coding sequence (GenBank: XP_025195360.1) was further analyzed, and its interactions with RMES1A and RMES1B were confirmed by SLC, BiFC, and Co-IP assays in tobacco leaves or sorghum protoplasts (see below).

Immunoblotting and Co-IP assays

Total proteins were extracted from sorghum using extraction buffer (100 mM Tris–HCl [pH 7.5], 5 mM MgCl2, 1 mM EDTA, 0.5% Triton X-100, 1 mM DTT, and 1 mM PMSF). The resulting proteins were separated by 10% SDS–PAGE and immunoblotted as detailed in Jin et al. (2022). To detect target proteins fused with the FLAG, HA, or MYC tag, appropriate commercial antibodies were used at a 1:2000 dilution. The secondary antibody was goat anti-mouse IgG conjugated to horseradish peroxidase, used at a 1:5000 dilution (MBL, 330). The immunosignals were recorded using a chemiluminescence detector (Tanon 5200, Shanghai, China). MsEF1 polyclonal antibody was prepared in a rabbit using a specific MsEF1 polypeptide (FMKELIKTYREQGKLH) by Mabnus (Wuhan, China). The conserved actin protein or HSP82 served as a loading control.

For Co-IP assays, the coding sequences of RMES1A, asm1, RMES1B, BPH30, and MsEF1 were cloned into the pHZ205-MYC, pHZ204-HA, and pHZ206-FLAG vectors to generate ubiquitin-promoter-driven cassettes for expression of RMES1A-MYC, asm1-MYC, RMES1B-HA, BPH30-MYC, and MsEF1-FLAG in BTx623 protoplasts. Sorghum protoplasts were prepared using the stems and leaves of 14-day-old BTx623 seedlings grown under insect-proof conditions. The protoplasts were transfected with the indicated constructs as described by Zhang et al. (2011). The transfected protoplasts were incubated for 24 h at 28°C in the dark, then centrifuged at 450 g and 4°C for 5 min. Total proteins were extracted from the pelleted protoplasts using 300 μl of extraction buffer as described above. Immunoprecipitation was performed by adding 20 μl anti-MYC antibody-coupled protein G agarose (Beyotime, P2009) and incubating the mixture for 5 h at 4°C. The immunoprecipitated proteins were separated by 10% SDS–PAGE and immunoblotted using anti-MYC (MBL, M192-3), anti-FLAG (MBL, M185-7), anti-HA (MBL, M180-3), or anti-HA-HRP-DirecT (MBL, M180-7) antibody to assess the expression of target proteins in input samples or the presence of interacting protein(s) in the immunoprecipitates.

Subcellular localization and FRET assays

The coding sequences of target genes (RMES1A, RMES1B, and MsEF1) were amplified using gene-specific primers and cloned into pRHVcXFP (GFP or RFP) vectors carrying a ubiquitin-promoter-driven expression cassette (He et al., 2018). The resulting constructs, together with those expressing the nuclear marker bZIP63-RFP (Walter et al., 2004), the exocyst marker OsEXO70H3-RFP (Wu et al., 2022), and other organelle markers (for the endoplasmic reticulum, mitochondrion, Golgi, and peroxisome) (Nelson et al., 2007), were introduced into BTx623 protoplasts in appropriate combinations, and expression of the GFP or RFP fusion proteins was examined 24 h after transfection under a confocal microscope (Zeiss LSM 710). To assess the degree of co-localization, Pearson and Spearman correlation coefficients were calculated from 30 individual images using ImageJ software supplemented with the Pearson-Spearman correlation (PSC) co-localization program (French et al., 2008). GFP signals were examined using excitation at 488 nm and emission at 507–550 nm, and RFP signals were examined using excitation at 561 nm and emission at 588–630 nm. Expression of RMES1A-GFP, RMES1B-GFP, and MsEF1-GFP in the protoplasts was confirmed by immunoblotting (Supplemental Figure 16).

For FRET assays, BTx623 protoplasts co-expressing different combinations of constructs (with GFP or RFP tags) were examined under a confocal microscope (Zeiss LSM 980). Proteins with a GFP tag (RMES1A-GFP, RMES1B-GFP, asm1-GFP, and BPH30-GFP) acted as donors, and those with an RFP tag (RMES1A-RFP, RMES1B-RFP, BPH30-RFP, and MsEF1-RFP) as acceptors. For interacting proteins, excitation at 488 nm led to energy transfer from the GFP-tagged donor to the RFP-tagged acceptor, resulting in fluorescence emission at 561 nm. FRET signals were recorded and quantified according to the instructions provided by the Zeiss LSM 980 FRET system.

BiFC assays

The pRHV suite of BiFC vectors, developed by He et al. (2018), was used to generate constructs expressing VN-MsEF1, VN-asm1, VN-BPH30, VN-RMES1A, RMES1A-VC, and RMES1B-VC. The constructs were introduced into BTx623 protoplasts together with those expressing OsEXO70H3-RFP. The protoplasts were examined by confocal microscopy 24 h post transfection as detailed above, using an excitation wavelength of 514 nm for mVenus or 561 nm for RFP.

SLC assays

SLC assays were performed in tobacco leaves as described previously (Chen et al., 2008). In brief, the coding sequences of RMES1A, RMES1B, MsEF1, BPH30, and GFP were individually cloned into the pCAMBIA1300-NLuc or pCAMBIA1300-CLuc vector. The recombinant strains were infiltrated into leaves of 4-week-old tobacco in the indicated combinations, and luciferase signals were recorded and quantified 48 h after infiltration using a NightShade LB985 Plant Imaging System equipped with IndiGO software (Berthold, Bad Wildbad, Germany).

Blue native PAGE

BN–PAGE was performed according to the manufacturer’s protocol (BN1002BOX, Invitrogen). The RMES1A and RMES1B coding sequences were individually cloned into the bacterial expression vector pCold-TF (with Trigger Factor [TF] and a His-tag) (Qing et al., 2004). This facilitated the expression and purification of RMES1A and RMES1B fused to the TF chaperone and a His-tag (i.e., TF-His-RMES1A and TF-His-RMES1B). TF-His-RMES1A and TF-His-RMES1B were expressed in E. coli. The MsEF1 coding sequence was cloned into the pET21b vector (with a His-tag) to express the His-MsEF1 fusion protein. All recombinant proteins were purified by nickel affinity chromatography (HY-K0210, MedChemExpress, USA). To investigate the potential formation of homo-oligomers, TF-His-RMES1A (∼30 μg) or TF-His-RMES1B (∼30 μg) in elution buffer (250 mM NaCl and 100 mM imidazole in 20 mM Tris–HCl [pH 7.5]) was used directly for BN–PAGE. To test possible hetero-oligomer formation, TF-His-RMES1A (∼15 μg) + TF-His-RMES1B (∼15 μg) or TF-His-RMES1A (∼10 μg) + TF-His-RMES1B (∼10 μg) + His-MsEF1(∼10 μg) were mixed and incubated at 4°C for 4 h to permit sufficient interaction. Immediately before BN–PAGE, the protein samples were each mixed with 4× non-denaturing sample buffer (BN2003, Invitrogen), then separated by electrophoresis on a native PAGE 4%–16% Bis–Tris protein gel. Native protein markers (RTD6137, Real-Times, Beijing) were used to estimate the size of the protein oligomers.

DAB staining and measurement of H2O2

DAB staining of sorghum leaf samples was performed as outlined by Daudi and O'Brien (2012). H2O2 content was measured using a commercial kit (Invitrogen, A22188) following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Cell death assays in N. benthamiana

The leaves of 4-week-old N. benthamiana were infiltrated with Agrobacterium cultures carrying the indicated expression constructs (Supplemental Figure 15). Total proteins were isolated from the infiltrated area 48 h post agroinfiltration and immunoblotted (Ma et al., 2020). Cell death was examined 72 h post agroinfiltration, both visually and by staining the detached leaves overnight in 0.4% trypan blue solution and then bleaching them in chloral hydrate solution (5:2, w/v). Cell death was then recorded using a digital camera.

Callose staining and quantification

Callose deposition induced by MES feeding was examined by staining with 0.01% aniline blue, and the staining signals were recorded by confocal microscopy (Millet et al., 2010). The callose content of sorghum leaf samples collected before and after MES feeding was determined using a callose assay kit (Cominbio, PZZ-1-Y).

Silencing of MsEF1 by RNAi

T7-appended primers were used to amplify specific regions in MsEF1 and GFP. The resulting amplicons were used to synthesize MsEF1- or GFP-specific dsRNA using the Megascript RNAi Kit (Ambion, TX, USA). The purified dsRNA was checked for size, purity, and integrity on an agarose gel. Feeding assays using a dsRNA artificial diet were performed as described previously with some modifications (Whyard et al., 2009). In brief, adult MES aphids of similar size and developmental stage were collected and starved for 8 h, then fed an artificial diet supplemented with dsRNA specific for MsEF1 or GFP. As a mock control, aphids were similarly fed with an artificial diet lacking dsRNA. Seventy-two hours after artificial feeding, the aphids were collected, and the efficiency of RNAi was assessed by performing RT–qPCR to measure MsEF1 transcript levels. To evaluate the effects of MsEF1 silencing on effector-triggered resistance, the three types of aphids were transferred onto HN16 seedlings (10 aphids per treatment). Twenty-four hours later, the total number of aphids and the number of newly produced nymphs were recorded. H2O2 content of HN16 seedlings inoculated with the three types of aphids was also measured as described above.

Bioinformatic and phylogenetic analyses

RMES1A/RMES1B and MsEF1 homologs were identified by searching the NCBI database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). The phylogenetic relationships of RMES1A/RMES1B and their homologs with representative plant NLRs were investigated by constructing a phylogenetic tree (Figure 2H) as described previously (Kourelis et al., 2021; Contreras et al., 2023). The NBD sequences of the 2616 NLRs used to construct the tree are provided as Supplemental Data 6. The MsEF1 protein domain was predicted using HMMER (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/Tools/hmmer/search/hmmscan). A phylogenetic tree of MsEF1 and its homologs (Figure 5D) was constructed by the neighbor-joining method with 1000 bootstrap replicates using MEGA 11 (https://megasoftware.net/).

Protein structural modeling

The full-length RMES1A and RMES1B proteins were structurally modeled using AlphaFold3 (https://alphafoldserver.com/) (Abramson et al., 2024). ATP-binding sites were predicted using AutoDock Vina 1.2 (Eberhardt et al., 2021), then analyzed using PDBePISA at https://www.ebi.ac.uk/pdbe/pisa/. Protein structures were compared using UCSF ChimeraX (https://www.rbvi.ucsf.edu/chimerax).

Statistical analysis

All assays were performed with three independent biological replicates, each with at least three technical replicates. Numerical values are presented as the mean ± SE. Statistical analyses were performed using Student’s t-test or Tukey’s honestly significant difference (HSD) test in GraphPad Prism v8.1.2 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA).
