The relationship between curve of Spee modification and changes in temporomandibular joint parameters: a CBCT-based study
Authors: Badr Sultan Saif, Hu Wanqing, Yunshan Zhao, Gaoli Wang, Saba Ahmed Alhadad, Mazen Musa, Madiha Mohammed Saleh Ahmed, Xi Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe Curve of Spee (COS) is a critical aspect of occlusal anatomy that influences dental alignment and masticatory function. This study aims to evaluate whether COS modifications correlate with temporomandibular joint (TMJ) parameter changes following clear aligner therapy, and compare TMJ adaptations between patients with different initial COS depths. 50 adult patients with skeletal Class I and Class II malocclusions that underwent clear aligner orthodontic treatment were identified and divided into two groups based on their initial curve of Spee (COS) measurement. Group1: COS < 2 mm, and Group2: COS ≥ 2 mm. Pre- and post-treatment cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) scans were used to construct three-dimensional digital models and evaluate TMJ parameters for accurate measurements. Statistically significant changes were found in posterior joint space (PJS) for Group 1 (p = 0.033) and condylar length (CL) for Group 2 (p = 0.006). Independent samples t-test revealed significant differences in CL change scores (p = 0.024) between the two groups. There is a significant association between COS modification and PJS changes (p = 0.043), suggesting that COS modification influences posterior joint space. Clear aligner therapy induces distinct TMJ adaptations depending on initial COS depth: mild COS cases (< 2 mm) exhibit posterior joint space reduction, indicating condylar positional adaptation, while deep COS cases (≥ 2 mm) demonstrate condylar lengthening, suggesting osseous remodeling. Introduction

The temporomandibular joint (TMJ) is a highly specialized synovial joint composed of the mandibular condyle, articular disc, and the corresponding temporal cavity. This complex structure and associated ligaments, muscles, and connective tissues are essential in facilitating and guiding mandibular movements, such as opening, closing, and lateral excursions. Additionally, the TMJ can distribute and dissipate mechanical stresses generated during functional activities like mastication, grinding, and speech. Its unique anatomical and functional characteristics enable it to adapt to biomechanical demands while maintaining stability and mobility1.

The biomechanical integrity of the TMJ is closely linked to dental occlusion, as malocclusion or alterations in occlusal anatomy, such as changes in the Curve of Spee (COS), can influence the distribution of forces within the joint. The COS is defined as the anteroposterior curvature of the occlusal plane running from the incisal tips of the mandibular anterior teeth to the distal aspect of the last molar2. Leveling the COS is considered essential for achieving optimal occlusion, as proposed by Andrews, and its role in maintaining occlusal stability and masticatory function has been well-documented3. Disruptions in occlusal balance, such as excessive steepening or flattening of the COS, may lead to abnormal loading patterns, potentially resulting in TMJ dysfunction, pain, or degenerative changes4. Furthermore, patients undergoing orthodontic treatment to correct malocclusions often experience adaptive bone remodeling in the TMJ5, highlighting the need to investigate the correlation between orthodontic interventions and their impact on TMJ structure. Understanding this relationship is crucial for preventing the development of temporomandibular disorders (TMD) and ensuring long-term joint health.

Emerging evidence has highlighted the biomechanical relationship between dental occlusion and TMJ health. For instance, Zhu et al.6 demonstrated that flattening the COS during orthodontic procedures can influence condylar positioning, potentially contributing to altered loading patterns within the TMJ. Similarly, Hasan et al.7 reported that steepening the COS may increase strain on the joint, potentially harming TMJ health. These findings stress the importance of understanding how modifications to the COS through orthodontic interventions may impact TMJ parameters.

Clear aligners have gained significant popularity among adults due to their aesthetic appeal and comfort8. Prolonged use of clear aligners has been associated with alterations in occlusal plane orientation and potential improvements in muscle function9. However, there is limited evidence on whether modifications to the COS achieved through clear aligner treatment can induce significant changes in TMJ parameters. This knowledge gap ensures the need for further investigation into the relationship between COS modifications and TMJ changes.

Advancements in imaging techniques, such as cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT), have facilitated the assessment of TMJ structure, offering precise three-dimensional visualization of condylar position, joint space, and osseous morphology10,11. Unlike traditional two-dimensional imaging methods, CBCT provides a more comprehensive understanding of the changes occurring within the TMJ, making it suitable for evaluating the effects of COS modifications on TMJ parameters. Therefore, this study aims to assess whether modifications to the COS correlate with changes in TMJ parameters following clear aligner therapy and to compare TMJ adaptations between patients with different initial COS depths. The null hypothesis states that COS modifications through clear aligner treatment do not significantly alter TMJ parameters.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This retrospective study was conducted following the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the ethical committee of Xi’an Jiaotong University (XJTU1AF2022LSK-186). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. We have reviewed the records of patients who underwent Invisalign clear aligner therapy (Align Technology, San Jose, CA, USA), and selected 50 participants (18 males and 32 females, aged 18–40 years) who were treated for dental malocclusions between January 2019 and February 2024 at the First Affiliated Hospital of Xi’an Jiaotong University. The participants were divided into two groups based on their initial COS measurements: Group 1 (COS < 2 mm, n = 25) and Group 2 (COS ≥ 2 mm, n = 25).

Participants that matched the following inclusion criteria were included in this investigation:

(1) Adult participants aged 18–40 years with moderate dental malocclusion underwent clear aligner therapy, (2) Normodivergent skeletal pattern, (3) Availability of initial and final CBCT scans, (4) No presence of TMJ manifestations prior to orthodontic treatment or TMJ surgery, (5) Absence of systemic diseases or conditions affecting bone metabolism or joint health. Exclusion criteria were: (1) Use of intermaxillary and Class II elastics or other auxiliaries during treatment. (2) Skeletal discrepancies requiring surgical intervention, (3) A history of temporomandibular disorders (TMDs) or TMJ pain, (4) Presence of craniofacial syndromes or congenital anomalies, (5) Incomplete or poor-quality CBCT scans.

The clear aligner treatment included bonded attachments as specified by the manufacturer and the treating clinician. The treatment plan for all participants in this research included intrusion of the mandibular anterior teeth to level the Curve of Spee (COS). The virtual treatment plan was generated for each patient, ensuring controlled anterior teeth intrusion while maintaining posterior occlusal contacts. Patients were required to wear each aligner for 22 h daily, with each set changed every 10 days. All procedures were performed by a qualified orthodontist with significant clinical experience, ensuring accurate and consistent treatment delivery.

The sample size was determined based on previous research investigating TMJ adaptations to orthodontic treatment with a mean difference of 0.5 mm in condylar position with a standard deviation of 0.6 mm following occlusal changes12. A power analysis was conducted with G*Power software (version 3.1.9.7) to achieve 80% power and a significance level 0.05. The analysis indicated that a minimum of 23 participants per group was required to detect significant differences in TMJ parameters. Therefore, a total of 50 participants were recruited, with a minimum of 25 participants per group.

Data collection

Pre- and post-treatment CBCT scans were acquired to quantify three-dimensional changes in temporomandibular joint (TMJ) anatomy, including joint space dimensions, and osseous morphology. The scans enabled precise measurement of TMJ parameters listed in Table 1. CBCT scans were acquired using a KaVo 3D eXam machine (KaVo Dental, Bismarckring, Germany) with optimized settings for dentition and TMJ evaluation: voltage of 85 kV, tube current of 8 mA, and a medium field of view of 13 × 10 cm to adequately capture both dental arches and TMJ while minimizing radiation exposure. Scans were obtained with an exposure time of 17.8 s, voxel size of 0.3 mm, and slice thickness of 0.3 mm. The Frankfort horizontal plane was oriented parallel to the floor, and the midsagittal plane was perpendicular to the floor. For three-dimensional digital model construction, CBCT scan data in DICOM (Digital Imaging and Communication in Medicine) format were imported into Mimics 21.0 software (Materialise, Belgium) for 3D model construction. Hard tissues, including the TMJ, were segmented using a threshold range of 450–3065 Hounsfield units (HU) (Fig. 1). The segmented 3D digital models were then inspected for TMJ parameter measurements.

Measurements

To validate the actual tooth movements, we have performed 3D digital model superimpositions of pre and post-treatment mandibular arches based on stable mandibular bony structures (mandibular body and ramus), ensuring accurate assessment of lower anterior tooth intrusion movement. (Fig. 2). The superimposition has been employed using Geomagic control software (v2022; 3D Systems, Rock Hill, SC, USA).

(A) Pre-treatment mandibular model (transparent blue) superimposed on post-treatment model (solid gray), frontal view; (B) Lateral view. Models were aligned and visualized using Geomagic Control software (v2022; 3D Systems, Rock Hill, SC, USA; https://www.3dsystems.com/software/geomagic-control-x).

For COS measurement, the occlusal plane was constructed by fitting it to the lower incisors’ edges and the second molar’s distobuccal cusp on both sides. The perpendicular distances from the cusp tips of the canine, premolars, and mesiobuccal cusps of the first and second molars to the constructed occlusal plane were measured and summed to calculate the COS13. Measurements were performed for both sides of the mandibular arch, and the average of the two sides was recorded as the COS value14 (Fig. 3). For TMJ changes assessment, the anatomical landmarks of the pre-treatment and post-treatment constructed 3D digital models were identified in the horizontal, sagittal, and coronal planes, and measurements for each parameter were performed accordingly15,16. Paired t-tests comparing right vs. left TMJ parameters revealed no significant differences at baseline (p = 0.060–0.971) or post-treatment (p = 0.058–0.988). As bilateral symmetry was confirmed (Supplementary Table 1), measurements were averaged to represent overall TMJ changes while reducing data complexity. Therefore, each participant’s right and left sides were averaged for pre- and post-treatment measurements to evaluate the overall changes in TMJ parameters. This approach focused on the total difference or change in TMJ parameters rather than side-specific differences. The TMJ measurement parameters are detailed in (Table 1) and illustrated in (Fig. 4). The same investigator and co-investigator reassessed the selected measurements over a two-week interval using 10 cases to determine the intra- and inter-examiner reliability of the measurements under study.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using statistical software (SPSS version 27.0). The normality of the variables was confirmed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Patients baseline characteristics were evaluated using independent sample t-test (Table 2). To assess whether there were significant changes in the TMJ parameters after treatment, paired t-tests were used to compare initial and final measurements within each group. In contrast, independent t-tests were used to compare Group 1 and Group 2 changes. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

The reproducibility of the measurements approach was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC), which revealed excellent intrarater agreement (ICC = 0.97) and equally strong interrater reliability (ICC = 0.96), both indicating excellent consistency.

The mean achieved lower anterior teeth intrusion was 2.73 mm (SD = 0.72) in Group 1 and 3.62 mm (SD = 1.18) in Group 2. Paired sample t-test results for pre- and post-treatment measurements are presented in (Table 3). No statistical differences were found in pre-treatment and post-treatment skeletal measurements. A significant reduction in the COS was observed in both groups (p = 0.001), with Group 2 showing a more significant reduction (mean difference = -1.17 mm) compared to Group 1 (mean difference = -0.65 mm). Significant changes were also observed in posterior joint space (PJS) for Group 1 (p = 0.033) and condylar length (CL) for Group 2 (p = 0.006). No significant changes were observed in other TMJ parameters.

Discussion

This study has investigated the relationship between COS modification using clear aligners and changes in TMJ parameters. Occlusion-TMJ relationship remains a contentious topic in orthodontics, with researchers who advocate for a direct biomechanical influence17–21, and others who argue for minimal correlation22,23. Our findings contribute to this debate by demonstrating that clear aligner-mediated COS modifications induce TMJ adaptations, but these changes are site-specific and depth-dependent. While previous studies12,24–26 have examined TMJ adaptations to orthodontic treatment with clear aligners from different perspectives, this appears to be the first CBCT-based investigation specifically correlating quantitative COS modifications with 3D TMJ parameter changes in clear aligner therapy. This study’s three-dimensional (3D) measurement approach represents a significant advancement over traditional two-dimensional (2D) methods for evaluating TMJ changes. The strong correlation between intra- and inter-observer reliability confirms the precision of CBCT in identifying landmarks, making it superior for evaluating osseous structures in the TMJ region. Unlike previous studies27,28 that relied on 2D imaging techniques, which were limited to measuring cross-sectional distances between two points, the 3D methodology provides a comprehensive understanding of the actual spatial changes occurring within the TMJ. This approach eliminates the inherent limitations of 2D methods, which often underestimate measurements due to their inability to account for the entire 3D geometry of the joint29,30.

The COS is a critical parameter in orthodontic diagnosis and treatment planning. A deep COS is often associated with dental malocclusions, such as Class II division 1, and can lead to functional and aesthetic concerns31. The choice of a 2 mm cutoff for grouping in this study was based on previous literature and clinical relevance, where studies have shown that a 2 mm or greater COS depth is associated with significant occlusal discrepancies and may require more extensive orthodontic intervention32–34.

The results demonstrated significant reductions in the COS in both groups, with slightly greater changes observed in Group 2 compared to Group 1. These findings align with a previous study that reported clear aligners’ effectiveness in modifying COS35. The significant reduction in COS in both groups suggests that clear aligners can potentially apply the biomechanical forces required to flatten the curve.

In contrast, studies by Goh et al.36 and Ciavarella et al.37 reported limitations in the ability of Invisalign appliances to effectively level the Curve of Spee. This discrepancy can be attributed to the fact that their studies were conducted using a limited number of aligners and were based on retrospective evaluations of patients treated between 2013 and 2019. Clear aligner technology, including materials and treatment protocols, underwent significant advancements during this period. Unlike earlier studies, the present investigation was conducted from 2019 onward, following Invisalign’s introduction of SmartTrack aligner material, which may have contributed to the improved outcomes observed.

The effects of orthodontic treatment with clear aligners on the TMJ have been examined from various perspectives. For instance, Al‑Tayar et al.24 have reported that orthodontic intervention, including molar distalization with clear aligners, does not result in significant TMJ changes. Similarly, Al‑Somairi et al.26 reported that clear aligner treatment of Class I malocclusion has an insignificant effect on TMJ structures in extraction and non-extraction treatment approaches. The results of the present study align with these previous findings, demonstrating no significant changes in most TMJ parameters. However, two exceptions were observed: a reduction in the posterior joint space (PJS) in Group 1 and an increase in condylar length (CL) in Group 2. The decrease in PJS in Group 1 may result from the redistribution of occlusal forces through occlusal plane modification following COS leveling, potentially altering condylar positioning within the glenoid fossa6,7. Also, the increase in CL in Group 2 suggested an adaptive remodeling of the condyle in response to changes in the occlusal plane38,39.

Significant differences were observed between the two groups’ COS and CL change scores, suggesting that the initial COS depth may influence the magnitude of TMJ adaptation in specific sites. The greater COS reduction and CL in Group 2 indicate that patients with a deeper initial COS may experience more pronounced condylar remodeling during orthodontic treatment. Previous researches24–26,40 reported increased CL following occlusal changes induced by a stabilization splint or clear aligners treatment. However, the increase was not statistically significant. The increase in CL values post-treatment suggests a pattern of condylar adaptation to altered occlusal conditions. This strengthens the conclusion that occlusal changes lead to measurable alterations in TMJ parameters, particularly in patients with significant pre-treatment discrepancies41. While the treatment durations were comparable between COS groups (22.53 ± 2.98 vs. 21.09 ± 2.82 months; p = 0.084), it should be noted that this study was not specifically designed to isolate the effects of treatment duration from those of occlusal changes. The observed TMJ adaptations may result from a combination of biomechanical responses to COS leveling and time-dependent remodeling processes. The differential responses between groups suggest that initial COS depth may influence the nature of TMJ adaptation.

The regression analysis revealed a statistically significant inverse relationship between COS modification and PJS changes (p = 0.043), with COS reduction accounting for 5.4% of the observed variance in PJS (R² = 0.054). Specifically, for every 1 mm decrease in COS depth, the PJS diminished by approximately 0.374 mm, suggesting that occlusal leveling may induce condylar displacement or posterior articular eminence remodeling. While the explained variance was modest, this association underscores the measurable impact of COS flattening on joint space dimensions, consistent with the adaptive capacity of the TMJ to occlusal alterations. However, no significant associations were found between COS and other TMJ parameters, suggesting that the effects of COS modification may be limited to specific aspects of TMJ morphology.

The lack of significant changes in other TMJ parameters may reflect the inherent stability of these structures in response to occlusal changes with clear aligner treatment. Alternatively, it may indicate that the observed changes were within the physiological range of TMJ adaptation, as reported by previous studies24–26,40. However, further research with larger sample sizes and more extended follow-up periods is needed to explore the long-term effects of COS modification on TMJ health.

All TMJ measurements were captured immediately after aligner treatment, prior to any functional occlusal settling. While this provides insight into actual structural changes, it cannot assess long-term TMJ stability or late-onset adaptations.

The significant changes in COS and TMJ parameters observed in Group 2 highlight the need to carefully evaluate patients with a deeper initial COS. Clinicians should consider the potential effects of COS modification on TMJ morphology and function, particularly in patients with pre-existing TMJ conditions or risk factors for temporomandibular disorders (TMDs).

Conclusion

Clear aligner therapy produces differential TMJ adaptations based on initial Curve of Spee (COS) depth. Cases with initial mild COS showed significant posterior joint space reduction suggesting condylar positional adaptation, while those with deeper initial COS (≥ 2 mm) demonstrated significant condylar lengthening after treatment indicative of osseous remodeling. While all observed changes remained within physiological limits, these findings highlight the importance of considering the extent of initial COS depth when anticipating TMJ responses.
