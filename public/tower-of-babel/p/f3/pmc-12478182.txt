Rootworm resistance to Bt associated with increased injury to corn pyramids combining Bt proteins and RNA interference
Authors: Bruce E. Tabashnik, Yves Carrière
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SignificanceCrops genetically engineered to produce pest-killing proteins from Bacillus thuringiensis (Bt) control some major pests and reduce insecticide sprays. However, evolution of resistance to Bt crops has decreased these benefits. Analyses of published data for rootworms, the most damaging corn pests in the United States, show that their field-evolved resistance to Bt proteins is associated with increased root injury on corn producing Bt proteins and a recently introduced RNA interference trait targeting rootworms. This reduces yield and is especially concerning because of limited previous exposure to this new trait. Recommendations to sustain control include combinations of traits that are each highly effective, more host plants that do not target rootworms, and integrating transgenic corn with crop rotation and other tactics.

Crop plants genetically engineered to produce insecticidal proteins from the bacterium Bacillus thuringiensis (Bt) have revolutionized control of some major pests. The Bt proteins in such transgenic crops kill some key pests but are safe for people, wildlife, and most beneficial insects (1–4). The benefits of Bt crops include pest suppression and regional eradication in one case, reduced reliance on insecticide sprays, compatibility with biological control by natural enemies, and economic gains for farmers (3–9). In the United States, Bt plants accounted for 86% of all corn and 90% of all cotton planted in 2024 (10). Globally, Bt crops were planted on 50 million hectares in 2009, which more than doubled to 109 million hectares in 2019 (11). Of the Bt crop hectares planted worldwide in 2019, Bt corn accounted for 51.2%, Bt soy 25.3%, and Bt cotton 23.4% (11). Conservatively assuming that the annual total of Bt crop hectares has been constant since 2019, we estimate that the cumulative total area planted to Bt crops since their initial year in 1996 will reach 1.8 billion hectares in 2025. Nonetheless, the benefits of this approach have been diminished because some pests have evolved practical resistance to Bt crops, which is genetically based resistance that reduces the efficacy of Bt crops in the field (12). Practical resistance to Bt crops is documented for at least 31 cases in some populations of 11 pest species in eight countries (12–15). Thus, better understanding of how to manage resistance is urgently needed to sustain the efficacy of Bt crops.

While the first Bt crops each produced a single Bt protein, biotechnology companies and growers have largely shifted to Bt crops producing more than one Bt protein, which may control a broader range of pests and improve control of individual pest species (16–18). We use the term “pyramid” for crops that produce two or more Bt proteins or other transgenic traits that target the same pest and thereby aim to delay evolution of resistance by that pest (16). One of the key conditions favoring durability of pyramids is that pests are highly susceptible to each of the traits in a pyramid (19). The susceptibility to one Bt protein in a pyramid can be reduced by direct selection for resistance or cross-resistance caused by selection with another Bt protein. In a broader sense, cross-resistance can also occur when selection with Bt proteins individually or in combinations causes resistance to other combinations of Bt proteins, other insecticidal traits, or both (20–27).

Here, we focus on how resistance to Bt proteins affects the efficacy of recently introduced transgenic corn pyramids that target rootworms (Chrysomelidae: Coleoptera) by combining an RNAi interference trait with Bt proteins. Rootworms including the western corn rootworm (Diabrotica virgifera virgifera) and northern corn rootworm (Diabrotica barberi) are the most important pests of corn in the United States, with a mean annual estimated yield loss of $2 billion for 2023 and 2024 in addition to substantial control expenses (SI Appendix, Supplementary Methods).

Since 2003, corn genetically engineered to control rootworms has been planted in the United States (12, 28). Transgenic corn available now targets rootworms with one or two of these four Bt proteins: three Cry3 proteins (Cry3Bb, mCry3A, and eCry3.1Ab) and Gpp34/Tpp35Ab (previously called Cry34/Cry35Ab) (17, 29). Although some Bt proteins produced by transgenic corn are effective only against lepidopterans (16), we focus here on rootworm-active Bt corn, which accounted for more than half of the corn planted in the United States from 2009 to 2016 [the most recent year for which the rootworm-active corn adoption data are publicly available (30)]. Widespread practical resistance to rootworm-active Bt proteins, including the pyramid of Cry3Bb + Gpp34/Tpp35Ab (SmartStax, subsequently referred to as SSX), has been documented (31–38).

Rootworm resistance to Bt corn has encouraged adoption of transgenic corn targeting these pests with RNAi interference based on double-stranded RNA (dsRNA) that blocks production of sucrose nonfermenting protein 7 (DvSnf7), which is essential for intracellular protein sorting (39, 40). Corn with DvSnf7 is available only in pyramids: Cry3Bb + DvSnf7 and Cry3Bb + Gpp34/Tpp35Ab + DvSnf7 (SmartStax Pro, subsequently referred to as SSP), which were commercialized in 2024 and 2022, respectively (41–43). Although we are not aware of any evidence of field-evolved resistance to DvSnf7, experimental selection yielded resistance to DvSnf7 in western corn rootworm and to other dsRNAs in two other species of chrysomelid beetles (44–46).

Spurred by United States Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) reports of unexpected injury to roots of corn pyramids producing DvSnf7 and Bt proteins in 2023 and 2024 (32) and recent meta-analyses of rootworm resistance to Bt proteins (30, 38), we hypothesized that resistance to Cry3Bb and Gpp34/Tpp35Ab reduces the efficacy of Cry3Bb + DvSnf7 and SSP because the efficacy of these pyramids depends in part on efficacy of Cry3Bb and Gpp34/Tpp35Ab. To test this hypothesis, we analyzed the extensive field data reported in two landmark papers: Ye et al. (30) reports root injury from field trials in 10 states during 2005 to 2020; Head et al. (43) has been cited over 300 times and includes more than 40 field trials conducted in nine states from 2013 to 2015 to assess root injury and emergence of adult beetles (SI Appendix, Tables S1 and S2). We also compiled and analyzed root injury data from two additional sources: seven field trials performed by Monsanto in three states from 2013 to 2014 and nine university field studies conducted in three states from 2021 to 2023 (SI Appendix, Tables S3 and S4). Overall, our analysis included 998 data values for root injury or emergence for corn with and without traits targeting rootworms obtained from 2005 to 2023 in 12 published field studies (SI Appendix, Tables S1 to S4).

The results support the hypothesis that resistance to Cry3Bb and Gpp34/Tpp35Ab reduces the efficacy of the pyramids that produce one or both of these Bt proteins and DvSnf7. The observed cross-resistance to pyramids producing these Bt proteins and DvSnf7 is associated with increases in root injury and emergence of adult beetles. We also found that the efficacy of DvSnf7 was substantially lower for reducing root injury than emergence. Because root injury decreases yield, increased root injury to pyramids producing DvSnf7 has immediate practical consequences.

Definitions, Background, and Theory.

Root injury is measured on a scale from 0 to 3, with each 1.0 increase in root injury lowering yield by 15.2 to 17.9% (47–49). We define relative injury as the root injury for corn with one or more traits targeting rootworms divided by the root injury for control corn, which lacks rootworm-active traits but is otherwise similar to the corn targeting rootworms. Thus, relative injury = 0 when root injury for rootworm-active corn is 0, and relative injury = 1 when root injury is the same for rootworm-active and control corn. Following an established standard (30, 50), we define efficacy as 1 minus relative injury, which yields efficacy of 1 when relative injury = 0 and decreases to 0 as relative injury increases to 1. Although not common, when injury is greater for rootworm-active corn than control corn, relative injury is > 1 and efficacy is negative. In parallel, we define the same terms based on emergence of adult rootworm beetles, including relative emergence as emergence of adult beetles on rootworm-active corn divided by emergence of adult beetles on control corn.

Reduced efficacy can reflect evolution of resistance, environmental factors such as low Bt protein concentration in an individual plant or field, or both. However, extensive bioassay data show that evolution of resistance has caused the systematic reduction in efficacy of Bt corn against rootworms across many locations and years (30–38). These robust data provide the basis for using reduced efficacy of Bt corn against rootworms as a proxy for resistance. Moreover, by definition, practical resistance to Bt crops reduces their efficacy (12). Thus, we use the term “cross-resistance” here to describe reduced efficacy of a pyramid that includes DvSnf7 caused by selection for resistance that reduced efficacy of one or both Bt proteins in the pyramid.

To illustrate cross-resistance to pyramids despite no cross-resistance between the individual traits in the pyramids, we analyze some purposely simplified scenarios where no cross-resistance occurs between traits and the effects of the traits are independent such that their effects on relative injury or emergence are multiplicative (i.e., no synergy or antagonism). We refer to relative injury in these scenarios, but the same equations and conclusions apply for relative emergence.

In scenario A, each of the two traits in a pyramid has efficacy of 0.95 against susceptible insects, which means that on corn with either trait alone, the relative injury for susceptible insects is 0.05. Because of the traits’ independent action, relative injury is 0.05 × 0.05 = 0.0025 for the pyramid with both traits, which yields efficacy of 0.9975. If resistance evolves to trait 1 reducing its efficacy to 0 (relative injury = 1) but not to trait 2 (relative injury = 0.05), relative injury on the pyramid is 0.05 (1 × 0.05) and its efficacy is 0.95. In scenario B, the assumptions are the same except the efficacy of trait 2 against susceptible insects is 0.4, and the efficacy of the pyramid against susceptible insects is 0.97 (1 – [0.05 × 0.6]). For insects completely resistant to trait 1 and susceptible to trait 2, efficacy of the pyramid is 0.4. Thus, despite no cross-resistance between the traits, resistance to trait 1 can cause strong cross-resistance to the pyramid. In general, with two independently acting traits and no cross-resistance or resistance to trait 2, the efficacy of the pyramid is expected to decline linearly as resistance to trait 1 increases (SI Appendix, Supplementary Equations).

The principles described above for two-trait pyramids also apply to pyramids with three or more traits. A simplified case directly relevant for our analyses below of rootworm field data entails a three-trait pyramid with efficacy = 0 for traits 1 and 2 (Bt proteins) because of field-evolved resistance and 0.4 for novel trait 3 (DvSnf7) to which populations have not been exposed. In this case, even with no cross-resistance between Bt proteins and DvSnf7, resistance to the Bt proteins is expected to cause cross-resistance to the three-trait pyramid. In addition, with complete resistance to the Bt proteins, the efficacy of DvSnf7 alone determines the efficacy of the three-trait pyramid. In general, some cross-resistance to a three-trait pyramid is expected with any resistance to the first two traits. The only exception would entail efficacy of the third trait equal to or greater than the efficacy of the pyramid producing all three traits (e.g., efficacy of the third trait = 1). However, this exception does not apply for DvSnf7 (43, 51, 52) and is unlikely for any pest control trait.

Results

Increased Injury to Roots of Rootworm-Active Bt Corn Relative to Control Corn Reflects Resistance to Bt Proteins.

The analyses that we conducted of the long-term university field trial efficacy data of Ye et al. (30) from five states show that relative to corn without traits targeting rootworms, injury to roots of rootworm-active corn increased significantly across years for corn producing Cry3Bb (R2 = 0.54, P = 0.002), Gpp34/Tpp35Ab (R2 = 0.57, P = 0.012), or both (i.e., SSX, R2 = 0.45, P = 0.012; Fig. 1). Together with the results from bioassays providing direct evidence that rootworm susceptibility to Cry3Bb and Gpp34/Tpp35Ab decreased significantly during the same period (33–38), the results in Fig. 1 based on relative injury provide compelling evidence of evolution of practical resistance to both Bt proteins. An alternative hypothesis is that increases in rootworm population density across years caused or contributed to the increased injury on rootworm-active Bt corn relative to control corn. However, rootworm injury to corn without traits targeting rootworms decreased during the same period (30), which does not support the alternative hypothesis.

Negative Association between Efficacy of Pyramids Producing Bt Proteins Plus DvSnf7 and Resistance to the Same Bt Proteins.

We used regression analyses of seven sets of previously reported data from eight states (SI Appendix, Tables S1 to S4) to test the hypothesis that rootworm resistance to Bt corn causes cross-resistance to pyramids producing the same Bt proteins plus the RNAi interference trait DvSnf7. As a proxy for resistance, we used root injury or emergence of adult beetles on corn producing Cry3Bb or Cry3Bb + Gpp34/Tpp35Ab relative to paired control corn without traits targeting rootworms. Consistent with the hypothesis and the theory explained above, all seven analyses demonstrate a significant negative linear association between efficacy of corn pyramids that produce DvSnf7 together with Cry3Bb or Cry3Bb + Gpp34/Tpp35Ab and relative injury or emergence of adult rootworm beetles on corn producing the same Bt proteins without DvSnf7 (Figs. 2 and 3 and SI Appendix, Figs. S1 and S2 and Table S5). The mean slope of the regression, which indicates the decrease in efficacy as relative injury or relative emergence on Bt corn without DvSnf7 increases from 0 to 1, was −0.66 (SE = 0.21) and the mean R2 was 0.67 (SE = 0.06, P < 0.02 for each regression, mean n = 18 paired observations per regression, Figs. 2 and 3 and SI Appendix, Figs. S1 and S2 and Table S5).

Limited Effect of Rootworm Population Density on Efficacy.

For each of the seven analyses testing for effects of relative injury or emergence on efficacy of Bt corn plus DvSnf7 described above (Figs. 2 and 3 and SI Appendix, Figs. S1 and S2), we also used multiple regression to determine whether efficacy was affected by population density (root injury or emergence on control corn). In five of the seven cases (Figs. 2A and 3A and SI Appendix, Figs. S1 A and Band S2), no data transformation was required (Methods), and the effect of density on efficacy was not significant (P = 0.99, 0.90, 0.83, 0.44, and 0.06, respectively). In one other case, the association between efficacy of SSP and relative injury on control corn was significant and positive for both the untransformed and transformed data (Fig. 2B and SI Appendix, Tables S6 and S7). However, the absolute value of the regression coefficients reflecting the strength of the relationship with SSP efficacy was 5- to 11-fold less for root injury to control corn than for relative injury to SSX (SI Appendix, Tables S6 and S7). In the fifth case, the association between efficacy of SSP and relative emergence on control corn was significant, positive, and weak for the transformed data but not significant for the untransformed data (Fig. 3B and SI Appendix, Tables S8 and S9). Thus, the overall effect of population density on efficacy was not strong or consistent. Furthermore, consistent with the results above from simple linear regression, a significant negative association between relative injury to SSX and SSP efficacy occurred in all multiple regression analyses including those with a significant effect of population density.

Efficacy of DvSnf7 and Bt Proteins: Lower for Reducing Root Injury than Adult Emergence.

The data of Head et al. (43) enable direct comparison of the efficacy of DvSnf7 and Bt proteins based on reduction in root injury versus reduction in adult emergence. We first calculated the efficacy of DvSnf7 alone as the efficacy of Cry3Bb + DvSnf7 for field trials where Cry3Bb alone had no efficacy (based on relative root injury or emergence >1 on corn producing only Cry3Bb, Figs. 2A and 3A). In these cases, the efficacy of Cry3Bb + DvSnf7 can be attributed to DvSnf7. This approach yielded mean efficacy of DvSnf7 based on root injury (0.113) significantly lower than based on emergence (0.800, t test, P = 0.031, Table 1). Although this is the simplest, most direct, and most relevant way to calculate efficacy of DvSnf7 alone, only six cases were available where the relative injury or emergence on Cry3Bb corn was >1, and data for Cry3Bb + RNAi were reported from the same field trial. For SSX, relative injury or emergence was <1 in all cases we analyzed (Figs. 2 B and C and 3B).

To obtain estimates based on larger sample sizes, we also calculated the efficacy of DvSnf7 alone by comparing root injury or emergence on Bt corn pyramids with and without DvSnf7. Based on this less direct approach for Cry3Bb + DvSnf7 versus Cry3Bb, the efficacy of DvSnf7 alone is significantly lower for reducing root injury (median = 0.432) than adult emergence (0.896, U-test, P = 0.0015, Table 1). In parallel, for the contrast between SSP (with DvSnf7) and SSX (same Bt proteins without DvSnf7), the efficacy of DvSnf7 alone is significantly lower for reducing root injury (0.200) than adult emergence (0.901, U-test, P < 0.0001, Table 1). The median efficacy of DvSnf7 from the three comparisons above is 4.5-fold lower against root injury (0.200) than emergence (0.896, Table 1).

For Bt proteins, the efficacy was also lower for reducing root injury than adult emergence, but the difference between the two effects was smaller than for DvSnf7 (Table 1). The overall median efficacy for Cry3Bb, Gpp34/Tpp35Ab, and both proteins (in SSX) is 0.833 for root injury versus 0.984 for emergence, which is only a 1.2-fold difference (Table 1). The median efficacy for Cry3Bb + DvSnf7 and SSP was 0.848 based on root injury versus 0.996 for emergence, also a 1.2-fold difference (Table 1).

For the seven Monsanto field trials during 2013 and 2014 reported by EPA and the nine university field studies conducted from 2021 to 2023 (SI Appendix, Tables S3 and S4), we cannot make direct comparisons between root injury and emergence because emergence was not reported. Analysis of the root injury data for Cry3Bb + DvSnf7 corn relative to Cry3Bb corn from the seven Monsanto field trials yielded median efficacy of DvSnf7 of 0.400. Based on root injury to SSP relative to SSX, median efficacy of DvSnf7 was 0.400 in the seven Monsanto field trials and 0.548 in the nine university field studies.

Discussion

The results of our analyses of the data from 2013 to 2015 on root injury and adult emergence provide compelling evidence that rootworm resistance to corn producing rootworm-active Bt proteins was associated with reduced efficacy of corn producing the same Bt proteins together with the RNAi trait DvSnf7. Direct selection with DvSnf7 can be excluded as a cause of the reduced efficacy of Bt proteins plus DvSnf7 because these results precede the initial commercialization of DvSnf7 in 2022. The leading candidates for factors contributing to the observed outcome are cross-resistance to DvSfn7 caused by resistance to Cry3Bb, Gpp34/Tpp35Ab, or both; and cross-resistance to Bt corn + DvSnf7 caused solely by resistance to the Bt proteins without cross-resistance to DvSnf7.

The available data rule out strong cross-resistance between Cry3Bb and DvSnf7, but they do not exclude weak cross-resistance between these two traits. Moar et al. (52) found no significant association between resistance to Cry3Bb and DvSnf7 across 12 field populations in diet bioassays of western corn rootworm sampled in 2012. In a more direct and rigorous test, they found that the Gass-R strain with at least 50-fold resistance to Cry3Bb relative to the related Gass-S strain had statistically significant, 2.7-fold cross-resistance to DvSnf7 relative to Gass-S based on the conservative criterion of no overlap between strains of the 95% confidence limits of the concentration killing 50% of the insects (LC50). Beetle emergence in greenhouse bioassays was higher for Gass-R than Gass-S on corn producing DvSn7 dsRNA alone and with Bt proteins, but in post hoc multiple comparisons with limited statistical power, the difference between strains was statistically significant only for DvSn7 with Cry3Bb (39, 52).

In related work, strains of western corn rootworm resistant or susceptible to DvSnf7 had close to 100% mortality at two high concentrations of Cry3Bb, but no results with lower concentrations were reported (44). Selection for resistance to VTP-ase subunit A dsRNA caused statistically significant 4.2-fold cross-resistance to Cry3Aa in the chrysomelid beetle Leptinotarsa decemlineata (45). We did not find published data evaluating cross-resistance between Gpp34/Tpp35Ab and DvSnf7 or other dsRNAs. Nonetheless, the mode of action is so different between Gpp34/Tpp35Ab and DvSnf7 that strong cross-resistance between them is unlikely.

The most likely explanation for cross-resistance to corn producing rootworm-active Bt proteins and DvSnf7 in the field data from 2013 to 2015 is resistance to the Bt proteins shared between the corn with and without DvSnf7. Indeed, such cross-resistance is generally expected when resistance occurs to one or more components of a pyramid, as explained in the introductory section about theory.

For the 17 sets of observations from nine studies conducted from 2021 to 2023 that we compiled and analyzed, two sets are from 2021, seven from 2022, and eight from 2023. Thus, for these recent data, we cannot exclude the possibility of some exposure to corn producing DvSnf7 and direct selection for resistance to DvSnf7. However, based on root injury, the median efficacy of DvSnf7 in field trials was 0.548 for 2021 to 2023 versus 0.200 and 0.400 in the 2013 to 2015 data reported by Head et al. (43) and the EPA (SI Appendix, Table S3), respectively. These results do not support the hypothesis that the more recent observations reflect resistance to DvSnf7 caused by direct selection in addition to the effects of cross-resistance between the pyramids without and with DvSnf7.

We conclude that the cross-resistance to SSP caused by extensive resistance to the rootworm-active Bt proteins shared between SSP and previously adopted Bt corn contributed to the reports of unexpected injury to SSP and Cry3Bb + DvSnf7 in 2023 and 2024 (32), just 1 to 2 y after SSP was commercialized in 2022. Of the total of 372 cases of unexpected injury for 2023 and 2024 reported by Bayer to EPA (32), 85% were for SSX RIB (refuge-in-a-bag, a seed blend with 95% traited seeds and 5% lacking rootworm-active traits) and 15% for SSP (54 cases) or Cry3Bb + Snf7 (1 case) (SI Appendix, Table S10). To put these reports in context it would be useful to know the percentage of corn planted to each product, but such data are not publicly available.

Underestimation of the resistance to Cry3Bb and Gpp34/Tpp35Ab in the field and its effect on the efficacy of SSP is probably a key factor underlying the difference between the field outcomes and predictions from modeling that field-evolved resistance to SSP would take 3 to >25 y after SSP was widely adopted [with 95% of all corn planted to SSP, a 5% seed blend refuge, and no other host plants (43)]. The extensive resistance to Cry3Bb and Gpp34/Tpp35Ab observed by 2022, including more than 1200 reports of unexpected injury to rootworm-active corn from 2019 to 2022 (31, 32) as well as complete resistance to both proteins in bioassays of rootworms from some problem fields in Iowa and Nebraska (36, 38) likely reflect resistance allele frequencies that were close to 1 for both proteins in many populations by 2022. By contrast, the maximum initial resistance allele frequencies assumed in the modeling were 0.50 for Cry3Bb and 0.10 for Gpp34/Tpp35Ab (43).

Our analyses of the adult beetle emergence data from 2013 to 2015 reported by Head et al. (43) yielded estimates of DvSnf7 efficacy from 0.800 to 0.901, similar to their estimates of 0.80 to 0.95. Whereas they did not report efficacy based on root injury, our analysis of root injury from the same study shows that DvSnf7 efficacy was only 0.113 to 0.432 with 2- to 7-fold lower efficacy for reducing root injury than emergence in three direct comparisons. In additional datasets from studies that measured either root injury or emergence, DvSnf7 efficacy was also lower for root injury than emergence: 0.400 and 0.548 in our analyses of root injury from seven Monsanto field trials and nine field university studies versus 0.86 for emergence of the Cry3Bb-resistant Gass-R strain in the greenhouse on noncommercial corn producing only DvSnf7 (52).

The relatively low efficacy of DvSnf7 against root injury has immediate implications for farmers and, together with resistance to the rootworm-active Bt proteins, probably contributed to the reports of unexpected injury to pyramids producing Bt proteins and DvSnf7 (32). Considering these reports and the results here, we recommended revising the popular “Handy Bt Corn Trait Table” for 2025 (17) to indicate widespread resistance to the rootworm-active Bt proteins in all the pyramids that produce them, including the pyramids that also produce DvSnf7.

We hypothesize that DvSnf7 has lower efficacy for reducing root injury than adult emergence because it kills rootworms slowly (40, 51, 53), which extends feeding even for individuals that do not eventually emerge as adults. We also suggest that the smaller difference for Bt proteins between efficacy in reducing root injury versus emergence reflects faster decreases in feeding caused by the Bt proteins relative to DvSnf7 (51).

We encourage reporting of efficacy based on root injury because this metric is directly related to yield, has immediate consequences for growers, and is likely to provide an earlier signal of field-evolved resistance to facilitate proactive management. We also propose coordinated large-scale annual monitoring and reporting based on root injury, analogous to the sentinel plot program for tracking lepidopteran resistance to Bt crops (54). Compared with root injury, adult emergence data are more difficult to obtain, not as directly related to yield, and probably not as useful for early detection of resistance. Nonetheless, knowledge of efficacy based on adult emergence is valuable because it is directly related to rootworm resistance, reproduction, and dispersal, making it particularly useful for modeling evolution of resistance and rootworm population dynamics.

Predictions from realistic modeling, the well-documented history of evolution of rootworm resistance to Bt corn, and empirical evaluation of refuge function indicate that the seed blends with 5% nontraited corn currently mandated by the EPA are not adequate to substantially delay evolution of resistance to corn pyramids producing DvSnf7 (28, 55–57). Moreover, Ye et al. (30) demonstrated that growers have suffered substantial economic losses from their tendency to overplant rootworm-active corn despite the extra cost for its seed and the reduced benefits in increasing yield because resistance has decreased efficacy. While their analysis focused on 2005 to 2016, the extent of this overplanting has most likely increased since then because of the reduced rootworm injury to corn that does not target rootworms (which diminishes potential yield benefits from rootworm-active corn) and increased rootworm resistance to Bt corn (30). Thus, the time is ripe to increase the refuge percentage to at least 20% for pyramids with two or more highly effective traits and 50% for plants with only one effective trait, as recommended previously by the majority of the Scientific Advisory Panel convened by EPA (28, 55). Greater diversity of offerings by seed companies including more corn hybrids that lack rootworm-active traits could facilitate this change (30). Government incentives for such diversity as well as for increasing crop rotation and implementing other integrated pest management tactics could also be useful (18, 30, 58).

Management of resistance to DvSnf7 in the immediate future is likely to affect the initial efficacy and durability of two rootworm-active pyramids that are in the pipeline at EPA: IPD072Aa + DvSSJ1 and Mpp75Aa + Vpb4Da + DvSnf7 (29). DvSSJ1 dsRNA targets the rootworm gene SSJ1, while the three other new rootworm-active traits are bacterial proteins: IPD072Aa from Pseudomonas chlororaphis, Mpp75Aa (previously called Cry75Aa) from Brevibacillus laterosporus, and Vpb4Da (previously called Vip4Da) from Bt (59–62). Rapid evolution of resistance to DvSnf7 could reduce or eliminate the efficacy of this trait in the three-trait pyramid, making it more like a two-trait pyramid. Moreover, experimental selection for resistance to DvSnf7 dsRNA in western corn rootworm caused cross-resistance to other dsRNAs (44) and cross-resistance between dsRNAs occurred in two other species of chrysomelid beetles (45, 46). Thus, resistance to DvSnf7 is likely to cause cross-resistance to DvSSJ1 dsRNA, leaving the new two-trait pyramid with only one effective trait. With widespread resistance to Cry3Bb and Gpp34/Tpp35Ab, many rootworm populations experience DvSnf7 as a single trait. Acting now to preserve rootworm susceptibility to DvSnf7 could have major benefits for the efficacy and durability of future as well as current transgenic corn pyramids.

Methods

Literature Search and Field Data Analyzed.

We searched for relevant data using Google Scholar and Scopus, and by searching relevant EPA publications and the journal Arthropod Management Tests. We included publicly available datasets that report field data on root injury or adult emergence enabling comparisons among rootworm-active Bt corn with and without DvSnf7 and control corn without rootworm-active traits. We analyzed data from refs. 30 and 43, seven Monsanto field trials reported by EPA (63), and nine publications listed in SI Appendix, Table S4.

We analyzed the university field trial data in Fig. S9 of Ye et al. (30) that summarizes results for root injury on rootworm-active Bt corn relative to control corn from 403 pairs of field observations from five states: Illinois (IL), Iowa (IA), Indiana (IN), Nebraska (NE), and South Dakota (SD). We focused on these data because they represent the years 2005 to 2020, which was the most recent year in their study, whereas 2016 was the most recent year included in their other analyses. The mean sample size was 9.4 pairs of field observations per rootworm-active Bt protein set (Cry3Bb, Gpp34/Tpp35Ab, or both). Compared with data from the previous and subsequent years, relative injury was low for Cry3Bb corn in 2017 (0.02) and Gpp34/Tpp35Ab corn in 2018 (0.08). The sample size for each of these points was only two pairs of field observations and they could be considered outliers. Excluding them would increase R2 and reinforce the conclusions. Nonetheless, mean root injury on control corn was not unusually low in 2017 or 2018, and we retained both points in the regression analyses reported in the Results to be as comprehensive and objective as possible.

We analyzed the field data reported previously by Head et al. (43) on root injury (which they refer to as root damage rating) (SI Appendix, Table S1) from 41 trials from 2013 to 2015 in the five states listed above and in Colorado (CO), Minnesota (MN), and Wisconsin (WI) based on the Iowa State University 0 to 3 nodal injury scale (47) and their data on emergence of adult beetles (SI Appendix, Table S2) in 14 field trials from 2014 and 2015 in IL, IN, and MN. Twelve of these 14 field trials also evaluated root injury and two evaluated only adult emergence. Thus, we analyzed data from a total of 43 of their field trials, 29 with only root injury data, 2 with only emergence data, and 12 with both. We did not analyze the root injury data from one field trial (Peterson, MN in 2014) because this trial is missing the data for control corn. We did not analyze the emergence data from the four field trials in IA during 2015 from their Table 7 because these trials lack Bt corn without DvSnf7. For 10 of the site-years we analyzed for 2014 and 2015, the emergence data for western and northern corn rootworm enabled calculation of the percentage of adults for each species, which yielded a mean of 68.2% (range: 21.6 to 99.7%) for western corn rootworm.

We analyzed the data for root injury from the seven Monsanto field trials conducted in 2013 to 2014 in IA, IL, and NE reported by EPA (63) (SI Appendix, Table S3). Unlike all the other field trials we analyzed in which rootworm infestations were entirely natural, rootworm eggs were added to corn plants to augment natural infestations in these seven Monsanto field trials (63). For control corn, the mean root injury was 1.4 (SE = 0.09) for the 7 augmented field trials versus 0.97 (0.10) for the 41 nonaugmented trials from 2013 to 2015 of Head et al. (30).

We also compiled and analyzed 17 sets of observations from university field trials of root injury to four types of rootworm-active corn (Cry3Bb, Cry3Bb + DvSnf7, SSX, and SSP) and paired control corn without traits targeting rootworms from university field trials conducted from 2021 to 2023 in IA (n = 3), NE (n = 10), and SD (n = 4) as reported in 9 publications (SI Appendix, Table S4).

Analyses of Root Injury, Adult Emergence, and Efficacy.

We calculated relative injury as one minus efficacy from the data of Ye et al. (30) on mean annual efficacy for corn producing Cry3Bb, Gpp34/Tpp35Ab, or both. Relative injury equals root injury on rootworm-active corn divided by root injury on control corn. So, efficacy equals 1–relative injury and 1–efficacy equals relative injury. We used linear regression analysis to test for increases across years in injury to rootworm-active corn producing Cry3Bb, Gpp34/Tpp35Ab, or both relative to control corn based on data from Ye et al. (30) (Fig. 1, three regressions).

We also used regression analyses to test for the association between efficacy of rootworm-active Bt corn + DvSnf7 and relative injury or emergence on corn with the same Bt proteins (either Cry3Bb alone or Cry3Bb + Gpp34/Tpp35Ab) but without DvSnf7 (two initial regressions each for Figs. 2 and 3, two for SI Appendix, Fig. S1, and one for SI Appendix, Fig. S2). In all but two of the ten aforementioned regression analyses for Figs. 1–3 and SI Appendix, Figs. S1 and S2, the data meet the assumption of homoscedasticity. In the two other cases (association between efficacy of SSP and relative injury or relative emergence on SSX based on data from Head et al. (43), Figs. 2B and 3B), we conducted regression analyses for both the untransformed data and with the data transformed to meet the assumption of homoscedasticity by using ranks (64). In both cases, analysis of the untransformed and transformed data reveals that the association between efficacy of SSP and relative injury or emergence on SSX was statistically significant and negative (SI Appendix, Tables S5−S9). We also conducted multiple regression analyses to evaluate how efficacy of rootworm-active Bt corn + DvSnf7 is affected by rootworm population density (injury or emergence on control corn) and relative injury or emergence on corn with the same Bt proteins but without DvSnf7. For the data in Figs. 2B and 3B, we conducted the multiple regressions for the untransformed and transformed data (SI Appendix, Tables S5−S9). We focus on the results for the untransformed data in the main text because they can be interpreted more readily.

For the emergence data of Head et al. (43), efficacy did not differ significantly between western and northern corn rootworm for corn with each of the five trait sets (Cry3Bb, Cry3Bb + DvSnf7, Gpp34/Tpp35Ab, SSX, and SSP, P > 0.15 in each of five separate paired t-tests with n = 6 or 10 pairs per test, data from SI Appendix, Table S2). Thus, in all analyses, we evaluated the emergence data together for the two species.

We first calculated the efficacy of DvSnf7 alone as the efficacy of Cry3Bb + DvSnf7 for the six field trials from Head et al. (43) where Cry3Bb alone had no efficacy (relative root injury or emergence >1 on corn producing only Cry3Bb, Figs. 2A and 3A). In these cases, the efficacy of Cry3Bb + DvSnf7 can be attributed to DvSnf7. To obtain estimates based on larger sample sizes, we also calculated the efficacy of DvSnf7 alone by comparing root injury or emergence on Bt corn pyramids with and without DvSnf7. We calculated the efficacy of DvSnf7 alone as 1 minus the root injury (or emergence) on corn producing Cry3Bb + DvSnf7 divided by the root injury (or emergence) on corn producing Cry3Bb alone. Analogously, we calculated the efficacy of DvSnf7 as 1 minus the root injury (or emergence) on SSP (Cry3Bb + Gpp34/Tpp35Ab + DvSnf7) divided by the root injury (or emergence) on SSX (same Bt proteins without DvSnf7). We calculated the efficacy of corn producing Cry3Bb, Gpp34/Tpp35Ab, or both as 1 minus relative injury (or emergence). We used U-tests to determine whether efficacy differed significantly between values based on root injury versus emergence for DvSnf7, Cry3Bb, Gpp34/Tpp35Ab, and Cry3Bb + Gpp34/Tpp35Ab from the data of Head et al. (30).

Our indirect calculations of efficacy used a similar principle as those of Head et al. (30), but they did not explicitly define efficacy or report it for some traits, and they did not calculate efficacy based on root injury. For four of five estimates of trait efficacy based on their adult emergence data, our values are nearly identical to theirs (based on our calculations of one minus the ratios in their Table 10), as expected. However, values of efficacy for Cry3Bb of −0.294 and 0.092 (calculated as one minus the ratios 1.294 and 0.908 in their Table 10) are much lower than our estimate of 0.496. Following their methods (including natural log transformation and back-transformation), we could not reproduce the results for Cry3Bb in their Table 10, which may reflect computational errors.
