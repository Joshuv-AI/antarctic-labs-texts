Intercostal Vein Approach for Balloon-occluded Retrograde Transvenous Obliteration of Gastric Varices: A Case Report
Authors: Hidetaka Hayashi, Hiro Kiyosue, Seika Furuie, Taiga Matsumoto, Hiroyuki Uetani, Seitaro Oda, Yoshitaka Tamura, Katsuya Nagaoka, Yasuhito Tanaka, Toshinori Hirai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIsolated gastric varices are often treated with retrograde transvenous obliteration through a gastrorenal shunt. However, treatment is challenging when major shunts are absent. We report a rare case of balloon-occluded retrograde transvenous obliteration performed through an intercostal vein. In a 48-year-old man with idiopathic portal hypertension, recurrent isolated gastric varices developed after previous endoscopic and embolization treatments. Imaging indicated the left gastric vein as the inflow vessel and the left 9th intercostal vein as drainage route. Under ultrasound-guided puncture of the intercostal vein, microballoon-occluded retrograde venography was performed, followed by ethanolamine oleate infusion and N-butyl cyanoacrylate embolization. The varix achieved thrombosis without complications. This case highlights the feasibility of intercostal vein access for retrograde transvenous obliteration when conventional routes are unavailable.Keywords: balloon-occluded retrograde transvenous obliteration, gastric varices, intercostal vein

Introduction

Balloon-occluded retrograde transvenous obliteration (BRTO) as a specific type of retrograde transvenous obliteration is one of the effective treatments for gastric varices. Successful treatment requires a precise understanding of the anatomical structures and hemodynamics of the varices [1]. BRTO is generally performed through a gastrorenal shunt, which serves as the major drainage route in approximately 84% of cases [2]. Alternative pathways—such as gastrocaval and phrenicopericardial shunts—may also function as primary outflow routes and can serve as potential approach routes for retrograde transvenous obliteration [2-4]. In patients with a history of BRTO, recurrent varices may develop as alternative collateral veins become the dominant drainage route, which must be considered when planning reintervention [5, 6].

Here, we report a rare case of BRTO performed through the intercostal vein, which had become the primary drainage route after occlusion of the original shunt after prior treatment. Written consent has been obtained from the patient.

Case Report

Patient background

A 48-year-old man with idiopathic portal hypertension had an isolated gastric varix that had been treated four years earlier. At that time, BRTO through the gastrorenal shunt was initially attempted; however, balloon-occluded retrograde venography failed to adequately visualize the gastric varix. After a multidisciplinary consultation between interventional radiologists and gastroenterologists, the patient instead underwent endoscopic injection sclerotherapy (EIS) using 70% NBCA, with concurrent endovascular embolization of the major drainage routes, including the pericardiophrenic vein, the gastrorenal shunt, and the hemiazygos vein (Figure 1). Although this approach reduced the primary bulk of the varix, a portion remained patent, and complete embolization of the inflow and outflow vessels was not achieved. The patient also had coexisting esophageal varices, for which endoscopic variceal ligation and additional EIS were performed two years and one year earlier, respectively.

Clinical course

Despite initial treatment, follow-up examinations revealed gradual re-enlargement of the gastric varix. Consequently, the patient was referred to our department for retreatment. Laboratory tests showed preserved hepatic function (Child-Pugh class A, score 5). The platelet count was persistently low, approximately 50,000/μL, attributable to hypersplenism from splenomegaly. At the time of this examination, there were no esophageal varices requiring treatment.

Imaging and angiographic findings

Contrast-enhanced CT showed enlargement of a fundal gastric varix contiguous with the left gastric vein and an enlarged left 9th intercostal vein (Figure 2). To further delineate the vascular anatomy, angiographic studies were performed. A 4-Fr catheter was placed in the superior mesenteric artery. To determine the optimal timing for portal vein visualization, portography was performed by injecting 20 mL of contrast medium (350 mgI/mL, OptirayⓇ; Guerbet Japan, Tokyo, Japan) at a flow rate of 4 mL/sec using a power injector. Subsequently, CTAP was performed by injecting 75 mL of contrast medium diluted to half-concentration (175 mgI/mL) at a rate of 2.5 mL/sec, timed to coincide with the pre-determined portal phase. Similarly, digital subtraction angiography was performed through the splenic artery (3 mL/sec, 15 mL) and the left gastric artery (1.5 mL/sec, 10 mL). CTAP and CT venous phase images were obtained, respectively, to evaluate the detailed hemodynamics. Transarterial portography, CTAP, and CT venous phase confirmed the left gastric vein as the inflow vessel to the varix and the left 9th intercostal vein, through the ascending and transverse portions of the inferior phrenic vein, as the outflow drainage route (Figure 3). CT during left gastric arterial venography revealed complex downstream collateral venous pathways beyond the left 9th intercostal vein. The left 9th intercostal vein formed peripheral anastomoses with the 10th intercostal vein. The 10th intercostal vein coursed posteriorly and medially, draining into the left paravertebral vein and the epidural venous plexus, and subsequently entered the left renal vein through the L2 lumbar vein. In addition, the left 9th intercostal vein ascended through the paravertebral vein and epidural venous plexus, continuing into the hemiazygos vein. At the T9 level, this pathway communicated with the right paravertebral vein and further ascended to drain into the azygos vein. The previously embolized drainage pathways—the pericardiophrenic vein, gastrorenal shunt, and hemiazygos vein—remained completely occluded.

Interventional procedure

Because no conventional drainage routes, including the gastrorenal shunt, gastrocaval shunt, or pericardiophrenic vein, were available, a percutaneous transvenous approach through the intercostal vein was selected for BRTO. Under ultrasound guidance, the left 9th intercostal vein (approximately 3 mm in diameter) was punctured with a 21-gauge needle (Micropuncture Introducer Set, Cook Medical, Tokyo, Japan). A 3-Fr introducer sheath was inserted and subsequently upsized to a 4-Fr sheath (Figure 4a). Through this sheath, a 4-mm × 10-mm microballoon catheter (Septer-C, Terumo, Tokyo, Japan) was advanced over a 0.014-inch microguidewire (CHIKAI, Asahi Intecc, Aichi, Japan) into the intercostal vein and positioned adjacent to the gastric varix. During navigation of the microballoon catheter, multi-planar volume reconstruction and maximum intensity projection images of contrast-enhanced CT and CTAP were used as imaging references to guide catheter advancement along the access route. Selective balloon-occluded venography delineated the entire gastric varix and confirmed stasis of blood flow. Under balloon occlusion, a total of 6 mL of 5% ethanolamine oleate with contrast was slowly injected into the varix. The injection was performed incrementally while ensuring retention; adequate stagnation of the sclerosant within the varix was achieved and maintained for several minutes (Figure 4b). Subsequently, the variceal outflow tract adjacent to the varix was embolized using a 1:1 mixture of NBCA and Lipiodol through the microballoon catheter. After obliteration of the gastric varix, the punctured intercostal vein was similarly embolized with approximately 0.5 mL of a 1:1 NBCA–Lipiodol mixture (Figure 4c).

Follow-up and outcome

Post-procedural CT confirmed satisfactory retention of the sclerosant within the gastric varix with no leakage into other vessels. Contrast-enhanced CT on day 5 indicated complete thrombosis of the gastric varix. No complications occurred, and the patient was discharged on post-procedure day 6. Follow-up CT at six months showed no residual or recurrent gastric varix (Figure 5).

Discussion

This case illustrates a rare access route for BRTO through the intercostal vein. Although BRTO is commonly performed through a gastrorenal shunt [1, 6], recurrent or residual gastric varices may develop alternative portosystemic drainage pathways after initial therapy. To our knowledge, only one previous case of BRTO through the intercostal vein has been reported by Minamiguchi et al. [7]. In that report, BRTO was successfully performed through the intercostal vein after failed attempts through the gastrocaval and pericardiophrenic veins. Similarly, in the present case, the intercostal vein served as the dominant efferent route after previously established collateral pathways had been occluded. This finding should not be interpreted as implying that prior embolization generally causes dominance of the intercostal vein but rather reflects patient-specific hemodynamic redistribution.

Anatomically, intercostal veins rarely function as the dominant efferent route for gastric varices and are typically narrower than other collateral vessels such as the inferior phrenic or pericardiophrenic veins. Detailed anatomical and radiological studies have shown that intercostal veins form extensive anastomotic networks both anteriorly and posteriorly, communicating with the internal thoracic vein, the azygos–hemiazygos system, and the paravertebral venous plexuses [8]. In addition, longitudinal intersegmental anastomoses between adjacent intercostal levels create a complex collateral network along the thoracic wall [8]. Because of these multidirectional and segmental anastomoses, intercostal veins usually serve as secondary collateral pathways. However, when other major drainage routes are hypoplastic or have been occluded, redistribution of portal outflow through these pre-existing networks may occur. In the present case, this hemodynamic redistribution allowed the intercostal vein to become functionally dominant. Consequently, BRTO through the intercostal vein requires a downsized system—including a smaller sheath, catheter, and microballoon—and careful procedural technique. The segmental and anastomotic architecture of the intercostal veins may disperse intravascular pressure across multiple channels, which may be the reason they often remain relatively narrow despite functional dominance. These anatomical constraints necessitate thorough preprocedural imaging and careful planning.

There are also potential risks associated with the intercostal vein approach, particularly bleeding at the puncture site. Bleeding from the punctured intercostal vein can be minimized using a micropuncture kit and tract embolization with NBCA. In addition, inadvertent puncture of the intercostal artery is a potential concern, which can be mitigated by real-time ultrasonographic guidance during needle insertion. Because the intercostal vein may course adjacent to the lung parenchyma in some patients, deep needle advancement at certain intercostal levels may increase the risk of pneumothorax.

Other interventional treatment options, including percutaneous transhepatic obliteration (PTO), transjugular intrahepatic portosystemic shunt (TIPS), and partial splenic embolization, may be considered as alternative treatments for gastric varices in the absence of major drainage routes [9]. PTO provides direct access to the afferent vein and is effective in certain cases; however, it carries a notable risk of puncture-related complications, including intraperitoneal bleeding and hepatic subcapsular hematoma [10], particularly in patients with advanced cirrhosis, thrombocytopenia, or ascites. TIPS is advantageous in patients with diffuse portal hypertension and uncontrolled variceal bleeding; nonetheless, its procedural complexity and the potential for hepatic encephalopathy may limit its utility. In the present case, owing to thrombocytopenia related to splenomegaly, BRTO through an accessible intercostal efferent vein was considered a safer and more targeted therapeutic option.

Clinically, this case highlights the importance of reevaluating venous hemodynamics in patients with recurrent gastric varices. The emergence of new dominant drainage pathways may necessitate adjustments in treatment strategy. In such scenarios, imaging modalities such as contrast-enhanced CT, CT during arterioportography, and angiography are essential for delineating collateral circulation and guiding procedural planning.

In conclusion, BRTO through the intercostal vein may be a viable therapeutic option in selected cases of recurrent gastric varices, particularly when conventional drainage routes are inaccessible. Familiarity with alternative venous pathways and flexibility in interventional techniques are key to achieving successful outcomes.
