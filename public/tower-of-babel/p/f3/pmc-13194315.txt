Insomnia and women: exploring vulnerability, impact, and intervention
Authors: Hae Ri Kim, Soo In Kim, Weon-Jeong Lim, Somi Jeong, So Hyun Ahn, Eunju Jeong, Sun-Young Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThis review examines why women experience insomnia at higher rates than men, addressing how biological, psychophysiological, and sociocultural mechanisms interact to increase vulnerability and informs gender-specific treatments.MethodsA literature search of PubMed, PsycINFO, and Scopus (2000–2024) identified studies examining sex or gender differences in insomnia. Findings were synthesized across biological, psychophysiological, and sociocultural domains, with attention to treatment implications.ResultsThe vulnerability of women to insomnia is multifactorial. Biologically, sleep is influenced by hormonal fluctuations that occur during the menstrual cycle, pregnancy, and menopause. Genetic studies have shown higher heritability in women (~ 59%) than in men (~ 38%), with certain risk genes (e.g., MEIS1) exerting stronger effects in women. Neuroimaging findings link female insomnia to alterations in emotion regulation regions (e.g., limbic circuitry), whereas male insomnia is more associated with sensory-processing networks. Psychophysiologically, women exhibit greater cognitive-emotional arousal, including rumination, and heightened stress-related sleep reactivity than men. Socioculturally, these vulnerabilities are amplified by gendered stressors, particularly work–family conflict, while men’s insomnia is more closely related to occupational strain. Pharmacologically, sex-informed treatment targets orexin-mediated hyperarousal, GABAergic signaling, and hypothalamus–pituitary–adrenal axis activity. Non-pharmacologically, women-specific care extends cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia to address rumination, emotional regulation, and work–family stress within a gender-sensitive framework.ConclusionInsomnia in women is best conceptualized as a diathesis–stress condition. Mechanism-based gender-informed interventions are needed to optimize clinical outcomes.

Introduction

Insomnia is one of the most common sleep disorders worldwide, affecting a substantial proportion of the adult population and posing a serious public health concern because of its profound impact on the quality of life [1–3]. While both men and women experience insomnia, decades of epidemiological studies have consistently demonstrated that women are more likely to suffer from insomnia and that their symptoms are often more severe and persistent [4–6]. This gender disparity persists across the female lifespan, yet a comprehensive understanding of the underlying mechanisms remains limited [4, 6, 7].

Women’s vulnerability to insomnia cannot be attributed to a single factor; rather, it emerges from the complex interplay of biological, psychophysiological, and sociocultural influences. Biologically, women undergo dynamic hormonal fluctuations across the reproductive stages, including the menstrual cycle, pregnancy, and menopause, which directly affect sleep–wake regulation [5, 6, 8]. Genetic research further suggests that the heritability of insomnia is higher in women, while neuroimaging studies have revealed functional alterations in brain circuits related to emotional regulation that are strongly associated with insomnia in women [9–11].

These biological predispositions are amplified by psychophysiological characteristics [12]. Compared to men, women exhibit greater sleep reactivity to stress and are more prone to cognitive-emotional arousal before bedtime, such as rumination and worry [13]. These features render the female sleep system particularly vulnerable, and facilitate both the onset and chronic maintenance of insomnia [14].

Moreover, these vulnerabilities are deeply embedded within gendered social roles and cultural contexts [15, 16]. Women are disproportionately exposed to work–family conflict and caregiving responsibilities, which exacerbate sleep disturbances, whereas men’s insomnia is more commonly linked to occupational stressors such as long working hours [16, 17]. Insomnia, therefore, is not merely a physiological disorder but a multifaceted condition that manifests through different pathways depending on gender [14, 18].

Despite these insights, current clinical approaches largely remain gender-neutral and fail to account for women’s unique biological rhythms, cognitive-emotional tendencies, and sociocultural burdens [13–15, 19]. This gap raises critical questions. What do gender-sensitive interventions look like? How can a gender-informed model of insomnia be developed and applied in clinical practice? This review aims to address these questions by examining three domains: (1) biological mechanisms, including hormonal, genetic, and neurocircuitry factors; (2) psychophysiological processes, such as cognitive-emotional arousal and sleep reactivity; and (3) sociocultural and role-based influences [5, 14, 17, 20]. By integrating evidence across these dimensions, the present work also aims to provide a multidimensional explanation for women’s vulnerability to insomnia and to highlight the need for gender-tailored clinical strategies and future research directions [6, 8].

Methods

We conducted a narrative review of studies published between 2000 and 2025 using PubMed, PsycINFO, and Scopus databases. Search terms included combinations of “insomnia,” “sex differences,” “gender,” “sleep reactivity,” “hyperarousal,” “hormonal factors,” and “cognitive-emotional arousal.” Additional terms such as “sex-informed pharmacology” and “gender-specific CBT-I” were used to identify studies on treatment approaches. Studies were included if they (1) examined insomnia in relation to sex or gender differences, (2) addressed female-specific life stages (e.g., menstrual cycle, pregnancy, and menopausal transition), or (3) investigated relevant biological, psychophysiological, or sociocultural mechanisms. Non-English articles and case reports were excluded from analysis. Priority was given to studies directly addressing sex or gender differences in insomnia. However, studies with indirect relevance were also included when they provided complementary evidence of the underlying mechanisms.

To ensure the scientific value of this review, we prioritized literature using the British Association for Psychopharmacology (BAP) evidence hierarchy, which provides a structured framework for both pathophysiological mechanisms and therapeutic interventions [21] (Table 1). For complex etiology such as hormonal mechanisms, neurophysiological changes, genetic factors in female insomnia, we weighted evidence from large-scale observational studies (Category I) more heavily. For gender-specific treatments, priority was given to randomized controlled trials (Category I [PCT]). Furthermore, additional relevant articles were identified through manual citation tracking to capture seminal studies that might have been omitted in the initial database search. This approach allows for a rigorous assessment of the strength of evidence across the multi-faceted causes and treatments discussed.

Mechanisms of gender disparity in insomnia

Biological factors

Hormonal variations

Hormones play crucial roles in regulating the balance between sleep and wakefulness. Women, in particular, experience significant hormonal fluctuations during specific periods, such as the menstrual cycle, pregnancy, and menopause, leading to pronounced difficulties in sleep maintenance and delayed sleep onset.

Women of reproductive age: menstrual cycle-related hormonal fluctuations

Animal models have been used to directly investigate the impact of reproductive hormones on sleep. Recent systematic reviews have reported that the removal of sex hormones eliminates sex differences in rodent sleep [22, 23]. Furthermore, these studies have shown that the differences that vanished after gonadectomy were restored upon readministration of estrogen and progesterone, suggesting that adult sex hormones are critical for sexual dimorphism in sleep behavior [22].

Research on the specific mechanisms underlying hormonal alterations during sleep has yielded consistent results. A systematic review of animal studies has reported that the administration of estrogen or estrogen with progesterone suppresses sleep and promotes wakefulness in females [22]. Specifically, experimental studies in female rats have shown that estradiol injection increases wakefulness, whereas the duration of rapid eye movement (REM) and non-REM sleep decreases [22, 24]. Although progesterone alone had little effect, when it was administered in conjunction with estrogen, sleep was significantly reduced, indicating that the sleep-suppressing effect was amplified when the two hormones acted together [22]. These findings from animal studies mirror those of human studies on the menstrual cycle [20, 22]. Growing evidence from longitudinal studies suggests that sleep architecture changes across the menstrual cycle, with most sleep disturbances occurring during the luteal phase [20]. During the luteal phase, when both estrogen and progesterone levels are high, women tend to experience increased sleep onset latency and awakenings and lower sleep efficiency and quality compared to the follicular phase [22]. Although progesterone has sedative properties through its GABAergic metabolites, its thermogenic effects and the arousal-promoting influence of estrogen may contribute to sleep disturbances during the luteal phase [19, 25, 26].

During pregnancy

Academic consensus has established that pregnancy is a significant etiological factor of insomnia, with dynamic recalibration of the hormonal system as the primary biological driver. A recent meta-analysis compiling data from multiple studies indicated that insomnia prevalence increased from approximately 25% in the first trimester to over 40% in the third trimester [27, 28].

A complex interplay of several key hormones contributes to the high prevalence of insomnia symptoms during pregnancy [20, 27, 29] While progesterone levels rise sharply in early pregnancy and exert a known soporific effect, this hormone concurrently disrupts sleep continuity [20, 29]. It acts as a respiratory stimulant, which can lead to unstable breathing during sleep and increase nocturnal arousals [30]. Furthermore, it elevates the core body temperature, which can interfere with the initiation and maintenance of deep restorative sleep [29]. High estrogen levels throughout gestation also contribute to insomnia symptoms. This hormone suppresses REM sleep and alters normal sleep architecture [20, 29]. Additionally, its vasomotor effects can lead to physical discomfort such as nasal congestion and leg cramps, which act as direct causes of nighttime awakening and thus impair sleep maintenance [30]. Other hormones essential for parturition and lactation, such as oxytocin and prolactin, contribute to a more fragmented sleep architecture and increased frequency of nocturnal awakenings. While prolactin has been associated with promoting deep sleep (slow-wave sleep), it also plays a dual role by facilitating maternal arousal for nighttime feeding, thereby interrupting consolidated sleep [20, 30]. Elevated estrogen levels and alterations in cortisol secretion rhythms during pregnancy may modify interactions with melatonin signaling, leading to a mismatch between sleep propensity and circadian timing signals [29, 31]. This circadian misalignment is thought to play a key role in exacerbating the difficulties associated with sleep initiation and nocturnal awakening.

As pregnancy progresses, placental corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH) is secreted in increasing amounts, which continuously activates the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis, the body’s central stress response system [31, 32]. This sustained HPA axis activation leads to a state of hypercortisolism, with total cortisol secretion increasing two to three times [31, 32]. Consequently, the typical diurnal rhythm of cortisol, which peaks in the morning and reaches a nadir at night, is blunted, resulting in relatively elevated evening levels [31]. Elevated nocturnal cortisol levels promote a state of physiological and cognitive hyperarousal that directly interferes with sleep initiation and maintenance [31, 32]. In support of this finding, a longitudinal study found that higher evening cortisol levels at 36 weeks of gestation were significantly correlated with anxiety symptoms, which in turn mediated a decline in sleep quality and exacerbated insomnia symptoms [33]. It is hypothesized that a vicious cycle may form, wherein poor sleep could lead to an abnormal increase in placental CRH, which in turn further stimulates the HPA axis, increases cortisol secretion, and ultimately worsens insomnia [31, 32].

These fundamental hormonal shifts act as a trigger, precipitating a range of secondary physical conditions that further disrupt sleep. A growing body of evidence suggests that the presence of a growing uterus affects bladder capacity, fetal movements, and positional discomfort, leading to frequent awakening and disrupted sleep quality during pregnancy [20, 27]. In addition, the prevalence of specific sleep-related disorders increases during pregnancy, leading to secondary insomnia. Iron deficiency, folate deficiency, and hormonal changes are considered the primary contributors to restless legs syndrome (RLS). According to a recent systematic review and meta-analysis, the prevalence of RLS in the third trimester of pregnancy is as high as 22.9% [34]. Concurrently, weight gain and hormone-induced edema of the upper airway increase the risk of obstructive sleep apnea (OSA). This risk may be further amplified in late pregnancy by estrogen-related nasal congestion, uterine enlargement with diaphragmatic elevation and reduced lung volumes, and progesterone-related changes in ventilatory control [35, 36]. A recent review reported that the prevalence of OSA can be as high as 37% in high-risk pregnant women, particularly those with obesity or chronic hypertension [37]. Clinically, pregnancy-related OSA tends to become more prominent in the third trimester, and has been associated with adverse obstetric outcomes, including gestational hypertension, preeclampsia, and gestational diabetes [35, 38, 39].

During perimenopause

The menopausal transition is a period in a woman’s life when the prevalence of insomnia increases sharply [40, 41]. Several studies have suggested that sleep disturbances during menopause are associated with extreme changes in follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) and estradiol levels, which may induce insomnia through two main pathways: an indirect pathway via vasomotor symptoms (VMS) and a direct pathway that affects the brain’s sleep-regulating systems [40, 42, 43].

The most well-known mechanism of menopausal insomnia is VMS, or hot flashes, triggered by hormonal changes [44, 45]. The physiological basis of VMS is hypothalamic dysfunction induced by estrogen withdrawal [45, 46]. When estrogen levels decline, the thermoregulatory center of the brain is disrupted, causing an exaggerated heat-dissipation response (hot flash) to even small changes in the core body temperature [45]. A recently published meta-analysis provided clear statistical support for this finding, showing that women experiencing hot flashes were 2.7 times more likely to suffer from sleep disturbances than those who did not [47]. In other studies, hot flashes were the major factor increasing the prevalence of insomnia disorders in middle-aged women, and the severity of hot flashes was correlated with an increased likelihood of persistent insomnia symptoms [44, 46]. The impact of VMS on sleep is associated with all aspects of perceived sleep disruption and contributes to poor sleep initiation and maintenance [44]. These subjective reports were substantiated by objective data from polysomnography (PSG). Many hot flashes are linked to total wakefulness after sleep onset (WASO) in PSG [46, 48].

During menopause

In addition, hormonal changes during menopausal transition directly affect the sleep regulation systems of the brain [40, 42]. A key factor contributing to sleep disturbances in menopauses is decline in estrogen and progesterone levels, which control sleep by modulating serotonin and GABA [40, 42]. Specifically, estrogen acts directly on sleep-regulating nuclei in the hypothalamus and influences the serotonin system, which is crucial for regulating the sleep–wake cycle [40]. Simultaneously, progesterone is well-regarded for its sedative properties, largely mediated by its metabolite, allopregnanolone. This metabolite acts on GABA and benzodiazepine receptors to induce sedative, anxiolytic, and hypnotic effects. Thus, the menopausal decline in progesterone levels results in a reduced endogenous GABAergic sleep-stabilizing effect [49]. The direct effects of these hormones can be reversed using hormone replacement therapy (HRT) [40, 43]. A randomized clinical study reported significant changes in sleep electrophysiological activity, including an increased frequency of REM sleep and reduced wake time, following administration of oral micronized progesterone [49]. Notably, a systematic review suggested that combined estrogen and progesterone therapy was associated with reduced sleep latency and fewer awakenings [40, 43]. Beyond insomnia symptoms, menopause is also a critical period for the emergence or worsening of OSA in women. Although OSA is less prevalent in premenopausal women than in men, its prevalence increases markedly after menopause and may approach that observed in men of similar age [50, 51]. As progesterone has respiratory-stimulant and upper-airway dilator effects, while estrogen may help maintain upper airway muscle tone, these hormonal changes may also increase vulnerability to upper airway collapse during sleep and susceptibility to OSA [52, 53]. In addition, postmenopausal changes in body fat distribution, particularly increased central adiposity, may further contribute to OSA risk [52, 54, 55].

Genetic and neural circuitry mechanism

Genetic risk factors

Recent large-scale genomic studies have shown that insomnia has a polygenic architecture involving the complex interplay of multiple genes. Notably, twin studies, a classic method in genetic research, suggest a substantial genetic basis for insomnia symptoms with differences between sexes [56, 57]. According to a prospective study by Lind et al. that followed twins for 10 years, the heritability of insomnia symptoms was estimated to be 38% in men and 59% in women, and this genetic influence remained stable over time [9]. Several large-scale genome-wide association studies have consistently identified the myeloid ectropic viral integration site 1 (MEIS1) gene as a core genetic risk factor for insomnia across the sexes [10, 56, 57]. Hammerschlag et al. further demonstrated that the lead single nucleotide polymorphism in MEIS1 (rs11385155) has a stronger association in women (odds ratio [OR] = 1.20) [57]. The differences in genetic architecture between men and women become clearer not only through the different effect sizes of common genes such as MEIS1, but also through genes that show sex-specific effects [56, 57]. Hammerschlag et al. identified a sex-specific gene network in females centered on MEIS1 consisting of homeobox transcription factors, suggesting that MEIS1 participates in a female-specific regulatory network that may contribute to sex differences in insomnia susceptibility [57]. Watanabe et al. demonstrated that genetic loci not detected in combined-sex analyses became significant when analyses were stratified by sex, identifying a genome-wide significant locus on chromosomes 7 and 9 in men and women, respectively [58]. These results suggested the presence of sex-specific genetic influences. Lane et al. further corroborated these findings by reporting sex-specific associations, with KRT8P18 and NT5C2 linked to insomnia in women, and CADM1 and SLC8A3 in men. Using linkage disequilibrium score regression, they showed that insomnia shares strong genetic correlations with depression, anxiety, and neuroticism, with these associations being more pronounced in females, suggesting a stronger genetic overlap between insomnia and psychiatric vulnerability [56].

Neutral circuitry differences

A pioneering study by Dai et al., which directly compared male and female patients with insomnia, found that all patient groups exhibited increased amplitude of low-frequency fluctuation (ALFF) primarily in the temporal and occipital lobes relative to healthy controls, indicating a shared hyperarousal mechanism characterized by heightened sensory processing and attentional sensitivity [14, 59]. However, sex-specific differences were also observed. Compared with healthy women, female insomnia patients demonstrated reduced ALFF in the limbic regions, including the left anterior cingulate cortex, left posterior cingulate/precuneus, and bilateral posterior cerebellum, as well as in key nodes of the default mode network (DMN), such as the bilateral medial prefrontal cortex and left inferior parietal lobule [59]. These alterations indicate functional impairments in the neural circuits involved in emotional regulation, self-referential processing, and autonomic control [60]. Recent findings by Zhang et al., focused exclusively on female patients with insomnia, further support and extend these observations [61]. Female patients showed decreased ALFF in the left posterior cerebellum and right lingual gyrus, and regional homogeneity analyses revealed dysfunction across major intrinsic networks, including the DMN and salience network [61]. In contrast, the brain activation pattern in male patients with insomnia is markedly different from that in female patients [59]. In a study by Dai et al., male patients with insomnia, compared to healthy men, showed decreased ALFF only in limited regions of the occipital cortex; notably, there were no significant ALFF differences in the prefrontal or limbic areas, which were prominent in women [59]. This indicates that for men, changes at the level of the sensory cortex are the primary feature rather than alterations in higher-order brain function circuits, such as emotional and cognitive control [62].

Psychophysiological mechanisms

Gender-specific differences in cognitive interpretation, emotional reactivity, and physiological sensitivity to stress have been proposed as key factors that disrupt sleep via different pathways, and explain the gender gap in insomnia. Accordingly, gender-related psychophysiological mechanisms of insomnia can be conceptualized across three domains: cognitive arousal, emotional arousal, and sleep reactivity. Although conceptually distinct, these domains are highly interactive and collectively play a central role in the onset and chronification of insomnia [8, 11, 14, 63, 64].

Cognitive and emotional arousal

A growing body of evidence suggests that cognitive arousal, a state of excessive and uncontrollable mental activity, is a key mechanism underlying insomnia. Emotional arousal perpetuates insomnia, referring to the subjective emotional reactions and heightened affective states that follow stress or the experience of sleep failure [11, 14, 64]. A previous study on adults with insomnia suggested that pre-sleep arousal manifests differently across genders [8, 63]. In men, cognitive arousal appears to be primarily linked to an internal sleep-related locus of control, indicating that the perceived pressure to regulate sleep plays a central role [8]. By contrast, women’s cognitive arousal is embedded within a broader network of cognitive and emotional processes. For women, internally attributing sleep difficulties is closely intertwined with elevated worry, negative affect, and depressive symptoms, reflecting a more diffuse cognitive–emotional activation pattern [8, 63]. That is, women with insomnia are not only preoccupied with worries about sleep but also exist in an emotionally sensitive and agitated state regarding sleep itself [11, 63].

Sleep reactivity

Sleep reactivity indicates the degree to which an individual’s sleep is disrupted by stress, and is considered a key vulnerability marker for insomnia development [65, 66]. A recent comprehensive review identified female sex, family history of insomnia, genetic factors, and environmental stress as key determinants of sleep reactivity [65, 67].

Sex differences in this trait have been analyzed directly in a large population-based twin study, which reported that women have substantially higher levels of sleep reactivity than men despite a similar underlying genetic architecture [66]. The same twin study concluded that the observed sex difference in sleep reactivity was more strongly influenced by non-shared environmental factors than genetic factors [66]. This finding suggests that gender-specific experiences and ways of processing social and psychological stress may have a significant impact on sleep sensitivity [12, 68].

Social and role-based influences

Regional, cultural, and ethnic influences on gender differences in insomnia

While gender differences in insomnia prevalence are consistently observed worldwide, the magnitude and statistical significance of these differences vary across regional and cultural contexts [6]. Meta-analytical evidence suggests that female predominance of insomnia is greater in North America and Europe than in East Asia [69, 70]. This variability underscores the role of sociocultural environments, including cultural norms, health perceptions, and gender role expectations, in either amplifying or attenuating biological sex differences [71, 72]. Furthermore, variations in diagnostic frameworks (e.g., DSM-5, ICD-10, and ICSD), self-perception, and symptom reporting may contribute to cross-national inconsistencies. These interpretations are further supported by a cross-national comparative study of Japan, South Korea, and Taiwan. This study showed that female sex significantly predicted health dissatisfaction in Korea (OR = 1.31, p = 0.044), but not in Japan (OR = 0.98, p = 0.888). Notably, Japanese adults showed a significantly lower age-adjusted prevalence of insomnia lasting more than two weeks and a substantially higher refusal rate for household income reporting compared with Korean and Taiwanese adults. These findings suggest cultural differences in social inhibition and reporting tendencies, which may contribute to gender differences in the prevalence of insomnia [73]. Therefore, comparative analyses across regions, ethnic groups, and social structures are essential to clarify how cultural and societal contexts shape gender-related vulnerabilities to insomnia.

Across ethnic groups, women also experienced a higher insomnia prevalence than men, but the disparities were greatest among racial/ethnic minorities. Black and Hispanic/Latina women reported shorter sleep duration and insomnia than White women. These differences were mainly attributed to higher exposure to racial discrimination, chronic psychosocial stress, and heightened physiological arousal, which disrupts sleep regulation [70, 71].

Social role–based gender differences in insomnia

Gender differences in insomnia often stem from differences in the social roles and burdens they entail. Numerous studies show a consistent pattern of how the imbalance in role expectations and responsibilities experienced by men and women in different domains—the workplace and the home—leads to gender differences in insomnia [17, 74, 75]. First, gender differences in insomnia can be understood within the context of gendered role demands across the workplace and home [17, 74]. Generally, for women, role conflict can be explained by the concept of work–family conflict. Conflict occurs when the demands of work and family roles cannot be met simultaneously. It is divided into work-to-family conflict (WFC), in which stress from work spills over to the family, and family-to-work conflict (FWC), in which stress from home spills over to the workplace [17]. Notably, the impact of this conflict shows clear gender differences. In high WFC, the OR for men was higher than that for women; however, in cases of high bidirectional conflict, the OR for women was higher than that for men [17]. This suggests that men are more vulnerable to stress originating in the workplace, whereas women are more vulnerable to complex stresses from both work and home [17, 75]. Sidani et al. explained the psychological basis for this phenomenon by analyzing how women’s attempts to meet work and home role expectations increase their psychological distress and limit their sleep time. Owing to this multi-role burden, women tend to experience higher pre-sleep arousal, which directly leads to difficulty in falling asleep [76].

Clinical implications

Comorbidities with higher prevalence by gender

Insomnia is associated with a wide range of psychiatric and physical health conditions [77]. Emerging evidence suggests that the strength of these associations may differ according to sex [5]. The impact of insomnia on disease risk and symptom severity appears to be more pronounced in women than in men [78].

Female predominance in mood disorders triggered by insomnia

Insomnia is more than just a lack of sleep; it is also a significant predictor of an individual’s mental health. Numerous studies have demonstrated its association with mood disorders, particularly depression. A large-scale meta-analysis by Li et al., which synthesized data from 172,077 participants across 34 cohort studies, found that individuals with insomnia symptoms had a 2.27 higher relative risk of developing future depression than those without [79]. Insomnia in women appears to be more likely to progress to depression due to gender-specific stress-related vulnerability. Women are more prone to cognitive and emotional hyperarousal following stress exposure, which may prolong negative affect and increase susceptibility to depression [80, 81]. Sleep loss impairs emotional regulation, intensifies negative effects and reduces positive effects, thereby facilitating the development of depressive symptoms [80].

Sleep-related breathing disorders: obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) as a secondary cause of insomnia during pregnancy and in the postmenopausal period

Sleep-related breathing disorder, particularly OSA, should be considered as secondary causes of insomnia in women during pregnancy and in the postmenopausal period. Although OSA has traditionally been regarded as a male-predominant disorder, it may be under-recognized in women because symptoms often overlap with insomnia, fatigue, mood disturbance, and menopausal complaints [82, 83]. Especially, given that the prevalence of OSA increases markedly during pregnancy and after menopause, it is important to carefully evaluate whether sleep disturbances during these periods may be attributable to OSA [82–85]. Treating insomnia without addressing coexisting OSA may leave an important physiological cause of sleep disruption untreated.

Fibromyalgia and chronic pain

Insomnia has been closely associated with chronic pain conditions and this relationship appears to be particularly pronounced in women, especially those with fibromyalgia [86]. With 94.7% of female patients reporting sleep disturbances, insomnia may increase vulnerability to pain amplification in women [87].

Several mechanisms can explain this gender-specific association. First, women exhibit distinct biological vulnerabilities; sleep deprivation is associated with greater reductions in pain thresholds in women than in men [88]. Women exhibit less efficient endogenous pain inhibitory capacity, and sleep disturbance or emotional arousal can further impair the descending pain modulation systems mediated by the serotonergic and noradrenergic pathways [89–91]. This is supported by longitudinal data showing that the risk ratio for fibromyalgia in women surges from 1.39 to 2.66 as insomnia symptoms increase [92].

Second, emotional dysregulation induced by insomnia serves as a key mediator that lowers pain thresholds in women [93]. Finan et al. highlighted that sleep debt diminishes positive affect and increases pain catastrophizing, which qualitatively worsens pain experience in female patients [89]. Essentially, the emotional distress triggered by lack of sleep disrupts inhibitory pain pathways, creating a vicious cycle of pain amplification [86, 93–95]. Consequently, baseline sleep quality remains the most significant predictor of pain severity at long-term follow-ups (β = 0.13) [93].

Gender-informed treatment approaches

Pharmacological approaches

The pharmacological approach to insomnia centers on three key systems [14, 96]. The orexin system plays a critical role in regulating sleep–wake cycles and maintaining wakefulness [97, 98]. Second, the GABA system is the main inhibitory neurotransmitter in the brain for sleep induction, and most hypnotics act as positive allosteric modulators of GABA-A receptors [14]. Finally, the HPA axis plays a pivotal role in the sexually dimorphic effects of chronic stress [14, 96]. Accordingly, we reviewed treatment methods classified according to these three mechanisms [96].

Hyperarousal and the orexin system

Neurobiological hyperarousal is the primary pathophysiology of insomnia. This refers to a state where the brain’s arousal-regulating systems are abnormally activated despite adequate sleep opportunities, leading to deficits in sleep onset and maintenance [14, 99]. Orexin, a neuropeptide secreted by the hypothalamus, is a pivotal regulator that stabilizes these wakeful states [97].

The orexin system in female patients with insomnia responds sensitively to fluctuations in sex hormone concentrations. Basic research using animal models suggests that estradiol exerts inhibitory tonic control in hypothalamic orexin neurons. However, the sharp decline in estrogen levels during the menopausal transition leads to the inhibition of this mechanism, resulting in the hyperactivation of orexin neurons. These biological shifts force the brain into a state of persistent hyperarousal, forming the pathological basis for chronic sleep maintenance difficulties and early morning awakenings [97, 99].

Given the central role of orexin-mediated hyperarousal, pharmacological strategies targeting the orexin system, particularly dual orexin receptor antagonists (DORAs), are of particular importance in the treatment of insomnia [98, 100]. Sex differences in the clinical efficacy of DORAs are primarily attributed to their pharmacokinetic characteristics. Phase 3 clinical data for suvorexant revealed that at equivalent doses, the area under the curve and maximum plasma concentration were approximately 17–25% higher in women than in men [98]. Sex-based differences in the activity of the primary metabolic enzyme CYP3A4 combined with lower systemic clearance in women are major mechanisms explaining the higher drug exposure observed in females [101]. In addition, suvorexant is a highly lipophilic drug; therefore, a relatively higher body fat percentage in women can significantly affect its volume of distribution [101]. These elevated exposure levels provide a mechanistic basis for the more pronounced improvements in WASO observed in the female patient population [100]. Therefore, it is important to consider these pharmacokinetic characteristics when prescribing DORAs to female patients [100].

The GABAergic inhibitory system

Traditional insomnia medications target GABA, the primary inhibitory neurotransmitter in the brain. The GABA system is particularly important in explaining the mechanisms of female insomnia because progesterone and its metabolites (such as allopregnanolone) are known to directly modulate the activity of GABA-A receptors [102–104]. That is, progesterone enhances GABAergic inhibition by increasing GABA-A receptor sensitivity and promoting neurosteroid-mediated chloride channel activity, thereby exerting sedative and sleep-stabilizing effects [102, 104]. Therefore, women’s responsiveness to GABAergic drugs can vary with hormonal changes, such as during the menstrual cycle or menopausal transition [104, 105]. Accordingly, clinicians may consider adjusting the timing or dosage of GABAergic medications in relation to hormonal fluctuations to optimize efficacy and minimize adverse effects [105, 106].

Hypothalamic-Pituitary -Adrenal (HPA) axis dysfunction and cortisol regulation

Women tend to have higher HPA axis reactivity to stress. This leads to increased cortisol secretion and central nervous system hyperarousal, which are the major mechanisms that exacerbates insomnia [107–109]. The role of reproductive hormones is particularly important, as gonadal hormones heavily interact with both glucocorticoid receptor expression and action, thereby contributing to this sex difference by directly participating in the regulation of the HPA axis [107, 110, 111]. Consequently, drugs that directly target HPA axis overactivity may be particularly effective in female patients with insomnia. These drugs could, for example, inhibit cortisol synthesis or modulate the sensitivity of the glucocorticoid receptor [112, 113]. A clinical study supports this hypothesis, showing that when a cortisol-lowering drug was administered, female patients with insomnia showed a significantly greater improvement in sleep efficiency than male patients. This suggests that female insomnia is more closely linked to HPA-axis dysfunction and indicates the potential use of cortisol levels or GR polymorphisms as biological markers to predict sex differences in treatment responses [108, 114].

Cognitive-behavioral & psychosocial intervention

Enhancing cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) for women

Cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) is the effective first-line non-pharmacological treatment for insomnia [115, 116]. However, considering that the rate of comorbidity of insomnia with depression and anxiety disorders is significantly higher in women than in men, an enhanced approach that reflects women’s psychological characteristics beyond the standard CBT-I is necessary [117, 118]. Beyond correcting dysfunctional beliefs about sleep, it is effective to expand cognitive restructuring to address ruminative thoughts related to daytime stressful events and to integrate mindfulness and acceptance to non-judgmentally notice and let go of anxious thoughts and feelings that arise at bedtime, thereby lowering pre-sleep arousal levels derived from negative emotions [13, 119].

An expansion of behavioral techniques is also required. By incorporating the principles of behavioral activation, a core component of depression treatment, into CBT-I, encouraging patients to plan and engage in activities that provide pleasure and a sense of accomplishment during the day, the vicious cycle of reduced positive affect due to sleep deprivation can be mitigated [120–122].

Social role & lifestyle modification

Lifestyle modifications for insomnia should consider the social roles and cultural contexts that are differentially imposed based on gender, going beyond individual willpower [123–125]. In particular, work-family conflict was significantly associated with a higher risk of insomnia symptoms, and as a result, they tended to experience more stress, worry/rumination, and depression/anxiety as perpetuating factors than men [126–128]. Therefore, for women, work–life boundary settings and emotional burden management should be carefully considered as part of insomnia interventions [129, 130]. Ultimately, individual lifestyle modifications can achieve the greatest effect when accompanied by policy and cultural support that alleviates gender-specific social role burdens [129, 130].

Conclusion

Insomnia in women is a complex, multifaceted process in which innate biological vulnerabilities are amplified by psychophysiological mechanisms and triggered by gender-specific social stressors. Consequently, future research and clinical practice related to insomnia must shift toward a gender-optimized paradigm that considers biological, psychological, and sociocultural factors.
