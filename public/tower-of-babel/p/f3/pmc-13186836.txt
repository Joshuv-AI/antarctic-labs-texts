Associations of Blood Soluble Fas and IGF-I with Risk of Hemorrhagic Stroke: The Circulatory Risk Communities Study (CIRCS)
Authors: Kenichi Ariyada, Tomoko Sankai, Kazumasa Yamagishi, Isao Muraki, Yuji Shimizu, Hironori Imano, Takeshi Tanigawa, Hiroyasu Iso
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAlthough several studies have examined the associations between apoptosis-related factors or growth factors and cardiovascular diseases, evidence on their prospective association with hemorrhagic stroke remains limited.MethodsWe conducted a nested case-control study within the large cohort of the Circulatory Risk in Communities Study in Japan. A total of 98 cases (60 intracerebral hemorrhage and 38 subarachnoid hemorrhage; 34 men and 64 women, aged 35 to 83 years at baseline) were included, along with 196 controls matched by age, sex, community, and year of sample collection. We measured serum levels of the soluble form of Fas and insulin-like growth factor-I among cases and controls. The odds ratios of total hemorrhagic stroke and its subtypes were calculated using conditional logistic regression analyses.ResultsThe median follow-up period from baseline to onset was 7.0 years. The multivariable odds ratios (95% confidence intervals) of total hemorrhagic stroke associated with a 1-standard deviation increment of soluble Fas (2.3 ng/mL) and insulin-like growth factor-I (40.5 ng/mL) were 0.93 (0.66–1.32) and 0.61 (0.41–0.89), respectively. The inverse association with insulin-like growth factor-I levels was similar for subarachnoid hemorrhage and intracerebral hemorrhage: the respective multivariable odds ratios were 0.53 (0.28–0.98) and 0.62 (0.37–1.04).ConclusionsThe present study identified an inverse association between insulin-like growth factor-I levels and the risk of hemorrhagic stroke.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12975-026-01447-4.

Introduction

Hemorrhagic stroke, including subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH) and intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH), accounts for approximately 40% of all strokes and tends to have a poorer prognosis than ischemic stroke [1, 2].

Apoptosis may contribute to the development of cardiovascular disease [3], and Fas is a major receptor for apoptosis-mediated cell death, usually located on the cell surface [4]. By contrast, insulin-like growth factor-I (IGF-I) is a polypeptide with a sequence homologous to insulin that influences vascular function and atherosclerosis through various mechanisms, including its anti-inflammatory and anti-apoptotic effects [5, 6]. In this context, we hypothesized that higher levels of blood soluble Fas (sFas) and lower levels of blood IGF-I would be associated with the risk of hemorrhagic stroke as well as other cardiovascular diseases.

Researching biomarkers is important for understanding the mechanisms of hemorrhagic stroke. Although several studies have examined the associations between apoptosis-related factors or growth factors and cardiovascular diseases, most have focused on coronary artery disease and ischemic stroke [7–16]. There is limited evidence on the prospective associations between these factors and the risk of hemorrhagic stroke.

To evaluate these biomarkers, we investigated the associations between serum sFas and IGF-I levels and the risk of SAH and ICH among middle-aged Japanese men and women using a nested case-control study design.

Methods

Study Population

We performed a prospective nested case-control study within the Circulatory Risk in Communities Study (CIRCS), an ongoing dynamic community cohort study initiated in 1963 [17]. The participants were from three rural communities: Ikawa Town in Akita Prefecture in northwestern Japan, the Noichi district in Konan City in Kochi Prefecture in southwestern Japan, and the Kyowa district in Chikusei City in Ibaraki Prefecture in northeastern Japan. The source cohort comprised a total of 11,444 participants (4356 men and 7088 women) aged 30 years or older who underwent blood sampling at the 1984–2003 baseline surveys. After excluding 246 participants with a history of stroke, a total of 11,198 participants remained for the analysis. Informed consent was obtained by community leaders or from individual participants verbally, which was common practice in Japanese communities at that time. The Ethics Committees of the University of Tsukuba approved the study procedures (Approval No. 86; December 26, 2011).

Case Selection

The first-ever incidences of stroke between April 1984 and March 2010 were ascertained through systematic active registration. Registration of stroke endpoints was conducted in the following three steps. First, cases with potential stroke onset were identified using various information sources, including death certificates, national insurance claims, official ambulance use records, and questionnaire records from community-based annual health check-ups. Second, we obtained information and completed stroke onset survey forms detailing neurological symptoms, medical history, and findings from diagnostic tests, including computed tomography and magnetic resonance imaging, based on hospital records. Finally, the diagnoses of SAH or ICH were made by two or more well-trained physicians according to imaging examinations and the criteria of the National Survey of Stroke [18], which require the presence of sudden or rapid-onset focal neurologic deficits lasting at least 24 h, or until death. During the study period, a total of 191 hemorrhagic stroke cases (134 ICH and 57 SAH) as first-ever strokes were registered.

Control Selection

These cases were matched with two controls by age (± 0 years), sex, community, and year of sample collection using the risk-set sampling, with controls selected from participants at risk at the time of case occurrence. Overall, a total of 98 cases (60 ICH and 38 SAH) that were fully matched with two controls were included (Fig. 1).

Baseline Examination

Blood samples were obtained at annual examinations before stroke onset, collected into plastic serum separator gel tubes, and the serum was separated by centrifugation within 30 min. The samples were stored at -80 °C in an ultracold freezer from each baseline until 2015. Serum sFas levels were measured by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay using a SpectraMax 340PC384 absorbance microplate reader (Molecular Devices, USA). Serum IGF-I levels were measured by electrochemiluminescence immunoassay using a Cobas 8000 e801 analyzer (Roche Diagnostics K.K., Japan). Samples from cases and their matched controls were analyzed together in 2015 to minimize potential batch effects. All assays were conducted at Kotobiken Medical Laboratories, Inc. (Tsukuba, Japan).

Participants were provided with a self-administered baseline questionnaire covering lifestyle factors such as smoking and drinking status (current, past, or never), as well as medical history, including prescriptions. Furthermore, participant data such as blood pressure, body measurements, and serum lipid levels were collected and made available through annual health check-ups conducted in community and workplace settings. Hypertension was defined as systolic blood pressure equal to or greater than 140 mmHg, diastolic blood pressure equal to or greater than 90 mmHg, and/or the use of antihypertensive medication. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight (kg) divided by height squared (m2). Serum total cholesterol was measured in the laboratory. According to the Osaka Medical Center for Health Science and Promotion, a member of the Cholesterol Reference Method Laboratory Network [19], the precision and accuracy of the lipid measurements in all laboratories were satisfactory. Fasting was defined as abstaining from all food and drink, except water, for more than 8 h. Diabetes mellitus was defined as a fasting glucose level equal to or greater than 126 mg/dL, a non-fasting glucose level equal to or greater than 200 mg/dL, and/or the use of antidiabetic medication.

Statistical Analysis

Analysis of covariance was used for continuous variables, while logistic regression was applied for categorical variables to test differences in baseline characteristics between cases and controls. Odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the risk of incident total hemorrhagic stroke, SAH, and ICH were calculated using conditional logistic regression analysis. We calculated the ORs for each tertile of sFas and IGF-I among controls, using the lowest tertile (T1) as the reference, as well as per 1-standard deviation (SD) increment. For clinical interpretability, we also estimated ORs per 10 ng/mL increment in IGF-I levels. The median of each tertile was used for calculating the p for trend. Multivariable ORs were calculated after adjusting for systolic blood pressure (continuous), antihypertensive medication use (yes or no), current smoking status (yes or no), and current drinking status (yes or no). We further adjusted for BMI (continuous), diabetes mellitus (yes or no), and total cholesterol (continuous). Additional analyses on ICH by hemorrhage location (lobar and deep ICH) were also conducted. We also conducted additional analyses stratified by the duration of sample storage (short and long), with the cutoff defined by the median storage duration (25 years). All p values for statistical tests were two-tailed, and p values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant. SAS (version 9.4; SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA) was used for all analyses.

Results

The median follow-up period from baseline to onset was 7.0 years, with 38 cases of SAH and 60 cases of ICH. Table 1 shows the baseline characteristics of cases and controls for total hemorrhagic stroke, SAH, and ICH. The proportions of men were 21.1% and 43.3% for SAH and ICH, respectively. The mean baseline and onset ages were higher for ICH (62.8 and 70.8 years) than for SAH (58.9 and 64.9 years). Both systolic and diastolic blood pressure levels were higher in cases than in controls for total hemorrhagic stroke and ICH. Similarly, the proportion of hypertension was also higher (71.4% versus 48.5% for total hemorrhagic stroke and 76.7% versus 48.3% for ICH). Mean levels of total cholesterol and sFas did not differ between cases and controls, whereas mean IGF-I levels tended to be lower in cases compared with controls for both SAH and ICH. Serum sFas levels were not associated with the risk of total hemorrhagic stroke, SAH, or ICH. Serum IGF-I levels were inversely associated with the risk of total hemorrhagic stroke: the multivariable OR (95% CI) for 1-SD increment was 0.61 (0.41–0.89), and that for 10 ng/mL increment was 0.88 (0.80–0.97) (Table S1). Additional adjustment for BMI, diabetes mellitus, and total cholesterol did not alter the association between IGF-I and the risk of hemorrhagic stroke (Table 2). Similar associations were observed for both SAH and ICH. The results were similar when ICH was classified by hemorrhage location, with multivariable odds ratios per 1-SD increase of 0.60 (95% CI, 0.33–1.09) for deep ICH and 0.62 (95% CI, 0.22–1.70) for lobar ICH.

Discussion

In this nested case-control study within a large prospective cohort of middle-aged Japanese adults, we found that a growth factor was inversely associated with the risk of hemorrhagic stroke, whereas no significant association was observed for an apoptosis-related factor. To our knowledge, this is the first study to evaluate the associations between serum sFas and IGF-I levels and the risk of total hemorrhagic stroke and its subtypes.

A pathological finding of SAH is the presence of cerebral aneurysms, which show disappearance of the internal elastic lamina; degeneration of the vascular wall, including loss of medial smooth muscle cells; and infiltration of inflammatory cells in the adventitia [20]. These changes are more pronounced in ruptured cerebral aneurysms [21]. Apoptosis of vascular wall cells has been suggested to contribute to the formation and rupture of cerebral aneurysms in basic research [22, 23]. Pathological findings of ICH are generally characterized by fibrinoid necrosis, lipohyalinosis, and Charcot-Bouchard aneurysms, which are caused by an increase in blood pressure affecting the vessel wall [24]. The contribution of apoptosis is well established in atherosclerosis but remains unclear in hypertensive arteriolosclerosis [25]. Conversely, an association between apoptosis and cerebral amyloid angiopathy, a principal cause of lobar hemorrhage, has been suggested in basic research [26]. A previous nested case-control study showed a positive association between sFas levels and the risk of mortality from SAH, but not ICH [27]. That study suggested a potential role of apoptosis-related factors in the development or prognosis of mortality from SAH, whereas evidence for a role of apoptosis in ICH has been limited. In the present study, no association was observed between serum sFas levels and the risk of either SAH or ICH. Circulating sFas may reflect systemic apoptotic processes and does not necessarily represent local apoptotic activity within the cerebrovascular wall; therefore, it may not adequately capture vascular wall apoptosis relevant to hemorrhagic stroke.

Several previous studies have suggested that IGF-I accelerates the proliferation of vascular smooth muscle cells and prevents their apoptosis [28, 29]. However, circulating IGF-I levels may also serve as a marker of systemic aging or frailty [30]. Another nested case-control study within the Japan Collaborative Cohort Study (55 ICH cases and 55 controls) reported that lower IGF-I levels were inversely associated with the risk of mortality from ICH (per 1-SD increase: OR 0.31, 95% CI 0.14–0.71) [31], which was consistent with (but somewhat stronger than) the present findings.

Strengths and Limitations

The strength of this study was that it was conducted within a large cohort of the general population in Japan, where the incidence of hemorrhagic stroke is relatively high. Our study was based on systematic surveillance for incident stroke and employed standardized epidemiological methods for measuring biomarkers.

This study has several potential limitations. First, only single-point measurements of biomarkers and risk factors at baseline were available, which could lead to regression dilution bias. Second, statistical power was limited, particularly in subtype analyses (SAH and ICH), location-specific analyses (deep and lobar ICH), and tertile-based analyses; therefore, these findings should be interpreted with caution. Nevertheless, these analyses may still provide meaningful insights into the association between IGF-I and hemorrhagic stroke, and possibly its underlying mechanisms. Third, although we adjusted for potential confounders, we cannot rule out residual confounding from unmeasured factors, such as antithrombotic medication use, which was not available in this study. Fourth, the study population was limited to Japanese residents, so generalizability should be considered with caution. Nevertheless, because biomarkers and risk factors for hemorrhagic stroke may be common across racial and ethnic groups, the evidence may provide a basis for extrapolation to other populations. Lastly, we used frozen serum samples to estimate sFas and IGF-I and did not examine long-term changes in the samples. A previous report showed that these biomarkers remained stable in serum samples stored for up to nine years at -70 °C in another Japanese cohort, where sample management methods were nearly identical to those in our study [32]. As additional analyses stratified by storage duration yielded similar results, it is unlikely that long-term storage affected the findings.

Conclusion

The present study found that blood IGF-I levels were inversely associated with the risk of total hemorrhagic stroke and its subtypes, suggesting IGF-I may reduce the risk of hemorrhagic stroke through its protective effects on blood vessels.
