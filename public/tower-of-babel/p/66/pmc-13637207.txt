Influencing surgical career choice among final-year medical students: a multi-center cross-sectional study in Türkiye

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe distribution of physicians among medical specialties have become increasingly misaligned to population healthcare needs, contributing to global shortages in several surgical fields.AimsOur aim was to analyze factors influencing specialty selection among final-year medical students across Türkiye.Materials-methodsThis multicenter cross-sectional study was conducted between May 2018 and July 2022. An anonymous online questionnaire was administered to final-year medical students in Türkiye, collecting data on sociodemographic characteristics, career intentions, and factors motivating or deterring interest in surgical specialties.ResultsA total of 910 final-year medical students (median age: 24, IQR: 23–25) completely filled our survey. Approximately half of the participants reported interest in pursuing a surgical specialty. Male students were more likely to pursue surgery than female students (p < 0.001). Plastic and reconstructive surgery (12.6%) was the most desired surgical field, followed by ear nose and throat (11.4%). More than half of the students who had a surgeon in the family preferred surgical residency. The COVID-19 pandemic was not associated with a change in overall specialty selection but had a significant impact on motivators and deterrents from surgical specialties. The most common factors motivating students to pursue surgical specialties were prestige, high income expectation and dynamic nature of surgical fields, while deterrents were largely structural, including poor work-life balance and gender inequality.ConclusionInterest in surgical fields appears to be shaped by a balance between professional rewards and systemic deterrents. Addressing modifiable training- and system-level barriers may be critical to sustaining future interest in surgical careers.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12909-026-09873-6.Keywords: Surgical career, Türkiye, Medical student, Survey

Introduction

The distribution of physicians across specialties is increasingly misaligned with population needs and shortages in core specialties such as surgery, internal medicine, and pediatrics have increased over the past decade globally [1]. Concurrently, medical students are increasingly drawn to specialties associated with more controllable lifestyles such as dermatology, ophthalmology, and aesthetics surgery. Global projections from the Association of American Medical Colleges estimate a shortage of 13,500 − 86,000 physicians by 2036, including 20,200 − 40,400 in primary care and 10,100 − 19,900 in surgical specialties [2]. While some students pursue their childhood dreams, for many others speciality preference forms during medical school. Specialty choice not only influences one’s career (and life) satisfaction, but can also impact the composition of the healthcare force in the upcoming decades. Declining interest in disciplines such as general surgery have jeopardized healthcare systems [3] as such specialties have largely been associated with rigorous and (often unpredictable) working hours, high risks, and high burnout rates. Many soon-to-be physicians cite factors including predictable hours, work-life balance, high compensation, and professional satisfaction as the main determinants of medical specialty choice, rendering some surgical specialties less desirable. Other factors that influence speciality decision-making include personal or demographic, motivational, and cultural factors. In addition, global trends in education as well as unprecedented challenges of the time can impact career-decision for medical students. Following the COVID-19 pandemic, parameters such as work-life balance and expected income have also become increasingly central to specialty decisions. The pandemic exposed the vulnerability of healthcare systems as many physicians were overworked, leading to widespread burnout [4, 5]. Thus, following the pandemic, a growing number of medical students have shown interest for specialties allowing a greater flexibility of working hours. In contrast, interest in surgical fields has continued to decline owing to extensive hours and poor work-life balance.

Here, we characterize the factors affecting the choice of specialty for final-year medical students between 2018 and 2022 in Türkiye, a country where interest in surgical specialties has decreased significantly over the past decade. Understanding and analyzing the factors deterring students from pursuing careers in surgery can guide the development of better programs in medical school and residency.

Methods

Study design & participants

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, approved by the Institutional Review Board and Ethics Committee of Koc University (2018.119.IRB3.084), and all surveys were completed anonymously and voluntarily (Supplementary Fig. 1). Between 2018 and 2022, final-year medical students in seven medical schools (Koç University School of Medicine, Hacettepe University Faculty of Medicine, Cerrahpaşa Faculty of Medicine, İstanbul University Faculty of Medicine, Ankara University Faculty of Medicine, Gazi University Faculty of Medicine, University of Health Sciences, Gülhane Medical Faculty) across Türkiye were invited to answer a questionnaire about their future careers as residents via an online survey platform (Supplementary Fig. 2). Participants were asked about their preference for a specialty and how they rated the influence of motivational factors on their choice. The online questionnaire was distributed to 1003 final-year students who consented to participate in the study and then received the survey link. Of these 1003 students, 910 students completed the questionnaire in full. The response rate was 91% (N = 910); 52.6% were female students (N = 479), and 47.4% were male students (N = 431).

The questionnaire

The study utilized a structured, self-administered online questionnaire developed following a review of prior studies examining medical students’ specialty preferences and career related decision-making. The questionnaire consisted of 4 domains: (1) sociodemographic and education-related characteristics, (2) career intentions and interest in surgical residency, (3) factors motivating interest in surgical fields, and (4) factors deterring pursuit of surgical fields. Sociodemographic variables and core career intention items (e.g. consideration of surgical residency abroad) were collected using single-choice questions. Academic self-ranking was assessed using pre-defined percentile categories. Motivating and deterrent factors influencing specialty selection were assessed using multiple-response questions, allowing participants to select all applicable options. For students expressing interest in surgical fields, the degree to which specific factors influenced their decision was evaluated using a three-point Likert-type scale (“do not agree”, “agree to some extent”, and “completely agree”). For analytical clarity, deterrent items were grouped into conceptually coherent domains: (1) Workload and work-life balance (e.g., long working hours, number of shifts, lack of post-call rest), (2) Training quality and structure (e.g., insufficient hands-on training, lack of standardization), (3) Legal-financial risk (e.g., malpractice concerns, income-risk imbalance), (4) Workplace culture and hierarchy (e.g., bullying, non-objective hierarchy), (5) Gender inequality, (6) System-level factors (e.g., violence against physicians, surgeon migration abroad), and (7) Educational environment in medical school. Individual item frequencies were retained in the results tables to preserve transparency.

The sociodemographic questions included information such as the age and gender of the students and the presence of a physician or surgeon in their family (Supplementary Fig. 2). The students were asked about the geographical location of their medical schools and the characteristics of their institution (whether it was a foundation or a state university). The students were asked about their motivations for entering medical school, confidence in succeeding on their first Turkish Residency Exam, and aspirations to pursue residency abroad. They were also asked about their interests for surgery and their desired field of further training. Participants who showed no desire to pursue surgical specialties were asked about their reasons to pick non-surgical specialties (one of the 24 reasons for not preferring a surgical field). Those interested in surgery were asked about several factors: at what age they decided to pursue a surgical field, their preferred surgical specialty, whether fellowships or further surgical training was available in their surgical branch, and the requirement for additional mandatory service requirement. They were also asked to compare residency training in Türkiye versus abroad. Finally, those who wanted to choose a surgical specialty were asked why they wanted to choose Surgery, and they were asked to choose the most suitable one from nine different answers.

COVID-19 related survey items and cohort definitions

The core questionnaire remained consistent throughout the study period. Additional items assessing the perceived impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on specialty selection were introduced (Supplementary Fig. 3). Participants were categorized into pre-COVID (academic years prior to March 2020) and post-COVID (March 2020 and onwards) cohorts.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were utilized to summarize the participating students’ sociodemographic characteristics and school-related variables. Continuous variables were presented as means and standard deviations, while categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages. Chi-square tests were employed to examine associations between categorical variables, including gender differences in career preferences and motivations for entering medical school. A significance level of p < 0.05 was set for all statistical tests. Data analysis was performed using SPSS version 28.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY).

We conducted a multivariable binary logistic regression to evaluate factors independently associated with considering a surgical specialty. Covariates were selected a priori and included age, sex, university type (state vs. foundation/private), perceived academic performance percentile (top 5%, top 10%, top 25%, ≥ 50%), intention to pursue residency abroad, COVID period (pre vs. post), and presence of a surgeon among first- or second-degree relatives. Results are presented as adjusted odds ratios (aORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

Specialty choice of students expressing interest in surgery

Perspectives on continuing their surgical training at a foreign institution also showed variation with 32% of the respondents expressing that they believed receiving surgical training abroad would provide them with better learning conditions compared to institutions in Turkiye. A majority of students showed interest in surgical fields because they believed surgery enabled patients to experience a faster resolution of problems. The second most important factor motivating students to pursue surgery was the dynamic and fast-paced nature of surgical fields. Prestige and the idea that patients respect surgeons more than other physicians as well as high-income expectations were other factors commonly impacting the decision to pursue surgical residency.

Plastic, Reconstructive, and Aesthetic Surgery was the most desired residency among participants who intend to specialize in a surgical discipline, with 12.6% of participants indicating their interest for the field. Following that, Ear, Nose, and Throat (ENT) surgery ranked second with a selection rate of 11.4% among the participants, while General Surgery secured the third position with a selection rate of 9.9%.

Residency preferences of students expressing interest in surgery

Tables 7 and 8 represent factors influencing specialty preferences for male and female students. Surgical vs. nonsurgical career

The data presented in Table 9 compares the perspectives of individuals considering specializing in surgical fields with those who prefer nonsurgical specialties. The presence of a surgeon in first- and second-degree relatives does not seem to influence specialty selection (p = 0.13). Similarly, having a medical doctor in the family did not significantly impact the student’s choice for surgical versus non-surgical specialty selection (p = 0.41). Main motivations for starting medical school differed significantly between the two groups (p = 0.01). Those considering a surgical residency are more likely to be motivated by the prestige associated with the profession (36.9%) compared to their colleagues without a desire to major in Surgery (31.6%). In multivariable analysis (Table 10), male sex was independently associated with higher odds of considering a surgical specialty (aOR 1.56, 95% CI 1.19–2.04; p = 0.001). Factors such as age, sex, university type (state vs. foundation/private), perceived academic performance percentile (top 5%, top 10%, top 25%, ≥ 50%), intention to pursue residency abroad, COVID period (pre vs. post), and presence of a surgeon among first- or second-degree relatives did not demonstrate an independent association with considering a surgical specialty after adjustment.

PreCOVID vs. postCOVID

The data presented in the survey also provides the opportunity to observe possible changes in the participating students’ sociodemographic characteristics and school-related variables in the pre- and post-COVID periods (Table 11). Considering residency abroad, there is a marginal increase in the percentage of students interested in pursuing residency abroad in the post-COVID period (49.3%) compared to the pre-COVID period (43.9%); the difference is not statistically significant (p = 0.14). A similar trend is observed in the consideration of surgical residency: 50.4% in the post-COVID period compared to 52.7% in the pre-COVID period (p = 0.53). There is a subtle change in terms of the perception of better education abroad: While 68.8% believed in better conditions in the pre-COVID period, this rate was 60.5% in the post-COVID period (p = 0.1), which is not statistically significant.

A comparison of factors affecting students choosing surgical careers between pre- and post-COVID periods reveals notable motivation changes (Table 12). More students were motivated by high-income expectations in the post-COVID (40.8%) period compared to the pre-COVID period (24.3%). Similarly, surgeons’ desire for prestige and recognition increased from 35.9% to 46.4%. On the other hand, the importance attributed to academic expectations experienced a significant decrease, falling from 26.4% before COVID-19 to 7.9% in the post-Covid period. Despite these changes, the dynamic and fast-paced nature of surgery remained the most common factor influencing students’ career choices and remained unchanged between the pre-COVID and COVID periods.

Discussion

The study addresses gaps in the literature regarding specialty preferences and motivations for pursuing surgical specialties in final-year Turkish medical students. This also presents a rare opportunity to compare career choices and motivations across genders. The impact of sociodemographic factors, including familial backgrounds, on career preferences were also compared. Furthermore, this study examines the effects of the COVID-19 pandemic on students’ career decisions in view of the interface of global events and individual career aspirations.

Motivators and deterrents of surgery

Our survey results provide valuable insights into final-year Turkish medical students’ demographic characteristics, motivations, and career preferences, highlighting many important issues. Motivators were predominantly intrinsic and professional including faster patient response times, the dynamic nature of surgery, perceived prestige, and high income expectations. In contrast, deterrents were largely systemic and structural, centering around workload intensity, lack of work-life balance, malpractice risk, and negative training environments. These findings aligned strongly with findings from literature where academic interests, controllable lifestyles, mentors, career opportunities, income, and prestige have been identified as factors influencing specialty selection [6]. Importantly, this divergence suggests that declining interest in surgery may be driven less by the nature of surgical work itself but rather on modifiable training and health-system factors, highlighting opportunities for targeted educational interventions. Accordingly, interventions aimed at optimized training environments and strengthening mentor-mentee relationships may support medical student interest in surgical fields. In our cohort, Plastic, Reconstructive and Aesthetic surgery was the most frequently selected surgical specialty. This could be explained by students’ perception that plastic surgery can combine high prestige, high earning potential, procedural variety, predictable work-life balance. Several studies in literature have also identified that students are drawn to stimulating and flexible careers, qualities they commonly associate with plastic surgery [7, 8]. Many surgical specialties inherently possess procedural variety and high prestige, however, earning potential as well as training and workplace environments differ significantly among specialties. Student interest in other surgical fields can be supported by implementing structural changes and enabling high earning potential, more flexible career pathways, and stronger work-life balance in various less-desired surgical specialties.

Gender and career choices

The gender distribution among medical students reveals a balanced representation, with 52.6% identified as female and 47.4% as male. This aligns with existing literature on gender distribution in medical education [9]. However, our study found a statistically significant difference between male and female students’ preference for surgery as a career. Female students were less likely to prefer surgery than male students, raising questions about whether certain aspects of surgery appeal to males or if external factors influence this gender distribution [10].

Our results align with other findings in literature. Global studies have also shown that women are significantly underrepresented in surgical specialties. Significant gender disparities seen in fields like orthopedics and general surgery, with women comprising 22% of general surgeons in the US and 18% in the UK [11–13]. Such trends have been explained partly by societal gender norms and expectations as well as implicit and explicit biases from clinicians, educators, and patients [14]. Despite female surgeons having fewer post-operative complications, studies have shown that women earn significantly less than men in surgery [15, 16]. In addition, unequal distribution of home obligations, disproportionate funding, pay gaps, higher rates in mistreatment, less surgical autonomy during residency and fewer opportunities in senior leadership has historically deterred women from pursuing surgical fields [17]. In our study 44% of females compared to 8% of males listed gender inequality as their reason for not pursuing surgical fields. Both females and males listed long working hours, high risk nature, and lower work-life balance as other factors deterring them from pursuing surgery.

Our results also showed that there were some gender differences in the surgical specialties preferred by students. For example, OBGYN is the most preferred specialty among female students, and this is similar to the study done in an Australian medical school [18]. The results that more female students than male students choose OBGYN can be explained by the fact that the number of female doctors in these specialties has consistently been higher than male doctors for years [19]. These trends could be explained by several factors. Women could have more same-sex mentors, motivating their interests to pursue OBGYN. Male medical students in OBGYN rotations more commonly report being excluded from clinical opportunities and may have less clinical exposure, thereby leading to lower interest for the field [20].

Influence of having a relative in medicine and surgery

Our survey shows gender differences in familial background among medical students. Although the likelihood of a family history of being a medical doctor is similar for both male students and female students, a significant disparity emerges when it comes to surgeons in first- or second-degree relatives. A higher percentage of male students (43.1%) reported having surgeon relatives compared to female students (29.5%), and this difference was statistically significant (p = 0.017). This could suggest that potential mentors in surgical fields may direct male students toward surgical career paths. The influence of role models within the family, particularly in surgical fields, could be a critical factor in shaping career goals [10]. Contrary to prior studies suggesting a positive correlation between having a surgeon in the family and pursuing a surgical specialty after medical school, a statistically significant difference was not observed in our cohort. This disparity might reflect a contextual shift in student priorities in choosing a specialty where a greater emphasis is placed on work-life balance, predictable schedules, and workplace environment. Implementing enforceable weekly-hour limits and guaranteed post-call rest may directly address some work-life balance concerns deterring students from pursuing surgical fields.

The data presented also provides the opportunity to compare the perspectives of individuals considering specializing in surgical fields with the perspectives of those who prefer nonsurgical specialties. According to our survey results, there is no significant difference between the two groups, whether one of their family members is a non-surgical physician or surgeon. In contrast, other studies in literature have shown that both having a surgeon in the family and having a family member from medicine have statistically significant effects on choosing surgical specialities [10]. Additionally, the significant difference in motivations for starting medical school (p = 0.01) between those considering surgical and nonsurgical residency is important. This indicates that intrinsic motivations to choose medicine as a profession significantly vary and could be a key factor in the decision-making process for future surgical professions.

Pre-COVID vs. post-COVID

The COVID-19 pandemic marked an important event in medical history, exposing the fragility of healthcare systems and shifting perceptions about specialty selection. Understaffed healthcare systems compelled physicians into working extended hours under immense psychological burden [4, 5]. The increased burnout rate among physicians during and following the pandemic revealed the poor work-life balance in medicine, prompting many medical students to place a greater emphasis on achieving a greater work-life balance in their future career. Although the COVID-19 pandemic has widely been discussed as a driver of shifts in medical specialty selection, our findings suggest that its impact was more pronounced on motivational factors than on overall specialty choice. Despite the lack of statistical significance, our findings suggest a consistently high level of interest in pursuing residency abroad among medical students in Türkiye, alongside a prevailing perception that training opportunities outside Türkiye may offer advantages. These advantages could include perceptions of local training conditions, work-life balance concerns, and economic considerations among other factors. Notably, this perception appears to have modestly declined in the post-COVID-19 period, which may reflect evolving views on global training systems or increased confidence in local training environments.

While the proportion of students interested in surgical specialties did not differ significantly between pre- and post-COVID cohorts, analysis of factors influencing students’ surgical career choices highlighted a significant increase in the importance of high-income expectations and the desire for prestige among surgeons post-COVID period. This aligns with studies from Great Britain and countries from West Africa and Middle East [21–23]. Conversely, the diminishing importance of academic expectations leads to the consideration of shifting priorities, as also reported by previous studies [24, 25]. Similarly, the notable increase in the “risk imbalance rate under malpractice laws” shows evolving concerns, signaling potential shifts in social and economic impacts. As stated in the literature, both results demonstrate that the COVID-19 pandemic significantly impacted career choice, career perspective, and ideal workplace [26–28]. However, regardless of the COVID-19 pandemic, lack of a monotonous work life remained an important factor motivating final-year students to pursue surgical fields. Excessive working hours and poor work-life balance were some of the most important factors deterring students from surgical fields both in the pre- and post-COVID periods, consistent with previous literature [28, 29]. Notably, poor work-life balance and long, unpredictable hours have been cited as the primary deterrents across literature for years. It has been well established that chronically overworked residents face higher burnout rates, discouraging future physicians from pursuing surgical fields.

Türkiye-specific context

Türkiye-specific deterrents, particularly violence against physicians and emigration of surgeons (and physicians), emerged as prominent concerns influencing medical student decisions. Rising incidents of violence against healthcare workers have been documented in Türkiye and globally and an increased number of doctors in Türkiye have emigrated to other countries in the past few years [30, 31]. With 47.9% and 35.3% of students expressing concern regarding the increased violence and emigration of doctors, respectively, in the post-COVID period, our results demonstrate that outward migration and concerns over safety might reinforce negative perceptions regarding professional security, training continuity, and long-term career prospects (Table 13). In our cohort, perceptions of non-objective surgical hierarchy also emerged as a prominent deterrent to pursuing careers in surgery, reflecting concerns about fairness and transparency within training environments. While hierarchical structures are inherent to surgical environments globally, their perceived lack of objectivity may be particularly prominent in the context of Türkiye. Considered alongside other system-level challenges such as workplace violence and physician migration, this finding may suggest that the organizational structure and training conditions play a critical role in shaping medical student decisions. Enhancing transparency, accountability, and mentorship within surgical hierarchies may present a potential point for intervention in surgical training.

Limitations

Our study has certain limitations. The cross-sectional design makes it more difficult to prove a relationship between the factors investigated and career decisions. The lack of standardized quantitative data has limited our analysis. For instance, reliably recorded working-hour data is inadequate and unmeasured overtime further restricts our ability to quantitatively assess the impact of workload on specialty preference. Due to the concise design of the questionnaire, certain factors influencing specialty choice such as perceived personal fit and preference for procedural work were implied but not explicitly assessed. Because the sample is restricted to final-year medical students in Türkiye, self-reported data may be prone to response biases and not generalizable to other populations. Furthermore, regional variations within Türkiye were not considered, which could impact career decisions because of different local circumstances. In order to establish a solid understanding of medical students’ career choices, future research should incorporate longitudinal studies, a wider range of diverse student samples, and more thorough assessments of contributing factors. In addition, data collection was achieved using a self-administered questionnaire. Responses may have been influenced by social desirability bias, with participants potentially overreporting socially valued motivations or underreporting stigmatized concerns related to specialty selection. Although the survey was anonymous, this bias cannot be fully excluded and should be considered when interpreting the findings. Finally, the questionnaire was not validated using psychometric methods. Although it was developed based on a review of prior literature and major domains examined in studies exploring medical specialty selection, the absence of formal validation may limit precision and generizability of results. Finally, the lack of more recent data beyond 2022 limits our ability to assess the longer-term impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on specialty preferences.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this comprehensive study on the surgical specialty preferences and motivations of final-year Turkish medical students fills a critical gap in the existing literature by providing a multifactorial analysis of demographic characteristics, familial influences, and motivational factors that shape their surgical career choices. This study also highlights post-COVID shifts in career considerations. Findings reveal actionable points of structural intervention in medical education and training. Policy measures ensuring safer and more sustainable working conditions, transparent training pathways, and early mentorship opportunities can help improve perceptions of surgical careers.
