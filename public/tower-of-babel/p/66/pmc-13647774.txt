Incidence, mortality and risk factors of invasive pulmonary aspergillosis in critically ill patients during the COVID-19 era: a global systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Zijing Zhou, Bin Luo, Xiwen Zhang, Qing Li, Yingzi Huang, Yi Yang, Haibo Qiu, Ming Xue, Jianfeng Xie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundInvasive pulmonary aspergillosis (IPA) represents a growing threat to critically ill patients, with mortality rates exceeding 50%. The burden of IPA has been exacerbated during the COVID-19 era due to prolonged ICU stays and widespread use of immunosuppressive therapies. This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to synthesize global evidence on the incidence, mortality, and risk factors of IPA in ICU patients.MethodsObservational studies reporting IPA incidence, mortality, or risk factors in ICU settings from their inception between November 1, 2019 to October 11, 2024 for eligible studies were retrieved from PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and Cochrane Library. Study quality was assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS). Meta-analyses were performed using R software to calculate pooled estimates with 95% confidence intervals (CI). Multiple subgroup analysis was conducted.ResultsFifty-six studies involving 15,385 ICU patients (2,381 IPA cases) were included. The pooled incidence of IPA was 11.9% (95% CI: 9.7–14.6%). Subgroup analyses revealed higher incidence in mechanically ventilated patients (15.0%, 95% CI: 11.7–19.0%). Factors associated with an increased risk of IPA included male sex (OR = 1.29), smoking history (OR = 1.52), chronic lung diseases (OR = 1.30), chronic kidney disease (OR = 1.70), chronic liver disease (OR = 1.79), heart disease (OR = 1.20), malignant tumor (OR = 1.75), hematological malignancy (OR = 2.65), glucocorticoids use before ICU (OR = 2.13), immunosuppression (OR = 2.26), EORTC host factors (OR = 2.13), vasopressor use (OR = 3.28), mechanical ventilation (OR = 3.69), and renal replacement therapy (OR = 2.31). The overall mortality rate was 59.6% (95% CI: 55.0–64.0%), escalating to 61.9% in mechanically ventilated patients. Mortality-associated factors included age ≥ 65 years (OR = 2.85), ICU-administered glucocorticoids (OR = 1.95) and mechanical ventilation (OR = 1.93).ConclusionsDuring the COVID-19 era, IPA remains a life-threatening complication in critically ill patients, particularly those with COVID-19, and is associated with immunosuppression, organ dysfunction, and invasive therapies. Our findings, predominantly reflecting CAPA epidemiology, generate the hypothesis that early screening in high-risk populations and tailored antifungal strategies warrant prospective evaluation to determine whether they improve outcomes.Trial registrationEthical approval and patient consent were not required for this meta-analysis, as it was based on previously published studies. The protocol for this meta-analysis was registered on PROSPERO: International prospective register of systematic reviews (CRD42025631579).Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12941-026-00878-z.

Background

Invasive pulmonary aspergillosis (IPA) remains a formidable challenge in the realm of critical care, with mortality rates exceeding 50% despite advancements in antifungal therapies [1]. The primary risk factors for IPA include immunosuppressive conditions (e.g., hematological malignancies, organ transplantation, long-term use of corticosteroids, and immunosuppressive agents) and severe respiratory viral infections (e.g., influenza and respiratory syncytial virus pneumonia), which often lead to respiratory failure and prolonged mechanical ventilation [2, 3].

Between 2020 and 2024, especially since COVID-19 pandemic, the landscape of respiratory viral infections in ICUs has undergone significant changes compared to the period before. During this time, the dominant pathogens shifted from seasonal influenza, respiratory syncytial virus, and others to SARS-CoV-2, leading to a surge in ICU admissions and overwhelming healthcare systems [4]. The pathophysiology of SARS-CoV-2 infection, characterized by direct pulmonary epithelial and endothelial damage, immune dysregulation, and a hypercoagulable state, created a fertile environment for secondary fungal infections [5]. Additionally, the widespread use of immunomodulatory therapies, such as high-dose corticosteroids, IL-6 receptor antagonists, and JAK inhibitors, further increased the risk of IPA in critically ill patients [6]. These changes not only increased the incidence of IPA but also altered the traditional risk factor profile, with patients lacking pre-existing immunodeficiencies now representing a significant proportion of the high-risk population.

The diagnosis and management of IPA during 2020–2024 also introduced significant heterogeneity. New diagnostic criteria, such as those released by the ECMM/ISHAM for COVID-19-associated pulmonary aspergillosis in 2020, added complexity to the interpretation of IPA incidence and mortality rates [7]. Moreover, the widespread use of bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) and advanced molecular diagnostic techniques, such as metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS), likely increased detection rates [8]. While these diagnostic advancements are beneficial, they necessitate careful subgroup analyses in meta-analyses to avoid biased pooled results. Additionally, the “immunity debt” phenomenon observed in 2023–2024, characterized by more severe and atypical seasonal outbreaks of other respiratory viruses, has posed new challenges for IPA risk stratification [9, 10]. The following impacts on IPA incidence, mortality, and risk factors remain controversial. It is important to acknowledge that the persistently high mortality of IPA may not solely stem from diagnostic or preventive shortcomings. Therapeutically, no novel antifungal class with clear superiority over established first-line agents (e.g., voriconazole, isavuconazole) has emerged in the last two decades. This therapeutic plateau may structurally limit the extent of mortality reduction achievable through early detection alone, underscoring the need for both improved diagnostics and novel therapeutic strategies. Therefore, this meta-analysis aims to elucidate the epidemiological changes in IPA during the 2020–2024 period and compare them with data from before 2019 to highlight new challenges and opportunities for intervention in the ICU setting. It is important to note that the vast majority of patients included in studies from this period (2020–2024) were diagnosed with COVID-19. Therefore, this meta-analysis primarily reflects the epidemiology and risk profile of COVID-19-associated pulmonary aspergillosis (CAPA), and its findings should be interpreted with caution when generalizing to classical IPA in non-COVID critically ill populations.

Methods

Approval

This present study was registered with PROSPERO (CRD 42025631579) and conducted following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [11]. Ethical approval and patient consent are unavailable.

Literature search strategy

We conducted a comprehensive literature search in the PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and Cochrane Library databases between 31 Dec 2019 and 11 Oct 2024.The search terms included but were not limited to: “Intensive Care Unit”, “critically ill patient”, “Pulmonary Fungal Disease”, “Pulmonary Aspergillosis”. The detailed search strategy is provided in Table S1.

The titles and abstracts were independently screened to determine whether a study should be included by two authors (MX and ZZ) independently. Then the full texts were reviewed according to the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Any discrepancies were resolved by a consensus on the inclusion or exclusion of a study after a discussion with a third author.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

We included trials with the following features: [1] observational studies (cohort, case-control, or cross-sectional) reporting IPA incidence, mortality, or risk factors in ICU patients [2], confirmed IPA diagnosis per EORTC/MSG criteria or validated biomarkers (e.g., serum galactomannan). The diagnostic criteria for IPA were clearly provided, including but not limited to the criteria listed in Table S2 [3]. study period commencing on or after November 1, 2019.

We excluded trials with the following features: [1] non-original research: reviews, case reports, observational studies with fewer than 50 participants, non-peer-reviewed studies (including conference abstracts, letters, and guidelines), and animal/in vitro experiments [2]. accessibility issues: Studies for which the full text was not available or complete data could not be obtained [3]. duplicate publications: studies that were duplicate reports of previously published data. Case series (with n ≥ 50) were included to capture early pandemic data comprehensively. Their potential for selection bias was assessed through a subgroup analysis based on study design (cohort/case-control vs. case series), as presented in the results.

Data extraction and quality assessment

All available data including characteristics of the selected studies, details of the population enrolled including study design, country or region, study period, sample size, patient demographics, diagnostic criteria of IPA, incidence, potential risk factors and mortality were independently extracted by two investigators (MX and ZZ). Mortality data were extracted with a preference for in-hospital all-cause mortality; when unavailable, ICU mortality was used. Organ support variables (mechanical ventilation, vasopressor use, renal replacement therapy) were collected as documented in the primary studies, but data on their precise timing relative to IPA diagnosis were inconsistently reported. Disagreements between the two investigators were resolved by a consensus after discussing with a third author (XZ). The quality of the included studies using the Newcastle-Ottawa scale (NOS) for observational studies was rated [12], with scores ≥ 7 indicating high quality (Table S3). Low-quality studies (NOS score < 7) were included in the primary meta-analyses to maximize data comprehensiveness. Their potential influence was assessed through prespecified sensitivity analyses.

Statistical analysis

The pooled incidence and mortality rates of IPA in ICU patients were calculated using double arcsine transformation, with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Multiple subgroup analyses were performed based on the primary diagnostic criteria used (ECMM/ISHAM CAPA vs. other criteria), study design, center, country, sample size, age, gender, comorbidities of diabetes or chronic lung disease, receiving mechanical ventilation, history of glucocorticoids use before ICU, glucocorticoids use during ICU in term of IPA incidence and mortality.

Meta-analyses were performed using R statistical software with the meta and metafor packages. Pooled incidence and mortality rates were calculated using random-effects models. Pooled estimates for each potential risk factor were generated if data from at least three studies were available, and the odds ratios (ORs) with 95% CIs are presented. Heterogeneity was quantified using I2 statistics, with I2 > 50% indicating substantial heterogeneity. Publication bias was examined by Egger’s test and Sensitivity analyses were performed.

For risk factor analysis, odds ratios (ORs) were pooled from the most adjusted model available in each primary study. If only unadjusted estimates were reported, these were used. The source (adjusted vs. unadjusted) is considered when interpreting the associations.

While a more restricted analysis focusing on a single diagnostic criterion would increase methodological homogeneity, our inclusive approach aimed to comprehensively map the epidemiology of IPA as it was diagnosed in real-world practice during 2020–2024. We argue that the stratified presentation of incidence by diagnostic criteria (ECMM/ISHAM CAPA vs. others) allows clarifying to appreciate the influence of diagnostic criteria while maintaining a comprehensive overview of the pandemic-era burden.

Results

Summary of study characteristics

The flow diagram shows the study selection process in Fig. 1. Overall, we identified 1,696 articles after removing duplicates of 2,363 records initially searched from PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and Cochrane Library database. Fifty-six studies [8, 13–67] with 15,385 patients were ultimately included in this meta-analysis after retrieving 86 full-length manuscripts, of which 30 studies were excluded: 11 lacking IPA participants, 17 without clear IPA diagnostic criteria, and 2 initiating prior to November 1, 2019.

Given that 98.8% of the enrolled patients had COVID-19, the following analyses predominantly characterize COVID-19-associated pulmonary aspergillosis (CAPA) within the ICU setting during the study period.

IPA incidence and risk factors

As shown in Table 3 and Figure S1, fifty-three studies [13–28, 30–34, 36–62, 64–67] entered analysis for IPA Incidence and risk factors. Among 15,024 ICU patients, 2,037 IPA cases were reported, giving an overall incidence of 11.9% (95% CI: 9.7–14.6%, Figure S1). Subgroup analyses revealed higher rates (15.0%) in mechanically ventilated patients (Figure S2). COVID-19 accounted for 98.0% (14,731/15,024) of the ICU patient cohort. The incidence of IPA was 11.9% in COVID-19 patients (Figure S3). Among the small subset of non-COVID-19 patients (n ≈ 188, including those with influenza or community-acquired pneumonia), the incidence was 15.5%. Due to the severe sample size imbalance and potential confounding, this numerical difference should not be interpreted as a reliable or generalizable estimate. Among COVID-19 patients receiving mechanical ventilation, IPA incidence was 14.6% (Figure S4).

Significant heterogeneity was observed in IPA incidence among ICU patients across studies (Figure S1). Sensitivity analyses stratified by study quality showed comparable incidence estimates between high-quality studies (14.0%, 95% CI: 10.9–17.9%) and low-quality studies (11.9%, 95% CI: 8.6–16.3%), while case series reported a lower incidence (5.2%, 95% CI: 3.0–9.0%; p for subgroup difference = 0.005). The primary estimate of 11.9% remained robust, as it was consistent with both high- and low-quality study subgroups (Figure S5). When stratified by diagnostic criteria, the pooled IPA incidence was 11.4% (95% CI: 8.8–14.5%) for ECMM/ISHAM CAPA vs. 14.2% (95% CI: 10.3–19.2%) for other criteria (p for subgroup difference = 0.28; Figure S6). As presented in Table 3, subgroup analysis indicated heterogeneity was associated with study type (cohort/case-control/case series), but not with research methodology, study center, national income level or sample size. Patients with mechanical ventilation (15.0% vs. 4.0%, p<0.01) or pre-ICU glucocorticoid use (38.0% vs. 21.1%, p<0.01) exhibited a significantly higher incidence of IPA compared to those without respective risk factors. However, no significant differences in IPA incidence were observed based on gender distribution and comorbidities including diabetes status, or chronic lung disease). Sensitivity analysis indicated that the results of IPA incidence among ICU patients were highly robust and not influenced by any single study (Figure S7-17).

Pooled analyses identified significant predictors of IPA in ICU patients (Fig. 2). Demographic and clinical predictors include male sex (OR 1.3, 95% CI 1.1–1.5, I2 = 0%, p < 0.001), smoking history (OR 1.5, 95% CI 1.3–1.9, I2 = 0%, p < 0.001), and comorbidities such as chronic lung disease (OR 1.3, 95% CI 1.0–1.6, I2 = 19%, p = 0.021), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease(OR 2.0, 95% CI 1.2–3.3, I2 = 67%, p < 0.001), chronic kidney disease (OR 1.7, 95% CI 1.4–2.1, I2 = 21%, p < 0.001), chronic liver disease (OR 1.8, 95% CI 1.3–2.5, I2 = 16%, p = 0.001) and chronic heart disease (OR 1.2, 95% CI 1.0–1.4, I2 = 35%, p = 0.023). Immunocompromising conditions strongly associated with IPA include malignant tumor (OR 1.8, 95% CI 1.4–2.2, I2 = 40%, p < 0.001), hematological malignancy (OR 2.7, 95% CI 1.6–4.4 I2 = 52%, p < 0.001), pre-ICU glucocorticoid use (OR 2.1, 95% CI 1.4–3.2, I2 = 60%, p < 0.001), immunosuppression (OR 2.3, 95% CI 1.3–4.0, I2 = 75%, p = 0.006, specific definitions listed in Table S4) and EROTC host factors (OR 2.1, 95% CI 1.4–3.2, I2 = 80%, p = 0.011). Organ support interventions including mechanical ventilation (OR 3.7, 95% CI 3.0–4.5, I2 = 40%, p < 0.001), vasopressor use (OR 3.7, 95% CI 3.0–4.5, I2 = 40%, p < 0.001), and renal replacement therapy (OR 2.3, 95% CI 2.0–2.8, I2 = 0%, p < 0.001) were strongly associated with an increased risk, indicating the need for early screening and antifungal intervention in high-risk populations. Other previously reported-risk factors, including obesity, hypertension, diabetes, glucocorticoid use during ICU treatment and ECMO therapy showed non-significant associations (Figure S18-37).

The CAPA-specific risk factor profile was largely consistent with the main IPA analysis. Comparable effect sizes were observed for male sex, smoking history, chronic organ diseases (lung, kidney, liver, heart), malignancies, immunosuppressive conditions, and organ support interventions (mechanical ventilation, vasopressors, renal replacement therapy). Notably, glucocorticoid use during ICU stay was significantly associated with increased risk of CAPA occurrence in the main IPA analysis but showed no significant association in the CAPA-specific analysis (OR 1.20, 95% CI 0.87–1.65), suggesting potential attenuation of risk in the COVID-19 population or residual confounding. Other non-significant factors, including obesity, hypertension, diabetes, and ECMO, were consistent between both analyses (Figure S38-58).

IPA mortality and risk factors

Fifty-six studies with 2,349 IPA patients reported 1,344 death events. Overall mortality was 59.6% (95% CI: 55.0–64.0%), rising to 61.9% among subpopulations receiving mechanical ventilation (Figure S58-59). Significant heterogeneity in IPA mortality among ICU patients was observed across studies (Figure S61). As presented in Table 4, subgroup analysis indicated heterogeneity was not associated with study type, research methodology, study center, national income level or sample size. Sensitivity analyses stratified by study quality showed consistent mortality estimates across subgroups. Among 36 high-quality studies (1,812 patients), pooled mortality was 57.6% (95% CI: 52.8–62.4%); among 13 low-quality studies (435 patients), 64.8% (95% CI: 53.9–74.3%); and among 7 case series (102 patients), 56.7% (95% CI: 38.8–73.1%, Figure S62). Subgroup differences were not significant (p = 0.48). Mortality was similar between studies using ECMM/ISHAM CAPA criteria (58.3%, 95% CI: 53.1–63.3%) and other criteria (63.3%, 95% CI: 55.0–63.9%; p = 0.34; Figure S63).

Mortality in IPA patients showed no significant association with sex, diabetes, chronic lung disease, or mechanical ventilation. Sensitivity analysis indicated that IPA mortality was robust and not influenced by any single study (Figure S64-73).

Pooled analyses identified significant predictors of mortality in IPA patients (Fig. 3). Of note, while the pooled odds ratio indicated that mechanical ventilation was significantly associated with increased odds of death (OR = 1.93, 95% CI: 1.05–3.56), the subgroup comparison of mortality rates between ventilated and non-ventilated IPA patients did not reach statistical significance (61.9% vs. 49.2%, p for subgroup difference = 0.35). This discrepancy likely reflects the limited sample size and statistical power of the non-ventilated patient subgroup (n = 55), which broadens the confidence intervals and precludes a definitive direct comparison. Therefore, the finding from the pooled odds ratio, derived from a larger number of studies, should be considered the more robust estimate. Age ≥ 65 years emerged as a potent demographic risk factor (OR 2.9, 95% CI 1.9–4.3, I2 = 5%, p < 0.001). Treatment-related risk factors significantly associated with increased mortality included glucocorticoid use during ICU stay (OR 2.0, 95% CI 1.1–3.7, I2 = 0%, p = 0.034) and mechanical ventilation (OR 1.9, 95% CI 1.1–3.6, I2 = 46%, p = 0.035). No significant associations with mortality were observed for sex, obesity, smoking history, chronic lung disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, chronic kidney disease, chronic heart disease, diabetes, hypertension, pre-ICU glucocorticoid use, malignancy, or transplantation (solid organ or stem cell) (Figure S74-88).

CAPA-specific analyses demonstrated similar mortality burden: the pooled CAPA mortality was 59.6% (95% CI: 54.9–64.0%; Figure S89), increasing to 61.9% (95% CI: 55.8–67.6%) in mechanically ventilated CAPA patients (Figure S90). CAPA-specific mortality risk factors were largely consistent with the main IPA mortality findings. Age ≥ 65 years (OR 2.83, 95% CI 1.85–4.32) and mechanical ventilation (OR 1.93, 95% CI 1.05–3.56) remained significant predictors, with effect sizes nearly identical to the primary analysis. ICU glucocorticoid use showed a borderline association (OR 1.92, 95% CI 1.00–3.67, p = 0.050). Notably, hypertension-not significant in the main IPA mortality analysis—emerged as a significant risk factor for CAPA death (OR 2.00, 95% CI 1.09–3.65, p = 0.025). Other demographic and clinical factors were not significantly associated with CAPA mortality, consistent with the main analysis (Figure S91-106).

Discussion

This global meta-analysis delineates the evolving epidemiology of invasive pulmonary aspergillosis (IPA) in critically ill patients during 2020–2024. It is crucial to emphasize that our patient cohort overwhelmingly (98.8%) consisted of individuals with COVID-19; therefore, the findings primarily reflect the characteristics of COVID-19-associated pulmonary aspergillosis (CAPA) and its impact on ICU populations during the pandemic era. The pooled incidence of 11.9% (95% CI: 9.7–14.6%) substantially exceeds pre-2020 IPA rates [1], reflecting the seismic impact of COVID-19. Notably, incidence surged to 15.0% in mechanically ventilated patients and peaked during viral waves (e.g., 15.5% in early 2021), suggesting a strong association between SARS-CoV-2 pathophysiology of epithelial destruction, lymphopenia, and hyperinflammation [68–70] and the development of fungal invasion. Concurrently, immunomodulatory therapies (e.g., corticosteroids increasing mortality risk: OR = 1.95) and the 2023–2024 “immunity debt” phenomenon [9, 71, 72] expanded the at-risk population beyond classically immunocompromised hosts.

Diagnostic and study type heterogeneity emerged as a critical confounder. While 85% of cases adhered to ECMM/ISHAM criteria [7], variable adoption of diagnostic criteria contributed to significant incidence heterogeneity (I²> 50%). Liu et al. demonstrated that the M-AspICU criteria yielded a four-fold higher diagnostic rate for IPA compared to the EORTC/MSG ICU criteria [73]. This underscores an urgent need for standardized ICU-specific diagnostic protocols integrating mycological and molecular methods. Meanwhile, subgroup analysis suggests that the heterogeneity might related to study types: the incidence in case series studies is significantly lower than that in case-control and cohort studies, indicating potential selection bias in case series studies.

Risk stratification paradigms have undergone a significant shift. Beyond established factors like hematologic malignancy and transplantation [2], co-existence of chronic organ dysfunction as novel predictors emerged. Chronic organ dysfunction imposes distinct vulnerabilities. Chronic pulmonary disease patients, particularly those with COPD, are susceptible to secondary pulmonary fungal infections due to structural alterations from chronic inflammation and histories of recurrent glucocorticoid therapy [74]. This expanded risk profile is now formally recognized. The 2024 FUNDICU diagnostic criteria explicitly classify moderate/severe COPD and decompensated cirrhosis as predisposing conditions for pulmonary aspergillosis in non-classically immunocompromised hosts [3]. For chronic liver disease, cirrhosis-associated immune dysregulation (e.g., neutrophil dysfunction, impaired phagocytosis) heightens infection risk [75] and immunosuppressive treatments (e.g., for glomerulonephritis) coupled with uremia-induced immune impairment create permissive conditions among patients with chronic kidney disease [76]. Demographics also played unanticipated roles- male sex (OR = 1.3) and smoking (OR = 1.2) conferred significant risks, suggesting underrecognized genetic/epigenetic vulnerabilities [77, 78]. These findings support the development and validation of ICU-specific risk scores incorporating acute physiologic insults. It is important to note that the factors associated with increased risk of IPA diagnosis and mortality—mechanical ventilation, vasopressor use, and renal replacement therapy—are foremost indicators of profound critical illness. Their association likely reflects the shared severe pathophysiological state (e.g., immune dysregulation, epithelial injury) that predisposes to both IPA and the need for advanced organ support, rather than a direct causal relationship. Thus, they should be interpreted as flags identifying a patient population at extreme risk, necessitating heightened diagnostic vigilance, rather than as modifiable causal targets.

Mortality remained alarmingly high at 59.6% (escalating to 61.9% in ventilated patients) [79, 80], primarily driven by age ≥ 65 years (OR = 2.85) and modifiable iatrogenic factors: ICU-administered glucocorticoids (OR = 1.97) and mechanical ventilation (OR = 1.93). These findings are consistent with previous reports: Wang et al. found that pneumocystis pneumonia patients receiving invasive mechanical ventilation during hospitalization had an approximately 29-fold higher mortality risk compared to those not requiring ventilation [81]. Notably, baseline comorbidities showed non-significant mortality associations, implying that acute critical illness burdens, rather than chronic conditions-dictate outcomes. Our findings support the rationale for heightened clinical suspicion and early diagnostic evaluation for IPA in high-risk subgroups such as mechanically ventilated COVID-19 patients with viral pneumonitis or shock, utilizing diagnostic approaches that may include BAL galactomannan and molecular tools. Li et al. demonstrated an association between corticosteroid use and worse outcomes in patients with invasive fungal disease [82], which seemed to be similar findings with our study. While corticosteroid use (both pre-ICU and during ICU stay) was associated with higher odds of IPA and mortality, our analysis is limited by heterogeneous reporting of dose, duration, and timing relative to IPA diagnosis across the included studies. Consequently, our findings highlight the need for vigilance in corticosteroid-treated patients but cannot support specific recommendations for dose or duration reduction, which must be balanced against the established therapeutic benefits of corticosteroids in conditions like severe COVID-19. Future prospective, interventional studies are required to determine whether protocolized screening combined with pre-emptive or early targeted antifungal therapy in these identified high-risk subgroups can improve clinical outcomes.

It is important to recognize that the persistently high mortality of IPA is not solely due to diagnostic or preventive limitations. Over the past two decades, no novel antifungal class has demonstrated clear superiority over established first-line agents such as voriconazole or isavuconazole. This therapeutic stagnation inherently restricts the mortality reduction achievable through early detection alone, emphasizing the equal need for improved diagnostics and novel therapeutic strategies.

Study strengths include robust methodology (PRISMA-adherent, PROSPERO-registered) and unprecedented pandemic-era generalizability (15,385 patients from 56 studies). Limitations involve unresolved diagnostic heterogeneity, including variable adoption of diagnostic criteria and differing biomarker thresholds across centers. Diagnostic misclassification bias remains a key consideration when interpreting the pooled estimates. Additionally, geographic inequity (74.3% of patients from high-income countries) limits the generalizability of our findings to low- and middle-income country ICU settings, where diagnostic resources, fungal ecology, and patient demographics may differ substantially. Future research must prioritize ICU-specific diagnostic standardization and equity-focused studies in resource-limited settings, where IPA burden may be substantially underestimated due to constrained diagnostic capabilities. Additionally, the apparent discrepancy between the subgroup comparison and pooled odds ratio for mechanical ventilation as a mortality predictor (non-significant p for subgroup difference vs. significant pooled OR) underscores the importance of statistical power; the smaller non-ventilated subgroup (n = 55) limits direct comparisons, whereas the pooled OR benefits from a larger effective sample size and should be considered the more reliable estimate. Furthermore, the analysis is based on all-cause mortality, as IPA-attributable mortality was rarely reported. This may overestimate the direct mortality burden of IPA.

Conclusion

IPA remains a lethal complication in ICUs, with incidence and mortality amplified by the convergence of viral pandemics, immunomodulatory therapies, and life-support interventions. Moving forward, our findings generate the hypothesis that ICU-specific, risk-adapted strategies—potentially integrating early biomarker screening, judicious immunomodulation, and prompt antifungal therapy—could be explored in future interventional studies to mitigate this evolving threat. Prospective validation is required before any clinical recommendations can be made.
