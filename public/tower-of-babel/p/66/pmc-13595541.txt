The relationship between physical activity levels and measures of cognition and disease severity in prodromal Parkinson’s disease: A longitudinal study
Authors: Homa Seyedmirzaei, Amin Nakhostin-Ansari, Nazanin Rafiei, Alireza Soltani Khaboushan, Amirhussein Abdolalizadeh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPhysical activity has been studied for its potential role in reducing motor symptoms of Parkinson’s disease (PD). However, its impact on its prodromal phase remains underexplored.MethodsWe included 108 people with prodromal PD from the Parkinson’s Progression Markers Initiative. We assessed their four-year longitudinal data of Physical Activity Scale for the Elderly, Movement Disorders Society-sponsored revision of the Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale scores 1–3, Hopkins Verbal Learning Test-Revised, Letter-Number Sequencing test, and the Symbol Digit Modalities Test.ResultsOur linear mixed-effects model regression showed that physical activity (as assessed by the total, household-related, leisure-related, and work-related scores) had no significant associations with disease severity and cognitive function (p-values > 0.05).ConclusionsOur study found no significant associations between physical activity levels and cognitive function or PD symptoms. However, methodological limitations of the study suggest the need for further research to better understand the effects of physical activity on prodromal PD.Trial registrationThis study is a secondary analysis of de-identified data obtained from the Parkinson’s Progression Markers Initiative (PPMI) observational cohort and did not directly involve prospective participant enrollment or intervention by the investigators. Background

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is a neurodegenerative disorder, often recognized with bradykinesia accompanied by at least one other motor symptom, such as rigidity, rest tremor, and postural instability [1]. The degeneration targets dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra [2], leading to the gradual emergence of debilitating motor symptoms. According to the World Health Organization, the number of people with PD was over 8.5 million in 2019, showing a significant rise since the reported 6.1 million in 2016 [3, 4]. The number of cases is predicted to exceed 17 million by 2040 [5], highlighting the urgent need for effective strategies to manage and prevent the progression of this disease [6].

The prodromal phase of PD is when an individual presents with non-specific features without evident clinical motor signs [7]. This phase often precedes the clinical stage, occurring up to 30 years before the onset of motor symptoms, and is thought to indicate the early stages of neurodegenerative progression [8, 9]. While there are limited studies on this topic and some controversy exists, it has been shown that certain aspects of cognitive functioning, such as executive function and memory, are affected during the prodromal phase of PD, which may explain why more than 10% of individuals with PD have some degree of cognitive impairment at the time of diagnosis [10]. Other non-motor symptoms during this period may include rapid eye movement (REM) sleep behavior disorder, constipation, autonomic dysfunction, hyposmia, and depression [11, 12]. These symptoms significantly impact quality of life and impair certain aspects of people’s daily living, even during the prodromal stage of PD, highlighting the need to understand the prodromal phase of PD to gain insights into the early stages of neurodegeneration [13, 14].

The gradual, progressive nature of PD has prompted researchers to explore potential factors that may impact the course of the disease. Among these factors, physical activity has been a subject of investigation, yielding diverse findings [15]. Physical activity encompasses all forms of bodily activities, including both structured and unstructured activities, with exercise being a specific subset [16]. Several studies have indicated that higher levels of physical activity are linked to a reduced risk of developing PD [17–20], while some studies have yielded contradictory results [21, 22]. The potential mechanisms by which physical activity might influence PD progression could be attributed to its beneficial effects on brain health, which may include enhanced dopamine release [23, 24], reduced inflammation [25], and improved mitochondrial function [26]. Furthermore, there appear to be a bidirectional association between the non-motor symptoms of PD and physical activity. There are investigations on the beneficial effects of physical activity on non-motor symptoms such as cognitive function, sleep, and mood disorders in people with PD [27–31]. On the other hand, it has been shown that PD non-motor symptoms, such as depressive mood and leg swelling, can reduce physical activity levels in individuals with PD [32]. These findings render physical activity a promising area of investigation for understanding and potentially mitigating PD progression. Additionally, given the negative impact of non-motor symptoms of PD on quality of life and daily functioning, and the potential of physical activity to have beneficial effects on these aspects [13, 14, 33], it could be a promising intervention for individuals with this condition at the prodromal stage.

While there are limited studies suggesting a reduced odds of non-motor PD symptoms among PD-free individuals with higher physical activity levels, studies examining the effects of physical activity on motor and non-motor symptoms in this stage remain quite scarce [34]. Notably, there is a lack of longitudinal studies examining the impact of physical activity on disease severity and cognitive function during the prodromal phase of PD. This study employed an exploratory approach to address this topic by providing insights into the relationship between physical activity and the mentioned features in prodromal PD, thereby contributing to the development of early intervention strategies and disease management protocols. We hypothesized that individuals in the prodromal phase of PD with higher physical activity levels would experience a slower progression of PD symptoms over time.

Methods

Study participants

Our study sample was obtained from the Parkinson’s Progression Markers Initiative (PPMI) cohort, a longitudinal multicenter observational study of the progression of PD. In brief, PPMI has recruited participants with de novo PD, people with a high probability of being in the prodromal phase of PD, and people with parkinsonism without evidence of dopaminergic deficit on dopamine transporter imaging alongside healthy controls. Their assessment included longitudinal collection of clinical data and measurements of neuroimaging, blood, urine, and cerebrospinal fluid. The PPMI study is registered at clinicaltrials.gov under the code NCT01141023. Further information on the PPMI study protocols is available at https://www.ppmi-info.org/study-design.

We downloaded the required data from the PPMI website in August 2024. People with prodromal PD made up our sample. In summary, according to PPMI, the inclusion criteria for prodromal PD were as follows: 60 years of age and older, no clinical diagnosis of PD, other parkinsonism, or dementia, having risk factors or clinical features of PD (including REM sleep behavior disorder, known PD-related genetic risk variants, and first-degree family history of PD), hyposmia, and a suggestive dopamine transporter imaging on visual inspection. Further details are provided on the PPMI website at https://www.ppmi-info.org/study-design/study-cohorts#prodromal/. In our study, when obtaining the data on prodromal PD, we included those who had attended clinical visits and filled out the questionnaire on physical activity. Participants should have had complete clinical and physical activity/cognitive function data on five consecutive checkpoints during four years of follow-up (baseline, 12 months, 24 months, 36 months, and 48 months) to be included in our analysis.

Standard protocol approvals and ethics

The institutional review board approved the assessments at each site, and all PPMI subjects provided informed consent before participation. Our study was conducted following the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki [35]. Moreover, we adhere to the publication policy of PPMI and the Data & Publication Committee of the PPMI study granted us permission to publish our research.

Measurements

We extracted the data of participants regarding their age, sex, education, race, and date of visits as well as their physical activity, cognitive function, and severity of PD signs and symptoms.

Physical activity

The regular physical activity of participants was assessed with the Physical Activity Scale for the Elderly (PASE) questionnaire. Being a brief self-report measure, this questionnaire evaluates the intensity, frequency, and duration of physical activity over the previous week to calculate a total PASE score. The PASE questionnaire provides a total score reflecting the typical activities of older adults and includes sections assessing household, leisure, and work-related activities. The leisure section of the PASE includes questions assessing the frequency of involvement in sitting activities, outdoor walking, light activities, moderate activities, strenuous activities, and strength exercise. Each item could be answered as never, seldom (1–2 days), sometimes (3–4 days), or often (5–7 days). If participants did not select “never” for an item, they were also asked to report the type of activity performed and the average number of hours per day spent on those activities (< 1 h, 1–2 h, 2–4 h, > 4 h). The household section of the PASE included three items. The first two assessed involvement in light and heavy housework, each could be answered as yes or no. The third item evaluated involvement in home repairs, lawn work/ yard care, outdoor gardening, and caring for others, each of which could also be answered as yes or no. The work-related section of PASE assesses participation in these activities. If participants answered “yes,” they were further asked to specify the type of activity within four intensity categories and report the average number of hours per week spent on these work-related activities. Each item score was calculated by multiplying either the average number of hours per day spent on the activities within that item or, for yes/no items, participation in those activities (scored as 1), by empirically derived weights for that item. The score for each section was the sum of its item scores, and the total PASE score was the sum of all item scores, with higher scores indicating higher levels of physical activity. The reliability and validity of PASE have been demonstrated in previous studies, and it has been shown to correlate with objective measures of physical activity [36–39].

Severity of PD signs and symptoms

The severity of PD was assessed via the Movement Disorders Society–sponsored revision of the Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale (MDS-UPDRS) [40]. This tool provides four subscales, namely (1) non-motor experiences of daily living (score range 0–52), (2) motor experiences of daily living (0–52), (3) motor examination (0-132), and (4) motor complications (0–24). In the PPMI database, all scores were available for people with prodromal PD, except the motor complications, which was, consequently, not analyzed in this study.

Cognitive function

We used variable tools to assess the cognitive function of the participants. We evaluated the verbal recent memory via the delayed recall T score of the Hopkins Verbal Learning Test-Revised (HVLT-R) [41], working memory via the total score of the Letter-Number Sequencing (LNS) test [42], and processing speed via the total score of the Symbol Digit Modalities Test (SDMT) [43]. Test-retest reliability, construct validity, and concurrent validity of HVLT-R have been confirmed in previous studies [41, 44, 45]. Additionally, SDMT is shown to be a valid measure for the assessment of cognition [46, 47].

Statistical analysis

In the present study, we presented continuous variables as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and categorical variables as frequencies with percentages. To check the possible associations between physical activity and measures of cognitive function and disease severity, we implemented a linear mixed-effects model with a random intercept. In this model, markers of cognitive function and disease severity acted as the response variable; each score of PASE (PASE total score, PASE leisure score, PASE household score, and PASE work score) served as the predictor variable. The fixed-effects covariates were age, sex, and the visit time, while the participant ID number was a predictor variable with random effects. Due to the high distribution of 0 scores for MDS-UPDRS, we used the Zero-Inflated Negative Binomial model of regression. Moreover, to overcome the multiple testing problem, we used the Benjamini-Hochberg False Discovery Rate method to correct the p-values where applicable [48]. Finally, corrected p-values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant in our study.

To run the analyses and visualizations in our study, we used R version 4.4.3 (R Core Team [2020]. R: A language and environment for statistical computing. R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). We used the following packages in this study: dplyr (version 1.1.4), ggplot2 (version 4.0.1), lme4 (version 1.1–36), lmerTest (version 3.1-3), glmmTMB (1.1.10), Matrix (version 1.7-2), performance (version 0.15.3), DHARMa (version 0.4.7), and car (version 3.1-3).

Results

A total of 108 people with prodromal PD were included in our study. All participants had matched data of physical activity, severity of PD signs, and cognitive assessments in five consecutive time points during a four-year follow-up. The mean age was 63.71 years, females comprised 44.4% of the total sample, and the majority of participants were White (76.9%). The mean total PASE score at baseline was 147.64 (SD = 78.64). Discussion

Individuals with prodromal PD are likely the group that could possibly benefit from interventions aimed at preventing or slowing the progression of PD, with lifestyle modifications, including regular exercise, among the suggested and feasible options in this regard [49, 50]. Although previous studies have examined the impact of physical activity on the progression of PD [39, 51–53], to the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to evaluate how physical activity levels might be associated with changes in different PD symptoms in people with prodromal PD over time. At baseline, comparing our findings with the normative values reported for the measures used in the study [41, 54–56], the subjects in our study mainly had no or only subtle levels of disease severity by MDS-UPDRS and cognitive impairment, and our findings indicate that neither the total PASE score nor any of its domains was significantly associated with cognitive function or PD signs and symptoms and their changes over a four-year follow-up period.

Studies on the association between physical activity and disease severity and cognition in PD are scarce. In one study, Maraki et al. found that people with prodromal PD had lower physical activity levels, lower gait speed, and more motor complaints compared to controls [57]. In a 25-year cohort study, Hughes et al. assessed the association between baseline physical activity levels and physical activity levels during the study period with various non-motor symptoms in the middle-aged general population without PD. They found that higher baseline physical activity levels were associated with a lower risk of daytime sleepiness, body pain, and constipation at follow-up. Additionally, greater cumulative physical activity over the study period was linked to a reduced risk of constipation, daytime sleepiness, and probable rapid eye movement sleep behavior disorder [34]. However, in our study, we were unable to find any association between physical activity levels and memory, cognitive function, PD signs and symptoms progression. One reason for the differences between our findings and those reported in these two studies may be methodological differences, such as the inclusion of individuals without PD and longer follow-up periods. Follow-up duration may be particularly important, given that the prodromal phase of PD can last for decades [8, 9], and a four-year follow-up may not be sufficient to identify the impact of physical activity on symptom progression.

Based on the findings from our study, it appears that while the physical activity levels of individuals with prodromal PD are lower than those of adults without prodromal symptoms [57], physical activity levels might not be significantly associated with changes in certain symptoms in this phase, at least in terms of cognition, memory, and PD signs and symptoms. A possible explanation for this observation could be that physical activity earlier in life may be more crucial in the development and progression of prodromal symptoms. With some studies indicating that individuals with PD may experience a higher prevalence of certain symptoms up to 30 years before diagnosis, the pathology of PD may begin long before the clinical manifestations of its prodromal stage [8]. Furthermore, the decrease in the number of dopaminergic neurons might start to decrease much earlier than the beginning of the disease, and inflammation during childhood might contribute to the development of PD in later stages of life [58, 59]. Therefore, contrary to common belief [49, 50], it appears that interventions aiming to prevent Parkinson’s disease should target younger high-risk individuals, such as those with a family history of PD or those with environmental exposures that increase the risk of PD, before the onset of prodromal symptoms, rather than those already in the prodromal stage, in order to maximize the neuroprotective and anti-inflammatory benefits of physical activity [60–63]. However, given the limitations of our study discussed above, there is still a need for further studies on the effects of physical activity in individuals in the prodromal phase of PD before making any strong recommendations.

We found no association between physical activity levels and different domains of cognitive functioning. In a similar longitudinal study on individuals with early PD, higher PASE scores in the work domain were associated with a slower decline in SDMT scores, but not in Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) scores, an indicator of global cognitive function [53]. Another study on individuals with early PD found that higher baseline PASE total and leisure scores were associated with higher MoCA scores over the following two years. Additionally, higher PASE work domain scores were associated with higher SDMT scores over the following two years [39]. It is also shown that regular exercise can reduce cognitive decline in people with PD [64, 65]. The association between higher PASE work scores and better cognitive functioning among people with PD may be due to the cognitive demands of work tasks, rather than the direct impact of work-related physical activities on processing speed [53]. Additionally, the heterogeneity across findings of different studies on people with PD and a lack of studies on people in the prodromal phase emphasizes the need for further studies on how physical activity, particularly its impact on cognitive function, affects individuals in prodromal and main phases of PD. Lack of significant findings in our study may also be attributed to several reasons. First, although executive and cognitive functioning begin to decline during the prodromal stage, years before the onset of PD, these dysfunctions are generally less severe compared to those diagnosed with PD [66]. As a result, since there might be little cognitive impairment at this stage and thus limited room for improvement, physical activity may have minimal effects on cognitive functioning at prodromal phase of PD. This issue may apply to other variables assessed in the study too, and the effects of physical activity on symptoms during the prodromal phase of PD might be more observable when there are more pronounced declines in function and more severe symptoms have developed. Second, different types of physical activities have varying effects on individuals with PD. In particular, studies have shown that aerobic exercise, resistance training, and moderate to vigorous intensity activities have positive impacts on cognitive function in people with PD [50]. This is an aspect that was not assessed in our study or in the studies mentioned above. As a result, the lack of a significant association in our findings may be attributed to the specific types of activities predominantly undertaken by our participants.

This study had several limitations worth noting. First and foremost, despite including all eligible subjects from the PPMI database, our study was constrained by a small sample size, which may have reduced its statistical power. Consequently, future studies with larger sample sizes are needed to more accurately assess the associations between physical activity and the progression of prodromal PD. This is particularly important, as larger sample sizes increase statistical power and allow for more robust subgroup and sensitivity analyses. Second, the PASE measures physical activity levels over the past seven days [36], which may not accurately reflect physical activity status between assessments. Therefore, using tools that assess physical activity levels over longer periods, such as the past year total physical activity questionnaire [67], could offer more valuable insights into the impact of physical activity on the progression of prodromal PD. Objective measures of physical activity, such as wearable sensors, might also be helpful in this regard, allowing for a more accurate assessment of physical activity levels. Additionally, since PASE is a self-report questionnaire, it may be prone to self-report bias [68]. The PASE questionnaire also does not differentiate between types of exercise, such as aerobic or resistance training, which may have varying effects on individuals with prodromal PD [50]. Third, since this study was observational rather than interventional, we were unable to control for confounding variables, which may have influenced our findings. Fourth, we only followed participants for four years, and since it may take up to 20 years for prodromal PD to progress to full PD [69], our study may not capture the subtle impacts of physical activity on this progression. Therefore, future studies with longer follow-up periods are needed to more accurately assess the influence of physical activity on the progression of prodromal PD. Fifth, we did not compare the characteristics of participants included in our study with those who were excluded, and given that they may have different characteristics, this is another limitation of our study. Finally, because we modeled the associations using linear mixed-effects models, potential non-linear relationships between physical activity and the outcomes may not have been fully captured. If such non-linear relationships exist in this setting, our analytical approach might have limited ability to detect them. Therefore, future studies with larger sample sizes applying non-linear or more flexible modeling approaches may provide further insight into the associations between physical activity and different signs and symptoms in people with prodromal PD.

Conclusions

This observational study investigated how physical activity levels might be associated with prodromal PD signs and symptoms. We did not find significant associations between total physical activity levels or specific activity domains with cognitive function, recent memory, working memory, processing speed, or PD signs and symptoms. However, the limitations of our study, such as the limited number of outcomes, highlight the need for further studies to better understand the effects of physical activity on individuals with prodromal PD. Additionally, our preliminary findings suggest that physical activity during the prodromal stage of PD might not have significant beneficial effects on cognition and PD motor and non-motor symptoms, and this stage may not be the most appropriate time to guide interventions aimed at slowing neurodegenerative progression in individuals at high risk of PD.
