Preoxygenation algorithm: sequential PSV and PEEP versus tidal volume breathing. a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Çağın Tanrıverdi, Selvinaz Yüksel Tanrıverdi, Erkan Tomatır
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPreoxygenation is crucial for airway management safety. The standard method for preoxygenation is tidal volume breathing (TVB). This study aimed to determine whether an algorithmic approach, which involves stepwise adding pressure support ventilation (PSV) and positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) when TVB is inadequate, would improve preoxygenation.MethodsTwo hundred patients planned for general anesthesia and having end-tidal oxygen (ETO2) < 90% after 3 min TVB were randomly divided into two: TVB was continued or 6 cmH2O PSV was added. After 1 min PSV (i.e., at minute 4), patients still with ETO2 < 90% were divided into two again: either PSV was continued or 4 cmH2O PEEP was also added to PSV. The primary outcome was the time required to achieve ETO2 ≥ 90%. Adverse effects were noted.ResultsAfter 4 min, patients with adequate preoxygenation were significantly higher with PSV (76%) compared with TVB (52%, P < 0.001). After 5 min, it was 97% and 100%, respectively. The mean durations of adequate preoxygenation with TVB, PSV, and PSV-PEEP between 4 and 5 min were 274.90 ± 18.55, 268.94 ± 12.24, and 252.38 ± 7.46 s, respectively. The differences between TVB and PSV-PEEP (P < 0.001) and between PSV and PSV-PEEP (P = 0.001) were significant. Total time was significantly longer with TVB (237.17 ± 40.31 s) compared with PSV (222.63 ± 27.24 s; P = 0.003).ConclusionsAn algorithmic approach, stepwise adding PSV and PEEP in patients inadequately preoxygenated with standard TVB, may improve adequate preoxygenation in all patients and reduces the time required. This statistical difference was significant, and although its clinical relevance may differ based on patient condition, it may support safer airway management in critically ill or high-risk patients. No serious adverse effects were observed.Trial registrationNCT06736197.Keywords: Algorithm, Stepwise, Tidal volume breathing, Positive end expiratory pressure, Preoxygenation, Pressure support ventilation

Background

The most common problems in airway management are difficulty, delay, or failure to secure the airway. Inadequate preoxygenation remains a clinically significant issue in anesthetic practice, as it directly increases the risk of hypoxemia during induction and tracheal intubation, especially in cases of unanticipated difficult airway or prolonged intubation attempts. More than 90% of cases of difficult tracheal intubation and difficult mask ventilation cannot be anticipated by preoperative assessment (Nørskov et al. 2015). Preventing hypoxemia is critical because it can lead to severe complications such as arrhythmia, brain injury, or even death. Prolongation of nonhypoxemic apnea time is crucial to provide safe airway management (Artime et al. 2020). Filling the functional residual capacity of the lungs with oxygen is the main way to increase nonhypoxemic apnea time. From a physiological perspective, general anesthesia leads to decreased respiratory drive, hypoventilation, and reduced minute ventilation. Additionally, the supine position and muscle relaxation lower FRC, promoting rapid desaturation and atelectasis (Bouroche, 2015; Nimmagadda et al. 2017; Crístian et al. 2024).

Preoxygenation is performed before induction of anesthesia and tracheal intubation and is routinely recommended for all patients (Bell 2004). Adequate preoxygenation is an end-tidal oxygen (ETO2) value of 90% or higher (Berry et al., 1994).

Three minutes of spontaneous tidal volume breathing (TVB) of 100% oxygen with a face mask is the conventional method of preoxygenation (Hamilton and Eastwood 1955). Later, techniques such as deep breathing (Gold et al. 1981) vital capacity breathing (Russell et al. 1987), noninvasive positive pressure ventilation (NIPPV) (Herriger et al. 2004) and high flow nasal oxygen (HFNO) (Ng et al. 2018) have also been described. Deep breathing can reduce ETCO2 (Nimmagadda et al. 2001) and HFNO requires special equipment. It has been reported that NIPPV techniques such as pressure support ventilation (PSV) and positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) can increase the success rate to 100% (Arab et al. 2016); however, adverse effects such as discomfort and gastric distension may be observed in patients (Delay et al. 2008; Bouroche, 2015); Tianliang 2019). PSV supports spontaneous inspiration by providing a set inspiratory pressure, thereby increasing tidal volume and alveolar ventilation without requiring full control. PEEP prevents alveolar collapse by maintaining positive pressure at end-expiration, thus increasing FRC and reducing atelectasis. We implemented a stepwise preoxygenation strategy, initially applying pressure support ventilation (PSV) to assist spontaneous breathing and evaluate its effect on preoxygenation. In patients who did not achieve adequate preoxygenation at this stage, positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) was subsequently added to prevent alveolar collapse and increase functional residual capacity (FRC), thereby enhancing oxygenation. This stepwise approach was intended to avoid exposing all patients to PEEP unnecessarily, and instead apply it selectively only to those who required additional support beyond PSV. We applied PEEP after PSV because its effect on inspiratory volume is limited during spontaneous breathing. This stepwise approach reduces unnecessary use of PEEP and helps minimize potential hemodynamic side effects. In published studies, a single preoxygenation technique was generally used, determined at the beginning for each patient, and did not change throughout the administration; combining different techniques in a stepwise approach was not encountered.

In this study, we hypothesized that not all patients require NIPPV to achieve adequate preoxygenation, and that a stepwise, algorithmic escalation—starting with TVB and adding PSV and PEEP only when necessary—could optimize the risk–benefit balance. Specifically, our primary objective was to evaluate whether this algorithmic approach improves the success rate and shortens the time required to reach target ETO2 (≥ 90%) in patients who initially fail to achieve adequate preoxygenation with conventional TVB.

Methods

This prospective, randomized controlled single-center trial included 200 adult patients aged 18–65 years, classified as American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status I to II, who were scheduled for elective surgery under general anesthesia. Patients who had not received premedication and had an end-tidal oxygen concentration (ETO₂) of less than 90% after 3 min of spontaneous tidal volume breathing with 100% oxygen were included in the study.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: patients who were hemodynamically unstable and required oxygen support in the preoperative period; patients undergoing thoracic or emergency surgery; patients with high intracranial or intraocular pressure for whom positive pressure ventilation may be indirectly harmful; patients with a body mass index (BMI) ≥ 35 kg/m2; as altered respiratory mechanics in obese individuals could limit generalizability of the study results; pregnant patients; and male patients with beards that may cause leakage in mask ventilation.

All patients received 100% oxygen through a tight-fitting anatomical face mask connected to the anesthesia workstation. The mask was manually adjusted by the anesthesiologist to ensure an effective seal and minimize leaks. Oxygen was delivered at a fresh gas flow rate of 12 L/min with the adjustable pressure-limiting (APL) valve fully opened. The anesthesia machine was flushed with oxygen for 30 s prior to initiating preoxygenation.

To ensure effective oxygen delivery during the preoxygenation period, a continuous capnographic waveform was monitored to confirm both spontaneous ventilation and an adequate facemask seal. In addition, the movement of the reservoir bag was observed to verify proper inhalation and exhalation patterns, indicating adequate tidal volume exchange. These measures ensured that the fraction of inspired oxygen (FiO₂) delivered approached 1.0 and that expired gas measurements were reliable. End-tidal oxygen (ETO₂) was continuously monitored using a gas analyzer, and adequate preoxygenation was defined as achieving an ETO₂ value of 90% or higher.

Randomization was performed using a computer-generated block randomization sequence (blocks of 4), with allocation concealed in sealed, opaque envelopes opened immediately before intervention.

Patients meeting eligibility criteria and exhibiting ETO₂ < 90% after 3 min of TVB were randomized to.

Group TVB: continued spontaneous breathing with 100% oxygen for an additional 2 min using a facemask, without positive pressure support.

Group TVB: continued spontaneous breathing with 100% oxygen for an additional 2 min using a facemask, without positive pressure support.

Group PSV: received pressure support ventilation (PSV) at 6 cmH₂O for 1 min in spontaneous mode, using the anesthesia workstation). No PEEP was initially applied.

Group PSV: received pressure support ventilation (PSV) at 6 cmH₂O for 1 min in spontaneous mode, using the anesthesia workstation). No PEEP was initially applied.

In the PSV group, patients with ETO₂ < 90% after 4 min were further divided into two groups.

Group PSV: continued PSV at 6 cmH₂O without additional modifications,

Group PSV: continued PSV at 6 cmH₂O without additional modifications,

Group PSV-PEEP: received PSV at 6 cmH₂O with additional PEEP of 4 cmH₂O.

Group PSV-PEEP: received PSV at 6 cmH₂O with additional PEEP of 4 cmH₂O.

All ventilatory support interventions were performed using spontaneous mode without sedation or neuromuscular blockade.

The primary outcome of the study was the time (in seconds) required to achieve an end-tidal oxygen concentration (ETO₂) of ≥ 90%. The secondary outcomes included peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO₂), end-tidal carbon dioxide (ETCO₂), heart rate, and non-invasive arterial pressure (systolic and diastolic). In addition, adverse events such as patient discomfort, gastric distension, or mask intolerance were also recorded.

Statistical methods

A power analysis was performed based on the rates of patients who could not achieve adequate preoxygenation with tidal volume ventilation and positive pressure support ventilation in a similar study (Hanouz et al. 2015). This analysis calculated that 198 participants (at least 99 per group) would be required to achieve 80% power at a 95% confidence level.

Data were analyzed using SPSS (Statistical Package for the Social Sciences: IBM SPSS Statistics 25 software; Armonk, NY: IBM Corp.). Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation, while categorical variables were presented as frequency and percentage. Before proceeding with the relevant analyses, normality assumptions for continuous variables were assessed by comparing the Kolmogorov–Smirnov and Shapiro–Wilk statistical values, evaluating skewness and kurtosis values, and interpreting Q-Q plots. Since the data showed a normal distribution, parametric tests were used. The difference between independent groups was analyzed using an independent samples t-test and one-way ANOVA, and the difference between dependent groups was analyzed with paired samples t-test. The post hoc tests were conducted to understand the differences between which groups. After testing the assumption of homogeneity of variances, the Bonferroni post hoc test was performed. If normality assumptions were not met, non-parametric tests were used. The difference between independent groups was analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U test, while the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used for dependent groups. A χ2 test was used to determine the differences between categorical variables. Statistical significance was considered at P < 0.05.

Discussion

Most anesthesia malpractice claims, including death and brain damage, arise from difficult airway management during the induction period (Peterson et al. 2005). Routine preoxygenation of all patients is an effective measure that prolongs the safe apnea time (Bell 2004). It can be considered like the ‘safety belt’ of airway management.

In the present study, in patients in whom adequate preoxygenation could not be achieved at the end of the third minute with the standard technique, adequate preoxygenation was achieved in approximately half (52%) of these patients when TVB was continued for 1 min, whereas this rate increased to approximately three quarters (76%; P < 0.001) when PSV was added for 1 min. This time difference was statistically significant and may be more important clinically for high-risk or critically ill patients than for patients without clinical risk factors.

By the end of the 5 min, the proportion of patients in the TVB group who had reached adequate preoxygenation was 97%, while in the PSV group, this rate was 100%. Hanouz et al. (2015) reported in their study, in which they used one of three methods from the beginning, that the rates of inadequacy at 250th second were 27% with TVB, 15% with PSV, and 10% with PSV-PEEP, and all patients achieved adequate preoxygenation with all three methods by the end of the fifth minute. Despite notable methodological differences between the two studies, the rates can be regarded as similar once the necessary adaptations are made.

Baillard et al. (2019) reported that the rate of inadequate preoxygenation with 5 min of TVB was 30% in their studies investigating the risk factors for difficult preoxygenation. This rate is much higher than the rate in the present study, and the possible reason is that patients at risk of difficult preoxygenation were excluded in the present study. When different methods were compared in terms of the time to reach adequate preoxygenation, it was shown that the order was PSV-PEEP < PSV < TVB (Hanouz et al. 2015; Taxak et al. 2023); the findings in the present study are also similar. Additionally, in another study conducted by Hanouz et al. (2018) it was emphasized that the PSV-PEEP application can also prevent air leakage, which may occur due to mask-face incompatibility.

Different pressure values have been used for NIPPV in preoxygenation; Delay et al. (2008) reported a pressure of 6 cmH2O for PSV and 4 cmH2O for PEEP, while Hanouz et al. (2015) preferred higher pressures (12 and 6 cmH2O respectively). In the present study, low pressure levels were chosen as 6 cmH2O PSV and 4 cmH2O PEEP. Especially at high pressure levels, NIPPV and PEEP can affect hemodynamics by decreasing venous return and cardiac output (Kallet et al. 2009). Hemodynamics did not change significantly in the present study; previous studies have also reported no significant change in hemodynamics when applying NIPPV with PEEP for preoxygenation (Delay et al. 2008; Taxak et al. 2023). It can be considered that the choice of low pressures and short processing time may have played a role in this.

The adverse effects that may be observed due to NIPPV include discomfort and gastric distension. In the present study, discomfort and epigastric distension were rare, and none of the patients required discontinuation of the procedure. In the study conducted by Delay et al. (2008) gastric distension was scored from 0 to 100 during laparoscopy by a surgeon blinded to the preoxygenation method, and it was reported a modest, although significant, increase in gastric distension and no adverse effects with NIPPV techniques compared with TVB. Georgescu et al. (2012) reported that they stopped the procedure in one (6%) of 15 patients undergoing NIPPV for preoxygenation because of discomfort. It seems that the adverse effects, such as discomfort and epigastric distension due to NIPPV for preoxygenation, are rare and clinically insignificant.

It should be noted that the present study has some limitations. The study focused on the percentage and duration of patients in whom algorithmically applied preoxygenation was successful, but the duration of nonhypoxemic apnea was not examined due to ethical concerns. Since the method is unsuitable for a blind study, the risk of bias could not be completely excluded. Epigastric distension due to positive pressure was evaluated by observation only; a reliable measurement method could not be used. In addition, since patients in special risk groups such as the elderly, pregnant women, children, and the morbidly obese were not included in the study, possible differences regarding these groups could not be analyzed.

This study suggests that a stepwise preoxygenation strategy, applying PSV and PEEP only when tidal volume breathing is inadequate, offers a practical and efficient approach that can be easily integrated into clinical practice. It does not require additional equipment or personnel, making it feasible in most operating room settings. While applicable to general populations, its clinical relevance may be even greater in critically ill patients with limited respiratory reserve, where achieving adequate preoxygenation is particularly crucial. Building on these findings, future randomized trials might aim to evaluate the applicability of this algorithm in high-risk populations, such as individuals with obesity, pediatric patients, or those undergoing emergency surgery or rapid sequence induction. Additionally, monitoring mask leakage via inspired oxygen fraction (FiO₂) may provide valuable insights to improve preoxygenation efficiency, and studies using standardized and objective tools to assess patient comfort could further clarify the clinical advantages of this stepwise approach.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the hypothesis that an algorithmic approach based on the stepwise addition of PSV and PEEP in patients who cannot be adequately preoxygenated with TVB will provide adequate preoxygenation in a shorter time and in all patients has been confirmed.
