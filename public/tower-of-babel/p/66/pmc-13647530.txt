Association of parental moderate-to-vigorous physical activity with child overweight/obesity, physical fitness and MVPA guideline adherence: dose–response analyses among schoolchildren
Authors: Junyu Wang, Yixin Liang, Shengyong Wu, Shenghui Guo, Yong Yang, Zheng Zhu, Yang Liu, ZhenBo Cao, Yan Tang, Peijie Chen, Jie Zhuang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesTo examine associations between parental weekly moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (MVPA) and offspring BMI-defined overweight/obesity, MVPA guideline compliance, and physical fitness attainment, and to characterise dose–response patterns.MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional analysis of 18,479 linked parent–child dyads (Grades 4–12) from the Shanghai site of a school-based surveillance programme (2016–2020). Parents reported MVPA using the International Physical Activity Questionnaire-Short Form. Child outcomes were BMI-defined overweight/obesity (WHO reference), MVPA guideline compliance (≥ 60 min/day), and fitness attainment (Chinese National Student Physical Fitness Standard total score ≥ 80). Associations across parental MVPA quartiles (Q1 as reference) were estimated using logistic regression with cluster-robust standard errors to account for school-level clustering, adjusting for prespecified child and family covariates. Restricted cubic spline models were used to assess non-linearity and to visualize dose–response relationships.ResultsOverall, 26.1% of children were classified as overweight/obese. Higher parental MVPA was associated with lower odds of offspring overweight/obesity and higher odds of fitness attainment and MVPA compliance, with the strongest association observed for MVPA compliance in Q4 compared with Q1 (adjusted OR 1.82, 95% CI 1.64 to 2.04). For overweight/obesity, spline models showed no strong evidence of non-linearity (p = 0.17), suggesting that the association may be broadly compatible with a linear pattern, although lower estimated odds were observed above approximately 127 min/week and attenuated around 560 min/week. Fitness attainment and MVPA compliance demonstrated non-linear associations (p < 0.001), with larger gains in child MVPA compliance at higher parental MVPA levels.ConclusionHigher parental MVPA was consistently associated with healthier offspring BMI status, higher likelihood of fitness attainment, and substantially greater likelihood of meeting MVPA guidelines, with suggestive dose–response patterns rather than statistically confirmed thresholds for all outcomes. Family-oriented strategies that help parents achieve at least moderate weekly MVPA may represent a scalable lever to improve child movement behaviors and related health indicators.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28484-w.

Introduction

The global rise in overweight and obesity among children and adolescents has become a major public health concern, significantly impacting cardiometabolic health, musculoskeletal function, and psychosocial well-being throughout the lifespan [1]. This challenge is particularly pronounced in China, where increasing body mass index (BMI) distributions reflect a growing prevalence of pediatric obesity [2]. International surveillance data further highlight the scale of this burden. The WHO European Childhood Obesity Surveillance Initiative (COSI), which uses standardized height and weight measurements among primary school-aged children, reported that nearly 411,000 children aged 6 to 9 years were measured across 33 countries in its fifth round of data collection from 2018 to 2020. More recent COSI data from 2022 to 2024 included approximately 470,000 children across 37 countries and showed that 25% of children aged 7 to 9 years had overweight, including obesity, and 11% had obesity [3–5]. This insufficient physical activity, compounded by poor physical fitness profiles, exacerbates the risk of obesity. These patterns highlight the need for targeted interventions that address the dual challenge of low physical activity and excess adiposity in children.

The family environment plays a pivotal role in shaping children’s physical activity behaviors and overall health outcomes. As key socialization agents, parents influence their children's activity patterns through direct role modeling, shared routines, and creating opportunities for active engagement [6]. Numerous studies suggest that higher parental physical activity is associated with greater levels of activity in children, although the strength of these associations often varies, and objective indicators such as physical fitness yield inconsistent results [7]. Beyond influencing movement behaviors, parental activity habits may also affect child adiposity through both direct and indirect pathways—by shaping household routines, dietary habits, and environmental opportunities for physical activity [8].

While interest in the intergenerational transmission of physical activity has grown, several methodological gaps hinder the interpretation of current findings. Many studies classify parental activity in binary terms or assume linear relationships between parental and child activity levels, which may obscure nuanced dose–response patterns [9]. Moreover, the potential for nonlinear relationships has not been systematically explored, despite the plausible impact of time constraints and behavioral mechanisms on activity levels. Additionally, the influence of factors such as child sex, developmental stage, and socio-economic context on these relationships remains underexplored [10]. Furthermore, research has often isolated parental moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (MVPA) as a determinant of either child physical activity or BMI, without integrating multiple child health indicators—such as MVPA compliance, physical fitness, and weight status—into a unified framework.

To address these gaps, this study utilizes data from a large-scale school-based surveillance program in Shanghai, examining the associations between parental weekly MVPA and three critical child health outcomes: overweight/obesity, MVPA guideline compliance, and fitness attainment. Parental MVPA was analyzed both categorically (quartiles) and continuously, with a focus on potential nonlinear dose–response relationships. Subgroup analyses were conducted to assess the influence of key sociodemographic factors. This comprehensive approach aims to enhance the evidence base for family-centered strategies to improve child movement behaviors, fitness attainment, and weight-related health outcomes in Chinese children and adolescents.

Methods

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional analysis utilized data from the Shanghai site of the Physical Activity and Fitness in China—The Youth Study, a school-based surveillance program conducted annually from 2016 to 2020. Data were collected each year between October and November [11, 12]. A multistage, stratified cluster sampling design was employed to ensure representation across both urban and rural areas, as well as various socioeconomic strata. Specifically, four urban districts and four rural towns were randomly selected from Shanghai’s 16 districts. Within each selected area, two primary schools, one junior middle school, and one senior high school were randomly chosen, with one to two classes per grade (Grades 4–12) selected from each school.

Across five survey waves, a total of 23,760 parent–child dyads were invited to participate. After exclusions for incomplete or invalid questionnaires, as well as missing linkage or key data, 18,479 matched parent–child pairs were included in the final analytical sample (Fig. 1). The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shanghai University of Sport. Procedures

Trained staff conducted standardized procedures across all participating schools. Students completed supervised, self-administered questionnaires in the classroom, while parents completed parallel questionnaires at home [13–15]. Each parent–child dyad was linked by a unique student identifier. Following the completion of questionnaires, students underwent standardized fitness testing, which included anthropometric measurements and performance tests, such as the 50-m sprint, sit-and-reach, standing long jump, pull-ups for boys (or one-minute sit-ups for girls), and a 1,000-m run for boys (or 800-m run for girls). All tests were administered using calibrated equipment and standard instructions.

Measures

Exposure: parental MVPA

Parental MVPA was assessed using the Chinese version of the International Physical Activity Questionnaire Short Form (IPAQ-SF) [16]. The total weekly minutes of MVPA were calculated by summing the reported minutes of moderate and vigorous activity [12]. Parents who reported no moderate or vigorous physical activity during the past week were assigned 0 min/week of MVPA and retained as valid observations if their questionnaires were otherwise complete and internally consistent. Implausible or internally inconsistent responses were excluded according to established criteria (e.g., more than 960 min per week in any activity category or more than 16 h per day) [17]. Parental MVPA was categorized into quartiles (Q1 to Q4), with Q1 representing the lowest activity group. Additionally, parental MVPA was analyzed continuously in dose–response models.

Primary outcome: child weight status

Child weight status was determined through height and weight measurements taken during on-site assessments. BMI was calculated as weight (kg) divided by height (m2) [18]. Weight status was categorized according to age- and sex-specific BMI cutoffs from the World Health Organization’s growth reference for school-aged children and adolescents [19]. For the primary analyses, children were classified as either overweight or obese versus normal weight.

Secondary outcomes: child MVPA compliance and fitness attainment

Child MVPA was assessed through a student questionnaire capturing the frequency and duration of moderate-to-vigorous physical activity over the past week. Weekly MVPA minutes were calculated and averaged to estimate daily minutes. MVPA compliance was defined as meeting the WHO guideline of at least 60 min of MVPA per day [3]. Because self-reported MVPA may contain implausibly high values due to recall error or over-reporting, a sensitivity analysis was conducted after excluding child MVPA values above the 99th percentile. This conservative, data-driven cutoff was used to assess whether extreme high-end reports influenced the estimates while preserving most of the observed distribution. The lower tail was not truncated because zero or very low MVPA values are plausible indicators of inactivity and are directly relevant to classifying non-compliance. Excluding these values could artificially increase the estimated compliance rate and weaken the contrast between inactive and active children.

Physical fitness was evaluated using the Chinese National Student Physical Fitness Standard. Test component scores were converted to age- and sex-specific points and summed to generate a total score on a 100-point scale [12]. The binary outcome, fitness attainment, was defined as achieving a rating of "excellent" or "good" (total score ≥ 80) versus a rating below this threshold [18]. This cutoff was selected because a good-or-excellent rating represents a favorable and policy-relevant benchmark in the Chinese school-based physical fitness surveillance system. Children scoring below 80, including those rated as pass or fail, were classified as not attaining this benchmark rather than as having poor fitness. Therefore, this outcome was intended to capture achievement of a desirable fitness standard, conceptually consistent with MVPA guideline compliance, rather than to define an adverse clinical endpoint.

Covariates

Potential confounders were selected based on prior research linking parental activity, child physical activity, and adiposity. Child-level covariates included age, sex, grade, ethnicity (Han or minority), residential area (urban or rural), and survey year. Family-level covariates included parental age, parental education, only-child status, and annual per capita household income, categorized as < 9,000 RMB; 9,000–30,000 RMB; 30,000–100,000 RMB; and > 100,000 RMB [20].

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SAS software (version 9.4). To account for school-level clustering, robust sandwich standard errors were used, with school as the clustering unit. Descriptive statistics were calculated for the baseline characteristics of the sample, and differences across parental MVPA quartiles were evaluated using regression models for continuous variables and Rao-Scott chi-square tests for categorical variables, adjusting for clustering.

For the association analyses, cluster-adjusted logistic regression models were used to estimate odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for each outcome across parental MVPA quartiles, with Q1 as the reference group. Separate models were fitted for the primary outcome (child weight status) and the secondary outcomes (MVPA compliance and fitness attainment). Each outcome was analyzed using both unadjusted and multivariable models, adjusting for all prespecified covariates. Linear trends across MVPA quartiles were tested by modeling the median parental MVPA value within each quartile as a continuous variable.

To characterize dose–response relationships and assess non-linearity, restricted cubic spline (RCS) logistic regression models were used with parental MVPA modeled as a continuous variable. Knots were placed at the 5th, 25th, 50th, 75th, and 95th percentiles of parental MVPA. A five-knot specification was selected a priori because the large analytic sample provided sufficient degrees of freedom while allowing flexible assessment of potential non-linearity. Methodological guidance indicates that five knots are appropriate for large samples and require four degrees of freedom for the exposure term, balancing flexibility with model parsimony [21]. For each RCS model, the overall association was assessed using a Wald test for the joint significance of all spline terms, whereas non-linearity was assessed using a Wald test for the joint significance of the non-linear spline terms. Subgroup analyses were performed based on child sex, grade group, only-child status, residential area, and household income. Effect modification was tested by including interaction terms between parental MVPA and subgroup variables in the fully adjusted models.

Primary analyses were conducted using complete case data. Sensitivity analyses examined the robustness of the findings by excluding students in examination grades (Grade 9 and Grade 12) and adjusting for school level (primary, junior middle, senior high). For the MVPA compliance outcome, an additional sensitivity analysis was performed after excluding child MVPA values above the 99th percentile to evaluate the influence of extreme high reports. In cases of non-trivial covariate missingness, multiple imputation using chained equations was performed under the assumption of missing at random, and pooled estimates were derived using Rubin’s rules. All tests were two-sided, and statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Notable differences in characteristics such as BMI, weight status, and survey year were observed across the parental MVPA quartiles (all p < 0.05). The prevalence of overweight or obesity decreased from 27.67% in Q1 to 25.02% in Q4 (p = 0.0306), which was consistent with the lower odds observed for Q4 in the regression models. Furthermore, parental characteristics, including only-child status, household income, and parental education, also varied significantly across MVPA quartiles (all p < 0.0001) (Table 1).

Parental MVPA quartiles and child weight status

Table 2 summarizes the associations between parental MVPA quartiles and child overweight/obesity. Sensitivity analyses

Sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of the primary findings. Excluding students in examination grades (Grade 9 and Grade 12) did not substantially alter the associations between parental MVPA and child outcomes. The direction and magnitude of the odds ratios remained consistent with the main multivariable models. For example, the odds of child overweight or obesity in the highest MVPA quartile (Q4) compared to Q1 were adjusted OR 0.88 (95% CI 0.80–0.98, p = 0.0276), showing no major deviation from the primary analysis.

Further adjustments for school level (primary, junior middle, senior high) showed no significant attenuation of the associations. The adjusted OR for the Q4 vs Q1 comparison remained 0.85 (95% CI 0.75–0.96, p = 0.0042), reinforcing the robustness of the associations.

For the MVPA compliance outcome, excluding child MVPA values above the 99th percentile produced similar estimates. The highest parental MVPA quartile remained strongly associated with meeting the MVPA guideline compared with Q1 (adjusted OR 1.78, 95% CI 1.60–1.98, p < 0.0001), indicating that the association was not driven by extreme high-end self-reported values.

For cases with non-trivial covariate missingness, multiple imputation using chained equations was applied under a missing-at-random assumption. The imputation models showed pooled estimates consistent with the complete-case analyses. For instance, the association between parental MVPA and child overweight or obesity remained significant with an adjusted OR 0.86 (95% CI 0.76–0.98, p = 0.0187), confirming the stability of the findings even with imputed data.

Discussion

Childhood overweight and obesity remain highly prevalent in China, and identifying modifiable, family-level determinants of children’s movement behaviors is increasingly important for prevention. In this large school-based surveillance sample from Shanghai (2016–2020), higher parental weekly MVPA was consistently associated with a more favorable child health profile across BMI-defined overweight/obesity, physical fitness attainment, and MVPA guideline compliance. Notably, the associations differed by sociodemographic subgroups and exhibited interpretable dose–response patterns. Compared with the lowest parental MVPA quartile, the highest quartile was associated with lower odds of child overweight/obesity and higher odds of achieving fitness standards and meeting the 60 min/day MVPA guideline. For overweight/obesity, however, the RCS analysis did not show statistically significant non-linearity, indicating that the association may be closer to a gradual inverse relationship than a clear threshold effect. In contrast, MVPA compliance showed stronger evidence of non-linearity, with larger gains at higher parental MVPA volumes. Given the cross-sectional design, these findings should be interpreted as associations rather than evidence of a one-way pathway from parental MVPA to child outcomes, because children’s weight status, sport participation, or activity preferences may also influence parental activity behaviours.

Higher parental MVPA was associated with lower odds of child overweight/obesity, supporting the view that parental activity habits may represent an upstream correlate of pediatric adiposity. This pattern is broadly consistent with prior observational evidence linking more active parents with leaner offspring [22] and is plausible through behavioral and contextual mechanisms. More active parents may shape children’s energy balance through role modelling and social learning, as well as through concrete family practices such as co-participation in activity, active transport choices, weekend routines, and household limits on sedentary time [21]. Parental MVPA may also co-occur with a broader health-oriented home environment—healthier food availability, more regular sleep routines, and greater support for organized sport—which can shift long-term energy balance and reduce obesity risk [23].

Several features of our findings may help refine interpretation. First, the association was clearest for the highest MVPA quartile, suggesting that, in real-world settings, modest differences in parental activity may be insufficient to offset dominant drivers of weight gain, including academic time pressure, screen-based leisure, and obesogenic food environments. Second, subgroup patterns pointed to important contextual dependence. The inverse association was more evident in boys than girls, potentially reflecting sex differences in activity opportunities, parental encouragement, and sociocultural norms around sport participation, as well as sex-specific adiposity patterning during adolescence [24]. Associations were stronger in urban than rural students, which may indicate that parental support is more actionable in environments with greater access to facilities, parks, and active transport infrastructure; rural contexts may be shaped more by unmeasured occupational activity, commuting constraints, or fewer organized opportunities [25]. Developmental heterogeneity was also notable, with clearer signals in Grades 4–6 and 10–12 but not 7–9, a period often characterized by heightened academic demands and reduced discretionary time, which may weaken translation of parental activity into shared routines. Stronger associations among only children and middle-income households may similarly reflect differences in parental time and resource allocation, with only-child households potentially concentrating support on a single child, and middle-income families being better positioned to translate parental behaviors into opportunities than families facing more binding resource constraints [26]. Although the spline curve suggested lower estimated odds beyond approximately 127 min/week and attenuation near 560 min/week, the non-linearity test was not significant. These values should therefore be viewed as descriptive points on the curve rather than evidence of a definitive threshold. Behaviorally, this pattern may still be plausible because moderate parental MVPA may reflect more active family routines and shared opportunities for movement, whereas additional parental MVPA may occur in domains less directly shared with children, such as commuting or adult-only exercise sessions [27]. Wider uncertainty at the highest MVPA levels also cautions against over-interpreting the extreme right tail, where sparse data and greater measurement error are likely.

Parental MVPA was positively associated with children’s fitness attainment, indicating that children of more active parents were more likely to achieve an excellent or good rating on the Chinese National Student Physical Fitness Standard. This endpoint reflects attainment of a favorable national fitness benchmark rather than the presence or absence of poor or unqualified fitness. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted as associations with achieving a desirable school-based fitness standard, consistent with Chinese policy targets for student physical fitness surveillance, rather than as direct evidence on risk factors for adverse fitness status. This supports the hypothesis that parental activity may shape children’s fitness attainment through modelling, co-participation, and the establishment of an activity-supportive home routine that accumulates into functional benefits over time. The association appeared stronger among girls, urban students, non-only children, and high-income households, consistent with the notion that parental behaviors translate more effectively into child fitness when families have greater access to safe activity spaces, organized sport, and time resources, and when siblings provide additional opportunities for shared play and informal training [28]. Weaker associations in older grades may reflect competing academic demands and developmental shifts in autonomy and peer norms, which can reduce parental influence on structured, fitness-relevant activity [29]. Importantly, the fitness dose–response relationship was non-linear, with the most favorable pattern concentrated around moderate parental MVPA and attenuation at very high volumes. This may reflect diminishing returns once a household already supports regular activity, and/or a shift in the nature of parental activity at high levels (e.g., adult-focused training) that does not necessarily increase children’s exposure to fitness-building behaviors [30]. From an intervention perspective, these findings support promoting achievable parental MVPA targets alongside explicit encouragement of family-based activity, while recognizing that structural barriers may limit translation into fitness gains in lower-resource contexts.

Consistent with the idea that parental MVPA most directly influences children’s movement behaviors, associations with child MVPA guideline compliance were stronger and more graded than those observed for overweight/obesity and fitness attainment. Prior research typically finds modest but consistent parent–child concordance in physical activity, often attributed to social learning, shared routines, and parental facilitation of opportunities for sport and active play [31]. Our findings extend this evidence by demonstrating a clear, non-linear dose–response: the likelihood of meeting the 60 min/day guideline increased beyond 127 min/week of parental MVPA and continued to rise across the exposure distribution, with particularly steep gains at higher parental MVPA levels. This association remained similar after excluding child MVPA values above the 99th percentile, suggesting that it was not driven by extreme high self-reported activity values. Subgroup patterns were broadly consistent across sex, residence, grade group, and only-child status, suggesting that parental modelling and logistical support may be relevant across developmental stages and settings. In contrast, household income showed the most evident heterogeneity: improvements were more graded and evident at lower parental MVPA levels in lower-middle-income households, whereas in other strata differences were largely concentrated among the most active parents. This pattern may reflect a combination of ceiling effects in less constrained households and structural barriers—such as time scarcity and limited access to safe activity spaces—that restrict how effectively parental activity translates into children’s daily MVPA in more constrained environments [32]. These results suggest that increasing parental MVPA to achievable weekly levels may be a practical lever for improving child MVPA compliance, but the socioeconomic gradient highlights the importance of pairing behavioral targets with environmental and programmatic supports.

Several limitations should be considered. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference, and bidirectionality remains plausible if parents increase activity in response to a child’s weight status or sport participation. For example, parents of children with overweight or obesity may become more physically active to encourage healthier behaviors or support weight management, while highly active children may also motivate parents to participate in shared physical activities. Thus, the observed associations may reflect parental modelling, child-driven influences, or reciprocal processes within the family environment. Parental MVPA and child MVPA compliance were self-reported, introducing recall and social desirability bias; differential misclassification across sex, age, and socioeconomic strata could influence subgroup contrasts and contribute to wider uncertainty at exposure extremes. Although zero and very low child MVPA values were retained because they are plausible indicators of inactivity and directly relevant to defining non-compliance, some values may still reflect under-reporting. If such under-reporting was non-differential with respect to parental MVPA, it would be more likely to attenuate than inflate the observed associations. The study was conducted in Shanghai, and differences in the built environment, school schedules, and access to organized sport may limit generalizability to less urbanized regions. Although analyses adjusted for multiple prespecified covariates, residual confounding by unmeasured factors (e.g., parental BMI and diet, household dietary practices, screen time, sleep, and neighborhood safety/opportunity) is possible. In particular, children’s eating patterns and broader family dietary behaviours were not assessed, including meal regularity, snacking, sugar-sweetened beverage consumption, and overall diet quality. These factors may influence child weight status and may also cluster with parental MVPA and other household health behaviours. In addition, parental health status was not assessed. Conditions such as diabetes, hypertension, cardiovascular disease, or other chronic illnesses may affect parents’ ability to engage in MVPA and may also reflect shared familial metabolic risk or health behaviors related to child weight status. Finally, fitness attainment was dichotomized to aid interpretability, but this may obscure meaningful shifts within the underlying score distribution, particularly in older grades. Because this binary outcome focused on achieving a good-or-excellent benchmark, it does not directly identify determinants of poor or unqualified fitness. Future studies should additionally examine poor or unqualified fitness as an adverse endpoint when sufficient event numbers are available.

Future studies should prioritize longitudinal and quasi-experimental designs to clarify temporality and test whether changes in parental MVPA precede changes in children’s MVPA, fitness trajectories, and weight status. Incorporating device-based measures of activity and sedentary time, repeated assessments across key developmental transitions, and richer data on home, school, and neighborhood environments would strengthen causal interpretation and help identify mediating pathways. Future work should also collect parental health information, including major cardiometabolic conditions, to better account for familial health background and potential confounding. Dietary assessment should also be incorporated to examine how children’s eating patterns and family dietary environments interact with physical activity behaviors in shaping weight-related outcomes. Given the income patterning observed for MVPA compliance, further work should examine how material constraints, time scarcity, and access to safe activity spaces modify the effectiveness of parental behavior change across socioeconomic contexts.

From a public health perspective, these findings reinforce the family as a central setting for improving children’s movement behaviors and downstream health. Parental MVPA appears to be a practical marker and potentially a scalable lever for increasing child MVPA compliance, with additional relevance for fitness and weight-related outcomes. The dose–response patterns suggest that achievable increases in parental MVPA may be beneficial, although the overweight/obesity curve should not be interpreted as evidence of a statistically confirmed threshold. Policy and programme efforts could therefore complement school-based approaches by embedding family-oriented strategies within community and primary care systems, alongside investments that reduce barriers to routine MVPA (e.g., safe and affordable community activity spaces, active transport infrastructure, and structured after-school opportunities). Integrating parent-focused MVPA counselling with child health services and creating supportive environments for whole-family activity may help enhance equity and amplify the population impact of obesity prevention and fitness promotion initiatives.

Conclusion

In this large school-based surveillance sample of Shanghai parent–child dyads, higher parental weekly MVPA was consistently linked to a healthier child profile across BMI-defined overweight or obesity, MVPA guideline attainment, and fitness attainment. Compared with the lowest activity quartile, the highest parental MVPA quartile showed lower odds of child overweight or obesity and higher odds of child MVPA compliance and fitness attainment. Dose–response analyses suggested a risk reduction beginning at approximately 127 min/week of parental MVPA, with attenuation after about 560 min/week for BMI status, while associations with MVPA compliance and fitness attainment were non-linear. Effect sizes varied by sex, residence, grade, only-child status, and household income, supporting targeted family-based strategies that promote achievable parental MVPA to improve child activity, fitness attainment, and healthy BMI trajectories.
