Subretinal parasitic lesion mimicking viral retinitis managed with combined medical therapy and laser photocoagulation
Authors: Thanh Ngoc Tran, Hai Xuan Ho, Thoa Thi Doan, Hong Son Cung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Parasitic infections can present with variable manifestations and may mimic viral retinitis, particularly when intraocular inflammation is prominent. We report a case of a 54-year-old woman experiencing worsening blurred vision in her right eye for over a month. She was previously diagnosed with acute retinal necrosis and treated with corticosteroids and acyclovir, but her condition continued to decline. Fundus examination showed dense vitritis and an ill-defined area of retinitis along the superior temporal arcade. Serologic tests were positive for Toxocara IgG and Toxoplasma gondii IgG, while PCR testing of aqueous humor for viral pathogens was negative. Antihelminthic therapy was initiated, resulting in gradual improvement in media clarity and revealing a subretinal parasitic lesion within the area of retinitis. Once confirmed to be extramacular, focal laser photocoagulation was performed. During follow-up, intraocular inflammation subsided, the subretinal parasite fragmented, and best-corrected visual acuity improved to 20/25 and remained stable. This case highlights that parasitic infections should be considered in the differential diagnosis of infectious uveitis that does not respond to initial treatment in regions where parasitic infections are common. Combining focal laser photocoagulation with medical therapy may help control inflammation and preserve vision when a distinct extramacular subretinal parasite is detected.

Introduction

Infectious etiologies account for a significant proportion of uveitis worldwide. In developing settings, they represent nearly half of differentiated cases, whereas reports from developed countries in the Asia-Pacific region estimate infectious causes in approximately 17% of cases, most commonly related to Herpesviridae and parasitic organisms [1]. Because many patients remain undifferentiated, the true burden of infectious uveitis may be underestimated.

Parasitic infections are notable contributors but may go unrecognized because of their diverse clinical manifestations involving either the anterior or posterior segment. Persistent inflammation unresponsive to antiviral or antibacterial therapy should prompt consideration of a parasitic etiology. Certain classic manifestations, such as peripheral granulomatous lesions or focal necrotizing retinitis, may suggest Toxocara infection or ocular toxoplasmosis, respectively.

However, in some cases, a nematode infiltrating the subretinal space has been identified as the source of inflammatory retinopathy. Gass and Scelfo characterized this entity as diffuse unilateral subacute neuroretinitis (DUSN), a nematode-associated form of parasitic uveitis [2]. DUSN is characterized by an early inflammatory phase that may present with vitritis and multifocal outer retinal lesions, followed by progressive retinal degeneration. Nematodes of varying sizes, including Toxocara canis, Ancylostoma caninum, and Baylisascaris procyonis, have been proposed as causative agents. Because serologic findings may be inconsistent, diagnosis is primarily clinical and may be confirmed by direct visualization of an intraocular worm [3].

Despite these recognized patterns, parasitic uveitis does not always follow classical descriptions. Prominent vitritis and retinitis may resemble acute retinal necrosis or other viral retinitis, potentially delaying appropriate diagnosis and management. We report a case of subretinal parasitic infection initially presumed to be acute retinal necrosis, highlighting the diagnostic challenges and therapeutic considerations when parasitic uveitis presents with atypical inflammatory features.

Case report

A 54-year-old female farmer from a rural province presented to Vietnam National Eye Hospital with moderate ocular pain, redness, and progressive blurred vision in her right eye for more than one month. Two weeks earlier, she had been evaluated at another tertiary center. Serologic testing was negative for HIV, HSV, and CMV and positive for VZV IgG. Aqueous humor polymerase chain reaction was negative for HSV, VZV, and CMV. She was diagnosed with acute retinal necrosis and treated with intravenous acyclovir and oral corticosteroids for one week; however, her symptoms continued to worsen.

She denied systemic symptoms and had no known systemic disease. She did not recall prior varicella or herpes zoster infection and had not received vaccination. She reported consuming vegetables from her home garden but denied eating raw or undercooked meat. She also reported frequent contact with soil during farming activities. She denied any previous systemic symptoms suggestive of parasitic infection, including skin rash, pruritus, fever, or flu-like illness. She did not own pets and reported no close contact with dogs or cats.

On presentation, best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) was counting fingers at 1 m in the right eye and 20/25 in the left eye. No relative afferent pupillary defect was detected. Anterior segment examination of the right eye revealed ciliary injection, mild corneal edema, scattered small keratic precipitates, anterior chamber cells (2 +) and flare (2 +), and focal posterior synechiae. The lens was clear. Vitreous examination demonstrated anterior vitreous cells (3 +) and dense vitreous opacity (3 +). Anterior chamber inflammation was graded according to the Standardization of Uveitis Nomenclature (SUN) criteria. Vitreous cells and haze were graded according to established grading systems described in the uveitis literature [4]. Through the hazy media, yellow-white areas with indistinct, feathery borders were observed along the superior temporal arcade, adjacent to and superior to the macula (Fig. 1). B-scan ultrasonography showed dense vitreous echoes with a hyperechoic retinal elevation superior to the macula. Optical coherence tomography (OCT) was limited due to media opacity. Laboratory evaluation as part of a uveitis workup revealed positive Toxoplasma gondii IgG (>650 IU/mL) with negative IgM and positive Toxocara IgG (1.63 OD). Testing for gnathostomiasis IgG, syphilis, and tuberculosis was negative. Peripheral eosinophil count, liver, and renal function tests were within normal limits.

Given limited visualization and the absence of confirmatory testing, differentiation between Toxocariasis and Toxoplasmosis was not established. Empirical therapy with trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (960 mg twice daily) and albendazole (400 mg twice daily) was initiated after discussion of potential risks and benefits. Oral prednisolone (1 mg/kg/day) was started on day 3. Topical prednisolone acetate 1% three times daily and atropine 1% twice daily were prescribed for anterior segment inflammation.

Ten days later, ocular discomfort had improved. BCVA increased to counting fingers at 4 m. Anterior segment examination showed resolution of ciliary injection and corneal edema, with anterior chamber cells (1+) and flare (1+). The pupil remained dilated with partial release of posterior synechiae under topical atropine. The lens was clear. Vitreous inflammation improved, with anterior vitreous cells reduced to (2+) while dense vitreous opacity (3 +) persisted but appeared partially clearer (Fig. 2). A 532-nm laser was applied at 340 mW, with a spot size of 200 µm and a duration of 200 ms, for a total of 57 shots. Circumferential barrier burns were first placed around the lesion to achieve mild retinal whitening, followed by direct application to the parasitic structure (15 shots). Periodic liver and renal function tests, along with complete blood counts, remained normal during treatment. Albendazole was continued, and oral corticosteroids were temporarily increased to 1 mg/kg/day to control post-laser inflammation.

One week after laser photocoagulation, BCVA remained 20/100. Anterior chamber cells were absent, with residual flare (1 +). The pupil remained dilated with released posterior synechiae. Vitreous examination showed no active anterior vitreous cells and residual vitreous opacity (2 +). The retinitis appeared inactive, and laser burns were visible surrounding and over the lesion (Fig. 4B). Corticosteroids and topical treatments were gradually reduced, and albendazole was continued.

Three weeks after laser, BCVA improved to 20/40. Anterior chamber cells were absent, with mild residual flare (1+). Vitreous opacity improved to (1+) without active vitreous cells. The subretinal structure appeared fragmented into smaller reflective segments within the atrophic area (Fig. 4C). The previous retinitis was replaced by well-demarcated retinal atrophy (Fig. 4E). Corticosteroids and topical therapy were further tapered, and albendazole was maintained.

Five weeks after laser treatment, BCVA remained stable at 20/25. Anterior chamber cells and flare were absent. Vitreous opacity remained stable at (1+) without active inflammation. The prior retinitis was replaced by localized retinal atrophy, and the subretinal structure was no longer identifiable as a distinct linear form (Fig. 5). Because of financial and geographic constraints, long-term follow-up at our facility was not possible. The patient was advised to have regular check-ups at a local eye hospital to watch for reactivation. She was also instructed on basic hygiene, such as avoiding raw or undercooked food, thoroughly washing vegetables, and undergoing periodic deworming. One month after finishing treatment, she reported stable vision and no recurrence of eye symptoms.

Discussion

Diffuse unilateral subacute neuroretinitis (DUSN) is an uncommon nematode-associated retinopathy characterized by an early inflammatory phase followed by progressive retinal degeneration. The condition is most often described in individual case reports and small clinical series, and its true prevalence remains uncertain, likely reflecting underrecognition in endemic and resource-limited settings. Because specific serologic or parasitologic confirmation is frequently unavailable, diagnosis is primarily based on clinical findings and, when possible, visualization of a subretinal worm. However, the parasite is identifiable in only 25%-40% of cases, and clinical presentation may vary depending on the host inflammatory response and the location of the organism within the subretinal space [5], [6].

This case shows that parasitic posterior uveitis may initially mimic viral retinitis. Prominent vitritis and an ill-defined area of retinitis may obscure fundus details and raise suspicion for acute retinal necrosis, especially when visualization is limited. Serum VZV IgG positivity alone has limited diagnostic value because it indicates previous exposure rather than active infection, whereas intraocular fluid PCR is more helpful in confirming viral retinitis [7]. In this patient, viral PCR results were negative, and the clinical course worsened despite antiviral treatment, prompting consideration of other infectious causes. Ocular toxoplasmosis should be considered in such cases, as dense vitritis is associated with a retinitis focus not confined to the peripheral retina. In endemic areas, other parasitic causes beyond toxoplasmosis should also be included in the differential diagnosis, even if classical fundus features are initially obscured by intense inflammation. Such diagnostic difficulty has been described previously in the early literature on DUSN, when the disease was first thought to be a viral inflammatory retinopathy. Gass and Scelfo later identified a motile subretinal nematode in patients with a similar clinical picture, establishing the parasitic origin of the syndrome. Further review of earlier cases from the 1950s indicated that what had been considered an inflammatory retinopathy of unknown cause was likely related to a subretinal nematode infection [4].

In this case, the exact nematode species could not be identified. The subretinal organism was estimated to be approximately 1059 µm in length based on fundus imaging, and elevated serum anti-Toxocara IgG levels detected by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) indicated prior exposure. Reported cases of nematode-related retinopathy generally involve two size groups: smaller worms measuring around 400–1000 µm, including Toxocara canis and Ancylostoma caninum, and larger worms measuring approximately 1500–2000 µm, such as Baylisascaris procyonis [3]. The estimated size in our case falls within the range reported for larger nematodes. However, Toxocara serology may cross-react with other helminth infections, especially in endemic areas, and therefore cannot definitively identify the causative species [8]. More specific intraocular tests, such as detection of anti-Toxocara antibodies in intraocular fluid or calculation of the Goldmann-Witmer coefficient, were not performed. While surgical removal with histopathologic examination could allow precise identification of the parasite, this invasive approach carries potential surgical risks. Moreover, precise species identification would likely have limited impact on immediate clinical management, which is primarily guided by the presence of an active subretinal parasite and associated intraocular inflammation.

Regarding management, given the positive Toxocara IgG and Toxoplasma gondii IgG results, empirical antimicrobial therapy was initiated while the exact etiology remained uncertain. Trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole was chosen as part of the initial treatment because several studies have reported comparable efficacy to the traditional pyrimethamine-sulfadiazine regimen for ocular toxoplasmosis. In addition, this regimen is widely used as an alternative because of its favorable safety profile, lower cost, and greater availability in many clinical settings [4]. Medical therapy with albendazole and systemic corticosteroids achieved partial control of intraocular inflammation but did not eliminate the visible subretinal parasite. Evidence for the ability of oral antihelminthic agents to achieve intraocular concentrations sufficient for a direct parasiticidal effect remains limited. Nevertheless, albendazole has been widely used in reported cases, either when the worm cannot be visualized, when laser therapy is not feasible, or as adjunctive treatment. Although the optimal dosage and duration have not been clearly established, regimens of 400 mg once or twice daily for several weeks have been commonly described in the literature, partly extrapolated from experience in neurocysticercosis and small clinical series of DUSN [9], [10], [11], [12]. In this case, albendazole 400 mg twice daily was continued for 9 weeks, longer than most published regimens, because of a prolonged inflammatory phase with dense vitritis, which delayed adequate visualization and laser application. Antihelminthic therapy was initiated before corticosteroids and continued through the corticosteroid taper to ensure coverage against ongoing parasitic activity. The extended course was well tolerated, with no gastrointestinal symptoms, normal liver and renal function, and normal complete blood count results on periodic monitoring, consistent with albendazole's generally favorable safety profile at standard doses [13].

As vitritis decreased and fundus visualization improved, the parasite was identified outside the macula. Focal laser photocoagulation was then used to directly target the organism and manage the inflammation. Early laser treatment has been linked to better visual outcomes, likely by reducing further inflammatory damage before permanent retinal changes occur. Additionally, it has been proposed that laser-induced disruption of the blood-retina barrier might help albendazole penetrate the eye more effectively, potentially enhancing the overall treatment response [3].

Laser parameters reported in previous cases typically include spot sizes of 200–300 µm, durations of 100–350 ms, and powers of 150–400 mW (532–577 nm lasers), titrated to achieve a light-to-moderate white burn applied directly over and surrounding the parasite [9], [10], [11], [14]. In our case, a higher power (340 mW) was required because of the surrounding retinitis to achieve adequate burns. Although some reports describe minimal post-laser inflammation, acute inflammatory reactions related to parasite death have also been documented [15]. For this reason, systemic corticosteroids were temporarily increased after laser therapy in our patient and then gradually tapered.

The patient's clinical course, including resolution of retinal edema, gradual breakdown of the subretinal parasite, and improved visual acuity, suggests that a combined approach of laser photocoagulation, antihelminthic therapy, and corticosteroids may be beneficial in selected cases with a visible extramacular parasite and significant inflammation. Because clinical presentations vary and standardized treatment protocols are lacking, management should be individualized based on lesion location, inflammation severity, and response to initial therapy.

Conclusion

Parasitic uveitis can present with varied manifestations and should be considered in endemic regions when inflammation fails to respond to initial therapy. In selected cases with a visible extramacular subretinal parasite, a combined approach using antihelminthic therapy, corticosteroids tailored to the inflammatory response, and focal laser photocoagulation may yield favorable visual outcomes.
