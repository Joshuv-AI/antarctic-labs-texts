T cell-associated immunity induced by heterologous recombinant BCG and purified protein vaccination confers cross-variant protection against SARS-CoV-2
Authors: Fábio Mambelli, Alba M Gimenez, Ana Carolina V S C de Araujo, Karla K S Ramos, Jéssica P Farias, Carla L de Freitas, Gabriela P Paludo, Nelson Cortes, Rúbens P dos S Alves, Paola Minoprio, Luciana C C Leite, Luis Carlos S Ferreira, Sergio C Oliveira
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPreviously, we have developed a recombinant BCG-based vaccine (rBCG) expressing a chimeric protein with spike and nucleocapsid epitopes (rChimera) to combine BCG’s innate immune training capacity with SARS-CoV-2-specific adaptive immune responses. The heterologous prime-boost rBCG/rChimera+Alum regimen induced strong humoral and cellular immunity, conferring protection against the ancestral Wuhan strain in K18-hACE2 mice. Here, we used knockout mice lacking functional B cells, CD4+, or CD8+ T lymphocytes expressing hACE2 to uncover the mechanisms of anti-viral immune protection, while also evaluating vaccine efficacy against JN.1. Vaccination induced high titers of anti-rChimera antibodies with modest neutralizing capacity against the Wuhan SARS-CoV-2 strain. Nonetheless, vaccinated B lymphocyte KO mice still showed reduced viral loads, suggesting that humoral immunity may not represent the predominant protective mechanism in this model. Vaccinated mice displayed a strong Th1-biased cellular profile characterized by increased IFN-γ production and multifunctional CD4+ T cell responses, alongside activated CD8+ T cells responses. CD4+ and CD8+ T cell-deficient animals exhibited loss of vaccine-associated protection, supporting an important contribution of T cells to viral control. Additionally, immunized IFN-γ knockout mice revealed only partial protection, suggesting that IFN-γ contributes to, but is not strictly required, for vaccine-induced immunity. We also observed that macrophages derived from vaccinated animals displayed enhanced inflammatory responsiveness following heterologous stimulation. Remarkably, vaccination conferred cross-protection against JN.1, despite lack of neutralization antibodies. These findings indicate that rBCG/rChimera+Alum vaccination induces an integrated innate and predominant T cell protective immunity cross-reactive with JN.1, supporting the rBCG-based platforms as a promising approach for COVID-19 vaccine development.KEYWORDS: COVID-19, SARS-CoV-2, vaccine, variants, JN.1, BCG

Introduction

The coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic, caused by the Severe Acute Respiratory Syndrome Coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2), has been one of the most disruptive global health crises in modern history. With nearly 7 million deaths worldwide since its emergence in late 2019, it has placed an unprecedented burden on health systems.1,2 Due to its high transmissibility, it quickly spread across the globe, requiring a coordinated international effort to control it. The rapid development and large-scale distribution of COVID-19 vaccines were crucial in mitigating this tragedy.3–6 Currently, vaccination has shifted the pandemic scenario to one of better clinical control, with substantially reduced rates of hospitalization and deaths.7 Nevertheless, the persistence of SARS-CoV-2 circulation continues to challenge public health, leaving the emergence of variants of concern (VOCs) and of interest (VOIs) as an ongoing threat, risking new outbreaks.8,9 Numerous mutations on spike protein have conferred immune escape to both vaccine- and infection-induced neutralizing antibodies (nAbs), jeopardizing disease control.1,10,11 Most recent Omicron sublineages, such as JN.1, have rapidly disseminated worldwide, becoming dominant in several regions, and giving rise to multiple descendants, such as KP.2, KP.3, LP.8.1, NB.1.8.1, and XFG which are under continuous international monitoring.12–15 Given the complex dynamic context of variant emergence and antigenic escape, vaccine approaches capable of eliciting broad and durable cross-variant immunity are critically needed, especially in populations where updated vaccines are not widely accessible.

Our group has previously developed a vaccination strategy based on a recombinant BCG (rBCG-ChD6) strain expressing a chimeric protein (rChimera), incorporating immunodominant epitopes from SARS-CoV-2 proteins.16 This strategy aimed to combine the strong activation of the innate immune system and training properties of BCG17–20 with the specific adaptive immune responses against SARS-CoV-2. For the rChimera design, we selected immunodominant epitopes from the spike and the nucleocapsid proteins,21,22 the latter due to its high conservation among beta-coronaviruses. We showed the rBCG priming with following boosting with rChimera in association with alum elicited both strong humoral and cellular immune responses, leading to protection against challenge with the ancestral Wuhan strain in K18-hACE2 mice. Despite these promising findings, key gaps remained unaddressed. In this study, we investigated immune mechanisms underlying vaccine-induced protection. By using knockout models and comprehensive immunological analysis, we aim to determine how this heterologous immunization regimen mediates protection. Notably, we determined that the immune protection induced by the prime-boost immunization regimen induced cross-reactive T cell-associated responses capable to neutralize the JN.1 variant and, thus, reduce immune pathology. Through this approach, our work builds upon the findings of our previous study and addresses critical gaps, providing insights for the development of next-generation COVID-19 vaccines with cross-variant protection.

Methods

Ethics statement and mice

Experiments involving animals were approved by the institutional IACUC and conducted under Brazilian Federal Law No. 11.794, with every effort made to reduce animal pain and distress. Experimental protocols were previously approved by the Ethics Committee for Animal Experimentation of the University of São Paulo (USP), under permit #5208110923. Mice were housed in ventilated rack systems under standard 12-hour light/dark cycles with environmental enrichment (red mouse igloo and foraging materials). K18-hACE2 mice (Jackson Laboratory, strain #034860) were bred with knockout strains lacking CD4 (CD4−/−, strain #002663), CD8 (CD8−/−, strain #002665) or mature B cells (μMT, Ighm−/−, #006785) to generate models for immune compartment depletion studies. In vivo experiments were performed with 6- to 8-week-old mice (n = 5), with males and females evenly distributed among experimental groups. Animals were transferred to the Biosafety Level 3 (BSL-3) facility at the Institut Pasteur of São Paulo (IPSP) for SARS-CoV-2 infection and subsequent experimental procedures.

Bacteria, proteins, viruses, and chemicals

The recombinant Mycobacterium bovis BCG and the Escherichia coli CodonPlusTM strain, both harboring the codon-optimized rChimera sequence for their respective expression system (obtained as described in16), were used for the immunization and protein expression protocols. Briefly, the rBCG was cultured to mid-exponential phase in Middlebrook 7H9 broth supplemented with 0.05% Tween 80, 0.1% glycerol and 10% oleic acid-albumin-dextrose-catalase. Subsequently, cells were then harvested by centrifugation and then suspended in 10% cold glycerol, being later stored at −80 °C. rBCG stocks were subsequently validated for rChimera expression by western blotting.

For protein expression, rChimera was produced in E. coli CodonPlus™ (Agilent Technologies, California, USA) cultured in LB medium (kanamycin 50 µg/mL) at 37 °C until reaching an OD600 nm of 0.8. Expression was induced with 1 mM isopropyl-β-D-thiogalactoside for 4 hours under the same conditions. Bacterial pellets were collected by centrifugation and lysed in phosphate buffer (10 mM Na2HPO4, 10 mM NaH2PO4, 0.5 M NaCl, 10 mM imidazole) by sonication. After centrifugation, inclusion bodies were solubilized in 8 M urea-containing buffer and purified by affinity chromatography (HiTrap column, Cytiva) using an imidazole gradient (0.1–0.2 M). The eluted fractions were dialyzed against PBS at 4 °C and protein concentration was determined using the BCA™ Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

The SARS-CoV-2 ancestral Wuhan strain (engineered to express NanoLuc luciferase while retaining infectivity, as previously reported23) and JN.1 variant were obtained from the Institut Pasteur of São Paulo virus repository and used for in vivo and in vitro assays. Viruses were cultured in VERO cells and titers were quantified as previously described.24 All reagents were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich, CO unless otherwise stated.

Immunization and challenge of mice

For in vivo experiments, groups of 6- to 8-week-old mice (n = 5, including male and female homogenously distributed throughout the experimental conditions) were immunized subcutaneously in the nape of the neck following a prime-boost strategy. Experimental groups included C57BL/6 (wild type and knock out for IFN-γ) and K18-hACE2 mice (wild type and knock out for CD4+, CD8+ T cells and mature B cells). At day 0, mice were administered the first dose of vaccine containing 1 × 106 CFU rBCG or PBS. Four weeks later, mice were boosted with 25 μg rChimera associated with 100 μg Al(OH)3 (Alum – Alhydrogel® adjuvant 2%, Invivogen) or PBS and Alum. On day 50 (3 weeks after the boost), independent experiments were carried out for each outcome: mice were (1) either intranasally challenged with 2–5 × 104 PFU SARS-CoV-2 (Wuhan or JN.1 strains) at a BSL-3 facility, (2) or sacrificed and the spleens and lungs were harvested for further cellular analysis. Challenged animals were monitored daily and euthanized 3–6 d post-infection. Blood, spleen, and lung samples were harvested and processed according to the experiment requirements.

Viral load determination by RT-qPCR

Lungs harvested 3–6 d post-infection were homogenized in TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher) using a TissueLyzer LT (QIAgen) and total RNA was extracted according to the manufacturer instructions. Viral load was quantified by real-time quantitative PCR using the AgPath-ID One-step RT-PCR Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to manufacturer recommendations. Reactions were carried out as previously described25 employing primers and probes specific for the SARS-CoV-2 Envelope (Table 1). Samples were analyzed in duplicate and compared against a standard curve generated from serial 10-fold dilutions of SARS-CoV-2 viral RNA stocks, starting from 7.4 × 1010 copies. Amplification was performed on a QuantStudio 5 Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fischer Scientific). Viral loads were interpolated from CT values relative to the curve. Alternatively, for imaging-based assessment of viral load, infected mice (n = 3) were intraperitoneally administered the nLuc substrate furimazine (1:30 in 200 µL) 10 minutes before imaging. Ex vivo bioluminescence signals from the lungs were captured using the Newton bioluminescence imaging system (Vilber) under identical exposure settings for all samples.

Determination of cytokine levels by qPCR

Cytokines levels were determined by qPCR in spleens and lungs harvested from immunized mice. Spleens were harvested 20 d after the booster dose and were mechanically disrupted, treated with ammonium-chloride-potassium buffer, and then washed and resuspended in supplemented RPMI (10% FBS, 1% penicillin-streptomycin). For cytokine determination, 96-wells plates were seeded with 1 × 106 cells/well and spleen cells were further stimulated with rChimera (25 μg/mL) or RPMI. Cells were harvested in TRIzol (Thermo Fisher Scientific) reagent for further isolation of total RNA according to the manufacturer instructions. Lungs were harvested 3 or 6 d post-infection and were homogenized in TRIzol (Thermo Fisher Scientific) reagent using a TissueLyzer LT (QIAGEN).

Two micrograms from total RNA obtained from spleens and lungs were reverse-transcribed and used for cDNA preparation as previously described.26 For cytokine determination, the cDNA obtained was submitted to quantitative PCR using SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Applied Biosystems) and the primers described in Table 1. The PCR reaction was performed in a final volume of 10 μL in a QuantStudio5 Real-Time PCR instrument (Applied Biosystems) as previously described.26 All data are presented as relative expression units, normalized to a housekeeping gene. Measurements were conducted in triplicate.

Spleen culture and determination of cytokine levels by ELISA

Spleens of immunized mice were harvested 21 d post-booster vaccination and spleen cells were cultured as previously described.16 Briefly, spleens were mechanically disrupted and then treated with ammonium-chloride-potassium buffer for erythrocyte depletion. Cells were washed and resuspended in RPMI medium containing 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. Plates were seeded with 1 × 106 spleen cells/well for culture in the presence of Polymyxin B (30 μg/mL). Cells were then stimulated with Concanavalin A (5 μg/mL), LPS (1 μg/mL), rChimera (25 μg/mL), or RPMI for 24–72 hours at 37 °C, 5% CO2. Culture supernatants were harvested for cytokine levels determination using the Duoset ELISA kit (R&D Diagnostic, Minneapolis, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Assays included CXCL10, IL-6, TNF-α and IFN-γ.

Histopathological analysis

Lungs were harvested 3 or 6 d post-challenge, fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formaldehyde and embedded in paraffin. Four micrometer-thick tissue sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) for histological analysis. The slides were scanned using a high-resolution digital slide scanner, and the resulting images were analyzed with QuPath (v.0.5.0) software. Regions of interest (ROIs) were manually annotated, and cellular density (cells/mm2) was obtained using automated cell detection algorithms followed by manual verification to ensure accuracy. Also, to assess the degree of alveolar integrity and tissue damage, threshold segmentation was applied to binarize images, allowing quantification of the proportion of non-parenchymal (air space) area relative to the total field.

Determination of anti-rChimera antibodies

Blood samples were obtained by submandibular bleeding from mice at day 50 (before challenge), and at 3 and 6 d post-infection. The levels of anti-rChimera antibodies were determined by ELISA as described elsewhere.27 In brief, microtiter plates (Sarstedt) were coated with purified rChimera (10 µg/mL) in carbonate-bicarbonate buffer and incubated overnight at 4 °C. After blocking with PBS 1% BSA, sera were serially diluted and incubated at room temperature for 1 hour. Bound antibodies were detected with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated anti-mouse total IgG (1:1500) (Promega). The colorimetric reaction was developed with 3,3,’5,5’-Tetramethylbenzidine substrate and the reaction was stopped with sulfuric acid. Absorbance was detected at 450 nm in a microplate reader (BioRad).

Assessment of the neutralizing activity from anti-rChimera antibodies

Sera from immunized mice were collected at different timepoints, heat-inactivated, and serially diluted twofold. Diluted sera were incubated with 100 TCID50 SARS-CoV-2 per well in 96-well plates for 1 hour under constant agitation. Virus-sera mixtures were then transferred to Vero E6 or CCL81 cells (2.5 × 104 cells/well) seeded in 96-well plates and incubated for 1 hour at 37 °C, 5% CO2 with constant agitation. Eight replicates were done for each sera dilution. For infection controls, DMEM was used as a negative control, and the virus incubated with PBS served as a positive control. As in vitro neutralization controls, previously available sera from CoronaVac-immunized mice were used. After incubation, virus-containing media were removed and replaced with fresh DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. Cells were maintained for 96 hours, and cytopathic effects were evaluated microscopically. Neutralization rate was calculated with the formula Neutralization % = (number of wells without CPE/total number of wells) × 100. Results were expressed as neutralization percentage vs. serum dilution.

ELISpot

Antibody- and IFN-γ-secreting cells specific to rChimera were quantified using ELISpot assays. Protocols were adapted from previously described methods for B-cell ELISpot28 and for T-cell ELISpot.29 Reagents were kindly provided by Professor Dr. Eduardo L. V. Silveira (School of Pharmaceutical Sciences, University of São Paulo). For antibody-secreting cells, ELISpot plates (Millipore) were coated with rChimera (10 µg/mL) and incubated overnight at 4 °C. Plates were washed with PBS containing 0.05% Tween 20 and blocked with RPMI supplemented with 10% FBS for 2 hours in 5% CO2 at 37 °C. Spleen cells were seeded in threefold dilutions starting at 1 × 106 cells and cultured overnight under the same conditions. After incubation, wells were washed and incubated with biotin-conjugated anti-mouse IgG (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 90 minutes at room temperature. Plates were then washed and incubated with HRP-conjugated streptavidin (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 2 hours in the dark at room temperature. Spots were revealed using 3-amino-9-ethylcarbazole (AEC) substrate (BD Biosciences), washed with running water, dried, and counted.

For IFN-γ-secreting cells, ELISpot plates were coated with anti-mouse IFN-γ capture antibody (10 µg/mL) and incubated overnight at 4 °C. Plates were washed and blocked as described above. Spleen cells (5 × 105 cells/well) were cultured overnight in the presence of rChimera (25 µg/mL) at 37 °C, 5% CO2. After incubation, the wells were washed and then incubated with biotinylated anti-mouse IFN-γ antibody for 90 minutes at room temperature. This was followed by a two-hour incubation with HRP-conjugated streptavidin in the dark at room temperature. Spots were developed using an AEC substrate (BD Biosciences), washed, dried, and then enumerated.

Flow cytometry analysis of spleens and lungs

Single-cell suspensions from spleens or lungs were seeded in 96-well round-bottom plates containing complete RPMI medium (2 × 106 cells/well for spleen cells and 1 × 105 cells/well for lung cells). Cells were stimulated with rChimera protein (25 µg/mL), Concanavalin A (5 µg/mL), or medium for 6 hours at 37 °C, 5% CO2, as previously reported,30 with adjustments. For Intracellular Cytokine Staining (ICS), brefeldin A (BD Biosciences) was added during the last 4 hours of stimulus. Stimulated cells were then labeled with LIVE/DEADTM aqua fluorescent reactive dye (Invitrogen), blocked with anti-mouse CD16/32 Fc Block (clone 2.4G2, BD Biosciences) and stained with the fluorophore-conjugated antibodies listed in Table 2. For ICS, cells were fixed and permeabilized with Cytofix/Cytoperm commercial kit (BD Bioscience) and stained according to antibodies listed in Table 2. Approximately 500,000 events were acquired for spleen cells and 70,000 events for lung cells on an LSR Fortessa flow cytometer (BD Bioscience) and analyzed using FlowJo software v10 (Tree Star). Gating strategy is depicted at Supplementary Figure 1.

Functional assessment of macrophages derived from immunized mice

Femurs and tibias of previously immunized K18-hACE2 mice (n = 3) were harvested 20 d after the booster dose with rChimera or PBS in association with alum for generation of bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) as previously described26 with modifications. BMDMs were generated in the presence of L929-conditioned medium (LCCM) and isoniazid antibiotic (5 µg/mL), the later to avoid the presence of viable BCG during downstream in vitro analysis. On the seventh day, BMDMs were washed, counted and 5 × 105 BMDMs/well were seeded in 24-well plates in RPMI with 10% FBS. Macrophage yield was assessed by flow cytometry revealing purity of approximately 95% of macrophages in all groups tested (Supplementary Figure 4). Cells were then incubated for 18 hours with RPMI or LPS (1 µg/mL); or for 4 hours with LPS 1 µg/mL plus the addition of 20 µM nigericin sodium salt in the remaining 45 minutes of incubation. Culture supernatants were harvested for IL-1β, IL-6 and TNF-α determination by ELISA using the Duoset ELISA kit (R&D Diagnostic, Minneapolis, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Statistical analysis

Data analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (La Jolla). Normality was assessed with the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. For comparisons involving two groups, Student’s t-test was applied to normally distributed data, whereas the Mann–Whitney test was used for nonparametric datasets. For experiments including more than two groups, one-way or two-way ANOVA was performed, followed by Tukey’s or Bonferroni’s post hoc tests to correct for multiple comparisons. Differences were considered statistically significant when p < .05.

Results

Immunological characterization of the chimeric vaccine against SARS-CoV-2

Immunodominant peptides derived from the spike and nucleocapsid proteins of the ancestral SARS-CoV-2 strain (Wuhan) were selected to guide the rational design of a chimeric recombinant protein as previously reported by our group.16 The resulting antigen, termed rChimera, was engineered for expression in E. coli and purified by chromatography. In parallel, the same antigenic sequence was incorporated into the Mycobacterium bovis Bacillus Calmette-Guérrin (BCG) vaccine, generating a recombinant strain (rBCG-ChD6). A heterologous prime-boost immunization scheme, consisting of a single rBCG dose followed by a booster with purified rChimera protein formulated in alum adjuvant (Figure 1(A)), conferred marked protection to K18-hACE2 mice challenged with SARS-CoV-2 ancestral Wuhan strain. Vaccinated animals were protected from infection-associated weight loss over 6 d post-challenge (Figure 1(B)), displaying significant reduction of viral load in the lungs (Figure 1(C,D)) when compared to unvaccinated controls, as previously reported.16 These results demonstrate that the rBCG/rChimera+Alum regimen effectively limits viral replication and mitigates disease severity, supporting its potential as a protective vaccine platform against SARS-CoV-2.

Despite our previous report showing this heterologous rBCG/rChimera strategy elicits robust humoral and cellular immune responses, leading to protection against challenge with the ancestral Wuhan strain, its precise mechanisms remained unclear. For this reason, a deeper understanding of the immunological mechanisms responsible for protection in this model is critical to guide the development of next-generation COVID-19 vaccine formulations.

To assess the humoral immune response elicited by the rBCG/rChimera+Alum immunization regimen, mice had blood collected both after the booster dose and after challenge. We observed that vaccination induced robust antigen-specific humoral responses, marked by high titers of anti-rChimera IgG antibodies 21 d after the booster dose (Figure 2(A) - before challenge). However, these antibodies displayed limited neutralizing activity against the SARS-CoV-2 ancestral strain in vitro, achieving 50% neutralization at a 1:5 dilution and 25% at a 1:10 dilution (Figure 2(B) - before challenge). Interestingly, ELISpot assays revealed increased frequencies of rChimera-specific antibody-secreting cells in the spleens of immunized mice 30 d post-booster dose, when the cells were restimulated ex vivo with rChimera (Figure 2(C)).

Upon SARS-CoV-2 challenge, sera analysis showed a pronounced increase in anti-rChimera antibody titers (Figure 2(A) - after challenge). This increase was accompanied by a marked elevation of anti-rChimera antibody-secreting cells in the spleen (Figure 2(C)), as well as an enrichment of plasmablast populations (CD3−CD19+CD38+CD27−CD138+) detected by flow cytometry (Figure 2(D)). The antigenic recall triggered by viral exposure not only increased the magnitude of antibody responses but also improved their functional quality, as sera collected after challenge exhibited higher neutralizing activity (75% at a 1:10 dilution and 25% at a 1:20 dilution) (Figure 2(B) - after challenge). However, intraperitoneal passive transfer of anti-rChimera-containing sera into naïve recipients failed to confer protection, as no statistical difference in the viral loads was observed against the PBS-treated controls (Supplementary Figure 2). Collectively, these findings suggest that although vaccine-induced antibodies can modestly neutralize viral entry, protection was not observed in this model for the assessed experimental settings.

Given the limited contribution of neutralizing antibodies (nAbs), we next investigated the cellular immune mechanisms underlying protection in vaccinated mice. When spleen cells were stimulated ex vivo with rChimera, qPCR analysis revealed markedly higher transcription levels of IFN-γ (Figure 3(A)), IFN-β (Figure 3(B)), TNF-α (Figure 3(C)), and IL-6 (Figure 3(D)) in vaccinated animals when compared to controls, indicating activation of both type I and type II interferon pathways. Also, culture supernatant evaluated by ELISA demonstrated similar findings at the protein level. Vaccinated animals displayed increased production of IFN-γ (Figure 4(A)), TNF-α (Figure 4(B)) and IL-6 (Figure 4(C)). However, CXCL10 significant levels were not detected (Figure 4(D)). ELISPOT assays confirmed an increase in the frequencies of IFN-γ-secreting cells specific to rChimera 30 d post-booster dose, with a significant increase observed in mice 3 d post-infection (Figure 3(E)). Taken together, these results indicate the induction of a robust Th1-skewed immune profile.

Flow cytometric analysis corroborated these findings showing that vaccinated mice displayed a significant expansion of activated CD4+ T cells producing TNF-α (Figure 5(A)), IFN-γ (Figure 5(B)) and IL-2 (Figure 5(C)) when compared with controls. Similarly, the frequency CD8+ T cells producing TNF-α was also increased in the vaccinated mice (Figure 5(D)). No significant difference was observed when assessing CD8+ T cells producing IFN-γ or IL-2 (Figure 5(E,F)). These results indicate that both helper and cytotoxic T cell compartments are effectively engaged, reinforcing the central role of cellular immunity in vaccine-induced protection.

Cellular immunity is indispensable for vaccine-mediated protection

To further elucidate the relative contribution of B and T lymphocyte subsets to the observed protection, we conducted SARS-CoV-2 challenge experiments in knockout mice expressing the transgenic hACE2 allele. As described in the methods, K18-hACE2 mice were bred with Ighm−/− cell, CD4−/−, and CD8−/− T cell deficient animals to generate hACE2-expressing mice lacking functional B cells (B KO), T CD4 cells (CD4 KO), and T CD8 cells (CD8 KO). When viral loads in the lungs were assessed 6 d post-infection, we observed vaccinated B KO mice remained protected, whereas CD4+ and CD8+ T cell-deficient mice lost protection and exhibited viral loads comparable to unvaccinated controls (Figure 6). Accordingly, vaccinated K18-hACE2 and B KO mice also presented, at 6 d post-infection, diminished weight-loss when compared to their respective unvaccinated controls (Figure 6), contrasting to CD4+ and CD8+ KO experimental groups. Interestingly, knockout mice displayed lower absolute viral loads than wild type animals (PBS-immunized controls). Cellular analysis showed constitutive depletion of CD4+ or CD8+ T cells was associated with reciprocal expansion of the remaining T cell compartment in the spleens of these mice, independently of vaccination status (Supplementary Figure 3). This is suggestive of compensatory alterations in immune homeostasis within these knockout strains and highlights comparisons between genotypes that should be interpreted cautiously.

Altogether these findings suggest that T cells, rather than mature B cells and antibodies, are the critical effectors mediating the vaccine-induced protection in this model, further supporting that the rBCG/rChimera+Alum regimen primarily elicits a T cell-dependent protective immunity.

Vaccination induces enhanced inflammatory responsiveness in macrophages upon heterologous stimulation

Given the well-established association between BCG vaccination and trained immunity, we also investigated whether the subcutaneous immunization with rBCG/rChimera+Alum regimen could induce functional alterations in bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs). For that, bone marrow cells were collected from vaccinated mice or control groups and differentiated into BMDMs (Supplementary Figure 4), being subsequently stimulated in vitro with a heterologous stimulus (LPS).

Upon LPS stimulation, BMDMs derived from vaccinated animals produced higher levels of IL-1β (Figure 7(A)) and IL-6 (Figure 7(B)) than macrophages from PBS-administered mice. In contrast, TNF-α production did not differ significantly between groups (Figure 7(C)). These findings suggest that immunization with the rBCG/rChimera+Alum regimen induces enhanced inflammatory cytokine responsiveness to heterologous stimulation, a hallmark of trained immunity induced by BCG.

The rBCG/rChimera+Alum regimen provides cross-variant protection against the SARS-CoV-2 JN.1 strain

Finally, to assess whether vaccine-induced immunity extends to currently circulating SARS-CoV-2 variants, we challenged immunized animals with the highly immune-evasive JN.1 strain. Our comparative analysis of the most recent SARS-CoV-2 lineages revealed that, although Omicron-derived subvariants display extensive mutations within the spike protein, the nucleocapsid protein remains relatively conserved, particularly within the region incorporated in our rChimera construct (Table 3). As expected, sera from vaccinated mice were subjected to in vitro neutralization assays and failed to neutralize JN.1 infection, exhibiting complete loss of neutralizing activity, even in low dilutions (Supplementary Figure 5).

Vaccinated mice maintained significantly lower pulmonary viral loads in the lungs when compared to unvaccinated controls (Figure 8(B)), indicating cross-variant protection. This was accompanied by increased frequencies of pulmonary CD3+ T cells (Figure 9(A)) when compared to PBS controls, suggesting enhanced lymphocyte accumulation in the lungs. In addition, vaccinated mice exhibited increased frequencies of Granzyme B-producing CD4+ T cells (Figure 9(B)) and CD8+ T cells (Figure 9(C)), indicating the presence of activated effector lymphocytes in the pulmonary environment. Analysis of IFN-γ-producing T cell subsets revealed a modest increase in the frequency of CD8+ T cells producing IFN-γ in vaccinated animals (Figure 9(E)), whereas no significant differences were observed in the CD4+ compartment (Figure 9(D)). Although limited by the low frequency of cytokine-producing events, these findings support the presence of a vaccine-associated pulmonary cellular immune response following SARS-CoV-2 JN.1 infection which may contribute to the diminished viral loads in the lungs of previously immunized animals (Figure 8(B)).

Corroborating these findings, histological analysis demonstrated vaccinated animals presented overall preservation of the lungs (Figure 10(B)), thin septa, and only mild inflammatory infiltration, accompanied by lower cell infiltration (Figure 10(C)) in the alveoli and a higher percentage of free alveolar spaces (Figure 10(D)). Unvaccinated controls (Figure 10(A)) presented severely disrupted lung architecture, thickened alveolar septa, diffuse inflammatory infiltrates, and marked reduction of free alveolar space, consistent with extensive inflammation (Figure 10(C,D)). Interestingly, IFN-γ-deficient mice showed partially reduced viral loads in the lungs when compared to controls (Figure 8(C)) but presented lesser ability to control viral replication as the wild type vaccinated animals (Figure 8(B)). This is suggestive that the protective mechanism does not rely exclusively on the IFN-γ pathway and that other cytokines might play a more predominant role in the protection observed in this model. However, these findings should be interpreted with caution, as they may reflect combined effects on vaccine-induced immune priming and antiviral effector responses. Altogether, these findings suggest that the rBCG/rChimera+Alum vaccination regimen elicits a T-cell dominant immune response capable of conferring protection against immune-evasive SARS-CoV-2 variants, such as JN.1.

Discussion

The COVID-19 pandemic has been one of the major threats of modern history, causing millions of deaths and heavily disrupting global health systems. Due to vaccination efforts, severe cases and mortality rates have decreased significantly, leading to the transitioning in 2023 from emergency status to long-term disease management of COVID-19, as declared by the World Health Organization (WHO).1 However, ongoing viral circulation and the emergence of immune-evasive variants like the heavily mutated Omicron sublineages remain a threat to vaccine development. For that matter, continuous investment in research for next-generation vaccines capable of inducing broad and long-lasting cross-variant immunity remains of utmost importance.

Our group has previously developed a recombinant BCG-based vaccine (rBCG-ChD6) expressing a chimeric protein containing both spike and nucleocapsid immunodominant epitopes (rChimera) from SARS-CoV-2 ancestral strain.16 This strategy was thought to combine strong innate activation conferred by BCG20 with specific adaptive responses against SARS-CoV-2 antigens. We have reported a heterologous vaccination regimen consisting of one dose of the rBCG followed by a booster of purified recombinant rChimera in association with Alum was capable to confer protection against the ancestral SARS-CoV-2 strain in K18-hACE2 mice.16 Protection was accomplished by diminished viral loads in the lungs with tissue architecture conservation. Here, we further evaluated the mechanisms involved in the protection, as well as tested the efficacy of this model against the immune-evasive JN.1 variant, aiming to guide next-generation COVID-19 vaccine design. We observed that the rBCG/rChimera+Alum immunization regimen induced both humoral and potent Th1-skewed dominant cellular immune responses, significantly reducing viral load in the lungs of challenged mice.

Regarding the humoral component, our results demonstrated the vaccination induced antigen-specific antibody responses, as evidenced by high titers of anti-rChimera IgG antibodies detected post-boost, as well as by the increased number of anti-rChimera specific antibody-secreting cells and plasmablasts in response to rChimera ex vivo stimulation. Vaccines capable of inducing great amounts of antibodies with neutralizing activity are essential for viral defense, as they lead to rapid protection against viruses by blocking viral entry into host cells, mitigating infections at early phases.33,34 For this reason, the rChimera protein was designed incorporating key regions derived from the Receptor Binding Domain (RBD) from spike, including epitopes with high binding affinity for human MHC I and II alleles and also regions predicted for several B cell epitopes which could afford to humoral response mediated by T cell help.16 However, the contribution of antibodies to the protection appears to be limited in this model, as they exhibited low neutralizing activity against infection in vitro and failed to confer protection upon intraperitoneal passive transfer. This suggests a limiting factor regarding their functional quality rather than abundance. In these settings, it is worth considering that neutralization assays may not fully capture the spectrum of antibody-mediated antiviral functions, and their sensitivity can vary depending on assay conditions. In addition, passive transfer assays might be influenced by variables such as antibody dose, distribution, functional quality, breadth, and others, which may impact their ability to reveal protective effects.

Additionally, using μMT (Ighm KO) animals expressing hACE2, which are mice lacking mature B cells, we demonstrated that vaccinated mice remained protected upon viral challenge. However, it is important to highlight that μMT strain lacks mature B cells due to a targeted disruption of the immunoglobulin heavy chain μ constant region, resulting in impaired B cell development. While the maintenance of protection in μMT mice suggests that antibodies are not the primary mediators of viral control under these conditions, the use of this model does not allow us to fully exclude a contributive role of B cells or antibody-mediated functions, including potential Fc-mediated effector functions or synergistic interactions with cellular immunity. Taken together, while our data suggest that antibodies are not sole or predominant mediators of protection in this model, the contribution from humoral immunity cannot be completely excluded and further studies employing complementary approaches (such as acute B cell depletion) would be required to more precisely define the role of B cells in vaccine-induced protection in this model.

We observed that the prime-boost immunization regimen induces a potent Th1-skewed cellular immune profile, characterized by elevated transcription of IFN-γ, IFN-β, TNF-α, and IL-6 cytokines, accompanied by elevated production of IFN-γ, TNF-α, and IL-6 cytokines, alongside the expansion of polyfunctional CD4+ and CD8+ T cells producing cytokines upon antigen restimulation. Also, genetic ablation of either CD4+ or CD8+ T subsets abrogated vaccine efficacy, demonstrating the importance of the cellular components to reduced viral burden. The T cell-dependent response profile aligns with protective immune phenotypes typically associated with both SARS-CoV-2 natural infection and vaccine-induced immunity previously reported.35–37

Among the cellular subsets evaluated, CD4+ T cells appear to play a crucial role in the protective immunity induced by rBCG/rChimera+Alum vaccination. The increased frequency of CD4+ T cells producing IFN-γ, TNF-α, and IL-2 suggests a multifunctional phenotype associated with enhanced antiviral efficacy. Particularly, IFN-γ production is consistent with a Th1-oriented antiviral response, previously associated with adequate protection against respiratory viral infections.38,39 TNF-α may contribute synergistically with IFN-γ by amplifying macrophage activation and local inflammatory responses. The partial protection observed in IFN-γ-deficient mice against JN.1 challenge suggests that compensatory cytokine pathways within the T cell compartment might also play a role in viral control, but further experiments are required to confirm this hypothesis. It is also important to highlight the fact that IFN-γ deficiency may have had some effect on BCG priming, given the known role of IFN-γ in controlling mycobacterial replication and shaping adaptive immunity.40,41 Therefore, the partial loss of protection observed in IFN-γ knockout mice should be interpreted with caution, as it may reflect combined effects on vaccine-induced immune priming and vaccine-induced antiviral effector responses. Nonetheless, these observations taken altogether highlight the versatility of T cell immunity elicited by this heterologous vaccine.

However, it is important to highlight the protection induced by this platform likely involves coordinated interactions between adaptive and innate immune compartments rather than isolated contributions of antibodies or T cells alone. In addition to the adaptive immune responses observed in vaccinated animals, our findings also suggest the induction of functional innate immune alterations associated with the subcutaneous immunization with the rBCG-based regimen. BMDMs derived from vaccinated mice exhibited increased IL-1β and IL-6 production following heterologous stimulation with LPS, consistent with previous reports describing long-term innate immune reprogramming induced by BCG vaccination.26,42 Recent studies have further demonstrated that BCG vaccination can also reshape pulmonary immunity. As observed by others, interaction between adaptive lymphocytes and tissue-level innate immune responses leads to IFN-associated activation of alveolar macrophages, myeloid, and epithelial cells, and therefore improved control of viral infections.43–45 In this context, our findings support the possibility that the protective phenotype induced by this immunization regimen may emerge from integrated trained innate and adaptive immune responses rather than exclusively T cell-mediated mechanisms. Further investigation is required to elucidate the precise contribution of these innate pathways to the protection observed in this model.

An interesting observation in our study is how vaccination with the prime-boost strategy led to reduced viral loads in mice challenged with JN.1 despite anti-rChimera antibodies completely losing neutralizing activity. We also observed in these mice increased lymphocyte infiltrates, higher frequencies of granzyme B-producing CD4+ and CD8+ T lymphocytes, CD8+ T cells producing IFN-γ and preserved lung architecture. These results suggest that vaccine-induced protection relies on mechanisms not exclusively dependent on nAbs, consistent with the pattern observed against the ancestral strain. This observation aligns with the growing evidence supporting a role for T cell-mediated immunity in cross-variant protection against SARS-CoV-2 variants of concern, which continue to accumulate mutations in the spike protein.11,46,47 Here, the rChimera construct was designed containing several immunodominant epitopes from the nucleocapsid protein, which is highly conserved across SARS-CoV-2 variants.16,22 In contrast, the spike protein accumulates a higher number of mutations, particularly in antigenically relevant regions. This differential pattern of epitope variability suggests nucleocapsid-derived epitopes may be more consistently preserved and therefore more likely to sustain T cell recognition across variants. However, antigen-specific responses targeting nucleocapsid vs. spike were not directly assessed in this study, and thus the relative contribution of each antigen cannot be determined. Therefore, the extent and mechanism of protection against JN.1 remain to be fully established, and future studies (such as adoptive transfer and epitope mapping assays) are required to confirm these preliminary indications.

In conclusion, the heterologous rBCG/rChimera+Alum vaccination regimen elicits a potent immune response against Wuhan strain and extends protection against JN.1 variant. Our findings support an important contribution of T cell-mediated immunity to vaccine-induced protection while also suggesting innate immune mechanisms associated with the rBCG platform might play a role in the protective phenotype observed in this model. This strategy provides conceptual basis for durable and broad-spectrum vaccination against evolving respiratory viruses such as SARS-CoV-2.
