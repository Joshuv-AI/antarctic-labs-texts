Synthesis, Biological Evaluation, and Docking Study of Rhinacanthin-Related Derivatives as α‑Glucosidase Inhibitors
Authors: Felicitas Ene, Dung Thi Kim Le, Duangjai Todsaporn, Thanyada Rungrotmongkol, Warinthorn Chavasiri
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study describes the design, synthesis, and in vitro evaluation of 25 rhinacanthin-related derivatives as potential α-glucosidase inhibitors. The synthesized compounds were characterized using 1H and 13C NMR spectroscopy as well as mass spectrometry. Most of the synthesized compounds demonstrated significant inhibitory activity, with IC50 ranging from 1.45 ± 0.04 to 31.91 ± 3.31 μM, compared to the positive control, acarbose (IC50 = 836.00 ± 47.19 μM). In particular, compounds 6 and 12 exhibited the highest anti-α-glucosidase activity and acted as competitive inhibitors, with K i values of 14.11 and 4.14 μM, respectively. Molecular docking analysis was performed and revealed that key π–π stacking interactions with residue F300, together with hydrogen bonds involving N241, H279, E304, and R312, contributed significantly to the enhanced α-glucosidase inhibitory activity of the active compounds. These findings suggest that compounds 6 and 12 are promising candidates for developing α-glucosidase inhibitors with potential antidiabetic effects.

Introduction

Diabetes, a highly prevalent chronic disease, results from metabolic abnormalities that lead to hyperglycemia. Diabetes has been categorized into type 1 diabetes, type 2 diabetes (T2D), gestational diabetes mellitus, and other forms, with T2D being the most common globally. The International Diabetes Federation (IDF) reported that approximately 537 million adults aged 20 to 79 had diabetes in 2021, with projections of 643 million by 2030 and 783 million by 2045. T2D is characterized by elevated blood glucose due to the body’s resistance to insulin and inadequate insulin production by pancreatic β-cells. As the disease progresses, it leads to severe complications such as foot ulcers, cognitive deficits, neuropathy, kidney disorders, and cardiovascular disease. Current treatments do not effectively address the underlying metabolic dysfunction of diabetes. Type 1 diabetes is treated with insulin injections, while type 2 diabetes often involves oral medications and lifestyle changes. Noninsulin therapies aim to reverse underlying physiological abnormalities causing elevated blood sugar levels. Such therapies hinder glucose absorption, suppress hepatic gluconeogenesis, and prevent kidney reabsorption of glucose. Antidiabetic medications with these effects include α-glucosidase inhibitors, metformin, and sodium-glucose co-transporter-2 (SGLT-2) inhibitors. Among these, α-glucosidase inhibitors are a noninvasive treatment option with minimal side effects.

Inhibiting α-glucosidase helps control postprandial hyperglycemia by temporarily delaying the intestinal carbohydrate absorption, thereby suppressing the rise of postprandial hyperglycemia. While acarbose, voglibose, and miglitol are oral antidiabetic agents available on the market, they are associated with gastrointestinal side effects. Recently, there has been a growing effort to identify more effective α-glucosidase inhibitors.

Over the past few decades, there has been an increasing focus on the use of natural products as therapeutic compounds, particularly in the context of preventing and treating type 2 diabetes. Natural products have played an important role as a valuable source of effective diabetes treatments. Galega officinalis contains guanidine compounds like galegine, which have been shown to reduce blood glucose levels. Diri et al. reviewed 290 plant-derived bioactive compounds with α-glucosidase inhibitory activity, including extracts from Chrysophyllum caimito, Ensete superbum, Mallotus japonicus, Xylosoma congestum, and Podocarpus macrophyllus.

Rhinacanthus nasutus (R. nasutus) (L.) Kruz, commonly known as “dainty spurs” or “snake jasmine”, is an ethnomedicinal shrub belonging to the Acanthaceae family. It is extensively cultivated in Southeast Asia and has been used for decades in traditional medicine. Research has shown that the rhinacanthin extract possesses antidiabetic properties. The methanol extract was reported to ameliorate hyperglycemia in streptozotocin-induced diabetic rats. A recent study reported the formulation of orodispersible tablets for pediatric patients using rhinacanthin-rich extract (RRE). By combining the RRE with β-cyclodextrin and PVP K30, the formulation significantly enhanced the solubility and potentially the bioavailability of the active compounds. This innovative approach supports the therapeutic potential of rhinacanthin in managing diabetes.

Rhinacanthin C (RC) is the major compound found in R. nasutus. Reports have shown that it exhibits various pharmacological activities, including antidiabetic, anticancer, anti-inflammatory, antiviral, antiallergic, and antiproliferative effects. In 2017, a study reported on the AG inhibitory effects of three rhinacanthinsRC, rhinacanthin D (RD), and rhinacanthin N (RN)and found that their α-glucosidase (AG) inhibition varied according to their ester substituents, with RC having the best AG inhibitory activity. Molecular docking analysis from this work revealed that the formation of hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions between RC and α-glucosidase are key factors enhancing the activity. Therefore, in this work, we have chosen RC as a parent compound for modification. In addition, benzoates and cinnamate esters have been reported to show significant AG inhibitory activity. , This evidence from the literature has motivated us to design and synthesize rhinacanthin-related ester derivatives with various ester substituents, including alkyl, benzoate, and cinnamate derivatives, for AG inhibition (Figure ).

Experimental Section

Chemistry

General

α-Glucosidase from Saccharomyces cerevisiae (EC 3.2.1.20) and p-nitrophenyl α-d-glucoside (p-NPG) were supplied by Sigma-Aldrich. The other commercially available reagents were purchased and used without further purification. RN0 was obtained from the hydrolysis of Rhinacanthin C isolated from Rhinacantus nasutus. 1H and 13C NMR spectra were recorded in CDCl3 by using a JEOL NMR 500 MHz spectrometer for 1H and a 125 MHz spectrometer for 13C. High-resolution mass spectral analysis (HRMS) data were measured by means of the ESI technique. All solvents used in this research were distilled prior to use except those which were reagent grades. Thin layer chromatography (TLC) was performed on aluminum sheets precoated with silica gel (Merck Kieselgel 60 PF254).

Extraction and Isolation of Rhinacanthin C

Dried roots of Rhinacanthus nasutus (L.) Kruz (10 kg) were ground and extracted with EtOAc under reflux for 3 h, and the procedure was repeated twice. The extract was filtered and concentrated in vacuo by using a rotatory evaporator to obtain a dark-brown crude extract (112 g, 1.12% yield). The EtOAc extract was subjected to silica gel column chromatography (CC) eluting with n-hexane–EtOAc in order of increasing polarity (9.5:0.5, 9:1, and 8:2) to afford four fractions (A–D). Fraction B (47 g) was further rechromatographed on silica gel CC and eluted with n-hexane–EtOAc (9.5:0.5) to furnish two subfractions (B1 and B2). Oiling out of B2 with MeOH afforded RC as a red oil (10 g, 8.9% yield). It was further characterized using 1H and 13C NMR, and the data were compared with previous reports in the literature. The obtained compound was identified as RC.

Preparation of Starting Material RN0

2-Hydroxy-3-(3-hydroxy-2,2-dimethylpropyl)-1,4-naphthoquinone (RN0, 1,4-naphthoquinone alcohol) can be obtained by the hydrolysis of either RC or the R. nasutus extract. In this research, both methods were investigated, but the latter was preferred due to its higher yield. The isolation of RC proved to be more complex and time-consuming. Hydrolysis of RC

RN0 and RN1 can be obtained from the hydrolysis of RC in aqueous NaOH and EtOH, with yields of 24 and 76%, respectively. RN1 was identified as rhinacanthone, one of the natural products derived from R. nasutus. The formation of RN1 was observed during the acidic workup of the hydrolysis of RC. It could be explained that under strongly acidic conditions such as HCl, the hydrolyzed product (RN0) formed a closed-ring structure (RN1), which caused a poor yield of the targeted product (RN0). As reported by Howie and co-workers, RN1 can be converted to the open-ring yellow diol (RN0) by refluxing with aqueous NaOH. To achieve a better yield of RN0, compound RN1 can be converted to RN0 with a yield of 99%.

Hydrolysis of the EtOAc Extract of R. nasutus

The naphthoquinone esters commonly called “rhinacanthins” are a major class of compounds found in R. nasutus. At least 14 naphthoquinone esters have been isolated from the roots, including rhinacanthins A–D and G–Q. All rhinacanthins share a similar core structure, 2-hydroxy-3-(3-hydroxy-2,2-dimethylpropyl)-1,4-naphthoquinone but differ in their ester substituents, which is analogous to that of compound RC. In order to increase the quantity of RN0, the EtOAc extract of R. nasutus was hydrolyzed by refluxing with aqueous NaOH and EtOH to obtain RN0 and RN1. Then, RN1 was further converted to RN0 in good yield, as explained in this work. This is the first report of obtaining RN0 from the hydrolysis of the R. nasutus extract in excellent yield.

Structural identification of compounds RN0 and RN1 was carried out by using 1H and 13C NMR analyses. The structures of RN0 and rhinacanthone (RN1) were elucidated by comparison with the reported NMR databases.

General Procedures for the Preparation of Ester Derivatives 1–25

To a stirred solution of RN0 (104.0 mg, 0.4 mmol) in DCM (10 mL) were added carboxylic acid (0.4 mmol), EDC·HCl (92.85 mg, 0.45 mmol), and DMAP (61.08 mg, 0.45 mmol) with stirring for 6 h at room temperature. The reaction was monitored using TLC. The reaction mixture was then diluted with DCM (20 mL) and washed with 5% HCl solution (3 × 10 mL). Subsequently, the organic layer was washed with aqueous NaHCO3 solution (3 × 10 mL) and brine and then dried over anhydrous Na2SO4, filtered, and concentrated. The resulting crude mixture was purified by column chromatography using n-hexanes–EtOAc (9:1). The structures were elucidated by using 1H and 13C NMR spectroscopic data.

Results and Discussion

Isolation and Identification of RC

The roots of Rhinacanthin nasutus were collected from Mueang district, Sakon Nakhon province, Thailand in 2022. The air-dried roots were ground and extracted by refluxing with EtOAc. The solvent was evaporated in vacuo using a rotatory evaporator to obtain the crude extract. Isolation and purification of RC were carried out using column chromatography and recrystallization methods. The structure of the isolated compound was elucidated by spectroscopic analysis, including 1H and 13C NMR.

Synthesis of 1,4-Naphthoquinone Ester Derivatives 1–25

In order to obtain naphthoquinone ester derivatives 1–25, RC was hydrolyzed to 2-hydroxy-3-(3-hydroxy-2,2-dimethyl­propyl)-1,4-naphthoquinone. Target compounds 1–25 were then prepared through esterification with a variety of aliphatic, cinnamic, and benzoic acids, as shown in Scheme . All synthetic derivatives were purified via column chromatography, and their structures were elucidated by 1H NMR, 13C NMR, and HRESIMS.

It is worth noting that all RN0 derivatives (1–25) showed stronger inhibitory activity than the starting material RN0 (IC50 > 200 μM) and acarbose (IC50 836.00 ± 47.19 μM). Among them, compound 12 exhibited the strongest inhibitory activity (IC50 = 1.45 ± 0.04 μM). To gain insight into the SAR, the RN0 derivatives were divided into two categories: group A (compounds 1–12) and group B (compounds 13–25). Group A focused on introducing various chain lengths from 1 to 18 and incorporating double bonds to investigate the effects of these modifications on activity. In group B, compounds 13 (benzoic acid derivatives) and 17 (cinnamic acid derivatives) served as template molecules, and different functional groups (methoxy, chlorine, nitro, and methylenedioxy) were added to examine their effects.

In group A, the length of the chain in the aliphatic esters significantly influenced the activity, with longer chains enhancing the activity. For instance, compounds 1–4 exhibited a very small inhibitory effect, less than 70%, while compounds 5–8 displayed potent activity, exceeding 70% at 50 μM. The improvement in AG activity for compounds with longer chains could be due to increased hydrophobicity. As the carbon chain increases, hydrophobic interactions between the derivatives and the nonpolar amino acid residues of AG become stronger, leading to improved inhibitory activity for longer-chain derivatives. This is in line with the findings of Rita et al., who identified synthesized chrysin derivatives as nonsaccharide α-glucosidase inhibitors, and Chen et al., who investigated novel embelin derivatives for α-glucosidase inhibition. Both studies highlighted the role of hydrophobicity in the enzyme activity. Furthermore, when the length was similar, the introduction of double bonds in the long alkyl chains slightly enhanced the activity. For example, compounds 3 and 9 have the same long chain length, but 9 exhibited better inhibitory activity than 3. Similarly, the findings for compounds 8, 10, 11, and 12, which also have similar lengths, highlighted the significance of double bonds in AG inhibition. Increasing one double bond enhanced the activity by nearly 7.93-fold, with the IC50 value of 25.68 ± 1.14 μM for compound 8 compared to the IC50 value of 3.24 ± 0.04 μM for compound 10. Further introduction of two and three double bonds improved the activity slightly, with IC50 values of 3.10 ± 0.27 μM (11) and 1.45 ± 0.04 μM (12), making compound 12 the most effective in the series. This enhancement is likely due to increased hydrophobic interactions between the compounds and AG.

In the second series (group B), cinnamate esters typically demonstrated greater activity than benzoate esters. Specifically, compounds 17–20, which contain a cinnamic moiety, exhibited higher α-glucosidase inhibitory activity than compounds 13–16, which contain a benzoic moiety, despite having the same substituent group. Among compounds 13–16 (containing benzoyl), compound 13 without a substituent group displayed no activity at 50 μM. Compounds 14–16 with different substituent groups demonstrated stronger inhibitory activity than compound 13, suggesting that the introduction of substituent groups (such as NO2, OCH2O, or Cl) could effectively enhance the inhibitory activity. Similarly, substituent groups at the benzene ring of cinnamic acid (18–21) displayed stronger inhibitory activity than 17 (without a substituent group), reconfirming that the introduction of a substituent group was vital for α-glucosidase inhibition. Furthermore, compounds with electron-donating substituents, specifically compound 19 (IC50 = 28.84 ± 0.74 μM), demonstrated enhanced inhibitory activity compared to compound 18 (IC50 = 31.91 ± 3.31 μM). Among the halogen substituents studied, the 4-chloro group in compound 20 exhibited the most potent inhibitory effect, with an IC50 value of 17.61 ± 1.49 μM. To further investigate the impact of disubstitution and the position of chlorine atoms, we synthesized and tested compounds 22 (2′,4′-diCl), 23 (3′,4′-diCl), and 24 (2,6-diCl). The results showed that dichloro at positions 2′,4′ (22) and 3′,4′ (23) significantly enhanced the inhibitory activity, reducing the IC50 values to 10.59 and 10.04 μM, respectively. However, when the chlorine groups were moved to the 2′,6′ positions, as in compound 24, the activity decreased, resulting in an IC50 value of 19.34 ± 1.34 μM. Thus, these findings imply that the 4′ position is very crucial to the AG inhibitory activity. Finally, a comparison 25 (IC50 = 12.60 ± 0.64 μM) with 19 (IC50 = 28.84 ± 0.74 μM) suggested that introducing an additional double bond into the carbon linkage can increase the anti-α-glucosidase activity.

Overall, several key factors are important for further optimizing the structures of derivatives 1–25 as α-glucosidase inhibitors. First, extending the chain length of the derivatives enhances the α-glucosidase inhibitory activity, with a significant increase observed when additional double bonds are introduced. Second, the incorporation of both electron-donating groups (such as methylenedioxy) and electron-withdrawing groups (such as nitro, chlorine, and bromo) in the para position of the aromatic ring is crucial for improving anti-α-glucosidase activity. Additionally, disubstitution with chlorine at positions 2,4 and 3,4 further enhances this activity (Figure ).

Structure–activity relationship of synthesized compounds 1–25.

One limitation of this study is the absence of OH-substituted aromatic derivatives in the second series (group B). Although phenolic OH groups can enhance α-glucosidase inhibition through hydrogen bonding, their incorporation posed synthetic challenges. Specifically, the presence of free OH groups on both alcohol and acid components interfered with esterification, reducing yields and selectivity. Employing protecting groups could resolve this but was impractical due to the limited availability of the natural alcohol precursor. Future work may address this limitation by using alternative synthetic strategies or improving access to starting materials.

Kinetic Study

After the AG inhibitory activity of all of the derivatives was evaluated, the two most potent compounds, 6 and 12, were selected for the kinetics study to understand the mode of inhibition of each compound and their interaction with the enzyme. Lineweaver–Burk plots were used to determine the type of inhibition, as shown in Figures A and A. The resulting plots of 1/V vs 1/[S] of compounds 6 and 12 generated a group of straight lines that intersected in the y axis. In addition, the data showed no change in V max, while K m increased. This result suggests that the mode of inhibition is competitive. In a competitive mode, the inhibitors bind to the active site of the enzyme, preventing the substrate from binding and leading to an increase in substrate concentration (K m). In addition, the inhibition constants (K i) of compounds 6 and 12 to α-glucosidase were further calculated to be 14.11 and 4.14 μM, respectively, based on internal plots of slope versus the concentration of the tested compounds (Figures B and B).

Molecular Docking

Molecular docking was performed to identify the binding interaction between the synthesized compounds and the α-glucosidase enzyme. As can be seen from Figure , the top-ranked conformations of the selected compounds were effectively inserted into the active site of the α-glucosidase enzyme and engaged in various interactions with amino acid residues of the target enzyme. The binding of 6 and 12 was calculated to be −8.2 and −8.7 kcal/mol, respectively. Compounds 6 and 12 formed identical hydrogen bonds with N241, H279, E304, and R312 residues in the binding pocket of α-glucosidase. The presence of a hydrogen bond between E304 and α-glucosidase inhibitors has been reported in several previous studies. − The long chains of compounds 6 and 12 can be inserted into the hydrophobic region of the active site, which also contributes to their binding susceptibility. While both compounds 6 and 12 formed similar alkyl−π interactions with Y71, H111, F157, F158, F177, and A278, as well as a π–π interaction with F300, compound 12 exhibited additional interactions, including alkyl−π interactions with F311 and R321, sigma−π interactions with F177 and H239, and an anion−π interaction with E304. This may explain why the activity of compound 12 (IC50 = 1.45 ± 0.04 μM) is slightly better than that of compound 6 (IC50 = 2.60 ± 0.64 μM). The additional interactions might have helped stabilize the 12/AG complex. These results suggest that hydrophobic and hydrogen bond interactions could play an important role in improving the AG inhibitory activity of the active compounds.

Conclusions

We synthesized and characterized a series of rhinacanthin ester derivatives (1–25) as potent α-glucosidase inhibitors. Most of the synthesized compounds exhibited better α-glucosidase inhibition (IC50 = 1.45 ± 0.04 to 31.91 ± 3.31 μM) compared to the positive control, acarbose (IC50 = 836.00 ± 47.19 μM). In the rhinacanthin aliphatic series, an increased chain length improved the AG inhibitory activity due to enhanced hydrophobicity. Compounds with longer chains displayed higher activity than those with shorter ones, with compound 12 showing the highest activity (IC50 = 1.45 μM). In the second series, cinnamate esters demonstrated better activity than the benzoate analogs. The position, nature, and number of substituents on the benzoate or cinnamate esters significantly affected their activity. Lineweaver–Burk plot analysis of potent compounds 6 and 12 revealed that they inhibited AG via a competitive mechanism. Additionally, molecular docking studies revealed that compounds 6 and 12 were bound to α-glucosidase through hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions. In summary, this study has identified a new class of rinacathin ester derivatives as promising lead candidates for the development of novel α-glucosidase inhibitors. Future research may focus on comprehensive in vivo validation, detailed toxicity assessment, and strategic structural modifications to develop derivatives with enhanced drug-like properties suitable for clinical applications.
