A Novel Rabbit Model of Angle-Closure Glaucoma Via Ciliary Block and Elevated Trans-Lens Pressure Difference
Authors: Xuanli Zheng, Fengping Zhao, Huan Chen, Haishuang Lin, Jiaqian Li, Junfan Zhang, Xiaojie Wang, Jingjing Zuo, Guangying Luo, Yuanbo Liang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeAngle closure is a hallmark of primary angle-closure glaucoma (PACG), but the upstream biomechanical drivers initiating this process remain poorly understood. It has been recently proposed that ciliary block elevates the trans-lens pressure difference, initiating anterior chamber shallowing. However, this mechanism lacks direct in vivo validation. To address this gap, we established and characterized a novel rabbit model of angle-closure glaucoma induced by ciliary block–mediated trans-lens pressure difference.MethodsThirty rabbits were divided into three groups: intraocular lens with a capsular tension ring (IOL+CTR), IOL only, and untreated control (n = 10 each). Ciliary block was induced in the IOL+CTR group via extracapsular lens extraction and implantation of an oversized (13-mm) CTR. Over 8 weeks, we monitored intraocular pressure (IOP), anterior chamber dynamics, and trans-lens pressure differences using tonometry and manometry, followed by histopathological evaluation.ResultsThe IOL+CTR group developed sustained ocular hypertension, with a significant trans-lens pressure gradient. Multivariate analysis identified this gradient as the potential primary driver of prominent anterior chamber shallowing and angle closure, features absent in the IOL and control groups. Consequently, the IOL+CTR animals exhibited classic glaucomatous optic neuropathy, characterized by a 50% reduction in retinal nerve fiber layer thickness and a markedly excavated optic nerve head. Furthermore, progressive exophthalmos occurred in 80% of these animals.ConclusionsWe successfully developed a reproducible rabbit model in which ciliary block–induced trans-lens pressure difference acts as a pivotal mechanistic link to anterior chamber shallowing, angle closure, and sustained IOP elevation, recapitulating key features of human angle-closure glaucoma.

Glaucoma, a chronic and irreversible blinding disease driven by optic nerve damage and retinal ganglion cell apoptosis,1 is projected to affect 111.8 million people by 2040.2 Notably, primary angle-closure glaucoma (PACG) carries a 10-fold higher risk of severe vision loss compared to open-angle forms,3,4 largely due to its complex biomechanical pathogenesis involving dynamic angle closure—a process still poorly defined at the mechanistic level. Currently, common animal models for angle-closure glaucoma include those using anterior chamber cyanoacrylate injection or laser photocoagulation inducing angle closure, which primarily simulate the disease through mechanical obstruction or induced angle closure.5–7 Nevertheless, these models predominantly use angle closure and elevated intraocular pressure (IOP) as criteria for successful modeling, thereby failing to reveal the upstream mechanisms that initiate disease progression.

Among the potential upstream mechanisms, the role of pressure dynamics across the lens has emerged as a compelling but unverified explanation. Liang et al.8 proposed the “trans-lens pressure difference and ciliary body blockage” hypothesis to explain the occurrence of a shallow anterior chamber and angle closure. This hypothesis posits that the pressure difference across the anterior and posterior surfaces of the lens (i.e., the trans-lens pressure difference) is a key factor leading to anterior displacement of the lens–iris diaphragm. Consequently, it remains to be experimentally validated whether ciliary block can lead to an increase in the trans-lens pressure difference, thereby further causing a shallow anterior chamber, angle closure, and subsequent angle-closure glaucoma.

Intriguingly, a vital clue emerged from clinical practice. Postoperative malignant glaucoma has been observed in some patients following cataract surgery with capsular tension ring (CTR) implantation, where ultrasound biomicroscopy (UBM) has revealed ciliary body block and reduced ciliary lens space.9 These clinical observations provide a compelling rationale for an experimental model that mimics this condition. Therefore, our study was designed to leverage this clinical insight by using an oversized CTR to intentionally induce ciliary block in an animal model. This approach allows us to experimentally investigate the role of ciliary block–induced trans-lens pressure differences in angle-closure pathogenesis.

Materials and Methods

Animals

The rabbit was selected as the animal model for this study due to its large eye size, which is highly suitable for complex surgical manipulation. A total of 30 male New Zealand White rabbits (6–8 months old and 2.5–4 kg; Yuhang Kelian Farm of Laboratory Animals, Hangzhou, China) were used in this study. All animals were housed under controlled conditions (temperature, 16°C–26°C; 12-hour light/dark cycle; illumination, 180 lux) with ad libitum access to food and water. All procedures followed the ARVO Statement for the Use of Animals in Ophthalmic and Vision Research and were approved by the Animal Management Authority and the Institutional Review Board of Wenzhou Medical University Eye Hospital.

Experimental Groups

The rabbits were randomly divided into three groups: (1) intraocular lens (IOL) group (n = 10), which received extracapsular lens extraction with IOL implantation; (2) IOL+CTR group (n = 10), which received extracapsular lens extraction with IOL plus CTR implantation; and (3) control group (n = 10), which included age- and weight- matched untreated animals from the same cohort. A surgical control group of aphakic eyes was intentionally omitted, as anterior chamber depth (ACD)—a primary outcome measure in this study—cannot be measured accurately and reliably without the posterior support provided by an IOL.

Anesthesia and Periprocedural Care

All procedures involving live animals (surgery, UBM, and in vivo pressure monitoring) were performed under a standardized protocol for anesthesia and periprocedural care, designed to ensure animal survival and well-being throughout repeated interventions. Pre-anesthesia, the rabbits were fasted for 4 hours prior to the procedure to reduce the risk of aspiration. Anesthesia was induced via intravenous injection of a combination of pentobarbital (30 mg/kg, P3761; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) and lidocaine (0.5 mg/kg; Shanxi Taiyuan Pharmaceutical, Shanxi, China). Topical proparacaine eye drops (Alcaine; Alcon, Geneva, Switzerland) were applied to the cornea for local analgesia.10 The rabbit was placed on a heated blanket for the operation, with the blanket temperature set to 38°C to prevent hypothermia; vital signs (including heart rate and respiratory rate) were monitored. After the operation, the rabbit was placed in a quiet, warm environment for resuscitation until it regained full consciousness. When it could independently transition from a lateral position to a prone or standing position and walk without ataxia, the rabbit was returned to the cage.11

Surgical Intervention

All surgeries were performed by a single surgeon (XZ) on the right eye under general (pentobarbital, 30 mg/kg; lidocaine, 0.5 mg/kg) and topical (proparacaine) anesthesia. Following pupil dilation with intracameral tropicamide, a standard extracapsular lens extraction was performed via a clear corneal incision, involving capsulorhexis, hydrodissection, and aspiration of the nucleus and cortex. For the IOL group, a foldable one-piece hydrophobic acrylic IOL (+40.0 diopter [D]; Eyebright Medical, Beijing, China) was implanted into the capsular bag. The IOL power (+40.0 D) was chosen based on previous literature and common practice for pseudophakic rabbit models, as the primary goal of the study was to investigate anatomical and pressure changes rather than achieve a specific refractive outcome.12 For the IOL+CTR group, an oversized 13-mm poly(methyl methacrylate) (PMMA) CTR (CTR-1315; Eyebright Medical) was implanted alongside the IOL to intentionally induce ciliary block, as its diameter exceeds the typical 10- to 12-mm rabbit capsular bag diameter reported in the literature, thereby ensuring consistent and firm apposition against the ciliary body to induce a mechanical block.13 Finally, the corneal incision was sutured with 10-0 nylon (Fig. 1). Postoperatively, a regimen of topical tobramycin–dexamethasone eye drops (TobraDex, three times daily; Alcon) and levofloxacin eye ointment (twice daily; Xingqi Pharmaceutical, Shenyang, China) was administered for a week to prevent infection and control inflammation.

Ocular Tonometry

IOP was measured with a TonoVet Plus rebound tonometer (iCare, Helsinki, Finland) at baseline, 12 hours after surgery, and then once per day for 8 weeks. Apart from IOP measurements on the day of surgery, all postoperative IOP measurements were uniformly conducted between 10:00 AM and 12:00 PM.

Ultrasound Biomicroscopy

UBM (SW-3200L, 50 MHz; Suowei Electronic Technology Co., Ltd., Tianjin, China) was performed at 3 days post-surgery and weekly thereafter by a single experienced physician (XZ). For each examination, rabbits were anesthetized as previously described. UBM scans were acquired from five orientations (superior, inferior, nasal, temporal, and central axis). For analysis, two independent examiners (XZ, FZ), masked to the experimental groups, selected an optimal image from each set of scans. An optimal image was defined as one that was well-centered on the pupil or limbus and clearly visualized key anatomical structures, including the cornea, scleral spur, iris, ciliary body, and anterior IOL surface. The built-in software for the UBM system was used to measure the ACD as the distance from the posterior corneal endothelium to the anterior IOL optic along the central visual axis. The angle-opening distance at 500 µm (AOD500) was measured as the perpendicular distance from the trabecular meshwork to the anterior iris surface, at a point 500 µm anterior to the scleral spur. The scleral spur was identified based on the differential tissue density between the collagen fibers of the scleral spur and the longitudinal muscle of the ciliary body.14 To prevent infection, each eye was treated with the levofloxacin eye ointment (twice daily) and TobraDex eye drops (three times daily) for 3 days immediately following the procedure.

In Vivo Studies for Monitoring IOP Distribution in Animal Models

Direct IOP measurements in the anterior and vitreous cavities were performed using calibrated blood pressure transducers (Tru-Wave PX260; Edwards Lifesciences, Irvine, CA, USA) connected to a display monitor (iPM 8; Mindray, Shenzhen, China). The system was prepared by connecting the transducer to the catheter via a saline-filled silicone tube, from which all air was meticulously removed to ensure accurate signal transmission. Before each session, transducers were calibrated against a mercury manometer to confirm reading accuracy.

Measurements were conducted at 3 days post-surgery and weekly thereafter by a single operator (XZ). For each session, under standard anesthesia, a baseline non-invasive IOP was first measured with the TonoVet Plus rebound tonometer. The anterior chamber and vitreous cavity were cannulated with 26-gauge catheters via tunneled limbal and scleral incisions, respectively. To prevent leakage, each catheter was tunneled for 0.5 mm within the cornea or sclera, respectively, before entering its target cavity. Throughout the procedure, external pressure on the globe was avoided, and the transducer height was precisely aligned with the catheter tip to negate hydrostatic pressure artifacts. After signal stabilization, IOP was recorded every 5 seconds for 10 minutes. The final value used for analysis was the mean pressure calculated from a stable 5-minute interval within this recording period. Postoperatively, a 3-day regimen was administered to prevent infection and control inflammation. This protocol involved the topical application of TobraDex eye drops three times daily and the levofloxacin eye ointment (twice daily).

Histological Examination

At the 8-week endpoint, rabbits were euthanized under deep anesthesia via a 10-mL marginal ear vein air injection. Eyes were immediately enucleated and fixed in formaldehyde, acetic acid, and saline (FAS) fixative (G1109; Servicebio, Wuhan, China) for a minimum of 48 hours at 4°C. Following fixation, globes were processed for paraffin embedding, and 5-µm sections were prepared through the pupil and optic nerve head (ONH). The sections were then stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) according to standard protocols and imaged using an optical microscope (Axioplan 2; Carl Zeiss Microscopy, Jena, Germany). For quantitative analysis, retinal thickness and ganglion cell layer (GCL) cell counts were measured from a standardized region approximately 1 mm superior and inferior to the ONH.

Statistics

Statistical analyses were conducted using Prism 9.0 (GraphPad Software, Boston, MA, USA). Data are presented as mean ± SD, with statistical significance set at P < 0.05. Longitudinal data (IOP, ACD, and trans-lens pressure difference) were analyzed by two-way repeated-measures ANOVA, followed by Tukey's multiple comparisons test. Endpoint parameters at 8 weeks were compared among groups using a one-way ANOVA with Tukey's post hoc test. The relationship between the trans-lens pressure difference and ACD was modeled using curve fitting analysis (Python 3.13), with the strength of the association quantified by the coefficient of determination (R2). A detailed description of implementation of the algorithm is provided in Supplementary File ACD-ΔIOP.py.

Results

Ciliary Block Induces Sustained Ocular Hypertension

To investigate the role of ciliary block in angle-closure pathogenesis, we developed a novel rabbit model by implanting an oversized CTR alongside an IOL, following the experimental protocol outlined in Figure 2A. This intervention resulted in a rapid and sustained elevation of IOP. As shown in the longitudinal IOP monitoring (Fig. 2B), the IOL+CTR group exhibited a severe hypertensive spike within 24 hours of surgery, with IOP reaching a peak of 52.4 ± 7.1 mm Hg. This represented a threefold increase from the preoperative baseline of 14.5 ± 0.7 mm Hg (P < 0.001). Although the pressure gradually declined from this acute peak, it remained significantly elevated throughout the 8-week monitoring period, stabilizing at a mean of 23.1 ± 1.7 mm Hg at the final time point (Figs. 2B, 2C). In contrast, the IOL group, after a transient initial spike, exhibited a sustained IOP reduction, settling at 12.5 ± 0.5 mm Hg. (Figs. 2B, 2C). This pressure was significantly lower than the stable baseline of the untreated control group (P < 0.05), indicating that lens extraction itself may facilitate aqueous outflow and isolating the CTR as the driver of hypertension.

Ciliary Block Generates a Pathological Trans-Lens Pressure Gradient

To directly measure the pressure dynamics within the eye, we performed real-time manometry in both the anterior and vitreous chambers (Figs. 3A, 3B). We first validated this technique, confirming that cannulation did not significantly alter IOP (Fig. 3C) and that direct measurements strongly correlated with non-invasive tonometry (anterior chamber, r = 0.884 and intraclass correlation coefficient [ICC] = 0.871; vitreous chamber, r = 0.817 and ICC = 0.797; P < 0.001) (Supplementary Figs. S1, S2). Real-time pressures were subsequently recorded for 10 minutes (Fig. 3D).

Direct manometry revealed a balanced pressure state in control groups. The untreated control and IOL groups showed minimal trans-lens pressure differences (ΔIOP = IOPvit – IOPant) of 1.2 ± 1.6 mm Hg and 0.7 ± 1.1 mm Hg, respectively. In contrast, the IOL+CTR group developed a significant pathological pressure gradient of 8.5 ± 1.6 mm Hg (P < 0.001 vs. controls). This was driven by a dramatically elevated vitreous chamber IOP (29.7 ± 2.4 mm Hg) relative to the anterior chamber (21.2 ± 2.8 mm Hg) (Figs. 3E–G). This pressure imbalance was not a transient event. Longitudinal analysis over the 8-week study period demonstrated that this elevated pressure gradient was sustained in the IOL+CTR group, whereas the ΔIOP in both control groups remained consistently near zero (Fig. 3H).

Pathological Pressure Gradient Drives Anterior Chamber Shallowing and Angle Closure

The two surgical groups showed opposing changes in ACD. The IOL group exhibited significant and stable chamber deepening (final ACD, 4.59 ± 0.11 mm). In contrast, the IOL+CTR group experienced dramatic initial shallowing followed by partial recovery, but its final ACD (4.06 ± 0.15 mm) remained significantly shallower than the IOL group (P < 0.001) (Figs. 4J–L).

UBM imaging further confirmed significant angle structure alterations. Specifically, in the IOL+CTR group, the iris was anteriorly displaced, resulting in complete iridocorneal contact and subsequent angle closure in all eyes (Fig. 4F). For quantitative assessment, the AOD500 was measured. Although no significant difference in AOD500 was observed preoperatively between the groups (P = 0.54), the IOL+CTR group subsequently exhibited a significantly smaller mean AOD500 (0.07 ± 0.03 mm) compared to both the IOL-only (0.52 ± 0.03 mm; P < 0.001) and control groups (0.25 ± 0.04 mm; P < 0.001) (Figs. 4M, 4N). These findings quantitatively demonstrate that CTR-induced ciliary block directly causes severe angle closure. Significantly, our quantitative analysis demonstrated that dynamic anterior chamber remodeling was strongly governed by the trans-lens pressure differential (ΔIOP). This model showed great goodness-of-fit, R2 = 0.7082, adjusted R2 = 0.6981, F(5, 174) = 84.48, P < 0.001, providing a rigorous quantitative framework that directly links pressure gradients to anatomical reorganization of the anterior chamber (Figs. 4O, 4P).

This pressure-driven remodeling led to complete, adhesive iridocorneal angle closure, which was confirmed by UBM and histology and absent in the IOL-only group (Figs. 4D–I). High-resolution UBM and histological analyses confirmed the underlying mechanism: The oversized CTR created a direct apposition with the ciliary body, establishing the physical block responsible for the pressure gradient and its downstream consequences (Figs. 4F, 4I).

Sustained Ocular Hypertension Induces Glaucomatous Optic Neuropathy

The IOL+CTR group developed significant neurodegeneration characteristic of glaucoma. Histological analysis revealed marked ONH excavation and severe retinal degeneration (Figs. 5A, 5B), pathologies absent in the IOL and control groups. Consequently, total retinal thickness was significantly reduced in the IOL+CTR group (91.6 ± 4.1 µm) compared to controls (192.3 ± 3.2 µm; P < 0.001) (Fig. 5C). This neurodegeneration specifically targeted retinal ganglion cells (RGCs), causing substantial thinning of the ganglion cell–inner plexiform layer (GCIPL) (Fig. 5D). This structural change corresponded with a profound loss of RGC density within the GCL (P < 0.001) (Fig. 5H).

Sustained Ocular Hypertension Induces Ocular Morphological Changes

Beyond the internal anatomical changes, the chronic hypertension in the IOL+CTR group also compromised the global structural integrity of the eye. The most common complication was progressive exophthalmos, which developed in 80% of the animals (8/10) by the 8-week endpoint. In one severe case, this structural failure manifested as corneal ectasia, leading to a keratoconus-like deformation.

Discussion

The upstream biomechanical drivers that initiate angle closure in glaucoma remain poorly understood, representing a critical gap in our understanding of the disease. To address this, we recently proposed a novel hypothesis positing that a pathological trans-lens pressure difference, generated by ciliary block, acts as the primary initiator of anterior chamber shallowing and subsequent angle closure. Until now, this concept has remained theoretical, lacking direct in vivo experimental validation. This study, by developing a novel and robust rabbit model of ciliary block, provides direct experimental evidence to substantiate this hypothesis. Our direct, dual-chamber manometry suggests that a sustained pressure gradient acts as a key quantitative driver of the subsequent anterior chamber shallowing and angle closure, thereby establishing a clear biomechanical pathway from ciliary block to the hallmark features of angle-closure glaucoma.

A central finding of this study is the in vivo quantification of a pathological pressure gradient in a ciliary block model. Our direct manometry revealed a substantial and sustained trans-lens pressure difference (ΔIOP) of approximately 8.5 mm Hg in the IOL+CTR group, which compartmentalized the eye and starkly contrasted with the equilibrium in control groups. This finding significantly advances previous work that has identified pressure discrepancies in ex vivo porcine eyes or in glaucoma models not specific to ciliary block.15,16 For example, Seo et al.17 reported a smaller gradient of approximately 3.8 mm Hg in a model of hyaluronic acid injection, but the markedly larger ΔIOP in our model is directly attributable to the specific mechanical obstruction we induced. The mechanical basis for this compartmentalization was evident in UBM and histological analyses, which showed the oversized CTR in direct apposition with the ciliary body, creating a physical “dam” that obstructs normal aqueous humor flow. This provides a direct and powerful biomechanical explanation for how pressure can accumulate behind the lens–ciliary body diaphragm, elevating the concept of a trans-lens pressure difference from a theoretical possibility to a quantifiable, pathological driver of disease.

The direct biomechanical consequence of this pressure gradient was a dramatic remodeling of the anterior segment architecture. The most compelling evidence for this causal link is the inverse correlation we identified between the ACD and the magnitude of the trans-lens pressure difference (R2 = 0.708). Our data support a “piston” model, where the elevated posterior pressure physically forces the IOL–iris diaphragm forward. This pressure-driven displacement culminates in both a central reduction in ACD and the angle closure that we observed uniformly in the IOL+CTR group—a pathology entirely absent in the wide-open angles of the IOL-only cohort. Therefore, our findings establish a clear and sequential pathogenic cascade: Ciliary block initiates a trans-lens pressure gradient, which drives the anterior displacement of the lens–iris diaphragm, resulting in both central anterior chamber shallowing and peripheral angle closure. This cascade provides a comprehensive mechanical explanation for the key anatomical changes that define angle-closure glaucoma

The superiority of our model lies in its ability to simulate the upstream biomechanical trigger of angle closure, a feature that distinguishes it from previous approaches that typically induce pathology at a more downstream point. Conventional models using laser photocoagulation, aqueous replacement, or microbead injections often suffer from inconsistent IOP elevation, low success rates, and a failure to replicate the initiating anatomical changes.18,19 For example, laser-induced models reported sustained hypertension in only 16.7% of cases.20 Furthermore, the investigation of ACD as a primary outcome has been historically limited. Reports of ACD shallowing have predominantly come from spontaneous genetic models, such as those in Basset hounds or mutant quails, whose unpredictable onset make them ill suited for controlled mechanistic studies.21,22 Although an acute model using transient limbal compression did report ACD shallowing accompanying extreme IOP spikes, it failed to capture the chronic progression of the disease.23 In contrast, our study prospectively established a quantitative link between the upstream pressure gradient and chronic ACD shallowing in an angle-closure model. This conclusion is powerfully reinforced by our IOL group, where the procedure alone led to a deeper anterior chamber and a sustained reduction in IOP, isolating the CTR-induced ciliary block as a primary driver of the entire pathological cascade.

The ciliary block–initiated cascade culminated in glaucomatous neurodegeneration, evidenced by severe ONH excavation and RGC loss. This recapitulation of end-stage pathology confirmed that the induced pressure gradient is a sufficient trigger for the full spectrum of glaucomatous damage. Notably, the sustained hypertension was dynamic, with IOP gradually declining from its peak, a dynamic consistent with findings in other chronic hypertension models.10 This likely reflects a compensatory reduction in aqueous humor production due to ciliary body hyposecretion under chronic stress.

Nevertheless, we acknowledge several limitations inherent to this model. First, anatomical differences between the rabbit and human eye necessitate caution when directly extrapolating these findings. Second, the acute onset and severity of hypertension in our model, although ideal for mechanistic investigation, more closely resemble malignant glaucoma or an acute angle-closure crisis rather than the more insidious progression typical of chronic PACG. The resulting corneal edema in some animals also posed challenges for long-term observation. These limitations, however, pave the way for critical future refinements. Future work could employ a range of CTR sizes to create a tunable model that induces varying degrees of ciliary block. Such a platform would enable the investigation of disease thresholds—determining the minimum pressure gradient required to initiate angle closure.

In conclusion, our work provides strong in vivo evidence for the trans-lens pressure difference as a quantifiable, pathogenic driver of angle-closure glaucoma. By establishing an in vivo platform that directly links ciliary block to anterior segment collapse, we have transitioned a key hypothesis into strong evidence. The novel rabbit model developed here serves as a critical preclinical platform to design and test novel therapeutic strategies. These future interventions can now target the upstream biomechanical cause of the disease, shifting the focus beyond merely managing its downstream consequences.
