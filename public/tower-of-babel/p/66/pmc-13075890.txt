Basilar Artery Dolichoectasia With Multiple Vessel Compression (“Vessel Sandwich”) in Trigeminal Neuralgia: A Case Report and Literature Review
Authors: Valentina Corpus-Gutiérrez, Maria Isabel Ocampo-Navia, Maria del Pilar Castro Valle, Carlos Ramirez-Caballero, Edgar Barrios-Vidales, Paula Barajas-Solano, Jose Chaves-Moreno, Alberto Daza-Ovalle, Nestor Guzman-Ruiz, Francisco Pérez-Pinto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND AND IMPORTANCE:Basilar artery (BA) dolichoectasia is a rare vascular anomaly characterized by elongation, dilatation, and tortuosity of the BA. A dolichoectatic BA can cause neurovascular compression of the trigeminal nerve, resulting in classical trigeminal neuralgia (TN). Diagnosis and surgical management are often challenging due to the complex anatomy and the frequent presence of multiple neurovascular conflicts.CLINICAL PRESENTATION:We report the case of a 70-year-old man with multiple comorbidities and an 8-year history of right-sided TN refractory to pharmacological and interventional treatments. High-resolution brain MRI revealed BA dolichoectasia compressing the trigeminal nerve, with no additional neurovascular conflicts identified. Intraoperatively, multiple offending vessels were identified compressing the trigeminal nerve (BA, superior cerebellar artery, superior petrosal vein, and anterior inferior cerebellar artery). The patient underwent a successful 360° microvascular decompression, achieving complete decompression of all neurovascular conflicts. Postoperative recovery was uneventful, and the patient experienced immediate and sustained pain relief without complications.CONCLUSION:TN caused by BA dolichoectasia with multiple neurovascular conflicts represents a complex diagnostic and surgical challenge. However, meticulous intraoperative exploration and complete decompression can result in rapid and long-lasting symptom resolution. This case underscores the importance of comprehensive preoperative imaging and individualized surgical planning.

Neurovascular conflict is the main cause of trigeminal neuralgia (TN), typically involving the superior cerebellar artery (SCA) (75%), anterior inferior cerebellar artery (AICA) (10%), or venous structures (7%).1,2

A single neurovascular conflict occurs in approximately 73% of TN cases [including those involving the vertebrobasilar system (VBS)], whereas multiple vessel conflicts are present in about 27% of cases.3,4

Dolichoectasia of the VBS accounts for approximately 2% of all TN cases.5,6 Its surgical treatment through microvascular decompression (MVD) is the treatment of choice but presents considerable technical challenges due to the complex and distorted vascular anatomy.1,7

Cerebral dolichoectasia is an alteration on morphology of intracranial arteries, with the basilar artery (BA) being the most commonly affected vessel and is characterized by elongation, dilatation, and tortuosity of the artery.8 Basilar dolichoectasia has a reported prevalence of less than 0.05 percent in angiographic studies and autopsies.5 This anomaly can cause trigeminal nerve compression syndromes.9

The aim of this article was to report a rare case in which TN was caused by a dolichoectatic BA and multiple neurovascular conflicts, despite the fact that preoperative imaging demonstrated an apparently single conflict involving the BA, and to highlight the key technical aspects and challenges encountered during MVD.

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board, and written informed consent was obtained from the patient for publication of all clinical information and imaging. This report adheres to the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

CLINICAL PRESENTATION

A 70-year-old man was referred for evaluation of severe facial pain at a tertiary-level institution in Colombia in August 2024. His medical history included arterial hypertension, smoking, and obstructive sleep apnea.

The patient had experienced right-sided TN for 8 years, with pain localized to the maxilar and mandibular divisions (V2 and V3) of the trigeminal nerve. The pain was described as electric shock-like and continuous, with nocturnal paroxysms that significantly disrupted sleep and impaired mastication. On the visual analog scale, the pain intensity was rated 10/10. MRI of the brain using Fast Imaging Employing Steady-State Acquisition (FIESTA) sequences revealed dolichoectasia of the BA, with clear contact against the cisternal segment of the trigeminal nerve on the right side, without evidence of contact between the trigeminal nerve and any other vascular structure (Figure 1).

Pharmacological treatment included pregabalin, carbamazepine, and tapentadol for baseline pain control, with tramadol and phenytoin used during acute exacerbations.

Before considering surgery, interventional management was chosen due to age and multiple comorbidities. The patient underwent a Gasserian ganglion block, which provided approximately 80% pain relief lasting 2 months.

The Gasserian ganglion block was performed under fluoroscopic guidance, identifying the foramen ovale through submental and oblique views. The needle was advanced toward the target, performing intraprocedural redirections. Proper needle placement within Meckel cave or the trigeminal cistern was confirmed by observing the spontaneous egress of cerebrospinal fluid on stylet removal and by administering 0.1 to 0.4 mL of radiopaque contrast. Once localization was verified, the therapeutic agents were administered.

Owing to refractory symptoms despite medical and interventional therapies, surgical MVD of the right trigeminal nerve was indicated.

Despite the imaging finding of a single neurovascular conflict involving the BA, multiple neurovascular conflicts were identified intraoperatively. A neurovascular conflict caused by the dolichoectatic BA was confirmed, and in addition, neurovascular conflicts were observed with the SCA, the superior petrosal vein (SPV), and the AICA. A complete 360° decompression was successfully performed without intraoperative complications.

Postoperatively, the patient experienced immediate and complete pain relief without complications observed on postoperative computed tomography (Figure 2). Given the patient's complete postoperative pain relief and the absence of neurological symptoms, additional MRI was not considered necessary.

Noncontrast postoperative cranial computed tomography demonstrating the absence of early complications, such as intracranial hemorrhage.

At the 12-month follow-up, complete pain relief persisted.

DISCUSSION

Key Results

Classical TN is caused by a neurovascular conflict that leads to demyelination and atrophy of the nerve, making it hyperexcitable and thus generating the classically described symptoms.1,7 Dolichoectasia of the BA has been described as a causative factor in 2% of the cases.5,6

Our patient had acquired risk factors associated with BA dolichoectasia,6,9 including arterial hypertension and advanced age, which increase the production of vascular factors that modify vessel morphology. In addition, male sex has been predominant in this pathology.5

He was initially refractory to medical management, which led to interventional treatment with a Gasserian ganglion block, achieving 80% pain relief. However, as reported in the literature,1 pain recurred 2 months later.

Despite his age and multiple comorbidities, the patient presented with pain of maximum intensity refractory to medical and interventional management, so he was taken to surgery for MVD, where multiple vascular conflicts were observed, despite the presence of a single conflict on preoperative imaging (Figure 5), even after a retrospective review of the preoperative MRI. This highlights the need for thorough exploration during the surgical procedure, as neuroimaging may suggest the involvement of only one vascular structure in the neurovascular conflict, whereas additional vessels may be contributing to the compression.

High-resolution T2-weighted sequences, such as FIESTA, demonstrate higher specificity than 3-dimensional (3D) time-of-flight magnetic resonance angiography (MRA), and even than the combination of both techniques, for the diagnosis of neurovascular conflict.10-12 This is attributable to the superior contrast between anatomic structures provided by high-resolution T2-weighted imaging.13

However, the addition of 3D time-of-flight MRA provides complementary information regarding the vascular anatomy and the precise offending vessel.13-16 Moreover, the combined use of high-resolution T2-weighted imaging and 3D time-of-flight MRA has been shown to achieve higher sensitivity than high-resolution T2-weighted sequences alone.10

Therefore, 3D time-of-flight MRA cannot replace FIESTA for the evaluation of neurovascular conflict; nevertheless, its addition may aid in excluding other multiple vessel compressions and in providing more detailed characterization of the neurovascular relationships identified on FIESTA.

When the VBS is involved, it can be associated with compression by other vessels in 74% of cases,17 as occurred in our case. This can be explained by elevation of the trigeminal nerve due to the dolichoectatic BA, pushing it toward the SCA or SPV, so that the nerve is ultimately compressed by a “vessel sandwich.”18 In cases of multiple vascular conflicts, all offending vessels should be decompressed18 since it is not possible to determine which of the vessels is causing the pain, or if all are contributing.

Two of the conflicts found (SCA and SPV) are commonly reported;2 however, an unusual finding was the BA being the primary compressive structure due to its prominent mass effect on the trigeminal nerve and the need for mobilization during decompression. It was dilated and had a tortuous, redundant course, which allowed it to exert compression at this level and met the criteria described for basilar dolichoectasia.9

In addition, the BA was markedly atherosclerotic, making its identification difficult. Interestingly, this supports the notion that cardiovascular risk factors are significantly associated with this pathology.9

The morphological characteristics make vascular microdissection of the BA challenging, primarily due to the fragility of the arterial walls associated with the existing dolichoectasia, and the difficulty to separate the dilated artery from the nerve in such a narrow space as the cistern.5

For decompression, both the interposition technique (involving the insertion of an implant between the offending vessel and the nerve) and the transposition technique have been described.5

Interposition carries the risk of worsening pain due to indirect compression from the inserted material and granuloma formation.19 The transposition technique involves significant risks, including injury to perforating branches leading to brainstem infarction and mobilization of atherosclerotic plaques due to manipulation.2,5,18 These risks were particularly relevant in our patient, so the interposition technique was chosen.

Although the evidence is controversial,3,20 unusual offenders such as dolichoectasia of the BA may be associated with better postoperative outcomes in complete pain relief.18,20 These favorable outcomes may support the decision for surgical management.

Limitations

The main limitations of this report include its single-case design, which limits generalizability, the absence of a control group, the inability to establish causal relationships, and a relatively short follow-up period.

Interpretation

These findings should be interpreted cautiously, considering the study objectives, methodological limitations, and existing literature.

Generalizability

The generalizability of this report is inherently limited by its single-case design. Nevertheless, the observations may be extrapolated to selected patients with TN related to BA dolichoectasia with similar demographic characteristics, particularly older patients with cardiovascular risk factors, and refractoriness to medical and interventional treatments.

CONCLUSIONS

Classical TN is caused by vascular compression, which in rare cases may be attributed to dolichoectasia of the BA. This condition increases the likelihood of multiple neurovascular conflicts and is associated with the presence of cardiovascular risk factors and presents significant surgical challenges. Nevertheless, when complete decompression is attained, patients can experience immediate and sustained relief from pain.
