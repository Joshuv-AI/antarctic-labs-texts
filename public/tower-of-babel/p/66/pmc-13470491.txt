Visual learning at fast and slow timescales is driven by distinct plasticity rules in primate inferotemporal cortex
Authors: Krithika Mohan, Ulises Pereira-Obilinovic, Stanislav Srednyak, Nicolas Brunel, David J Freedman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe learn to recognize a vast array of familiar objects, a process involving learning-related changes in inferotemporal cortex (IT) activity. A challenge to discovering mechanisms of familiarity learning is that it spans multiple timescales from minutes to days, and is accompanied by simultaneous changes in cellular, synaptic, and network properties. We leverage an integrated experimental-theoretical approach, using IT recordings in two male macaques during familiarity learning within and across sessions to infer underlying plasticity mechanisms. We identified two timescales of learning-related changes spanning minutes to days, consistent with distinct synaptic and cellular mechanisms. Across sessions, averaged responses gradually decreased with familiarity, consistent with synaptic plasticity. In contrast, within-session changes, including rapid response decay and increased spontaneous activity, aligned with intrinsic plasticity mechanisms. Recurrent networks endowed with learning rules inferred from experiments replicated the observed learning dynamics, supporting our hypothesis of distinct learning mechanisms - slow, synaptic plasticity at long timescales and fast, intrinsic plasticity at short timescales.Subject terms: Learning algorithms, Long-term memory, Short-term memory, Network models

Introduction

Primates have a remarkable perceptual ability to recognize a vast array of objects, and to store object information in memory in order to recall whether a particular object has been seen previously. Indeed, we learn to identify previously seen objects as familiar, and discriminate them from new, unseen ones. This ability to recognize familiar images includes items that were viewed very recently (seconds or minutes ago), as well as objects seen days, months, or even years earlier. This perceptual, mnemonic ability is crucial for us to recognize and recall familiar patterns, items, and individuals experienced over a lifetime. Visual familiarity - the concept that links the perception of an object to past experience - is a gateway to memory and is thus a fundamental ingredient for creating a meaningful internal model of the world.

Visual familiarity arises through learning and is encoded in the activity of single neurons and populations in the primate inferotemporal cortex (IT), a key node in the ventral visual stream that is critical for object recognition1–3. Single IT neurons often respond to novel, never-before-seen images with higher average firing rates than familiar images that have been seen thousands of times4–6. In addition to encoding long-term familiarity acquired over timescales of days, IT populations show reduced firing rates even after a single exposure to novel images during both passive and active tasks7. This familiarity-related decrease in IT firing rates has thus been observed across two vastly different timescales - one over long timescales of days, and the other over shorter timescales of minutes.

Uncovering the synaptic and cellular learning rules underlying familiarity learning in the primate brain is particularly challenging, as learning occurs over timescales spanning multiple orders of magnitude from seconds-minutes to days-weeks. Stably recording from large neuronal populations in the primate visual system, particularly in deep structures like the inferotemporal cortex, over long learning durations has until recently been beyond the limit of current neurophysiological or imaging approaches. Recent studies using chronic electrophysiological recordings have tracked neural response changes across consecutive days to examine the timescales of plasticity over long-term familiarization. However, these efforts have largely been limited to specific functional regions, such as face patches8 or to more accessible superficial areas like V29. Furthermore, any learning-related changes in synaptic connectivity or intrinsic cellular properties cannot be readily measured directly nor tracked over long durations in the primate. Thus, the impacts of learning on cellular and network properties must be inferred from sparse recordings from a small fraction of the network, and without ground truth knowledge of key network properties like connectivity and cell types. In the current study, we greatly extend our ability to identify candidate learning mechanisms across multiple timescales through tight interaction between experimental recordings during learning, a theoretical model-based approach for inferring learning rules from experimental data, and analysis of artificial neural networks that incorporate learning rules inferred from experiments.

Over long timescales (across days), neural selectivity (e.g., sharpness of tuning) has often been shown to be greater for familiar than novel images despite lower average firing rates10,11. Moreover, long-term familiarity with an image has also been shown to produce highly specific increases in firing rates among neurons that strongly and selectively prefer that image11. These experimental observations support Hebbian theories of learning according to which repeated presentations of novel stimuli produce enduring changes in synaptic connectivity through activity-dependent synaptic plasticity12,13. Indeed, unsupervised Hebbian learning rules characterized by synaptic depression quantitatively explain neural signatures of familiarity learning over timescales of days14. While synaptic mechanisms are believed to slowly modify neural connectivity over days, it remains unclear whether the same plasticity rules could also govern the rapid neural changes observed as images become familiar over shorter timescales of minutes.

Rapid changes in neural circuits are often observed after behavioral learning within minutes to hours, especially in conditioning experiments15. These changes include enhanced membrane excitability and increases in spontaneous activity that are robust in neurons from conditioned animals but not naïve ones16. The intrinsic excitability of neurons and its plasticity constitute a non-synaptic mechanism that is thought to be important for the initial formation of ensembles, which in turn induces synaptic mechanisms such as potentiation or depression to facilitate learning over longer timescales17. It remains untested whether these non-synaptic mechanisms could mediate the rapid modulations in firing rates observed after familiarity learning over short timescales.

Changes in neural encoding with familiarity have typically been examined at either short or long timescales, but never together in a single primate study7,11,18. In rodents, repeated visual stimulation drives plasticity at two distinct timescales: rapid potentiation within seconds-minutes and slower, gradual changes across days-weeks19,20. Human fMRI studies likewise show distinct patterns, with short-timescale responses tracking individual repeated face stimuli and long-timescale responses reflecting the average face stimulu21. While these studies have provided static snapshots of the learning process, they reveal little about the dynamics of learning and the cellular or synaptic plasticity rules that govern the transition from novel to familiar. We investigate the neural dynamics of visual learning at multiple timescales - starting from short within-session timescales of single image presentations to long timescales of thousands of image presentations across many sessions. We recorded from IT cortex in monkeys while they viewed successive presentations of large numbers of initially novel stimuli that were in turn presented many times across successive days of experiments. This paradigm enabled us to examine changes in neural encoding and to infer underlying learning rules, as initially novel stimuli became increasingly familiar over the course of a recording session, and across multiple daily recording sessions.

IT circuits carry a robust signal of graded visual familiarity with strongest responses to novel images, followed by a progressive decrease in average responses to intermediately familiar and highly familiar images. Over long timescales, population-averaged visual responses slowly decreased as novel images became familiar over days, while at short timescales, average visual responses relative to baseline decreased with repeated exposures within single sessions. Although visual responses changed with learning over both timescales, the underlying mechanisms differed at long versus short timescales. First, firing rate changes at long and short timescales were uncorrelated in individual neurons. Second, short-timescale firing rate changes were strongly correlated between novel and familiar stimuli, suggesting that a single shared process mediates within-session changes. This shared short-timescale process was consistent with increased excitability, supported by the observation of increased spontaneous activity within single sessions. These data can be captured by a computational model with two different plasticity mechanisms, an intrinsic plasticity mechanism operating on a timescale of minutes-to-hours, and a synaptic plasticity mechanism operating on much longer timescales, on the order of days-to-weeks. Our combined experimental and theoretical approach unveils the multiscale dynamics of learning in cortical circuits and suggests that the neural substrates of visual learning are mediated by distinct and complementary plasticity rules operating at different timescales.

Results

Dimming detection task

To investigate the timescales of visual learning in IT, we first familiarized two monkeys with images randomly chosen from a large library (~500 highly familiar images) comprising objects, patterns, and scenes. During familiarization, monkeys were trained to perform a “dimming” detection task in which they detected and reported a decrease in image luminance in a series of rapidly presented images that varied in their familiarity to the monkeys (Fig. 1). The purpose of this task was to ensure that the animals were actively attending to each image, and to encourage them to be similarly task-engaged across the session. Both monkeys successfully performed the dimming detection task with a mean accuracy of 99% and a mean reaction time of 326 ms on dimming trials (Monkey B: accuracy 99.26%, reaction time 313 ms; Monkey S: accuracy 98.02%, reaction time 350 ms).

Dimming detection task.

Monkeys detected a decrease in the luminance of an image (i.e., dimming) and reported it through the release of a manual touch-bar. Each trial started with a 500 ms fixation period, followed by image presentation for 400 ms with an inter-stimulus interval of 150 ms. In half of the trials, up to five images were shown, and dimming occurred on any of the five images, and monkeys were rewarded for releasing the touch-bar to report dimming (top: example trial depicts dimming on the second image; RT is reaction time). In the remaining half of the trials, five images were always presented, no dimming occurred, and monkeys were rewarded for holding the touch-bar through the trial. The images varied in their levels of familiarity to the monkeys. The images were novel (never seen before), intermediately familiar (~50–400 views), or highly familiar (~1000 views) and were presented approximately 50 times within a single session.

To examine how learned familiarity is encoded at multiple timescales, we tested highly familiar images with two sets of initially novel images that became familiar over long and short timescales. The first one consisted of images that were presented many times over multiple daily sessions (50 repetitions daily over a week; intermediately familiar), and the second one consisted of images that were presented many times within a single session but not shown again in subsequent sessions (50 repetitions in each session; novel). As the intermediately familiar images were presented over multiple days during recordings, they enabled us to track learning-related changes over long timescales of days to weeks. Similarly, since the novel images were presented multiple times within single sessions, they allowed us to ask how familiarity learning occurred over short timescales of minutes to hours. In each recording session, 120 unique (40 highly familiar, 40 intermediately familiar, and 40 novel) images were presented about 50 times during the dimming detection task. The images varied in their familiarity to the monkeys as follows: (i) highly familiar (viewed at least 1000 times over 3–5 months before recording), (ii) intermediately familiar (viewed 50–400 times over one week during consecutive recording sessions), and (iii) novel (never seen before that recording session).

IT neurons encode graded image familiarity

We recorded from IT neurons using 24-channel linear arrays while two monkeys performed the dimming detection task with images that were highly familiar, intermediately familiar, or novel (Monkey B: N = 306 single units, Monkey S: N = 233 single units). We labeled a neuron as visually selective if it responded reliably to at least one out of 120 images in the presented image set (p < 0.05, N-way ANOVA, see Methods). Out of 539 recorded neurons, 465 (86.3%) neurons were visually selective (81.7% (250 of 306) in monkey B, 92.2% (215 of 233) in monkey S). We compared responses of these well-isolated, visually selective neurons to the three image categories to determine whether they encoded different levels of familiarity. Many cells encoded learned familiarity in a graded manner, with most of them responding with the highest firing rates to novel, lower firing rates to intermediately familiar, and lowest firing rates to familiar images (Fig. 2a, b). Consistent with this, mean population responses also revealed a similar progression, with higher firing rates to novel, followed by intermediately familiar, and then highly familiar images (Fig. 3a). We found that 30% of neurons were significantly modulated by whether an image was familiar or not, after accounting for the visual selectivity to individual images (Monkey B: 28.8%, Monkey S: 28.7%; ANOVA p < 0.05). While most familiarity-selective neurons (74%) showed strongest firing rates to novel images, a smaller proportion showed the opposite trend - responding with higher firing rates to highly familiar or intermediately familiar images, and lowest to novel images (Fig. 2c) (10%, Monkey B: 11.1%, Monkey S: 7.5%; Tukey’s HSD criterion, p < 0.05), indicating substantial heterogeneity in single neuron response patterns. We note that because of the design of our paradigm in which multiple images are shown in rapid succession on each trial, there is a strong presentation-order effect, with a pronounced suppression in initial firing rates for all images following the first in the sequence (Supplementary Fig. 1). This is consistent with previous reports of initial suppression especially under rapid image presentations with short inter-stimulus intervals22–24. Crucially, the suppression is uniform across all familiarity levels, suggesting that it could be mediated by independent mechanisms in IT circuits.

We also used a population-level decoding approach to confirm our findings from the single-neuron-based mean population responses. We quantified the amount of category information in the IT population using linear support vector machine (SVM) classifiers trained to decode whether population responses were elicited by images from novel, intermediately familiar, or highly familiar categories. This analysis revealed strong novel vs. familiar decoding during the image presentation period (Fig. 3b; 85.7% during 280–480 ms from image onset using all visually selective neurons (N = 465)). Because the classifiers were trained and tested on different images (but from the same novel, intermediately familiar, and highly familiar categories), the robust decoding indicates that image familiarity is encoded in IT populations in an abstract format that generalizes beyond specific image features. In sum, both the single-neuron mean population measure and the population-level decoder revealed robust representations of learned familiarity.

Time course of familiarity encoding at long timescales

We next asked how neural representations of image familiarity evolved over long timescales with visual experience across multiple sessions, specifically using intermediately familiar images. We quantified response changes to intermediately familiar images as they were repeatedly presented over eight consecutive recording days with approximately 50 presentations on each day. Firing rates to intermediately familiar images progressively decreased with visual experience over days (or hundreds of views). Moreover, average responses to intermediately familiar images were consistently lower than novel images and higher than familiar images through eight consecutive days of visual learning (Fig. 3c). Although each day sampled different neural subpopulations, electrode coordinates showed no consistent drift along the AP or ML axes, so the progressive attenuation in firing is unlikely to reflect systematic shifts in recording sites (Supplementary Fig. 2).

To characterize the dynamics of learning across sessions, we quantified the time point at which responses to intermediately familiar images differed from novel images. We used a generalized linear model to fit firing rates as a function of number of days (or views) of exposure, image category (N/IF/F), and neuron number. The model revealed that firing rates to intermediately familiar images were significantly different from novel images for the first time on day four (or 200 views) and remained so until day eight (or 400 views) in both monkeys (two-sided Wald t-test from a quasipoisson GLM with log link, t(34972) = − 2.33, p = 0.01; pre-specified day-wise contrasts, unadjusted for multiple comparisons, see Methods and Supplementary Table 5, model results separated by monkey in Supplementary Tables 6 and 7). Thus, the effects of learning over long timescales emerge slowly in IT after multiple days (or hundreds of views) of visual experience.

We measured the learning rate across days to estimate how long it takes for IT firing rates to saturate in response to intermediately familiar images. Importantly, “Day X” denotes the number of prior days the monkeys had already viewed the intermediately familiar (IF) image set - e.g., “Day 1” means the IF set had been seen on one previous day (~50 repetitions/image); the familiar set had thousands of prior repetitions, and novel images had zero prior exposure. For each day, we computed the difference between mean responses to novel (N) and intermediately familiar (IF) images and plotted this across days (Fig. 3d, orange line). To account for the fact that recordings were from different neural populations on each day, we normalized responses by computing relative firing rate differences within each day, i.e., firing rates to intermediately familiar images were computed relative to novel images from the same day. We observed a smooth increase in the normalized response difference (N-IF/N+IF) over successive days of repeated presentations of intermediately familiar images (Fig. 3d, orange line). We fit the response difference with an exponential function, capturing the rise and eventual plateau in the response. The inferred time constant was nine days, indicating that it would take more than one week of about 50 views/day of initially novel images for IT firing rates to saturate. Although we report the time constant in terms of “days”, it is possible that the time course of familiarization also depends on other factors such as exposure time of images, number of views, number of sleep cycles, or a combination of all the above. Since the number of days and number of views are correlated here, we cannot isolate the individual roles of these factors, and their relative contributions to familiarity learning will need to be disambiguated in future studies. Finally, we confirmed that the response reduction occurred only for intermediately familiar images and not for highly familiar images. The firing rate difference between novel and highly familiar images (N-F/N+F) remained stable across days and was best fit by a constant line (Fig. 3d, blue line). Linear regression of the data did not improve the fit since the resulting slope was not significantly different from zero (F(1, 6) = 1.25, p = 0.31), indicating that firing rate differences tend to saturate once images have become highly familiar.

To validate the robustness of these single neuron-based analyses, we performed population decoding to examine how decoding accuracy evolved over long timescales. To do this, we trained and tested image category decoders on neural activity acquired within each day. For each day, we trained three classifiers to decode whether a presented image was - (i) novel or intermediately familiar (N-IF), (ii) highly familiar or intermediately familiar (F-IF), and (iii) novel or highly familiar (N-F), using firing rates averaged over the late image presentation period (280-480 ms after image onset). Specifically, we asked whether decoding accuracy remained stable, increased, or decreased across days. We found a substantial increase in the decoding accuracy between novel and intermediately familiar images across days, indicating a gradual emergence of image familiarity (Fig. 3e, N-IF, linear fit: slope = 3.7, F1,6 = 25.3, p = 0.002). While the other two decoders (N-IF and N-F) exhibited slightly upward trends across days, the increases were much smaller in magnitude and less robust compared to the strong effect observed in N-IF (Fig. 3e, N-F, linear fit: slope = 2.4, F1,6 = 6.06, p = 0.05; F-IF, linear fit: slope = 1.5, F1,6 = 1.33, p = 0.30). Consistent with the findings from the single neuron-based metric, population-level classifiers provide further evidence for continued learning of intermediately familiar images over long timescales. These results highlight two findings: first, progressive response reduction is a neural correlate of familiarity-related learning, and second, neural representations of image familiarity emerge over multiple days in IT, suggesting a role for slow, synaptic mechanisms that reorganize connectivity patterns over learning at long timescales.

Time course of familiarity encoding at short timescales

We have seen so far that on average IT activity decreases slowly with familiarity over long timescales of days. We next ask the question of what happens over shorter timescales. How do familiarity-related responses change with repeated image presentations in a single recording session? To examine this, we selected visually selective neurons that were present from the start of the recording session and remained well-isolated for up to at least 25 presentations (~1.5 hours into the recording session) of each image. As the number of repetitions exceeded 25, we observed a significant decline in the number of neurons that remained well-isolated from the start of recording, primarily due to drift and neuronal instability. Therefore, to only consider units that were reliably isolated from noise and other units, we included the first 25 presentations in our analyses of within-session learning (Monkey B: N = 106 single units, Monkey S: N = 78 single units, Supplementary Fig. 3).

During each recording session, we observed a modest but significant rise in firing rates with successive presentations of both novel and familiar images (Fig. 4a, Linear model; Novel: slope = 0.02, intercept = 7.3, F1,6 = 15.7, p = 10−4; Familiar: slope = 0.04, intercept = 5.6, F1,6 = 383, p = 10−15). This finding is unexpected because most previous studies report repetition-induced suppression, especially for novel stimuli. The parallel increases for both image types suggest a shared, familiarity-independent influence. We therefore examined changes in baseline activity, an index of global excitability, across the session. Baseline firing rates, measured on a trial-by-trial basis in a time interval prior to image presentation, rose steadily throughout the session in both monkeys (Fig. 4b, c), implying that the apparent stimulus-evoked increases were largely driven by this underlying change in baseline activity.

To isolate rate changes driven solely by image familiarity, we computed average firing rates relative to baseline activity. Average firing rates relative to baseline decreased with repetitions of initially novel and intermediately familiar stimuli, but not familiar stimuli (Fig. 4d, e, Supplementary Fig. 4, Linear model; Novel: slope = − 0.04, intercept = 3.85, p = 10−7; Novel: slope = − 0.04, intercept = 3.85, p = 10−7;Familiar: slope = − 0.009, intercept = 2, p = 0.02; ANCOVA for novel versus familiar: p = 10−4). Here, baseline firing rates were computed for each image on a trial-by-trial basis as the mean firing rate during the pre-stimulus fixation period of that trial. In contrast to the slow emergence of long-term familiarity over days, within-session firing rates relative to baseline decreased rapidly over the first few presentations of novel images, after which they reached a stable value. Consequently, the mean firing rate difference between novel and familiar images gradually decreased with repetitions. An exponential model fit to this firing rate difference between novel and familiar images yielded a time constant of 10, indicating that within-session firing rates to novel stimuli attained a plateau after ten repetitions (Fig. 4f). While the mean firing rate difference between novel and familiar images decreased, it never reached zero within a single session, thus suggesting an ongoing process of learning that takes days (or hundreds of views), not hours (or tens of views), for images to become highly familiar.

While mean firing rates relative to baseline were averaged over all novel and familiar images, we wondered whether learning over short timescales affected responses to the most preferred novel and familiar images - particularly since previous work has suggested that the impact of familiarity on neuronal firing rates could depend on how well matched a repeated image was to neuronal tuning. We therefore examined neuronal tuning by calculating maximum firing rates for each neuron - firing rates elicited by the most selective novel and familiar images. Unlike the mean, maximum firing rates to the best novel and familiar images remained stable over repetitions within a session (linear fit for best novel: slope = -0.04, intercept=15.19, p = 0.05; linear fit for best familiar: slope = -0.01, intercept = 12.3, p = 0.3), with greater firing rates for novel than for familiar images. Analyses of image-ranked responses revealed that the highest-driving images (top ranks) do not exhibit the same short-timescale suppression, while suppression becomes more apparent for more moderately driven images (Supplementary Fig. 5). Moreover, grouping images by initial response magnitude (instead of image familiarity) showed that mean firing rates within each category showed no substantial change over repetitions within a session, confirming that the observed effects are independent of response strength and truly reflect familiarity learning (Supplementary Fig. 5d).

Mechanisms of visual response changes over short timescales

Repeated presentations of a visual stimulus induce changes in visual responses on two timescales, a long timescale on the order of days, and a short timescale on the order of minutes. What is the relationship between neural changes that occur at long versus short timescales? Specifically, we asked whether changes at both timescales could be explained by a single plasticity process. To answer this question, we compared changes in visual responses at both long and short timescales. In single sessions, long-term changes were computed as the difference between mean firing rates to novel and familiar images, while short-term changes were computed as the difference between mean firing rates during ‘early’ (repetitions 1-12) versus ‘late’ presentations (repetitions 13–24). These short-term changes were computed separately for novel and familiar images and were computed from mean firing rates without baseline subtraction. Consistent with our previous findings that mean firing rates decrease over long timescales but increase over short timescales, long-term changes in mean rates were uncorrelated with short-term changes in mean rates, for both novel and familiar images (Fig. 5a). The lack of correlation between timescales suggests that neuronal changes over long and short timescales could be mediated by distinct learning mechanisms.

What mechanisms explain familiarity-related changes over short timescales? The observed changes in neural activity over short timescales could be due to changes in intrinsic excitability, changes in synaptic inputs due to synaptic plasticity, or both. Our earlier observations of a session-wide rise in baseline firing (Fig. 4b, c) already point to a role for neuronal excitability, so we took that drift as a starting point for further analysis. While the mean baseline activity increased slightly over the course of a session (by approximately one spike/second), there was a strong heterogeneity in baseline changes from neuron to neuron, with some neurons decreasing, and others increasing their baseline rates by more than five spikes/second. We then wondered whether changes in baseline activity were correlated with changes in overall mean firing rates, computed without baseline subtraction. Figure 5b shows that this is indeed the case - neurons with a decrease in baseline activity tended to show a decrease in mean visual responses, and vice versa. Short-term changes in non-baseline-subtracted firing rates were also strongly correlated between novel and familiar images (Fig. 5c). Together, these results suggest that shared baseline fluctuations, or a common excitability-like modulation, contribute to overall firing-rate changes for both novel and familiar stimuli within a session.

We then turned to the baseline-subtracted visual responses and asked whether their short-timescale changes could be explained by changes in neuronal input-output functions or transfer functions alone, independent of changes in synaptic inputs. Neuronal f-I curves quantify how neuronal firing rates depend on synaptic input, and thus can represent a measure of excitability. To test this idea, we divided trials into two sets, trials 1–12 (early presentations), and trials 13–24 (late presentations), and asked if changes in f-I curves inferred from visual responses to novel images could predict changes in firing rates to familiar images. We used a method introduced by Lim et al.14 to infer for each neuron its f-I curve during the two epochs (early and late), using quantile functions of its visual responses to novel images, and the quantile function of a Gaussian distribution (see Methods for details and assumptions). We then used these f-I curves to predict the distributions of firing rates of the same neuron to familiar images during the late presentations, given visual responses during early presentations (Fig. 5d, e). In most neurons (45 out of 66 neurons) with significant changes for early vs. late presentations for familiar images (p < 0.05, two-sample Kolmogorov-Smirnov test), we find that the distributions of firing rates predicted in this way were not statistically different (p < 0.05, two-sample Kolmogorov-Smirnov test) from the observed distributions. We then asked whether the same f-I curve changes could explain the presentation-dependent reduction in the firing-rate difference between novel and familiar images described in Fig. 4d–f. To check this, we computed the predicted average firing rates in response to familiar images during the late presentations, and compared it with the data (Fig. 5f). We find a remarkable agreement between the data and the model that assumes changes occur only through changes in the neuronal f-I curve between the early and late part of the trial. Overall, these results suggest that long- and short-timescale changes are consistent with independent plasticity mechanisms, and that short-timescale modulations are compatible with intrinsic plasticity mechanisms.

Inferred IT learning rules over long timescales

We next asked what mechanisms could be responsible for changes in the statistics of visual responses with familiarity on long time scales. Such changes cannot be explained by intrinsic excitability changes, because statistically significant differences between the statistics of responses to novel and familiar images are present at any given time during the experimental protocol, provided familiar images have been presented a sufficient number of days before the current session. One possibility is that these changes occur due to changes in synaptic connectivity in IT cortex. This hypothesis is consistent with the analysis of Lim et al. 14, which showed that changes in distributions of visual responses with familiarity can be explained by an unsupervised synaptic plasticity rule that changes synaptic strengths between neurons as a function of the firing rates of pre and post-synaptic neurons, ΔJij = f(ri)g(rj), where ri and rj are the firing rates of the post and pre-synaptic neurons, respectively. Lim et al. 14 developed a procedure to infer the dependence of the plasticity rule on the post-synaptic firing rate, f(ri), from distributions of visual responses to novel and familiar stimuli. This procedure is explained in detail in Methods and is illustrated in Fig. 6 for one example neuron.

This procedure proceeds along the following steps: (1) The input-output transfer function of a neuron is inferred from its distribution of visual responses to novel images, using the assumption that the distribution of inputs is Gaussian (see Fig. 6a–c for an example neuron), similarly to the procedure used in Fig. 5g–i. Thus, the transfer function is the function that maps a Gaussian to the specific distribution of visual responses of this neuron (see Methods for a discussion of the Gaussianity assumption). (2) The knowledge of the transfer function then allows us to infer the distribution of inputs for familiar images, given the distribution of visual responses to such images. For many neurons, this distribution is significantly different from the Gaussian distribution of novel inputs (see Fig. 6b for an example neuron). (3) The next step is to infer the dependence of the learning rule on the post-synaptic firing rate that is consistent with this difference in input distributions. Analysis of a recurrent network model with a plasticity rule of the type ΔJij ∝ f(ri)g(rj) shows that f(r) is proportional to the difference between the quantile functions of familiar and novel inputs Δh (see Methods). We then plotted the resulting function f(r), and fitted it with a linear function of the rate, a + br. (4) To evaluate the significance of the fitted coefficients a and b, we used a shuffling procedure in which novel and familiar responses were shuffled multiple times, and fitted the resulting fshuffled(r) with a linear function. This shuffling procedure generates a 95% confidence interval for the parameters a and b. Neurons with significantly negative/positive a (below/above the 95% confidence interval) were termed ‘negative’/‘positive’ respectively, while neurons with significantly negative/positive b (below/above the 95% confidence interval) were termed anti-Hebbian/Hebbian, respectively (see examples in Fig. 6d, e).

Applying this procedure to the data, we found a marked diversity in inferred plasticity rules. In some neurons, the inferred Δh(r) is approximately constant, with fluctuations around a value that can be positive or negative. In others, Δh(r) either decreases or increases with firing rate. We found examples of all categories. The largest fractions of neurons were ‘negative’ (13% of all recorded neurons, see Fig. 6d for a few examples) and anti-Hebbian (13%, see examples in Fig. 6e), while fewer neurons were ‘positive’ (7%), or Hebbian (5%). The largest proportion of negative neurons, compared to the positive ones, is consistent with the observation of a gradual and slow decrease in average firing rates observed in the data over long timescales. In negative neurons, the mean firing rate in response to familiar images is significantly lower than the mean rate in response to novel images. In positive neurons, the opposite occurs - the mean firing rate is larger for familiar images. In Hebbian neurons, the distribution of firing rates is broader in familiar compared to novel, leading to an increased selectivity to familiar images, while in anti-Hebbian neurons, the opposite occurs, with a decreased selectivity to familiar images.

Plasticity rules inferred from differences in statistics of neural responses to novel and highly familiar images reveal the asymptotic properties of the learning process in IT cortex, but they do not contain any information about the timescales of learning. To investigate the time course of familiarization in the model, we employed a model whose connectivity matrix is modified by repeated presentations of images during a session. In this model, changes in the connectivity matrix from one session to the next are due to two terms: an unsupervised plasticity term, which depends on visual responses of pre and post-synaptic neurons to all presented images during the session, and a decay term that prevents the connectivity matrix from growing in an unbounded way. This model is similar to previously introduced ‘palimpsest’ models25,26. The network model is described in detail in Methods. In this model, we derived equations that describe how the visual response to a presented image depends on the number of sessions during which this image was presented (see Methods). We fitted this model to the data and found that it was able to describe well the dynamics of familiarity learning on long timescales, with the observed time scale of about 10 days (see Fig. 6f). Thus, the data is consistent with a combination of intrinsic plasticity operating at a fast time scale of minutes to hours, and synaptic plasticity operating at a much slower time scale of days.

Discussion

In this study, we employed a closely interacting experimental and theoretical approach to infer the cellular and synaptic learning rules that underlie visual familiarity learning across timescales spanning minutes to days. We recorded neural activity from the inferotemporal cortex in monkeys as they viewed initially novel images many times over multiple daily sessions and studied the neural processes of familiarity learning both within and across sessions. Neural populations in IT cortex maintain a detailed record of image familiarity over timescales that span a thousandfold range from tens to hundreds to thousands of image presentations. Over both long and short timescales, learned familiarity in IT is represented by a progressive decrease in average firing rates, with most neurons responding strongly to novel images, weaker to intermediately familiar, and weakest to highly familiar images. While IT contained a robust code for familiarity at multiple timescales, we found that the mechanisms of learning differed at long versus short timescales. Over long timescales, neural encoding of initially novel images gradually became similar to highly familiar images over hundreds of repeated presentations, consistent with a slow plasticity mechanism operating at a timescale of days. Over short timescales, however, neural responses to initially novel images decreased rapidly over a small number of presentations within a session, consistent with a second plasticity mechanism with a timescale of minutes to hours. We showed that changes at fast timescales are consistent with changes in excitability, while changes at slow timescales are consistent with changes in synaptic connectivity.

Our results show that long-term familiarity leads to reduced firing rates on average, specifically during the late phase of the neural response. This is consistent with many prior studies that primarily compared novel stimuli with highly familiar ones7–9,11,27. Even though our recordings sampled different neural subpopulations on each day, the timescales of plasticity that we observe closely match those reported in chronic recordings in IT face patches, with firing-rate reductions emerging gradually and accumulating over days to weeks8,9. However,18 showed that familiar images can lead to higher responses than novel images, in conditions where familiar images are much less likely to be presented than novel images. Recent data have also shown that familiarity leads to increased selectivity, and that putative excitatory neurons (but not inhibitory neurons) show increases in firing rates to their ‘best’ stimuli (the ones eliciting the largest response)11. Furthermore, it was shown that these data are consistent with a Hebbian learning rule dominated by synaptic depression, which describes the impact of familiarity over long timescales14. While most neurons in our recordings were well described by a depression-dominated learning rule (‘negative’ neurons that exhibit a decrease in average responses with familiarity), a much smaller fraction of neurons followed a Hebbian rule, and maximum responses (or ‘best’ responses) were not amplified with familiarity. We observed no changes in maximum firing rates or neural selectivity to novel and familiar stimuli between putative excitatory and inhibitory neurons. One potential source of discrepancy between the results of our study and the one of11 is that we used smaller sets of familiar and novel images (40 in each) compared to11 (125 in each). To understand whether this difference might underlie the smaller fractions of Hebbian neurons in our study, we reanalyzed the data of11, considering only randomly chosen 40 novel and 40 familiar stimuli. We found that this leads to a drop in the fraction of observed Hebbian neurons (from 20% considering the full sets of 125 stimuli, to 8% using only 40), while the fraction of anti-Hebbian neurons increased from 3% to 7%. Thus, the discrepancy between the two data sets can in large part be explained by the difference in numbers of images used in each session. Note that two recent studies that examined familiarity coding in IT face patches also found no differences in selectivity between novel and familiar faces8, but they also used smaller image sets. This suggests that to detect the Hebbian nature of plasticity curves in IT cortex, stimuli sets of at least on the order of 100 images should be used.

Previous physiological work has focused largely on comparing neural responses and coding in two broad sets of stimuli, one entirely novel, the other highly familiar, providing little insight into the dynamics of learning. A few experiments have tracked responses to initially novel stimuli as they become familiar within single sessions, but stimuli were presented only a handful of times (1-10 repetitions)7,28,29, and responses were often averaged over repetitions5,6. A recent study used chronic recordings across many primate brain areas, including the temporal cortex, to track novel-to-familiar transformations during learning both within sessions and across days. The authors found learning-related changes at multiple timescales: novelty-related activity changed gradually across days and also showed repeated cycles of rapid within-session learning each day30. In line with these studies, we found that baseline-subtracted visual responses to initially novel stimuli, but not familiar stimuli, decreased rapidly over the first few presentations, with a pronounced drop after the first presentation that further plateaued after approximately ten repetitions. In contrast, raw firing rates increased within single sessions as stimuli were repeatedly presented, revealing a short-timescale increase in baseline activity. These short-timescale changes appeared to be mediated by mechanisms distinct from those operating over long timescales: average rate changes over short and long timescales were uncorrelated across individual neurons. Moreover, short-timescale rate changes were strongly correlated between novel and familiar images, suggesting that they reflected a general session-wide modulation rather than an image-specific familiarity effect. We propose that a steady presentation of novel stimuli (alongside familiar stimuli) may lead to rapid cellular changes, such as increased neural excitability, which could serve as a mechanism for short-term learning. Such rapid modulations in firing rates and excitability may be a general mechanism by which the brain learns to adapt to the current statistical context over fast timescales. The precise interplay between fast and slow forms of plasticity and the network-level mechanisms of how intrinsic plasticity interacts with synaptic plasticity to bridge timescales will be important to pursue in future studies.

Both intrinsic and synaptic plasticity have been proposed to play a role in memory formation31. Intrinsic plasticity leading to changes in neuronal excitability has been proposed to participate in the formation of ‘engrams’17, while synaptic plasticity has been assumed to lead to long-term information storage12,13. It has also been argued that these two mechanisms occur at two different time scales, with intrinsic plasticity potentially occurring at a faster time scale than synaptic plasticity32, though the recent discovery of behavioral time scale plasticity in the hippocampus and neocortex puts this separation of time scales into question33,34. Our experimental and theoretical results are consistent with a separation of time scales, with excitability changes occurring at a fast time scale (within a session), while synaptic plasticity occurs at a slower time scale (across sessions). We cannot rule out synaptic contributions to short-term (within-session) changes: applying our inference method to early vs. late epochs produces many neurons with significant positive or negative Δh(r) curves (Supplementary Fig. 6), so these within-session effects could reflect either intrinsic excitability shifts or short-timescale synaptic plasticity - importantly, they are uncorrelated with the long-term familiarization effects, consistent with distinct short- and long-timescale mechanisms. An important question for future studies is how neuronal activity driven by sensory stimuli gets converted into synaptic plasticity changes that are only detectable in their impact on visual responses days later, and not during the same session, consistent with slow integration processes at the single synapse level.

Our theoretical model is a generalization of ‘palimpsest’ models that are able to continually store new inputs, at the price of forgetting older ones25,26,35. Here, we fit the model to experimental data. In our model, as in previous studies14,36, a decrease in average visual responses with familiarity occurs because the dependence of the unsupervised synaptic plasticity rule on post-synaptic firing rates is biased towards depression. Other recent theoretical studies have proposed different mechanisms to account for changes in neuronal responses with familiarity. One study proposed that spike-timing-dependent plasticity of inhibitory-to-excitatory synapses could lead to a decrease in average firing rates with familiarity37. Another study examined the impact of unsupervised plasticity rules similar to the ones introduced previously14 in network models composed of four neuronal populations (excitatory neurons and PV, SOM, and VIP inhibitory interneurons), and showed that these rules can be tuned to reproduce various novelty effects in mouse visual cortex38. While these models reproduce familiarity effects on average visual responses, they do not lead to the increase in selectivity observed in some studies, unlike the unsupervised Hebbian rule of14. In parallel with data-driven, analytically tractable models such as ours, emerging machine learning and artificial neural network approaches tackle novelty detection by learning representations and prediction rules from data. For example, unsupervised and self-supervised representation learning can detect novelty as deviations in prediction error39,40, while reinforcement learning-style models treat novelty as an intrinsic value signal that shapes exploration and familiarity-driven policy updates41. Incorporating model-based predictions from such learned representations is an important future direction, complementing our framework by providing scalable, data-driven hypotheses about what architectures and plasticity rules mediate familiarity learning at multiple timescales.

Familiarity-related changes in neural responses have been observed in several areas outside of IT cortex, including visual area V29, perirhinal cortex4,18,42, temporal pole42,43, hippocampus and prefrontal cortex. Although most of these regions exhibit reduced responses on average to familiar stimuli, some studies indicate that memory-related areas, like the perirhinal cortex, show increased activity in response to personally familiar objects and faces42,43, suggesting a role for the perirhinal cortex in encoding elements of episodic experience. The brain-wide representation of visual familiarity suggests that visual object information is combined with long-term memories of those objects into a mixed code, but the precise site of plasticity during learning remains unknown and will be an interesting topic for future studies.

Altogether, our work gives insight into the plasticity rules underlying experience-dependent changes in cortical circuits and the relationship between learning rules and neuronal encoding at multiple timescales. We hypothesize the existence of distinct neural substrates for long- and short-term familiarity, with a role for synaptic plasticity at long timescales (days-weeks) and cellular plasticity at short timescales (minutes-hours). We validated this hypothesis by inferring learning rules through computational modeling, which provides tools to link experimental data to underlying plasticity rules and cortical dynamics.

Methods

Experimental methods

Subjects

Two male adult (13-15yr old) rhesus macaques (Macaca mulatta) weighing 12 kg and 14 kg were surgically implanted with a headpost and a recording chamber (20 mm inner diameter, Crist Instruments). All surgeries followed sterile, aseptic technique with the animals under isoflurane anesthesia. All surgical and experimental procedures were in accordance with the University of Chicago’s Animal Care and Use Committee and US National Institutes of Health guidelines.

Behavioral task and stimuli

Monkeys were trained to perform an RSVP (Rapid Serial Visual Presentation) dimming detection task in which they were required to detect and indicate (by releasing a manual touch bar) a subtle decrease in the luminance of the stimulus. The detection and report of image dimming encouraged monkeys to direct their attention to the presented stimuli. Each trial started when the animals grasped the manual lever and fixated at a central fixation spot for 500 ms. Once gaze fixation was acquired, a sequence of images was presented at central fixation - each shown for 400 ms with a 150 ms inter-stimulus interval. On half of the trials, up to five images were presented sequentially, and one image dimmed immediately after its 400 ms presentation. Dimming occurred with equal probability across the five image positions. Monkeys were rewarded for maintaining central fixation throughout the sequence and for releasing the touch-bar within 500 ms of the dimming event. On the remaining half of the trials, five images were always presented, and no dimming occurred. In these trials, monkeys were rewarded for holding the touch-bar for 300 ms after the final image. Trials were aborted if fixation was broken at any point during image presentation.

The stimuli used in this study were 150 × 150-pixel (4∘ of visual angle) color images downloaded from Flickr using the program Bulkr (example images in Fig. 1). The image set consisted of equal numbers of natural and man-made objects and scenes. Monkeys were first familiarized with a large library of 200 images that were viewed at least 1000 times during the dimming detection task. This image familiarization phase lasted for 3–5 months before neural recordings. After familiarization, in every recording session, 120 unique images that systematically varied in their familiarity were presented as monkeys performed the dimming detection task. Each recording session consisted of 40 novel, 40 intermediately familiar, and 40 highly familiar stimuli that were each presented at least 30 times within a single session. The novel images consisted of unique images that were never seen before a given recording session and were replaced with a new set of images for every subsequent recording session. The intermediately familiar images consisted of images that were viewed 50-500 times over a week during neural recordings and were retained over several consecutive recording sessions until they were cumulatively presented ~500 times (~8 days). The highly familiar images were randomly drawn from the previously familiarized library of images. The highly familiar image set (200 images) was shared across monkeys, with 40 randomly selected per session. In contrast, intermediately familiar and novel images were unique to each monkey. However, the number of presentations per image category was matched across monkeys both within and across days.

In the dimming detection task, monkeys were required to maintain fixation within ± 2∘ of a 0.3∘ circular fixation point at the center of the screen for the duration of the trial. Eye movements were monitored and stored using an EyeLink infrared eye tracking system (SR Research Ltd., Ontario, Canada) at a sampling rate of 1 KHz. Precise control of task events, stimulus presentation, monitoring and storing of behavioral events, and fluid-reward delivery were controlled via a MATLAB-based toolbox, NIMH MonkeyLogic 2.0. Stimuli were displayed using a 21-inch CRT monitor running at 75 Hz, 1280 × 1024 resolution, 24-bit color, positioned 57 cm from the monkeys’ eyes.

Electrophysiological recordings

Structural magnetic resonance imaging scans (MRI) were used to identify area IT and to determine stereotaxic coordinates for recording chamber placement. IT recordings were targeted to the lower bank of the STS (areas TE, TEO) and the ventral surface of the inferior temporal cortex lateral to the AMTS (area TE). IT recordings were conducted between AP coordinates 14–22 mm and ML coordinates 13-18 mm. Single units were recorded extracellularly with 24-channel V-Probes, a dura-piercing guide tube, and were advanced using a NAN Microdrive system (NAN Instruments). Neurons were not pre-screened for visual selectivity. Neuronal waveforms were amplified, digitized, and stored for offline sorting to verify the quality and stability of neuronal isolation (Plexon Inc.).

Quantification and statistical analysis

Data analysis and epoch-based analysis

All analysis code used custom scripts written in MATLAB R2018-2024, Python 3.12, and RStudio 2024.09.1. All analyses were conducted on fixated images in all trials, excluding images with incorrect responses, fixation breaks, and early responses. All reported results are combined across two monkeys, as results were qualitatively and quantitatively similar in both monkeys.

The baseline period was a 400-ms epoch prior to the onset of the first image on each trial. The image presentation period was a 400-ms epoch aligned to image onset, offset by 80 ms to account for neural response latency. Peri-stimulus time histogram plots for single neurons were averaged over all images in a given category (novel, familiar, intermediately familiar), and spike trains were convolved with a 20-ms boxcar kernel. For rank plots, firing rates were averaged over the image presentation period for each image, and images in each category were sorted by firing rates and plotted in ascending order. Mean peri-stimulus time histogram plots for neural populations were averaged across all visually-selective neurons and averaged over all images in a given category, and spike trains were convolved with a 20-ms boxcar kernel. Maximum peri-stimulus time histogram plots for neural populations were averaged across all visually-selective neurons with firing rates measured for the best image in each category (the image that elicited the highest firing rates), and spike trains were convolved with a 50-ms boxcar kernel.

Quantifying neuronal stability

The stability of recorded units was quantified by manually rating every recorded neuron with a score that ranged from 1-5. The stability score indicates the signal-to-noise ratio of a neuron and depends on a number of factors such as the distance of the neuron from the electrode, length of the recording session, electrode drift, etc. A score of 5 indicates that the recorded unit was a single, well-isolated unit with little to no noise, and nearly all recorded spikes belonged to that unit. Conversely, a score of 1 indicates that the unit likely consisted of multiple single units that could not be resolved into independent units, or was significantly impacted by noise and electrode drift. For all analyses described here, we only included well-isolated units with a stability score greater than or equal to three.

Quantifying visual selectivity

Single neurons were defined to be visually selective if they responded with significantly different firing rates to at least one out of 120 images in the presented image set. Visual selectivity for each neuron was quantified using a linear model with image identities as categorical factors (i.e., 120 factors corresponding to 120 images), and was tested for significance with an F-test (p < 0.05). To account for global changes in firing rate due to familiarity that could interact with image selectivity, we used a linear model with image identity (1 to 120 images), repetition (1 to 50 repetitions), and image familiarity (novel, familiar, and intermediately familiar) as factors, and we tested whether individual factors were significant using an F-test (p < 0.05). Tukey’s HSD test (p < 0.05) was used to further quantify whether neurons responded with significantly higher mean firing rates for novel, familiar, or intermediately familiar images.

Linear and exponential regression

To calculate the rate of familiarization over long timescales, we fit population-averaged firing rate differences between novel and highly familiar images using a linear model. For each day, we computed an index of familiarity-related change: (N-F)/(N+F), where N (or F) was the mean across all recorded neurons of firing rates elicited by novel (or familiar) images during the image presentation period (see above). We used a constant linear model to fit (N-F)/(N+F) over multiple days, and the intercept of this zero-slope line represents the asymptote of the learning process. Similarly, we computed a normalized firing rate difference between firing rates to novel and intermediately familiar images (N-IF)/(N+IF) across days. We used an exponential function: y=a1−e−x/τ to fit (N-IF)/(N+IF) over days, where x is the number of days images were already viewed, and y is the normalized firing rate difference. The slope parameter ’a’ was set to the intercept inferred from the linear model. We used a non-linear least squares method to estimate the time constant τ of the learning process across days.

Generalized linear model

To model neuronal firing rate changes over days, we employed a generalized linear model (GLM) using a quasi-Poisson distribution to account for over-dispersion. Firing rates of single neurons were modeled as a function of predictor variables, which included the number of days images were previously viewed (day), familiarity of the image (N/IF), their interaction (day × N/IF), and neuron identity, which was modeled as a categorical variable.

Quantifying sparseness

Sparseness was calculated from number of stimuli n and response to i-th stimuli ri as: Sparseness = (1 − A)/(1 − 1/k) where A=∑i=1nri/n2/∑i=1nri2/n44.

Population decoding

We performed time-resolved population decoding of image familiarity (novel, familiar, immediately familiar) from neural firing-rate vectors using a multiclass linear SVM (one-vs-all ECOC). Decoding was performed separately for each time bin to produce a time course within each recording session. For cross-day comparisons, firing rates were averaged over 280–480 ms after image onset, as the effects of familiarity are strongest in this epoch. Because the neural data were collected through a combination of single-electrode recordings and V-probe recordings, we concatenated units across sessions to create a larger pseudo-population. Decoder accuracy is estimated with stratified K-fold cross-validation (K = 20). For each time bin, we compute accuracy across folds and average across repeated random subsamples to get a robust estimate.

The statistical significance of decoding accuracy above chance was determined using a bootstrap analysis. For the epoch-based decoding analysis, we constructed a null distribution by shuffling the labels for image familiarity and then calculated decoding accuracy for the shuffled distribution. Average classification accuracy was considered significantly greater than chance if the value was greater than 95%(p < 0.05) or 99%(p < 0.01) of the values from the null distribution. For the time-course based decoding analysis, we used a bootstrap analysis to compare classification accuracies to chance at each time point. Average classification accuracy was significantly different from chance if 97.5%(p < 0.05) or 99.5%(p < 0.01) of values from one category were greater than the other category.

Analysis of changes in neural activity over short timescales

We investigated whether changes within sessions are consistent with excitability changes and independent of changes at long timescales. First, we grouped the image repetitions into early repetitions (first 1–12 repetitions) and late repetitions (13–24 repetitions). We average the firing rates across early and late repetitions for each image. Using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test to compare the response distributions for early vs. late repetitions, we found that 61 and 66 neurons have significant changes in the distribution (p < 0.05) for novel and familiar images, respectively. Of those neurons, 50 showed changes for both novel and familiar images.

We inferred the changes in the transfer function for the early and late repetitions using the same method as in refs. 14,36. Briefly, for each neuron, we compute the quantile-quantile plot (q-q plot) of a standard normal distribution versus the empirical firing rate distribution across images. This q-q plot approximates how normalized input currents, by their mean and standard deviation, are transformed into firing rates. The underlying assumption is that input currents are Gaussian, which is a sensible assumption supported by the central limit theorem for a large number of synaptic inputs for a neuron.

We use these two transfer functions (one for the early part of the session, the other for the late part of the session) to predict the firing rates for late repetitions of familiar images, assuming that the changes are due only to changes in the transfer function. First, we use the inferred transfer functions to predict input currents for familiar images. Specifically, we use the inverse of the transfer function inferred for early repetitions of novel images to compute the input current corresponding to each familiar image for early repetitions. Second, we use the transfer function inferred for late repetitions of novel images to compute the predicted firing rates for late repetitions of familiar images from the previously computed input currents for familiar images. In both steps of this analysis, we assume that the changes in familiar firing rates are due only to changes in the transfer functions for early and late repetitions inferred from the novel images.

After the above procedure, we computed the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test to compare the response distributions for late repetitions of familiar images versus the predicted distribution. We found that 45 out of 66 (~68%) neurons show no significant difference (p < 0.05) between the response distribution and the theoretical prediction assuming the changes are due to changes in the transfer function as described above.

While a Gaussian distribution of inputs is expected if neurons receive a large number of uncorrelated inputs from pre-synaptic structures, one might expect this assumption to break down for naturalistic inputs. However,14 showed that the shape of Δh(r) curves is robust to the appearance of non-Gaussianity of input distribution, and in particular that the sign of Δh(r) is independent of the choice of input distribution.

Sigmoidal f(r)=as+bs1+exp(−cs(r−ds))

Note that both linear and sigmoidal fits with bl(bs) > 0 are consistent with Hebbian plasticity, while bl(bs) < 0 is consistent with anti-Hebbian plasticity. Note also that sigmoidal curves were used by Pereira and Brunel36 to fit data from ref. 11.

To check the significance of fitted parameters, we generated shuffled data (see below for details of shuffled data generation), and computed the distribution of parameters in the shuffled data. We computed the bounds of a 95% confidence interval, generated from shuffled data, a neuron was classified as ‘negative’/‘positive’ if the corresponding value of a is below the lower bound/larger than the upper bound of the 95% confidence interval, while it was classified as anti-Hebbian/Hebbian if b was smaller than the lower bound/larger than the upper bound of the 95% confidence interval of this confidence interval. Note that neurons could be classified as both negative/positive and anti-Hebbian/Hebbian, if both their a and b parameters were outside their respective confidence intervals. Note also that neurons could be classified as Hebbian/Anti-Hebbian using the linear fit (LH/LA)) or the sigmoidal fit (SH/SA), or both. We found little difference in classifications using linear and sigmoid functions, and thus only report the classification using a linear function.

The numbers and fractions of neurons in all classes are reported in Supplementary Table 1, when the full temporal interval is used to compute visual responses. We also report the corresponding numbers when only an early temporal interval is used in Supplementary Table 2.

We also performed a similar analysis using data recorded by ref. 11 (Supplementary Table 3). This data consists in visual responses of 88 ITC neurons from 125 novel and 125 familiar images. To understand whether the striking difference in the number of neurons classified as Hebbian is due to a difference in the number of images used (40 vs 125), we considered only a random subset of 40 visual responses out of the 125. The corresponding results are shown in Supplementary Table 4.

Note that while the procedure to compute the function Δh(r) is identical to the one introduced by Lim et al.14, the classification procedure used here is different. In ref. 14, we first selected neurons based on whether distributions of novel and familiar responses were significantly different using a Mann-Whitney U test. We then classified neurons as Hebbian when the smoothed function Δh(r) was negative at low rates and positive at high rates; positive/negative when it was positive/negative at both low and high rates. Here, we have chosen a different statistical procedure that relies on first fitting the function Δh(r) using either a linear or a sigmoidal function, and then determining whether the fit parameters are significantly different from zero using a shuffling procedure. Note that both procedures give very similar fractions of neurons in each category.
