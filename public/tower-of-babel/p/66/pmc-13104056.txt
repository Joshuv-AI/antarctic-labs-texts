Ruptured Sinus of Valsalva Aneurysm
Authors: Samuel Terrazzino, Katarina Winant Halenarova, Laurent Perrin, Bachar El Oumeiri, Mihai Strachinaru
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCase SummaryWe address a case of a young patient with a history of asymptomatic ventricular septal defect, presenting with sudden-onset dyspnea requiring prolonged mechanical ventilation, who developed decompensated right heart failure. A sinus of Valsalva aneurysm rupture, leading to aortopulmonary fistula, was diagnosed. The patient underwent surgical repair with bovine patch and commissural annuloplasty and was discharged after an uneventful postoperative course.Take-Home MessageThis rare case of a ruptured sinus of Valsalva aneurysm in a patient with a known ventricular septal defect demonstrates that the coexistence of 2 left-to-right shunts is a diagnostic challenge requiring a thorough echocardiographic evaluation and a multidisciplinary approach, but one that can be successfully managed with timely surgical intervention.Key words: congenital heart defect, hemodynamics, hypoxemia, pulmonary hypertension, ventricular septal defect, valve repair

Case Presentation

A 25-year-old man with history of asymptomatic ventricular septal defect (VSD) presented to the emergency department of a primary hospital with febrile flu-like symptoms and acute dyspnea over the previous 7 days. The severity of hypoxemia rapidly required intubation and mechanical ventilation. Electrocardiogram showed sinus tachycardia of 150 beats/min. The patient was transferred to a tertiary hospital with suspected viral pneumonia. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) at admission revealed an important interventricular shunting in the basal anteroseptal region with a dilated right ventricle and plethoric inferior vena cava, initially thought to belong to the known VSD. Infectious causes were ruled out.

Take-Home Message

•The coexistence of 2 left-to-right shunts (a ruptured sinus of Valsalva aneurysm in a patient with a known ventricular septal defect) is a diagnostic challenge requiring a thorough echocardiographic evaluation and a multidisciplinary approach, but one that can be successfully managed with timely surgical intervention.

The coexistence of 2 left-to-right shunts (a ruptured sinus of Valsalva aneurysm in a patient with a known ventricular septal defect) is a diagnostic challenge requiring a thorough echocardiographic evaluation and a multidisciplinary approach, but one that can be successfully managed with timely surgical intervention.

Despite aggressive diuretic treatment, the patient remained dependent on mechanical ventilation, with the first early extubation failing owing to pulmonary edema and concurrent bacterial pneumonia, treated consequently. After a second extubation failure, the patient was tracheotomized for concurrent severe intensive care unit (ICU)–acquired weakness (extensive neurological investigation returned negative) but remained mechanically ventilated. Besides a second ventilator-acquired pneumonia, he progressively developed acute right heart failure symptoms: type 1 cardiorenal syndrome requiring continuous renal replacement therapy, as well as congestive hepatopathy.

The patient was then transferred to cardiac ICU. Hemodynamic evaluation by pulmonary artery catheter revealed a cardiac output of 20 L/min, venous oxygen saturation of 88% to 92%, pulmonary wedge pressure of 32 mm Hg, right atrial pressure of 18 mm Hg, and moderate pulmonary hypertension (mean pulmonary artery pressure: 45 mm Hg). Nevertheless, with aggressive fluid removal, the patient became hypotensive and developed symptoms of low cardiac output (encephalopathy, increased lactate level). Repeated TTE and transesophageal echography (TEE) demonstrated right-left shunting, initially attributed to the known VSD, with persistent right-sided congestion.

While awaiting multidisciplinary evaluation, weaning of noradrenaline with permissive hypotension (mean arterial pressure: 50 mm Hg) improved the patient's respiratory, hepatic, and renal status. The case was presented to a heart team involving imaging cardiologists, interventional cardiologists, and cardiac surgeons to discuss the VSD closure, but the indication for surgery was temporary withheld pending new imaging studies.

TTE performed by an experienced imaging expert revealed a right sinus of Valsalva aneurysm (SVA) that had ruptured into the right ventricular outflow tract, creating an aortopulmonary fistula (green arrow in Figures 1A and 1B, Videos 1 and 2), independent from the known VSD (yellow arrow in Figure 1C, Video 3). This was corroborated on TEE (SVA base diameter: 26 mm, dome height: 17 mm, rupture: 10 mm) (Figure 1D, Video 4) and on thoracic computed tomography (green arrow in Figures 1E and 1F). No coronary insertion anomaly was found, and the aortic valve was tricuspid.

The patient was rediscussed with the surgical team and underwent an aortic root repair with bovine pericardial patch and commissural annuloplasty of the aortic valve. The small VSD was not closed. Recovery was uneventful, with rapid weaning from mechanical ventilation despite ICU-acquired muscle weakness; and tracheostomy decannulation was performed within few days after surgery.

Discussion

A sinus of Valsalva aneurysm (SVA) is the least frequent among aneurysms of the aorta.1 Congenital SVA prevails in Asian men and results from abnormal bulbus cordis development. Its association with other anomalies such as VSD, aortic insufficiency, or bicuspid aortic valve is not uncommon.2 Acquired SVA may be of postsurgical, infectious (tuberculosis, endocarditis, syphilis), or of traumatic origin; or a consequence of connective tissue weakness (syndromic heritable thoracic aortic disease, ie, Marfan syndrome, Ehlers-Danlos syndrome). Vasculitis of the proximal aorta (ie, Takayasu arteritis, inflammatory aortitis) may also lead to SVA formation.

If untreated, SVAs may remain clinically silent for many years, with bacterial endocarditis as the main risk. Symptoms are sometimes caused by mechanical compression of the conduction system, leading to heart block,2 or by extrinsic narrowing of the coronary arteries, leading to myocardial ischemia.

Rupture generally occurs in the third or fourth decade of life, with a clinical scenario of dyspnea, chest pain, and congestion in a patient with a recent continuous murmur. It leads to progressive heart failure of poor prognosis unless diagnosed and treated surgically in time.3 Conversely, with appropriate surgical treatment, survival rates are close to 90% after 15 years.4

In this case, the rupture of a most likely congenital or hereditary SVA was considered spontaneous, precipitated by hemodynamic conditions. Alternatively, a hereditary aortic disease may be hypothesized; genetic testing is in progress. The diagnostic approach was particularly complicated by the coexistence of a known VSD. This case highlights the importance of a thorough and multimodal approach in the imaging evaluation of complex challenging cases, as well as a multidisciplinary approach in diagnosis and treatment.
