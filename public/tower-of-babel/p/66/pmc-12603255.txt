Flavonoids interfere with biofilm formation by targeting diguanylate cyclases in multidrug resistant Vibrio cholerae
Authors: Tuhin Manna, Subhamoy Dey, Monalisha Karmakar, Debarati Jana, Kartik Chandra Guchhait, Subrata Hazra, Saroj Ballav, Samaresh Paria, Fernando Berton Zanchi, Maidul Hossain, Amiya Kumar Panda, Chandradipa Ghosh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Rise of antibiotic resistance has led to increased treatment failures in severe cholera cases across the globe. The etiological agent Vibrio cholerae is known for its ability to persist in human gut and environmental settings through biofilm formation. Consequently, the targeting of biofilms is suggested as a viable alternative approach to address the pathogen alongside the rising challenge of drug resistance. Anti-biofilm efficacies of two flavonoids baicalein and fisetin were explored. Minimum biofilm inhibitory concentrations (MBIC) were 40 and 30 µg/mL and minimum biofilm eradication concentrations (MBEC) were 70 and 50 µg/mL for baicalein and fisetin respectively against studied multidrug resistant high biofilm-forming V. cholerae strains. Additionally, both the flavonoids were able to reduce accessory adhesion factors like auto-aggregation abilities, cell surface hydrophobicity of V. cholerae. Disintegration of biofilm moieties post treatment with flavonoids were visualized by scanning electron and atomic force microscope. Fisetin displayed greater efficacy than baicalein in both the studied strains. Both the flavonoids could reduce gene expressions levels of biofilm regulatory DGCs viz., cdgA, cdgH, cdgK, cdgL, cdgM and vpvC and three important biofilm regulators vpsR, vpsT and aphA that correspond to intercellular cyclic-di-GMP levels in V. cholerae. Besides, molecular docking and dynamic simulation studies also demonstrated superiority of fisetin over baicalein in targeting the DGC active site, potentially explaining its enhanced biofilm suppression. Presence of hydroxyl groups at C3, C4, C7, and C3’ positions for fisetin could be the underlying reason as the therapeutic efficacy of flavonoids is significantly contributed by the arrangement of hydroxyl groups. The present study gains its prominence by documenting, maiden insights on the capacity of studied flavonoids to target DGCs and interfere c-di-GMP signalling pathways, to exert their anti-biofilm properties.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-22969-1.

Introduction

Cholera, a forgotten pandemic, is still considered as a pertinent issue in the developing countries, specifically situated in global south (South and Southeast Asia, Africa, and Central and South America)1. The etiological agent Vibrio cholerae has caused seven cholera pandemics on a global scale, with the 7th pandemic is still continuing2,3. Interestingly, during COVID-19 pandemic and also after that a growing trend of cholera cases have been widely reported4. WHO has further stated that in 2023, the 7th cholera pandemic continued to surge with 535,321 cases, up from 472,697 in 2022 and a recent multi-country outbreak report revealed that 126% increased rate of mortality associated with cholera was witnessed in 20245. Treatment of cholera largely relies on rehydration therapies and subsequent use of antibiotics, but they have certain limitations especially for paediatric and geriatric patients and during severe outbreaks, sourcing contamination-free water to be used for rehydration therapy pose a significant challenge6. Besides rehydration therapy is meaningful in mitigating severe dehydration caused by V. cholerae infection, however it is insufficient on its own to counter the underlying infection7. Moreover, excessive use of antibiotics has led emergence of drug-resistant pathogens with increased degree of vulnerability of infections, and V. cholerae showing no exceptions. Throughout the years, the rise of multidrug-resistant (MDR) and extensive drug-resistant (XDR) V. cholerae strains have emerged as substantial public health issue due to their correlation with failures in treatment8,9. Moreover, the available cholera vaccines are expensive and offer short term remedy6, thus requiring alternate strategies for cholera management as well as to fight against the global pandemic of antibiotic resistance.

Besides the genetic antibiotic resistance another concern is antibiotic tolerance which is mostly phenotypical and mediated by biofilms10. Biofilm can be defined as a conglomerate of microorganisms affixed to a living or non-living surface11 to counter incoming stresses, viz., antibiotics, changes in environmental pH, fluctuations in temperature, osmotic stress, nutrient deprivations, etc., resembling the microbial version of the famous proverb ‘United we stand, divided we fall’. Apparently, NIH reported 65% of all microbial illnesses and 80% of chronic illnesses are due to biofilm-mediated infections where biofilm helps to resist antibiotics and bypass the immune system12,13 and as a consequence biofilm is labelled as ‘invisible fortress of bacteria’14. V. cholerae survives in aquatic environment as well as human gut by its ability to form biofilm15. Aggregated biofilms of V. cholerae play key role in the process of host colonization, pathogenesis and the transmission of disease16. The cholera pathogen dispersed from a preformed biofilm is known to be more pathogenic than planktonic, free-living cells17.

Evidence suggests that natural phytoextracts and their bioactive compounds derived from natural phytoextracts could inhibit and eradicate drug resistant bacterial biofilms18,19. Among plant based anti-biofilm agents flavonoids have drawn significant attention as they contain a hydrophilic part that improves penetration into the biofilm structure20,21. Flavonoids are diverse group of polyphenolic compounds that comprise a common C6-C3-C6 carbon skeleton, which consists of two benzene rings (A and B) connected by a three-carbon bridge forming a heterocyclic pyran ring (C)22 as shown in Fig. 1. This basic structure, capable of undergoing different modifications, can result in the formation of different subclasses, viz., flavones, flavonols, flavanones, isoflavones, anthocyanidins, and chalcones. Flavones, a subgroup of flavonoids, are characterized by their hydroxyl (-OH) groups, which significantly influence their biological activities23. Reports revealed various flavones, viz., 6-aminoflavone, 3,2-dihydroxyflavone, quercetin, naringenin, etc., have pronounced anti-biofilm properties specifically targeting Vibrio sp.24–26. Interestingly, trihydroxyflavones and tetrahydroxyflavones have relatively low antibacterial activities against bacterial pathogens (with MIC values 500–1000 µg/mL)27 which signify their potential as anti-biofilm agents, as effective biofilm targeting strategies prioritize the disruption of biofilm architecture over strong bactericidal activity, as this approach minimizes the selective pressure that leads to development of antibiotic resistance28. Previous studies have provided valuable insights into the mechanism of actions of these flavones but they have predominantly concentrated on the suppression of autoinducer-dependent quorum sensing as a mechanism for anti-biofilm activity. Initially, biofilm signalling was thought to be exclusively governed by autoinducer-dependent quorum sensing29; however, the ubiquitous second messenger cyclic-di-GMP (c-di-GMP) was also found to be significantly involved30,31. C-di-GMP is synthesized by diguanylate cyclases (DGCs) from two molecules of GTP. In V. cholerae six DGCs, viz., cdgA, cdgH, cdgM, cdgK, cdgL, and vpvC are prominently involved in biofilm formation31–33. Besides, due to exclusive presence in prokaryotes and absence in eukaryotes DGCs are considered as a potential drug target in various pathogens34–36 and targeting these DGCs could eventually inhibit biofilm formation and thereby disrupt survival of the pathogen37,38. In the present study an attempt was made to evaluate if flavonoids are involved in impeding these DGCs for exerting anti-biofilm activities. Two flavonoids, namely baicalein and fisetin with representing the category of trihydroxyflavones and tetrahydroxyflavones, respectively were chosen for the study. Baicalein is derived from Scutellaria baicalensis roots and reported to inhibit biofilm in different bacteria like Staphylococcus aureus, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Acinetobacter sp. etc.39–41. Similarly another flavonoid fisetin, found in fruits and vegetables and used in dietary supplements has been reported to possess anti-biofilm activities wide range of pathogens, viz., A. baumannii, S. aureus, S. dysgalactiae, K. pneumoniae42,43. As both baicalein and fisetin demonstrated profound anti-biofilm activities against a variety of infectious bacteria it was postulated that they may have inhibitory effects on V. cholerae biofilms as well. In this context the present research work was designed to explore the anti-biofilm properties of two flavonoids i.e. baicalein and fisetin, against high biofilm-forming MDR V. cholerae isolates and to ascertain whether these compounds inhibit the c-di-GMP signaling pathway. It is expected that the present work would help to add newer insights into cholera management strategies and contribute towards the target set by WHO for reducing cholera associated mortality upto 90% by 203044.

Materials and methods

Bacterial strains and phytochemicals

Multi-drug resistant V. cholerae strains TM14 (NCBI accession: PQ057051.1) and TM36 (NCBI accession: PQ060027.1) were selected from the laboratory collections of the research group due to their high biofilm-forming abilities. Both V. cholerae strains were regularly cultured at 37 °C using Luria-Bertani (LB) broth and LB agar plates. Selected flavonoids i.e. baicalein (CAS No. 491-67-8) and fisetin (CAS No. 528-48-3) were procured from Sigma-Aldrich, USA.

Biocompatibility assessments

For conducting the biocompatibility studies of the flavonoids, blood samples were collected from healthy adult human participants through venipuncture into 5 mL tubes containing EDTA. The experimental protocols pertaining to this study received approval from the Institutional Ethics Committee of Vidyasagar University, West Bengal, India for conducting experiments with human subjects (Approval No. VU/IEC-5/6–24 dated 6th September 2024).

Cell viability assay

Five (5) mL of blood was diluted in 1:1 ratio with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and subsequently layered on Histopaque 1077 (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) followed by centrifugation at 1500 rpm for a duration of 40 min at room temperature (15–25 °C). The upper layer of the buffy coat (lymphocytes) was carefully pipetted into a clean centrifuge tube and was washed three times and subsequently resuspended in RPMI 1640 complete media, supplemented with 10% (w/v) fetal bovine serum (FBS), and incubated for 24 h at 37 °C in a CO2 incubator. The cytotoxicity of studied flavonoids was quantitatively assessed after employing MTT reagents. The OD at test and reference wavelengths of 570 and 630 nm of the sample were quantified using a multiplate reader (BIO-RAD, Model 550,USA)19,45. 1% (10 mg/mL) Triton X-100 procured from Himedia, India was used as positive control and sterile Phosphate Buffer Saline (1x) was used as negative control for cell lysis.

Assessment of hemolytic activity

Blood samples were subjected to centrifugation at 3500 rpm for a duration of 10 min, followed by washing with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and the supernatant was discarded. 2% suspension of red blood cells (RBCs) was subsequently prepared using PBS; thereafter, 100 µL of this 2% RBC suspension was transferred into a microcentrifuge tube. Baicalein and fisetin at varying concentrations ranging from 50 to 100 µg/mL was administered to the RBCs and incubated at a controlled temperature of 37 °C for a period of 2 h. This was followed by centrifugation at 3000 rpm for 10 min. Subsequently, 100 µL of supernatant was carefully transferred to a 96-well microplate, and the optical density (OD) of the hemoglobin was quantified at a wavelength of 540 nm utilizing a microplate reader46. For the hemolytic assay 1% Triton X-100 (Himedia, India) was employed as positive control while 1x sterile PBS was used as negative control. ADMET profiling

Both baicalein and fisetin underwent comprehensive analysis for their ADMET characteristics utilizing the Swiss ADME server. It systematically assesses the compounds in terms of their physicochemical attributes, lipophilicity, aqueous solubility, pharmacokinetic profiles, drug likeness, and aspects of medicinal chemistry47. Additionally the toxicity profiles of the identified phytochemicals were evaluated through the application of online server ProTox-348. ProTox-3 predicted the LD50 of the selected flavonoids and categorized their toxicity class.

Anti-biofilm studies

To evaluate anti-biofilm activity of any biologically active compound generally the ability to inhibit biofilm formation and eradication of preformed biofilms are assessed. For conducting the anti-biofilm studies selected flavonoids were dissolved in DMSO due to its high solubility and low toxicity against bacterial cells49. Besides DMSO added bacterial cells were used as untreated positive control.

Minimum biofilm inhibitory concentrations (MBIC)

Microdilution technique was employed to estimate the MBIC of selected flavonoids, with the objective of examining the abilities of these compounds in inhibiting biofilm formation50,51. Series of concentrations of selected flavonoids were prepared resulting in a final concentration that varied between 10 and 100 µg/mL. In test tubes containing 3 ml of LB broth, bacterial samples were inoculated and incubated overnight for growth at a temperature of 37 °C in a shaking incubator. The following day a final volume of 500 µL was attained by incorporating 10 µL of flavonoid solution at desired concentration into the test tubes, alongside 10 µL of bacterial culture and 480 µL sterile LB broth. Following this, the samples had undergone incubation under static conditions for 18 h at 37 °C. The subsequent day, the quantification of biofilm inhibition in treated and untreated bacterial samples was carried out employing the standard spectrophotometric methodology for biofilm quantification utilizing crystal violet assay, as delineated previously using UV-1800 spectrophotometer, Shimadzu, Japan52. Biofilm metabolic activity

The influence of flavonoids on the metabolic activity of biofilms was evaluated utilizing slight modification of the methodology previously described53. The formation of biofilm was accomplished using standardized overnight cultures (OD600 ≈ 0.5), to which 5 µL of selected flavonoids at its MBIC concentrations were incorporated. Appropriate quantities of DMSO were added to the negative control wells. Triplicate wells were maintained at an incubation temperature of 37 °C for a duration of 4 h. Subsequent to the incubation period, 50 µL of MTT solution (resulting in a final concentration of 0.45 mg/mL) was added to each well and incubated at 37 °C for 4 h. Finally, 50 µL of a solubilizing solution was administered to each well. The optical density at 450 nm was measured utilizing a microtiter plate reader (BioRad, USA).

Quantification of carbohydrate and protein contents of the EPS

For the quantification of bacterial extracellular polymeric substances (EPS) production, the methodology delineated previously was adopted with subsequent modifications51,54. Bacterial strains were cultured in LB broth overnight, after that they were normalized to uniform optical densities. An inoculum of 50 µL of the culture with and without the flavonoids were added in LB broth for a duration of 24 h and kept statically in an incubator maintained at 37 °C. Subsequently, biofilm cells were separated via centrifugation at 11,000 rpm and 4 °C for a 10 min. The supernatants were collected and subjected to treatment with two volumes of chilled absolute ethanol to facilitate the precipitation of EPS, followed by incubation overnight at 4 °C in a static environment. The resulting EPS pellets were obtained through centrifugation at 11,000 rpm and 4 °C for 30 min. Next, EPS pellets were allowed to desiccate at 60 °C followed by dissolving in deionized water. The quantification of carbohydrates and protein content in EPS of treated and untreated bacterial samples was performed utilizing the phenol-sulfuric acid method54,55 and Lowry assay51,56.

Microscopic studies

To visualize changes in morphological changes in the biofilm architecture different microscopic techniques were used.

Optical microscopy

Biofilm microscopic imaging was conducted by the method outlined by Selvaraj et al.57. Biofilms were grown on glass slides, both in the absence and presence of flavonoids for a duration of 24 h at a temperature of 37 °C. Subsequently, the glass slides were subjected to wash with sterile PBS solution and permitted to air dry. Then, the slides were stained with a 0.4% (w/v) crystal violet solution and observed at a magnification of 100X magnification using oil immersion interface using microscope (Axioscope A1, Zeiss, Japan).

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

Imaging of bacterial biofilms was performed using standardized protocol described previously26,51. Biofilms were grown on glass slides (1 cm²), both in the absence and in the presence of flavonoids, for a period of 24 h at a temperature of 37 °C. Following this incubation period, the glass slides underwent a thorough wash with sterile PBS solution and were subsequently allowed to air dry. Utilizing a scanning electron microscope (SEM) (EVO-60, Zeiss, Germany), the flavonoid-treated (at their respective MBIC values) and untreated biofilms were visualized. Subsequently biofilms were rinsed with PBS (1x) and fixed employing a 2.5% (v/v) glutaraldehyde and 1% (w/v) osmium tetroxide solution for a duration of 1 h at a temperature of 4 °C in PBS. Following the fixation process, biofilm cells had undergone dehydration for 6 min with treatment of graded ethanol (50–100%, v/v). Prior to imaging the dried samples were coated with a thin layer of gold using a sputter coater (Quorum-SC7620) functioning under vacuum with an accelerating voltage of 15 kV.

Atomic force microscopy (AFM)

AFM studies were used to evaluate the biofilm thickness that corresponds to interpretation regarding extent of structural integrity of EPS matrix of bacteria using predefined methodology43,58. Selected bacterial strains were cultivated overnight in Luria-Bertani (LB) broth, subsequently subjected to centrifugation (5000 rpm for 15 min) and rinsed thrice with PBS (1x) and finally resuspended in the PBS solution. Flavonoids at their respective MBIC values were added and no substance addition was done in the untreated controls. Subsequently the samples were spread on thin glass coverslips and incubated for 8–10 h. Finally, the samples were examined under XE7 Atomic Microscope, Park Systems, South Korea in tapping imaging mode using a silicon tip on a nitride lever (cantilever).

Nucleotide leakage assay

Nucleotide leakage assay upon flavonoid treatment was done in accordance with previously described protocol25,59 with few modifications. Overnight cultures of V. cholerae were subjected to washing twice in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), subsequently re-suspended in the PBS buffer, calibrated to an optical density (OD600) of 0.4, and exposed to selected flavonoids at their half-MBIC concentrations at a temperature of 37 °C. At predetermined intervals (0, 2, 4, and 8 h), samples were harvested, centrifuged at a rotational speed of 10,000 rpm for a duration of 10 min and the resultant supernatants were filtered through a microporous membrane with a pore size of 0.22 μm. Finally, the absorbance readings at 260 nm were obtained using a spectrophotometer (UV-1800, Shimadzu, Japan).

Auto-aggregation assay

Bacterial auto-aggregation was evaluated in accordance with previously established methodologies25 Initially V. cholerae cells were cultured in 2 mL LB broth for a duration of 24 h at a temperature of 37 °C. Bacterial cells were collected through centrifugation at 10,000 rpm for a duration of 8 min followed by a wash with PBS, and subsequently resuspended in PBS. Preliminary optical density (ODinitial) measurements were obtained at a wavelength of 620 nm employing a UV/VIS spectrophotometer (UV-1800, Shimadzu, Japan). Next, selected flavonoids were added and in the untreated control was kept without addition of any compound for 4 h at 37 °C. Finally, the ODfinal of treated and untreated cells were recorded.

Hydrocarbon adhesion assessment

Cell surface hydrophobicity (CSH) was evaluated by examining the capacity of V. cholerae to adhere to hydrocarbons in presence or absence of flavonoids using standardized methodology51,60. Bacterial strains with and without selected flavonoids was cultured in 2 mL LB broth for a duration of 24 h at a temperature of 37 °C. Bacterial cells were harvested by centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 10 min and subsequently washed with PBS followed by resuspension in 4 mL of PBS. Initial optical density (ODinitial) values were recorded at 600 nm utilizing a UV/VIS spectrophotometer (UV-1800, Shimadzu, Japan) prior to vortexing. In the next step 1 mL of toluene was added and thoroughly mixed. Suspensions were kept in strictly static condition for 30 min to facilitate phase separation. Quantification of Indole production

Indole production assessment was performed using methods delineated by Faleye et al.25. Bacterial cells were cultivated in the presence or absence of selected flavonoids at a 200 rpm and temperature of 37 °C under shaking conditions for a duration of 24 h. Subsequently, 1 mL of bacterial culture was centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min, after which 300 µL of Kovacs reagent (HiMedia, India) was added to the supernatants and permitted to react for 2 min at ambient temperature (15–25 °C). Thereafter, 50 µL of the resultant pink product was diluted in 1000 µL of a HCl-amyl alcohol solution, which was prepared by the combination of 35% HCl (75 mL) with amyl alcohol (225 mL), and the absorbances were quantified at a wavelength of 540 nm with a spectrophotometer (UV-1800, Shimadzu, Japan).

Gene expression studies

The expression levels of biofilm regulatory DGCs cdgA, cdgH, cdgK, cdgL, cdgM and vpvC along with c-di-GMP induced biofilm regulators aphA, vpsT and vpsR were quantitatively assessed through qRT-PCR25,26. Aliquots of a culture that had been incubated overnight in LB broth (OD600 = 1), diluted in a 25 mL flask containing sterile LB culture media (1:100) were subsequently cultured both with and without flavonoids at their respective MBIC values. This was followed by incubation at 37 °C for a duration of 24 h under static conditions. Adherent cells were harvested by scraping with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and were then subjected to centrifugation at 13,000 rpm for 5 min at a temperature of 4 °C. RNA extraction from the resultant pellet was conducted utilizing HiMedia bacterial RNA isolation kit in accordance with the instructions outlined by manufacturer. Gene expression studies using isolated RNA as template were conducted with Hi-Quanti One Step SYBr Based RT‐PCR Kit (HiMedia, India) in StepOnePlus RT-PCR (Applied Biosystems, USA). The primers used for these gene expression quantifications are presented in Table S1 in the supplementary section. The cycle threshold values (Ct) for the selected genes were obtained and fold change expressions were calculated using 2−ΔΔCT method using 16s rRNA as endogenous control25.

Retrieval of protein and ligand structures

V. cholerae DGC protein VC0395_0300 (PDB ID: 6EIB)61 having the signature GG(D/E)EF domain was downloaded from the protein data bank in pdb file format. The structures of the ligand viz., baicalein (CID: 5281605) and fisetin (CID: 5281614) were downloaded in sdf format from Pubchem server.

Preparation of protein structure

The structure was then split into monomer from the downloaded homo-dimeric form using SplitChain module of PDB Tools server (http://cao.labshare.cn/Tools/)62. Next the 3D monomer structure was evaluated using the structure validation server i.e. saves v6.1 tool (https://saves.mbi.ucla.edu/) for validation of Ramachandran plot and stereochemistry.

Molecular docking

Cavity detection guided docking study was performed using CB-Dock2 (https://cadd.labshare.cn/cb-dock2/)63. CB-Dock2 outperformed previous blind-docking techniques in terms of success rates for top-ranking poses whose root-mean-squared deviation (RMSD) was within 2Å from the location in the X-ray crystallography structure. The docked ligand protein interactions were visualized using PLIP tool64.

Normal mode analysis using iMODS

Normal mode analysis simulations were executed on the iMODS tool (https://imods.iqfr.csic.es/)65 to study the stability of protein-ligand docked complex66. iMODS uses parameters viz., B-factors, deformability, eigenvalues and variance to analyse magnitude of protein-ligand motion65.

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations of the optimal docked complexes (models exhibiting the highest binding affinity) were executed to ascertain the stability and flexibility. MD simulations were conducted utilizing the Visual Dynamics webserver67 with Gromacs 2024.168. Topology for each ligand was generated through the ACPYPE tool69. The operational parameters were delineated as follows: water model (SPC), box type (cubic), and box distance of 1 nm. The force field employed for the simulations was AMBER 99 over a duration of 100 ns.

Statistical analysis

Mean values and standard deviations of each quantitative variable were calculated by performing each experiment in biological triplicates involving independent samples, each subjected to the same treatment to capture the biological variability. All quantitative data were represented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Two-tail student’s t test was performed to compare the anti-biofilm efficacy by baicalein and fisetin against V. cholerae. Differences were considered significant at p < 0.05.

Results and discussion

Biocompatibility studies of flavonoids

Determination of cell viability using lymphocytes

The results derived from the 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) assay conducted on human blood lymphocytes unequivocally demonstrate that both baicalein and fisetin exhibit minimal levels of cytotoxicity, thereby facilitating their safe applications at significantly higher concentrations. The viability of lymphocytes treated with baicalein and fisetin ranged between 91.2 and 75.8% and from 84.5 to 72.3%, respectively, with concentrations spanning from 50 to 100 µg/mL (Fig. 2). These observed cell viabilities post baicalein and fisetin treatments could infer that these flavonoids are non-toxic, and also consistent with earlier literature19.

Detection of hemolytic concentrations

Hemolytic abilities of baicalein was found to range between 15.5-18.9% and 16.9–19.9% with fisetin in the concentration range of 50–100 µg/mL (Fig. 2). Another flavonoid naringenin is reported to display 25% hemolytic activity at 50 µg/mL, considered to be low hemolytic activity26. Hence it could be stated with low toxicity towards human RBCs, both the flavonoids were biocompatible. Low toxicity of a bioactive compound is considered to be essential for using it for therapeutic purposes.

The present findings imply that with substantial low cytotoxicity against human lymphocytes and erythrocytes, baicalein and fisetin could be considered safe to be used as therapeutic agents.

ADMET studies

Studies on pharmacokinetic characteristics and associated toxicity are considered very important for consideration of any bioactive compound as potential therapeutic agent70. In vitro studies reveal that both the studied flavonoids are non-toxic to human lymphocytes and RBCs. In silico studies on the drug-like properties, viz., absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion, and toxicity (ADMET) were studied using in silico approaches as they are rapid and cost-effective alternatives to clinical trials. Pharmacokintetic properties of the studied compounds fetched through SwissADME server47 are listed in Table 1. Baicalein and fisetin expressed drug likeness as there was no violation of Lipsinki’s rule of five and simultaneously having bioavailability score of 0.5570. Both the studied flavonoids have logP between 0 and 5, known as ‘sweet spot’ for oral drugs71. As predicted by SwissADME, fisetin has lower log P value (iLogP of 1.50 and XLogP3 of 1.97) compared to baicalein (iLogP of 2.43 and XLogP3 of 3.16) indicating its higher hydrophilicity and lesser lipophilicity. Optimal logP value resides typically between − 1.0 and 2.0, ensuring the drug’s amphiphilicity followed by absorption and transport through membrane72. Besides, both baicalein and fisetin have been predicted to have high GI absorptions and inabilities to cross blood-brain barrier, that further strengthen their candidature as therapeutic agent73.

Anti-biofilm activities of flavonoids

Minimum biofilm inhibitory concentrations (MBIC)

Anti-biofilm activity of flavonoids, i.e. baicalein and fisetin against high biofilm forming MDR V. cholerae strains TM14 and TM36, MBIC values were determined. The lowest concentration at which any bioactive compound can inhibit bacterial biofilm production ≥ 50% is considered as MBIC19,50. MBIC of baicalein and fisetin against both V. cholerae strains were found to be 40 µg/mL and 30 µg/mL respectively (Fig. 3a). Both these compounds have reported for anti-biofilm activities against other bacterial pathogens43,74. For baicalein in case of Pseudomonas aeruginosa, the MBIC value was 128 µg/mL40 whereas for Staphylococcus aureus it was found to inhibit biofilm at concentrations ranging between 32 and 64 µg/mL75. Present findings demonstrated that fisetin had higher efficacy than baicalein as at each tested concentration it showed higher degree of biofilm inhibition against both V. cholerae strains. As fisetin possesses four -OH groups in comparison to three -OH groups of baicalein, it could contribute to the higher effectiveness to fisetin (Fig. 1) as the -OH functional groups are essential for efficacy against bacterial system. Flavonoids require -OH groups at C3, C5, C7, and C3′ for optimal therapeutic efficacies76. -OH groups in baicalein are located at positions C5, C6 and C7 while -OH groups in fisetin at positions C3, C4, C7 and C3’, further add up to the enhanced efficiency of the later (Fig. 1). Previous reports revealed that fisetin can inhibit biofilm production of different K. pneumoniae strains with MBIC value ranging between 12.5 and 62.5 µg/mL43. Similarly, it was also found to inhibit Acinetobacter baumanni biofilms up to 86% at a concentration of 100 µg/mL. A prior study highlighted that tetracycline, the most common drug used in cholera outbreaks showed poor anti-biofilm activity against MDR V. cholerae isolate 03/17-1677. This further points out the downside of treating bacterial biofilms with antibiotics as it results in ‘Goldilocks’ effect, i.e., efficacy can only be achieved at lower concentrations. At higher concentrations aggregation of antibiotics occur at the site of entry of bacteria and antibiotics encounter challenges in reaching the site of action facilitating the development of more biofilms78. It is known that antibiotics like tetracycline, amoxicillin, cotrimoxazole have shown ‘Goldilocks’ effect against V. cholerae biofilms77 which further highlights the need for new pharmaceutical agents for cholera treatments. A recent report stated that V. cholerae biofilm could be inhibited by other flavonoids viz., quercetin and naringenin at a concentration of 50 µg/mL26. Hence it could be stated that both these flavonoids, especially fisetin might possess greater therapeutic potential against cholera biofilms due to their lower MBIC values observed in the present study. SEM studies were conducted to visualize and confirm the reduction of biofilm biomass of V. cholerae post flavonoid treatments (Fig. 4). Substantial reduction of the biofilm could be observed after flavonoid treatments.

Minimum biofilm eradication concentrations (MBEC)

Eradication ability of pre-formed V. cholerae biofilm by the studied flavonoids was also assessed. MBEC is defined as the lowest concentration at which a bioactive compound could eradicate 50% of a pre-formed biofilm50,79. Similar to biofilm inhibitory activity, both the flavonoids had the ability to eradicate pre-formed V. cholerae biofilms with MBEC values of 70 µg/mL and 50 µg/mL for baicalein and fisetin, respectively (Fig. 3b). The time-dependent biofilm eradication abilities of both the flavonoids were visualized under light microscope by staining the treated and untreated biofilms using crystal violet (Figure S1; supplementary section). In chronic infections, the greater therapeutic challenge lies in eradicating the preformed biofilms. Prior studies on flavonoids reported flavone and DHMB to eradicate preformed Vibrio biofilms at a concentration of 100 µg/mL concentrations25. As previously mentioned, antibiotics struggle considerably against preformed biofilms. For instance, against preformed cholera biofilms MBEC values for ceftriaxone and erythromycin were found to be 512 µg/mL and 1024 µg/mL, respectively. Conversely, doxycycline managed to eradicate V. cholerae biofilm at the concentration of 32 µg/mL79. Besides, the present study documents the biofilm-disrupting capacity of baicalein and fisetin on glass borosilicate materials, which is commonly used in food and water storage and processing, suggesting their applications in removal of Vibrio biofilm from water and food contact materials. This observation further aligns with the recommendation that novel control measures have to incorporate additional characteristics like biofilm inhibitory properties, to prioritize the efficacy of managing bacterial pathogens in diverse needs80.

Decline in biofilm metabolic activity

Biofilm metabolic activity is a complex and multifaceted process influenced by various factors, including environmental conditions, microbial interactions and metabolic signaling to maintain biofilm homeostasis and influence microbial fitness81. Modified MTT assay revealed that upon treatment with baicalein and fisetin the biofilm metabolic activity was reduced to 49.1 and 47.7% for V. cholerae TM14, while the reductions were 57.2 and 56.7% for V. cholerae TM36, respectively. Previously, plan derived natural compounds such as trans-cinnamaldehyde was reported to significantly reduce the metabolic activity of methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA) biofilms82 Similarly, benzyl isocyanate could reduce the biofilm metabolic activity upto 59% for S. aureus51. Polyphenols have also reported to inhibit biofilms of food pathogens by reducing metabolic activity of biofilms83. Other hydroxyflavone like quercetin and catechin were involved in suppressing metabolic activities by altering energy metabolism pathways to target Bacteroidetes and Firmicutes, two major bacterial phyla in the gut84. Flavonoids due to their polyphenolic structure, have the ability to inhibit key enzymes in metabolic pathways, such as phosphofructokinase and isocitrate dehydrogenase, thereby probably led to reduction of the efficiency of the Embden-Meyerhof-Parnas pathway and the tricarboxylic acid cycle, respectively85 and this enzymatic inhibition might be crucial in reduction of bacterial metabolic activity. Bacterial metabolic activity within biofilms is intricately linked to its ability to form, maintain, and disperse these structures, that are crucial for its survival and suppression of this activity may result in inhibition of biofilm formation or formation of poor quality of biofilm matrix.

Quantification of extracellular polymeric substance (EPS) constituents post flavonoid treatments

Biofilm matrix is primarily composed of extracellular polymeric substances such as polysaccharides, proteins, nucleic acids. EPS enables the bacteria to have the ‘dynamic biofilm mode of life’ by keeping them in long-term close proximity by forming synergistic micro-consortia86. The polysaccharides are known to be the prime constituent of EPS. It was observed that upon treatment with baicalein and fisetin at their respective MBIC values the carbohydrate content of extracted EPS was significantly (p < 0.05) reduced by 44.2 and 53.5% respectively. In terms of protein contents of EPS, the reduction was 42.7 and 46.6% for baicalein and fisetin, respectively (Fig. 5). The above propositions have further been substantiated by the atomic force microscopic (AFM) studies that could visualize and estimate the thickness of the biofilm matrix of V. cholerae TM14 and TM36. Figure 6 displays the AFM images of flavonoid treated and untreated biofilms. As measured in the tapping mode of AFM, the untreated biofilms of V. cholerae TM14 and TM36 had thicknesses of 1.06 μm and 1.31 μm, respectively. Treatments with both flavonoids substantially reduced biofilm thickness to 0.57 –0.38 μm in TM14 and 0.71 –0.52 μm in TM36. These observations derived from AFM study corresponds to the quantified carbohydrate and protein amounts from the EPS of V. cholerae strains. Similar findings were observed in a former study conducted on K. pneumoniae which showed reduction of biofilm matrix to 0.54–0.82 μm when treated with the flavonoid quercetin compared to untreated control (matrix thickness of 1.10–1.40 µm43). Previous reports on V. cholerae indicate that reduced EPS component are directly linked with impaired biofilm production54, thereby highlighting the fact that treatments with flavonoids of interest in the present study led to the formation of poor quality EPS matrix. Formation of such a poor quality of EPS matrix might be linked with interrupted c-di-GMP signalling action87 as c-di-GMP has been reported to be correlated proportionally with the concentration of polysaccharides and proteins in tightly bound EPS, thereby participating in enhancing the aggregation ability of these structures88. Hence, the present observations suggest the flavonoid might have interrupted the c-di-GMP signalling pathways to exert their activities which were studied in later section of the work.

Bacterial nucleotide leakage activity

Effects of studied flavonoids on their ability to penetrate biofilm cells were assessed using time-dependent nucleotide leakage assays. Figure 7a and b that correspond to nucleotide leakage from V. cholerae strains TM14 and TM36, respectively indicated that among the two flavonoids at their respective MBIC values baicalein possessed superior activity as within the first 2 h of incubation it caused significantly higher (p < 0.05) nucleotide material leakage than fisetin. This release of nucleic materials from bacterial cells resembles damage in bacterial cell membrane89. A previous study with flavonoids revealed 6-aminoflavone, 3,2-dihydroxyflavone and 2,2-dihydroxy-4-methoxybenzophenone caused nucleotide leakage from V. parahaemolyticus (OD260 ranging between 0.1 and 0.2 in 8 h timespan) at concentration of 40 µg/mL25. Hence it could be concluded that both the flavonoids under consideration in the present investigation have potent membrane penetration potential. The present observations are further supported by the fact that generally flavonoids have the ability to penetrate bacterial membrane and cause damage90. Previously it has been reported that other phyto-molecules, viz., berberine, piperine, and rutin inhibit bacterial biofilm formation potentially affecting the transport and leakage of nucleotides by disrupting bacterial membrane functions89.

Prevention of bacterial auto-aggregation

Adhesion factors play key roles in colonization, polysaccharide production, which are very important for the biofilm lifestyles of Vibrios91. Auto-aggregation prevention abilities of flavonoids were determined at their half-MBIC concentrations and it was observed that both flavonoids can inhibit the auto-aggregation abilities of V. cholerae (Fig. 7c). Specifically, reductions were 29.8 and 37.2% for strain TM14 whereas 33.7 and 37.9% reductions were observed for TM36 strain by baicalein and fisetin treatments, respectively. Inhibition of auto-aggregation is linked to interrupting Vibrio surface proteins like flagella and type IV pili and modifications at their interfaces that are involved in surface attachments25. The present observations further support the biofilm inhibitory mechanisms of baicalein and fisetin, and goes in line with previous findings that suggested other flavonoids i.e. 3,2-dihydroxyflavone and 2,2-dihydroxy-4-methoxybenzophenone could reduce auto-aggregation in V. parahemolyticus25. Another previously carried out research work focused on cholera biofilm suggested the flavonoids quercetin, naringenin, and pyrogallol could inhibit auto-aggregation of V. cholerae26. A recent study unraveled the fact that bacterial auto-aggregation was promoted by elevated intracellular c-di-GMP levels92. Hence the reduction of auto-aggregation upon flavonoid treatment might be related with deterrence of c-di-GMP signalling.

Reduction of bacterial hydrocarbon adhesion

Bacterial cell surface hydrophobicity constitutes one of the principal factors influencing the adhesion to either biotic or abiotic surfaces93. Apart from adherence to host cells, the hydrophobicity of V. cholerae also influences its interaction with abiotic surfaces, such as plastics and hydrocarbons94. V. cholerae strains are evidently hydrophobic, particularly when cultured in chemically defined synthetic media95. The present findings reveal that at their half-MBIC values baicalein (20 µg/mL) could reduce the cellular hydrophobicity by 19.7 and 24.8%, respectively for TM14 and TM36 strains (Fig. 7d). On the other hand, fisetin (15 µg/mL) diminished surface hydrophobicity by as much as 40.5 and 43.3% for TM14 and TM36, respectively (Fig. 7d) displaying its greater efficacy. Observed higher impact of fisetin to reduce cell surface hydrophobicity might be attributed by its higher hydrophilicity compared to baicalein signified by relatively lower logP value (Table 1). It is well known that hydrophilic bioactive compounds have a strong affinity for water due to their polar -OH groups, allowing them to interact with the aqueous environment around the cell membrane96 resulting in disruption of the hydrophobic interactions between the cell membrane and other hydrophobic molecules like proteins or lipids that contribute to overall cell surface hydrophobicity97,98. Previous studies suggested that other flavonoids, viz., dihydroxy-4-methoxybenzophenone (10 µg/mL) and 6-aminoflavone (10 µg/mL) cause 41 and 49% reduction in cellular hydrophobicity in other Vibrio sp.25. Similarly benzyl isocyanate isolated from Psidium guajava leaves were also able to reduce cell surface hydrophobicity of S. aureus in the range of 34–52%51. Cellular hydrophobicity is crucial for the bacterium’s ability to form biofilms and adhere to surfaces, which are essential for its survival and virulence properties99. In V. cholerae these properties are integral to its ecological fitness and pathogenic potential, influencing its ability to adhere, aggregate, and persist in various environments94. Moreover as mostly used food and water containers made of stainless steel, plastic and other hydrophobic materials that facilitate Vibrio colonization and biofilm formation25, the ability to reduce cellular hydrophobicity by the studied compounds particularly fisetin might add up its potential to be used as a surface disinfectant agent as well.

Decrease in Indole production

It is well known phenomenon that indole acts as extracellular signalling molecule to stimulate biofilm formation in a range of bacteria13,100. In V. cholerae it is formed by the metabolic breakdown of tryptophan and known to influence master biofilm regulators VpsR and VpsT to promote biofilm formation101. It was observed in the present study that the flavonoids substantially decreased indole production (Fig. 7e). Baicalein elicited a reduction of 44.6 and 38.3%, whereas fisetin resulted in the suppression of 51.4 and 51.9% in indole synthesis for the V. cholerae strains TM14 and TM36, respectively. It could therefore be interpreted that upon flavonoid treatment indole formation has been hindered in the tested V. cholerae strains. Prior reports reveal that other flavonoids like flavone and 6-amino flavone could inhibit indole signalling in Vibrio sp. 25. Similar mechanisms might have occurred in the present investigation as well. However, indole is also known to be involved to influence c-di-GMP signalling systems in V. cholerae as it induces CdgA101, one of the key biofilm regulatory DGC31. Observed results indicate that both baicalein and fisetin might have exerted their anti-biofilm activity in such a manner that can hinder the c-di-GMP signalling cascade. To decipher the molecular mechanisms qRT-PCR mediated gene expression studies were therefore undertaken.

Downregulation of DGC expressions

Gene expression studies of six key biofilm regulatory DGCs viz., cdgA, cdgH, cdgK, cdgL, cdgM and vpvC were conducted for further understanding of the mode of action of flavonoids at molecular levels in relation to c-di-GMP signalling. Both flavonoids significantly (p < 0.05) downregulated DGC expressions compared to untreated control. Relative fold change of DGC expressions upon treatment with the flavonoids in comparison to untreated control is shown in Fig. 8. Results revealed that baicalein downregulated expressions of cdgA, cdgH, cdgK, cdgL, cdgM and vpvC by 39.3, 43.8, 38.5, 37.9, 31.3 and 40.5% in TM14 and 36.1, 42.9, 39.0, 33.3, 31.4 and 42.0% in TM36, respectively. On the other hand, fisetin induced downregulation of the studied DGCs viz., cdgA, cdgH, cdgK, cdgL, cdgM, vpvC were found to be in 48.1, 67.5, 47.9, 62.2, 75.8, 67.1% in TM14 and 45.5, 71.3, 42.7, 62.7, 69.1 and 59.5% in case of TM36. Fisetin exhibited higher inhibitory influence than baicalein in case of all the DGCs in both strains. Previously, catechol-containing sulfonohydrazide compounds also reported to inhibit DGC PleD in Caulobacter crescentus102. Triterpenoid saponin extracted from the Pisum sativum hindered DGC activity to display anti-biofilm activities against Gluconoacetobacter xylinus103. Interestingly, fisetin exerted high activities against cdgH and cdgM as its expression levels were reduced by approximately two-thirds. As these two DGC play key roles in biofilm-mediated survival of V. cholerae in the human gut against bile induced stress31 fisetin may have the potential to exert its effects against survival of the pathogen.

Relative quantification of cellular c-di-GMP levels involved in biofilm signalling

It is well known that c-di-GMP is a global second messenger in bacterial systems and it is involved in various physiological processes of bacteria including biofilm formation, motility, virulence, and DNA repair104,105. But due to very high specificity of c-di-GMP regulatory system in V. cholerae there is no significant correlation between global intracellular c-di-GMP levels and biofilm production, rather c-di-GMP functions via vpsR-mediated transcription106. C-di-GMP bound VpsR promotes vpsT transcription107 and post-activation VpsT and VpsR induce expression of vps gene cluster to promote biofilm formation108. In addition, aphA expression levels directly correspond to in vivo c-di-GMP mediated activation of VpsR106 Observed results reveal that both the flavonoids could significantly (p < 0.05) suppress aphA expression levels (44.2 and 38.5% reduction by baicalein and a drastic 68.5 and 63.8% reduction by fisetin in TM14 and TM36, respectively (Fig. 8). Thus, it could be inferred that due to higher inhibitory effects exerted on DGCs by fisetin treatment on V. cholerae resulted in low c-di-GMP levels to be available for VpsR. Another flavonoid quercetin was found to downregulate V. cholerae aphA expressions upto 50%26. Despite having lesser number of -OH groups (four) in fisetin it exhibited superior activity in suppressing aphA expression over pentahydroxy flavone, quercetin reported. This might be due to the fact that quercetin possesses -OH in C5′ that results in lowered actions against bacterial targets109.

Present observation also revealed that expression of vpsR was downregulated by 48.8 and 44.2% by baicalein and 59.8 and 55.5% by fisetin for TM14 and TM36 respectively (Fig. 8). Despite fisetin causing high levels of downregulation of aphA (corresponding to low c-di-GMP levels) vpsR expressions were not proportionately suppressed because VpsR is capable of operating effectively in the absence of c-di-GMP in spite of its sensitivity towards c-di-GMP110. Analysis of vpsT expressions in present study revealed that its expression was downregulated by 48.7 and 45.4% in TM14 and TM36, respectively upon baicalein treatment. Post fisetin treatment 68.5 and 66.3% down regulation was observed in vpsT expressions. It is most relevant here to state that observed downregulation of vpsT expression was more intense than that observed in case of vpsR because vpsT activation is c-di-GMP dependent unlike vpsR111. From all these above-mentioned observations it could be interpreted that both the flavonoids, especially fisetin might have exhibited their anti-biofilm activity by targeting the DGCs and thereby is capable of impeding the associated c-di-GMP signalling cascade.

Molecular Docking studies

Experimental results obtained through in vitro studies were further substantiated by in silico investigations to understand interactions of these flavonoids with DGCs at atomic levels. Molecular docking with the GGEEF active site of V. cholerae DGC protein VC0395_0300 (PDB ID: 6EIB)61 was used for in silico investigations. The monomeric form of VC0395_0300 was verified using structure verification tools viz., PROCHECK, ERRAT and Verify3D integrated in the saves server. PROCHECK revealed 93.9 and 5.7% residues were present in the most favoured region and additional allowed regions of the Ramachandran Plot (Figure S2a; Supplementary section). ERRAT revealed the overall quality factor to be 99.3 (Figure S2b;﻿ Supplementary section) and finally Verify3D demonstrated 84.91% residues have scored ≥ 0.1 in 3D-1D score (Figure S2c; ﻿ Supplementary section) signifying the structure was usable for further analysis. Molecular docking studies for baicalein and fisetin were executed using CB-Dock263. It was found that baicalein and fisetin exhibited docking scores of -6.3 Kcal/mol and − 6.9 Kcal/mol, respectively. Figure 9 shows the interactions of flavonoids and the DGC active site. For baicalein it could be observed that Asp195, Asn198, Phe199, Lys200, Leu201and Lys306 amino acid residues are involved in the docking process and for fisetin the interacting residues are Asn198, Phe199, Lys200, Glu238, Lys306 and Arg310. It has been already reported by Chouhan et al. for the GGEEF active site of studied DGC that the substrate binding interface comprises of Asp195, Phe199, Lys200, Asn203, His208, Asp212, Glu238, Tyr303 and Lys306 residues61. Thus it could eventually be stated that both the flavonoids were found suitable to bind to the active site and thus have aptitude to inhibit substrate binding. The detailed interaction patterns along with bond distances between the DGC active site and flavonoids are presented in Table 2. Baicalein complex exhibited four times higher hydrophobic interactions than fisetin-complex. On the other hand, the fisetin-complex exhibited a much higher number of H-bonding (Table 2). Interestingly, fisetin was found to be involved in H-bonding with Glu238, i.e., the third position acidic amino acid of signature GG(D/E)EF domain and prior reports on another DGC JcaA of Candidatus sp. revealed this third position acidic amino acid is responsible for deprotonation of 3’-OH of ribose sugar present in GTP resulting in nucleophilic attack on alpha phosphate of target GTP molecule112,113. Thus, fisetin binding involving the Glu238 might eventually block the incorporation of GTP in the active site resulting in failure of formation of c-di-GMP. Interestingly, another flavonoid catechin which has striking resemblance in positioning of -OH groups with fisetin such that both with -OH groups at 3, 3′, and 4′ was previously reported to affect cellular signalling pathways involving cyclic nucleotides114. From this observation it could be stated that due to this structural similarity fisetin could have inhibited signalling pathway of another cyclic nucleotide, i.e., c-di-GMP.

Normal mode analyses (NMA) using iMODS

iMODS server mediated normal mode analysis was performed for investigations of conformational fluctuations of the docked complexes66. The parameters deformability and B-factor analysed the mobility profiles of docked complexes. Peaks approaching a value of 1 in the deformability graph (Fig. 10a and b) represents regions with high levels of flexibility of the docked complexes115. From the present study it was observed that the baicalein-complex found to be more flexibile due to higher peaks observed in deformability graph (Fig. 10a) compared to deformability graph of fisetin-complex (Fig. 10b) with low amplitudes, majority residing below 0.4. Hence fisetin-complex was considered to be more rigid structurally. In both complexes, the patterns of peaks derived from NMA simulations exhibited notable similarities with the PDB peaks (Fig. 10c and d), suggesting that the interaction patterns may closely resemble those observed in experimental outcomes116. Eigenvalue is indicative of the necessary energy required to induce deformation in the structure, with lower eigenvalues being correlated with enhanced structural stability115,116. The obtained eigenvalues for the baicalein and fisetin-complexes were 5.095948e− 04 and 2.734878e− 04, respectively (Fig. 10e and f). It represents the overall stability of docked complexes with fisetin-complex exhibiting relatively higher stability. Eigenvalues are inversely related with variance, where the fisetin-complex exhibited higher isolated variance (Fig. 10h) over baicalein-complex (Fig. 10g) suggesting higher stability of the fisetin-complex.

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulation analyses

Gromacs based MD simulation studies were conducted using Visual dynamics platform67 to assess the conformational dynamics of protein-ligand complexes. Root mean-square deviation (RMSD) serves as an indicator of the cumulative deviation of the backbone from the predetermined setpoint throughout the duration of the simulation. RMSD values of each complex were calculated from the energy minimized structures obtained from molecular docking studies. From the RMSD plots presented in Fig. 11a it could be observed that three complexes studied achieved equilibrium within a short span of 10 ns. From all the studied complexes it was found that the average RMSD values for unbound protein, baicalein-protein and fisetin-protein complexes are 0.24, 0.26 and 0.25 nm, respectively suggesting all the complexes were stable as RMSD values < 0.3 nm is considered within the acceptable range for protein-ligand interactions117. Figure 11a revealed that till 60 ns RMSD of baicalein-complex was within 0.3 nm and during 60–90 ns it crossed 0.3 nm several times. In contrast, the fisetin bound complex exhibited a backbone deviation ranging from 0.1 Å at the initiation and remained confined within 0.3 nm till the end of the MD trajectory. This observation substantiates the robust interactions between ligand and active site of the DGC118. So it could be stated that although both studied flavonoids could bind to the DGC active site i.e. GGDEF domain effectively but fisetin-protein complex appeared with the potential to exhibit higher stability and as a result fisetin might act as a robust inhibitor of the GGDEF site of DGC.

Root Mean Square Fluctuation (RMSF) corresponds to total flexibility of the protein structure119. The root mean squared fluctuations (RMSF) of the amino acid residues are shown in Fig. 11b. The unbound protein along with two docked complexes exhibited a similar kind of fluctuation pattern across all the amino acid residues. The amino acid residues at 204th and 205th positions showed fluctuations with higher RMSF values for baicalein-protein complex than the unbound protein but the fisetin-complex conversely exhibited lower RMSF at these positions. Moreover, at the 208th, 209th, 210th, 211st and 212nd positions baicalein and fisetin complexes deviations as RMSF values were found to be lower than the unbound protein. Among these residues His208 and Asp212 were reported to form the catalytic domain of the studied DGC61 and such less fluctuations of these protein-flavonoid complexes further indicated the interaction of the residues with the ligands during the course of simulation120. Figure 11b delineates that fisetin mostly displayed lesser RMSF than all other complexes and the unbound DGC active site throughout the entire trajectory. Average RMSF values for the unbound protein was 0.12 nm, while the corresponding values for the docked complexes of baicalein and fisetin were 0.13 and 0.10 nm, respectively. It is suggested that lower average RMSF is linked to low conformational fluctuations120. Hence, baicalin-complex displays high atomic fluctuations due to higher flexibility of residues while fisetin-complex is assumed to be relatively stable and compact than baicalein-complex as well as the unbound DGC protein.

SASA values denote the total surface area that is available to external solvents. Therefore, elevated SASA values signify expansion in the structural flexibility, thereby facilitating increased accessibility to the external environment and low SASA value denotes stable interactions within the entities of the complex119. Present observation revealed average SASA for unbound protein to be 90.54 nm2 and for baicalein and fisetin-complexes values were 94.51 and 91.7 nm2 suggesting compact organization with high interaction potential for the fisetin-protein complex (Fig. 11c). Higher SASA of the baicalein docked complex is indicative of relative lower degree of interaction of the residues and subsequently lower in complex stability.

Radius of gyration (Rg) demonstrating the compactness of the system119 was estimated for all bound and unbound complexes (Fig. 11d). It is generally estimated by assessing the distribution of atoms within a protein in relation to its rotational axis or center of mass121. Unbound protein displayed average Rg of 1.61 nm and among protein ligand complexes its value was 1.61 and 1.60 nm for baicalein and fisetin complex, respectively (Fig. 11d). So, fisetin had marginally lower Rg values are indicative of the slightly higher compactness of the system119. From the obtained MD simulation results it could be confirmed that it connects with the observations attained by in vitro studies that fisetin has superior activity against V. cholerae biofilms and it induces higher inhibition over DGCs to target c-di-GMP regulatory cascade to attenuate the biofilm production abilities.

Conclusion

According to the recommendations put forth by the WHO, the concurrent use of antibiotics alongside rehydration therapy is used to yield synergistic effects in the management of severe cholera infections. But the rise of antibiotic resistance is emerging as a significant global health crisis. Oral vaccines, that are employed as short-term remedial measures are prohibitively expensive, and effective long-term remedial measures remain to be elusive. Consequently, alternative strategies are required to effectively address this critical issue. Given that biofilm represents the primary mode of survival and persistence of V. cholerae within the human intestinal mucosa as well as in aquatic environments, targeting the biofilm of this pathogen may be considered a viable alternative strategy. The anti-biofilm potential of two flavones namely baicalein and fisetin were explored and from all the studied propositions it was found both had abilities to interfere with DGCs and associated biofilm signalling cascade to exhibit anti-biofilm activities (proposed biofilm inhibitory mechanism is shown in Fig. 12) with fisetin possessing superior efficacy. Besides gene expression analysis and in silico investigations could unravel the supremacy of fisetin over baicalein in targeting the DGC, which cause higher biofilm supressing activity of fisetin. Its higher bioactivity might be contributed by the positioning of its -OH groups at positions C3, C4, C7 and C3’.

Both baicalein and fisetin were non-toxic to human lymphocytes and RBCs at their studied concentration range. Additionally, due to the ability to reduce cell surface hydrophobicity these flavones could also be used as disinfectants in surface sterilization purposes for the removal of biofilms. Thus, it could be proposed that these flavones could possibly act as anti-biofilm agents as well as surface sterilizing agents for water and food containers for future cholera management strategies. The present study unveils the potential of flavones to interact with DGCs and subsequently modulate c-di-GMP signalling pathways to exert anti-biofilm activity for the inaugural instance. However, for further use in vivo studies are essential to assess the toxicological profiling as well as bioavailability of these compounds to necessitate future clinical trials which remains to be the future prospects of the study.
