Social Support and Strain Amongst Early Childhood Educators: Insights from Experiences in the Workplace
Authors: Meghan C. Orman, Ligia I. Reyes, Caleb Gilbert, Ella M. Freimuth, Zachary G. Meyerson, Beatriz A. Carmona, Laura L. Bellows
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Early childhood education (ECE) educators play a central role in young children’s learning and development, yet they often work in environments marked by chronic stress, limited resources, and variable support. Social support is widely recognized as important for educator well-being, but less is known about how ECE educators experience different forms of support in their day-to-day work. Likewise, while social strain has been linked to stress and burnout, little research has examined how ECE educators encounter strain alongside support. This interpretive description qualitative study draws on interviews with 27 educators from center- and home-based programs across New York State to examine how they describe and navigate workplace social support and strain. Through our analysis, we identified four types of social support – instrumental, informational, emotional, and appraisal – in educators’ accounts, each relevant to managing daily demands and feeling professionally supported. We also identified recurring patterns of social strain, including unmet support needs, misalignment with families or colleagues, and moments of relational withdrawal. In response to strain, educators described a range of adaptive strategies, from practical problem-solving to emotional self-regulation. Together, these findings offer a more nuanced account of how support and strain coexist in ECE settings and may inform theory, future research, and efforts to strengthen the relational conditions that shape educator well-being and workforce sustainability.

Early childhood education (ECE) educators are essential to children’s healthy development, yet they face persistent occupational stressors in the United States – including low pay, high turnover, inadequate staffing, and emotionally demanding labor – that compromise their well-being and the quality of care they provide (Cumming, 2017; Jeon & Ardeleanu, 2020; Kwon et al., 2021). These challenges not only impact educators’ mental and physical health (Carmona et al., 2025), but also their relationships with children (Jeon et al., 2018; Whitaker et al., 2015) and their long-term commitment to the field (Kwon et al., 2021). Recent research during and following the COVID‑19 pandemic has further underscored the vulnerability of the ECE workforce, demonstrating increased educator stress and exhaustion (Quinn et al., 2022), decreased psychological health (Kwon et al., 2022), and continued workforce instability (CSCCE, 2025). As concerns about ECE workforce sustainability – defined as fostering supportive environments that nurture long-term educator competence and motivation (Gutu et al., 2023) – continue to grow, there is an urgent need to understand the relational and organizational dynamics shaping educators’ workplace experiences.

A growing body of literature suggests that social support may play an important role in reducing burnout and promoting educator well-being (Kwon et al., 2021; Schaack et al., 2020; Yuh & Choi, 2017). Social support is broadly defined as the perception that one is cared for, valued, and embedded in a network of trust-based relationships (Cobb, 1976; House et al., 1988). In occupational health research, social support is viewed as a salutogenic (i.e., health-promoting) buffer against job-related stress (Feeney & Collins, 2015; Drageset, 2021) and a predictor of job satisfaction and well-being (Nielsen et al., 2017). In the ECE context, social support is often conceptualized as part of the overarching work climate and measured through perceived relationships with coworkers, supervisors, or administrators (Jorde-Bloom, 2016; Kwon et al., 2021; Yuh & Choi, 2017).

Framed within Bakker and Demerouti’s (2007) Job Demands–Resources (JD-R) model, social support is considered a critical job resource that can offset the effects of high demands and reduce the risk of burnout (Tian & Isa, 2024). The bioecological model further underscores the importance of strengthening social relationships across roles (e.g., between educators, families, coworkers, and administrators) as a pathway to supporting educator and child well-being (Bronfenbrenner & Morris, 2007; Navarro et al., 2020). However, existing literature has primarily treated social support as a unidimensional construct (e.g., “workplace relationships”; Cumming, 2017) or as conflated with outcomes (e.g., feeling respected – a type of appraisal support – as being a precursor to stress; Quinn et al., 2022). In this way, research on social support has primarily focused on outcomes (e.g., association with burnout or job satisfaction), rather than examining how educators experience distinct forms of support in everyday work interactions.

Equally important is the role of social strain in shaping educator health, well-being, and workforce sustainability. Social strain refers to negative or stressful social interactions that heighten psychological stress and can undermine physical and emotional health (Friedman et al., 2012; Pierce & Quiroz, 2019). Social strain is a distinct construct from support, with independent effects on stress and well-being (Chen & Feeley, 2014; Newsom et al., 2005). For example, recent research examining social support and strain simultaneously has found that they relate differently to educator health and well-being, with social strain showing a stronger association with burnout than social support (Reyes et al., 2024).

Importantly, social strain is not simply the absence of support, but rather reflects actively stressful or undermining interactions or situations that may exist despite otherwise generally supportive environments (Pierce & Quiroz, 2018). While a lack of support may result in emotional isolation or loneliness, social strain can actively erode job satisfaction, diminish commitment to the profession, and contribute to burnout (Cumming, 2017; Newsom et al., 2005). In ECE contexts, strain may be triggered by relational dynamics (e.g., poor communication, lack of recognition) or by structural conditions like chronic understaffing or minimal supervision (Cumming, 2017; Jeon et al., 2018). Understanding and addressing social strain is therefore important to advancing ECE workforce sustainability.

Despite growing interest in social support and strain, the specific types of support and strain experienced by ECE educators remain underexplored. Across disciplines, researchers have identified and utilized various social support typologies, including instrumental or practical (e.g., providing a ride), informational (e.g., sharing job-hunting resources), emotional (e.g., listening after a difficult day), and appraisal (e.g., recognizing a coworker’s efforts) (American Psychological Association, 2018; House et al., 1988; Langford et al., 1997). Yet, research on social support in ECE settings rarely examines support by type. For example, the widely used Early Childhood Work Environment Survey (Jorde-Bloom, 2016) assesses “collegiality” and “supervisor support,” but does not differentiate among instrumental, informational, emotional, or appraisal support – making it unclear which types matter most for educator well-being. One notable exception is Yuh and Choi’s (2017) study in South Korea, which found that emotional, instrumental, and appraisal had distinct impacts on ECE educators’ job satisfaction and quality of life. Additionally, recent conceptual models, such as Tian and Isa’s (2024) framework of social support in preschool settings, offer promising directions for exploring the role of distinct support types in ECE contexts but require empirical testing.

Similarly, social strain in ECE settings is often examined broadly, operationalized as interpersonal conflict, exclusion, or isolation, or indirectly. For example, ECE research often groups relational stressors (e.g., conflict with colleagues or families) under broad categories such as “job demands,” alongside non-relational stressors like workload or time constraints (e.g., Aboagye et al., 2024; Kwon et al., 2021). This approach limits attention to the specifically social dimensions of strain, including how it arises through everyday interactions, misalignments, and/or unmet relational needs.

Further, prior research often examines social support and social strain as separate constructs, which is important for understanding their distinct associations with burnout, job satisfaction, and well-being. Within the JD–R model, social support is conceptualized as a job resource (R) and relational strain as a job demand (D); each can independently shape well-being and professional performance, but they may also interact (Li et al., 2025). Scholars have noted that these dynamics between social support and strain often unfold simultaneously and cannot be fully understood when studied in isolation (Cumming, 2017; Pierce & Quiroz, 2019). For example, supportive and straining interactions may arise within the same relationships (Rook et al., 2012) or may vary across roles or contexts (Walen & Lachman, 2000). Further, social support may buffer the effects of strain, although this buffering can weaken when strain is high or persistent in close relationships (Shin & Gyeong, 2023). To date, however, little empirical work has explored how ECE educators experience this interplay of social support and strain as part of their everyday work. Understanding the lived, dynamic experience of this interaction is important for designing interventions that both strengthen support and reduce relational strain across ECE settings.

The Present Study

This study used an interpretive description qualitative approach (Sandelowski, 2000; Thompson Burdine et al., 2021) to examine how early childhood educators across diverse New York State (NYS) settings experienced social support and social strain in their workplaces. The interpretive description approach is well suited to applied research that seeks to identify thematic patterns in participants’ subjective accounts and use those insights to inform future practice or intervention development (Thompson Burdine et al., 2021). Our analytic process was informed by a pragmatic orientation: we began by attending closely to participants’ descriptions, developed preliminary codes through team discussion, and refined these codes as patterns became clearer across interviews. Guided by this orientation, we explored the following research questions in this study:

Methods

Participants

Participants were ECE educators (n = 27) from across NYS who had previously completed a survey on social experience in the workplace as part of StayWell ECE, a larger sequential, exploratory mixed methods study on educator health and well-being. An online survey examining NYS educators’ health and wellbeing (n = 1,423; Carmona et al., 2025) captured burnout levels as measured by Maslach Burnout Inventory – Educator Survey (Maslach et al., 1996). A follow-up survey was sent to respondents to explore their social experience (n = 263) and included questions about demographics and center type. For the present study, a subset of survey respondents were purposefully selected within a stratified system to ensure representation across varying burnout levels (high and low) and program types (Small Center, Large Center, Head Start, family childcare). This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board at Cornell University (protocol # 0144439). Implied consent was obtained for the survey and verbal consent for interviews.

The final sample was 27 ECE educators who were primarily female (96.3%) and predominantly White (88.9%), with a high level of education (70.3% held a degree higher than a bachelor’s) (Table 1). Nearly half (48%) were considered low-income and more than half (63%) reported high burnout levels. Of the 27 educators, 21 worked in center-based settings (Head Start (n = 10), Large Centers (n = 6), Small Centers (n = 5)) and 6 were family childcare providers. Participant characteristics did not differ between centers. Because this study focused on identifying patterns across experiences rather than comparing program subtypes, we did not further disaggregate center-based programs by type. Data Collection

A semi-structured interview guide was developed by the research team and included 11 questions focused on sources of social support (Aim 1) and strain (Aim 2) and their impact on educators’ health. To assess content validity, two experts in public health and qualitative research methodology reviewed the interview guide, providing feedback on the relevance, clarity, and organization of the questions. Face validity was evaluated through pilot interviews with two participants. Research staff completed a five-phase training protocol covering qualitative interviewing techniques, research ethics, and pilot interview practice (Goodell et al., 2016).

Interviews were carried out remotely using Zoom (Zoom Video Communications, 2024) by two members of the research team (moderator and notetaker) and lasted between 20 and 71 min. Following each interview, the research team engaged in a debriefing process to summarize participants’ responses, reflect on interview flow, and document any methodological observations. Summaries of key points from the debriefs were later shared with participants as part of member checking. Zoom’s automated transcription feature was used to generate transcripts, which were subsequently verified by a research team member who reviewed the audio recordings alongside the text to correct any errors.

Data Analysis

Within our interpretive description approach, we utilized a systematic, pragmatic orientation toward data analysis, combining the Rigorous and Accelerated Data Reduction (RADaR) technique with content analysis focused on pattern recognition. RADaR is a flexible, systematic approach designed to rapidly reduce large qualitative datasets while maintaining analytic rigor, particularly suited for applied intervention research (Watkins, 2017). Such rapid approaches to content analysis have been found to be rigorous and produce meaningful outcomes through less time-intensive processes (Nevedal et al., 2021).

The analysis process included: (1) segmenting transcripts by interview guide question, (2) extracting relevant text into a Microsoft Excel matrix, (3) transferring tabulated data into narrative format in Microsoft Word, (4) coding narrative data by interview question to identify key words and phrases across participants, and (5) identifying patterns or categories among the codes across all interview questions within each research question. Coding was conducted independently by two trained analysts, with a third senior researcher double coding a random 20% subsample. As the interpretive description approach acknowledges the role of researchers in shaping the analytic process (Thompson Burdine et al., 2020), researchers debriefed regularly throughout coding to build consensus, resolve disagreements, and maintain reflexivity throughout the analytic process. Following coding, narrative summaries of patterns identified for each research question were written and meaningful quotes that reflected common or illustrative participant experiences were incorporated.

Although social support typologies are well established in the literature, we did not begin coding with predefined categories. This decision reflected the design of the interviews, which invited educators to describe their experiences of support and strain broadly rather than prompting discussion of specific support types. We therefore began with an inductive coding approach to remain grounded in participants’ language and priorities (Saldaña, 2016). During this initial phase of analysis, it became clear that educators’ accounts aligned closely with established forms of social support (i.e., instrumental, informational, and emotional). Subsequent rounds of coding incorporated these categories deductively, and a later analytic pass distinguished appraisal support as a fourth, conceptually distinct form. In contrast, because there are no well-developed typologies of social strain, we coded strain inductively across all cycles and allowed patterns to emerge from participants’ accounts.

To verify existing patterns and explore potential patterns not identified by researchers, ChatGPT GPT-4 (OpenAI, 2025) was used. ChatGPT and other large language models have been found to be powerful analytic tools for verifying and abstracting patterns and themes in qualitative analysis (Wachinger et al., 2024), particularly when used in a deductive way (Zhang et al., 2025). In this case, narrative summaries and interview codes identified by the research team were uploaded to ChatGPT by support type, which was prompted: “What kind of ‘patterns’ (not themes) are you seeing in this description of [our] findings for types of [instrumental] supports ECE educators receive?” In this way, ChatGPT was deducing patterns by support and strain type based on the researchers’ narrative summaries and codes. ChatGPT’s output was carefully compared with existing patterns and new patterns were discussed and verified by researchers. This was repeated for each type of social support and strain.

Instrumental Support

Participants described instrumental support as tangible assistance that helped them manage the practical demands of their work. This support included both administrative assistance, such as coordinating schedules, navigating regulatory requirements, or arranging substitute coverage, and hands-on help in the classroom, such as providing breaks, assembling materials, or stepping in during high-stress moments. Educators described these forms of support as helping them manage daily demands and navigate challenging classroom situations.

Physical Presence and Responsiveness

Educators emphasized the importance of having someone physically present to assist during intense moments or provide relief throughout the day. This form of support – described as the ability to “step in,” “tap out,” or “take a breather” – allowed participants to attend to basic needs, de-escalate situations, and regroup emotionally. Participants described this support as coming from co-teachers, administrators, or other staff members in center-based settings. Some participants noted that they appreciated when this support was offered proactively rather than requiring them to ask, and when it was experienced as supportive rather than evaluative. One participant described how, when they were visibly overwhelmed in the classroom, a staff member stepped in without being asked so they could step outside and regroup. Participants described these moments as reinforcing a shared sense of responsibility among staff. Family childcare providers did not typically report having access to this type of immediate, in-person support.

Support Across Roles

Participants in center-based programs described receiving instrumental support from individuals in a range of roles, including other teachers, supervisors, and staff such as receptionists, nurses, and family workers. Educators described these contributions as helpful both logistically and emotionally, noting that assistance from outside their immediate teaching team reduced feelings of isolation and helped distribute responsibility during demanding moments.

Instrumental Support and Decisions About Staying in the Field

Participants frequently discussed instrumental support in relation to emotional exhaustion and decisions about remaining in a position or leaving a workplace. Several described how access to basic support – such as the ability to step out briefly or receive help during difficult moments – made their work feel more manageable. As one participant explained, having someone available helps “not burn out because you know that somebody is there and has your back.” Others contrasted supportive environments with previous workplaces where such assistance was absent, describing those earlier experiences as contributing to burnout and eventual departure. These accounts highlight how participants linked instrumental support to their capacity to sustain themselves in the work.

Where Participants Wanted More Instrumental Support

Participants, particularly those in center-based settings, expressed a desire for increased staffing during busier parts of the day, such as transitions, lunch, or rest time. They also wanted more consistent break coverage and planning time. Importantly, participants emphasized that additional staff were most helpful when they were responsive, attentive, and engaged in the work. Several noted that inadequately trained or disengaged staff added to their burden rather than alleviating it. Participants also described wanting administrators to provide more hands-on classroom support and to advocate for additional resources when children had specialized needs or ongoing behavioral challenges. Some participants additionally noted wanting greater involvement from families, such as following through on classroom recommendations at home or responding to communication, while recognizing the barriers families may face.

Informational Support

Participants described informational support as guidance that helped them meet professional expectations and develop their practice. This included regulation-oriented information, such as guidance on meeting state or programmatic requirements, as well as practice-oriented information, such as advice from peers or specialists on supporting children’s learning, managing team dynamics, or addressing challenging behaviors. Participants often contrasted instrumental support, which helped them manage the day-to-day demands of the classroom, with informational support, which they described as supporting their growth and problem-solving as educators.

Layered Expertise Across Roles

Participants described receiving informational support from a range of sources, including administrators, co-teachers, behavioral specialists, therapists, coaches, and licensors. Each source provided different types of knowledge, ranging from compliance-related guidance to pedagogical strategies. Educators described using this information to navigate external requirements, address classroom challenges, or improve their practice, particularly when guidance came from individuals with classroom experience or specialized expertise.

Informational Support as Collaborative

Participants described informational support as more helpful when it was collaborative, timely, and experienced as nonjudgmental. Several noted that this made it easier to ask questions, share uncertainty, and seek guidance from colleagues. One participant explained, “It’s really great that [the other teacher and I] can collaborate and work with each other… she’s been teaching longer than I have, and so I can ask her different things,” adding that “it definitely is like a weight lifted off.”

Gaps and Uneven Access to Informational Support

While many participants described receiving informational support, others reported limited or inconsistent access, particularly among family childcare providers. One family childcare provider shared, “I don’t really have any support,” when describing the absence of colleagues with whom to consult or problem-solve. Even within center-based programs, participants described variation in how information was shared. In some settings, administrators proactively asked staff what kind of guidance they wanted through surveys or informal check-ins. In others, communication focused primarily on compliance-related information. Participants described these differences as shaping their ability to make informed decisions and contributing to feelings of professional isolation.

Where Participants Wanted More Informational Support

Participants expressed a desire for more hands-on, practice-based guidance, particularly from administrators or experienced staff who could offer insight in real time. One educator explained, “It would be nice to have someone in my classroom to be able to observe more… and can give you kind of real practical advice about how to deal with the situation.” Others described wanting better communication with families, particularly through in-person conversations rather than digital platforms. Family childcare providers expressed a desire for more consistent access to peer feedback and professional development opportunities. Across participants, clear and timely channels for exchanging information were described as important for applying guidance in everyday practice.

Emotional Support

Participants described emotional support as expressions of empathy, understanding, and solidarity, most often exchanged among fellow educators. These forms of support were described as reciprocal and embedded in everyday interactions, such as informal check-ins, shared venting, or recognizing when someone was having a difficult moment. Participants emphasized the importance of these relationships given the emotional labor inherent in early childhood education.

Emotional Visibility Through Leadership Check-Ins

In addition to peer support, some participants described receiving emotional support from supervisors or administrators through brief but meaningful check-ins. These interactions included asking how someone was doing, creating space to talk, or acknowledging stress. Participants described these moments as emotionally relieving, particularly during periods of overwhelm or exhaustion. However, not all participants reported experiencing this type of support, highlighting variability across centers and leadership approaches.

Structured Facilitated Emotional Support

In a small number of cases, emotional support was described as being facilitated through formal organizational resources, such as access to therapy through Employee Assistance Programs or wellness-related benefits. Participants who had access to these resources described them as helpful for coping with the demands of the work, particularly when support from colleagues felt insufficient.

Relational Isolation in Family Childcare Settings

Family childcare providers described experiencing limited opportunities for emotional support within their professional settings. While some reported close relationships with families, most described relying on personal relationships outside of work, such as family members or friends, to process stress. Participants described this lack of embedded peer support as contributing to feelings of isolation and highlighting differences between center-based and home-based care contexts.

Where Participants Want More Emotional Support

While many participants described strong emotional bonds with colleagues, others expressed a desire for greater understanding and emotional visibility from administrators and families. Some participants described wanting administrators to more regularly acknowledge stress or check in informally, while also noting that administrators themselves often face significant pressures. From families, participants described wanting to be seen and treated as people rather than solely as workers. As one participant noted, “Sometimes just a ‘How are you doing?’ would make a big difference.”

Appraisal Support

Participants described appraisal support as recognition and affirmation from supervisors, coworkers, and families. This recognition took both explicit forms, such as verbal appreciation or praise, and symbolic forms, such as awards or public acknowledgment of classroom efforts. Participants described these moments as affirming their professional competence and reinforcing the value of their work.

Feeling Trusted and Heard

In addition to explicit recognition, participants described feeling affirmed when they were trusted with decision-making or treated as knowledgeable professionals. Educators described feeling valued when asked for input, assigned meaningful responsibilities, or supported in pursuing areas of interest. One assistant educator described feeling accomplished when a lead teacher asked them to create a lesson plan. Others described feeling validated when colleagues or supervisors listened to them and maintained high expectations. Participants noted that the absence of this kind of recognition made it easier to feel uncertain or discouraged in their roles.

Validation Through Child and Family Feedback

Participants also described experiencing appraisal support through feedback from children and families, particularly when they observed positive changes in children’s engagement or development. Examples included children who became more comfortable with school or parents responding positively to classroom updates or milestones. Participants described these moments as affirming the significance of their work. As one participant explained, “I need to know that what I’m doing is important… It’s most necessary for me to know that I feel that what I’m doing is important for humanity.”

Where Participants Want More Appraisal Support

Despite experiencing moments of recognition, many participants expressed a desire for more consistent and intentional appraisal support, particularly from administrators and families. Participants described the emotional toll of feeling unseen in their daily work. One participant shared, “Our lead should come in and just see what we’re doing. They never check in.” Others emphasized the importance of small, relational gestures, such as checking in or acknowledging effort. Participants also expressed a desire for greater recognition from families, not only through verbal appreciation but through forms of relational engagement that signaled acknowledgment of their work, such as sharing feedback about children’s growth or following through on classroom activities.

Persistent Unmet Relational Needs

Across interviews, participants described recurring situations in which unmet support needs – particularly related to staffing, recognition, and classroom management – accumulated over time and were described as emotionally exhausting. These experiences were described as situations in which educators needed support and did not receive it, rather than interpersonal conflict or relational breakdown. Educators described long hours, ongoing behavioral challenges, and minimal interpersonal support during or after these events. Some participants also described having to train and accommodate less experienced staff while simultaneously managing their own classroom responsibilities, without corresponding support or acknowledgement, which added to rather than alleviated strain.

While many participants continued to push through these challenges, they emphasized how emotionally draining it became when no one stepped in or acknowledged the pressure they were under. As one educator put it, “You do have to ask teachers like, ‘Are you okay? [Just try] to make sure because our well-being is put to the test all the time.” Others described the added exhaustion of not being listened to: “When we can’t voice our own opinion, or we got some kind of change that just happened. That is exhausting.” For some, persistent exhaustion was linked to questioning their decision to stay in the profession and their perspective on the field as a whole. “I would never tell anybody at this stage of the game to be an early childhood educator,” said one participant. “It’s really a thankless job, and we’re not getting the support.”

Social Misalignment and Interpersonal Tension

In contrast to persistent unmet relational needs, many educators described strain that developed within ongoing relationships, even when support was sometimes present, but expectations, roles, or values were misaligned. This pattern was especially salient in interactions with families and coworkers. Participants described feeling disregarded or added stress when families failed to follow through with classroom activities at home, disregarded school policies (e.g., sick child protocols), or were consistently late for pickup. Participants described these experiences alongside feelings of being overlooked or deprioritized and a broader sense that some families viewed them less as partners in care and more as a service to be used.

Participants also described interpersonal tension arising from working alongside coworkers who were inexperienced, disengaged, or indifferent to the responsibilities of being an early childhood educator. One participant described “coworkers not doing their job, like sitting on their cell phone, not helping with the kids” as a source of ongoing strain. Even in the absence of overt conflict, ongoing misalignment and interpersonal tension accumulated over the course of the day, wearing on participants’ emotional capacity. As one participant said, “You do get those times where [a coworker] does something and you’re like, ‘What the heck did you do that for?’… That can really grade on you very quickly throughout the day.”

Relational Withdrawal and Disengagement

While participants consistently expressed deep care for the children in their programs, many also described periods of relational withdrawal and emotional distancing that often preceded feelings associated with burnout. Participants described these experiences as emerging gradually in the context of ongoing strain, including persistent behavioral challenges, unreciprocated efforts from families or colleagues, and limited opportunities to recover emotionally. Educators described these experiences as a gradual depletion of emotional capacity rather than a sudden disengagement from work.

Educators described depersonalization as a way of coping with ongoing strain in their relationships at work, with noted impacts on how they interacted both in and out of the classroom. One participant described this process as cumulative and difficult to contain:[This feeling of disconnection] builds, and it builds, or it builds backwards, right? Then my self-esteem—it starts to eat away at that… I know it’s not about me, but when you put your heart and soul into something and it’s not working, how do you not take it into other areas of your life?… Then I feel like I’m not helping anybody. What am I doing?

Small Acts of Resilience and Resistance

Although participants described persistent social strain, many also talked about the everyday ways they tried to manage it. These strategies were often small and practical, such as adjusting classroom routines to better meet children’s needs, using humor to diffuse tense moments, or focusing on small successes with children. Participants emphasized the importance of staying flexible in the face of daily challenges. As one educator explained, “You gotta be open minded. If something goes wrong, you can’t just focus on that for the rest of your day, you have to try to figure out how to cope with it or how to fix the situation.” Another noted, “It’s easy to be defeated, but you know, it’s just kind of finding a creative solution.”

For some participants, these efforts went beyond coping and took on a more oppositional tone. In these accounts, educators described using strain as motivation to speak up or push for change rather than disengage. One participant shared, “I’m too stubborn to [go on autopilot]… I don’t quit. I do quite the opposite. I’m a squeaky wheel… I’ll just keep pushing for change.” Participants described these approaches as ways of continuing to function in demanding environments and, at times, responding actively to conditions they found frustrating.

Participants also described internal strategies they used to get through difficult moments, including breathing or counting techniques to stay calm. Several said they reminded themselves that “tomorrow’s gonna be a new day” as a way of maintaining perspective. Outside the classroom, educators mentioned activities such as walking, exercising, reading, journaling, or spending time with loved ones as ways to decompress and separate work from home life. At the same time, many noted that exhaustion and additional caregiving or job responsibilities often made it difficult to engage in these activities as consistently as they would like.

Discussion

Research on ECE has consistently documented high levels of stress, burnout, and workforce instability (Cumming, 2017), particularly in the years surrounding the COVID-19 pandemic (Kwon et al., 2022; Quinn et al., 2022). Much of this work has examined social support and social strain as separate predictors of educator well-being, often focusing on outcomes such as burnout, job satisfaction, or turnover intentions. Building on this literature, the present study offers a closer look at how educators themselves describe experiences of support and strain in their everyday work and how these experiences intersect within relational and organizational contexts.

Across participants’ accounts, social support and social strain were described as co-occurring rather than mutually exclusive. Educators reported that the absence of support often co-existed with experiences of strain, while ongoing strain shaped how support was perceived and accessed. Participants also described situations in which supportive and straining interactions emerged from the same relationships, such as feeling practically supported by a colleague while simultaneously feeling emotionally overlooked. Taken together, these findings suggest that examining support and strain in isolation may obscure how educators experience workplace relationships in practice. Understanding how these dynamics intersect may therefore contribute to efforts to improve ECE work environments.

Differentiating Types of Social Support in ECE Settings

This study adds nuance to existing research by distinguishing four types of social support – instrumental, informational, emotional, and appraisal – that educators described using in their daily work. Although prior ECE research highlights the importance of supportive relationships (Jeon et al., 2018; Kwon et al., 2021), support is often measured as a single construct (e.g., “collegiality” or “supervisor support”), which can obscure how different kinds of support are actually experienced in practice (Gottlieb & Bergen, 2010). Prior research has established these types of support in other caregiving fields, including nursing (Langford et al., 1997), medicine (Kelly et al., 2019), public health (Lee & Cho, 2019), and social work (Haugstvedt, 2022). Our study extends this work by clarifying what these types of support may look like in ECE settings.

Further, these qualitative patterns are consistent with research suggesting that support types often co-occur but serve somewhat different relational and emotional functions (Mathieu et al., 2019; Oliveira et al., 2021; Schaack et al., 2020). Prior studies have found, for example, that instrumental and emotional support relate differently to stress, strain, and job attitudes (Mathieu et al., 2019), and that instrumental support may be most effective when accompanied by emotional support (Morelli et al., 2015). Although our study does not assess the causal impact of specific support types, educators’ accounts align with this emerging evidence that particular forms of support may relate to different aspects of psychological and professional well-being. In our study, instrumental support was reported as helping participants handle the immediate physical and emotional demands of the job; informational support supported their decision-making and professional development; emotional support was connected with a sense of morale and making them feel less alone; and appraisal support shaped their sense of being valued for their work. Importantly, participants did not describe any one type as sufficient on its own. Rather, they discussed needing multiple types of support across relationships and contexts. By describing these distinctions through educators’ own perspectives, this study provides a clearer picture of how different support types appear in daily practice and how educators view them as shaping different parts of their psychological and professional well-being.

Social Strain as a Relational Experience

In addition to describing multiple forms of support, participants reported persistent experiences of social strain that were distinct from, though often intertwined with, support. Consistent with prior research, strain was not described simply as the absence of support, but as the presence of ongoing relational challenges, including misalignment with families, inconsistent communication with coworkers, and limited recognition of educators’ emotional labor (Pierce & Quiroz, 2019).

Educators in center-based settings described strain arising even in environments where some forms of support were present, particularly when expectations or values were misaligned. For example, participants described situations in which they received practical assistance from colleagues while simultaneously experiencing frustration or tension related to unequal workloads, underqualified support, or lack of follow-through. These accounts support prior findings that support and strain can coexist within the same relationships and that increasing support alone may be insufficient to reduce strain (Romm et al., 2021; Shin & Gyeong, 2023).

Participants also described how strain accumulated over time and, in some cases, shaped their relational engagement at work. Rather than indicating a lack of commitment to children, educators described moments of withdrawal or emotional distancing as ways of managing depleted emotional capacity. These findings echo broader ECE literature linking chronic relational strain to emotional exhaustion and depersonalization (Cumming, 2017; Schaack et al., 2020), while also highlighting how educators actively navigate these conditions in their daily practice.

Coping, Resilience, and the Limits of Individual Adaptation

In the face of ongoing strain, participants described a range of everyday strategies for managing stress, including creative problem-solving, emotional self-regulation, and reframing difficult situations. These strategies were described as ways to get through the day rather than as long-term or sustainable solutions. This echoes findings by Schaack and colleagues (2022), who reported that educators who remained in the ECE field often relied on creative problem-solving to cope with workplace demands. Some participants also described moments when strain motivated them to speak up, advocate for changes, or push back against conditions that felt unfair or unsustainable. These accounts align with emerging evidence that ECE educators engage in active, problem-solving coping mechanisms as a response to challenging work conditions and as a means of maintaining resilience in the workplace (Beltman et al., 2020; Dede et al., 2024), particularly in the years following Covid-19 (Kaveri et al., 2024).

Importantly, participants did not frame resilience as a replacement for support or structural change. Instead, their accounts suggest that individual coping strategies often served as temporary responses in environments where strain was persistent and resources were limited. This aligns with critiques cautioning against over-reliance on resilience narratives that place responsibility for well-being on individual educators rather than addressing relational and organizational conditions (Andrew, 2015; Corr et al., 2014), including within early childhood settings (Beltman et al., 2020).

The Interplay of Social Support and Strain

Although social support and strain are often examined independently, educators’ accounts suggest they frequently coexist in daily practice. This is consistent with the JD-R model, which frames social support as a resource that can buffer demands and relational strain as a job demand with its own influence on well-being (Bakker et al., 2023; Bakker & Demerouti, 2007). Prior research has shown that supportive and straining interactions can arise from the same relationships (Rook et al., 2012; Walen & Lachman, 2000). Participants in this study described similar experiences of feeling both supported and strained by colleagues or families, sometimes within the same interaction. For example, having another teacher present often eased classroom demands, yet that same relationship could generate strain when expectations or responsibilities were misaligned. Comparable patterns have been noted among early childhood educators in Australia, who likewise reported navigating support and strain within the same workplace relationships (Beltman et al., 2020). Our findings reflect these dynamics in the context of New York State ECE settings.

Educators’ accounts also echo prior work emphasizing that support does not automatically counteract strain, and strain is not simply the absence of support (Pierce & Quiroz, 2018). Instead, ongoing misalignment, interpersonal tension, or accumulated exhaustion shaped how educators interpreted, accessed, or felt able to use available support. This resonates with recent evidence suggesting that social support may buffer relational demands only under certain conditions and may be less effective when strain is high or persistent (Li et al., 2025). Understanding this lived, everyday interplay is important for designing interventions that strengthen supportive relationships while addressing the relational demands that contribute to stress.

Social Support, Strain, and Workforce Sustainability

Findings from this study contribute to ongoing discussions about ECE workforce sustainability by showing how educators experience support and strain in relation to emotional exhaustion, professional fulfillment, and decisions about remaining in the field. Consistent with prior research – and in line with the JD–R model – participants described supportive relationships with colleagues and leaders as important resources for coping with the emotional demands of their work (Cumming, 2017; Jeon et al., 2018; Kwon et al., 2021). At the same time, educators described how chronic strain, particularly when unaddressed, was linked with emotional fatigue, detachment, and questioning whether to stay in their role or the profession. This aligns with work showing that relational climate and organizational support are associated with teacher retention and professional commitment in early childhood settings (Grant et al., 2019; Schaack et al., 2020). Further, these accounts align with more recent literature showing that social strain has distinct and sometimes stronger associations with burnout than social support (e.g., Reyes et al., 2024). These findings suggest that efforts to support the ECE workforce may benefit from attending not only to the availability of support, but also to the relational conditions through which support and strain are produced.

Implications for Theory, Research, and Practice

This study advances understanding of social support and strain in ECE by foregrounding educators’ lived experiences and distinguishing among multiple forms of support and patterns of strain. Rather than proposing a new causal model, the findings offer conceptual clarification that may inform future theory building around relational processes in ECE work environments.

Future research would benefit from examining how experiences of support and strain evolve over time and across career stages, as well as how different combinations of support are associated with educator well-being in diverse settings. Longitudinal and mixed-methods designs may be particularly useful for exploring how fluctuations in support and strain relate to outcomes such as retention, mental health, and professional identity.

From a practical perspective, findings suggest that interventions aimed at supporting ECE educators should attend to both the quality and relational context of support, as well as to the reduction of ongoing strain. Leadership practices that emphasize relational attunement, recognition, and collaborative problem-solving may help strengthen emotional and appraisal support, while structural investments in staffing and communication may reduce sources of persistent strain. Importantly, participants’ accounts highlight the need to tailor support strategies to different ECE contexts, particularly for family childcare providers who reported greater relational isolation.

Limitations and Considerations

Findings from this study should be interpreted in light of several limitations. Participants self-selected into the study, which may limit the representativeness of the sample. Data were based on self-report and reflect educators’ perceptions of support and strain; however, perceived support is often a stronger predictor of well-being than objective measures (Nurullah, 2012). The sample was predominantly White and drawn from a single U.S. state, which may limit the generalizability of findings across more diverse cultural, geographic, and policy contexts. Future research should prioritize more diverse samples to better understand how experiences of support and strain vary across settings and communities.

Despite these limitations, this study provides a foundation for advancing conceptual and applied understanding of social support and strain in ECE environments, emphasizing the importance of relational context in shaping educators’ everyday work experiences.
