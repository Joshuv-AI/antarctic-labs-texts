ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe symposium IBD 2024: Charting a New Course in IBD was held in Brisbane, Australia, in November 2024. This was the fifth such meeting for the Australian inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) healthcare community. This symposium provided an overview of current and emerging management strategies for ulcerative colitis (UC) and Crohn's disease (CD). Key topic areas covered included the importance of treating to a minimum of endoscopic remission; important considerations for treatment choice, treatment switch, and treatment de‐escalation; treatment during pregnancy; and emerging therapeutic options. This supplement provides a detailed summary of each of the symposium presentations, as well as key take‐home messages.

Introduction

On November 22–23, 2024, the symposium IBD 2024: Charting a New Course in IBD was held in Brisbane, Australia. This was the fifth such meeting for the Australian inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) healthcare community. The meeting was sponsored by an unrestricted educational grant from Takeda Australia, but they were not involved in the content development.

The independent steering committee of Professor Rupert Leong, A/Professor Jake Begun, A/Professor Réme Mountifield, and Professor Miles Sparrow developed the agenda and recruited the speakers. Two international speakers, Professors Uma Mahadevan and Vipul Jairath, joined members of the organizing committee to discuss key issues and approaches to the management of patients with IBD.

An overview of current and emerging management strategies for ulcerative colitis (UC) and Crohn's Disease (CD) was provided over four sessions. Session 1—Shooting for the Stars—saw lively debates between the international speakers, with Debate 1 arguing whether endoscopic remission is a sufficient treatment target in UC and Debate 2 arguing whether endoscopic remission is a sufficient treatment target in CD.

Session 2—Navigating the Constellation of Drugs in IBD—focused on various treatment modalities including immunomodulators, anti‐integrin therapies, interleukin (IL) inhibitors, Janus kinase inhibitors (JAKi), and other advanced therapies. The use of advanced therapy during pregnancy was also discussed by Professor Mahadevan.

In Session 3—Anchoring your Strategies: The Evolution of IBD Management—Australian clinicians presented on local IBD treatment strategies and ongoing Australian studies (PREDICT‐UC [1] and the DUST study [2]). Professor Jairath also gave an overview of the history of IBD treatment strategies.

In Session 4—Over the Horizon: The Future in IBD—speakers presented on new pharmacotherapeutics in IBD management, the evolution of surgical resection in CD, the place of microbiome testing in IBD, and the role of fecal microbiota transfer (FMT) in IBD treatment. Professor Mahadevan also presented an overview of the future of IBD prevention, prophylaxis, and potential for cure in the next generation.

Included in this Journal of Gastroenterology and Hepatology supplement is a summary of each of the symposium presentations. We hope that you find these summaries useful and informative.

Session 1: Shooting for the Stars

Advances in the medical management of IBD have altered treatment targets. Remission is often described in terms of patient‐reported symptoms, endoscopic appearance, or histologic features [3]. Endoscopic remission has long been associated with better outcomes than symptomatic remission in both UC and CD, and more recent evidence demonstrates that histologic remission is associated with better outcomes than endoscopic remission in UC. Similarly, while transmural healing is considered an adjunctive treatment target in CD, accumulating evidence suggests that this may be beneficial. Currently, there is no single unified definition for remission of UC or CD. As such, Debates 1 and 2 presented arguments for and against the topic: Endoscopic remission is enough in ulcerative colitis and endoscopic remission is enough in Crohn's disease, respectively.

Debate 1: Endoscopic Remission Is Enough in Ulcerative Colitis

In this debate, Professors Uma Mahadevan and Vipul Jairath presented arguments for and against the topic: Endoscopic remission is enough in ulcerative colitis. Professor Mahadevan presented evidence to support the idea that endoscopic remission in UC is an adequate treatment target, even though deeper (histological) remission is preferred. Professor Jairath presented evidence to support the idea that endoscopic remission in UC is not enough, and further treatment should be employed to achieve histologic remission.

For: Endoscopic Remission in UC Is Enough (Uma Mahadevan)

There is no question that the deeper the healing, the better the prognosis for the patient. Histologic remission is regarded as more favorable than endoscopic remission. Current evidence suggests that there is an incremental benefit of achieving endoscopic and histologic remission in patients with UC. A systematic review and meta‐analysis has shown that UC patients with endoscopic remission have a 14% risk of relapse after 12 months, while those with both endoscopic and histologic remission have a relapse risk of 5% [4]. However, from this analysis, it is estimated that only one in eight patients treated with currently available drugs would achieve histologic remission, and only a small subset of these patients would benefit from this level of remission.

Due to limitations, histologic healing is currently not regarded as a formal target by many treatment guidelines [5, 6]. The selecting therapeutic targets in inflammatory bowel disease (STRIDE) consensus guidelines identify endoscopic healing as the long‐term treatment goal and only recommend histologic remission as an adjunct treatment target to endoscopic and symptomatic remission [5, 6]. Similarly, the American Gastroenterological Association (AGA) Clinical Practice Guideline on the Role of Biomarkers for the Management of ulcerative colitis also recommends endoscopic remission as an end‐target in UC [7]. In conclusion, endoscopic remission is enough as it indicates adequate healing and reduces the chance of excessive testing and treatment burden to patients.

Against: Endoscopic Remission in UC Is Not Enough (Vipul Jairath)

There is heterogeneity in disease course and progression in UC disease [8]. Evidence strongly suggests that the treatment of symptomatic remission alone is inadequate, with data showing that treating based on symptoms alone can lead both to undertreatment and overtreatment of disease [9]. Treating to endoscopic remission rather than symptomatic remission leads to a lower relapse rate, and lower risks of hospitalization, colectomy, colorectal cancer, and pouchitis [10, 11, 12, 13]. A meta‐analysis of 13 studies also found that mucosal healing at the first endoscopic evaluation after the initiation of UC therapy was associated with improved long‐term outcomes [14].

Although STRIDE consensus guidelines recommend histologic remission only as an adjunct treatment target to endoscopic and symptomatic remission [5, 6], there is a mounting body of evidence showing that patient outcomes are better when histological remission is achieved. IBD clinical trials as far back as the 1960s showed that patients who have no bowel inflammation in their biopsy specimen are much more likely to remain symptom‐free than those with bowel inflammation [15], suggesting that the rectal biopsy has greater value than the sigmoidoscopy. Histologic remission was found to predict fewer complications [16, 17] and reduce the risk of neoplasia in UC [18] than endoscopic remission. Furthermore, treating to histological remission has been shown to achieve better outcomes than endoscopic remission alone; a meta‐analysis of 17 studies found that patients achieving both endoscopic plus histologic remission have a substantially lower risk of clinical relapse compared with patients achieving endoscopic remission alone [4].

Many of the currently available pharmacologic therapeutics can achieve histologic remission. For example, 5ASA or steroids can induce histological healing [19], vedolizumab reduces both Geboes and Robarts (RHI) scores at 52 weeks [20], and ustekinumab increases the proportion of patients with histoendoscopic mucosal improvement at 52 weeks [21]. As such, although there are downsides to the quest for deeper healing (such as excessive testing and treatment burden to patients, out of pocket costs, and treatment‐related complications [22, 23]), we should aim for histologic remission to achieve the best possible outcome for patients given that current treatments have the potential to achieve histologic remission.

Debate 2: Endoscopic Remission Is Enough in Crohn's Disease

In this debate, Professors Uma Mahadevan and Vipul Jairath presented arguments for and against the topic: Endoscopic remission is enough in Crohn's disease. Professor Mahadevan presented evidence to support the idea that endoscopic remission in CD is an adequate treatment target, while Professor Jairath presented evidence to support the idea that endoscopic remission in CD is not enough, and further treatment should be employed to ensure transmural healing.

For: Endoscopic Remission in CD Is Enough (Uma Mahadevan)

There is no doubt that deep remission (i.e., endoscopic and clinical remission) prevents the progression of CD [24]. Accordingly, both the STRIDE and AGA guidelines recommend both as formal treatment targets [6, 25]. However, current guidelines do not list transmural healing as a formal long‐term treatment target; rather, these guidelines only suggest the consideration of transmural healing in CD management [6, 25]. Evidence suggests that there is no association between histology and relapse in patients with CD. A meta‐analysis of CD histology and relapse has shown no association between histologic activity and relapse in CD [26]. In an analysis of biopsies from patients with CD who had achieved clinical and endoscopic remission, histologic remission was not associated with clinical relapse within 2 years [27]. Furthermore, a post hoc analysis of the prospective STORI cohort showed that, despite correlations with endoscopy and biomarkers, histological scores did not predict clinical relapse in CD patients in remission [28].

Another barrier to the use of histologic remission as a treatment target is the available testing tools. Currently, there are no validated scoring systems for CD. Disease severity is assessed using scoring systems originally developed for UC and focuses on neutrophils. These existing scoring systems may not adequately capture histological markers of CD, including eosinophils, granulomas, and pyloric gland metaplasia [29]. Additionally, disease lesions in CD are patchy and transmural, making the measurement of disease activity via biopsy difficult. Furthermore, the timing of biopsy may not capture histologic healing as it may take longer to observe than endoscopic and symptomatic healing [29].

In conclusion, ideally clinicians would like to see clinical, endoscopic, histologic, and transmural remission in patients with CD. However, chasing histologic healing in patients who have achieved clinical and endoscopic remission may not be appropriate in practice, especially in patients who have failed multiple prior treatments. In such cases, endoscopic remission is likely enough, particularly since only 61% of patients with clinical/endoscopic remission achieved histologic remission [30].

Against: Endoscopic Remission in CD Is Not Enough (Vipul Jairath)

Emerging evidence suggests that inflammation in CD is the driver of disease relapse and complications. Patients with no inflammation following treatment have been shown to have a lower risk of relapse [31] and a lower risk of infection [32]. It is now evident that treating to symptomatic remission as a treatment goal is suboptimal in CD, as symptoms poorly correlate with inflammation [33]. Although treatment to symptomatic remission reduces complications, one‐third of patients with CD still suffer from complications over a 2‐year follow‐up period, meaning that treatment of inflammation is inadequate when treating to symptomatic remission [34]. Treating to endoscopic remission in CD yields significantly greater benefits than treating to symptomatic remission alone [10, 31]. Benefits include reduced risks of treatment failure, CD‐related hospitalization, and relapse [10, 31]. Mucosal healing is also associated with improved long‐term outcomes in CD [35].

Despite the benefits, treatment of symptoms and endoscopy alone still leads to complications, and most available treatments do not lead to complete mucosal healing. Treatment of transmural healing has shown additional prognostic benefit over endoscopic healing in reducing complications. Transmural healing (combination of endoscopic and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) healing) in CD reduces the risk of major outcomes (surgery, progression of bowel damage, and hospitalization) compared to both MRI and endoscopic mucosal healing [36]. Currently available therapeutics have been shown to achieve inflammatory healing. For example, both anti‐tumor necrosis factor (anti‐TNF) [37, 38] and vedolizumab [39, 40] therapy have been shown to induce radiologic remission in CD. In conclusion, evidence is mounting to suggest that the absence of all visible inflammation—mucosal and submucosal—should be the goal for CD patients, alongside normal quality of life.

Session 2: Navigating the Constellation of Drugs in IBD

Treatment Withdrawal: What, When and How? Aviv Pudipeddi

A common question encountered by clinicians in IBD management is when medical therapy should either be de‐escalated or withdrawn. There may be a variety of reasons for clinicians and patients to consider treatment withdrawal, including safety concerns with immunosuppressive therapy, pill burden, sustained remission, and life circumstances. It is important for clinicians to be aware of the relapse risks upon treatment withdrawal, considering relapse risk differs across medication classes, particularly mesalazine, immunomodulators, and advanced therapies, either as monotherapy or combination therapy.

Mesalazine is commonly used to treat patients with mild to moderate UC. Rectal mesalazine therapy is typically used for patients with proctitis or proctosigmoiditis; however, withdrawal of rectal mesalazine monotherapy leads to high relapse rates of above 80% to 90% at 1 to 2 years [41]. De‐escalation to rectal therapy three times per week instead of complete withdrawal significantly lowers rates of relapse at 6 months (29% versus 54%, p = 0.017) [42]. Oral mesalazine is commonly used together with rectal therapy due to superior efficacy rates compared to monotherapy. Patients' oral mesalazine doses are often de‐escalated from 4 g/4.8 g to 2 g/2.4 g, with a 26% risk of relapse if in clinical and endoscopic remission at the time of dose reduction versus 47.8% risk of relapse for nonremission patients at the time of dose reduction (p = 0.001) [43]. Once patients progress to advanced therapies, there is no efficacious benefit of continuing oral mesalazine, and treatment withdrawal should be considered [44].

Thiopurines are used as maintenance therapy in patients with CD and UC. Patients and clinicians may be concerned about the long‐term use of thiopurines due to risks (such as lymphoproliferative disorders, non‐melanoma skin cancers, and urinary tract cancers) and may want to initiate treatment withdrawal. A multicenter, retrospective study of IBD patients on azathioprine monotherapy in sustained clinical remission for at least 3 years identified that upon azathioprine treatment withdrawal, moderate‐to‐severe relapse rates at 12 and 24 months were 23% and 39% in CD, and 12% and 26% in UC, respectively [45]. Importantly, elevated C‐reactive protein and white blood cell counts were predictive of relapse, demonstrating that objective markers of disease activity should be assessed prior to treatment withdrawal [45].

Thiopurine withdrawal when used in combination with biological agents has only been studied in randomized controlled trials with infliximab and vedolizumab. The SPARE trial demonstrated that, for patients with CD in steroid‐free clinical remission for at least 6 months on infliximab/immunosuppressant combination therapy, there was no significant impact on 2‐year relapse‐free survival upon immunosuppressant withdrawal versus continuing combination therapy [46]. The VIEWS study identified lower rates of biochemical (fecal calprotectin) remission and histoendoscopic remission at 1 year following thiopurine withdrawal for vedolizumab‐treated patients with UC [47]. Histological activity and prior anti‐TNF exposure were identified as predictive factors of relapse [47]. Infliximab withdrawal was examined in the STORI trial, which found ~50% risk of relapse for patients with IBD on combination therapy with an immunomodulator and who were in steroid‐free clinical remission for more than 6 months [48]. Importantly, biochemical and endoscopic disease activity predicted relapse.

There is limited data on vedolizumab or ustekinumab withdrawal. One retrospective study found a 64% risk of relapse at 18 months for patients in steroid‐free clinical remission who underwent vedolizumab withdrawal [49]. For tofacitinib, a double‐blind, randomized, parallel‐group study examined the risk of relapse at 30 months following de‐escalation from 10‐ to 5‐mg tofacitinib twice daily for patients with UC in stable clinical remission for greater than 6 months [50]. Relapse risk was 50% in the 5 mg twice daily group, versus 37% in the group maintained on 10 mg twice daily. Higher rates of relapse were noted in the setting of prior anti‐TNF failure or mild endoscopic activity (Mayo endoscopic Score 1) at the time of de‐escalation [50].

The increasing number of medical therapies, together with the availability of effective monitoring tools, has led to greater interest in the concept of treatment withdrawal in IBD. The consideration of treatment withdrawal should be individualized for each patient. A structured approach is required to reduce the likelihood of a significant relapse (Table 1). Recent data suggest that treatment withdrawal can be implemented effectively; however, shared decision‐making with patients is crucial, so they are aware of the risks of relapse upon treatment withdrawal and the close follow‐up required.

Blocking Trafficking: Anti‐Integrin, Sphingosine‐1‐Phosphate Modulators (S1P) (Vipul Jairath)

Drug development over the last decade has evolved with growing interest in interleukin inhibition (e.g., IL‐23 inhibitors), cell trafficking interference (e.g., vedolizumab and S1P inhibitors), and enzyme inhibition (e.g., JAK inhibitors). Dysregulated leukocyte recruitment is considered a hallmark feature of IBD, as signified by accelerated recruitment or enhanced retention signals (likely chemoattractant chemokines) at sites of inflammation [51]. The molecules that mediate the sequential steps of the leukocyte adhesion cascade (i.e., capture/tethering, rolling, activation, and firm adhesion) allow leukocytes to escape the circulation and migrate to sites of inflammation. Drugs that target the leukocyte cascade into the bloodstream have been investigated over the years with varying success.

A promising therapeutic approach in IBD includes targeting molecules involved in intestinal T‐cell homing and retention, such as integrins. Integrin blockade can either be broad or gut‐selective. Natalizumab, an older drug, is an anti‐α4 integrin antibody that blocks both α4β7:MAdCAM‐1‐mediated leukocyte trafficking to the gut as well as α4β1:VCAM‐1‐mediated trafficking to inflamed tissues, including the central nervous system (CNS). Inhibiting the α4β1:VCAM‐1 interaction is thought to reduce immune surveillance of the CNS and facilitate the development of progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy (PML). Due to the association with PML, and the availability of alternative agents that are not associated with PML, natalizumab is not likely to be used in IBD patients who fail currently available medical therapy [52]. In comparison, vedolizumab, an anti‐α4β7 antibody, acts to block the α4β7:MAdCAM‐1 interaction. A subset of memory T lymphocytes preferentially migrates into the gastrointestinal tract and causes inflammation that is characteristic of IBD and pouchitis. Vedolizumab selectively inhibits adhesion of these cells to MAdCAM‐1. Since MAdCAM‐1 is expressed selectively in the gut, vedolizumab does not induce PML [52].

Even after a decade in the life cycle of vedolizumab, new studies continue to emerge. In the REPREVIO study for the prevention of postoperative CD recurrence, vedolizumab (300‐mg IV) given at Weeks 0, 8, 16, and 24 reduced severe endoscopic recurrence in 10 (23.3%) of 43 patients versus 23 (62.2%) of 37 patients in the placebo group at 26 weeks (difference −38.9% [95% CI –56.0 to −17.3]; p = 0.0004) [53]. The VERDICT study is a prospective, multicentre, randomized controlled trial (NCT04259138) to determine the optimal treatment target in moderately‐to‐severely active UC, with the primary outcome being the rate of UC‐related complications in each group at 2 years [54]. Patients are randomly assigned to three treatment target groups: Group 1 (corticosteroid‐free [CSF] symptomatic remission), Group 2 (CSF symptomatic remission + CSF endoscopic remission), and Group 3 (CSF symptomatic remission + CSF endoscopic remission + CSF histologic remission, also termed disease clearance). In this study, vedolizumab (300‐mg IV) was escalated according to a treatment algorithm dependent on the patient's baseline UC therapy until the target was achieved at weeks 16, 32, or 48. In analysis to date, in patients with observed data and a Week 16 visit, 64.1% (75/117), 42.2% (73/173), and 40.2% (94/234) in Groups 1, 2, and 3, respectively, achieved disease clearance. In patients with observed data and a Week 32 visit, 81.6% (80/98), 61.7% (87/141), and 53.3% (112/210), respectively, achieved disease clearance by Week 32. The interim results show improvement across all endpoints from Week 16 to 32 and continue to support the efficacy of vedolizumab in achieving the stringent endpoint of corticosteroid‐free disease clearance [55]. The study is still ongoing.

The VECTORS study (NCT06257706) is also underway. VECTORS is a study evaluating transmural healing as a treatment target in Crohn's disease, measured through noninvasive intestinal ultrasound (IUS) in patients treated with vedolizumab. The primary outcome measure is the percentage of participants with corticosteroid‐free endoscopic remission at Week 48.

S1P is a bioactive sphingolipid mediator involved in the immune response. S1P signaling plays an essential role in lymphocyte trafficking and inflammation, and its immunologic functions are mediated by G protein‐coupled S1P receptors 1–5 [54]. Increased S1P levels at an inflammation site exacerbate the inflammatory process by recruiting immune cells and inflammatory mediators. However, S1P also activates a negative feedback loop, thus having a dual role in the inflammatory response [56]. The S1P receptor family (S1P1–5), as with other small molecules, has the potential for off‐target effects.

Ozanimod, the first S1P mediator to be approved for UC, is highly selective for S1P1 and S1P5 receptors. The ozanimod pivotal study (True North) consisted of two phases, induction and maintenance [57]. During the induction period, at Week 10, a significantly greater proportion of patients treated with ozanimod achieved clinical remission (primary endpoint) compared with placebo (18.4% vs. 6.0%; p < 0.0001). In terms of speed of onset of effect, improvements with ozanimod versus placebo in rectal bleeding score were seen starting at Week 2. In the maintenance period, the proportion of patients who achieved clinical remission at Week 52 was significantly higher with ozanimod versus placebo (37.0% vs. 18.5%; p < 0.0001). After stratifying by prior tumor necrosis factor (TNF) exposure, a significant difference was observed for clinical response (ozanimod vs. placebo, 36.9% vs. 18.5%, p = 0.008) but not clinical remission at Week 10. In bionaïve patients, significantly greater responses were seen with ozanimod versus placebo across outcomes (p < 0.001). Absolute lymphocyte counts (ALC) decreased at Week 5 and remained low with ozanimod treatment, in accordance with the expected mechanism of action. Lymphocytes are sequestered; their function is not impaired. Since S1P receptors are expressed on the atrioventricular node, bradycardia may be observed occasionally with the first dose of ozanimod. These first‐dose cardiac effects can be mitigated through dose‐titration; however, ozanimod should not be used in patients with known AV nodal block or recent myocardial infarction. Macular edema (ME) may be associated with S1P receptor modulator nonselective binding to other S1P receptors [58], and patients with underlying eye conditions should be monitored.

Etrasimod, a more recent S1P compound in development, was studied in the ELEVATE clinical development program. The program utilized a unique “treat‐through” design where patients were randomized to etrasimod 2 mg or placebo for 52 weeks (ELEVATE UC 52) [59]. From Week 12, patients whose disease had not improved or had worsened compared with baseline could discontinue and enroll in an open‐label extension study (ELEVATE UC OLE; up to 5 years). A 12‐week induction study was conducted (ELEVATE UC 12) [59]. Most patients (~70%) in these studies were naive to biologic agents or JAK inhibitors.

Exposure to anti‐TNF‐α antibody occurred in 20% to 25% of patients, and anti‐integrin antibodies in 9% to 14%. In ELEVATE UC 52, the treatment arm showed almost 20% higher clinical remission versus placebo (p < 0.001) at Week 12, and 25% higher at Week 52 (p < 0.001). All key secondary endpoints were statistically significant. There were significant differences in symptomatic remission between etrasimod and placebo on Day 11 (p = 0.034), based on e‐diary data. Of note, patients with isolated proctitis had clinical remission with etrasimod versus placebo at Weeks 12 and 52 (p < 0.001). Clinical remission was statistically significant for bionaïve patients at Weeks 12 and 52 and decreased by almost 50% in bioexperienced patients but remained significant.

A first‐dose reduction in heart rate has been shown to be an on‐target effect of all S1P receptor modulators with activity at S1P1; reduction in the heart rate does not continue with continued administration of etrasimod. Patients with a history of recent MI or known nodal issues would not be selected for etrasimod. Macular edema is not a significant issue, except in patients with diabetes. Mild transaminase elevations are common, require monitoring, but are rarely significant.

Blocking Interleukin‐23—With and Without Interleukin‐12 (Mayur Garg)

Since its discovery in 2000, interleukin (IL)‐23 has become recognized as a crucial cytokine mediating the initiation and perpetuation of inflammation in multiple conditions, including psoriasis, psoriatic arthritis, and IBD. The IL‐23 receptor was identified as a high‐risk gene locus for CD in 2006 [60]. Physiologically, the IL‐23/IL‐17 axis is thought to be responsible for protection against extracellular organisms such as Klebsiella, S. aureus , C. albicans , P. carinii, and Citrobacter rodentium [61]. IL‐23 is secreted by CD64‐expressing myeloid cells in response to endogenous or exogenous stimuli, and in turn stimulates “type 17” cells (such as T helper 17 (Th17), T cytotoxic 17 (Tc17), natural killer T cells, and type 3 innate lymphoid cells) to secrete inflammatory cytokines including IL‐22, IL‐17A, TNF‐α, and interferon‐γ (INF‐γ) [62].

IL‐12 is also a crucial cytokine mediating the initiation and perpetuation of inflammation in multiple conditions. IL‐12 mediates its effects via Th1 cells, promoting the production of IL‐10, IFN‐γ, and IL‐2, and is responsible for preventing intracellular infection by organisms such as mycobacteria and salmonella [62].

Ustekinumab is a monoclonal antibody against the p40 subunit of IL‐12 and IL‐23 and was approved for psoriasis in 2009, CD in 2016, and UC in 2019. The efficacy of ustekinumab for CD and UC was established in the sentinel UNITI and UNIFI studies, and long‐term extension studies have shown clinical remission to persist in most responders out to 4–5 years [63, 64, 65, 66]. Real‐world evaluation studies have confirmed these results, together with an excellent safety profile [67, 68]. Data in fistulizing disease remain limited to date [69]. The effectiveness of ustekinumab has been demonstrated in peripheral arthralgias and arthritis, as well as for skin and ocular manifestations of IBD in small series, but not in axial arthritis [70, 71]. Reinduction and/or dose escalation to 4‐week dosing has been shown to recapture clinical response in about half of all patients losing response to 8‐week ustekinumab in IBD [72]. Data regarding the need for concomitant immunomodulators with ustekinumab remain conflicting, with most retrospective studies so far showing no additional benefit. Ustekinumab has been shown to be as effective as adalimumab in patients with CD in a prospective trial [73], and more effective than vedolizumab in retrospective studies [74], as initial therapy and in anti‐TNF inadequate responders, but comparative data in UC remain scant [75]. Upregulation of IL‐23R in mucosal tissue in nonresponders to anti‐TNF therapy provides some physiological evidence for the use of ustekinumab and specific IL‐23 inhibitors in these patients [76].

Antibodies to the p19 subunit of IL‐23 have recently been developed and studied in IBD, psoriasis, and psoriatic arthritis. There are three main types, including guselkumab, mirikizumab, and risankizumab. All are in various stages of development and regulatory approval in these conditions. Risankizumab was shown to be more efficacious than ustekinumab in the treatment of psoriasis [77]. They have been shown to be efficacious in bionaïve and bioinadequate responders in CD and UC, with maintenance of response superior to placebo [78, 79, 80, 81]. In patients with CD previously exposed to anti‐TNF, the open‐label SEQUENCE study demonstrated the superiority of risankizumab over ustekinumab in achieving endoscopic remission at Week 48 [82]. Studies of guselkumab and mirikizumab have not demonstrated as significant a delta over ustekinumab, though they were not powered for these analyses [81]. Emerging data suggest risankizumab may be used in patients failing to respond to ustekinumab, with about half achieving a response. No new safety signals have so far been detected with the IL 23 p19 antibodies. Guselkumab is the only one of the three with a wild‐type Fc portion, allowing it to bind CD64 on myeloid cells, potentially able to enhance its effect. However, it is uncertain whether this will translate into a significant clinical difference in effectiveness between the different IL 23 p19 inhibitors.

Inhibiting Janus Kinase: The Good and the Bad (Jakob Begun)

Janus kinase (JAK) family of proteins was identified and characterized in the 1990s and consists of the highly homologous proteins JAK1, JAK2, JAK3, and TYK2 [83]. These cytoplasmic kinases are responsible for initiating signaling cascades downstream from transmembrane receptors. Extracellular cytokines and growth factors bind to and induce dimerization of their cognate receptors, which exposes tyrosine residues for phosphorylation by associated JAKs. After phosphorylation, signal transducer and activator of transcription (STAT) proteins are able to dock and are subsequently phosphorylated by the associated JAK. STAT phosphorylation leads to their import into the nucleus and, through their transcription factor activity, leads to upregulation of downstream genes. There is selective association of JAKs with different types of tyrosine kinase receptors (which is cell‐type specific) leading to differential recruitment of STAT proteins to phosphorylated receptors. As such, there is a wide variety of downstream gene activation depending on which receptors are activated and which JAK/STAT proteins are recruited [84]. IBD, similar to other immune‐mediated inflammatory diseases, is generally characterized by an increase in pro‐inflammatory cytokine signaling, and several of these cytokines' activities are modulated by JAK/STAT signaling, including well‐characterized cytokines such as IL‐23 and IL‐6 [84].

Tofacitinib is a JAK inhibitor with a short half‐life that selectively inhibits JAK1 and JAK3, with minimal inhibition of JAK2 and Tyk2. Tofacitinib is approved for a variety of indications [85]. The Phase 3 OCTAVE trials in UC demonstrated that tofacitinib 10‐mg BD induced remission in patients with UC, and that in responders who were rerandomized to tofacitinib or placebo, both the 5‐mg BD and 10‐mg BD doses of tofacitinib were able to maintain remission after 12 months of therapy [86]. Post hoc analysis demonstrated rapid response in patients, with a significant reduction in stool frequency and rectal bleeding as early as Day 3 [87]. Unlike many biologic therapies, the difference in response rates to tofacitinib and placebo was not significantly lower in bioexposed patients compared to bionaïve [88]. The Phase 2 trials of tofacitinib in CD did not demonstrate significant clinical remission rates after 8 weeks of induction compared to placebo, although the decrease in the Crohn's Disease Activity Index (CDAI) was more significant in the tofacitinib groups, and Phase 3 trials were not pursued in CD [89].

Upadacitinib is a selective JAK1 inhibitor with low inhibition of JAK3 and low activity against JAK2 and Tyk2. Upadacitinib has been approved across a variety of indications [85]. The parallel Phase 3 trials U‐ACHIEVE and U‐ACCOMPLISH demonstrated the efficacy of upadacitinib 45 mg daily in inducing clinical remission in patients with active UC after 8 weeks of treatment [90]. The maintenance trial of upadacitinib responders demonstrated that upadacitinib 30 and 15 mg daily was able to maintain clinical remission after 12 months of therapy [90]. Similar to tofacitinib, the response to upadacitinib is rapid, with a significant reduction in stool frequency, urgency, and rectal bleeding seen as early as 3 days. The Phase 3 trials U‐EXCEL and U‐EXCEED demonstrated the effectiveness of upadacitinib in CD, with significantly higher clinical remission and endoscopic response rates in patients receiving upadacitinib 45 mg daily for 12 weeks compared with placebo [91]. Rerandomization of upadacitinib induction responders in the U‐ENDURE trial demonstrated significant rates of clinical remission and endoscopic response in both the groups treated with 15 and 30 mg daily [91]. Similar to tofacitinib, previous exposure to biologics does not have a significant impact on upadacitinib response rates in either UC or CD [91].

In Australia, real‐world data gathered for UC with tofacitinib and upadacitinib has demonstrated impressive rates of clinical remission above 70% for both agents after 8 weeks of induction therapy [92]. Given the large effect size and efficacy speed of both tofacitinib and upadacitinib, both of these agents have been investigated in acute severe colitis (ASUC), a severe form of UC that is considered a medical emergency. In patients with steroid‐refractory ASUC, tofacitinib 10‐mg TDS reduced the need for colectomy compared to patients receiving tofacitinib 10‐mg BD and historic controls [93]. Similar early experience with upadacitinib 45 mg daily or 30‐mg BD has demonstrated efficacy in this population, even after failure of infliximab rescue therapy [94, 95]. An interesting randomized placebo‐controlled trial from India demonstrated that treatment with tofacitinib 10‐mg TDS from the first day of admission with ASUC was associated with a lower need for rescue therapy and colectomy [96]. Retrospective multicenter Australian data indicate that rescue therapy with either upadacitinib 45 mg daily or tofacitinib 10‐mg TDS appears similarly effective in ASUC and is being used more commonly for patients who have not responded to infliximab rescue therapy or when infliximab rescue is contraindicated [97].

The safety of JAK inhibitors is an important consideration for clinicians and physicians prescribing JAK inhibitors for patients with IBD. A relatively common adverse event noted in trials of upadacitinib, and less so tofacitinib, is the incidence of acne vulgaris, which appears to be a dose‐related effect [98]. JAK inhibition is associated with decreased antiviral interferon responses, and consistent with this, the Phase 3 trials of tofacitinib and upadacitinib in IBD have demonstrated an increased risk of shingles due to varicella zoster virus (VZV) reactivation. A network meta‐analysis of IBD medications demonstrated the risk of VZV was highest with upadacitinib and tofacitinib compared to biologic medications [99]. Vaccination with recombinant VZV is recommended for patients over 18 years receiving JAK inhibitors. Another key safety concern among patients receiving JAK inhibitors is the risk of malignancy and major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE). The ORAL surveillance trial was an FDA‐mandated Phase 4 safety study of tofacitinib in rheumatoid arthritis patients with cardiovascular risk factors compared to adalimumab. This trial demonstrated a higher incidence of malignancy (mainly lung cancer) and myocardial infarction in patients treated with tofacitinib compared to adalimumab [100]. Most of this risk was in the group of patients receiving a 10‐mg BD dose of tofacitinib, who were over 65 years old and were active or former smokers. As a result of this trial and other safety data, all JAK inhibitors carry a warning regarding malignancy and MACE and recommend careful consideration of risk and benefits compared to other treatments in patients over age 65. Analysis of the OCTAVE data in UC did not demonstrate any MACE or malignancy safety signal, which may reflect the lower risk of UC patients in this trial compared to the ORAL surveillance population [101].

When considering the use of JAK inhibitors in IBD it is important to consider the “good and the bad.” There are clear benefits of JAK inhibitors in IBD including their efficacy, rapid onset and washout, and oral route of administration. Their efficacy in difficult‐to‐treat populations including bioexposed patients and patients with a high inflammatory burden such as severe colitis make choosing a JAK inhibitor an attractive therapeutic option. The safety profile of JAK inhibitors is distinct from biologic therapies; JAK inhibitors should be used with some degree of caution in patients over 65 years or those with cardiovascular risk factors. The risk of Shingles is important but can be modified with the use of proactive vaccination. Finally, due to concerns of teratogenicity, JAK inhibitors should not be used in patients who are pregnant or breast feeding [102]. In summary, JAK inhibitors represent an important tool in the arsenal of IBD medications and, when used in appropriate patients, can improve outcomes in IBD.

IBD Medication Safety in Pregnancy and Early Childhood Development (Uma Mahadevan)

Women with IBD will often carry the diagnosis during their childbearing years. While much attention is focused on the safety of medication during pregnancy and lactation, it is disease activity that carries the highest risk to maternal and fetal health. The majority of IBD medications can and should be continued throughout pregnancy and lactation [103].

Preconception Counseling

Women with IBD should be in documented remission for at least 3 months prior to attempting conception [104]. Medications should be optimized and a clear plan for pregnancy should be communicated to all providers. Nutritional status should be assessed, and basic health care maintenance completed [104].

Disease Activity

Active disease is associated with a clear increased risk of maternal adverse events, including spontaneous abortion, preterm birth, and small for gestational age infants [105]. Infant adverse events associated with active disease include an increased neonatal intensive care unit stay and infections. Assessment of disease activity should occur in the preconception period and throughout pregnancy based on patient disease severity.

Medications During Pregnancy

5‐Aminosalicylates and thiopurines should be continued throughout pregnancy. Given recent warnings, those on thiopurines should be monitored for intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy. Methotrexate is a teratogen and should be stopped at least 3 months prior to attempting conception [104]. Steroids can be used if needed but are associated with adverse outcomes, likely intertwined with underlying disease activity.

The placenta develops a receptor, FcRn, around Week 14 of gestation. Large molecules like monoclonal antibodies will not cross during the first trimester during the key period of organogenesis (Weeks 3–8). This will reduce the risk of congenital malformations. However, small molecules will cross during this time, and animal studies have shown teratogenicity at supratherapeutic doses, and in the case of upadacitinib, at doses used in humans [106].

The antibody transfer becomes increasingly efficient with 80% of antibody transfer occurring in the third trimester. This, coupled with the infant's immature reticuloendothelial system, can result in supratherapeutic levels of monoclonal antibody at birth that can take 6 months to clear. All biologics can be continued throughout pregnancy on schedule given the physiology of placental transfer, the efficacy of the medication, and existing data, particularly for TNF agents, showing no increase in infant adverse events to 4 to 5 years of age [106]. There is clear evidence of disease flare and adverse events associated with stopping the biologic early. On the other hand, small molecules such as S1P modulators and JAKi should be avoided in pregnancy unless there is no viable alternative to maintain maternal health.

Medications During Lactation

5‐Aminosalicylates, thiopurines, and corticosteroids can be continued during lactation. Methotrexate, S1P agents, and JAKi should not be taken during lactation [106].

Infant Development

Based on the validated ages and stages questionnaire, offspring of mothers with IBD achieve similar milestones to the general population and other offspring of mothers with IBD regardless of biologic and thiopurine use [106].

Vaccines

Inactive vaccines can be given to the offspring of mothers with IBD on schedule regardless of in uteromedication exposure. Based on recent data, live rotavirus vaccine can be given on schedule despite in utero biologic exposure. However, the live Bacillus‐Calmette‐Guerin (BCG) vaccine should be avoided until 6 months of age or serum concentrations of drug are not detectable [106].

Summary

Management of the pregnant patient is a multidisciplinary project with involvement of the gastroenterologist, high‐risk obstetrician, pediatrician, and sometimes surgeon, nutritionist, and mental health professional. Maintenance of disease remission is vital to a healthy pregnancy.

Session 3: Anchoring Your Strategies—The Evolution of Ibd Management

Landmark IBD Strategy Studies—What Have We Learned to Date? (Vipul Jairath)

The STRIDE recommendations are well recognized. STRIDE provides treatment targets, essentially surrogate endpoints, to obtain meaningful clinical outcomes for patients. These targets can be divided into early assessment (symptoms), intermediate assessment (biomarkers), and long‐term assessment (endoscopy, imaging), with various trials now following these strategies [6].

Strategy Trials of Early Intervention

Evidence has been generated over two decades to support early combination therapy based on symptoms reducing longer term complications. The step‐up top down (SUTD) study from 2008 was a forward‐thinking seminal trial; the concept and planning of this trial occurred as infliximab became available [107]. It was the first randomized controlled trial (RCT) to compare therapeutic strategy approaches rather than drug therapy by comparing early combined immunosuppression (ECI) therapy with the conventional management (CM) (step‐up) approach. This study was the first to assess corticosteroid (CS)‐free clinical remission as an endpoint and mucosal healing as a secondary endpoint of clinical importance. It was also the first to include CD patients with very short disease duration (2–2.5 weeks).

The SUTD study found that more patients in the ECI group achieved CS‐ and surgery‐free remission than the CM group. Infliximab was administered intermittently; however, the mucosal healing rate in the ECI group was found to be twice that of the conventional management group at Week 104 (p = 0.028) [107].

Another landmark study was PROFILE, a biomarker‐stratified trial in early (diagnosed within 6 months, biologic naïve) moderate to severe CD [108]. In this study, patients were randomized to either top‐down or accelerated step‐up treatment strategies with infliximab and immunomodulator therapy for 48 weeks. Patients were randomized within biomarker groups to early step‐up therapy. Dose escalation was based on symptoms as per the Harvey‐Bradshaw Index (HBI) and C‐reactive protein (CRP) or fecal calprotectin levels. A large difference in treatment effect was seen between early combination therapy and step‐up care, including high rates of endoscopic remission not previously seen.

The early safety concern regarding combination therapy potentially leading to more adverse events was not confirmed through registry data, with the TREAT registry showing inflammation, not treatment, as the main driver of infection [109]. This was supported by registry data where patients with CD who responded to adalimumab had a lower risk of developing serious infections compared to nonresponders [32]. These findings indicate that the initiation of advanced therapy for CD may lower the risk of serious infections through effective disease control and avoidance of corticosteroids. Top‐down therapy results in higher rates of steroid‐free remission and a lower infection rate.

Strategy Trials Based on Symptoms

Treating to symptomatic remission has been shown to decrease the risk of complications in patients with active CD. REACT 1 was a pragmatic study on real‐world treatment practice [34]. It was the first cluster‐randomized trial with frequent symptom assessment and algorithmic treatment escalation in CD. Hospitals were cluster randomized to early combined ECI or CM following treatment algorithms. Mean HBI scores at baseline indicate low disease activity. For the primary endpoint of symptomatic remission, no difference between groups was seen after 2 years. The results showed that the treatment strategy was effective; however, it was not reflected when measuring symptoms. The next landmark trial, CALM study, used calprotectin and CRP as biomarkers. The CALM study looked at the effect of tight control management in patients with early disease (< 1‐year duration), where patients were randomized to symptomatic management based on Crohn's disease activity index (CDAI) or a biomarker (CRP or calprotectin) [110]. This study demonstrated that proactive intensification of therapy based on biomarkers of inflammation was better than symptom‐based intensification. It was one of the first studies to use mucosal healing as an endpoint, demonstrating a between‐group difference of 15% (p = 0.010). The CALM study represents the cornerstone of a tight control strategy in CD in achieving the target of endoscopic remission.

Strategy Trials Based on Endoscopy/Imaging

Treating to symptoms and biomarkers in early disease has resulted in improved endoscopic remission compared to symptoms alone. The REACT 2 was a cluster‐randomized study comparing the effectiveness of an enhanced‐care (EC) algorithm incorporating early combination therapy and treatment intensification targeting the absence of ulcers to a step‐care (SC) approach targeting clinical remission [111]. The study found no difference in CD‐related complications between EC and SC when the treatment policies were applied at the cluster level. Large risk reductions (25%–29%) in CD‐related complications were observed in patients with CRP‐defined active disease, despite a modest (32.9%) effect of the algorithms in achieving ulcer healing. This study demonstrates that treating to a target of ulcer healing is more effective than symptom‐based management in patients with evidence of active inflammation.

Treating to endoscopic remission has shown a decrease in the risk of complications compared to clinical remission. The STARDUST study involving treat‐to‐target versus standard‐of‐care in CD also showed treating to target of mucosal healing is more effective than a clinically driven maintenance strategy [112].

Infliximab Dosing in Acute Severe Ulcerative Colitis—Lessons Learned From PREDICT‐UC (Christopher F.D. Li Wai Suen, Matthew Choy, Danny Con & Peter De Cruz)

Acute severe ulcerative colitis (ASUC) is a life‐threatening medical emergency that occurs in up to 25% of patients with UC and is associated with a mortality of up to 1% [113]. Approximately 30% of patients fail first‐line therapy with intravenous corticosteroids and 20% fail second‐line rescue therapy with either infliximab or cyclosporin, resulting in the need for emergency colectomy [113]. Although higher dosed or more frequently dosed infliximab regimens have entered clinical practice, there have been no head‐to‐head comparisons, and evidence to indicate whether such practice improves outcomes has been conflicting [113, 114].

PREDICT‐UC [1] was a study of 138 patients with corticosteroid‐refractory ASUC based on Truelove and Witts' criteria [115]. It comprised three components: firstly, a randomized control trial of initial infliximab dose comparing standard, 5 mg/kg versus intensified, 10 mg/kg, with outcomes assessed by Day 7; secondly, a randomized induction strategy study assessing outcomes from Day 8 to Month 3; and thirdly, an exploratory maintenance cohort study exploring outcomes to Month 12.

In the initial dose RCT [1], there was no difference in the proportion of patients who achieved a clinical response by Day 7 (defined as a reduction in Lichtiger score [116] to under 10 as well as a decrease of at least 3 points from baseline, improvement in rectal bleeding and a reduction in stool frequency to 4 or less per day) between infliximab 5‐mg/kg (61%) and 10‐mg/kg (65%) doses. No differences were observed between the 5‐mg/kg versus 10‐mg/kg doses in other clinical outcomes, such as rates of colectomy, time to clinical response, or change in Lichtiger score. There were also no differences in the proportion of patients experiencing adverse events (17% in the 5‐mg/kg group versus 20% in the 10‐mg/kg group). In patients with a baseline albumin of less than 25 g/L, a Day 7 response occurred in nine (64%) of 14 patients in the 10‐mg/kg group versus 14 (45%) of 31 in the 5‐mg/kg group (RR 1.43, 95% CI 0.86–2.39, p = 0.17). In patients with a baseline albumin of 25 g/L or higher, the respective response rates were 18 (62%) of 29 versus 38 (67%) of 57 (RR 0.97, 0.85–1.10, p = 0.59). We observed a similar trend with CRP ≥ 50 versus < 50 mg/L.

The second component of PREDICT‐UC [1] was a randomized induction strategy study comparing intensified versus accelerated versus standard induction strategies. In the intensified induction strategy (IIS), initial responders received a further dose of 10 mg/kg at Week 1, while nonresponders received that dose as a stat salvage dose at the time of nonresponse between Days 3 and 7. In the accelerated induction strategy (AIS), initial responders received two further doses of 5 mg/kg at Weeks 1 and 3, while nonresponders received the second dose as a stat 10‐mg/kg salvage dose. In the standard induction strategy (SIS), initial responders received two further doses of 5 mg/kg in the next 6 weeks, while nonresponders received an extra stat salvage dose of 5 mg/kg. The proportion of patients achieving a clinical response at day 14 did not differ between the three strategies (74% in the IIS group, 73% in the AIS group, and 68% in the SIS group). Patients in the IIS and AIS groups achieved remission earlier than the SIS group but lost this advantage by Month 3. There were no differences in any of the secondary outcomes. Clostridioides difficile infection was more frequent in the AIS group (seven patients [15%]) versus the SIS group (one [2%]) or IIS group (none; p = 0.0050).

Finally, 78 of the original 138 patients entered the maintenance cohort study. Sixty (77%) patients received thiopurine monotherapy; 10 (13%) received infliximab and thiopurine combination, and eight (10%) received infliximab monotherapy. There were higher rates of flares in patients treated with thiopurine and infliximab monotherapy. An ulcerative colitis endoscopic index of severity (UCEIS) of 0–1 at Month 3 was significantly associated with endoscopic, steroid‐free remission and combined clinical and endoscopic remission at 12 months. On multivariable analysis, infliximab use for maintenance was significantly associated with clinical and endoscopic remission at 12 months.

The lack of a difference in the primary outcome in PREDICT‐UC may relate to infliximab overall being a highly potent induction agent in ASUC; therefore, detecting a significant difference in efficacy, particularly among the subgroup of patients with more severe disease, would require an unfeasibly larger sample size. High infliximab clearance has been identified as a significant factor associated with poor outcomes in ASUC [117]. However, it remains unknown whether high clearance reflects severe disease or whether it can be overcome by more intensive dosing. The fact that infliximab doses of 10 mg/kg in PREDICT‐UC resulted in numerically higher response rates in patients with albumin < 25 g/L and/or CRP ≥ 50 mg/L provides a signal that higher dosing may be able to counteract high clearance in some patients. Intensified and accelerated arms achieved remission earlier than the standard arm but lost this advantage by Month 3. This is most likely because the gap between the last infliximab induction dose to Month 3 was too large (8 to 11 weeks from last induction dose). This suggests that giving more infliximab prior to Month 3 may be beneficial.

Given the absence of a difference in the primary outcome of clinical response by Day 7 between a first dose of 5 and 10 mg/kg, an initial dose of 5 mg/kg should be adequate for most patients. In sicker patients, with albumin < 25 g/L or CRP ≥ 50 mg/L, a 10‐mg/kg dose may be beneficial. Intensified and accelerated strategies achieve earlier remission rates without higher adverse events compared to standard strategies, though outcomes are similar at 3 months; there may be an opportunity to consolidate outcomes by giving more infliximab prior to Month 3. Continued infliximab maintenance therapy after successful ASUC induction results in higher clinical and endoscopic remission rates at Month 12. Endoscopic assessment at Month 3 following ASUC can identify patients who may benefit from therapeutic optimization. Future work aligned with PREDICT‐UC will investigate biomarker and pharmacokinetic profiling to help inform a personalized approach to ASUC.

Transmural Healing as a Treatment Target in CD—Are We There yet? (Rose Vaughan)

Objective disease assessment is now acknowledged as the cornerstone to preventing progressive disease and disability in CD. Historically, clinical remission was the treatment target; however, it has become clear that treating to clinical remission does not alter the natural history of IBD [118]. This is because clinical symptoms correlate poorly with mucosal inflammation; indeed, ongoing inflammation is common and can be seen in over 50% of patients who achieve clinical remission [119]. Uncontrolled smoldering disease activity leads to both reduced quality of life and increased rates of disability [120]. Thus, current treatment goals have broadened. In the immediate and short‐term, symptomatic response and remission are targeted treatment goals, but these goals are insufficient in preventing disease progression in the longer term. Therefore, objective control of inflammation has emerged as an intermediate and long‐term treatment target, as most notably published in the STRIDE 2 guidelines [6].

Repeated endoscopic reassessment to objectively assess for mucosal healing is both expensive and poorly accepted by patients. As such, noninvasive techniques including IUS and MRI can be used to assess radiographic transmural healing. Radiographic assessment of healing is feasible even in the setting of stricturing disease limiting endoscopic assessment. Additionally, transmural healing has been associated with improved clinical outcomes. When compared to endoscopic mucosal healing, radiographic transmural healing may well reflect a deeper therapeutic target when treating CD. Transmural healing may portend a lower likelihood of relapse and adverse outcomes when compared to endoscopic mucosal healing [121, 122]. This makes IUS an attractive alternative to magnetic resonance enterography (MRE) to easily assess for sonographic transmural healing, as IUS is a noninvasive, low‐cost, easily repeatable, and well‐tolerated method that needs no preparation and can be performed as a point‐of‐care assessment to allow real‐time decision‐making.

We recently explored the clinical implications of achieving sonographic healing in patients who were in clinical remission with CD [123]. We retrospectively examined consecutive patients with CD who had a baseline IUS and evidence of Harvey‐Bradshaw Index ≤ 4 indicative of clinical remission, and more than 6‐month hospital follow‐up. Time from IUS to medication escalation, corticosteroid use, and disease‐related hospitalization or surgery was compared by the presence or absence of sonographic healing. Two hundred and two patients were included in the study, and 61% had evidence of sonographic inflammation at IUS. Over the follow‐up period (19 months; IQR 13–27), medication escalation occurred in approximately half of patients, corticosteroid use in one‐quarter, hospitalization in one‐fifth, and CD‐related surgery in just under one in seven patients. Poorer clinical outcomes, including an increased risk of medication escalation, corticosteroid use, hospitalization, and surgery, were demonstrated in those with evidence of sonographic inflammation. Sonographic healing was found to be significantly associated with an increased odds of medication‐escalation‐free survival and corticosteroid‐free survival, but not with hospitalization or surgery. Those who achieved transmural healing took on average 3 years before needing medication escalation compared to 1.1 years if they had evidence of sonographic inflammation at baseline IUS. This demonstrates the utility of stratifying patients in remission by sonographic healing status.

Other published results show we can expect rates of transmural healing to range from 15% to 40% [124]. In our study [123], 40% of patients achieved sonographic healing, leaving most patients in clinical remission with objective evidence of sonographic inflammation. If we raise the target to sonographic healing above that of biochemical or endoscopic remission, it is not yet established what the risks and benefits of treatment escalation will be. Therefore, despite the benefits of achieving transmural healing, current drug therapies fail to achieve this in a large proportion of patients. If transmural healing was the only target, there would be a risk of exhausting treatment options for patients who switch therapy and still fail to achieve this treatment target. It is clear more effective therapies are needed. However, we can feel confident that if transmural inflammation is seen, then simple strategies to optimize treatment should be taken. In those patients who achieve sonographic transmural healing, their improved prognosis can be acknowledged, and less stringent clinical surveillance or follow‐up may be considered.

In conclusion, IUS is a powerful tool to inform levels of disease activity in patients with CD. The presence of transmural healing confers favorable clinical outcomes, and we should adopt serial objective assessment in patients. Prospective studies incorporating sonographic transmural healing as endpoints are awaited, and IUS is well placed to be incorporated within the treat‐to‐target era [125].

Dilatation Ustekinumab in Crohn's Disease—The DUST Study (John Chetwood)

Symptomatic stricturing CD represents a challenging treatment cohort with a high proportion requiring surgery [126]. Endoscopic balloon dilation (EBD) has shown promise in surgery‐free management [127]. There are limited data evaluating the performance of different medications combined with EBD, particularly for newer agents such as ustekinumab. Here, we summarize a recent study, the DUST study [2], which assessed the safety and efficacy of EBD with immunomodulation for the management of stricturing CD.

The DUST study was a dual‐center retrospective cohort study of all adult patients who underwent CD stricture dilation between 2015 and 2023 at two centers in New South Wales, Australia. A total of 525 dilations were performed on 199 strictures in 94 patients. Strictures were subclassified according to concomitant use of a biological agent. The primary outcome was clinical success (1‐year of surgery‐free medication persistence without requirement for repeat EBD). Secondary outcomes included short‐term success (1‐year of surgery‐free medication persistence after index EBD), surgery‐free medication persistence, and adverse events. Propensity score matching was used to address baseline cohort imbalance.

Medical therapies being used (on intention‐to‐treat per luminal stricture) were immunomodulator monotherapy in 54/199 (27.1%), ustekinumab in 58/199 (29.1%), vedolizumab in 33/199 (16.6%) and anti‐TNF in 54/199 (27.1%) patients. Ustekinumab was associated with a greater long‐term and short‐term clinical success rate than anti‐TNF (both p = 0.03) and with greater long‐term clinical success than vedolizumab (p = 0.002). Independent predictors of medication failure were stricture length >4 cm, perianal disease phenotype, and use of a non‐ustekinumab agent. Using a propensity score matched model, ustekinumab was found to have superior long‐term clinical success and surgery‐free persistence than anti‐TNF (p = 0.026 and p = 0.021 respectively).

In conclusion, findings from the DUST study confirmed the safety and efficacy of EBD with immunomodulation for the management of stricturing CD. It demonstrated the efficacy and safety of using non anti‐TNF agents with EBD and that ustekinumab was associated with the highest clinical success and surgery‐free persistence rates.

Session 4: Over the Horizon—The Future in IBD

New Drugs and New Ways to Use Them (Vipul Jairath)

Many drugs used today for the treatment of IBD were not purposely developed for the condition. An example from the 1940s is Svartz's development of 5‐aminosalicylic acid (5‐ASA), initially used to treat rheumatoid arthritis and subsequently found to be active in ulcerative colitis [128]. In the 1950s, an adrenal hormone, initially known as “hormone G,” was isolated and later identified as a corticosteroid. It was subsequently studied in rheumatoid arthritis in an RCT, which was noteworthy for comparing against 5‐ASA rather than placebo. Truelove and Witts' seminal study of cortisone in UC soon followed [115]. In 1980, van Deventer et al. published a case report of the first use of a TNF antibody for CD in a 12‐year‐old female patient [129]. At the time, the TNF antibody was being developed for the treatment of sepsis. Drug discovery became a more intentional activity thereafter, and the development of newer therapies was purposeful drug discoveries.

In the context of IBD, three IL‐23p19 inhibitors are in the therapeutic landscape—mirikizumab, rizankizumab, and guselkumab—and the ability to differentiate between them will be important. The SEQUENCE study is a key trial that directly compares risankizumab to ustekinumab in patients with moderate‐to‐severe CD, particularly focusing on those who have failed TNF inhibitors [82]. Of note, the results demonstrated noninferiority of risankizumab for clinical remission at Week 26 (nominal p < 0.01, post hoc analysis for superiority) and superiority for endoscopic remission at Week 48 (p < 0.0001). The superiority of IL‐23p19 inhibitors over interleukin 12/23 (IL‐12/23) inhibitors is supported in other conditions, such as psoriasis, which share the same cytokine network [130]. Furthermore, the story continues to build with the VIVID study (mirikizumab versus ustekinumab in CD) [131] and the GALAXI 2 and 3 studies (guselkumab versus ustekinumab) [132]. This trend supports the use of an IL‐23 inhibitor as either a first‐line agent or in patients not responding to TNF inhibitors.

In a network meta‐analysis (NMA) of advanced first‐line therapy in luminal CD, rizankizumab ranked first for induction of clinical remission in all patients [133]. When data were analyzed separately, rizankizumab ranked first for both bionaïve and bioexposed patients. A separate NMA of CD trials for endoscopic outcomes suggested that JAK1 inhibitors and IL‐23p19 inhibitors were more effective among advanced therapies for induction of endoscopic outcomes [134]. Furthermore, a NMA in second‐line advanced therapy in luminal CD also ranked IL‐23p19 inhibitors first for induction of endoscopic remission [133]. Anecdotally, in Canada, IL‐23p19 inhibitors are being used as first‐line treatment for CD over ustekinumab, for patients who have failed TNF inhibitors, and for those with concomitant immune conditions. IL‐23p19 inhibitors are being used second line in UC. Looking further ahead, IL‐23 inhibitors could potentially be administered orally [135].

There is significant interest in the role tumor necrosis factor‐like cytokine 1A (TL1A) inhibition in IBD. TL1A is an upstream pro‐inflammatory cytokine expressed in antigen presenting cells that is upregulated in IBD [136] and binds to DR3 receptors found mainly in activated lymphocytes and innate lymphoid cells [137]. Three TL1A compounds are in development, including tulisokibart for UC [138]. Tulisokibart is a humanized monoclonal antibody that binds to TL1A and inhibits its interaction with DR3. Interestingly, a genetic‐based diagnostic test, using a machine‐learning approach, was developed as a companion test to help identify IBD patients more likely to respond to tulisokibart. Another aspect of interest is the research into next‐generation antibodies with increased potency and extended half‐lives, with potential dosing intervals of three times a year (NCT06448247).

The next treatment goal in IBD is to reach a therapeutic effectiveness of 80 to 90%, as is seen in dermatological conditions, and dual cytokine inhibition using advanced combination therapy is of interest [139]. While there may be benefits in refractory IBD patients and high‐risk–risk phenotypes, there are cautionary learnings from the rheumatoid arthritis field where advanced combination therapies led to more serious infections, more neutropenia, and injection site reactions and offered no added benefit [140].

The results from various combination studies will be of interest for the future: there are three studies looking at combination therapy of guselkumab and golimumab in IBD (VEGA [141]; DUET‐UC (NCT05242484); DUET‐CD (NCT05242471)), and the coinduction combination study of vedolizumab and upadacitinib (VICTRIVA (NCT06227910)), which looks at whether vedolizumab and upadacitinib given in combination reduce bowel inflammation and ulcers in the bowel compared to vedolizumab monotherapy in adults with moderately or severely active CD.

Other areas of development include orally delivered, protease‐resistant single‐domain antibodies (also known as VHHs (Variable domain of heavy chain of heavy‐chain antibody)) to deliver TNF inhibitors into the gut [142]; tyrosine kinase 2 (TYK2) compounds; antifibrotics for CD [143, 144]; and chimeric antigen receptor (CAR‐T) cell therapy, which could lead to a better understanding of disease biology and transformational efficacy [145].

In summary, looking to the future, by 2030 there may be an anti‐TL1A monoclonal antibody, with or without a predictive biomarker; approval of the first biologic combination therapy; the first oral α4β7 integrin agent; and possibly an oral TYK2 inhibitor, extended half‐life monoclonal antibodies, and an antifibrotic agent in Phase 3 trials.

Evolution of Surgical Resection in CD (David A. Clark)

Contemporary rates of surgical resection in CD have decreased over the last two decades as more therapeutic agents have become available [146]. Surgeons have endeavored to reduce recurrence rates after surgery with differing anastomotic configurations. Recently, a new antimesenteric, functional, end‐to‐end, handsewn ileocolic anastomosis (Kono‐S) [147] has shown a significant reduction in endoscopic recurrence and surgical recurrence rates in CD. The SuPREMe‐CD RCT [148] comparing the Kono‐S anastomosis with the stapled side‐to‐side anastomosis reported a significant reduction in postoperative endoscopic and surgical recurrence rates in patients who underwent Kono‐S anastomosis. Since the publication of the SuPREMe‐CD trial, many surgeons will have considered that there is sufficient evidence to implement Kono‐S into their practice [149]. It is important to recognize that the SuPREMe‐CD trial used the Rutgeerts score as a surrogate for clinical and surgical outcomes, and updated data are now available and were presented at the European Crohn's and Colitis Organization (ECCO) 2023 annual meeting [150]. Additionally, the interim results of the New York multicentre RCT, powered with a much greater sample size, raise questions regarding even these surrogate outcomes [151]. Furthermore, the SPICY trial [152] evaluating the extent of mesenteric resection has concluded, and these early results are interesting and found no effect of extended mesenteric resection on endoscopic recurrence. CD patients are a heterogenous cohort, and randomized studies are paramount to evaluate new surgical techniques in a reasoned, scientific, and methodical fashion [153].

The Current Landscape of Microbiome Testing and Microbial Manipulation Therapy (Craig Haifer)

In recent years, there has been a surge in interest and understanding of the profound influence the gut microbiome has on human health and disease. The microbiome's role extends beyond its traditional association with gastrointestinal conditions, encompassing significant implications for systemic diseases and influencing responses to therapies such as cancer immunotherapy. The identification of specific microbial signatures has shown promise in predicting patient outcomes, indicating a potential shift toward more personalized therapeutic strategies [154, 155]. However, integrating these findings into clinical practice remains challenging.

The proliferation of microbiome testing, largely marketed directly to consumers, further complicates the landscape. These tests claim to provide insights into gut health and often recommend tailored diets or supplements purportedly based on the results. For healthcare providers, this creates a paradox; while patients are increasingly interested in, and exposed to, such tests, there is insufficient data to justify modifying clinical care based on their results [156]. This disconnect highlights the urgent need for evidence‐based approaches to integrating microbiome testing into standard medical practice and treatment algorithms.

The field of fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) has historically been at the forefront of applying microbiome research to clinical treatment. However, progress in FMT research has plateaued over the past 3 years. This stagnation is largely attributed to the complex regulatory requirements, cost‐intensive nature, and logistical challenges of conducting high‐quality clinical trials in the field. Additionally, the variability in donor material has raised concerns regarding standardization and consistency in outcomes, limiting the scalability and broad adoption of traditional FMT. In response to these challenges, the focus has shifted toward developing next‐generation microbiome‐based therapies, which are often referred to as defined microbial consortia. These synthetic formulations are designed to be scalable, highly consistent, and tailored to elicit specific therapeutic effects without the dependency on donor stool. The commercial incentives associated with these therapies are driving rapid advancements, propelling developments that are now entering Phases 2 and 3 trials across the world [157]. However, this commercial nature also means that detailed information on these products and the results of any trials may not be disclosed until late‐stage development.

The evolution of microbiome‐based testing and therapies represents a significant paradigm shift within gastroenterology. While traditional FMT has laid the groundwork, the development of laboratory‐derived, donor‐independent microbiome products may redefine clinical approaches and improve patient outcomes. These advancements are poised to enhance the accessibility, consistency, and safety of microbiome‐based treatments. Future research must prioritize ways to incorporate both testing and treatment into existing clinical algorithms with the aim of enhancing not replacing traditional care models.

IBD Prevention, Prophylaxis and Cure in the Next Generation (Uma Mahadevan)

IBD is a chronic, relapsing inflammatory condition that primarily affects the gastrointestinal tract. Although the exact cause of IBD remains unknown, it is thought to result from a combination of genetic predisposition, environmental factors, and an aberrant immune response [158].

IBD has a strong genetic predisposition [159]. While this cannot be changed, early identification of those at high genetic risk can lead to proactive monitoring and early intervention. A healthy lifestyle may help reduce the risk of IBD. This can include a high‐fiber diet, avoidance of food additives and ultra‐processed foods, and avoidance of smoking [160, 161]. Exposure to certain environmental triggers such as pollution, infections, and antibiotic use in early childhood may play a role in IBD development [162]. Once a diagnosis is made, early aggressive therapy to achieve deep remission and even surgery may lead to long‐term prevention of flare and end‐organ damage. While there is no true “cure” for IBD at this time, the field is ripe for change with possibilities including stem cell therapy, variations of CAR‐T cells, and genetic‐based therapies.

Conclusion

This supplement contains a summary of presentations from the IBD 2024: Charting a New Course in IBD symposium. The steering committee thanks the presenters and authors for their contributions. Meetings such as this provide an invaluable opportunity for IBD clinicians to exchange professional knowledge.

A consistent message throughout the symposium was the importance of treating to a minimum of endoscopic remission, as this decreases the risk of future complications compared to clinical remission. However, Session 1 saw two debates on whether endoscopic remission is a sufficient treatment target in UC and CD, or whether deeper remission should be sought. Following both debates, the symposium audience polls showed that, in the current treatment landscape, most of the audience felt that in many cases endoscopic remission is not enough, and deeper healing should be sought. The polling result is unsurprising given the advances in the management and monitoring of IBD over the last decade, which have influenced treatment goals.

A range of pharmacotherapeutics used in the management of IBD was discussed during the second session. Given that the number of medical therapies available in the treatment of IBD has increased significantly over the last two decades, increasing consideration must be given to the choice of treatment, when to switch treatment, and when to de‐escalate treatment. The use of monoclonal antibodies, including anti‐interleukins, was discussed. While ustekinumab is an established anti‐IL‐23 antibody with proven efficacy for induction and maintenance in both CD and UC, emerging anti‐IL 23 p19 antibodies (guselkumab, mirikizumab, and risankizumab) have also shown efficacy in CD and UC, and early data suggest that they may be effective after ustekinumab failure in half of the patients with CD. The pros and cons of JAKi were also discussed in this session. JAKi exhibit certain benefits, including effectiveness in biologic‐naïve and biologic‐experienced patients, oral mode of administration, and rapid onset and washout periods. IBD treatment in pregnancy was discussed. The fine balance required in managing IBD disease activity with the safety of the fetus was discussed. While 5‐aminosalicylates and thiopurines are safe to continue during pregnancy, other IBD therapeutics should either be avoided or used with caution during the periconception and pregnancy periods. A multidisciplinary approach is needed to balance IBD disease activity with safety in pregnancy.

Session 3 saw presentations on key Australian IBD studies. The PREDICT‐UC study has demonstrated that infliximab is a safe and effective rescue therapy in ASUC and has provided critical information on optimal dosing, while the DUST study has confirmed the safety and efficacy of EBD with immunomodulation for the management of stricturing CD. The use of IUS as a tool to predict CD disease course was presented. IUS has been shown to be a powerful tool to assess transmural healing and thus prognosticate CD disease course.

The future of IBD management was discussed in Session 4. There are new drugs on the horizon for IBD management. Studies examining emerging IL‐23 inhibitors (guselkumab, mirikizumab, and risankizumab), TL1A inhibitors (tulisokibart), oral nanobodies, and antifibrotics. These emerging therapeutics have the potential to revolutionize the way UC and CD are managed in the near future. Other recent advancements have been in the area of microbiome testing and FMT therapy. There is likely to be an increase in patient access to FMT with the advent of laboratory‐derived, donor‐independent microbiome products. FMT therapy is poised to be an adjunct approach to aid traditional care models.
