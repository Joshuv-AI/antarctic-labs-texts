Protective effects of three natural extracts on ovalbumin-induced intestinal and skin damage in felines via the gut-skin axis: An in vitro study
Authors: Hengyan Wang, Tong Liu, Huasong Bai, Hongchen Jin, Zhanzhong Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe gut-skin axis plays a crucial role in mediating systemic immune and metabolic responses, particularly in allergic disorders. This study investigated the protective effects of three natural extracts—Heartleaf Houttuynia P.E. (HH), green tea polyphenols (GTP), and Ginkgo biloba extract (GBE)—against ovalbumin (OVA)-induced intestinal and cutaneous damage in feline cells via the gut-skin axis. Feline intestinal epithelial cells (FIECs) were exposed to OVA to establish an injury model, and their conditioned medium was transferred to feline skin cells (FSCs) to simulate inter-organ crosstalk. Among the tested compounds, all three extracts alleviated OVA-induced barrier dysfunction in both tissues, with HH exhibiting the strongest protective effects. HH significantly attenuated OVA-induced inflammation (IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α), oxidative stress (SOD, CAT, GSH, MDA), and disruption of barrier integrity (mucins, tight junction proteins, filaggrin, loricrin) in both FIECs and FSCs. Integrated metabolomic and transcriptomic analyses revealed that OVA disrupted core metabolic pathways—including pyrimidine metabolism, carbon metabolism, and quorum sensing—and activated inflammatory signaling networks such as TNF and IL-17. HH intervention effectively reversed these alterations by restoring folate biosynthesis, nicotinate metabolism, glycerophospholipid metabolism, and nucleocytoplasmic transport, while suppressing pro-inflammatory lipid mediators and bacterial quorum signals. Notably, HH modulated gut-skin communication by reprogramming both metabolic and transcriptional landscapes, thereby promoting cellular repair and homeostasis. These findings demonstrate that HH exerts multi-target protective effects on the feline gut-skin axis, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic agent for managing allergic dermatitis associated with intestinal hypersensitivity.Keywords: Gut-skin axis, Ovalbumin, Gut-skin barrier, Natural extracts, Oxidative stress, Multi-omics

Introduction

The gut-skin axis, a bidirectional communication network linking the gastrointestinal tract and the skin, has emerged as a critical regulator of systemic immune homeostasis and inflammatory responses [1]. This axis operates through multiple interconnected pathways, including immune cell trafficking [2], microbial metabolite signaling [3,4], neuroendocrine regulation, and metabolic crosstalk [5]. Accumulating evidence suggests that disruption of intestinal barrier integrity can lead to systemic inflammation and contribute to the pathogenesis of various skin disorders, such as atopic dermatitis, acne vulgaris, and psoriasis [6]. In both humans and animals, increased intestinal permeability (“leaky gut”) allows luminal antigens, bacterial lipopolysaccharides, and pro-inflammatory cytokines to enter systemic circulation, thereby triggering or exacerbating cutaneous inflammation [7].

Food allergens, such as ovalbumin (OVA), are well-known triggers of type I hypersensitivity reactions [8]. They can compromise intestinal epithelial barrier function by downregulating tight junction proteins (e.g., occludin, ZO-1) and mucins (e.g., MUC-2) [9,10], leading to an altered intestinal permeability [11,12]. OVA-induced intestinal inflammation activates dendritic cells and promotes T helper 2 (Th2) polarization, resulting in elevated levels of IL-4, IL-5, and IL-13—cytokines closely associated with allergic skin manifestations [[13], [14], [15]]. Notably, studies have demonstrated that intestinal exposure to allergens can induce distant skin inflammation via the gut-skin axis, even in the absence of direct cutaneous allergen contact [16]. This phenomenon highlights the importance of improving intestinal health for the management of allergic dermatoses.

Natural plant extracts have gained increasing attention due to their potential to modulate gut barrier function and systemic immunity. Heartleaf Houttuynia P.E. (HH), a traditional medicinal herb rich in flavonoids and volatile oils, has been shown to possess potent anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and antiviral properties [17,18]. Its active constituents, such as quercetin and hyperoside, inhibit NF-κB and MAPK signaling pathways, thereby suppressing the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines including TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β [19,20]. Moreover, HH has been reported to ameliorate colitis in murine models by enhancing tight junction expression and modulating gut microbiota composition [21]. Additionally, other natural extracts such as green tea polyphenols (GTP) and Ginkgo biloba extract (GBE) have also been shown to exhibit anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and free radical-scavenging activities [22,23]. However, whether these extracts can protect against allergen-induced skin damage via the gut-skin axis remains unexplored, particularly in companion animals such as felines.

Feline allergic dermatitis is a common clinical condition frequently linked to food hypersensitivity, with ovalbumin being one of the most prevalent dietary allergens [24]. Felines suffering from intestinal hypersensitivity often present with concurrent skin lesions, suggesting a possible gut-skin connection [25]. Despite this, mechanistic studies on the gut-skin axis in feline models are scarce, and effective interventions targeting this pathway remain lacking. Therefore, this study aims to investigate the protective effects of three natural extracts (HH, GTP, and GBE) against OVA-induced intestinal and skin damage using an in vitro feline gut-skin co-culture model [26]. Furthermore, we employ integrated metabolomic and transcriptomic analyses to explore the underlying mechanisms, with a particular focus on how HH intervention modulates metabolic and transcriptional networks to restore barrier integrity and suppress inflammation across the gut-skin axis.

Materials and methods

Detailed experimental protocols are available in the Supplementary Materials. The key procedures are summarized below. To ensure the chemical authenticity of the three natural extracts, each was subjected to ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography coupled with quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrometry (UHPLC-Q-TOF-MS) analysis.

Cell culture

Feline intestinal epithelial cells (FIECs, PETCC 189) and feline skin cells (FSCs, PETCC 2822) were obtained from The Global Pets' Cell Resource Center (PETCC, Wuhu, China). Both cell lines are immortalized cell lines derived from feline tissues. FIECs are an epithelial-like immortalized cell line isolated from feline small intestinal tissue, whereas FSCs are a fibroblast-like immortalized cell line derived from feline skin tissue. Both cell lines were cultured in M3-DMEM modified medium supplemented with 12% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere and were used at passages 5–10 for all experiments. OVA was used as the allergen. The natural extracts HH, GTP, and GBE were obtained from commercial suppliers.

Determination of non-cytotoxic concentrations and OVA injury concentrations

Cell viability was assessed using the CCK-8 assay as detailed in the Supplementary Materials. FIECs were treated with a series of concentrations of HH (1–320 μg/mL), GTP (1–320 μg/mL), or GBE (1–320 μg/mL) for 24 h. The non-cytotoxic concentrations were defined as those that did not significantly reduce cell viability compared with control. To determine the optimal OVA concentration for establishing an injury model, FIECs were exposed to OVA at 0.5, 1, 2, or 4 mg/mL for 24 h, and oxidative stress markers (superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT), glutathione (GSH)) were measured.

Experimental design and conditioned medium model

We established an in vitro gut-skin axis model using a conditioned medium (CM) approach. FIECs were treated for 24 h with one of the following: culture medium (Control), OVA (2 mg/mL), or OVA co-treated with a non-cytotoxic concentration of HH (40 μg/mL), GTP (1 μg/mL), or GBE (5 μg/mL). The CM from these FIECs was then collected and applied to FSCs for a further 24 h.

Assessment of inflammatory and barrier phenotypes

Following treatments, levels of key mediators were quantified in cell supernatants or lysates using commercial ELISA kits. Analyzed markers included: (i) pro-inflammatory cytokines: IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α; (ii) oxidative stress markers: SOD, CAT, GSH, malondialdehyde (MDA); (iii) intestinal barrier proteins: mucins (MUC-1, MUC-2, MUC-3) and tight junction proteins (Occludin, ZO-1, Claudin); (iv) skin barrier proteins: filaggrin (FLG) and loricrin (LOR).

Multi-omics profiling

Untargeted metabolomic profiling was performed on cell extracts using ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography coupled to high-resolution mass spectrometry (UHPLC-HRMS). For transcriptomics, total RNA was sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. Bioinformatic analyses included differential expression/abundance analysis and enrichment analyses of gene ontology (GO) terms and KEGG pathways.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed with four independent replicates. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were conducted using Origin 2019 software (OriginLab, USA). One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied after verifying normality (Shapiro-Wilk test) and homogeneity of variances (Levene's test). When variances were equal, standard ANOVA followed by Tukey's post-hoc test was used; when variances were unequal, Welch's ANOVA followed by Games-Howell post-hoc test was used. A p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

HH, GTP, and GBE mitigate OVA-induced damage across the gut-skin axis

According to the HPLC-MS results show that: The main components identified in HH are baicalin, protocatechuic acid, and quercitrin (Supplementary Fig. S1, Supplementary Table S1). The main components of GTP are epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG), epicatechin gallate (ECG) and epigallocatechin (EGC) (Supplementary Fig. S2 Supplementary Table S2). The main flavonoids identified in GBE are puerarin, daidzin, vitexin, rutin, etc (Supplementary Fig. S3, Supplementary Table S3).

Based on the results (Supplementary Fig. S4A), HH at 40 μg/mL, GTP at 1 μg/mL, and GBE at 5 μg/mL were selected for all subsequent experiments because they maintained cell viability above 90%. The lowest concentration that caused significant decreases in SOD, CAT, GSH markers was 2 mg/mL (Supplementary Fig. S4B–D), and this concentration was used for the OVA model group. Cytotoxicity of OVA at this concentration was also tested (Supplementary Fig. S4E–F), confirming no major reduction in cell viability. OVA challenge in FIECs significantly increased the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α) and induced oxidative stress, as indicated by elevated MDA and decreased SOD, CAT, and GSH levels. This OVA-conditioned medium (CM) triggered a similar inflammatory and oxidative stress response in FSCs. Co-treatment with HH, GTP, or GBE significantly attenuated these alterations in both cell types, with HH consistently showing significantly superior the restorative effect (Fig. 1A–D).

HH preserves barrier integrity in intestinal and skin cells

OVA exposure downregulated the expression of key intestinal barrier components (mucins MUC-1/-2/-3 and tight junction proteins Occludin, ZO-1, Claudin) in FIECs. The CM from OVA-injured FIECs similarly reduced the levels of these proteins, as well as the skin barrier proteins FLG and LOR, in FSCs. Treatment with the natural extracts, particularly HH, significantly counteracted these decreases and preserved barrier protein expression in both FIECs and FSCs (Fig. 2A–E).

Multi-omics profiling reveals HH reverses OVA-induced metabolic and transcriptional dysregulation

Metabolomic alterations in FIECs

In FIECs, principal component analysis (PCA) of metabolomic data showed clear separation among the Control, OVA, and HH groups (Fig. 3A). Venn diagram analysis identified differential metabolites (DMs) specifically regulated by HH intervention (Fig. 3B). The majority of metabolites were classified as organic acids and derivatives or lipids (Fig. 3C), and the number of DMs in each comparison is summarized (Fig. 3D). OVA challenge significantly altered metabolite levels (Fig. 3I), with KEGG enrichment analysis identifying “Pyrimidine metabolism” and “Carbon metabolism” as top disrupted pathways (Fig. 3J). HH intervention significantly reduced the pro-inflammatory lipid LysoPS(18:1(9Z)/0:0) (log2FC = −0.8), the folate intermediate 7,8-dihydroneopterin (log2FC = −0.8), the glycerophospholipids PC(14:0/18:1(9Z)) and PE(14:0/18:1(11Z)) (both log2FC = −0.8), and the tryptophan metabolite N′-formylkynurenine (log2FC = −0.8). These metabolic alterations were effectively reversed by HH (Fig. 3K), enriching pathways including “folate biosynthesis” and “glycerophospholipid metabolism” (Fig. 3L).

Metabolomic alterations in FSCs

PCA of FSC metabolomes also demonstrated distinct clustering among groups (Fig. 3E). HH specifically modulated a subset of metabolites (Fig. 3F), which were predominantly lipids and organic acids (Fig. 3G). The counts of DMs are shown in Fig. 3H. OVA-conditioned medium from FIECs induced widespread metabolic changes in FSCs (Fig. 3M), affecting pathways such as “Quorum sensing” and “Lysine biosynthesis” (Fig. 3N). HH intervention reversed a distinct set of metabolites in FSCs (Fig. 3O), primarily enriching “Nicotinate and nicotinamide metabolism” and “Glycerophospholipid metabolism” (Fig. 3P). HH treatment elevated several metabolites in FSCs. Notably, imidazoleacetic acid ribotide (log2FC = +1.8), an anti-inflammatory histidine metabolite; nicotinic acid mono nucleotide (log2FC = +0.1), a NAD+ precursor; PS(14:0/18:1(12Z)-O(9S,10R)) (log2FC = +0.1), a membrane phospholipid; and N-tetradecanoyl-l-homoserine lactone (log2FC = +0.1), a bacterial quorum-sensing molecule, were all increased by HH.

Transcriptomic alterations in FIECs

Transcriptomic PCA in FIECs revealed clear group separation (Fig. 4A). HH treatment reversed a substantial number of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) induced by OVA (Fig. 4B), as visualized in a cluster heatmap (Fig. 4C). The distribution of DEGs across comparisons is detailed in Fig. 4D. GO enrichment of OVA-induced DEGs showed involvement in immune and inflammatory processes (Fig. 4I). KEGG pathway analysis further confirmed enrichment of inflammatory pathways, including “TNF signaling” and “IL-17 signaling” (Fig. 4J). In contrast, DEGs reversed by HH were enriched for GO terms related to intracellular transport and cellular structure (Fig. 4K) and KEGG pathways such as “Nucleocytoplasmic transport” and “Homologous recombination” (Fig. 4L). HH downregulated genes involved in homologous recombination (ATM, BRCA2, BLM, RAD50, BRIP1) and nucleocytoplasmic transport (IPO9, TNPO1, KPNA3, NUP205, XPO7, CSE1L). These pathways are critical for DNA repair and nuclear trafficking – processes impaired under oxidative stress. Their downregulation by HH suggests reduced OVA-induced genotoxic stress and may facilitate nuclear import of anti-inflammatory transcription factors.

Transcriptomic alterations in FSCs

PCA of FSC transcriptomes confirmed distinct profiles (Fig. 4E). A summary of DEG numbers is presented in Fig. 4F, and a cluster heatmap shows the reversal of gene expression by HH (Fig. 4G). The overlap of DEGs is shown in Fig. 4H. OVA-induced DEGs in FSCs were enriched for GO terms associated with signal transduction and kinase activity (Fig. 4M) and KEGG pathways related to cancer and inflammation, such as “TNF signaling pathway” and “FoxO signaling pathway” (Fig. 4N). DEGs reversed by HH were enriched for GO terms involving metabolic processes and cytoskeleton (Fig. 4O) and KEGG pathways including “PI3K-Akt signaling” and immune regulation (Fig. 4P). HH downregulated multiple inflammation-associated genes, including IKBKB, FOS, MAP3K8, NFKBIA, CSF1, CSF2, CSF3, SOD2, TGFB1. These genes are involved in TNF, MAPK, PI3K-Akt, Toll-like receptor, and cytokine-cytokine receptor signaling. Their downregulation supports HH-mediated suppression of NF-κB and MAPK cascades and alleviation of oxidative stress in skin cells, correlating with restored skin barrier proteins (FLG, LOR).

Discussion

Our results demonstrate that all three natural extracts, especially HH, protect against OVA-induced barrier damage in feline intestinal and skin cells. HH pretreatment effectively alleviated the pathological cascade triggered by OVA: intestinal damage released pro-inflammatory and metabolically dysregulated mediators into the conditioned medium, which subsequently induced oxidative stress, inflammation, and barrier dysfunction in distant skin cells. This highlights HH's systemic protective potential via modulation of the gut-skin axis.

The observed increase in IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α secretion in OVA-stimulated FIECs aligns with known activation of NF-κB/MAPK pathways by food allergens [27,28]. Applying this conditioned medium to FSCs induced a hallmark allergic skin inflammation profile, characterized by elevated pro-inflammatory cytokines and reduced levels of the barrier proteins FLG and LOR [29,30], thereby supporting the clinical link between gut inflammation and remote skin barrier damage [31,32]. HH treatment effectively mitigated these OVA-induced inflammatory and oxidative stress responses in both cell types, corroborating the established anti-inflammatory and antioxidant profile of HH and its flavonoids [33]. Of the three extracts tested, HH demonstrated superior efficacy in restoring viability and barrier function. This may be due to its more diverse phytochemical composition (e.g., polysaccharides, volatile oils, alkaloids), which could confer enhanced immunomodulatory and cytoprotective capacity compared to GTP and GBE [34,35].

Multi-omics integration revealed that HH's protection involves a systemic rectification of metabolism. OVA challenge in FIECs induced dysregulation in pyrimidine, carbon, and methane metabolism, pathways crucial for energy and redox balance [36], potentially explaining the observed oxidative stress. HH counteracted this by restoring folate and purine metabolism, essential for DNA repair and regeneration, as supported by the upregulation of 7,8-dihydroneopterin [37,38]. Concurrently, HH regulated glycerophospholipid metabolism, lowering pro-inflammatory lipids like LysoPS to preserve membrane integrity and curb inflammation. These intestinal metabolic shifts were systemically transmitted to FSCs, where OVA-altered pathways (lysine/histidine metabolism, quorum sensing) pointed to amino acid deficiency and disrupted bacterial communication. The scarcity of l-lysine and l-glutamine likely undercut the substrate supply for synthesizing FLG and LOR. HH intervention normalized these changes, notably reducing the quorum-sensing molecule N-tetradecanoyl-l-homoserine lactone, indicating a capacity to improve skin health indirectly by modulating gut-derived microbial signals.

Transcriptomic data further corroborates the systemic mechanism of action. In FIECs, OVA activated immune-related pathways such as TNF signaling, IL-17 signaling, and neutrophil extracellular trap (NET) formation—hallmarks of Th17-driven inflammation [39,40]. In contrast, HH shifted the transcriptional profile toward cellular repair processes, including homologous recombination, nucleocytoplasmic transport, and microtubule-based movement. This suggests that HH not only suppresses inflammation but also actively promotes tissue resilience and homeostasis. In FSCs, OVA triggered pathways associated with cancer and cellular senescence (e.g., FoxO and Hepatitis B signaling), which are typically upregulated under chronic oxidative stress [41]. HH intervention reversed these alterations, enriching pathways related to methylation and organic acid biosynthesis, indicative of metabolic reprogramming toward anabolic and detoxification processes. In summary, KEGG pathway analysis revealed a clear dual mechanism: OVA elicits localized intestinal inflammation and systemic stress in distal skin, whereas HH counteracts this via a dual-pronged strategy—fortifying cellular repair processes in the gut, with the restored intestinal homeostasis subsequently translating into normalized immune signaling and enhanced cell survival in the skin. This integrated perspective strongly supports that HH alleviates OVA-induced damage by concurrently targeting local intestinal barrier integrity and systemic communication along the gut–skin axis.

Integrated metabolomic and transcriptomic analyses suggest that HH preserves membrane integrity and suppresses inflammation through a dual mechanism: directly modulating glycerophospholipid metabolism to limit membrane damage and LysoPS-driven signaling, while concurrently repressing the transcriptional programs of NF-κB and MAPK that amplify the inflammatory response. In OVA-injured FIECs, HH intervention significantly reduced the levels of PC (14:0/18:1(9Z)), PE (14:0/18:1(11Z)), and the pro-inflammatory lysophospholipid LysoPS(18:1(9Z)/0:0). The concomitant decrease in both parent glycerophospholipids and their lyso-derivative suggests that HH may suppress PLA2-mediated hydrolysis or enhance reacylation via the Lands cycle (i.e., lysophospholipid acyltransferase activity), thereby limiting membrane phospholipid degradation and preserving bilayer integrity. This interpretation is supported by recent studies identifying the Lands cycle as a central regulator of phospholipid remodeling, oxidative stress responses, and inflammation [42]. Importantly, LysoPS itself is a bioactive lipid mediator that promotes mast cell degranulation, macrophage cytokine production, and T-cell activation [43]. The reduction of LysoPS by HH directly correlates with the observed suppression of IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α, providing a direct mechanistic link between glycerophospholipid metabolism and inflammation resolution. At the transcriptional level, HH downregulated key inflammatory signaling genes in FSCs, including IKBKB (an NF-κB activator), FOS and MAP3K8 (components of the MAPK cascade), NFKBIA, and the colony-stimulating factors CSF1/2/3. These changes indicate that HH not only remodels the lipid landscape but also suppresses NF-κB and MAPK pathways, further dampening pro-inflammatory cytokine production.

Despite the novel findings presented in this study, several limitations should be acknowledged, which also provide important directions for future research. First, the in vitro conditioned medium model lacks immune cells, circulating factors, and neural/hormonal regulation, which may affect the magnitude and dynamics of the observed inflammatory responses (e.g., absence of immune cell amplification could underestimate cytokine levels). Second, the single time-point design (24 h) does not capture the dynamic evolution of the gut-skin axis response. Third, microbiome analysis was not performed despite the known role of the gut microbiota in modulating the gut-skin axis. To address these limitations, future studies should employ live animal models (e.g., OVA-sensitized cats or murine atopic dermatitis models) for validation with endpoints including histology and serum cytokine profiling, conduct time-course experiments to distinguish early from late events, and profile the feline gut and skin microbiomes using 16S rRNA or metagenomic sequencing before and after HH intervention. Despite these limitations, our results provide compelling evidence that HH protects the gut-skin axis by concurrently targeting inflammation, oxidative stress, metabolic dysregulation, and barrier dysfunction. In summary, the present findings should be interpreted as preliminary proof of concept obtained from an in vitro model. Any conclusion regarding the therapeutic potential of HH must be considered tentative pending rigorous in vivo validation. We are currently planning such studies in OVA-sensitized feline models to translate these promising in vitro observations into a clinically relevant context.

Conclusion

This in vitro study demonstrates that OVA-induced damage in feline intestinal epithelial cells can trigger inflammatory and barrier dysfunction in distal skin cells via a conditioned medium-mediated gut-skin crosstalk, highlighting the systemic impact of intestinal hypersensitivity. Among the three natural extracts tested (HH, GTP, and GBE), HH exhibited the protective effects against OVA-induced injury in both intestinal and skin cells. Integrated metabolomic and transcriptomic analyses revealed that HH alleviates inflammation, oxidative stress, and barrier disruption. These findings, suggest that HH is a promising multi-target candidate for the dietary management of food sensitivity-associated allergic dermatitis in felines. Further in vivo studies are required to validate these effects in clinical animal models.
