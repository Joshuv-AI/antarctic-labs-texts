Perioperative anesthetic management in pediatric patients with medically refractory pulmonary arterial hypertension undergoing surgical or transcatheter potts shunt: a retrospective case series
Authors: Liping Sun, Ning Ma, Xiafeng Yu, Lijun Fu, Yanjun Sun, Hao Zhang, Jijian Zheng, Jie Bai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH), a life-threatening disorder in children, necessitates palliative interventions like Potts shunt (surgical/transcatheter) for medically refractory cases. Perioperative anesthesia management remains challenging due to hemodynamic instability. This study describes our institutional anesthetic approach and summarizes the perioperative outcomes of 28 pediatric patients who underwent Potts shunt creation.MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted on 28 consecutive children who underwent either surgical or transcatheter Potts shunt at Shanghai Children’s Medical Center between 2021 and 2024. Perioperative data were extracted from structured electronic medical records.ResultsAmong the 28 patients, 18 underwent surgical Potts shunt creation (100% requiring cardiopulmonary bypass [CPB], median CPB duration: 91[82–108] minutes) and 10 underwent transcatheter procedures (20% requiring CPB). Anesthesia induction predominantly utilized ciprofol-alfentanil (60.7%) and s-ketamine (28.6%). Preoperative treprostinil was universal, with 7.1% of patients requiring additional vasoactive agents. Post-shunt hemodynamic support included norepinephrine (92.9%), dopamine (28.6%) and dobutamine (28.6%). Compared with the transcatheter group, surgical patients experienced significantly longer stays in the cardiac intensive care unit (CICU), (8[7–14] vs. 5[4–9] days, p = 0.005), longer durations of mechanical ventilation (2.5[1.5–10.5] vs. 1.8[1.0-4.3] days), and longer total hospitalizations (31[23–36] vs. 25.5[19–46] days). No intraoperative cardiac arrests occurred. The in-hospital mortality rate was 14.3% (4/28).ConclusionsBoth surgical and transcatheter reverse Potts shunt procedures represent feasible salvage therapies for children with refractory pulmonary hypertension, albeit with considerable perioperative risks. Targeted hemodynamic regulation and rigorous intraoperative emergency management are therefore essential to maintain circulatory stability, prevent pulmonary hypertensive crises, and ensure overall clinical safety.

Introduction

In China, the spectrum of pediatric pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH) predominantly includes idiopathic pulmonary arterial hypertension and congenital heart disease-associated pulmonary arterial hypertension [1]. Although targeted pharmacological therapies may provide symptomatic relief in pulmonary hypertension, surgical intervention remains an important option when progressive elevation of pulmonary artery pressure persists despite optimal medical management. Historically, atrial septostomy has served as a palliative intervention to decompress the right ventricle by creating a right-to-left intracardiac shunt [2]. However, this approach carries significant limitations, including potential tricuspid valve dysfunction and systemic hypoxemia secondary to atrial-level shunting, which may adversely affect cerebral and myocardial oxygenation [2].

Lung transplantation constitutes a further therapeutic alternative for end-stage PAH, yet its clinical utility in pediatric patients is constrained by major challenges. Beyond the inherent operative risks and substantial economic costs, a critical paucity of suitable donor organs often precipitates fatal outcomes during extended waiting periods [3].

Originally designed as a shunt connecting the descending aorta to the left pulmonary artery for infants with cyanotic heart disease, the Potts shunt was repurposed in 2004 when Blanc et al. pioneered the reverse Potts shunt for pulmonary hypertension (PH) [4]. This technique anastomoses the left pulmonary artery to the descending aorta, decompressing the pulmonary arterial system while preserving right ventricular function. Compared with atrial septostomy, the reverse Potts shunt offers superior physiological advantages by confining hypoxemia to the descending aortic territory, thereby preserving cerebral and coronary oxygenation [5]. Clinical validation led to its inclusion in the 2016 pediatric PH management guidelines. Emerging evidence shows comparable three-year survival rates to lung transplantation (76% in both cohorts) but significantly lower perioperative complication rates (35% vs. 81%) [6, 7].

Reverse Potts shunt can be performed via transcatheter or surgical approach. At our institution, the first pediatric surgical reverse Potts shunt in China was performed in 2021, and the first pediatric transcatheter procedure in 2023. This complex intervention carries exceptional anesthetic challenges, including PH crises, intraoperative cardiac arrest, and procedure-related mortality [8]. This article systematically reviews the perioperative anesthetic management of 28 pediatric cases undergoing reverse Potts shunt for refractory severe PH at our center, focusing on protocol optimization and complication mitigation.

Materials and methods

Patients

This retrospective observational study followed the STROBE reporting guideline. Consecutive pediatric patients diagnosed with PAH who underwent reverse Potts shunt procedure at our center between September 2021 and December 2024 were enrolled. All enrolled patients had received preoperative triple-targeted medical therapy but continued to show progressive PAH symptoms. Patients with incomplete perioperative data were excluded. Medical records were manually reviewed to extract relevant data. Preoperative data included patient demographics, World Health Organization (WHO) functional class (FC), cardiac function grading, and preoperative medication regimens. Intraoperative variables comprised medication administration, vital sign monitoring, duration of surgery and anesthesia, post-shunt upper-to-lower limb arterial partial pressure of oxygen (PaO2) gradient and need for extracorporeal support. Postoperative outcomes included immediate vasoactive medication use, duration of postoperative ventilation, reintubation requirement, cardiac intensive care unit (CICU) length of stay (LOS), and total hospital length of stay. Follow‑up ranged from 1 month to 3 years. Because this study focused on perioperative anesthetic outcomes, follow‑up radiologic data were not analyzed. No data were missing for the main perioperative variables.

Anesthesia management

All patients received standardized preoperative preparation, including 6‑hour fasting for solids and 2‑hour fasting for clear fluids. Oral midazolam (0.5 mg/kg) was administered 15–20 min before transfer to the operating room. Concurrently, ward‑based treatments continued, including oxygen therapy, vasoactive medications, and treprostinil. Upon arrival in the operating room, standard monitoring was established, comprising continuous electrocardiography (ECG), noninvasive blood pressure (NIBP), and pulse oximetry (SpO2) (Fig. 1). For surgical patients, external defibrillator pads were placed preoperatively, with emergency vasoactive agents and a defibrillator kept on standby.

Anesthesia induction began with 100% oxygen inhalation, followed by sequential intravenous administration of ciprofol (0.1–0.2 mg/kg), alfentanil (10–20 µg/kg), and/or s‑ketamine (0.5–1.0 mg/kg). After placement of an invasive arterial line in the upper extremity for pre‑ductal blood pressure monitoring, tracheal intubation was performed under deep anesthesia maintained with additional intravenous agents. Anesthesia was maintained using a combined intravenous‑inhalational approach: continuous infusion of ciprofol (0.5–1.0 mg/kg/h), sufentanil (1.0–2.0 µg/kg/h), and rocuronium (0.5–1 mg/kg/h), together with titrated sevoflurane inhalation.

Mechanical ventilation was delivered using pressure-controlled ventilation with volume guarantee (PCV-VG) mode with the following parameters: FiO2 50%-100%, tidal volume 6–8 ml/kg, positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) 4 cmH2O, and end-tidal CO2 (ETCO2) maintained at 30–35 mmHg. Following anesthesia induction, comprehensive monitoring was optimized. This included additional invasive arterial blood pressure monitoring via lower extremity arterial access for post-ductal measurement, left-sided central venous catheterization for continuous central venous pressure (CVP) monitoring, real-time bispectral index (BIS) monitoring and continuous regional cerebral oxygen saturation (rScO2) surveillance (Fig. 1). Postoperatively, a Swan-Ganz pulmonary artery catheter was placed through the right internal jugular vein to enable comprehensive hemodynamic monitoring and goal-directed therapy.

Selection criteria and surgical procedures of reverse potts shunt

Based on our single-center experience, the therapeutic strategy was individualized according to patient age, body weight, anatomical characteristics, cardiac function and perioperative risk. Patients aged ≥ 10 years, weighing ≥ 30 kg, with a distance between the descending aorta and left pulmonary artery < 10 mm, were preferentially scheduled for transcatheter reverse Potts shunt. Conversely, patients with younger age, lower body weight, unfavorable anatomy or severely impaired right ventricular function (i.e., high risk of pulmonary hypertensive crisis during interventional manipulation) underwent surgical reverse Potts shunt.

Method of surgical reverse Potts shunt

Under general anesthesia, extracorporeal circulation was established through intubation of the right internal carotid artery and jugular vein. Surgical repair was then performed through either a left fourth intercostal thoracotomy or median sternotomy, to create the connection between the left pulmonary artery and the descending aorta. The anastomosis of the pulmonary artery was located at the proximal end of the left pulmonary artery, and the anastomosis of the descending aorta was positioned at the closest distance to the pulmonary artery anastomosis. A GORE-TEX graft was placed between the left pulmonary artery and the descending aorta, with the size selected to have a cross-sectional area to descending aorta area ratio of 0.4 to 0.5, serving as a restrictive shunt. All patients were transported to the CICU for planned close postoperative monitoring.

Method of transcatheter reverse Potts shunt

Under general anesthesia, preemptive anatomical dissection of the right internal jugular vessels was performed to establish a potential emergency cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) access route. Femoral arterial and venous access was established via percutaneous sheath placement. After identifying the closest anatomical approximation between the descending aorta and the left pulmonary artery, excimer laser-assisted radiofrequency perforation was used to create an intervascular communication, followed by deployment of a size-matched covered stent. Stent dimensions were calibrated to achieve a cross-sectional area occupying 40%-50% of the descending aortic lumen at the implantation site. All patients were systematically transferred to the CICU postoperatively for comprehensive hemodynamic monitoring as protocol.

Data analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median with interquartile range (25%-75%, IQR). Between-group comparisons were performed using independent-samples Student’s t-test for normally distributed data and the Mann–Whitney U test for non-normally distributed data. Given the small sample size inherent to this rare surgical cohort, all intergroup comparisons were considered exploratory rather than confirmatory, accordingly, any reported p-values should be interpreted as descriptive statistics rather than definitive inferential evidence. All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics 23 (SPSS, IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY) and MedCalc Version 12.1.4.0 (MedCalc Software, Mariakerke, Belgium) software. A p-value < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

In the transcatheter reverse Potts cohort, one case required CPB support and another required ECMO during the intervention. After Potts shunt creation, the median PaO2 gradient between the upper and lower extremities was 50.3mmHg (13.6–147.2). Similar to the surgical cohort, all transcatheter patients required combined inotropic and vasopressor therapy intraoperatively, with continued hemodynamic support maintained curing transfer to the CICU. Intraoperative pharmacologic support consisted of norepinephrine (100%, 10/10), dopamine (30%, 3/10), dobutamine (10%, 1/10), and epinephrine (10%, 1/10). The median VIS was 15 (5.0–25.0).

In the surgical group, the median packed red blood cell transfusion volume was 150 ml (50–200). Additional plasma, platelets, cryoprecipitate and fibrinogen were administered as clinically indicated. Transfusion requirements were minimal in the transcatheter group, with no red blood cell transfusion needed in most cases. All patients received unfractionated heparin with activated clotting time (ACT) monitoring. Heparin was partially reversed with protamine sulfate after shunt creation in the surgical group, whereas no routine reversal was performed in the transcatheter group unless clinically indicated.

No intraoperative cardiac arrest occurred. ECMO support was required in five cases (four in the surgical cohort and one in the transcatheter cohort). In the surgical reverse Potts group, two patients required ECMO due to failure to wean from CPB caused by persistent cardiac dysfunction. Two additional surgical cases required emergent ECMO initiation in the CICU. One transcatheter patient required urgent ECMO support for an intraoperative acute pulmonary hypertensive crisis. A statistically significant intergroup difference was observed in CICU length of stay (median 8.0 days [7.0-13.5] in the surgical cohort vs. 5.0 days [4.0-9.3] in the transcatheter cohort, p = 0.005). Mechanical ventilation duration was 2.5 days [1.5–10.5] in the surgical group vs. 1.8 days [1.0-4.3] in the transcatheter group. Total hospitalization duration was 31.0 days [23.3–36.0] vs. 25.5 days [19.3–45.8], respectively. The overall mortality rate was 14.3% (4/28), including three deaths among ECMO-supported patients. Notably, all four deaths occurred in the surgical group, with no perioperative mortality in the transcatheter group. Overall successful recovery and discharge were achieved in 85.7% (24/28) of cases.

Discussion

Since the early 21st century, the Potts shunt has become established as an effective surgical intervention for pulmonary arterial hypertension [9–11]. This technique offers dual clinical benefits by preserving tricuspid valve function and preventing hypoxic complications in vital organs, particularly the heart and brain. However, reverse Potts shunt procedures carry substantial perioperative risks. In high-risk children, major cardiovascular adverse events occur in 15%-25% of cases, with perioperative mortality raging from 10% to 20%. Severe complications include pulmonary hypertensive crisis, cardiac arrest, acute heart failure, and hypoxic-ischemic brain injury [12]. Intraoperative severe hypoxemia occurs in up to 72% of patients and refractory hypotension in approximately 35% [2]. Up to 40% of critically ill children require perioperative extracorporeal support, with near 100% mortality in this subgroup [7]. Notably, current evidence regarding anesthesia management for reverse Potts shunt surgeries remains critically limited, which significantly complicates perioperative decision-making and risk mitigation strategies.

Our institution performed China’s first pediatric surgical reverse Potts shunt and first pediatric transcatheter Potts shunt procedures [13–15]. Through systematic clinical implementation and protocol refinement, we developed a specialized anesthesia management framework for these complex interventions. This protocol was associated with favorable perioperative outcomes and maintained hemodynamic stability in pediatric PAH patients undergoing reverse Potts shunts.

Our center has managed 28 pediatric cases (18 surgical, 10 transcatheter). Current stent-graft technology limits transcatheter interventions to older children with a descending aorta-left pulmonary artery distance < 10 mm. This anatomical constraint introduces an inherent selection bias, resulting in inevitable intergroup differences in baseline age, height, and weight. Consequently, comparisons of CICU stay, mechanical ventilation duration, and total hospital LOS are confounded by these unbalanced baseline characteristics and varying disease severity. Given the absence of statistical adjustment for confounding covariates in our single-center cohort, no definitive conclusions can be drawn regarding the superiority of either strategy. Intergroup differences in clinical outcomes should therefore be interpreted with caution, as they may primarily reflect differential patient selection rather than inherent differences in treatment efficacy. In our decision algorithm, surgical reverse Potts shunt remains preferred for patients with severe cardiac dysfunction, offering the critical advantage of rapid transition to ECMO support if postoperative hemodynamic instability occurs.

Anesthetic induction for reverse Potts procedures is a critical phase requiring meticulous hemodynamic control. Hemodynamic instability during induction may precipitate pulmonary hypertensive crises, which can be life-threatening in pediatric patients. Overly profound anesthesia risks hypotension and myocardial depression, whereas inadequate depth exacerbates pulmonary vascular resistance via sympathetic activation during airway manipulation [16, 17]. Our anesthesia protocol was designed to support right ventricular function in patients with PAH while avoiding adverse changes in systemic and pulmonary vascular resistance.

Notably, no intraoperative cardiac arrests occurred in our Potts shunt cohort. This favorable safety profile may be attributed to protocolized hemodynamic optimization strategy comprising: (1) preprocedural oral midazolam administration (20–30 min pre-induction) for anxiolysis and improved vascular access; (2) pharmacologically graded induction under continuous invasive arterial pressure monitoring, maintaining systolic blood pressure within 10% of baseline; and (3) anesthetic selection guided by pulmonary vasoreactivity, systematically avoiding agents known to elevate pulmonary vascular resistance [18]. Although propofol has complex and partly opposing effects on pulmonary vascular tone, enhancing hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction to preserve ventilation‑perfusion matching [19] while also causing direct vasodilation that may reduce pulmonary vascular resistance under certain conditions [20, 21], it was not used in this cohort due to the fragile right ventricular function and hemodynamic instability of PAH patients. Ketamine provides stable systemic hemodynamic support, but its perioperative application requires careful clinical weighing [22]. Recent evidence confirms that under controlled ventilation and normocapnia, ketamine does not cause clinically relevant elevation in pulmonary vascular resistance [22]. Earlier reports of unfavorable pulmonary hemodynamic responses were largely confounded by uncorrected hypercapnia and enhanced sympathetic tone. Nevertheless, ketamine’s inherent sympathomimetic effects may lead to tachycardia and increased myocardial oxygen demand, which are undesirable in patients with limited cardiac reserve and vulnerable right ventricular function [23]. Accordingly, ketamine was excluded from our anesthetic regimen. Appropriate opioid dosing effectively blunts stress-induced increases in pulmonary resistance without significant hemodynamic impairment. Ventilatory management demands equal vigilance: continuous ETCO2 monitoring during pre-intubation mask ventilation prevents both hypoxemia and hypercapnia-induced pulmonary resistance elevation, which is particularly important in patients with reduced pulmonary compliance [24]. To reduce the risk of pulmonary hypertensive crises secondary to inadequate anesthetic depth during patient transport, our protocol mandates continuous hemodynamic monitoring and strict maintenance of therapeutic anesthesia levels throughout the peri-transport period.

Both surgical and transcatheter reverse Potts shunt procedures carry significant anesthesia-related risks, requiring continuous intensive hemodynamic monitoring to promptly address procedure-induced circulatory fluctuations. Emergency preparedness with defibrillators and inotropic agents is essential throughout the intervention. Our multidisciplinary protocol mandates preoperative preparation of the right internal jugular vessels in transcatheter cases for rapid ECMO initiation. This strategy helped rescue two transcatheter patients who experienced intraoperative pulmonary hypertensive crises.

Post-shunt hemodynamic stability is closely related to shunt direction and flow characteristics. Excessive flow reduces total pulmonary blood flow while increasing systemic circulation, exacerbating right ventricular strain and predisposing to systemic hypoxemia, a key factor associated with to early mortality [25]. Conversely, insufficient flow fails to adequately decompress the right ventricle, potentially increasing long-term mortality risks. This underscores the importance of precise shunt sizing, as illustrated by a case in our surgical cohort that required intraoperative shunt diameter reduction from 7 mm to 5 mm during CICU management. Postprocedural cardiac support includes low-dose dopamine or epinephrine for inotropic support; norepinephrine as first-line vasopressor for PAH patients to maintain systemic vascular resistance while enhancing right ventricular contractility; proactive prevention and prompt treatment of hypotension to preserve coronary perfusion pressure [26].

Given the limitations of transesophageal echocardiography in shunt assessment, we employ intraoperative upper to lower limb SaO2 gradient monitoring. A gradient of 15–20% may suggest optimal shunt function, although anesthesia-specific targets require further investigation [2]. Pediatric cases receiving high FiO2 may show minimal upper to lower limb SaO2 gradient, yet significant PaO2 discrepancies may indicate bidirectional shunting. A decrease in SpO2 accompanied by an exaggerated upper to lower limb oxygen saturation gradient may suggest excessive pulmonary to systemic shunting through the Potts shunt, requiring right ventricular optimization via respiratory adjustment, titration of pulmonary vasodilators, and balanced vasoactive support.

All patients underwent right jugular cannulation for potential ECMO. However, one surgical case developed postoperative cerebral infarction, which may be related to an incomplete circle of Willis and right hemispheric hypoperfusion. Our refined neuroprotection protocol now includes preoperative MRI evaluation of circle of Willis integrity, right carotid clamping test before cannulation and continuous bilateral rScO2 monitoring.

This single‑center, retrospective, non‑randomized study has several limitations. Individualized procedural selection based on baseline characteristics and anatomical conditions inevitably introduced selection bias. The limited sample size and imbalanced baseline profiles precluded direct efficacy comparison between the two approaches. Only short-term in-hospital outcomes were analyzed, with no medium- or long-term follow-up. Despite these limitations, the single-center experience summarized here may provide practical references for individualized management of such critically ill children.

Conclusion

For children with severe and refractory pulmonary hypertension, both surgical and transcatheter reverse Potts shunt are viable salvage strategies. However, both approaches carry inherent procedural hazards and early mortality risks. The significantly shorter CICU LOS in the transcatheter group is largely attributable to milder baseline disease enabling percutaneous treatment and reduced operative trauma. Strict patient selection and standardized perioperative care are therefore essential. Intraoperative regulation of systemic vascular resistance and optimization of myocardial contractility can effectively stabilize hemodynamic fluctuations after shunt creation, which is critical for preventing pulmonary hypertensive crisis. Furthermore, adequate emergency protocols and timely management of unexpected events are indispensable to ensure anesthetic and perioperative safety in this high-risk pediatric population.
