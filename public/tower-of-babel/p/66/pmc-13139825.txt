Serial maternal filicide as evidence of Munchausen syndrome by proxy
Authors: Ahlem Mtiraoui, Oussama Ajmi, Asma Allaya, Amel Braham, Jaafar Nakhli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMunchausen Syndrome by Proxy (MSBP), or Fabricated or Induced Illness (FII), is a form of physical and emotional child abuse and maltreatment that remains frequently under‐detected due to a significant lack of clinical awareness. This case report aims to highlight the challenges of diagnosing MSBP, its impact on victims, and the ethical implications for healthcare professionals. A 37‐year‐old woman charged with filicide of two of her offspring and deliberate intoxication of her remaining 3‐year‐old boy was diagnosed with MSBP. Despite the tragic outcome for two siblings, the timely intervention saved the life of the third child. This case adds to the existing literature related to MSBP. It illustrates the difficulties posed by and the perpetrator's ability to deceive healthcare providers. It emphasizes the need for a sound understanding of victims’ and perpetrators' features to facilitate early evocation of the condition and thus prompt the adoption of adequate measures. To conclude, MSBP is a complex issue with significant diagnostic and ethical challenges. Early suspicion, careful evaluation, and a multidisciplinary approach are crucial for the protection of children and the appropriate management of perpetrators.

INTRODUCTION

Filicide—the homicide of a child by their parent—is an uncommon but tragic incident [1]. Both parents may be involved in the child murder. According to UNICEF, while mothers are more frequently involved in infanticide, filicide is mostly committed by fathers [2].

Several hypotheses regarding the driving forces of filicide have been advanced, and various categorizations based mainly on underlying motivations behind filicide, as well as sources of impulse, were proposed [3, 4]. With regard to maternal filicide, previous research has demonstrated that the majority of filicidal mothers were classified as severely mentally ill [3, 5]. Available data suggest that depression is the most common mental illness among filicidal mothers, while other relevant mental conditions related to child murder include psychosis and personality disorders [6]. As a matter of fact, up to 20% of women may develop mood disorders during the postpartum period [7]. While depression is often accompanied by invasive thoughts of fear of hurting the baby, severe psychotic depression is more prone to lead to suicide and infanticide [8]. Recent literature refines these data by highlighting a high prevalence of personality disorders among perpetrators; high levels of neuroticism and introversion, which compromise adaptive mechanisms, have been suggested as key vulnerability factors, with Cluster B traits or diagnoses being particularly prevalent in these cases [9, 10].

Munchausen Syndrome by Proxy (MSBP), also termed Fabricated or Induced Illness (FII), is a factitious disorder imposed on another, recently reclassified under somatic symptom and related disorders [11]. It involves a perpetrator, typically a primary caregiver, who knowingly fabricates symptoms or inflicts injury on a victim to satisfy underlying psychological needs [12]. Methods include fabricating medical history, falsifying clinical data, or intentionally inducing signs of illness, often by interfering with or withholding necessary treatments [13]. In the literature, the reported mortality rate associated with MSBP or FII ranges from 6% to 10% [13]. However, recent systematic reviews suggest that these figures may significantly underestimate the true prevalence of the disorder, as many cases remain undetected or are misclassified as unexplained sudden infant deaths [14]. While the caregiver may not initially intend to cause death, the high rate of clinical deception and repetitive harm leads to severe and often fatal outcomes.

A consistent but puzzling conclusion emerges from the literature review. In more than 90% of MSBP cases described, the perpetrator is a woman, typically the mother [15].

We here report the case of a 37‐year‐old woman diagnosed with MSBP, who was charged with filicide of two of her offspring and deliberate intoxication of her remaining 3‐year‐old boy.

CASE REPORT

Mrs. Z.K. was charged with the filicide of two of her offspring and the deliberate intoxication of her remaining 3‐year‐old boy and placed under arrest. She was referred to our psychiatry department for forensic psychiatric evaluation for criminal responsibility.

The following clinical history details the events leading up to her arrest and the subsequent evaluation.

Mrs. Z.K. had accompanied her 3‐year‐old boy, M., who had no previous pathological history, to the pediatric emergency department for recurrent epileptic seizures. Since status epilepticus was suspected, the boy was admitted right away to the pediatric intensive care unit. He had no fever and his vitals were normal. Blood samples and the lumbar puncture were negative. The cerebral scan was clear. He was put on an anticonvulsant treatment. Since no other epileptic convulsions occurred, he was discharged from the intensive care unit 4 days later and placed in the pediatric ward for further supervision. Once there, the epileptic seizures started to recur with no obvious reason. In fact, he had no associated symptoms and the serum level of his medication was correct. Doctors resorted to more and more comprehensive investigations. They opted for therapeutic escalation but the child continued to have seizures even though he was on multiple anticonvulsants. During the hospital stay, the nursing staff had observed a very caring and attentive mother, who never left her son's side. She was deeply concerned for her son, particularly given that she had lost two children earlier that year: A., 12 years old, who had epilepsy, and B., 9 years old, who died in the same circumstances. Her children had been admitted to the same pediatric unit where the staff exhibited great compassion towards her. She was very pleasant with the medical staff, but as the convulsions recurred, fearing for her boy's life, she had become more and more aggressive. The father, on the other hand, was rarely around and very distant from his wife.

By this time, some white residue on the child's juice bottle caught the attention of the nurse on call. The child was separated from his mother. He had no further seizures and medication was stopped gradually. Meanwhile, the toxicology test of the juice sample was positive for organophosphates. When presented with the evidence, the mother confessed to poisoning her son and her two other deceased offspring. She said she loved them and never intended to harm them but failed to provide a rationale for these actions.

To better understand the subject's background, her personal and social history were reviewed. Mrs. Z.K. was a 30‐year‐old stay‐at‐home mother. She had not finished primary school. She was married for 13 years to a casual laborer who was never around. She had a congenital solitary kidney and had been hospitalized several times for urinary infections. She had no history of psychiatric disorders.

The forensic psychiatric examination, soon after her arrest, showed no evidence of any major psychiatric illnesses or substance abuse disorders. There was no clinical evidence of a pathological personality or disruptive behavior. The retrospective analysis of her history did not identify any objective signs of intellectual disability. Mrs. Z.K. had normal developmental milestones and no academic failure despite leaving school early. She did not present any lasting difficulties in social adaptation, was autonomous, and had a developed relational network. During the clinical interview, Z.K. demonstrated a congruent affect and a euthymic mood. Her speech was coherent with no evidence of delusional activity. She demonstrated a rich vocabulary, understood complex ideas, and displayed sound judgment and reasoning. No history of physical, sexual, or emotional child abuse was reported. With regard to the crimes, she reported unsurmountable conflicts with her in‐laws and a perpetually absent and resigned husband.

To gain deeper insights, an additional projective assessment, using the Rorschach and TAT, was conducted to investigate the underlying personality structure [16]. Tests revealed great hesitation and a defensive attitude with an emotional reactivity adapted to the situation of the examination with no psychotic perceptual distortion or persecution. The tests ruled out a psychotic or borderline personality structure but did not allow further clarification of her psychic structure.

Thus, no psychiatric pathology possibly altering discernment was found. Considering the clinical presentation, the repetitive nature of the induced illnesses, and the clear intent to deceive the medical staff, MSBP was considered the most probable diagnosis. Mrs. Z.K. was held responsible for the filicide she had committed.

DISCUSSION

This complex case contributes to the existing literature on MSBP by highlighting the significant clinical challenges involved in its identification and the tragic consequences of diagnostic delays. Furthermore, it invites a deeper reflection on the status of MSBP as a formal diagnosis, as well as its complex ethical and legal implications within the forensic framework.

In this case, the victim presented recurrent inexplicable seizures that were unusual in their response to treatment and their evolution. The recurrence of symptoms when the child was no longer separated from his mother alerted pediatricians.

As previously highlighted, the low reported prevalence of MSBP (0.002%–0.27%) often results in a lack of consideration for it as a differential diagnosis when a child presents persistent, unexplained symptoms [13]. Here, despite the presence of similar cases in the family and the unusual course of symptoms, the default assumption of the mother's truthfulness and reliability contributed to the diagnosis wavering. As remarked here, the mother is habitually friendly, attentive, supportive and observant, often described as the “perfect mother” [16]. Indeed, caregivers perpetrating MSBP are adept at deceiving healthcare providers, often presenting as concerned and attentive parents. This can make it challenging to suspect abuse, particularly when there is no overt evidence of harm. As Meadow observed, “MSBP diagnoses are frequently made in hindsight, when a child's condition improves dramatically after separation from the caregiver” [17].

Nevertheless, common characteristics of MSBP have been described in the literature. A sound understanding of these features may facilitate early recognition of the condition and thus prompt the adoption of adequate measures.

In our case, Mrs. Z.K. poisoned her three children, resulting in the tragic death of the two older siblings earlier that year. Recurrence of the MSBP stands unfortunately to be a frequent situation. As Yates et al. asserted, 54.4% of perpetrators exhibited the same behavior [13]. Rosenberg et al. who examined the siblings of his 117 victims found 10 deaths that occurred in unusual circumstances [18]. Sheridan et al. discovered that among 451 victims with 210 siblings, 25% were known to be dead and 61.3% had similar symptoms or suspicious ones [19]. Trejo‐Hernández et al., for their part, identified 60% of the same pattern with other siblings [20]. Thus, the presence of similar symptoms in siblings should be alarming and should prompt physicians to consider the diagnosis of MSBP.

Regarding the victim profile, equal victimization rates have been reported across male and female victims [19]. Since MSBP primarily concerns youngsters, data concur that children under 5 years old may be at the greatest risk [21]. Filicide, especially of the offspring under the age of 5 years, is mostly committed by mothers [22]. Even though it is very uncommon to encounter a victim over 6 years of age, children up to 16 years old can also be affected as long as they depend on another for care [15]. In such cases, fathers are more often incriminated [23, 24]. In these older victims, the clinical presentation differs significantly from that of younger children. While infants and toddlers are typically passive recipients of harm, unable to comprehend or communicate the source of their distress, older children often evolve into a form of pathological collusion between the parent and the child [25]. To preserve the bond with a mother often perceived as the sole protector, the child may validate maternal falsehoods or even actively participate in symptom frication [25]. This situation transforms the child into an unreliable informant as they may continue to deceive clinicians even during solitary interviews, either out of a desperate need to protect the mother, “who may appear to be the perfect mother even to the victimized children themselves” or due to a genuine, distorted conviction that they are truly ill [25].

It is noteworthy that the eldest child of Mrs. Z.K. was diagnosed with epilepsy. The presence of a pre‐existing medical condition is a well‐recognized vulnerability factor, as genuine organic diseases associated to MSBP have frequently been reported [26, 27, 28]. In this context, the child's actual epilepsy may have served as a credible foundation for the mother to escalate or fabricate additional symptoms, further complicating the diagnosis process and the prognosis. Alternatively, it is equally possible that the epilepsy itself was entirely fabricated, with the mother inducing seizures to secure a chronic medical status for her children.

As reported in this case, the diagnostic of MSBP is less likely to be suspected when the mother, particularly the birth‐mother, is concerned. The psychiatric evaluation in this case showed no evidence of any major psychiatric illness, pathological personality or intellectual disability. Indeed, studies focusing on the psychiatric profile of the perpetrator reveal that mothers with MSBP might not exhibit an additional psychiatric condition [16]. Associated psychiatric disorders such as depression or personality disorder have been inconsistently reported [18]. Yet, authors agree: the perpetrator often suffers from Munchhausen syndrome (MS) himself, or at least some features of MS with reported or manifested illnesses of uncertain origin or course [19, 20, 29]. Although this diagnosis has not been definitively established in our case, it remains highly probable given the perpetrator's medical history, which includes a congenital solitary kidney diagnosed in childhood, resulting in recurrent healthcare‐seeking behavior.

In the literature, assumptions have been made about the life circumstances that might lead to the perpetration of MBP. It has been assumed that, from a psychodynamic perspective, the motivation behind a parent inducing illness in their child often stems from unresolved childhood trauma, such as neglect or abandonment [19, 20, 29, 30]. These individuals may seek to rectify these early deficits by establishing a positive and idealized relationship with medical professionals.

A history of physical or emotional abuse in a partner's relationship has also been described [19, 20, 29]. The non‐perpetrating caregiver is often, as portrayed here, emotionally absent, often presenting with marital problems [15]. Even though mothers may differ greatly in some aspects, one common feature seems to be loneliness and isolation. The extended family can also exert significant influence, exacerbating feelings of inadequacy [30]. In many cases, the illness becomes a central focus, elevating the mother's status within the family and providing a temporary respite from responsibilities and familial pressures. By assuming the role of the devoted caregiver, these mothers often experience a newfound sense of purpose and validation, temporarily alleviating the challenges faced within their family dynamics.

By convention, the assumed primary motive in MSBP is the attention or other perquisites that come from the relationship with the sick child [31]. By being the caregiver of a sick child, the perpetrator could be seeking help for themselves in some cases [31]. They might also be using the child to gain access to a caregiving milieu, to evade parental responsibilities, or to elude reality [32]. In any case, child homicide in MSBP may be a direct consequence of the perpetrator's unrecognized and untreated mental condition. Despite the absence of a characterized psychiatric disorder, these mothers often exhibit impaired emotion regulation, marked impulsivity, poor coping strategies, and an inappropriate expression of anger that turns into destructive and aggressive behavior under heavy psychological tension [33].

As previously proposed, another significant barrier to the diagnosis and management of MSBP is illustrated by the fragmentation of care and the multitude of medical and social service providers. This lack of coordination results in the loss of crucial information needed to establish an accurate diagnosis. Different professionals involved may have divergent views of the situation, making it difficult to develop a coherent and appropriate care plan. Delays in the transmission of information between different actors can prolong decision‐making and allow the situation to worsen [34].

Beyond the diagnostic challenges faced by healthcare professionals, MSBP raises numerous delicate ethical questions. Practitioners often feel ill‐equipped to deal with the complexities of MSBP [34]. On the one hand, there is a duty to protect the child, which is at the heart of medical practice. However, accusing a parent of such a serious act as child abuse carries significant risks, including the possibility of destroying a family. Healthcare professionals must therefore navigate between the duty of beneficence towards the child and the principle of parental autonomy. Moreover, the evidence of MSBP is often indirect and difficult to establish, which can lead to diagnostic errors and devastating consequences for the families involved. This uncertainty adds a supplementary dimension to the ethical complexity of these situations. Ethical dilemmas go beyond diagnosis and continue as professionals must decide on the appropriate course of action since these decisions can have long‐term consequences for the life of the child and family. Separating the child from his parents may be necessary to protect them, but it can also have negative psychological effects on him. The fear of the heavy consequences of this type of allegation makes professionals hesitate to denounce mothers until the occurrence of other events that would confirm their suspicions, which worsens the prognosis.

With regard to the foundation of MSBP diagnosis, there is substantial disagreement within the literature. While some authors consider Munchausen Syndrome and Factitious Disorder—by Proxy as diagnoses per se, others state that these disorders do not constitute “a diagnosis in a traditional sense but an observational description with implications regarding cause” [35]. It has even been suggested that the term MSBP be abandoned and replaced by “pediatric condition falsification” or “fabricated illness by carers” [34]. These latter terms refer to a genuine assault on a child, representing deliberate maltreatment and abuse. ‘MSBP is not a diagnosis, but a form of abuse. It is not something someone has, but what someone does’ as affirmed in forensic literature [36]. From this standpoint, the focus is clearly on proving the harm to the victim.

In our legal system as is the case in most countries, child abuse that is strongly suspected by a healthcare professional must immediately be communicated to the competent authorities independently of any other opposing measures. Article 31 of Tunisia's Child Protection Code, enacted in January 1996, mandates that any individual, including professionals bound by professional secrecy, must report any act that could endanger a child's health, on their physical or moral integrity to the Child Protection Delegate. Thus, in the Tunisian context, the fear of legal ramifications does not constitute a significant impediment to reporting. Rather, the primary hindrances are diagnostic challenges and ethical considerations [37]. Once the perpetrator is brought to justice, child withdrawal measures would ensure the protection of the young suspected to be an MSBP victim as well as their siblings. Apart from the mother, the victim can hypothetically start to recover. In the literature, legal proceedings in case of MSBP are almost consensual. Fundamentally there is no difference between MSBP and any other form of child maltreatment [38]. Yet there are scarce cases where the court has ruled out the accusations because no “malicious motive” was shown [39].

Actually, the duration of child placement and the therapeutic strategies to adopt seem to be less consensual. Surprisingly, the issue of therapeutic management of MSBP is poorly discussed in the scientific literature [35]. Does the separation of the child from the perpetrator mother have only positive aspects? Is the mother, having served her sentence, not likely to reoffend if no psychological interventions are undertaken? Only a few studies have examined the effectiveness of psychotherapy in reducing psychological damage, family problems, and the incidence of recurring abuse [40]. Despite the fact that proposing a generalizable treatment intervention is illusory given the great variability of the clinical presentations, perpetrator profiles, and family dynamics, psychological intervention should be focused mainly upon the parent–child relationship and its place in the dynamics of the family [41, 42]. In addition to the intervention dedicated to the child, comprehensive management must also address the perpetrator's psychopathology, often through a combination of individual psychotherapy and systemic family therapy. However, for any therapeutic process, the temporary separation of the child from the caregiver is indispensable to ensure the victim's safety while allowing the caregiver to engage in psychological work. Without such an approach, the risk of relapse remains alarmingly high.

From our point of view, actions might also be taken toward the perpetrator, including either alone and/or combined with systemic family therapy. The temporary separation of the child from the caregiver would be beneficial during the therapy.

CONCLUSION

In our case, M. and his deceased siblings were victims of MSBP. The diagnosis was made during hospitalization as the mother was caught poisoning him. M. was saved, but the diagnosis was made too late, and both his siblings could not be spared.

The unfamiliarity of healthcare professionals with this syndrome, coupled with the absence of clear and validated diagnostic protocols, can lead to misinterpretation and significant diagnostic delays in cases of MSBP. The complex relationships between the child, the family, and healthcare professionals, coupled with social and cultural pressures, can make it difficult for doctors to doubt parental accounts. Furthermore, the fragmentation of care and the lack of coordination between the different actors involved can hinder information sharing and prevent a comprehensive assessment of the child's situation.

Healthcare professionals should always consider MSBP whenever there is an atypical presentation of the symptoms, non‐responsiveness to conventional treatment or similar cases among siblings, particularly when dealing with a mother with medical knowledge, even if her affectionate demeanor may mislead them.
