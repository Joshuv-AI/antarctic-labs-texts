Use of ultrasound-guided percutaneous perineural corticosteroid injections for refractory cervical pain secondary to vertebral venous system abnormality in a dog
Authors: Alyssa J Carrillo, Federico R Vilaplana Grosso, Gabriel A Garcia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractA 2-year-old neutered male Greyhound was presented for evaluation of chronic, progressive right forelimb lameness and cervical pain. On presentation, the dog was ambulatory with mild to moderate right thoracic limb lameness and pain on cervical palpation with a right thoracic limb nerve root signature. Cervical magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) identified moderate right-sided C6 internal venous plexus enlargement. Medical management and physiotherapy did not result in clinical improvement. Repeat cervical MRI identified right-sided C5-T1 internal vertebral venous plexus enlargement with associated radiculopathy and possible dynamic spinal cord compression. Three ultrasound-guided perineural glucocorticoid injections (methylprednisolone acetate, 1 mg/kg per site) were performed at right C5-C6, C6-C7, and C7-T1 with improvement noted in lameness and cervical discomfort. No adverse effects were associated with the injections. This case highlights use of ultrasound-guided perineural glucocorticoid injections as a potential option for improving refractory cervical pain secondary to vertebral venous system abnormalities in a dog.

Introduction

The canine vertebral venous system is a collateral system of vessels composed of 3 intercommunicating networks including the external vertebral venous plexus, internal vertebral venous plexus, and basivertebral veins.1,2 This system communicates directly with the cranial venous sinus and bypasses the caval system representing an alternative route for blood return from the body.2,3 The intervertebral veins are often paired and exit the intervertebral foramen with the spinal nerve.2 The internal vertebral venous plexus extends along the vertebral canal floor and is most prominent in the cervical region in dogs whereas the external vertebral venous plexus encompasses the vertebral column.1,2,4

Enlargement of the vertebral venous system components can cause direct spinal cord compression, nerve impingement, or congestive spinal cord injury.3 Vertebral venous system abnormalities are well documented in humans and considered a rare but clinically important source of radiculopathy and myelopathy.5–12 Vertebral venous system abnormalities, secondary pathologic effects, and treatment options are rarely reported in the veterinary medical literature.3,13,14

Ultrasound-guided paravertebral perineural corticosteroid injections have been evaluated previously for the management of lateralized intervertebral disc extrusions with improvement or resolution in clinical signs documented.15,16 Use of ultrasound-guided perineural corticosteroid injections has not been described previously for management of refractory cervical pain secondary to vertebral venous system abnormalities.

Case description

A 2-year-old neutered male Greyhound was referred to the University of Florida Small Animal Teaching Hospital (UFSATH) for evaluation of chronic, progressive right forelimb lameness and cervical pain. Four months before presentation, the dog developed persistent right forelimb lameness and was started on gabapentin (18.7 mg/kg PO q12h), carprofen (2.3 mg/kg PO q12h), amantadine (3.1 mg/kg PO q24h), and tramadol (6.2 mg/kg PO q24h). Two months before presentation, the dog was evaluated by a board-certified veterinary surgeon, at which time cervical pain was noted on palpation and referral to a veterinary neurologist was recommended. Upon presentation to UFSATH, the dog was ambulatory with mild to moderate right thoracic limb lameness and pain on cervical palpation. Additionally, a right thoracic limb nerve root signature and muscle atrophy were identified.

Cervical magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) identified multifocal vertebral venous system abnormalities with moderate right-sided C6 internal venous plexus enlargement (Figure 1). Medical management adjustments including addition of pregabalin (3 mg/kg PO q12h), increased amantadine frequency (3.1 mg/kg PO q8-12h), and discontinuation of gabapentin, carprofen, and tramadol were made. Additionally, physiotherapy was recommended. The dog underwent 5 sessions of routine physiotherapy with static serial examinations and ultimately physiotherapy was discontinued given the lack of clinically relevant improvement.

Extending from C5 to T1, there is a mild to moderate enlargement of the right internal vertebral venous plexus (white arrow) compared to the left, most notable at the level of C6-C7. At T1-T2, the internal vertebral venous plexuses are symmetric and normal.

The dog’s discomfort continued to progress at home despite medication adjustments. Approximately 906 days after previous evaluation with UFSATH, the dog was reevaluated by the neurology service. On neurologic examination, the dog was ambulatory with moderate right thoracic limb lameness and painful cervical range of motion towards the right. Additionally, the right thoracic limb nerve root signature persisted, and the dog vocalized when attempting to rise from lying to standing. A repeat cervical MRI identified progressive right-sided C5-T1 moderate internal vertebral venous plexus enlargement with associated radiculopathy and possible dynamic spinal cord compression (Figure 2). Given lack of response to previous medical management and physiotherapy, ultrasound-guided paravertebral perineural glucocorticoid (methylprednisolone acetate 1 mg/kg per site) injections were performed at right C5-C6, C6-C7, and C7-T1 while the dog was under general anesthesia after MRI. The dog recovered uneventfully from general anesthesia, and no complications related to the procedure were observed. After the injections, activity restriction for 3 days was recommended.

Extending from C5 to T1, there is a mild to moderate enlargement of the right internal vertebral venous plexus (white arrow) compared to the left.

The dog was presented to UFSATH 4 weeks later for reevaluation and a second set of perineural glucocorticoid injections. The owner reported improvement since previous evaluation. On neurologic examination, right thoracic limb lameness and cervical range of motion were improved as compared with the previous evaluation with static intermittent right thoracic limb nerve root signature and vocalization when being assisted to rise. The dog was sedated with acepromazine (3.2 μg/kg IV) and butorphanol (0.3 mg/kg IV) and the procedure was repeated as described previously.

The dog was presented to UFSATH 6 weeks later for reevaluation and a third set of perineural glucocorticoid injections. The owner reported continued improvement since previous evaluation, but noted reoccurrence of mild discomfort 5 weeks after the previous injection. Neurologic examination confirmed continued improvement. Specifically, cervical range of motion yielded no discomfort, right thoracic limb lameness continued to improve, and the dog did not vocalize when rising. However, intermittent right thoracic limb nerve root signature persisted. The dog was sedated with acepromazine (3 μg/kg IV) and methadone (0.2 mg/kg total; 0.2 mL SC and 0.4 mL IV) and procedure was repeated as described previously. The dog recovered uneventfully from sedation. One month after the last injection, the owner reported that the dog continued to do well. Additional injections were recommended as needed.

Discussion

This case report describes a potential therapeutic pain management option for refractory cervical pain secondary to internal vertebral venous plexus enlargement and associated radiculopathy in a greyhound. Vertebral venous system abnormalities are a rarely reported clinically relevant manifestation of cervical pain in sighthounds.3 In our case, internal vertebral venous plexus enlargement was progressive and noted unilaterally from right C5 through T1. This region aligns with a previous study documenting the most common region for vertebral venous plexus abnormalities as being C4 through T1 in sighthounds.3 Similarly, this previous study documented most abnormalities unilaterally on the right side. However, a definitive cause for the asymmetrical distribution between right and left was not identified.3 The progressive nature of internal vertebral venous plexus enlargement identified in our case has not been documented previously, indicating a potential need for serial imaging in dogs with clinically relevant vertebral venous plexus abnormalities.

In our case, the dog experienced chronic, progressive, and refractory cervical pain despite medical management adjustments and routine physiotherapy. Pain was the most common clinical sign in a small population of affected sighthounds, and the clinical duration was chronic in most of them.3 Therapeutic pain management options have not been discussed previously for vertebral venous plexus abnormalities in veterinary medicine. In the human medical literature, numerous therapeutic options have been described for management of vertebral venous system abnormalities including transarterial embolization,9 surgical decompression, excision, or ligation,8,10 and anticoagulant treatment for thrombosed vessels.6 However, a majority of vertebral venous system abnormalities in human medicine are acquired.6,8–10 The underlying etiology of vertebral venous system abnormalities has not been further explored in veterinary medicine.

Given the lack of response to medical management and physiotherapy in our case, alternative pain management strategies were sought. Paravertebral perineural glucocorticoid injections recently have been described for management of pain and nerve root signature associated with foraminal intervertebral disc protrusions.15,16 These previous studies indicated that paravertebral perineural injections can be an effective treatment for pain associated with cervical foraminal intervertebral disc protrusion in some dogs.15,16 Although complete resolution of clinical signs did not occur in our patient, improvement in both lameness and discomfort was achieved. Additionally, the owner reported improvement in quality of life at home after the injections. No adverse effects were observed with local glucocorticoid administration in our case.

An inherent limitation of the current case report is that the outcome is described in a single patient. Although perineural glucocorticoid injections were associated with clinical improvement and lack of adverse effects in the current case, it remains unclear whether similar therapeutic benefit and lack of adverse effects would occur in a larger population. Additional prospective studies are needed to determine the ideal frequency of injections, safety, and efficacy of treatment.

In conclusion, our case highlights the use of ultrasound-guided percutaneous perineural glucocorticoid injections as a potential therapeutic option for refractory cervical pain secondary to vertebral venous system abnormality in a dog.
