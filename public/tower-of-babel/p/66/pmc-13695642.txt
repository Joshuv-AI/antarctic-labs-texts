ABSTRACT
BackgroundTP53 mutations are among the most frequent alterations in biliary tract cancer (BTC), but their prognostic relevance and predictive value for immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICI) remain unclear. We assessed clinico-molecular features, prognosis, and ICI efficacy by TP53 status in advanced BTC.MethodsPatients with advanced BTC from Japan and the United States who underwent tissue- or plasma-based next-generation sequencing were retrospectively analyzed. Tissue-based sequencing was used for the primary analysis, and plasma ctDNA sequencing was evaluated as an exploratory cohort. Outcomes were compared between TP53 wild-type (WT) and TP53-mutated groups. Treatment–TP53 interactions for non-ICI versus ICI-containing regimens were assessed using multivariable Cox models. Whole-transcriptome sequencing data were analyzed using TIDE and Hallmark gene set enrichment analysis.ResultsAmong 336 patients in the tissue-based cohort, 177 (52.7%) had TP53-mutated tumors. SMAD4, ERBB2, PTEN and CCNE1 co-alterations were enriched in TP53-mutated tumors, whereas IDH1, BAP1, and FGFR2 fusions were more frequent in WT tumors. TP53 mutations were independently associated with shorter PFS, whereas OS showed a similar but nonsignificant trend (PFS: HR 1.32, P = 0.04; OS: HR 1.32, P = 0.09). TP53 mutations were associated with shorter PFS in the non-ICI cohort but numerically longer PFS in the ICI cohort, with a significant treatment–TP53 interaction for PFS (P < 0.01). Similar trends were observed in the plasma cohort. Transcriptomic analyses (n = 59; all MSS) showed lower TIDE scores in TP53-mutated tumors.ConclusionTP53 mutations were associated with poor prognosis and may predict greater benefit from ICI, supporting biomarker-driven stratification.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00535-026-02439-9.

Introduction

Biliary tract cancer (BTC) comprises a diverse group of malignancies, including intrahepatic and extrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (ICC, ECC), gallbladder carcinoma (GBC) and ampullary carcinoma (AC). The incidence of BTC varies widely by subtype and geographic region, with cholangiocarcinoma showing a rising trend in high-income countries (0.35–2/100,000 annually) and an endemic prevalence in Southeast Asia, where incidence rates are up to 40 times higher [1]. While surgical resection remains the only curative treatment, most patients present with advanced disease, precluding surgery due to distant metastases or locoregional factors such as vascular invasion [2]. Even after curative-intent resection, recurrence is common [3]. Consequently, systemic therapies are the cornerstone of BTC treatment.

Recent advances in systemic therapy have improved outcomes for patients with advanced BTC. The combination of gemcitabine and cisplatin (GC) with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICI) has demonstrated superior disease control compared to conventional chemotherapy [4, 5]. However, clinical benefits remain modest, with a hazard ratio (HR) of approximately 0.8, and only a subset of patients derive significant benefit [4, 5]. The underlying determinants of treatment response remain poorly understood, emphasizing the need for predictive biomarkers to guide therapeutic strategies.

Among potential biomarkers, PD-L1 shows limited predictive value in BTC. In the KEYNOTE-158 study, while 58% of patients had PD-L1 ≥ 1%, only 6% exhibited a clinical response. The objective response rate (ORR) was 6.6% in PD-L1-positive and 2.9% in PD-L1-negative patients, with median progression-free survival (PFS) of 1.9 and 2.1 months, respectively [6]. DNA damage repair (DDR) gene mutations are candidate biomarkers for ICI efficacy. Approximately 25% of BTC may harbor DDR deficiencies [7], yet evidence for ICI efficacy remains limited. In a study of 86 patients with solid tumors, only 4 patient with BTC received pembrolizumab. Although the ORR was 25%, the small sample size makes it difficult to draw firm conclusions [8]. Similarly, microsatellite instability (MSI) and deficient mismatch repair (dMMR) have been explored as predictive markers. The KEYNOTE-158 study assessed pembrolizumab in advanced MSI-High (MSI-H)/dMMR tumors, including 22 BTC patients. The ORR was 40.9%, with 2 complete and 7 partial responses. Median PFS was 4.2 months, and median overall survival (OS) reached 24.3 months [9]. Despite MSI being a well-established biomarker, its prevalence in BTC is low (< 2%), and response to ICI remains inconsistent [10].

TP53 is among the most frequently mutated genes across malignancies and is central to genomic stability. Its mutations carry prognostic implications in many cancers, including BTC, and have been associated with response to ICI in NSCLC [11]. Although TP53 mutations are common in BTC, their prognostic and predictive value for ICI therapy remains unclear. To address this knowledge gap, we leveraged large-scale clinical and genomic datasets to investigate the prognostic impact of TP53 mutations in BTC and to explore their association with outcomes following ICI treatment.

Methods

Patients and study design

This retrospective observational study was conducted within the ONCOJACK framework, an international collaborative network integrating clinical and molecular data from patients with malignant tumors. Patients with unresectable locally advanced or metastatic BTC, including ICC, ECC, GBC, and AC, were included. Data were retrospectively collected from Japan and the U.S. In Japan, patients were identified from the SCRUM-Japan GOZILA and MONSTAR-SCREEN studies or were treated at the NCCHE. In the United States, patients were selected through clinically annotated medical records from the Mayo Clinic Comprehensive Cancer Center (Arizona, Minnesota, and Florida) and Duke Cancer Institute (North Carolina). The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) histologically or cytologically confirmed advanced BTC; (2) age ≥ 18 years; (3) receipt of any palliative systemic therapy; and (4) completion of comprehensive genomic profiling.

Genomic alterations were detected using tissue-based next-generation sequencing (NGS) platforms, including FoundationOne CDx (Foundation Medicine), Caris MI Profile (Caris Life Sciences), and the OncoGuide NCC Oncopanel System (Sysmex Corporation). Plasma ctDNA-based NGS platforms, such as Guardant360 CDx (Guardant Health) and FoundationOne Liquid CDx (Foundation Medicine), were also utilized. TP53 mutation status was determined based on TP53 alterations reported by each clinically validated platform. Variants classified as variants of uncertain significance were excluded from the analysis. All pathogenic or likely pathogenic TP53 alterations were included if they met the respective platform-specific reporting criteria. For the primary analysis, TP53 status was determined using tissue-based NGS results only. Patients who underwent plasma ctDNA-based NGS alone were not included in the primary analysis and were evaluated separately in the exploratory analysis. In liquid biopsy, genetic alterations with a variant allele frequency (VAF) greater than 1.0% were included. Results were reported according to the clinical thresholds defined by each platform.

The timing of comprehensive genomic profiling varied across cohorts. In the GOZILA study, plasma-based NGS was performed after disease progression on systemic therapy. In MONSTAR-SCREEN and MONSTAR-SCREEN-2, comprehensive genomic profiling was generally performed before first-line systemic therapy. In the National Cancer Center Hospital East cohort, profiling was performed in routine clinical practice under the Japanese insurance system, typically after failure of standard therapy. In the Mayo Clinic and Duke Cancer Institute cohorts, profiling was performed as part of routine clinical care before or during systemic treatment, according to institutional practice and tumor specimen availability.

The study was approved by local Ethics Committee at each center, and conducted in accordance with the protocol, the Declaration of Helsinki. Study protocols were approved by the institutional review boards (IRB) of participating institutions and registered in the University Hospital Medical Information Network (UMIN) Clinical Trials Registry (protocol IDs: GOZILA: UMIN000029315, MONSTAR-SCREEN: UMIN000036749, MONSTAR-SCREEN-2: UMIN000043899). Written informed consent was obtained from patients enrolled in the SCRUM-MONSTAR-SCREEN project. For retrospectively collected patient data, informed consent was obtained using an opt-out approach in accordance with institutional policies. This international integrated analysis study was approved by the IRB of the National Cancer Center Hospital East (NCCHE) (UMIN000049334).

Genomic co-alteration analysis

Genomic co-alteration analysis was performed in the tissue-based cohort. The frequencies of recurrent genomic alterations reported by each platform were compared between TP53 mutation and WT groups. Differences in alteration frequencies between the groups were assessed using Fisher’s exact test.

Analysis of the molecular pathways and response to immunotherapy

To explore the molecular and immunologic features associated with TP53 mutations in BTC, we analyzed whole-transcriptome sequencing (WTS) data from the MONSTAR-SCREEN-2 study. The Tumor Immune Dysfunction and Exclusion (TIDE) algorithm (http://tide.dfci.harvard.edu) [12] was applied to predict immunotherapy responsiveness. Higher TIDE scores reflect a greater likelihood of immune evasion and poorer ICI response, whereas lower scores indicate improved responsiveness. In parallel, gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed using Hallmark gene sets from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB; h.all.v2024.1.Hs.symbols.gmt), with enrichment defined as Family-Wise Error Rate P value < 0.05 [13].

Assessments

All efficacy analyses were restricted to patients receiving first-line systemic therapy. Two cohorts were used (Fig. 1): a prognostic cohort of advanced BTC to compare outcomes by TP53 status (mutated vs wild-type [WT]), and a first-line comparative cohort to compare non-ICI and ICI regimens. Tumor response was evaluated radiologically using CT/MRI in line with RECIST version 1.1 criteria [14]. An objective response was defined as a complete or partial response, and disease control was defined as a complete response, partial response, or stable disease as the best response. The OS was measured from the date of initiation of first-line treatment to the date of death from any cause or date of the last follow-up, or censored at the final follow-up examination. The PFS was defined as the period from the date of initiation of first-line treatment to the date of documentation of disease progression or death from any cause, or censored at the final follow-up examination.Fig. 1Flow diagram of the study patients. Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were compared with Fisher’s exact test and continuous variables with Mann–Whitney U or Kruskal–Wallis tests. OS and PFS were estimated by the Kaplan–Meier method and compared using the log-rank test. Prognostic factors were assessed by multivariable Cox proportional hazards analysis. The interaction effects between the treatment regimen and key covariates were tested within the multivariable Cox models by including multiplicative interaction terms, and the corresponding P values were reported as P for interaction. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05, and analyses were conducted using R (version 4.4.0, R Foundation, Vienna, Austria).

Liver metastasis was also more common in the TP53 mutation group (50.8% vs. 39.0%; P = 0.04). In the exploratory plasma ctDNA-based cohort, 514 patients were included: 252 in the WT group and 262 in the TP53 mutation group (Supplementary Table S1). Patients in the TP53 mutation group were older and more likely to have GBC and liver metastasis, whereas ICC was more frequent in the WT group.

Impact of TP53 mutations on the treatment response and survival

Treatment response and survival following first-line systemic therapy were evaluated in the tissue-based cohort. First-line regimens were similar by TP53 status (overall P = 0.53). Most patients received GC (WT vs TP53 mutation, 34.6% vs 38.2%), followed by GC + S-1 (27.7% vs. 32.8%) and GC + ICI (26.4% vs. 27.1%) (Table 1). Treatment responses were similar by TP53 status: WT vs TP53 mutation, ORR 27.7% vs 26.4% (P = 0.89) and DCR 76.3% vs 73.0% (P = 0.57). However, median OS was shorter in the TP53 mutation group, although the difference did not reach statistical significance (20.8 vs. 17.0 months, HR 1.32, 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.00–1.73; P = 0.05, Fig. 3A). Similarly, median PFS was significantly worse in the TP53 mutation group (6.9 vs. 5.4 months, HR 1.40, 95% CI 1.11–1.77; P < 0.01, Fig. 3B). In multivariable analysis, TP53 mutations were not independently associated with shorter OS (HR 1.32; P = 0.09, Fig. 3C), but were independently associated with shorter PFS (HR 1.32; P = 0.04, Fig. 3D). In a sensitivity analysis excluding patients with IDH1 mutations or FGFR2 fusions, the direction of association remained consistent after multivariable adjustment, although statistical significance was not reached (OS: adjusted HR, 1.22; P = 0.25; PFS: adjusted HR, 1.31; P = 0.06).Fig. 3Kaplan–Meier curves for OS (A) and PFS (B) according to the tissue-based TP53 mutation status in patients treated with first-line systemic therapy, and multivariable analyses conducted using Cox proportional hazards models to identify factors predictive of the OS (C) and PFS (D) in patients with advanced biliary tract cancer. Similar trends were observed in the exploratory plasma ctDNA-based cohort. Treatment responses did not differ significantly by TP53 status, with ORR of 21.1% versus 19.1% (P = 0.64) and DCR of 66.9% versus 63.4% (P = 0.45) in the WT and the TP53 mutation groups, respectively. Patients in the TP53 mutation group had significantly shorter OS (16.0 vs. 21.0 months; HR 1.28; 95% CI 1.02–1.61; P = 0.031) and PFS (5.7 vs. 6.8 months; HR 1.24; 95% CI 1.02–1.50; P = 0.030) than those in the WT group (Fig. S1).

Efficacy results for non-ICI vs. ICI-containing regimens according to TP53 status

Baseline characteristics of patients treated with non-ICI and ICI-containing regimens are shown in Supplementary Table S2. The ICI cohort included a higher proportion of female patients, and ICC, while TP53 status was balanced between cohorts. Supplementary Figure 2 presents ORR and DCR by TP53 status in the tissue-based cohort among patients receiving non-ICI (Fig. S2A) or ICI regimens (Fig. S2B). In the non-ICI group, ORR (27.5% vs. 23.1%) and DCR (80.4% vs. 76.9%) were comparable between the WT and the TP53 mutation groups, showing no significant differences. In contrast, in the ICI group, the TP53 mutation group showed a trend toward a higher ORR (21.4% vs. 32.0%, P = 0.35) and significantly higher DCR (47.6% vs. 70.0%, P = 0.03) than the WT group. In the non-ICI group (Fig. 4A, C), the TP53 mutation group had numerically shorter OS and significantly shorter PFS than the WT group. Conversely, in the ICI group (Fig. 4B, D), the TP53 mutation group showed a trend toward longer PFS, though not statistically significant, while OS remained similar between groups. Findings in the plasma ctDNA-based cohort were directionally consistent with those of the tissue-based cohort. Detailed efficacy outcomes according to TP53 status are summarized in Supplementary Table S3. Patients in the TP53 mutation group showed poorer outcomes in the non-ICI cohort, whereas this disadvantage was not observed in the ICI group. Although ORR and DCR differences did not reach statistical significance, the same general trend was observed (Fig. S3), along with similar patterns in survival and subgroup analyses (Figs. S4 and S5).

To further evaluate whether differences in post-progression management may have influenced OS, we assessed subsequent systemic therapy after progression according to TP53 status among patients with available post-progression treatment information. The proportion of patients receiving subsequent systemic therapy was comparable between TP53 mutation and WT tumors in both the ICI cohort (16/20, 80.0% vs. 11/17, 64.7%; P = 0.460) and the non-ICI cohort (59/91, 64.8% vs. 47/84, 56.0%; P = 0.279) (Additional file 10: Table S4). These findings suggest that differences in post-progression treatment are unlikely to fully account for the observed survival patterns.

Transcriptomic analyses of TP53-mutated BTC

Transcriptomic profiling of the MONSTAR-SCREEN-2 WTS dataset was conducted to assess immunologic and molecular features of TP53 mutation versus WT tumors. Among the analyzed samples (n = 59), no MSI-H cases were identified; thus, all analyses were conducted in the MSS context. The TP53 mutation group exhibited significantly lower TIDE scores compared with WT counterparts (P = 0.01; Fig. S6A, B). Furthermore, GSEA identified significant enrichment of hallmark pathways in the TP53 mutation group, most prominently oxidative phosphorylation (NES = 2.11, FWER P < 0.01), epithelial–mesenchymal transition (NES = 2.07, FWER P < 0.01), and hypoxia (NES = 1.74, FWER P < 0.01) (Fig. S6C).

Discussion

This study evaluated the clinical significance of TP53 mutations in patients with BTC using both tissue-based and plasma ctDNA-based analyses. In the tissue-based cohort, the TP53 mutation group showed poorer PFS and a similar trend toward shorter OS under systemic therapy and a significant treatment–TP53 interaction for PFS, both in the overall cohort and in the MSS subset, suggesting that the TP53 mutation group may derive greater relative benefit from ICI. In the plasma ctDNA-based cohort, the overall direction of association was broadly consistent with the tissue-based findings. The TP53 mutation group showed shorter OS and PFS in Kaplan–Meier analyses, although these associations were attenuated in multivariable models. In subgroup analyses, a significant treatment–TP53 interaction was observed for PFS, but not for OS. To our knowledge, this is among the largest studies to characterize the prognostic impact of TP53 mutations in advanced BTC and the first to examine their potential predictive relevance for ICI treatment across both tissue-based and plasma ctDNA-based settings.

In the tissue-based cohort, the TP53 mutation group had shorter OS and PFS than the WT group among patients with advanced BTC treated with systemic therapy. These findings align with previous studies across various malignancies including BTC, linking TP53 mutations to aggressive biology and resistance to standard therapies [15–19]. In this study, we confirmed this association in a larger cohort, further reinforcing the prognostic significance of TP53 mutations. p53 is a key tumor suppressor that regulates cell cycle arrest, apoptosis, and DNA repair. TP53 mutations not only disrupt these functions, enabling uncontrolled cell proliferation, but also promote tumor progression and metastasis [20]. For example, mutant p53 can enhance epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT), facilitating invasion and dissemination [21]. Consistent with this biology, liver metastasis was more frequent in the TP53 mutation group than in the WT group (58.3% vs 45.2%, P < 0.01), supporting the role of TP53 mutations in promoting metastatic spread.

Genomic co-alteration patterns differed substantially between the TP53 mutation group and the WT group. ERBB2, CDKN2A, SMAD4, PTEN, and CCNE1 alterations were enriched in the TP53 mutation group, whereas IDH1 and BAP1 mutations and FGFR2 fusions were more frequent in the WT group. These findings are consistent with previous reports showing mutual exclusivity between TP53 mutations and IDH1/BAP1 mutations and FGFR2 fusions in BTC [22, 23], reflecting distinct molecular subtypes. Leveraging a large, multi-institutional real-world dataset, we validate these genetic patterns and underscore the need for molecular stratification. We also characterized the spectrum of TP53 variants. Missense mutations predominated, with hotspots R175, Y220, R273, and R282, consistent with profiles in other solid tumors. These hotspot variants are known to exert not only loss-of-function but also gain-of-function activities, including enhanced invasion and migration, EMT, metabolic reprogramming, and resistance to therapy [24]. Notably, Y220C creates a druggable pocket and is currently the focus of targeted stabilizer development (NCT04585750), underscoring its potential clinical relevance. Given this biological heterogeneity, the functional subclassification of TP53 alterations may also be relevant to clinical behavior and treatment response in BTC.

A key finding of this study was the significant interaction between TP53 mutation status and treatment effect on PFS in the tissue-based cohort, observed both in the overall cohort and in the MSS subset. In the non-ICI group, the TP53 mutation group showed worse outcomes compared with the WT group, whereas in the ICI cohort, the TP53 mutation group showed a trend toward better responses than the WT group. These findings suggest that although the TP53 mutation group is associated with aggressive tumor biology and poor prognosis under conventional systemic therapy, it may also represent a subgroup that derives greater relative benefit from ICI-containing treatment. Similar, albeit less consistent, directional trends were observed in the plasma ctDNA-based cohort, supporting the overall robustness of this signal across sample types. One possible explanation for this apparent paradox is that TP53 mutations may simultaneously drive aggressive disease behavior and alter the tumor immune microenvironment in ways that enhance responsiveness to ICI. Consistent with this, the TP53 mutation group exhibited significantly lower TIDE scores than WT group, suggesting reduced immune dysfunction and exclusion. Furthermore, a multi-omics study of ICC reported that TP53 mutations were enriched in the immune-classical subtype with higher T-cell infiltration and an inflamed tumor microenvironment [25]. In NSCLC, TP53 mutations similarly associate with high immune infiltration and increased tumor mutational burden, with prolonged PFS and OS on ICI [18]. Nevertheless, despite these findings suggesting that the TP53 mutation group may be more responsive to ICI, our GSEA revealed enrichment of pathways such as oxidative phosphorylation, hypoxia, EMT, and MYC targets, which have been implicated in immune evasion and ICI resistance in other cancers [26–28]. This paradox likely reflects the biological heterogeneity of TP53-mutated tumors: while these tumors may be more responsive to ICI overall, concurrent activation of resistance mechanisms could limit the extent of benefit. From a therapeutic perspective, the TP53 mutation group represent a potentially ICI-sensitive subgroup, but rational combination strategies—such as targeting metabolic or hypoxia-related pathways—may be required to maximize benefit.

This study has several limitations. First, this retrospective design may introduce inherent selection biases. However, we sought to mitigate this limitation by integrating large, multi-institutional datasets from Japan and the U.S. Second, the ICI-containing subgroup was relatively small, limiting the statistical power to detect differences. Nevertheless, the treatment–TP53 interaction reached statistical significance in multivariable Cox analyses, supporting the robustness of this finding. Third, although functional subclassification of TP53 alterations may be clinically relevant, the number of gain-of-function cases was limited in our cohort, precluding a reliable analysis according to functional subtype. This issue should be addressed in future studies with larger sample sizes. Fourth, transcriptomic analyses relied on a small WTS (n = 59), so the findings should be interpreted cautiously. Notably, this subset did not include any MSI-H cases, which allowed us to assess the predictive significance of TP53 mutations with greater clarity, independent of this well-established determinant of ICI efficacy. Fifth, regional differences in treatment standards and access to ICI between Japan and the U.S. may have influenced outcomes. Yet, the inclusion of both regions strengthens the generalizability. Finally, the subgroup analysis including both non-ICI and ICI cohorts should be interpreted cautiously, as these groups likely differed in treatment era and patient selection. In particular, the non-ICI cohort may have included patients treated before the widespread use of ICIs, as well as patients receiving GCS for conversion purposes, who may have had better performance status or lower tumor burden, thereby introducing additional selection bias. Moreover, 31 of 219 patients (14.2%) in the non-ICI cohort subsequently received ICI-containing therapy in later lines, which may have confounded the interpretation of overall survival.

Conclusion

TP53 mutations in advanced BTC were associated with poor prognosis under systemic therapy and may predict enhanced benefit from ICI. In an era where ICI is becoming standard of care, these findings underscore the need for biomarker-driven patient stratification to optimize treatment strategies in BTC.
