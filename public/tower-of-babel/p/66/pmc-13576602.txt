Alteration of Acid‐Binding Capacity of Various Nursery Pig Diets and Its Influence on Properties of Digesta Along the Gastrointestinal Tract
Authors: Hyunjun Choi, Sung Woo Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe objective of this study was to investigate the effects of reduced dietary acid‐binding capacity (ABC) by acidifiers on the properties of digesta along the gastrointestinal tract of pigs. In Exp. 1, 144 pigs at 21 d of age [6.5 ± 0.9 kg body weight (BW)] were housed in pens (3 pigs/pen). In Exp. 2, 540 pigs weaned at 21 d of age (6.9 ± 0.9 kg BW) were housed in pens (3 barrows and 3 gilts/pen). In Exp. 1, the basal diet was supplemented with 0.25% bacitracin methylene disalicylate, 0.30 or 0.50% lactic acid‐formic acid blend, or 0.30% lactic acid‐formic acid‐benzoic acid blend. In Exp. 2, the basal diet was supplemented with 0.25% bacitracin methylene disalicylate, 0.25, 0.35, or 0.50% benzoic acid, or 0.30, 0.40, or 0.60% sodium benzoate. In both Exp. 1 and 2, pigs were fed diets in 3 phases: phase 1 (7 d in both Exp. 1 and 2), phase 2 (14 d in Exp. 1 and 17 d in Exp. 2), and phase 3 (21 d in Exp. 1 and 17 d in Exp. 2), respectively. The range of ABC at pH 4 in diets was 256 to 300, 194 to 220, and 129 to 176 meq/kg in phases 1, 2, and 3 of Exp. 1, and 234 to 273, 214 to 247, and 152 to 197 meq/kg in phases 1, 2, and 3 of Exp. 2, respectively. On d 42 in Exp. 1 and d 41 in Exp. 2, one pig representing the median BW in each pen was euthanized, and digesta from the stomach, proximal jejunum, distal jejunum, and cecum were collected to analyze the properties of digesta. The pH of digesta in the stomach was 4.0, which was lower (p < 0.05) than the pH of digesta in the proximal jejunum (6.0), distal jejunum (5.9), and cecum (5.5). The pH of digesta in the cecum was lower (p < 0.05) than the pH of the digesta in the proximal and distal jejunum. The dry matter (DM) content in stomach digesta was 32.5%, which was higher (p < 0.05) than the DM content of digesta in the proximal jejunum (14.9%), distal jejunum (14.4%), and cecum (14.3%). The ABC at pH 4 of nursery diets was not correlated with the ABC at pH 3 of freeze‐dried stomach digesta and pH of stomach digesta. However, the ABC‐3 of freeze‐dried stomach digesta was positively correlated (p < 0.05) with the DM content in stomach digesta (r = 0.57) and the pH of stomach digesta (r = 0.63) in nursery pigs. The DM content in stomach digesta was also positively correlated (p < 0.05) with the pH of stomach digesta of nursery pigs (r = 0.54). In conclusion, reducing dietary ABC by 40 to 50 meq/kg using acidifiers was not sufficient to reduce ABC or pH of stomach digesta in nursery pigs. However, the DM content in stomach digesta was positively correlated with its ABC and pH indicating that a decrease in the DM content and ABC of stomach digesta, if influenced by diets, is a primary factor contributing to reducing digesta pH in the stomach of nursery pigs.Keywords: acid‐binding capacity, dry matter of digesta, nursery pigs, pH of digesta

Introduction

Early weaning, typically performed at 14–24 days of age (Colson et al. 2006), shortens the reproductive cycle of sows, improving production efficiency and profitability. However, early weaning can cause environmental, physiological, and social stress to nursery pigs upon weaning (Campbell et al. 2013). One of the biggest challenges is the transition from sow's milk to solid diet (Moeser et al. 2017; Zheng et al. 2021), which can lead to reduced feed intake, impaired nutrient digestion, and growth retardation (Pluske 2016; Kim and Duarte 2021). These nursery pigs have limited hydrochloric acid (HCl) secretion, making it difficult to sufficiently lower the pH of stomach digesta. Nursery diets are nutrient‐rich and have a high acid‐binding capacity (ABC) compared to other growth stages (Lawlor et al. 2005a; Wang et al. 2023), creating an environment that further limits gastric acidification in pigs. As a result, an increase in the pH of stomach digesta can impair protein digestion and promote the growth of ammonia‐producing bacteria (Kratzer and Porter 1962; Rist et al. 2013). As the stomach is a non‐absorptive organ, the dry matter (DM) content in stomach digesta is highly influenced by diets. Due to the short mean retention time of stomach digesta of approximately 2–3 h (Wilfart et al. 2007; Dorado‐Montenegro et al. 2025), this can affect the ABC and pH of the stomach digesta. Based on previous findings, it was hypothesized that reduced dietary ABC by acidifiers would influence the properties of digesta, affecting the growth of nursery pigs by altering the DM and pH of digesta in the gastrointestinal tract, particularly in the stomach. To test this hypothesis, the objective of this study was to investigate the effects of reduced dietary ABC by acidifiers on the properties of digesta along the gastrointestinal tract of pigs.

Materials and Methods

The protocol of this experiment was reviewed and approved by the North Carolina State University Animal Care and Use Committee (Raleigh, NC).

Animals, Diets, and Feeding

In Exp. 1, a total of 144 pigs ([Landrace × Yorkshire] × [Hampshire × Duroc]; 82 barrows and 62 gilts) weaned at 21 d of age with an initial body weight (BW) of 6.5 ± 0.9 kg were assigned using a completely randomized design. Both barrows and gilts were randomly distributed across the pens, with each pen containing 3 pigs. In Exp. 2, a total of 540 pigs (PIC Camborough × DNA 600; 270 barrows and 270 gilts) weaned at 21 d of age with an initial BW of 6.9 ± 0.9 kg were assigned to a randomized complete block design, using the initial BW as a blocking factor. Each pen contained 6 pigs (3 barrows and 3 gilts). In Exp. 1, the basal diet was supplemented with 0.25% bacitracin methylene disalicylate (bacitracin: 0.03%), 0.30 or 0.50% blend of lactic acid and formic acid, or 0.30% blend of lactic acid, formic acid, and benzoic acid. In Exp. 2, the basal diet was supplemented with 0.25% bacitracin methylene disalicylate, 0.25, 0.35, or 0.50% benzoic acid, or 0.30, 0.40, or 0.60% sodium benzoate. Following Kim and Hansen (2022), the diets were formulated to meet or exceed the nutrient requirements for nursery pigs as estimated by NRC (2012) and were provided in 3 phases: phase 1 (7 d), phase 2 (14 d), and phase 3 (21 d) in Exp. 1, and phase 1 (7 d), phase 2 (17 d), and phase 3 (17 d) in Exp. 2. The range of ABC‐4 in the nursery diets was 256 to 300, 194 to 220, and 129 to 176 meq/kg in phases 1, 2, and 3 of Exp. 1 (Table 1), and 234 to 273, 214 to 247, and 152 to 197 meq/kg in phases 1, 2, and 3 of Exp. 2, respectively (Table 2). No therapeutic levels of Zn or Cu were supplemented to the diets in Exp. 1 or 2. The diets were provided in mash form. Pigs had free access to diet and water throughout the experimental period. The pigs in Exp. 1 were housed at the North Carolina State University Swine Evaluation Unit (Raleigh, NC) in pens (0.91 × 1.52 m2), and pigs in Exp. 2 were housed at the Central Crop Research Station (Clayton, NC) in pens (1.42 × 3.86 m2).

Sample and Data Collection

At the end of the feeding period (d 42 in Exp. 1 and d 41 in Exp. 2), one pig representing the median BW in each pen was desensitized using a penetrating captive bolt and euthanized by exsanguination of the vena cava. After euthanasia, digesta from the stomach, proximal jejunum, distal jejunum, and cecum were collected to analyze the properties of digesta. Digesta in the proximal jejunum was collected 1.5 m after the pyloric valve and digesta in the distal jejunum was collected 1 m before the ileocecal junction.

Chemical Analysis

Diet samples were finely ground and analyzed for DM (method 930.15), crude protein (CP; method 990.03), ether extract (method 920.39), neutral detergent fiber (method 2002.04), and acid detergent fiber (method 973.18) as described in AOAC (2005). The ground diet samples were used to measure pH using a calibrated pH meter (AB15 Basic, Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA), following the method of Lawlor et al. (2005a). In addition to diet samples, pH was measured immediately from the digesta in the stomach, proximal jejunum, distal jejunum, and cecum after collection, using a calibrated pH meter. Frozen digesta from the stomach, proximal jejunum, distal jejunum, and cecum were freeze‐dried and then ground. The ABC was determined as the amount of acid in milliequivalents required to lower the pH of 1 kg of sample to reach pH 4 (ABC‐4) for the diets in Exp. 1 and 2, and to reach pH 3 (ABC‐3) for the freeze‐dried stomach digesta (Lawlor et al. 2005a) in Exp. 1. Statistical Analysis

Experimental data were analyzed using the MIXED procedure in SAS (SAS Inst., Cary, NC). The statistical model included the location of digesta in the gastrointestinal tract (GIT; stomach, proximal jejunum, distal jejunum, and cecum) as a fixed effect, with the pig as the experimental unit, and the final BW of the pigs as a random variable. The least squares mean of each gastrointestinal location was calculated. To further investigate the physiological relationship between dietary characteristics and digesta in the GIT, the correlation coefficients (r) between the DM content and the pH of digesta in the stomach, proximal jejunum, distal jejunum, and cecum, between the ABC‐4 of nursery diets and the DM content and pH of digesta in the stomach, and between the ABC‐3 of freeze‐dried digesta in the stomach and the DM content and pH of digesta in the stomach, were determined using the Proc CORR procedure in SAS. The statistical significance and tendency were declared at p < 0.05 and 0.05 ≤ p < 0.10, respectively.

The pH of digesta in the stomach of nursery pigs was 4.0, which was lower (p < 0.05) than that of the proximal jejunum (6.0), distal jejunum (5.9), and cecum (5.5; Table 3). There was no difference in the pH of digesta between the proximal and distal jejunum, but the pH of digesta in the cecum was lower (p < 0.05) than that of the proximal and distal jejunum.

Discussion

In nursery pigs, diets are typically formulated with high levels of nutrients, particularly protein and minerals. However, these components increase the dietary ABC and raise the pH of stomach digesta (Lawlor et al. 2005a; Wang et al. 2023). This causes a major physiological challenge because nursery pigs have a limited capacity to secrete HCl (Cranwell et al. 1976). A strongly acidic gastric environment is essential to maximize the solubility and digestion of nutrients in diets and to kill ingested pathogens. Thus, various acidifiers have been supplemented to nursery diets to reduce ABC and the pH of stomach digesta (Lawlor et al. 2005b; Wang et al. 2023). However, in this study, reducing the ABC of nursery diets using acidifiers did not correlate with the ABC‐3 and pH of digesta in the stomach, suggesting that a reduction of 40–50 meq/kg in dietary ABC may have been insufficient to alter the gastric environment.

To put this into physiological context, suckling pigs are less affected by limited HCl secretion because their liquid milk diet does not require high acid buffering to dissolve, and the active fermentation of lactose into lactic acid provides a natural alternative to maintain a low gastric pH (Bertschinger et al. 2000). After weaning, however, both lactose intake and HCl secretion in the stomach are limited, resulting in an increase in digesta pH (Efird et al. 1982; Wang et al. 2023). Previous studies have reported that during the early post‐weaning period, the pH of stomach digesta frequently remains above 5.0 for 1 to 2 weeks (Schulman 1973; Lawlor et al. 2005b). Such a high pH in the stomach digesta of nursery pigs reduces protein digestion by limiting the activation of pepsinogen to pepsin and by promoting the growth of ammonia‐producing bacteria (Kratzer and Porter 1962; Rist et al. 2013). In contrast to these previous studies (Schulman 1973; Lawlor et al. 2005b), the pH range of stomach digesta in the nursery pigs of this study ranged from 3.2 to 4.8, representing a highly favorable and acidic environment for protein digestion (Lewis et al. 1957; Bottger and Holler 1974). This discrepancy may be attributed to a long‐term physiological adaptation and the age of the pigs at the time of sampling (Cranwell 1985). This extended feeding duration allowed sufficient time for the maturation of gastric HCl secretion (Hartman et al. 1961; Ma et al. 2002). Consequently, the magnitude of endogenous HCl production by these older, more developed pigs likely decreased the impact of a 50 meq/kg reduction in dietary ABC. These results reflect a stabilized and mature digestive system in the pigs. On the other hand, evaluating the early post‐weaning phase as a distinct physiological stage remains necessary to fully understand how dietary ABC interacts with a less mature gastrointestinal tract.

In addition to these findings, a positive correlation was observed in the stomach between the DM content or ABC of digesta and its pH. This strong correlation shows an important physiological fact that even after a 40‐d adaptation period, during which the gastrointestinal tract develops a relatively mature acid‐secreting capacity compared to the early post‐weaning phase, diet‐driven characteristics like digesta DM still directly influence the stomach environment and the physical state of the digesta. Because the stomach is not a main absorptive organ, the DM content in stomach digesta can be highly influenced by the diet. Additionally, given the short mean retention time of around 2–3 h in the stomach, there can be substantial variation in DM content in stomach digesta (Wilfart et al. 2007; Dorado‐Montenegro et al. 2025), potentially influencing digesta ABC and pH. In this study, the DM content in stomach digesta ranged from 20% to 40%, whereas the DM content in the small intestine ranged from 5% to 20%. A possible reason for the reduced DM content in the small intestine is the dilution of digesta caused by the secretion of various digestive fluids, including bile, pancreatic juices, and water. This dilution increases the pH of the digesta and explains why there is a lack of correlation between DM content and digesta pH in the small intestine.

As expected, the pH of digesta in the small intestine was higher than that in the stomach, which is consistent with the results of a previous study (Merchant et al. 2011). This increase is due to the secretion of bicarbonate and bile into the duodenum (Cheung and Porterfield 1979). Bicarbonate, secreted from the pancreas, neutralizes acidic gastric contents, thereby raising the pH of the digesta and creating a favorable environment for enzyme activity, mucosal protection, and nutrient digestion in pigs (Abello et al. 1987). Bile, which contains bile salts and alkaline substances, further neutralizes the acidic digesta or chyme (Sutor and Wilkie 1976). These effects continue distally, as shown by the pH of digesta observed in the jejunum in this study.

Conversely, the pH of digesta in the cecum was lower than that in the small intestine, which is consistent with the findings of a previous study (Crespo‐Piazuelo et al. 2018). This reduction is due to the microbial fermentation of undigested substances, leading to the production of short‐chain fatty acids (van Winsen et al. 2001). The digesta in the large intestine of pigs contains a greater microbial population than that in the small intestine. In the small intestine, bile acids exert bactericidal effects (Begley et al. 2005; Jiménez et al. 2014), whereas in the large intestine, a longer mean retention time of 25–40 h facilitates greater microbial activity and metabolite production (Wilfart et al. 2007). Thus, anaerobic bacteria, which dominate in the large intestine (Donaldson et al. 2016), produce short‐chain fatty acids under low‐oxygen conditions, contributing to the acidic environment observed in the cecal digesta in this study.

Collectively, these clear compartmental patterns demonstrate that although acidifiers help support gastric acid secretion in the stomach, their efficacy may be dependent on age, being more pronounced during the early post‐weaning phase than in the later stages. To improve gastric acid regulation in older nursery pigs, a higher dose of acidifiers may be needed to exceed their increased capacity to secrete HCl, relative to younger nursery pigs. Furthermore, pH shifts downstream of the stomach are driven by the pig's own digestive fluids and site‐specific microbial fermentation rather than dietary buffering traits.

Conclusions

Reducing dietary ABC by 50 meq/kg by acidifiers did not affect the ABC or pH of stomach digesta, indicating that gastric HCl secretion was sufficient to maintain a low digesta pH in the stomach of nursery pigs from 11 to 25 kg. In contrast, the DM content in stomach digesta was positively correlated with both the ABC and pH of stomach digesta. These findings suggest that a decrease in the DM content and ABC of stomach digesta, if influenced by diets, is a primary factor contributing to a reduction in digesta pH in the stomach; however, a reduction in dietary ABC by acidifiers should be greater than 50 meq/kg to effectively reduce the digesta pH in the stomach of pigs from 11 to 25 kg.
