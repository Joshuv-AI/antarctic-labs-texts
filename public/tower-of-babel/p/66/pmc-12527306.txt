A 55-year-old man with a left-sided goiter and local compression symptoms presented to our endocrinology clinic. He did not have dysphagia, dyspnea, or hoarseness. All laboratory results, including thyroid-stimulating hormone levels, were within normal limits. We performed ultrasonography of the thyroid, which revealed a hypoechoic, highly inhomogeneous nodular mass with blurred boundaries (46 × 25 × 24 mm, 14 cm3; Appendix 1, Figure S1, available at www.cmaj.ca/lookup/doi/10.1503/cmaj.250480/tab-related-content). The ultrasonographic characteristics suggested a malignant tumour (European Thyroid Imaging Reporting and Data System [EU-TIRADS] 4, intermediate risk of malignancy 6%–17%). However, the fine-needle aspiration cytology was benign (Bethesda II, risk of malignancy < 3%). Therefore, we opted for expectant management. Subsequently, the patient experienced increasing local discomfort. Follow-up ultrasonography 6 weeks later showed an increase in size (46 × 30 × 26 mm, 18 cm3) and a change in the ultrasonographic characteristics (EUTIRADS 5, high risk of malignancy 26% to 87%). Given the suspicion of a malignant thyroid tumour, we immediately scheduled the patient to undergo left hemithyroidectomy.

During surgery, we found that the left lobe of the thyroid was extremely indurated; widespread perithyroidal fibrosis with dense adhesions to the surrounding cervical structures suggested an infiltrative malignant tumour. We continued surgical dissection to explore the technical feasibility of a radical resection. We partially resected the strap muscles and the external muscle fibres of the esophagus. However, we observed extensive infiltration of the trachea and the left recurrent laryngeal nerve, so we stopped the operation and called for a team time-out. We reviewed the patient’s medical record and discussed the differential diagnoses, including peritumoral fibrosis and anaplastic carcinoma. Given the rock-hard appearance of the thyroid, we also considered Riedel thyroiditis. Based on these findings and further discussion, we considered that there were 3 surgical options: abort the operation and transfer the patient to a highly specialized surgical centre for radical multivisceral resection; perform a surgical excision biopsy and carry out an intraoperative frozen section examination; or continue with a nonradical resection of the left thyroid lobe while preserving the infiltrated structures. We proceeded with the last option and performed a near-total left-sided debulking operation. Postoperative vocal cord function was normal. Histology of the resected left lobe of the thyroid (55 × 50 × 25 mm, 38.5 g) demonstrated severe chronic inflammation that spread into the perithyroidal fatty tissues and the strap muscles, with multiple sites of phlebitis. The normal thyroid tissue was completely replaced with keloid-like fibrosis, with no signs of malignancy (Figure 1 and Figure 2). We diagnosed Riedel thyroiditis. The immunoglobulin (Ig) G4 fraction was within normal limits.

Two months after surgery, the patient reported right-sided neck pain. Ultrasonography revealed a diffusely enlarged right lobe of the thyroid (19 cm3). Treatment with prednisone (40 mg/d) over 3 months led to the resolution of his symptoms. Ten months after surgery, magnetic resonance imaging demonstrated a normal right lobe of the thyroid (12 cm3) and an empty left resection bed with no signs of disease recurrence.

Discussion

Thyroid nodules are common and their incidence increases with age. Neck ultrasonography and fine-needle aspiration cytology are used for diagnosis. The ultrasonographic and cytological classification systems, as well as clinical guidelines, may differ slightly among countries. However, local symptoms, size of the lesion, ultrasonographic characteristics, and cytological appearance should always drive clinical decision making. Final assessment by a multidisciplinary endocrine tumour board is recommended.1

Riedel thyroiditis is a rare, chronic inflammatory disease involving the thyroid gland and the surrounding cervical structures. 2–5 Its estimated incidence is 1 per 100 000 population,2–4 and it mainly affects female patients (81%) aged 30–50 years (mean age at onset 47 yr).2,3

Riedel thyroiditis has been considered an autoimmune process or a primary fibrotic disorder. Currently, it is regarded as the local manifestation of an IgG4-related systemic multifocal fibrosclerotic disease.2,3,6,7 The disease presents as progressive enlargement of the thyroid gland, which may be limited to 1 lobe or may affect the entire thyroid. The key feature of the underlying inflammatory-fibrosing process is its extension beyond the thyroid capsule with infiltration of adjacent structures, such as the trachea and the esophagus, thereby mimicking a locally invasive malignant tumour.3,8,9

Clinical examination shows a rock-hard indolent cervical mass that is not freely movable upon swallowing.3 Patients often report local compressive symptoms out of proportion to the demonstrable lesion.3 The main presenting signs are cervical swelling (89%), dysphagia (63%), dyspnea (50%), neck pain (41%), hoarse voice (41%), and stridor (31%).2 Eventually, normal endocrine tissues are replaced by fibrosis, leading to hypothyroidism (80%) and hypoparathyroidism (14%).2,3 About one-third of patients will develop extracervical disease activity (e.g., mediastinal and retroperitoneal fibrosis, sclerosing cholangitis, pancreatitis).3,7 Death is rare and usually results from tracheal compression and ensuing respiratory compromise.3

If the initial assessment leads to suspicion of Riedel thyroiditis, additional diagnostic tools beyond blood tests and neck ultrasonography are indicated (Table 1). Computed tomography and magnetic resonance imaging allow for precise delineation of the disease process. Encasement of the large cervical vessels is a pathognomonic feature, affecting carotid arteries (50%) and internal jugular veins (30%).3,7 Radiologic narrowing of the trachea is found in 75% of patients.2 Positron emission tomography with fluorine-18 fluorodeoxyglucose displays intense tracer avidity, which is useful in detecting extracervical areas of fibrosclerosis. 3 Thyroid scintigraphy, on the other hand, shows a low uptake of the radionuclide.3,7

Fine-needle aspiration cytology is usually nondiagnostic, which constitutes the greatest challenge in the diagnostic workup. 2,3,7 Definitive diagnosis must be established by histopathological examination, either from an open biopsy or through resective decompression surgery.3,7

The clinical and radiologic picture of Riedel thyroiditis mimics malignancy.9 Therefore, sarcoma, lymphoma, and, in particular, the paucicellular variant of anaplastic thyroid carcinoma must be ruled out. The fibrosing variant of Hashimoto thyroiditis can present in a similar fashion, albeit without extension beyond the thyroid capsule.3,8

Histopathological diagnosis of Riedel thyroiditis is based on observation of a fibroinflammatory process involving all or a portion of the thyroid gland; evidence of its extension into surrounding tissues, including the strap muscles; infiltrates of inflammatory cells without giant cells, lymphoid follicles, oncocytes, or granulomas; evidence of occlusive phlebitis; and no evidence of thyroid malignancy.2,3,8,10

Since the diagnosis of Riedel thyroiditis cannot usually be made using fine-needle aspiration cytology, an open surgical biopsy for tissue retrieval is required.3 If obstructive symptoms are pronounced and conservative therapy is unsuccessful, a pressure-relieving debulking surgery for airway decompression is required.3 In most cases, surgery is limited to the resection of the thyroid isthmus. The fibrosclerotic process, which extends beyond the thyroid capsule into the surrounding structures, leads to an obliteration of tissue planes, making precise surgical resection impossible.3 Radical thyroid resections should be avoided given high complication rates, including damage to the recurrent laryngeal nerves (vocal cord paralysis) and the parathyroid glands (hypoparathyroidism).2–4

Depending on the severity of symptoms, medical treatment is either a stand-alone intervention or applied in conjunction with surgery.2,3 Most published cases (70%) involved treatment with prednisone (40 mg/d) for an average of 3 months, which led to a reduction in size and consistency of the thyroid. With a median follow-up of 12 months, 90% of patients achieved either complete resolution of symptoms or substantial clinical improvement. 2 Another 14% of patients were treated with tamoxifen (20 mg/d) over a median period of 8 months.3 Tamoxifen was applied as a monotherapy or together with continued glucocorticoid medication.3 Finally, individual case reports have described therapies with azathioprine, rituximab, or mycophenolate mofetil.2,3

A marked discrepancy between the local compression symptoms and the actual size of the thyroid lesion appears to be characteristic for Riedel thyroiditis.3 Our patient experienced a rapid increase in size of the lesion before surgery, which is highly uncommon for differentiated thyroid cancers. Finally, neck ultrasonography revealed a mass with blurred boundaries, suggestive of an infiltrative process. In retrospect, these red flags should have prompted further diagnostic procedures, such as magnetic resonance imaging. The intraoperative team time-out, however, proved beneficial. Reflection on the intraoperative findings allowed for renewed consideration of Riedel thyroiditis and helped redirect the operation, avoiding radical surgery and the concomitant morbidity.

When Riedel thyroiditis is suspected and in the absence of severe symptoms of cervical compression, primary medical therapy should be attempted, which may obviate the need for surgery altogether.
