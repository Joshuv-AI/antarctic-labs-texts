Maturity‐onset diabetes of the young: A proposal for updated nomenclature
Authors: Mustafa Tosur
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractGiven the contemporary understanding of diabetes genetics, the term “maturity‐onset diabetes of the young (MODY)” warrants renaming. I propose adopting the term “monogenic diabetes” in conjunction with the specific gene name.

Maturity‐onset diabetes of the young (MODY) was initially described as a distinctive form of diabetes observed in children and young adults during the 1950s 1 . Dr. Fajans 2 faced opposition from leading figures in the field when presenting these cases during the inaugural International Congress of Endocrinology in 1960, as some contended the absence of this diabetes subtype in Europe. Subsequently, in 1964, Dr. Fajans coined the term “maturity‐onset type diabetes of childhood or of the young” to characterize this unusual diabetes at the 5th International Congress of Endocrinology. At the time, there were two main types of diabetes: juvenile‐onset type, typically manifesting in childhood and necessitating insulin therapy from disease onset, and maturity‐onset type, commonly emerging in adults and often manageable solely with oral agents until later stages. Dr. Fajans intended to convey the similarity of this novel diabetes to “maturity‐onset type” diabetes due to its manageable nature with oral agents, predominantly affecting children and young adults.

In 1974, Tattersall and Fajans introduced the acronym “MODY” for “maturity‐onset type diabetes of young people” 3 , 4 . Concurrently, they utilized “JOD” to denote “juvenile‐onset type diabetes,” referring to what is now recognized as “type 1 diabetes.”

Although this term and its acronym were pertinent in the 1960s and 1970s, several issues arise in the contemporary understanding of MODY. Primarily, the terms “juvenile‐onset type” and “maturity‐onset type” have been supplanted by “type 1 diabetes” and “type 2 diabetes,” respectively, in the current literature. Additionally, the emergence of the term “maturity‐onset diabetes of the young” from its original description as “maturity‐onset type of diabetes of childhood or of the young” has led to the omission of “type” and “childhood”. One could argue that “young” inherently encompasses “childhood,” yet the absence of “type” in the naming causes confusion. Without the word “type”, “maturity‐onset diabetes” implies that this form of diabetes manifests in maturity (i.e., adulthood), whereas the reality is quite the opposite. In contrast to the 1960s and 1970s, when patients were primarily characterized based on their clinical phenotype, the advent of genomic medicine now enables diagnosis through genetic testing, even in individuals aged 50–60 years. This development highlights the problematic nature of the term “young” within the definition, as it no longer exclusively applies to this specific patient demographic.

Given these considerations, it might be prudent to reassess the nomenclature and abbreviation of maturity‐onset diabetes of the young (MODY). I propose adopting the term “monogenic diabetes,” specifying the involved gene, to encompass both pediatric and adult populations (e.g., GCK monogenic diabetes, HNF1A monogenic diabetes, etc.; Figure 1). The designation of “neonatal diabetes” could still be retained to describe a distinct form of monogenic diabetes in infants, accompanied by its causal genes. For patients showing a typical monogenic diabetes phenotype without an identified genetic etiology, the term “unspecified monogenic diabetes” could be used. If an abbreviation is deemed necessary, “MONO” or “MDM” could serve as a succinct abbreviation for monogenic diabetes (e.g., GCK‐MONO or GCK‐MDM, HNF1A‐MONO or HNF1A‐MDM etc.).
