Cesarean section-induced changes in the gut microbiota facilitate metabolic disease in high-fat diet-induced obese mice
Authors: Line Fisker Zachariassen, Frederikke Uldall Fløe Mortensen, Caroline Märta Junker Mentzel, Ping-Ping Jiang, Pablo Atienza Lopez, Lukasz Krych, Nuria Canibe, Rikke Kaae Kirk, Andreas Vegge, Morten Arendt Rasmussen, Jakob Stokholm, Camilla Hartmann Friis Hansen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe global rate of cesarean section (CS) births is increasing. Growing evidence suggests that CS birth may alter the gut microbiota (i.e., dysbiosis) and increase the risk of immune and metabolic disorders, although confounding factors make causality difficult to establish. The studies presented here aimed to investigate the causal relationship between CS-induced gut dysbiosis and obesity in a diet-induced obese mouse model and explore potential microbiota-targeted therapies.ResultsIn the first study, male C57BL/6 mice were delivered via CS or vaginally (VD) and fed a high- or low-fat diet (HFD, LFD) for 12 weeks. In the second study, male germ-free BALB/c mice were transplanted with fecal microbiota from 1-month-old infants born by CS or VD and fed a HFD or HFD + human milk oligosaccharides (HMOs) for 16 weeks. CS in mice induced only minor differences in weight gain and had no effect on other metabolic endpoints, likely because there was no difference in the gut microbiota between the CS and VD mice. In contrast, mice colonized with the human CS microbiota weighed significantly more and developed greater insulin resistance than mice colonized with the VD microbiota. These phenotypic changes were accompanied by alterations in serum cytokines, adipokines and metabolic hormones as well as differential gene expression across multiple metabolic tissues. Notably, these manifestations were partially ameliorated by HMO supplementation and by administration of Bacteroides fragilis, a taxon depleted in the CS donor microbiota, which directly reduced circulating FGF-21 levels, implicating this bacterium in host metabolic regulation.ConclusionsCS-induced gut dysbiosis can increase the risk of developing obesity and insulin resistance, but without dysbiosis, the metabolic effects of CS birth in isolation are minimal, suggesting that promising therapeutic targets may be identified in the gut microbiome. Background

The rate of birth by cesarean section (CS) continues to rise worldwide. Global CS rates have increased significantly, from around 7% in 1990 to approximately 21% today, surpassing the WHO's recommended acceptable range of 10–15% [1]. There is a growing scientific consensus that children born by CS have a greater risk of developing immune-mediated and metabolic disorders [2–5]. However, the literature is inconsistent, as numerous confounding factors, such as maternal health status, the use of antibiotics, and formula feeding, present major challenges in demonstrating causality. Several studies have also shown disturbances in the early gut microbiota composition (i.e., dysbiosis) of children born by CS [6–8], which may explain the associated health consequences related to birth mode, as the gut microbiota plays a major role in determining health and disease [9–12]. CS-delivered children typically present, among other differences, with a greater abundance of Clostridium spp. and Enterococcus spp., along with a lower abundance of Bacteroides spp. and Bifidobacterium spp. [13–16].

The association between CS delivery and the risk of childhood obesity remains controversial. Some human cohort studies have reported an increased risk in boys born by CS [17, 18], others have reported an increased risk for both genders [19–21], and some have reported only an association, especially for elective [22] or emergency CS [23], or no increased risk at all [24, 25]. Previous studies have also shown increased weight gain following CS in mice fed a regular diet [26], as well as a dysbiotic gut microbiota and altered immune maturation [27–30], compared with those in vaginally delivered mice. The aim of this study was therefore to provide evidence for the cause-and-effect of CS-induced dysbiosis and obesity in a high-fat diet (HFD)-induced obese mouse model. The hypothesis was that CS-induced changes in the gut microbiota would enhance obesity and related metabolic phenotypes in (i) mice born by CS and in (ii) germ-free mice transplanted with the human fecal microbiome from CS-delivered infants after being subjected to a HFD. Furthermore, breastmilk contains high amounts of human milk oligosaccharides (HMOs), a microbiota-specific substrate with beneficial effects on the gut microbiota in especially CS-delivered children [31]. It was therefore further hypothesized that an HMO-supplemented diet could reverse CS-induced dysbiosis-mediated metabolic effects in HFD-fed mice. This research could thereby shed light on potential therapeutic interventions targeting the gut microbiota to mitigate potential long-term metabolic health consequences of CS delivery.

Methods

See supplementary methods for a detailed description of ethics and additional material and methods.

Experimental setup

Three experimental studies were performed. (i) First (Study 1, Figs. 1A and S1A), barrier-bred C57BL/6N mice were time-mated for 48 h, and the pups were delivered either vaginally (VD) or by cesarean section (CS) on day 20 of pregnancy. The pregnant mice were euthanized for CS delivery, and all pups were cross-fostered within 20 min post-birth. The pups were slightly rubbed against the foster mother’s pups to transfer smell and improve survival. This was also done for the vaginally born mice to avoid the experimental bias of only cross-fostering one group. After weaning at 4 weeks of age, a total of 49 male pups from 6 to 7 litters per group were fed a high-fat diet (HFD) with an energy density of 5.24 kcal/g (fat 60% kcal, carbohydrate 20% kcal, protein 20% kcal; Research Diets, New Brunswick, NJ), and 28 male pups from 4 litters per group were fed a low-fat diet (LFD) with an energy density of 3.85 kcal/g (fat 10% kcal, carbohydrate 70% kcal, protein 20% kcal; Research Diets) for 12 weeks. The males were housed with 3 to 4 littermates per cage throughout the study. (ii) For the second study (Study 2, Fig. 2A), pregnant germ-free BALB/c mice were inoculated with human microbiota in the last week of pregnancy, and the pups were inoculated at 0, 1, and 3 weeks of age as previously published [32]. Human fecal samples were selected from 1-month-old CS and VD infants. Donors were selected according to the following inclusion criteria for VD donors: born by VD, breastfed, low CS microbial score (see Stokholm et al. [33] for details on this score) at 1 month and 1 year, and for the CS donors: born by CS, breastfed, high CS microbial score at 1 month and 1 year. A total of 15 potential donor samples (CS, n = 5; VD, n = 10) met the inclusion criteria, and among these samples, 4 CS and 4 VD donors were selected according to differences in beta diversity. Sample collection, treatment and preparation of the inoculum are all described in detail in Zachariassen et al. [32], where the same donors were used, and detailed in supplementary methods. Male pups were housed in one isolator per group with either the VD human microbiome (HM-VD) or CS human microbiome (HM-CS) together with the female pups, which were used for a previously published study on atopic dermatitis [32]. The 21 male pups from 4 different litters in each group were housed with 2–3 littermates per cage after weaning at 4 weeks of age. At 6 weeks of age, the breeding diet was changed to an autoclaved HFD for 16 weeks before euthanization. An additional 10 mice from each group, from 3 to 4 litters per group, were euthanized at weaning while still on the breeding diet, and samples were collected for metabolomic and short-chain fatty acid analysis. (iii) The third experiment (Study 3, Fig. 5A) was similar to that of Study 2, except that the male pups, from 5 litters per group, were fed a HFD supplemented with 2.5% synthetic analogs of human milk oligosaccharides (HMOs) to investigate the potential of HMOs to restore the microbiome, as observed in CS-delivered infants on breast milk [34], and diminish the observed changes in the metabolic phenotype of HFD-fed mice with a CS-associated microbiome. The nonfucosylated neutral HMO lacto-N-neotetraose (LNnt) and the most abundant HMO in breast milk, fucosylated 2′-fucosyllactose (2′-FL), are widely studied and considered safe for infant nutrition [35]. LNnt and 2′-FL were therefore supplemented at a 1:9 ratio to the diet by replacing sucrose with an energy content similar to that of the original HFD. The HMO dose was calculated from the assumption that each mouse eats approximately 3.5 g per day and has a mean weight of 24.5 g from weeks 3 to 12 on the basis of Taconic growth charts on the HFD. Recalculation of a mouse dose from a human dose of 20 g per day, which is considered safe and well tolerated in phase I trials, according to metabolic weight, yielded a daily dose of 86.5 mg HMOs in total. The dietary recipes are described in supplementary methods. The primary read out weight was measured every second week and only until blood sampling and glucose tolerance tests commenced because of stress-related effects on weight. Food consumption was measured weekly as an average per mouse per cage. At the end of each experiment, all mice were anesthetized using 0.1 mL/10 g body weight of a mixture of 25% Midazolam (5 mg/mL midazolam, B. Braun, Melsungen, Germany) and 25% Hypnorm (0.315 mg/mL of fentanyl citrate and 10 mg/mL of fluanisone, Skanderborg Apotek, Skanderborg, Denmark). Blood was sampled from the retroorbital plexus of the eye into a heparin-coated capillary (Kruuse, Langeskov, Denmark). Hereafter, the mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation. Oral glucose tolerance test

An oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) was performed at the end of the experiments (Study 1: after 11 weeks on HFD and Studies 2 + 3: after 16 weeks on HFD). After 6 h of fasting from 6AM (light phase), baseline blood glucose was measured with a Freestyle Lite glucometer (Abbott Diabetes Care, Copenhagen, Denmark) from tail blood, immediately followed by the administration of 75 mg of glucose monohydrate (500 mg/ml) by oral gavage (0 min). Blood glucose was measured again at 15, 30, 45, 60, 90, and 120 min.

Hormone and cytokine multiplex analysis

Blood samples were collected at 14 weeks of age from 6 h fasted mice (Study 1) or at 22 weeks of age from nonfasted mice (Studies 2 + 3). The samples were subsequently centrifuged at 8000 × g for 8.5 min, after which the serum was stored at 80 °C until further analysis. Epididymal white adipose tissue (eWAT) was collected at euthanasia, weighed and homogenized in 500 µl of lysis buffer (stock solution: 10 ml of Tris lysis buffer, 100 µl of phosphatase inhibitor 1, 100 µl of phosphatase inhibitor 2, and 200 µl of protease inhibitor (MSD inhibitor pack, Mesoscale Discovery, Rockville, MD)) via a tissue blender (POLYTRON PT 1200 E, Kinematica, Luzern, Switzerland) and centrifuged (7500 g; 4 °C; 5 min). In Study 1, the serum insulin and leptin concentrations were measured with a Mouse Metabolic Kit, and eWAT cytokines were measured with a V-PLEX Plus Mouse Cytokine 19-Plex Kit (Mesoscale Discovery, Rockville, MD). In Study 2, serum and eWAT cytokines and hormones were measured with the V-PLEX Proinflammatory Panel 1 Mouse Kit and U-PLEX Metabolic Combo 1 Mouse Kit (Mesoscale Discovery), respectively. In Study 3, a customized U-PLEX Mouse Kit (Mesoscale Discovery) was used, which included only the cytokines and hormones that were found to be different between the groups in Study 2. All analyses were performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Plates were read on a MESO QuickPlex SQ120, and the data were analyzed by the standard software Discovery Workbench (Mesoscale Discovery). Measurements outside the detection range were assigned values of the lower or upper detection limits. The concentrations were extrapolated from a standard curve and normalized to the weight of the tissue.

Liver histology

In Study 2, 3–4 µm thick sections were cut from formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded liver samples and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). The stained slides were scanned on a whole slide image scanner (Hamamatsu S60) at 20×. The images were analyzed for the presence of steatosis via an in-house algorithm and VIS software (Visiopharm). The steatosis fraction was calculated as the area of steatosis/area of liver tissue × 100.

High-throughput qPCR of liver samples

The method and the liver gene panel used in Study 2 have previously been published [36]. Briefly, 8 × diluted cDNA from 17 liver samples (HM-VD, n = 8 and HM-CS, n = 9) was preamplified for 17 cycles via Fluidigm Preamp Master Mix according to the manufacturer’s protocol (Fluidigm, San Francisco, CA) and run in duplicate on a 96.96 Dynamic Array on a Fluidigm Biomark (Fluidigm). Calibration curves generated from pooled preamplified samples were used to determine primer efficiency (accepted efficiencies of 80–107%), and the sequences of primers used are available in supplementary methods. The data were analyzed via Genex 6 (MultiD Analyses AB, Gothenburg, Sweden) as previously described, with the exception that the data were normalized to 8 reference genes (Actb, Hprt, Pgk1, Ppia, Rplp2, Sdha, Tuba, and Ywhaz) [37]. Data were considered significant if the fold change between groups was ± 1.5 and p < 0.05.

RNA sequencing of epididymal white adipose tissue

The eWAT used in Study 2 was collected in RNAlater, transferred to 1 mL of QIAzol (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany), homogenized on a Tissuelyzer II using 5 mm stainless steel beads (Qiagen) for 2 × 2 min at 20 Hz, and then centrifuged for 5 min at 12,000 × g and 4 °C to manually remove the fat layer via a pipette tip. The RNA was purified via a RNeasy Lipid Tissue Mini Kit (Qiagen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, except that the chloroform was replaced with 1-bromo-3-chloropropane (Sigma‒Aldrich, Merck, Darmstadt, Germany). The RNA concentration and purity were measured via a DeNovix DS-11 FX + spectrophotometer, and RNA integrity was assessed via a 1.4% agarose gel. The RNA was sent to the commercial sequencing service at Novogene (Beijing, China) for RNA-seq library preparation, Illumina sequencing and bioinformatics processing of the raw reads via an in-house pipeline. Differential gene expression analysis was performed in R v4.3.2 via DESeq2 v1.40.2. Gene enrichment analysis was performed via the String app in Cytoscape v3.10.0 on all differentially expressed genes using the total list of genes from the countable as background, and enrichment plots were generated as previously described [38].

16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing

The gut microbial composition was evaluated by nanopore-based 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing in fecal samples collected from CS- and VD-delivered mice at weaning (Study 1) and at the time of euthanization of the VD and CS microbiome mice after 16 weeks on HFD ± HMO (Studies 2 + 3). See supplementary methods for detailed information on DNA extraction, PCR amplification of 16S rRNA, and sequencing using the Oxford Nanopore PromethION 2 Solo platform (Oxford Nanopore Technologies, Oxford, UK).

Metagenomic analysis

Metagenomic data analysis was performed on fecal samples from 5-week-old VD and CS microbiome mice before the start of HFD (Study 2). See supplementary methods for detailed information.

Metabolomics

Serum samples from the extra VD and CS microbiome mice euthanized at 4 weeks of age (n = 10 per group, Study 2) were profiled for untargeted metabolomics via an ultra-performance liquid chromatography-quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrometer (Premier QTOF MS, Waters, Manchester, UK) in both positive and negative ionization modes following a protocol previously described [39]. The obtained raw data were imported into R and processed with the XCMS [40] and CAMERA [41] packages. All MS features in each ionization mode were visualized via a PCA score plot, and the difference in the abundance of MS features between the two groups was tested by comparing two linear models with and without intervention. The p values obtained were further corrected by the false discovery rate (FDR) within each ionization mode. MS spectra for FDR significant MS features were searched for m/z against the human metabolome database (HMDB) [42], and fragmentation patterns and retention times were further manually matched to an in-house database of chemical standards for metabolite annotation.

SCFA analysis

The cecum contents were sampled from the extra VD and CS microbiome of mice euthanized at 4 weeks of age (Study 2) and stored on dry ice at − 80 °C until analysis. The SCFA concentration was measured by gas chromatography to assess bacterial fermentation and the metabolic activity of the gut microbiota, as previously described [43].

TaqMan qPCR of the colon and ileum digesta

RNA purification of ileum and colon digesta samples from Study 2, cDNA synthesis and qPCR were performed as previously described [29]. Briefly, RNA was purified with a MagMax-96 RNA Isolation Kit (Applied Biosystems, Waltham, MA) on the MagMax Express Magnetic Particle Processor (Applied Biosystems) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The RNA purity and concentration were assessed via a NanoDrop ND-1000 (NanoDrop Technologies), and the RNA integrity was assessed via a 1,4% agarose gel. cDNA was synthesized via the High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcriptase Kit (Applied Biosystems), and qPCR was performed via the TaqMan Fast universal advanced PCR Mastermix (Applied Biosystems) and the following TaqMan probes: Mm03024075_m1 (Hprt, reference gene), Mm01352366_m1 (Sdha, reference gene), Mm00449604_m1 (Muc1), Mm01276696_m1 (Muc2), Mm00500912_m1 (Ocld), Mm00493699_m1 (Tjp1), Mm00517635_m1 (Cldn15), Mm00516972_s1 (Cldn8), Mm00441127_m1 (Reg3g), Mm00445845_m1 (Retn1b), Mm00448160_m1 (Pla2g2a), Mm00520715_m1 (Pyy), Mm04205962_gH (Defa3), Mm00651548_g1 (Defa5).

Bacteroides fragilis colonization experiments for multiplex and qPCR analyses

Additional CS microbiome mice (Study 2) were co-colonized with Bacteroides fragilis to verify whether specific differences between HM-VD and HM-CS were driven by differences in the abundances of these taxa. Pregnant HM-CS mothers were co-colonized with B. fragilis NCTC9343 by oral gavage of 50 μL of 1010 colony CFU/mL, and their pups were also given 20 μL of the same suspension at days 0, 7, and 21 by dripping it into their mouth together with the CS microbiota inoculum. The female BALB/c pups continued on the irradiated Altromin 1314 breeding diet (Brogaarden) until they were euthanized at 8 weeks of age and were compared with VD and CS human microbiome-colonized female mice of the same age. Finally, germ-free male and female BALB/c pups on the irradiated Altromin 1314 breeding diet were likewise mono-colonized with the same bacteria, euthanized at 10 weeks of age and compared with germ-free mice. Serum was collected for multiplex analysis, and colon RNA was collected later for qPCR from both B. fragilis colonization experiments. These analyses were performed as described in the related sections.

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism version 10 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA) was used for statistical analysis of the clinical outputs, cytokines and histopathology. Significant differences between the CS and VD groups or between the germ-free and B. fragilis mono-colonized groups were tested in all studies by Student’s t test for parametric data and the Mann‒Whitney U test for nonparametric data. For data with several measurements from the same animal (BW, FC, OGTT), the groups were compared via two-way repeated measures models. In the study with three HM-VD, HM-CS, and CS-BF groups on a breeding diet, the groups were compared via ordinary one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s multiple comparisons. p values < 0.05 were considered significant. The effect of litter/cage was first taken into account with a two-way ANOVA with cage and group as factors, but cage effects were not evident in any of the analyses likely due to housing in open cages in both the barrier facility and isolators. For the high-throughput datasets (16S rRNA gene amplicon, metagenomic, RNA-sequencing, metabolomic, and high-throughput qPCR analyses), correction for multiple testing was applied using the false discovery rate (FDR), as detailed in the corresponding method sections. Further statistical analyses used for the high-throughput data are given in each specific related method section. Breeding mice were randomly allocated to the different groups (i.e., isolators in Study 2), and samples were randomized in all lab analyses. Outcome assessments that involved subjective evaluation, including histological scoring of liver steatosis, were performed by an operator blinded to group allocation.

Results

Birth by CS had minor effects on HFD-induced obesity in mice

To examine the effects of CS on obesity and metabolic dysfunction in mice, offspring that were delivered by CS or born by VD were fed a HFD from weaning (Study 1). The mice weighed the same amount at weaning, i.e., 4 weeks of age, when HFD feeding started (Fig. 1B), despite a minor difference of 0.16 g in birth weight between all the VD (1.36 g ± 0.39 g, mean ± SD) and the CS-delivered mice (1.2 ± 0.11 g, mean ± SD), as expected (p = 0.02, not shown). During the first 10 weeks on the HFD, the CS mice fed the HFD gained significantly more weight than the VD mice did, although the difference was minor (Fig. 1B). The CS-treated mice weighed, on average, 6% more than the VD-treated mice did during the 10-week period. In contrast, there was no effect of delivery mode on weight in the LFD groups, except for the difference in birth weight, which remained until 6 weeks of age (Figure S1B). The CS-delivered pups weighed on average 12% less than the VD pups did during the first 6 weeks of life until they caught up in weight. Weekly food consumption measured at weaning was not significantly different between the CS and VD groups in either the HFD (Fig. 1C) or LFD groups (Figure S1C).

To investigate the effect of delivery mode on metabolic dysfunction, an OGTT and serum sampling were performed on the HFD- or LFD-fed mice after 11 weeks. Birth via CS did not affect glucose tolerance regardless of diet (Figs. 1D and S1D). The fasting glucose levels, serum insulin levels and serum leptin levels were also similar in the VD and CS groups fed both diets (Figs. 1D–F and S1D-F), except for a significantly lower level of serum insulin in the CS-delivered mice than in the VD mice fed LFD (Figure S1E). Furthermore, low-grade inflammation was assessed by measuring cytokine levels in eWAT. Most of the proinflammatory cytokines and chemokines were unaffected by delivery mode, but the concentrations of the multifaceted cytokine IL-6 and the chemokines CXCL-1 and CXCL-10 were significantly greater in the CS mice than in the VD mice fed a HFD, indicating a minor increase in the inflammatory response in the adipose tissue (Fig. 1G). These differences were not detected in the LFD groups, in which the CS mice instead presented lower levels of IL-5 and MIP-2α in eWAT than did the VD mice (Figure S1G).

Delivery mode had no effect on the gut microbiota in the microbiota-transplanted mice

We have previously shown that mice born by CS experience intestinal dysbiosis, which partly resembles the gut microbiota differences observed in human infants related to birth mode [28]. Interestingly, we were not able to replicate our own findings in the current Study 1. The fecal gut microbiota clustered similarly in the principal coordinate analysis plot (p = 0.17; Fig. 1H), and the microbiota richness, i.e., alpha diversity, was similar between the groups at weaning (Fig. 1I), likely due to the improved fostering method of rubbing the CS-delivered pups with the foster mothers’ pups before transfer, which was performed to increase acceptance by the mothers and the survival of the pups. The new procedure may very well mimic an unintended microbiota transplant at birth and thus seem to have restored the microbiome in the CS-delivered pups.

The human CS microbiome enhanced the metabolic phenotype in HFD-fed mice

To determine whether CS-induced dysbiosis regulates obesity and metabolic dysfunction, we instead transplanted human microbiota from 1-month-old VD- and CS-delivered infants into germ-free mice and tested the response of the offspring to a HFD (Study 2). All recipient mice were vaginally delivered, and yet, the CS microbiome mice gained significantly more weight on a HFD (Fig. 2B). During the 16 weeks on the HFD, the CS microbiome mice gained 70% of their weight on average, whereas the VD microbiome mice only gained 50%. Interestingly, weight gain was accompanied by significantly impaired glucose tolerance (Fig. 2C). Blood glucose levels were significantly higher in the OGTT in the CS microbiome mice than in the VD microbiome mice. This metabolic phenotypic trait was supported by significantly increased levels of serum insulin, adipokine B-cell-activating factor (BAFF), and hepatokine fibroblast growth factor (FGF)−21 in the CS microbiome mice (Fig. 2D). The concentrations of IL-5, IL-6, and IL-10 were also significantly greater in the serum of the CS microbiome mice than in that of the VD microbiome mice (Fig. 2E). In eWAT, the concentrations of IL-1β and the chemokine CXCL-1 were increased, and the Th1 cytokines IL-12p70 and IL-2 were decreased in the CS microbiome mice (Fig. 2F). Compared with the VD microbiome, the CS microbiome thus seemed to favor a Th2-skewed immune response in the mice. Liver pathology was also assessed, but we detected no difference in the degree of steatosis among the mice (Fig. 2G, H). However, several genes were differentially expressed in the liver between the two groups. While genes related to cholesterol metabolism Klb, Csad, and lipogenesis Srebf1 were significantly downregulated, genes related to inflammation Lcn2, Saa2, and Tlr12 were significantly upregulated in the CS microbiome mice compared with the VD microbiome mice (Fig. 2I).

Finally, gene expression in the adipose tissue was assessed via RNA sequencing, which revealed several genes related to metabolism that were differentially expressed between the CS and VD microbiome mice (Fig. 3A). Differential gene expression analysis yielded 150 differentially expressed genes with adjusted p values < 0.05 out of 24,472 expressed transcripts. There was significant enrichment in pathways related to insulin resistance, insulin/glucagon signaling pathways, the AMPK signaling pathway and lipid transport genes (Fig. 3B–J). AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) plays a key role in regulating lipid metabolism, insulin sensitivity, and energy balance. Notably, the expression of the differentially expressed gene Srebf1 was downregulated in adipose tissue from the CS microbiome mice (Fig. 3D), whereas Saa2 was upregulated (Fig. 3I), similar to what was observed in the liver (Fig. 2I). Human milk oligosaccharides reduced microbial differences between the human VD and CS microbiome mice and partly restored the metabolic phenotype

The CS and VD microbiome mice were fed a HFD supplemented with HMOs to test whether HMO could restore the microbial dysbiosis of the CS microbiome mice, resulting in beneficial effects on the enhanced metabolic phenotype (Study 3). The fecal gut microbiota composition was therefore analyzed with 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing at the end of both Studies 2 and 3 from all four groups (VD and CS microbiome mice with and without HMOs in the diet). There was no difference in microbiota richness, i.e. alpha diversity, between the VD and CS microbiome mice regardless of HMO supplementation (Fig. 4A), but a lower number of observed ASVs was evident in the VD microbiome mice fed an HMO-supplemented diet than in the VD group without HMOs. PCoA revealed a clear separation between the VD and CS microbiome mice, which shifted upon HMO supplementation (PERMANOVA: p = 0.001, F = 18.82; Fig. 4B). The VD and CS groups still clustered separately from each other on the HMO diet, but both groups now resembled the VD microbiome control group without HMOs rather than the CS microbiome group (PERMANOVA: p = 0.001, F = 35.38). In particular, a significantly greater abundance of Gammaproteobacteria, which mainly consists of the family Enterobacteriaceae, and Staphylococcaceae, were observed in both the CS donor (Fig. 4D) and CS microbiome mouse samples (Fig. 4E) compared to the VD microbiome mouse samples, but this difference was not evident in the HMO-supplemented VD and CS microbiome mice (Fig. 4 F, G). Restoration of the CS microbiome by B. fragilis reversed the effects on FGF-21 but not other metabolic mediators

Additional CS microbiome mice from Study 2 were co-colonized with B. fragilis to restore one of the largest differences in the gut microbiota between the two groups. The mice were maintained on the breeding diet to separate microbiota-mediated differences from obesity-driven changes, and the metabolic mediators previously found to be different in HFD-induced obese mice were then measured via multiplex analysis. BAFF and FGF-21 concentrations were not different between the VD and CS microbiome-fed mice on the regular diet, but B. fragilis significantly elevated the serum BAFF and reduced the serum FGF-21 levels compared with those in the VD and CS microbiome mice (Fig. 5E, F). To verify the results and clarify whether the effect was in fact mediated by B. fragilis itself, germ-free mice were mono-colonized with B. fragilis, and the same analytes were measured in the serum. Similarly, B. fragilis tended to increase serum BAFF (p = 0.07) and significantly reduced FGF-21 compared with those in germ-free mice (Fig. 5G, H).

Microbiome-associated changes in colonic gene expression related to gut epithelial and barrier functions

To evaluate whether gut barrier function was affected, the expression of genes related to intestinal integrity and epithelial function was measured in the VD and CS microbiome mice in Study 2. The expression of the barrier-related genes Muc2 and Retn1b, encoding an antimicrobial peptide, was increased in the colons of the CS microbiome mice (Fig. 5I). We also measured serum endotoxin levels to test whether intestinal permeability was affected by the CS microbiome, but there was no significant difference between the two microbiome-associated groups, with a tendency (p = 0.07) toward higher endotoxin levels in the VD microbiome mice (Fig. 5J). We thus concluded that the gut barrier was largely unaffected.

Moreover, the Pyy gene, encoding the satiety hormone peptide tyrosine tyrosine (PYY), which reduces appetite, was lower in the colons of the CS microbiome mice than in those of the VD microbiome mice (Fig. 5I). Pyy gene expression was also reduced in the CS microbiome mice when their diet was supplemented with HMOs (Fig. 5K). However, the effect of the CS microbiome on colonic Pyy expression was not evident in the mice on the breeding diet (Fig. 5L), and B. fragilis had no effect on colonic Pyy expression in the two CS microbiome groups (Fig. 5L) or in the germ-free mice (Fig. 5M). Hence, the effect of the CS microbiome on colonic Pyy expression may be driven by other bacteria that remained different between the groups on HMO diets.

CS-induced dysbiosis altered intestinal microbial pathways and systemic metabolites

Fecal samples were collected from the VD and CS microbiome mice in Study 2 just before they were fed a HFD to investigate which microbial pathways were affected by delivery mode. The functional profiles from whole-genome sequencing of fecal samples from 5-week-old male CS and VD human microbiome mice fed a regular breeding diet before starting a HFD were significantly different according to principal component analysis (Fig. 6A). Differential functional pathways included, for example, genes involved in nitrogen metabolism, SCFA and alcohol conversion, different types of ATPases and energy cycles (Fig. 6B). We therefore collected cecum samples from additional CS and VD microbiome mice on the breeding diet, which were euthanized at weaning, at 4 weeks of age, and subjected them to SCFA analysis. In support of the results of the metagenomic analysis, the levels of several of the SCFAs differed between the groups. N-Butyric acid (17.8-fold), acetic acid (1.7-fold) and DL-Lactic acid (undetected in the VD group) were more abundant, whereas propionic acid (14.5-fold) and succinic acid (90.7-fold) were less abundant in the CS microbiome mice compared to the VD microbiome mice (Fig. 6C–G). Finally, metabolomic analysis of serum from the same 4-week-old mice also revealed a clear separation of the metabolomic profiles between the two groups (Fig. 6H, I). A total of 1523 metabolites were detected, and while the CS microbiome mice presented increased levels of indoleactic acid (2.4-fold), isovalerylcarnitine (2.6-fold), and pantothenic acid (2.6-fold), the VD microbiome mice presented increased levels of cholic acid (10.7-fold) and lysophosphatidylcholine 16:0 (2.2-fold) (Fig. 6J–N). Discussion

An increasing number of studies point to CS birth as a risk factor for subsequent metabolic disorders. Delivery mode is an important factor driving neonatal gut microbiota composition, and here, we provide evidence that alterations in the gut microbiota due to CS birth increase the risk of weight gain, glucose intolerance and changes in various molecular pathways related to metabolic disorders when transplanted into germ-free mice. CS-delivered mice have previously been shown to gain more weight than VD-delivered mice on a regular chow diet [26]. However, it is important to note that CS-delivered mice are generally lower in birth weight when delivered ½–1 day before term, as also observed in the present study, and will naturally gain more weight, whereas the adult body weight may not differ. Accordingly, we did not observe any difference in the adult weight of the mice born by CS or VD in our previous studies, where the mice were fed a regular chow diet [28]. In the present study, CS delivery had only a minimal effect on weight gain when the mice were fed a HFD and had no effect on the metabolic phenotype, likely due to missing differences in the gut microbiota. When another approach was followed by transplanting diverging microbiomes from human infants discordant to birth mode into germ-free mice, the metabolic consequences were pronounced. Mice colonized with the CS microbiota exhibited increased body weight, glucose intolerance, hyperinsulinemia, and elevated circulating IL-6, consistent with the low-grade inflammatory state commonly associated with obesity and insulin resistance [44]. These phenotypic changes were accompanied by altered metabolic regulation across multiple metabolic tissues. Although liver histology did not reveal increased lipid accumulation, gene expression profiles in both liver and adipose tissue suggested altered lipid turnover and enhanced inflammatory signaling. Specifically, gene expression of serum amyloid A and Tlr12 was increased in both tissues, consistent with previous associations between these genes, obesity, and impaired glucose homeostasis [45, 46].

Several circulating immune and endocrine mediators that regulate diet-induced obesity and metabolism were also found to be elevated in the CS microbiome mice. BAFF, a TNF-family ligand that regulates weight gain and lipid metabolism in adipocytes [47] and is elevated in HFD-fed mice [48], was increased in CS microbiome mice alongside IL-10, which can stimulate BAFF release from macrophages [49]. mice also presented increased levels of FGF-21, another mediator of glucose and lipid metabolism primarily secreted by metabolic tissues [50]. Several of the same genes that were differentially expressed in the livers of the CS microbiome mice are also regulated by FGF-21 treatment in mice [37], suggesting a possible link between elevated FGF-21 and altered hepatocyte function in the CS microbiome mice. These include beta-klotho (Klb), Srebp1, and Lcn2, which are also differentially expressed in obese individuals [51–53]. The effects of FGF-21 on liver integrity are partially mediated by the gut microbiota [54], and conversely, gut bacteria can modulate FGF-21 and Klb expression [55]; in particular, Bacteroides acidifaciens has been shown to regulate host FGF-21 and lipid metabolism in HFD-induced obese mice [56].

We therefore tested whether the largest difference between CS and VD donors, in Bacteroides spp., could account for the FGF-21 and BAFF changes. B. fragilis supplementation of CS microbiome mice, as well as mono-colonization of germ-free mice, reduced serum FGF-21, indicating a direct effect of this taxon. In contrast, the BAFF increase observed under HFD was absent in chow-fed mice, suggesting that the BAFF response is more likely a downstream consequence of the obese phenotype than a direct B. fragilis effect.

Several taxonomic shifts reported in obese individuals, including enrichment of Blautia and Ruminococcus spp. (Firmicutes), depletion of Bacteroides, Alistipes, and Parabacteroides spp. (Bacteroidetes), and a higher Firmicutes:Bacteroidetes ratio [57–60], were reproduced in our HFD-induced obese mice but also persisted in HMO-fed mice, in which the obesity response was no longer different between the VD and CS microbiome mice. Nevertheless, the increased Firmicutes:Bacteroidetes ratio paralleled persistent alterations in glucose tolerance and low-grade inflammation in HMO-fed mice.

In contrast to the Firmicutes:Bacteroidetes ratio, the high abundance of Gammaproteobacteria in the CS donor and CS microbiome mice, specifically the order Enterobacterales, was significantly reduced by HMO treatment and no longer differed between the two HMO-fed groups. Enterobacteriaceae can abnormally proliferate in the gut and has been found systemically in obese individuals [57, 61, 62]. These bacteria also represent an important source of active endotoxin, which can impair intestinal permeability, allowing the increased passage of inflammatory mediators and bacterial metabolites into the circulation, thus promoting low-grade inflammation, a potential cause of metabolic diseases [63]. Nonetheless, we did not observe any such effects on the intestinal expression of genes related to intestinal barrier integrity or serum endotoxin levels, suggesting other bacteria and mechanisms at play.

The gut microbiota regulates PYY via bile acids and SCFA-mediated stimulation of enteroendocrine L cells [64, 65]. Specifically, propionate, significantly reduced in CS microbiome mice, stimulates PYY secretion from colonic crypts, and PYY also increases insulin sensitivity [66, 67], potentially contributing to the impaired glucose tolerance and elevated insulin in CS microbiome mice on both diets. Although Bacteroidetes are well-known propionate producers, B. fragilis did not regulate PYY in the present study and is therefore unlikely to be responsible for the reduced Pyy expression. Instead, several other propionate producers, including Peptoniphilus spp. (Firmicutes) [68], were reduced in the CS microbiome mice regardless of diet and could potentially be involved. Firmicutes can produce propionate from succinate-pathway intermediates, and notably, genes in this pathway were differentially expressed between CS and VD microbiomes, while succinic acid was nearly depleted in CS microbiome mice. Succinic acid can directly stimulate lipolysis and limit adipose tissue expansion in HFD-induced obesity [69]. As an intermediate in microbial fiber fermentation [70], succinate may represent an important link between microbiome composition and host metabolism. Thus, the reduction of succinate-producing bacteria in the CS microbiome could impair mitochondrial succinate dehydrogenase activity and efficiency, consistent with our observations in CS microbiome mice, leading to reduced ATP production, increased reactive oxygen species generation, and metabolic dysfunction [71]. These effects may involve altered AMPK signaling in adipose tissue, although dedicated functional validation in future studies is needed to establish this potential link.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, although donors were carefully matched for feeding mode and CS microbial score, the limited number of donors means that donor-specific effects cannot be fully excluded, and the transplanted communities may not capture the full diversity of CS- and VD-associated human microbiota. Second, the recipient mice reproduced the major taxonomic differences distinguishing the donor groups, indicating successful transfer of the key microbiome features under investigation. However, a comprehensive quantitative donor-recipient comparison was not performed because donor and recipient microbiomes were generated and processed using different sequencing and analytical pipelines, preventing a direct quantitative comparison of community composition. Given known host-specific constraints on human-to-mouse engraftment, recipient microbiotas are not expected to fully recapitulate the human donor communities [72, 73]; rather, the critical consideration is whether the defining differences between donor groups are retained, which was confirmed in the present study. An additional consideration relates to the temporal exposure represented by the model. Donors were selected only if their microbiome profiles remained characteristic of their birth mode at 1 year of age, ensuring a representation of stable birth-mode-associated communities. However, because recipient mice were colonized from gestation onwards and microbial communities in gnotobiotic isolators are generally stable once established [74], the model assesses the effects of lifelong exposure to birth-mode-associated microbiota, unlike the human situation where CS-associated microbial alterations are most pronounced during early life.

Finally, the HFD itself may have altered microbiota composition and potentially modified some inter-group differences over time. Longitudinal microbiome profiling during dietary intervention would therefore strengthen interpretation of the observed metabolic phenotypes, and we highlight this as an explicit caveat and a priority for future work.

Conclusions

CS birth is increasingly recognized as a risk factor for metabolic disorders. The central finding of this work is that the CS-associated gut dysbiosis, rather than CS birth per se, drives the metabolic phenotype: CS birth in mice without an accompanying shift in the gut microbiota had minimal metabolic consequences, whereas transfer of the CS-associated human microbiota was sufficient to enhance diet-induced obesity and insulin resistance. Importantly, modulation of this microbiota through HMO supplementation partially ameliorated the metabolic phenotype and restored specific microbial taxa, demonstrating that these effects are, at least in part, reversible. Mechanistically, Bacteroides fragilis, a taxon depleted in the CS donor microbiota, directly reduced circulating FGF-21 levels, implicating this bacterium in the regulation of host metabolism, while alterations in PYY-related enteroendocrine signaling and microbial metabolites suggest additional pathways that may link the CS-associated microbiota to metabolic dysfunction. It is important to note that humanized gnotobiotic mouse models reproduce only part of the human gut ecosystem, and metabolic phenotypes observed after transfer into germ-free mice fed a high-fat diet (HFD) should therefore be extrapolated to human disease with caution. Nonetheless, these findings strongly suggest that the CS-associated gut microbiota is a key mediator of obesity and metabolic disease, and a promising target for preventing the long-term metabolic consequences of CS birth.
