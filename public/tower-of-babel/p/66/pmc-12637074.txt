A dual-enzyme-like nanozyme adjuvant alleviates tumor hypoxia to enhance photothermally ferroptosis-immune synergistic tumor therapy
Authors: Peng Chang, Dehong Chen, Xiaoyu Yang, Kuoye Tian, Ying Liu, Ke Li, Dan Chen, Wei Wang, Qi Pan, Yun Zeng, Yonghua Zhan, Wenhua Zhan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Immune adjuvants play a critical role in cancer vaccines and emerging therapies. However, conventional adjuvants may fail to effectively stimulate cytotoxic T lymphocyte (CTL)-mediated immune responses. Nanozyme-based adjuvants, particularly manganese-based nanozymes, demonstrate significant potential by integrating enzymatic activity with immune modulation. Herein, we developed a dual-enzyme-like nanozyme adjuvant (Mn3O4&MLT@PDA-AS1411, MMPA) through the integration of Mn3O4 nanozyme and melatonin (MLT) using polydopamine, which alleviates tumor hypoxia to enhance photothermally augmented ferroptosis-immune synergistic therapy. The ferroptosis is cooperatively enhanced by Mn3O4 nanozyme-mediated reactive oxygen species generation and glutathione depletion, synergizing with MLT-induced HIF-1α suppression. Concurrently, HIF-1α inhibition and ferroptosis activation cooperate to suppress HSP70 expression, thereby amplifying the mild photothermal therapy and synergistically inducing immunogenic cell death. Furthermore, the degradation product of MMPA, Mn2+, activates the cGAS-STING pathway and promotes dendritic cell maturation and CTL infiltration. In vivo studies demonstrate that this strategy effectively alleviates tumor hypoxia, resulting in the complete suppression of distant tumors in a metastatic model. This approach also first explores the promotion of ferroptosis and immune activation of MLT, providing an innovative strategy for multi-mechanism synergistic treatment.

Introduction

As a core tool in immunotherapy, immune adjuvants play a crucial role in tumor immunotherapy by enhancing antigen-specific immune responses [[1], [2], [3]]. However, conventional immune adjuvants, such as aluminum salts, primarily work by activating humoral immunity and are ineffective in promoting antigen cross-presentation, making it difficult to stimulate cytotoxic T lymphocyte (CTL) immune responses and trigger anti-tumor cellular immune responses. Moreover, traditional immune adjuvants typically require the use of expensive vaccine antigens in combination with them, which complicates the treatment regimen and significantly increases therapeutic costs [[4], [5], [6], [7]]. Therefore, there is an urgent need for a novel adjuvant that effectively boosts the CTL immune responses. Nanozyme-based adjuvants offer a novel approach to next-generation immune adjuvants, characterized by their ease of construction, cost-effectiveness, and effective anti-tumor properties. Notably, they can robustly stimulate CTL responses and effectively trigger anti-tumor cellular immunity [[8], [9], [10], [11]]. Among these, manganese-based nanozyme adjuvants perform well, and their inherent stimulator of interferon genes (STING) agonist properties can directly activate the cGAS-STING immune signaling pathway, promoting dendritic cell (DC) maturation and antigen cross-presentation [12,13]. More importantly, their multiple enzyme activities enable the catalytic conversion of endogenous H2O2 into hydroxyl radicals (·OH), while depleting glutathione (GSH), thereby promoting the accumulation of lipid peroxidation and ultimately inducing ferroptosis, a programmed cell death pathway driven by lipid peroxidation [14,15]. A large number of studies have demonstrated that ferroptosis can effectively stimulate anti-tumor immune responses by inducing immunogenic cell death (ICD), releasing damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) to activate DCs and recruit CTLs [[16], [17], [18]]. However, the unfavorable catalytic conditions in the tumor microenvironment (TME) severely limit the reactivity of nanozymes, inhibit the ferroptosis process, and consequently weaken the immune-activating effect of nanozyme adjuvants [19,20]. Therefore, enhancing the catalytic activity of nanozyme adjuvants effectively is critical to induce ferroptosis and activate immune responses.

The temperature is one of the most critical factors affecting the catalytic activity of nanozymes [[21], [22], [23]]. The Arrhenius equation demonstrates that increasing temperature can accelerate reaction kinetics, which reflects that photothermal therapy (PTT) serves as an ideal supportive manner to provide exogenous driving force for nanozyme adjuvants [24,25], which can not only directly kill tumor cells, but also significantly enhance the catalytic activity of nanozyme adjuvants, promoting reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation and accelerating GSH depletion to induce ferroptosis [26,27]. Notably, PTT itself can also cause mitochondrial damage in tumor cells, trigger ICD, rupture tumor cell membranes, and release tumor-associated antigens, thereby enhancing anti-tumor immune responses [28,29]. Therefore, the photothermally enhanced ferroptosis-immune synergistic therapy mediated by nanozyme adjuvants can not only achieve the efficacy of vaccine antigens but also activate a strong anti-tumor immune response. However, the hypoxic TME persistently upregulates the expression of hypoxia-inducible factor-1α (HIF-1α), enhances the capacity of tumor cells to clear lipid peroxidation, and inhibits ferroptosis [30,31]. Concurrently, HIF-1α upregulates heat shock proteins (HSPs) to form a thermal resistance mechanism, severely weakening photothermally ferroptosis-immune synergistic tumor therapy [32,33].

Current strategies to inhibit tumor HIF-1α expression are primarily categorized into two main approaches: oxygen-generating nanoplatforms and small-molecule HIF-1α inhibitors [34]. Although oxygen-generating nanoplatforms can increase the local oxygen partial pressure within a short period, the duration of the increase in oxygen partial pressure is short, and it is difficult to truly and effectively suppress HIF-1α expression [35]. Small-molecule HIF-1α inhibitors can directly block HIF-1α and its downstream signaling pathways, allowing for long-term inhibition of HIF-1α activity even in the presence of hypoxia within the TME [36]. Recent studies have demonstrated that melatonin (MLT), a naturally occurring endogenous indoleamine hormone, can effectively alleviate tumor hypoxia by inducing mitochondrial dysfunction and inhibiting the HIF-1α signaling pathway. However, due to the lack of tumor targeting by small-molecule inhibitors, MLT is easily metabolized after systemic administration, resulting in ineffective concentrations at tumor sites, which severely limits its therapeutic effectiveness [37,38]. Chen et al. developed a triphenylphosphonium-coupled MLT (Mito-Mel) to give MLT targeting function. Compared with free MLT, Mito-Mel significantly enhanced ROS generation and caused damage to the mitochondrial membrane potential, ultimately downregulating HIF-1α expression and inhibiting its efficacy in reversing tumor hypoxia [39]. Inspired by this, the development of a nanoplatform that can accurately deliver MLT and nanozyme adjuvants may effectively inhibit HIF-1α expression, enabling photothermally enhanced ferroptosis-immune synergistic tumor therapy.

Herein, we designed a dual-enzyme-like nanozyme adjuvant (Mn3O4&MLT@PDA-AS1411, MMPA) by integrating MLT and a Mn3O4 nanozyme wrapped in polydopamine (PDA), which enables tumor hypoxia alleviation to enhance photothermally augmented ferroptosis-immune synergistic therapy. Mn3O4 nanozyme-mediated peroxidase (POD)-like and glutathione peroxidase (GPx)-like activities of ROS generation and GSH depletion, as well as MLT-mediated HIF-1α suppression, synergized to induce cell ferroptosis. Concurrently, HIF-1α inhibition and ferroptosis activation cooperatively suppressed HSP70 expression, enhancing the mild PTT and synergistically inducing ICD. Furthermore, the degradation product of MMPA, Mn2+, which acts as a STING agonist, activates the cGAS-STING pathway, promotes DC maturation and CTL infiltration, thereby amplifying ICD-mediated systemic anti-tumor immunity. In the metastatic tumor model, MMPA combined with PTT achieved complete inhibition of both primary and distant tumors by activating anti-tumor immune responses. Notably, this study discovered for the first time the role of MLT in the ferroptosis pathway and immune activation, which not only expanded the therapeutic potential of MLT in tumors but also provided a practical new approach for the multi-mechanism synergistic treatment of hypoxic solid tumors (Scheme 1).Scheme 1Schematic illustration of MMPA adjuvant synthesis and its alleviation of tumor hypoxia to enhance photothermally ferroptosis-immune synergistic tumor therapy.Scheme 1

Results and discussion

Preparation and characterization of MMPA adjuvant

A one-pot method was employed to integrate Mn3O4 nanozymes, MLT, and dopamine (DA) into Mn3O4&MLT@PDA (MMP). The thiol-modified AS1411 aptamer was conjugated to the surface of the nanoplatforms through a reaction with PDA. AS1411 is a well-characterized DNA oligonucleotide that forms a stable G-quadruplex structure and exhibits high binding affinity for nucleolin, a protein overexpressed on the surface of lung cancer cells, thereby enabling the MMPA to specifically target lung cancer cells [40,41]. Spherical MMPA with diameters of approximately 110 nm were successfully synthesized, in which Mn3O4 nanozymes were uniformly distributed in a honeycomb-like structure within the nanoplatform, as demonstrated in the transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images shown in Fig. 1a and b. The energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) elemental mapping of divalent Mn (Fig. 1c) further confirmed the successful synthesis of the nanoplatform. Meanwhile, by analyzing the characteristic UV–Vis spectra of Mn3O4 nanozyme, MLT, MMP, and MMPA, it was confirmed that PDA effectively loaded both MLT and Mn3O4 NPs. Photothermal effects of MMPA adjuvant

The photothermal effect of the nanoplatform is crucial for achieving localized mild-temperature PTT in tumors. Therefore, we investigated the in vitro photothermal effect of MMPA adjuvant. Fig. 1h shows the photothermal profiles of PBS, MPA, and MMPA under 808 nm laser irradiation at 2.0 W cm−2. The temperatures of PBS, MPA, and MMPA increased by 1.6, 43.2, and 45.4 °C, respectively. Fig. S2a and S2b display the photothermal curves of MMPA exposed to varying powers of laser light and different concentrations of MMPA exposed to an 808 nm laser at 2.0 W cm−2, respectively, and the NIR thermal images are shown in Fig. S1c. Based on the temperature-changed curves after 808 nm laser irradiation (Fig. 1i), MMPA demonstrated excellent photothermal stability even after repeated heating. In addition, the photothermal conversion efficiency (PCE) of MMPA was calculated to be 51.3 %. Furthermore, to evaluate the in vivo photothermal treatment potential of MMPA and determine the optimal timing for photothermal treatment after injection of MMPA, we verified the in vivo photothermal performance of MMPA. As shown in Fig. S3, MMPA was injected into tumor-bearing mice via the tail vein. Laser irradiation was performed on the tumor sites of the mice at 0, 3, 6, and 12 h after injection. The results showed that the temperature increased most rapidly at the point of 6 h post-injection. Therefore, photothermal therapy was performed 6 h after administration. Based on this, Fig. S4 displays the results of the in vivo photothermal performance investigation. The temperature of the PBS group rose from 34.6 to 37.5 °C before irradiation and after 5 min of 808 nm laser irradiation; the temperature of the MMP group rose from 36.7 to 40.8 °C, and the temperature of the MMPA group rose from 37.3 to 45.3 °C. All these studies show that MMPA has excellent photothermal performance.

The controlled release of MLT from MMPA

Fig. S5 shows the standard curves of MLT. After the successful synthesis of MMPA, free MLT was separated from the nanoplatform by centrifugation, and the drug encapsulation efficiency (DEE) and drug loading content (DLC) of MLT were calculated to be 81.2 % and 7.6 %, respectively. The in vitro drug release curves of free MLT and MMPA are shown in Fig. 1j. With a release rate of more than 70 % after 12 h and 80 % after 24 h, free MLT exhibited the fastest drug release rate. PDA encapsulation successfully regulated MLT release, as MMPA was able to decrease the release rate by 16.5 % after 48 h.

In vitro Fenton-like and GPx-like activity of MMPA

Fig. 1k and l illustrate the Fenton-like activity of MMPA at varying H2O2 and Mn3O4 concentrations, respectively. The Fenton-like activities of MMPA increased significantly with increasing Mn3O4 concentrations. In addition, to further elucidate the effect of temperature on ·OH generation efficiency, we evaluated the Fenton-like activity of MMPA at different temperatures, as shown in Fig. S6. The absorbance at 665 nm at the end of the reaction at 43 °C was significantly lower than that at 25 °C, indicating that high temperature could substantially increase the efficiency of ·OH generation. These results suggest that MMPA has a good Fenton-like activity, which is essential for generating ROS storms in vivo.

Meanwhile, the GPx-like activity of MMPA was verified using the 5,5′-dithio-bis-2-nitrobenzoic acid (DTNB) assay. As shown in Fig. S7, MMPA significantly reduced GSH levels in a time-dependent manner, demonstrating its effective GPx-like catalytic activity.

In vitro magnetic resonance (MR) imaging of MMPA

To test the MR imaging contrast ability of MMPA, we used MMPA for comparison. As shown in Fig. S8a, the T1-weighted MR imaging signals of both the MMPA significantly increased with increasing Mn3O4 concentration. As shown in Fig. S8b, linear fitting was performed with 1000/T (s−1) as the vertical coordinate and MMPA concentration as the horizontal coordinate, and the relaxation rates of MMPA and MMPA incubated with 10 mM GSH solution were calculated to be 0.7464 and 2.771 mg−1 mL s−1, respectively, indicating that MMPA had a good MR imaging visualization ability and good responsiveness to GSH.

In vitro anti-tumor effects of MMPA

The cell viability after treatment with MLT, MPA, and MMPA was determined using the CCK-8 assay. As shown in Fig. 2b and c, within a specific concentration range, the cytotoxicity of MLT, MPA, and MMPA toward A549-HRE-LUC cells increased with higher concentrations. Subsequently, cell viability was evaluated under different material treatments and laser irradiation conditions. Fig. 2d demonstrates that laser irradiation alone exhibited negligible cytotoxicity, with cell survival rates in the MLT and MLT + Laser (L) groups remaining around 75 %. Due to the POD-like and GPx-like activities of Mn3O4 nanozyme, the cell survival rates in the MPA and MMPA groups decreased to 59.2 % and 50.2 %, respectively. Notably, cell viability in the MPA + L and MMPA + L groups dropped dramatically to 9.1 % and 3.2 %, respectively. Furthermore, similar cytotoxicity trends were observed in the LLC-HRE-LUC cell line (Fig. 2e, f, and 2g), indicating that the synergistic combination of Mn3O4 nanozyme, MLT, and PTT may serve as an effective therapeutic strategy.

Fig. 2h, S11, and S12 show the confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) images and quantitative data of MMPA labeled with IR676 after internalization by cells at 1, 3, 6, 9, and 12 h of incubation. The cell nuclei stained with DAPI emitted blue fluorescence, while the cytoplasm stained with IR676 emitted red fluorescence. With time, IR676 gradually accumulated in the cytoplasm, indicating that MMPA could be effectively delivered into cells and accumulate in the cytoplasm.

To illustrate the targeting effect of MMPA more clearly, Fig. 2i and S13 display IR676 fluorescence images and quantitative data for the PBS, MMP, and MMPA groups. Compared with that in the MMP group, the fluorescence intensity of IR676 in the MMPA group was significantly greater due to the increased expression of nucleolin on the cell surface of A549-HRE-LUC and LLC-HRE-LUC cells.

Calcein AM/PI dual fluorescence staining and flow cytometry were used to verify the toxicity of the nanoplatform to tumor cells, as shown in Fig. 2j. Cells in the PBS group emitted predominantly green fluorescence, regardless of whether laser irradiation was applied, indicating that no cytotoxicity was detected. Cells in the MLT and MLT + L groups exhibited slight cytotoxicity, and laser irradiation had a minimal effect on the cells in the MLT group. MPA and MMPA groups exhibited significant cytotoxicity, and MPA + L and MMPA + L groups showed significantly greater cytotoxicity, in which red fluorescence began to dominate, exhibiting extreme cytotoxicity after 808 nm laser irradiation was administrated to the cells, suggesting that the synergistic anti-tumor effect of MMPA with PTT can significantly increase the probability of cell death.

As shown in Fig. 2k and S14, we further verified the cytotoxicity using flow cytometry. Slight apoptotic and necrotic cell death was demonstrated in the MLT and MLT + L groups, whereas significantly greater cell deaths were observed in the MPA, MPA + L, MMPA, and MMPA + L groups. Notably, the MMPA + L group showed a considerably greater mortality rate than the MMPA group. These results also confirmed the results of the CCK-8 assay and Calcein AM/PI dual fluorescence staining, indicating that MMPA combined with PTT could effectively kill tumor cells in vitro [[42], [43], [44]].

MMPA-mediated activation of ferroptosis and potentiation of PTT in vitro

To clarify how MMPA combined with mild PTT effectively inhibits tumors, we conducted an in-depth investigation into their mechanisms of action. First, we verified the ability of the nanoplatform to alleviate tumor hypoxia. Subsequently, we hypothesized that the nanoplatform induces ferroptosis through both hypoxia alleviation and GSH depletion and also enhances the efficacy of PTT by suppressing HIF-1α expression, thereby achieving multi-pathway synergistic tumor suppression.

As shown in Fig. 3a–d, for the A549-HRE-LUC and LLC-HRE-LUC cell lines, we found that MLT could alleviate hypoxia, with the MMPA + L group exhibiting the most pronounced hypoxia-alleviating effect. We then further validated the effectiveness of hypoxia alleviation using Western blot. As demonstrated in Fig. 3e, effective inhibition of HIF-1α was achieved with increasing concentrations of MLT, starting from 200 μg mL−1, suggesting that MLT may inhibit tumor proliferation by suppressing HIF-1α expression. Next, we investigated whether the nanoplatform kills tumor cells via the ferroptosis pathway. As shown in Fig. 3g and h, except for the PBS group, the cell viability of all other groups increased to varying degrees after the addition of Fer-1. Notably, for the A549-HRE-LUC and LLC-HRE-LUC cell lines, the effect of Fer-1 on the MLT group was significantly higher under hypoxic conditions than under normoxic conditions, suggesting that MLT may enhance the tumor cell-killing efficacy of the nanoplatform by inhibiting HIF-1α-mediated suppression of ferroptosis.

Furthermore, since Mn3O4 nanozyme possesses POD-like and GPx-like activities, it may promote ferroptosis by inducing ROS storm and GSH depletion. As shown in Fig. 3i and j, the fluorescence intensity of the PBS group exhibited a statistically significant difference compared to the MLT group (∗P < 0.05) and a highly significant difference compared to the MPA group (∗∗∗P < 0.001). Additionally, a statistically significant difference was observed between the MMPA group and the MMPA + L group, indicating that Mn3O4 nanozyme effectively induces intracellular ROS production. At the same time, the photothermal effect significantly enhances it. Subsequent detection of intracellular GSH levels under different treatments (Fig. 3k) revealed that, compared to the PBS group, MPA and MMPA treatments significantly enhanced GSH depletion (∗∗P < 0.01). In contrast, MLT showed no significant correlation with GSH consumption. A highly significant difference was observed between the MMPA + L group and the MMPA group (∗∗∗P < 0.001), demonstrating that the photothermal effect markedly enhances the GPx-like activity of Mn3O4 nanozyme.

Lipid peroxidation levels, as one of the critical hallmarks of ferroptosis, are significant for verifying the occurrence and progression of ferroptosis. The BODIPY 581/591 C11 fluorescent probe exhibits red fluorescence when lipids are in a reduced state but shifts to distinct green fluorescence upon increased lipid peroxidation. As shown in Fig. 3l and m, the MLT group exhibited a slight increase in green fluorescence. In contrast, the MPA and MMPA groups showed a marked enhancement in green fluorescence accompanied by a significant reduction in red fluorescence intensity. For the photothermal treatment groups (MPA + L and MMPA + L), the green fluorescence intensity was substantially increased, while the red fluorescence intensity almost disappeared. The Malondialdehyde (MDA) assay results in Fig. 3n were closely aligned with those from the BODIPY 581/591 C11 probe. These findings indicate that both Mn3O4 nanozyme and MLT elevate tumor cell lipid peroxidation levels to varying degrees, while the combination of the nanoplatform with photothermal effect significantly enhances lipid peroxidation, thereby enhancing ferroptosis. Fig. 3o shows TEM images of A549-HRE-LUC cells after treatment with the various groups. After MLT treatment, the degree of mitochondrial shrinkage was relatively low. However, after treatment with MPA and MMPA, mitochondrial shrinkage was noticeable. Its volume decreased, and the density of the double-layer membrane increased, indicating that MPA and MMPA can cause mitochondrial dysfunction and significantly induce ferroptosis in cells.

Recent studies have shown that HIF-1α expression in tumor cells is closely associated with ferroptosis. HIF-1α not only upregulates glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) and SLC7A11 expression through transcriptional activation, thereby enhancing cellular antioxidant capacity and suppressing ferroptosis, but also promotes the formation of a ferroptosis-resistant tumor microenvironment by upregulating SLC1A1 and LDHA [45,46]. Given that we have previously demonstrated that MLT effectively inhibits HIF-1α expression, we further examined the intracellular GPX4 expression levels under different treatment conditions (Fig. 3p). Under normoxic conditions, MLT exhibited no significant effect on GPX4 expression. However, under hypoxic conditions, MLT reduced GPX4 expression. Concurrently, GPX4 expression was significantly decreased in the MPA, MPA + L, MMPA, and MMPA + L groups.

Previous studies have shown that mild PTT leads to an increase in intracellular HSP70 expression, thereby reducing the therapeutic efficacy of mild PTT. Here, we hypothesized that the inhibition of HIF-1α, lipid peroxidation, and ROS accumulation may collectively suppress HSP70 expression. As shown in Fig. 3q, HSP70 was significantly upregulated in the 43 °C-treatment group, whereas it was markedly downregulated in the MLT, MPA, and MMPA treatment groups, indicating that HIF-1α inhibition and ferroptosis may inhibit HSP70 expression. Notably, HSP70 remained at low levels in the MPA + L and MMPA + L groups. However, when Fer-1 was introduced to the MMPA + L group, HSP70 expression was significantly upregulated, further confirming the inhibitory effect of ferroptosis on HSP70.

These results demonstrate that MLT-mediated HIF-1α inhibition and Mn3O4 nanozyme-induced lipid peroxide accumulation and ROS storm, which effectively induce cellular ferroptosis. Concurrently, the inhibition of HIF-1α and the activation of ferroptosis synergistically inhibit HSP70 expression. This robust synergistic network provides an effective strategy for enhancing mild PTT.

In vitro immune activation by MMPA

Many studies have demonstrated that PTT and ferroptosis can activate the immune system against tumors by inducing ICD, a specific cell death modality, which would trigger the release of DAMPs. Under normal conditions, calreticulin (CRT) is localized in the endoplasmic reticulum; however, under stress, CRT translocates to the cell membrane surface, emitting an “eat-me” signal to immune cells to activate immune responses. As shown in Fig. 4a and S15, MPA and MMPA induced a small fraction of CRT expression on the cell surface, whereas negligible CRT expression was observed in the PBS and MLT groups. Notably, evident CRT exposure was detected in the MPA + L and MMPA + L groups, confirming that PTT significantly enhances ICD. During ICD, High mobility group box-1 (HMGB1) is released extracellularly, activating downstream signaling pathways and promoting immune activation. As demonstrated in Fig. 4b and S16, cells treated with MPA + L and MMPA + L exhibited higher HMGB1 release compared to other groups, further validating the potentiation of ICD by PTT. Quantitative fluorescence data for CRT and HMGB1 are presented in Fig. 4c and d. Additionally, ICD in tumor cells induces the release of ATP into the extracellular space, emitting a “find-me” signal to recruit anti-tumor immune effector cells. As shown in Fig. 4e, ATP release was elevated to varying degrees in all treatment groups compared to the PBS group, with MPA + L and MMPA + L groups showing significantly higher ATP levels than other groups. The Mn2+ generated by Mn3O4 at the tumor site not only upregulates ROS levels to induce ferroptosis and subsequent ICD but also acts as a STING agonist, activating the STING pathway and thereby further enhancing immune effects [47]. STING activation not only induces the production of type I interferons but also directly activates the NF-κB signaling pathway, thereby reshaping the tumor immune microenvironment. Furthermore, STING activation can trigger cellular pyroptosis or apoptosis, promoting the release of tumor antigens and DAMPs, which collectively enhance the antitumor immune response [48,49]. Therefore, we validated the expression of key proteins in the STING activation pathway using Western blot. As shown in Fig. 4g, compared to the PBS group, all experimental groups exhibited varying degrees of upregulated phosphorylation levels of STING, TANK-binding kinase 1 (TBK1), and Interferon regulatory Factor 3 (IRF3) after treatment, confirming the effective activation of the STING pathway by MMPA.

Meanwhile, Fig. S17 shows the STING activation capacity of MMPA in vitro. First, LLC-HRE-Luc cells were co-incubated with PBS, MLT, and MMPA, respectively (Fig. S17a). Then, the expression levels of cytokines including IFN-β and interleukin-6 (IL-6) in the cell culture supernatant were detected by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits. As shown in Fig. S17b and S17c, compared with the PBS or MLT treated group, higher secretion levels of IFN-β and IL-6 were found after MMPA treatment, indicating that the MMPA would contribute to the activation of the STING pathway in LLC-HRE-Luc cells. Meanwhile, the impact of MMPA on dendritic cell maturation was investigated by coincubation of bone marrow-derived dendritic cells (BMDCs) with PBS, MLT, and MMPA, respectively (Fig. S17d). As shown in Fig. S17e and S17f, in agreement with the observed results in LLC-HRE-Luc cells, more production of IFN-β and IL-6 was found in the supernatant of BMDCs culture medium after coincubation with MMPA, suggesting effective activation of the STING pathway in BMDCs. Furthermore, the maturation of BMDCs after coincubation with PBS, MLT, or MMPA were further studied by flow cytometry analysis. As shown in Fig. S17g and S17h, the maturation of BMDCs was promoted after coincubation with MMPA. These findings evidenced that the MMPA could effectively activate the STING pathway in both LLC-HRE-Luc cells and BMDCs and would contribute to the secretion of IFN-β and maturation of BMDCs in sequence to facilitate systemic antitumor immunity.

Meanwhile, Fig. S23 shows the result of 30-days toxicity testing for MMPA, further demonstrating the long-term safety of the nanozyme adjuvant.

Combined with MR imaging results, these findings demonstrate that MMPA effectively targets tumor sites, alleviates hypoxia in the solid TME, suppresses HIF-1α expression in tumor tissues, and thereby enhances therapeutic efficacy in vivo.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we developed a dual-enzyme-like nanozyme adjuvant for photothermally enhanced ferroptosis-immune synergistic therapy. In vitro cell experiments demonstrated that MLT-mediated HIF-1α inhibition and Mn3O4 nanozyme-mediated ROS storm and GSH depletion synergistically induced cellular ferroptosis. Additionally, the suppression of HIF-1α and the activation of the ferroptosis pathway cooperatively inhibited HSP70 expression, enhanced mild PTT, and induced robust ICD. Additionally, the released Mn2+ exhibited an efficient adjuvant effect by promoting the activation of the cGAS-STING signaling pathway, which in turn enhanced CTL infiltration and strengthened anti-tumor immune responses. Then, both cell culture and animal experiments indicated that MMPA adjuvant effectively inhibited HIF-1α expression in tumor cells and alleviated tumor hypoxia. Notably, this strategy exhibited moderate efficacy in immunocompromised BALB/c-nu mice but demonstrated a potent anti-tumor response in immunocompetent BALB/c mice by significantly inducing tumor ICD and enhancing CTL infiltration. Interestingly, MMPA combined with PTT achieved complete suppression of distant tumors in metastatic models, demonstrating a potent abscopal effect. This study not only reveals the potential of MLT in promoting the ferroptosis pathway and immunotherapy but also provides a novel strategy for multi-mechanism synergistic tumor therapy.

Materials and methods

Preparation of MMPA adjuvant

Preparation of Mn3O4 nanozyme. 0.17 g of MnAc2, 640 μL of oleic acid, 3.28 mL of oleylamine, and 15 mL of dimethylbenzene were added to a round-bottom flask. The reaction mixture was heated to 90 °C with magnetic stirring for 10 min. Subsequently, 1.0 mL of deionized water was added, and the mixture was reacted at 90 °C for 2.5 h. After cooling to room temperature, 40 mL of anhydrous ethanol was added. The precipitate (Mn3O4 nanozyme) was collected by centrifugation at 10,000×g for 5 min and was redispersed in cyclohexane.

Preparation of MMPA adjuvant. 200 μL of Tween-20 and 20 mL of deionized water were mixed and stirred at room temperature for 30 min. Subsequently, 1.336 mL of Mn3O4 nanozyme solution and 500 μL of MLT (5 mg mL−1) solution were added, and the mixture was ultrasonicated for 1 h. The solution was left to stand overnight until clarification. Then, 2.37 mL of DA (1.0 mg mL−1) solution was added, and the pH was adjusted to 8.5. The mixture was stirred for 6 h, followed by the addition of 10 μL of AS1411-SH (100 μM) solution with continued stirring for 2 h. The MMPA was collected by centrifugation at 14,000×g for 2 h and was redispersed in deionized water.

Characterization of MMPA adjuvant

The morphology of MMPA was investigated using TEM and analyzed elementally using X-ray EDS. The hydrated particle size and surface potential of MP, MPA, MMP, and MMPA were determined using a Malvern DLS particle size analyzer. The characteristic peaks of the UV–Vis spectra of AS1411 and MMPA were measured using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer. The in vitro stability of the nanoplatform was assessed by measuring the hydrated particle size of MMPA in PBS and 10 % FBS solution.

Release of MLT from MMPA adjuvant

The standard curve of MLT was established by preparing a 1.0 mg mL−1 solution of MLT, diluting it to 0.03125, 0.0625, 0.125, 0.25, and 0.5 mg mL−1 with deionized water, and measuring the absorbance at 280 nm using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer. The DEE and DLC of MMPA were calculated according to following equations:Drugencapsulationefficiency(wt%)=massofMLTinput−massoffreeMLTmassofMLTinputDrugloadingcontent(wt%)=massofMLTinputt−massoffreeMLTmassofMMPANPs

The in vitro release rate of MLT in the free MLT group (aqueous solution containing only MLT, MLT dose: 0.2 mg mL−1) or the MMPA group (MLT dose: 0.2 mg mL−1) were determined and compared via dialysis to investigate whether MMPA has a sustained-release effect on MLT compared to the free MLT group. Specifically, 500 μL of each group solution was added to the dialysis tube as the internal solution, and 10 mL of deionized water (with 0.1 % DMSO) was used as the external solution. The system was incubated at 37 °C for 48 h. Dialysis was performed using bags with a molecular weight cutoff of 3.5 KDa. The concentration of MLT in the external solution was quantified at 0.5, 1, 3, 6, 9, 12, 24, and 48 h to determine the cumulative release profile. Following each measurement, the external solution was replaced with 10 mL of deionized water.

Photothermal performance of MMPA adjuvant

The in vitro photothermal performance was assessed by adding 1.0 mL of the sample to a 1.5 mL centrifuge tube, with a fiber-optic collimator positioned vertically 3 cm above the tube. The photothermal performances of PBS, MPA, and MMPA were investigated by exposing them to an 808 nm laser (2.0 W cm−2), and temperature changes were recorded every 30 s for 5 min using an NIR thermal camera. The photothermal properties of the nanoplatform at different concentrations and laser powers were similarly evaluated.

Moreover, MMPA underwent five heating-cooling cycles with an 808 nm laser (5 min heating followed by natural cooling to room temperature). PCE of MMPA was calculated according to following equations:η=hAΔT−QSI(1−10−λ)where ℎ is the heat transfer coefficient; A is the laser irradiation area; ΔT is the increased temperature; QS is the thermal energy of quartz cuvette and solution absorbed from light; I is the laser power; λ is the absorbance at 808 nm.

All animal experiments were performed in accordance with the animal ethics of the affiliated institutions (No. KYLL-2022-1126). For an in vivo evaluation, tumor-bearing mice were injected with 200 μL of PBS, MPA, or MMPA. After 6 h, the tumor sites were irradiated with the 808 nm laser (2.0 W cm−2) for 5 min. Temperature changes before and after irradiation were recorded using an NIR thermal camera.

In vitro MR imaging of MMPA adjuvant

The MR imaging performance of MMPA was evaluated using a 0.5 T small animal MR imaging scanner. MMPA was diluted to five concentrations (0.522, 1.044, 2.088, 4.175, and 8.350 mg mL−1). To validate the GSH responsiveness, MMPA at these concentrations was incubated with a 10 mM GSH solution for 5 min prior to MR imaging evaluation. r1 values were calculated by a linear fit of 1000/T (s−1) to the sample concentrations (mg mL−1).

Mn3O4 nanozyme-mediated Fenton-like reactions

The concentrations of MB and NaHCO3 were maintained at 10 μg mL−1 and 25 mM, respectively. The H2O2 concentration was initially set to 8 mM to evaluate Fenton-like activity under different MMPA concentrations (0.522, 1.044, 2.088, 4.175, and 8.350 mg mL−1). Subsequently, MMPA was fixed at 200 μM to assess the activity under different H2O2 concentrations (0, 2, 4, 6, and 8 mM). All reactions were conducted at 37 °C for 30 min. Solution color changes were recorded, and the absorbance at 665 nm was measured after the reaction had completed.

In vitro cell culture studies

Stable transfection and luciferase activity validation

A549-HRE-LUC and LLC-HRE-LUC cells, which can be induced to express luciferase, were generated by lentiviral infection with the recombinant plasmid HRE-hCMVmp-LUC. To produce high-titer lentiviral particles, this plasmid was co-transfected into 293T cells with the packaging plasmids pSPAX2 and pMD2.G. Transfected A549 and LLC cells were then selected using 2.0 and 1.5 μg mL−1 puromycin, respectively.

For luciferase activity quantification, A549-HRE-LUC or LLC-HRE-LUC stable cells (5 × 104 to 1 × 105 cells per well) were seeded into 24-well plates. The plates were cultured under either hypoxic or normoxic conditions for 24 h, followed by imaging using a small animal imaging system. Bioluminescence intensity and region of interest (ROI) area were quantified with Living Image (Version 4.3.0).

In vitro cell viability

A549-HRE-LUC and LLC-HRE-LUC cells were cultured in Ham's F12-K and DMEM medium, respectively, supplemented with 10 % FBS, and incubated at 37 °C in a 5 % CO2 humidified incubator. The cytotoxicity of MLT, MPA, and MMPA were studied under normoxic and hypoxic conditions. When the cells reached approximately 80 % confluence in 96-well plates, the medium was replaced with fresh medium containing different concentrations of the sample, and the cells were incubated for 24 h. Then, 10 μL of CCK-8 reagent was added to each well, and the plates were incubated for 2 h. The absorbance of each well at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader to calculate the cell viability. To study the cytotoxicity of the nanoplatform under laser irradiation, 100 μL of fresh medium containing PBS, MLT, MPA, and MMPA were added to each well, and the plates were incubated in a hypoxic incubator for 24 h. After 6 h of incubation, cells in the laser-treated groups were irradiated with an 808 nm laser at 2.0 W cm−2, and cell viability was then calculated. Additionally, to verify that the nanoplatforms kill tumor cells via the ferroptosis pathway, cytotoxicity was assessed under normoxic and hypoxic conditions in the presence or absence of Fer-1.

Calcein AM/PI dual fluorescence staining was used to distinguish live and dead cells. When the cells reached approximately 80 % confluence in the wells, the old medium was removed, and the cells were washed three times with PBS. Next, 1.0 mL of serum-free medium containing PBS, MLT, MPA, or MMPA was added, and the cells were incubated in a hypoxic incubator for 24 h. After 6 h of incubation, the cells in the laser irradiation groups were irradiated with an 808 nm laser at 2.0 W cm−2 for 5 min. Living cells were stained with Calcein AM (green fluorescence, Ex/Em: 494/517 nm), and dead cells were stained with PI (red fluorescence, Ex/Em: 535/617 nm). After incubated with the dyes for 30 min, the cells were rinsed three times with PBS and visualized using a fluorescence microscope.

Cell uptake and cell affinity

When the cells reached approximately 70 % confluence in the wells, the old medium was removed. The cells were washed three times with PBS, and 1.0 mL of serum-free medium containing MMPA was added. The cells were then incubated for 1, 3, 6, 9, and 12 h. At each time point, the medium was removed, and the cells were washed three times with PBS. They were then fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde for 20 min and rewashed three times with PBS. After staining with 100 μL of DAPI solution (Ex/Em: 405/430–470 nm) for 15 min and three PBS washes, the cellular uptake of MMPA was observed using a confocal laser scanning microscope. IR676 emitted red fluorescence (Ex/Em: 620–670 nm), while DAPI stained nuclei blue. The fluorescence intensity was quantified using ImageJ software.

When the cells reached approximately 70 % confluence in the wells, the old medium was removed. The cells were washed three times with PBS, and 1.0 mL of serum-free medium containing either MMP or MMPA was added to the respective wells. The cells were then incubated for 6 h. After incubation, the cellular affinity of MMPA was observed using CLSM, and the fluorescence intensity was quantified using ImageJ.

Flow cytometry

When the cells reached approximately 80 % confluence, the old medium was removed, and the cells were washed with PBS. Then, 100 μL of fresh medium containing MLT, MPA, or MMPA was added to each well, and the cells were incubated in a hypoxic incubator for 24 h. For the laser irradiation group, the cells were irradiated with an 808 nm laser (2.0 W cm−2) for 5 min at the 6-h time point, then returned to the incubator for further incubation. At the end of incubation, the cells were collected into centrifuge tubes, centrifuged at 1,000×g for 5 min, washed twice with PBS, and then resuspended in PBS. Apoptosis was analyzed using the Annexin V-FITC/PI Apoptosis Detection Kit by flow cytometry.

In vitro bioluminescence imaging

The hypoxia-alleviating ability of MMPA was assessed using A549-HRE-LUC and LLC-HRE-LUC cells. When the cells reached approximately 80 % confluence, the old medium was removed, and the cells were washed with PBS. Then, 100 μL of fresh medium containing MLT, MPA, or MMPA was added to each well, and the cells were incubated under hypoxic or normoxic conditions for 24 h. Following incubation, bioluminescence signals were captured using a small animal imaging system, and the intensity was quantified with Living Image.

In vitro detection of ROS generation

A549-HRE-LUC cells were incubated with PBS, MLT, MPA, or MMPA for 12 h. For the laser irradiation group, the cells were irradiated with an 808 nm laser (2.0 W cm−2) for 5 min at the 6-h time point. After irradiation, the cells were further incubated for 6 h. Next, the DCFH-DA probe was added, and the cells were incubated for 30 min. Then, the old medium was removed, and the cells were gently rinsed three times with PBS. Finally, cellular fluorescence was observed using CLSM and quantified using ImageJ.

In vitro lipid peroxidation assay

A549-HRE-LUC cells were incubated with PBS, MLT, MPA, or MMPA for 12 h. For the laser irradiation group, the cells were irradiated with an 808 nm laser (2.0 W cm−2) for 5 min at the 6-h time point. After irradiation, the cells were further incubated for 6 h. Next, the BODIPY 581/591 C11 probe was added, and the cells were incubated for 30 min. Then, the old medium was removed, and the cells were gently rinsed three times with PBS. Finally, cellular fluorescence was observed using CLSM and quantified using ImageJ.

Cellular transmission electron microscopy

When the cells reached approximately 80 % confluence, the old medium was removed, and the cells were washed with PBS. Next, 100 μL of fresh medium containing MLT, MPA, or MMPA was added to each well, followed by 24 h incubation in a hypoxic incubator. The cells were collected into 1.5 mL centrifuge tubes and fixed with 2 % glutaraldehyde. After fixation, embedding, and sectioning, mitochondrial morphology was observed using TEM.

Stimulation of STING pathway in cancer cells and BMDCs

LLC-HRE-Luc cells (5 × 104 cells per well) were implanted in 24-well plates and incubated overnight, and then incubated with PBS, MLT, or MMPA. Then, the supernatant of the media was collected for IFN-β and IL-6 analysis. BMDCs from the bone marrow of 8-week-old C57BL6 mice were obtained according to the reported procedure. The obtained BMDCs (5 × 105 cells per well) with an initial maturation degree of about 10 % were implanted in 24-well plates and then incubated with PBS, MLT, or MMPA, respectively. Then, the supernatant of the media was collected for IFN-β and IL-6 analysis. Furthermore, BMDCs were incubated with PBS, MLT, or MMPA. After 12 h incubation, the maturation status of BMDCs was studied by flow cytometry after staining with anti-CD11c-FITC, anti-CD80-PE, and anti-CD86-APC.

In vitro induction of immunogenic cell death

For the immunofluorescence of CRT and HMGB1, A549-HRE-LUC cells were incubated with PBS, MLT, MPA, or MMPA for 12 h. For the laser irradiation group, cells were irradiated with an 808 nm laser (2.0 W cm−2) for 5 min at the 6-h time point. After irradiation, the cells were further incubated for 6 h. Subsequently, the cells were fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde for 20 min and blocked with 5 % BSA for 1 h. After blocking, cells were treated with either an anti-CRT antibody or an anti-HMGB-1 antibody, followed by an overnight incubation at 4 °C. The following day, cells were treated with respective fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies (Multi-rAb™ CoraLite® Plus 594 for CRT expression and CoraLite488 for HMGB-1 expression) for 1 h. After nucleus staining with DAPI for 5 min, cellular fluorescence was observed using CLSM and quantified using ImageJ.

For the ATP release assay, the culture media were centrifuged at 400×g for 5 min, and the extracellularly released ATP levels were quantified using an ATP Assay Kit, following the manufacturer's protocol.

Western blot

Cells were lysed with RIPA buffer, and proteins in supernatants (centrifuged at 14,000×g for 10 min) were quantified. SDS-PAGE separated protein lysates, which were transferred to PVDF membranes, blocked, and then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibody. Membranes were washed, incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibody for 1 h, rinsed six times (5 min each), and visualized using ECL detection reagent.

In vivo MR imaging of MMPA adjuvant

All animal experiments were performed in accordance with the animal ethics of the affiliated institutions (No. KYLL-2022-1126). MR imaging was performed after injecting 200 μL of MMP or MMPA intravenously into the mice (doses: 0.835 mg mL−1 Mn3O4). MR images of the tumors were captured before and at 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, and 6 h after injection.

In vivo tumor hypoxia monitoring

Subcutaneous tumor models were established in mice using A549-HRE-LUC cells, and tumor hypoxia was dynamically monitored via bioluminescence imaging. When tumor volumes reached approximately 500 mm3, mice were randomly divided into two groups and administered 200 μL of saline or MMPA via tail vein injection. Prior to and at 4, 8, 12, 24, and 48 h post-injection, the luciferase substrate D-luciferin (150 mg kg−1) was intraperitoneally injected. Bioluminescence imaging was performed 5 min after substrate administration, and signal intensity was quantified using Living Image.

In vivo anti-tumor efficacy of MMPA adjuvant in BALB/c-nu mice

Subcutaneous A549-HRE-LUC tumor models were established in BALB/c-nu mice to evaluate the tumor-suppressive efficacy of nanoplatforms in immunocompromised mice. Mice were randomly divided into 7 groups (n = 3): (G1) saline, (G2) Laser (L), (G3) MLT, (G4) MPA, (G5) MMPA, (G6) MPA + L, and (G7) MMPA + L (Mn3O4 dose: 0.835 mg mL−1, MLT dose: mg mL−1). Treatment was performed on a three-day cycle, in which the bioluminescence imaging was performed to characterize tumor changes on the first day of each cycle; on the second day, 150 μL of sample from each group was injected intravenously, and the mice in the laser irradiation group were irradiated with an 808 nm laser at 2.0 W cm−2 for 5 min at 6 h post-injection. The entire treatment cycle lasted 15 days. The bioluminescence images, tumor volumes (volume = 0.5 × length × width2), and body weights of the mice in each group were recorded during the treatment period.

In vivo anti-tumor efficacy of MMPA adjuvant in BALB/c mice

Subcutaneous LLC-HRE-LUC tumor models were established in immunocompetent BALB/c mice to evaluate the tumor-suppressive efficacy of nanoplatforms. Mice were randomly divided into six groups (n = 5): (G1) saline, (G2) MLT, (G3) MPA, (G4) MPA + L, (G5) MMPA, and (G6) MMPA + L (Mn3O4 dose: 0.835 mg mL−1, MLT dose: 0.5 mM). Treatment was performed on a three-day cycle, in which the bioluminescence imaging was performed to characterize tumor changes on the first day of each cycle; on the second day, 150 μL of sample from each group was injected intravenously, and the mice in the laser irradiation group were irradiated with an 808 nm laser at 2.0 W cm−2 for 5 min at 6 h post-injection. The entire treatment cycle lasted 12 days. The bioluminescence images, tumor volumes, and body weights of the mice in each group were recorded during the treatment period.

In vivo anti-tumor immune response analysis

After the treatment of section 4.11, the mice were euthanized to collect the tumor and the inguinal lymph nodes near the tumors. The lymph node cell suspensions and tumor cell suspensions were then prepared by using the enzyme mixture solution (neutral protease, collagenase II, and hyaluronidase) in DMEM medium. The cells from the inguinal lymph nodes were stained with anti-CD11c-FITC, anti-CD86-APC, and anti-CD80-PE for the DCs maturation study. Tumor cells were stained with anti-CD3-APC, anti-CD4-FITC, and anti-CD8-PE antibodies and then analyzed by flow cytometry. Furthermore, the TNF-α and IFN-β were measured by using an ELISA assay according to the vendor's instructions.

In vivo anti-tumor abscopal effect

The bilateral tumor model was established to evaluate the abscopal effect of MMPA with irradiation. First, LLC-HRE-LUC cells (2 × 106 cells per mouse) were subcutaneously injected into the right flank of each mouse. Four days later, LLC-HRE-LUC cells (2 × 106 cells per mouse) were subcutaneously injected into the left flank of each mouse to establish the distant tumor. The tumor-bearing mice were divided into six groups (n = 5): (G1) saline, (G2) MLT, (G3) MPA, (G4) MPA + L, (G5) MMPA, and (G6) MMPA + L (Mn3O4 dose: 0.835 mg mL−1, MLT dose: 0.5 mM). Only the primary tumor underwent treatment. The bioluminescence images, tumor volumes, and body weights of the mice in each group were recorded during the treatment period.

Staining of tissue sections

Paraformaldehyde (4 %) was used to fix the tumor tissues, followed by paraffin-embedded sections to examine the morphological and histological features. Histopathological analysis was performed using H&E and TUNEL staining to assess the levels of tumor cell proliferation and cell death. Furthermore, anti-HIF-1α, anti-GPX4, and anti-HSP70 antibodies were used to observe the protein expression levels.

Statistical analysis

The data were represented at least three times as mean ± SD. Two or more groups were compared using one-way ANOVA. No significant difference was indicated as (n.s.), P < 0.05 (∗), P < 0.01 (∗∗), and P < 0.001 (∗∗∗).
