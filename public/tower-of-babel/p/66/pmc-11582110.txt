Expanding Treatment Opportunities: Reviewing the Current State of Injectable Antiretrovirals for Treatment of HIV
Authors: Amanda Binkley, Matty Zimmerman, Christina Maguire
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAntiretroviral therapy has evolved significantly over the last 20–30 years, from requiring multiple tablets multiple times per day to single-tablet regimens and most recently, in 2021, long-acting injectable antiretrovirals. These long-acting antiretrovirals have expanded the treatment options for individuals with HIV who may have difficulty adhering to daily oral medications, difficulty taking oral medications, and/or individuals with multidrug-resistant HIV. This article reviews the currently available long-acting injectable antiretrovirals, including cabotegravir/rilpivirine, lenacapavir, and ibalizumab. The available data supporting these agents and current place in therapy will be discussed. Data supporting the use of additional long-acting injectable agents, broadly neutralizing antibodies, currently in the pipeline will be reviewed as well.Keywords: Long-acting antiretrovirals, HIV, Antiretroviral therapy

Key Summary Points

Injectable antiretroviral therapy for persons with HIV is available with Department of Health and Human Services guidelines recommending its use to optimize individuals’ antiretroviral treatment regimens.Cabotegravir/rilpivirine is recommended for individuals with HIV who are virally suppressed on current regimen with additional real-world data demonstrating potential utility in individuals not virally suppressed with higher rates of virological failure in those with lower CD4 counts.Lenacapavir, a first in class capsid inhibitor, is recommended to be added to an optimized background regimen in individuals who are heavily treatment experienced and/or have multidrug-resistant HIV.Ibalizumab, a monoclonal antibody, is utilized in combination with an optimized background regimen in individuals who have significant prior antiretroviral exposure and/or multidrug-resistant HIV.Broadly neutralizing antibodies which target different areas of the HIV envelope protein gp120, given in combination with other antiretrovirals, are currently under investigation and may offer additional treatment options for individuals with HIV.

Introduction

Significant advances have been made over the last 20 years optimizing antiretroviral therapy and treatment of HIV resulting in longer life expectancy and improved outcomes in patients with HIV. Despite these advances, those patients unable to take an oral regimen or adhere to a daily oral regimen had no alternative treatment options. In January 2021, the first injectable antiretroviral, cabotegravir/rilpivirine (CAB/RPV) was Federal Drug Administration (FDA) approved for treatment of HIV in patients who were virally suppressed on their current antiretroviral regimen. Since then, there have been two additional FDA-approved antiretrovirals, lenacapavir and ibalizumab, expanding the treatment armamentarium for HIV treatment. In addition to data supporting use of long-acting injectables for treatment, there is data supporting use of long-acting injectables for pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) which will not be discussed in here but will be discussed in a later review.

This article is based on previously conducted studies and does not contain any new studies with human participants or animals performed by any of the authors. This article will discuss the studies which led to the FDA approval of these agents for treatment of HIV, current place in therapy, and additional data published since their FDA approvals.

Long-Acting Antiretrovirals

Cabotegravir/Rilpivirine

Cabotegravir/rilpivirine long-acting injectable (CAB/RPV LA), also known as Cabenuva®, is a combination antiretroviral composed of cabotegravir (CAB), which has a high barrier to resistance when compared to the first-generation integrase strand inhibitors (INSTIs), combined with a second-generation non-nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitor (NNRTI), rilpivirine (RPV).

Currently, two dosing regimens are approved by the US FDA for CAB/RPV LA: 4-week regimen (CAB/RPV LA 600–900 mg × 1, followed by CAB/RPV LA 400–600 mg every 4 weeks) and 8-week regimen (CAB/RPV LA 600–900 mg monthly × 2 doses, then CAB/RPV LA 600–900 mg every 8 weeks). CAB/RPV LA is administered intramuscularly via a Z-track injection. The doses can be administered within 7 days prior to or after the targeted injection date. Individuals who do not receive their injection within that window period should be bridged with alternative antiretroviral therapy (ART) and then restart the CAB/RPV LA regimen (dosing scheme to be determined on the basis of time since last injection). When initiating CAB/RPV LA, oral lead-in therapy is optional and may be considered on the basis of individual-specific considerations. This may be a preferred option in participants who have had adverse events to other antiretroviral (ARV) regimens whereas oral lead-in may be skipped in those who have difficulty adhering to a daily oral regimen or are unable to tolerate oral medications [1].

When discussing CAB/RPV LA, it is critical to consider the pharmacokinetics. A secondary analysis of HPTN 077, a study which investigated the use of CAB for PrEP, evaluated the pharmacokinetic properties of CAB and factors associated with the phenomenon known as the “CAB tail.” CAB concentrations were measured and evaluated up to 228 weeks after the last injection. The half-life of CAB was an average of 6.6–11.5 weeks, with cabotegravir concentrations being above the lower limit of qualification in 63% of women and 22% of men 52 weeks after their last injection. A significant number of individuals, 42% of women and 13% of men, still had levels above the lower limit of qualification at week 76 post injection. The results from this pharmacokinetic analysis demonstrate that the median time since last injection to levels below the lower limit of qualification was 67.3 weeks for women and 43.7 weeks for men. This “CAB tail” is an important consideration when initiating individuals on therapy, as the clinical implication of levels within the detectable range but not treatment range could lead to development of resistance in individuals who discontinue CAB/RPV LA and are not restarted on an alternative regimen [2].

Clinical Trials

Orkin and colleagues published the First Long-Acting Injectable Regimen (FLAIR) trial, a phase 3 multicenter, randomized, open-label trial evaluating CAB/RPV LA. Enrolled participants were treatment-naïve individuals with an HIV RNA ≥ 1000 copies/mL at study screening. Individuals enrolled were initiated on ART with dolutegravir/abacavir/lamivudine or a combination of dolutegravir and two alternative NNRTIs for a total of 20 weeks. Those who had an HIV RNA < 50 copies/mL at week 16 were randomized to either receive CAB/RPV LA or continue with the current oral regimen (standard of care, SOC) for a minimum of 100 weeks. CAB/RPV oral therapy, cabotegravir 30 mg po daily and rilpivirine 25 mg po daily, was provided to those randomized to receive the CAB/RPV LA regimen for approximately 28 days to evaluate both efficacy and safety with the CAB/RPV regimen prior to initiation of the long-acting regimen. Participants in the long-acting arm received CAB 600 mg and RPV 900 mg as first injection, followed by CAB 400 mg and RPV 600 mg every 4 weeks into the gluteus muscle [3].

The primary endpoint of FLAIR was the percentage of participants with HIV RNA ≥ 50 copies/mL at week 48. A total of 283 individuals were enrolled in each treatment arm, with 2.1% of participants in the CAB/RPV LA arm and 2.5% of participants in the SOC arm having HIV RNA ≥ 50 copies/mL (− 2.8 to 2.1) meeting the pre-defined non-inferiority criteria. Four participants had confirmed virologic failure, three of whom developed NNRTI and INSTI resistance mutations while on CAB/RPV LA and one of whom had HIV-1 subtype AG, known to be a potential predictor of poor response to CAB/RPV LA [3].

Additional data published by Orkin et al. in 2021 detailed the results of the study participants through week 124. Those who were randomized to receive the oral SOC regimen were provided the option to enroll in the CAB/RPV LA arm with or without the oral lead-in therapy. Most of the participants, 92% (232/253), who were enrolled in the SOC arm initially elected to continue CAB/RPV LA through the extension phase of the study. Of the 283 participants who continued CAB/RPV LA from the initial randomization, 5% met the primary endpoint with HIV RNA > 50 copies/mL, while only 1% of participants in the direct to inject (n = 111) and oral lead-in therapy arms (n = 121) met this endpoint [4].

Injection site reactions were common in the participants who received CAB/RPV LA, with 86% reporting reactions with a median duration of 3 days. A total of 11% and 4% of participants experienced a grade 3 or higher event in the CAB/RPV LA arm and oral therapy arm respectively. At the end of week 48, the majority of participants, 91%, indicated a preference for the long-acting formulation as compared to oral therapy. The results of the study demonstrated that CAB/RPV LA was non-inferior to current SOC oral regimens [4].

CAB/RPV was evaluated in a phase 3, randomized, open-label, active-controlled, multicenter, parallel-group non-inferiority study known as the Antiretroviral Therapy as Long-Acting Suppression (ATLAS) study. Individuals enrolled in ATLAS were 18 years and older with HIV who were virologically suppressed, with HIV RNA < 50 copies/mL on their current regimen and randomized to receive one of two regimens: CAB/RPV every 4 weeks as compared to maintaining their current standard of care ARV regimen. The study defined current ARV SOC regimens as two NRTIs + INSTI, NNRTI or a PI. Individuals randomized to the CAB/RPV treatment arm received oral CAB/RPV daily for the first 28 days and then were started on CAB/RPV LA injections monthly. Patients were excluded if they had prior virological failure, documented resistance mutations to INSTI or NRTIs, hepatitis B, or antiretroviral regimen changes in the previous 6 months [5].

A total of 308 individuals were randomized in each treatment group and evaluated through week 48. The primary endpoint of the ATLAS study was the percentage of individuals with detectable viral loads, defined as HIV RNA > 50 copies/mL at week 48. The results demonstrated that 1.6% of participants in the CAB/RPV group meet the primary endpoint as compared to 1.0% in the SOC arm, meeting the pre-defined non-inferiority criteria. A total of 92.5% of individuals in the CAB/RPV arm and 95.5% of participants in the SOC arm achieved virologic suppression (HIV RNA < 50 copies/mL). Seven individuals had confirmed virological failure, three in the CAB/RPV arm and four in the SOC arm. Adverse events, primarily injection site reactions, were common; however, they did not lead to discontinuation in most individuals [5].

After the prior phase 3 studies demonstrated non-inferiority of CAB/RPV LA every 4 weeks, the ATLAS study was continued on into ATLAS-2M which evaluated CAB/RPV administered every 8 weeks as compared to every 4 weeks. ATLAS-2M was a randomized, multicenter, open-label, parallel-group study which enrolled individuals with HIV who were currently receiving first- or second-line SOC oral antiretroviral regimens and did not have any history of virological failure. Individuals who participated in the ATLAS study were also eligible for inclusion into the ATLAS-2M study if they met the following criteria: HIV RNA < 50 copies/mL and completion of the 52-week phase of the ATLAS study. All participants, who were enrolled outside of the ATLAS study were transitioned to oral CAB/RPV for 28 days, while those enrolled from the ATLAS study were continued on every 4-week CAB/RPV LA injections. Participants were then randomized to receive every CAB/RPV LA 4 weeks or every 8 weeks through week 52 with option to switch to CAB/RPV LA every 8 weeks from week 52 to 96 [6].

The primary objective of ATLAS-2M was HIV RNA ≥ 50 copies/mL at week 48 for every 4-week regimen as compared to every 8-week regimen. Overall, a total of 522 patients were enrolled in every 8-week treatment arm and 523 in every 4-week treatment arm, with 2% and 1% of participants meeting the primary endpoint of an HIV RNA ≥ 50 copies/mL, respectively (− 0.6 to 2.2). Confirmed virologic failure (CVF) occurred in 12 (2.3%) participants in the every 8-week arm and 2 (0.4%) in the every 4-week treatment arm, with the majority (70%) of those without any prior exposure to CAB/RPV prior to study enrollment. Upon further investigation five of the participants with CVF through week 96 and the two additional participants with CVF since week 96 experienced treatment-emergent resistance-associated mutations to both NNRTIs and INSTIs [6].

The LATTE-2 study was an open-label, randomized, phase 2b study evaluating efficacy and safety of every 4- or 8-week CAB/RPV LA. Participants enrolled received oral CAB 30 mg daily in combination with abacavir-lamivudine 600–300 mg daily through 20 weeks. Those who completed the 20-week induction phase demonstrating viral suppression (HIV RNA < 50 copies/mL) were randomly assigned to one of three treatment arms: CAB/RPV LA every 4 weeks, CAB/RPV LA every 8 weeks, or oral therapy with oral CAB and abacavir/lamivudine. Those participants who were randomized to the CAB/RPV LA treatment arms received 28 days of oral CAB/RPV prior to the first injection [7].

A total of 286 patients were enrolled and randomized, 115 to each of the CAB/RPV LA groups (every 4- or 8-week) and 56 to continue oral therapy. Therapy was continued on the basis of randomization through week 100, at which time participants receiving oral therapy could switch to the CAB/RPV LA arm in discussion with the trial investigators. The use of oral lead-in therapy was optional at this time [7].

In both arms, direct to inject (those participants originally randomized to receive CAB/RPV LA) and the participants who switched from oral to LA, one participant did not achieve viral suppression with HIV RNA ≥ 50 copies/mL at week 100. Of those participants in the CAB/RPV LA arm with virological data, 5% had HIV RNA ≥ 50 copies/mL at week 124. Withdrawal from the study due to adverse effects was uncommon and occurred in 15 participants in the CAB/RPV LA group and 3 participants switched from oral regimen to CAB/RPV LA during the extension phase. Results from the LATTE-2 study demonstrated that CAB/RPV LA remains a safe and effective antiretroviral regimen [7].

Most recently, the 12-month results from the Solar study were published which was a randomized, open-label multicenter, non-inferiority study evaluating CAB/RPV LA every 8 weeks as compared to bictegravir/emtricitabine/tenofovir alafenamide. Non-inferiority was demonstrated at month 12, with 1% of individuals on CAB/RPV LA and < 1% on BIC/FTC/TAF with HIV RNA ≥ 50 copies/mL. A higher number of individuals withdrew from the study in the CAB/RPV LA arm as compared to BIC/FTC/TAF as a result of adverse events, with 70% of individuals on CAB/RPV LA reporting injection site reactions, the majority (98%) of which were reported as grade 1 or 2 [8].

Real-World Data

Given the importance of adherence to timing of injections, it was vital to assess CAB/RPV’s performance in a real-world setting. The Observational Pharmaco-Epidemiology Research and Analysis Cohort trial described adherence and effectiveness of CAB/RPV [9]. Individuals with two or more injections were included; 322 individuals received a median of three injections. Of the 221 individuals with baseline viral load (VL) < 50 copies/mL and ≥ 1 follow-up VL, 95% maintained a VL < 50 copies/mL while four individuals experienced virologic failure (VF). Injections were administered on time primarily (90%) and of delayed injections, 60% were administered within 28 days (negating need for oral bridge) suggesting that in a real-world setting there is adherence to injections. There were 36 individuals initiated on CAB/RPV with a VL ≥ 200 copies/mL and 94% and 75% of these individuals had a VL < 200 copies/mL and < 50 copies/mL at last measurement, respectively [10].

More data was published to support use of CAB/RPV in patients with viremia. Outcomes were described in a CAB/RPV delivery program in 39 individuals who received CAB/RPV every 4-week injections; 15 of those individuals were viremic with a mean log104.67 copies/mL baseline VL (SD ± log101.16 copies/mL) [11]. Those viremic individuals received a median of 6 injections and 80% achieved and maintained viral suppression.

Additional real-world data suggests that the risk of VF does not differ between those on CAB/RPV vs. oral regimens; however, lower baseline CD4 count predicted a higher risk of VF (OR 0.85; 95% CI 0.72–1.00) [12]. Rates of VL > 50 copies/mL and VF are similar among those with and without BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2 [13].

Place in Therapy

Currently, CAB/RPV LA is a recommended option for patients who are virologically suppressed on current oral therapy and amenable to office visits every 1–2 months for injections per the Health and Human Services guidelines (HHS) [14]. Despite the data supporting the use of CAB/RPV in clinical trials as well as real-world data, there has not been a significant uptake of CAB/RPV due to a variety of reasons. Further data is necessary to determine the current utilization in practice and associated barriers to increased utilization in practice. Increased utilization of long-acting injectables, particularly CAB/RPV, may assist with increasing the number of individuals who are able to maintain viral suppression and achievement of the goals set forth in the Ending the HIV Epidemic initiative (Table 1).

Lenacapavir

Lenacapavir (LEN) is a first-in-class capsid inhibitor that was approved by the FDA in 2022 for the treatment of HIV-1 infection in heavily treatment-experienced adults with multidrug-resistant (MDR) HIV-1 infection failing their current antiretroviral regimen as a result of resistance, intolerance, or safety considerations. As a capsid inhibitor, LEN works at multiple stages of the HIV life cycle to prevent replication including nuclear transport, virus assembly and release, and capsid core formation. It is given as two subcutaneous injections in the abdomen every 6 months following initiation with oral tablets, dosed in accordance with one of two dosing strategies [15].

In terms of metabolism LEN is a substrate of CYP3A4, UGT1A1, and P-glycoprotein. LEN is contraindicated in strong CYP3A4 inducers as coadministration will significantly decrease concentrations of lenacapavir. Combined P-gp, UGT1A1, and strong CYP3A4 inhibitors are not recommended for coadministration with LEN because of significantly increased concentrations of LEN. LEN is also a moderate inhibitor of CYP3A4 and can affect the concentration of other medications that are CYP3A4 substrates. As a result of its long half-life, LEN may affect concentrations of CYP3A4 substrates up to 9 months following subcutaneous injection.

LEN is not affected by resistance mutations that affect the four main classes of antiretrovirals: nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors (NRTIs), NNRTIs, protease inhibitors (PIs), and INSTIs [15]. However, resistance can develop to lenacapavir during treatment. In cell culture seven substitutions have been identified in the capsid including L56I, M66I, Q67H, K70N, N74D/S, and T107N. In clinical trials, patients who met criteria for resistance analysis were found to have lenacapavir resistance-associated substitutions including M66I, Q67K + K70H, Q67H + K70R + T107S, and Q67Q/H [15–17]. M66I was associated with a 234-fold decrease in lenacapavir susceptibility, Q67H + K70R with a 15-fold decrease, and K70H with a 265-fold decrease [16].

The most common adverse effects associated with lenacapavir in clinical trials include nausea and injection site reactions occurring in 4% and 65% of patients, respectively [16, 17]. Injection site reactions ranged from erythema (31%), pain (31%), and swelling (36%) to nodules (25%) and indurations (15%). The duration of injection site reactions varied, with nodules and indurations taking longer to resolve. Excluding nodules and indurations, injection site reactions lasted for a median of 5 days. Nodules and indurations, on the other hand, took a median of 148 and 70 days to resolve, respectively. Lenacapavir was not associated with the development of laboratory abnormalities in clinical trials.

CAPELLA

Lenacapavir was investigated as an addition to optimized background therapy (OBT) in patients with MDR HIV in the CAPELLA trial [16]. This was a phase 3 trial including 72 patients with documented resistance to at least two medications from at least three classes of antiretrovirals, and with no more than two fully active medications that could be combined. The primary endpoint was the percentage of patients who had a viral load reduction from baseline of at least 0.5 log10 copies/mL by day 15 and was assessed in cohort 1 which included 36 patients randomized 2:1 to receive oral lenacapavir in addition to OBT, or placebo with continued OBT. Lenacapavir was given as 600 mg by mouth on days 1 and 2 and 300 mg on day 8. The primary outcome occurred in 88% of patients in the lenacapavir group and 17% in the placebo group (p < 0.001). Following day 15 patients in the lenacapavir group were started on subcutaneous lenacapavir given every 6 months, and patients in the placebo group were started on oral lenacapavir followed by subcutaneous lenacapavir given every 6 months. The 36 patients in cohort 2 were started on oral lenacapavir followed by subcutaneous lenacapavir every 6 months. At week 26, 81% of patients in cohort 1 and 83% of patients in cohort 2 had a viral load of < 50 copies/mL and 89% of patients in cohort 1 and 86% of patients in cohort 2 had a viral load < 200 copies/mL. This effect was sustained with 83% of patients in cohort 1 having a viral load of < 50 copies/mL at week 52 [46]. Overall, this study supports the addition of lenacapavir to OBT in patients with MDR HIV infection [16].

CALIBRATE

Lenacapavir has also been investigated in treatment-naïve patients [17]. This was done in the CALIBRATE trial, a phase 2, randomized, open-label trial in which treatment-naïve patients with HIV were randomized to receive (1) lenacapavir subcutaneous every 26 weeks with oral daily TAF/FTC, (2) lenacapavir subcutaneous every 26 weeks with bictegravir, (3) daily oral lenacapavir with emtricitabine and TAF, or (4) daily oral bictegravir, TAF, and emtricitabine. A total of 182 patients were randomized in a 2:2:2:1 fashion. Viral suppression was similar amongst all groups at week 54, occurring in 90%, 85%, 85% and 92% in groups 1, 2, 3, and 4, respectively.

Future Studies

Bictegravir/LEN vs. Stable Baseline Regimen

ARTISTRY-1 is a phase 2/3 study investigating the combination of lenacapavir + bictegravir (BIC) as a daily oral regimen for the treatment of HIV vs. a stable baseline regimen (SBR) [18, 19]. A total of 103 patients received lenacapavir + bictegravir (51 lenacapavir 25 mg, 52 lenacapavir 50 mg) and 25 patients continued SBR. At week 24 no patients in the LEN 25 mg + BIC or SBR groups had an HIV viral load > 50 copies/mL and one patient in the LEN 50 mg + BIC group had an HIV viral load > 50 copies/mL. This patient later suppressed without regimen change. In the phase 3 portion of the study, lenacapavir + BIC will be given as a single-tablet regimen. ARTISTRY-2 is a phase 3 study that is ongoing, estimated to enroll 546 participants [20].

Lenacapavir in Combination with Islatravir

Lenacapavir is being studied in combination with islatravir, an investigative nucleoside reverse transcriptase translocation inhibitor (NRTTI) as a once weekly oral regimen for the treatment of HIV [21, 22]. A phase 2 study investigated this combination compared to continuing bictegravir/TAF/emtricitabine in 104 patients with well-controlled HIV; 94.2% of patients in both groups remained virally suppressed with HIV viral load < 50 copies/mL at week 24. There was one participant in the lenacapavir/islatravir group with a viral load > 50 copies/mL (64 copies/mL) at week 24. This patient’s viral load was 251 copies/mL on day 1 and they resuppressed at week 30 without any change in regimen. Incidence of adverse effects was similar between groups, including no differences in CD4 and absolute lymphocyte count changes at week 24.

Lenacapavir in Combination with Teropavimab and Zinlirvimab

Lenacapavir is being studied in combination with broadly neutralizing antibodies (bNAbs) teropavimab and zinlirvimab, all given every 6 months, as a treatment regimen in virally suppressed patients with HIV [23–25]. Results from a phase 1b study of 21 patients with well-controlled HIV found the regimen to be generally well tolerated and one participant had viral rebound that was resuppressed on following reinitiation of their baseline ART [23–25].

Results

Place in Therapy

Current recommendations for LEN include consideration for addition to optimized background regimen in individuals who are heavily treatment experienced and/or have MDR HIV who are unable to achieve viral suppression on their current antiretroviral regimen [26]. Ideally the optimized background regimen would include at least one fully active agent; however, if that is not possible, the regimen should include a number of partially active agents.

Ibalizumab

Ibalizumab (IBA) was FDA approved in 2018 and is an IgG4 monoclonal antibody that binds to CD4 cell domains to prevent conformational changes in the CD4–HIV-1 envelope glycoprotein complex (gp120) that is required for viral entry [27]. IBA acts broadly on CCR5 and CXCR4-tropic strains as inhibition occurs prior to coreceptor engagement and has no drug–drug interactions or ARV cross-resistance; therefore, it is an attractive option for its indication of heavily treatment-experienced individuals with MDR HIV who are failing their current regimen. It has no antagonism with other ARVs and has synergistic activity when administered with enfuvirtide [28]. It is administered intravenously with a dose of 2000 mg over 30 min and a required 1-h observation period. Thereafter, 800 mg is administered intravenously either over 15 min or intravenous push every 14 days with a required 15-min observation period.

Clinical Trials

Two phase 2 studies were performed to identify dosing strategies for IBA. IBA was trialed at alternating 15 mg/kg and placebo doses through week 8 followed by 15 mg/kg every 2 weeks (arm A) versus weekly 10 mg/kg doses through week 8 followed by 10 mg/kg every 2 weeks (arm B) versus placebo (arm C) [29]. Individuals failing ART with triple class resistance and VL ≥ 10,000 copies/mL and CD4 ≥ 50 cells/mm3 with susceptibility to at least one oral ARV were included. At week 16, change in VL from baseline was log101.07 copies/mL in arm A, log101.33 copies/mL in arm B, and log100.26 copies/mL in arm C. By week 48, arm B had the most patients with VL ≤ 400 copies/mL (52%). Pharmacokinetic data obtained identified that trough concentrations were higher in arm B, but by week 16 those in arm A achieved an average of 42% greater overall exposure. Complete CD4 cell coating of IBA is associated with increased probability of improved outcomes, and arm A was associated with more consistent cell coating. While both dosing strategies were associated with virologic suppression, only arm A was associated with a significant increase in CD4 (86 cells/mm3, p = 0.05); data from this dose-finding trial was not sufficient to support a weight-based dosing strategy, which prompted a follow-up double-blind dose—response study of IBA 800 mg every 14 days versus IBA 2000mg every 28 days in individuals with VL ≥ 1000 copies/mL with decreased susceptibility to at least one NRTI, NNRTI, and PI at baseline [30]. The primary outcome of VL < 50 copies/mL at week 24 was achieved in 44% of those in the 800-mg arm and 28% of those in the 2000-mg arm (p = 0.16). Further pharmacokinetic analyses confirmed that 2000 mg doses achieved more rapid drug exposure while doses of 800 mg resulted in more stable drug exposure over the study period, which prompted the selection of 2000 mg once followed by 800 mg every 14 days.

An open label phase 3 study was performed to assess IBA in MDR HIV, which included those with resistance to at least one ARV in three different classes with HIV-1 RNA > 1000 copies/mL on ART [31]. The study had three phases: control phase days 0 through 6 in which patients continued their failing ART, functional monotherapy days 7–13 when a dose of IBA 2000 mg once was given on day 7, and day 14 through week 25 when optimized background regimen with at least one fully active drug was added and IBA was given 800 mg IV intravenously every 14 days. Forty individuals were included in this study with a mean baseline VL of log104.5 ± 0.8 copies/mL. The primary outcome of decrease in VL of at least log100.5 copies/mL at day 14 (assessing functional IBA monotherapy) was achieved in 83% of patients (p < 0.001). To assess IBA and addition of an optimized agent, additional outcomes were evaluated at week 25 and 43% and 50% of individuals achieved a VL < 50 and 200 copies/mL, respectively. There were seven individuals with virological failure and three additional with viral rebound; upon repeat testing nine of these patients had reduced susceptibility to IBA at baseline and three had reduced susceptibility to their background regimen. There are currently no commercially available resistance tests for IBA. The primary mechanism of resistance to IBA is a reduced expression or loss of glycosylation sites at the V5 loop of the gp120 complex. Presence of two glycosylation sites allows adequate inhibition with IBA (maximum percent inhibited 99%) versus only one glycosylation site (MPI 71%) versus no glycosylation sites (MPI 40%), rendering IBA inactive [32].

An open-label extension of the aforementioned trial allowed continued receipt of IBA for 27 patients until week 48 [33]. Viral suppression from baseline was stable from week 24 (log104.5 ± 0.8 copies/mL) to week 48 (log102.6 copies/mL). Three patients withdrew for non-drug-related reasons and seven withdrew to receive commercial IBA; of the 17 patients remaining, 47% achieved VL < 50 copies/mL at week 48. An additional phase 3 trial evaluated the efficacy and safety of intramuscular IBA to eliminate the need for peripheral intravenous line placement [34]. A treatment and study medication satisfaction questionnaire were given to patients after receiving two intravenous infusions of IBA and then again after four intramuscular (IM) administrations. Sixty-seven percent of the patients preferred IM administration and no patient lost viral suppression. Further data has been requested by the FDA prior to approval of IBA via IM administration.

Future studies aim to answer how IBA performs long term and compared to other regimens for MDR HIV. The Prospective and Retrospective Observational Study of MDR Patient Outcomes with and without IBA will evaluate use of IBA through month 36 and the extended comparison of IBA in trials versus other regimens in OPERA was completed in 2023; results are forthcoming.

The most common adverse reactions with use of IBA are diarrhea, dizziness, nausea, and rash [27, 35]. A warning is listed for IBA for risk of immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome (IRIS) and hypersensitivity/infusion-related reactions; one patient experienced IRIS during the initial phase of combination therapy but this has not been observed in other clinical trials to date. In the previously discussed phase 3 trial, 80% of patients experience at least one adverse drug reaction, most of which were mild to moderate, and only 18% of adverse events were related to IBA [33]. There were 16 serious adverse events reported by 11 patients.

Place in Therapy

IBA is currently recommended for use in persons with HIV (PWH) with heavily treatment-experienced and/or MDR HIV who cannot maintain virologic suppression on their current regimen [26]. IBA should be added to an optimized background regimen with at least one fully active agent with repeat HIV VL monitoring every 4–8 weeks to assess virologic response.

Broadly Neutralizing Antibodies for the Treatment of HIV

One emerging area of research regarding therapies for the treatment of HIV is broadly neutralizing antibodies (bNAbs). bNAbs are antibodies that recognize and prevent HIV from entering cells [35]. Additionally, bNAbs are thought to cause the immune system to destroy cells that are infected with HIV [35]. A number of bNABs have been identified, and these are categorized on the basis of where they target the HIV envelope protein gp120: the CD4 binding site, the V3 loop, and the V1/2 loop. Membrane proximal external region (MPER) antibodies target the transmembrane region of gp41 [36–38]. In addition to their novel mechanism, these agents have longer half-lives than traditional small-molecule ARVs allowing for less frequent dosing. bNAbs generally have a favorable safety profile, although a phase 1 trial investigating the safety and efficacy of 10E8VLS and VRC07-523LS was stopped early when five of eight patients who received 10E8VLS experienced moderate local reactogenicity and one patient experienced severe reactogenicity [37]. In terms of route of administration, bNAbs are given either intravenously or subcutaneously.

A variety of bNAbs have been investigated in phase 1 and phase 2 trials independently and in combination with other bNAbs. bNAbs are investigated given in combination in an effort to reduce the emergence of resistance by utilizing bNAbs that have different targets [38]. One such combination is 3BNC117 and 10-1074. The safety and efficacy of this combination was investigated in a phase 1 trial in which participants received infusions of the two bNAbs, or placebo, every 2 weeks for the first month then monthly through week 24 [39]. The infusions were well tolerated with no grade 3 or higher adverse effects. The combination was also found to be efficacious with five out of seven patients who received bNAbs maintaining viral suppression. Those two patients that received bNAbs who experienced viral rebound were noted to have bNAb resistance.

bNAbs are also being investigated in combination with small-molecule ARVs. For example, long-acting cabotegravir (LA CAB) is being investigated in a phase 2 study in combination with VRC07-523LS in virally suppressed PWH [40]. In this study LA CAB will be given every 4 weeks while VRC07-523LS will be given every 8 weeks with a planned duration of 48 weeks. Primary outcomes of interest include frequency of grade 3 or higher adverse effects and number of patients who experience virologic failure. The study concluded in April 2024 and enrolled 75 patients; results have not yet been published. Overall, the investigation of bNAbs for the treatment of HIV will provide patients with additional treatment options that avoid the need for daily medication administration and opportunities for improved adherence and disease control.

Agents in Development

Albuvirtide (ABT) is a fusion inhibitor that is in phase 3 of development for HIV treatment and has been available for use in China since 2018. ABT binds to the HIV-1 gp41 envelope protein to inhibit fusion [44]. The TALENT study was a phase 3, open-label, randomized, multicenter, noninferiority study in treatment-experienced adults. Individuals were required to have a HIV VL ≥ 1000 copies/mL with receipt of at least 6 months of treatment with two classes of ART [45]. Individuals were excluded if diagnosed with current AIDS-defining conditions, prior exposure to protease inhibitors or fusion inhibitors, or had various laboratory abnormalities. Patients were randomized to 320 mg ABT daily for 3 days followed by weekly intravenous infusion together with ritonavir-boosted lopinavir (LPV/r) daily versus LPV/r with two NRTIs with a primary endpoint of VL < 50 copies/mL at week 48. Non-inferiority was established with 80.4% of subjects in the ABT arm versus 66% of subjects in the NRTI group (95% CI − 3.0 to 31.9). The frequency of grade 3 and 4 adverse events was similar between the two groups. To the authors’ knowledge, ABT is only available for investigational use in the USA.

Co-infections and Additional Considerations

When using these long-acting injectables for treatment of HIV, one important consideration to keep in mind is their lack of activity against other common co-infecting viruses. In particular, these agents unlike some of the available oral antiretrovirals used do not cover hepatitis B virus (HBV). An estimated 7.5% of individuals with HIV are co-infected with HBV, requiring additional oral antiretrovirals for treatment of HBV even if eligible to receive long-acting antiretrovirals for treatment of HIV [41]. Healthcare providers need to be extra vigilant to evaluate for co-infection with HBV when switching oral antiretrovirals to long-acting injectables, to ensure the patient is continued on antiretrovirals with anti-HBV activity. Several different strategies are being investigated including ubiquitin and CRISPR technology for treatment of HBV, which could potentially be coupled with long-acting injectables for treatment of HIV to alleviate the need for oral antiretrovirals [42, 43].

Discussion and Conclusions

The advent of long-acting injectables for HIV has revolutionized treatment for individuals receiving these ARVs. The opportunity to offer individuals who were not able to take a daily oral regimen allows for the potential to increase the number of individuals who are virologically suppressed. In addition, the development of the newer injectable agents expands the treatment arsenal for individuals who have MDR-HIV with limited treatment options. For individuals who can access these long-acting injectables, these are well tolerated and effective at achieving viral suppression rates similar to current SOC. Despite these advances in care, unfortunately significant barriers to access these antiretrovirals still exist.

Prior to prescribing these long-acting injectables, providers should discuss the use of these agents ensuring a shared decision. Once the decision to start a long-acting agent is made, one of the first barriers encountered will be with regards to obtaining the medication and coverage by insurance. For these agents, insurance companies may cover these through either the pharmacy benefits or medical benefits. This distinction is critical, as procurement and place of administration may be dependent on the mode of insurance coverage. These agents must be administered by a licensed provider either in a clinic setting or infusion clinic setting depending on the route as well the type of insurance coverage (pharmacy vs. medical benefits).

Once insurance coverage is verified and the antiretrovirals are approved, the next logistical challenge is ensuring appropriate storage of the medication and staff to facilitate the administration of the injectables. Clinics/infusion centers should ensure they have the space to store these medications prior to implantation. In addition, those interested in initiation of these agents should develop workflow/guidance documents to identify responsible personnel and ensure adequate staff available to support implementation.

As with oral antiretrovirals, cost can be a significant barrier to implementation of the long-acting injectable agents. Patient assistance programs may be available to help patients who have high co-pays; however, these are not available to all patients depending on their insurance coverage. A team-based approach can be utilized to help work around these barriers and increase access to the long-acting antiretrovirals.

Despite these barriers, the availability of these long-acting injectables has revolutionized treatment of HIV, offering individuals an alternative option to increase their chance of achieving viral suppression.
