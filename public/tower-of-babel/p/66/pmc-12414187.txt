Researcher’s guide to preclinical animal models of acute extremity compartment syndrome
Authors: Catharina Constanze Gaeth, Robert Joseph Moritz, Max Seidelmann, Daniel Joseph Cognetti
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAcute extremity compartment syndrome (CS) is a serious medical complication triggered by factors such as trauma, vascular injury, or prolonged compression, resulting in elevated intracompartmental pressure (ICP) and tissue ischemia. Diagnosis remains challenging, mainly relying on the subjective evaluation of clinical symptoms. Different animal models have been used to study pathophysiology and evaluate diagnostic and therapeutic approaches. The aim of this review is to summarize and compare different animal CS models to provide insight for the development of more authentic and clinically relevant CS model. A comprehensive search of two databases was conducted. English-language animal studies focusing on CS were included. Human studies, non-acute extremity CS, and review papers were excluded. Data extraction and analysis focused on animal species, CS models, and clinically relevant assessment methods of CS. After screening, 90 studies met the inclusion criteria. Small animal (n=36, 40%) and canine models (n=31, 34.4%) were the most used animal species. A fluid infusion model (n=48, 53.3%) was the predominant induction method, followed by the ischemia-reperfusion (n=29, 32.2%) and the internal/external mechanical pressure models. Five studies used a combination CS model to achieve a more comprehensive representation of the clinical pathophysiology. Various diagnostic modalities were employed; ICP measurement (n=74, 82.2%) and evaluation of tissue oxygenation (n=10, 11.1%) were the most frequent device-based assessments. Biomarkers assessing muscle cell damage (n=15, 16.7%), antioxidant status (n=18, 20%) and inflammation (n=16, 17.8%) were analyzed as well as physical examination (n=12, 13.3%) and neuromuscular testing (n=10, 11.1%). There are a variety of validated animal species models, which can be considered depending on the research objectives. This review helps researchers evaluate model strengths and drawbacks before deciding on an experimental design.Keywords: Compartment Syndromes, Animal Experimentation, extremities

Background

Acute extremity compartment syndrome (CS) is a severe, potentially limb-threatening condition arising from multifactorial causes including long bone fractures, vascular injuries, edema, burns, prolonged compression or crush injuries. In the general population, the incidence of CS is estimated to be 7.3 cases per 100 000 in men and 0.7 per 100 000 in women, with an even higher prevalence observed in combat scenarios involving blast injuries, blunt trauma, and reperfusion after tourniquet (TQ) application.1,3

The pathophysiology of CS is characterized by increased intracompartmental pressure (ICP), resulting from the limited distensibility of fascia in response to swelling, hemorrhage, or reperfusion.4 5 When pressure within the fascial compartment increases, it diminishes the capillary pressure gradient (ICP>30 mm Hg, or compartment perfusion pressure: ∆p=diastolic pressure minus compartment pressure). This leads to a shift from aerobic to anaerobic metabolism, triggering a cascade of ischemia, inflammation, and neurovascular compromise.3 4 6 7

Diagnosis of CS typically relies on clinical signs and symptoms, such as disproportionate pain, pallor, pulselessness, paresthesia, poikilothermy, and palpable swelling. However, these findings require subjective interpretation, and several are latent findings, which often prompts intervention before they have a chance to arise.8 Objective measures, including ICP monitoring, local perfusion measurements, and serum biomarkers, can aid in diagnosis, though their empirical basis and specificity are limited.9 10 If there is concern for CS, fasciotomy is the gold-standard treatment, as untreated CS can result in severe complications, including neuropathy, contractures, amputation, renal failure, cardiovascular complications, and even mortality.5 7 However, fasciotomy is also not without its own morbidity.11

Given the clinical significance of CS, improving diagnostic methods and therapeutic approaches is critically important.3 However, clinical research can be challenging due to the condition’s relative rarity, variable severity, and the potential morbidity associated with delayed or inadequate treatment. As a result, animal models are an invaluable resource for investigating CS further. However, the wide array of animal species and CS induction methods used in the literature can make it difficult for researchers to determine which models are most appropriate for their specific research question. This is compounded by the fact that the drawbacks of individual models are often not well-documented and that most models incompletely replicate a realistic pathophysiology.12

Therefore, the purpose of this review is to compare and evaluate the different animal models and CS induction methods. By analyzing the advantages and limitations of each species, model, assessment technique, and outcome measure, we seek to guide researchers in refining CS models and fostering the development of clinically relevant approaches.

Methods

A comprehensive search was conducted using two databases, MEDLINE and SCOPUS, on March 11, 2024. The search was from inception to February 2024 using the search terms compartment syndrome and animal model (search strategy in the online supplemental file 1). All English-language animal studies focusing on the investigation of acute extremity CS were included. Human and cadaveric studies, as well as those examining non-acute limb compartment syndromes, for example, abdominal compartment or chronic exertional CS, were excluded. Additionally, review articles were not included. The software Covidence (Melbourne, Australia) was used to facilitate title, abstract, and full-text screening. Data extraction was performed using Microsoft Excel (Version 2402, Redmond, Washington, USA). The following information was extracted for each included study: author, title, year, aim of study, animal species, study size, CS induction method, diagnostic methods (device-based, laboratory, clinical, and functional), study limitations and study strengths. A quality check of extracted data was carried out by a second author.

Overview of identified models

Animal species

We identified several small and large animal species used to establish CS models. Rats and rabbits accounted for 40% of the models (n=36 studies)14,49 with 717 rats and 359 rabbits comprising the largest animal populations.

Canines were used in 34% (n=31) of the studies, with a total of 458 animals.50,80 Swine comprised 21.1% (n=19) of studies, with a total of 196 animals.81,99 There were two studies evaluating CS in primate models with 11 animals.100 101 Two studies categorized as “other” investigated CS in mice (6 animals) and turkey (24 animals).102 103 There was an average of 19.7 animals per study.

CS induction methods

Fluid infusion model

This model increases ICP via fluid infusion into a muscular compartment and was utilized in 53% (n=48) of studies.1821,25 27 35 48 52 It was predominantly employed in large animals, particularly canines (24 studies5154 57,67 69) or swine (10 studies84,8688 89 93). Autologous plasma was the primary infusion solution, used in 38% of studies, with several other solutions including saline and albumin being applied less commonly.5760 62,67 69 71 76

Collectively, these models use ischemia and reperfusion-based edema to induce CS. Ultrasound techniques, including contrast-enhanced ultrasound, were employed in five studies to assess compartmental shape, visualize tissue damage, and measure fascial displacement.48 86 88 90 92 In four studies, shear wave elastography was used to display tissue damage and assess tissue stiffness.48 92 93 102 MRI techniques enabled the visualization of post-ischemia edema and measurements of the compartment size.10 104

Clinical assessments

Few studies used clinical or functional assessments (13.3%, n=12), such assessments included measurement of limb circumference, ability to walk, and muscle force evaluation including isometric tetanic torques.16 19 21 30 31 36 38 39 54 65 69 71 73 100 Neuromuscular testing was used in 11.1% (n=10) of studies assessing muscle excitability, somatosensory evoked potentials, and nerve velocity.19 28 39 54 64 74 79 84 100 101

Anesthesia and analgesia

Various anesthetic protocols were employed in the studies reviewed. The predominant approach utilized volatile anesthesia with isoflurane. In the majority of swine studies, anesthesia was induced with an intramuscular combination of tiletamine, ketamine, and xylazine, subsequently maintained with 1% to 3% isoflurane.84 87 For most rats and rabbits, anesthesia was initiated with a 5% isoflurane induction dose, followed by a 1% to 2% maintenance dose.1519 20 22,25 While isoflurane was the most frequently used anesthetic, a minority of studies employed alternative methods such as intravenous propofol86 or ketamine/midazolam, and in some instances, volatile halothane or ether. Comprehensive reporting of analgesia was often insufficient, with some studies noting the administration of supplementary fentanyl, tramadol, or buprenorphine injections as required.

Discussion

Our review highlights the breadth of approaches used in CS research, underscoring the trade-offs and considerations inherent in choosing animal models, induction methods, and diagnostic strategies. The large volume of preclinical studies underscores the utility of animal models in the investigation of diagnostic and therapeutic approaches for CS as well as their ability to influence clinical practice. Our analysis revealed that canine and rat animal models are the most used animal models in CS research, with ICP as the leading diagnostic modality. Fluid infusion into a compartment was the predominant CS induction method, while only a small subset of studies employed a combination of injury mechanisms (“combined models”) to achieve a more realistic pathophysiologic representation of CS.

Animal species

Rat and rabbit animal models have proven reliable for replicating certain aspects of human CS pathophysiology.14 19 21 22 Rabbits, in particular, share anatomical similarities with human tibiae, fibula, and fascial compartments.14 18 44 49 Rats, though commonly used and economical for procurement and housing,14 22 31 have substantial experimental limitations both related to size and species. Their small limb size and body weight limit procedural transferability and the capacity for interventions, such as fasciotomies and probe insertion. Furthermore, rats have thinner skin and subcutaneous tissue as compared with humans,26 and their fascia allows for significant expansion, making it difficult to sustain increases in ICP.15 20 48 Additionally, compared with humans, rats and rabbits exhibit metabolic and cellular differences, including variations in macrophage responses and reduced ischemic tolerance12 22; although rats also recover more rapidly from injury.21 31

Canine models demonstrate a higher degree of fidelity with humans and are widely used for the investigation of CS.58 The development of CS in dogs due to traumatic injury or prolonged ischemia mirrors the pathophysiology in humans,34 and the beagle is frequently used to extrapolate findings to human physiology, including ICP dynamics or duration of time prior to tissue damage.52 However, despite the anterolateral compartment of the canine hindlimb closely resembling the size and anatomy (muscle groups and muscle mass) of the human leg,58 71 72 the fascia and skin of dogs are more flexible, leading to transient ICP elevations.72 In addition, dog muscles have greater oxidative capacity requiring cautious interpretation of diagnostic threshold values from humans.58 71

Swine models offer the closest anatomical and physiological parallels to humans, featuring skin with similar characteristics and four muscular compartments with inelastic fascia and comparable ICP and MAP values.20 81 86 88 91 103 These characteristics make swine the ideal non-primate species for studying physiological changes in intramuscular oxygenation, conducting surgical interventions, and testing new diagnostic devices.99

Non-human primates (NHPs) can be used to provide a high-fidelity model for CS, particularly in studies of nerve function, due to their close resemblance to human physiology and anatomy.100 101 However, the use of NHPs in CS research has declined in recent years, with the most recent NHP study in the current review dating back to 1993. This shift reflects the emergence of alternative animal models that have demonstrated comparable utility, particularly in the study of CS diagnostics.

CS induction methods

The fluid infusion model was the most widely cited induction method, owing to its simplicity and reproducibility for inducing elevated compartment pressure, suitable for testing the efficacy of diagnostic devices.21 However, its rapid ICP elevation contrasts with the gradual progression typically seen in clinical CS and lacks the trauma-related etiology of severe tissue injury or hemorrhage.35 58 86 Another disadvantage is the selective and, therefore, often limited infusion of fluids into a single compartment or muscle. Moreover, resorption of the fluid over time and the potential need for maintenance fluid infusion confounds ICP measurements. However, its biggest limitation based on its use in our lab and in correspondence with other laboratories is that it does not produce the degree of ischemia expected of CS even after 8 hours of elevated ICP.

The ischemic reperfusion model mimics a pathologic development mechanism of CS, making it applicable to a subset of acute trauma scenarios. It is logistically simple and can sometimes use a non-invasive strategy with a proximal TQ that does not require surgical access to the muscular compartment.55 83 However, the invasive internal ischemic models using a surgical approach and vessel ligation to induce ischemia may alter serum biomarkers and induce inflammation, thus confounding the effect attributable to the ischemia-reperfusion injury itself. Furthermore, the severity of CS, especially in this model approach, may be additionally influenced by several physiological parameters, including restoration of blood flow, blood pressure fluctuations, blood oxygen saturation, reactive oxygen species generation, and individual immune response.

Mechanical pressure models have provided essential insights into the consequences of elevated ICP on muscle ischemia and necrosis.46 Among these, the internal mechanical pressure model, using a balloon angiocatheter placed beneath the muscle compartment, has become a preferred technique, providing reliable and maintained ICP elevations throughout the reperfusion window (after balloon deflation) and thereafter, in contrast to the fluid infusion model. However, it should be noted that this technique does require a surgical insult to place the balloon under the compartment,83 91 which may independently affect serum biomarkers and histopathology outcomes.

Finally, several studies have incorporated a multimodal approach to approximate a more realistic etiology of CS.50 Multimodal models may better simulate the complex constellation of pathophysiology of CS typically seen in clinical scenarios. In designing future combined induction models, the goal is not increasing complexity, but rather a period of injury that would replicate a typical induction of CS and severity of CS as verified by ICP, histologic and laboratory analysis, and visualization of the effect of fasciotomy.

Diagnostic approaches

ICP is the cornerstone of CS model validation given its role as the “gold-standard” diagnostic criteria for CS.10 However, due to its overall low specificity, using ICP measurements as a sole diagnostic criterion for model validation should be cautioned against, consistent with clinical guidance.105 Many reported models in the literature cite ICP thresholds but do not validate that the models meet clinical definitions for CS or CS progression, such as visual assessments at the time of fasciotomy, clinical and functional outcomes, or the amount of histological necrosis (with and without fasciotomy). It also bears mentioning that in many of these species, there is no clear evidence that an ICP of 30 mm Hg or ∆p less than 30 mm Hg in humans corresponds to an equivalent measure in the animal extremity. Thus, we recommend verification of CS induction not only through ICP, but through tissue oxygenation, histologic analysis, clinical and/or surgical assessments to better approximate the clinical reality.

Laboratory assessments in animal models involve analyzing common, local and systemic clinically relevant markers for muscle damage, rhabdomyolysis, antioxidant status, and inflammation. Biomarkers such as CK, lactate, and IMA may each be used to support a CS diagnosis or at least indicate the presence of muscle ischemia. However, while serum markers have shown high specificity for CS (99.2%), their sensitivity (and ability to rule out CS) may be as low as 2.9%.104 106 Additionally, given the polytraumatized status of many patients with CS, biomarker thresholds as diagnostic criteria remain elusive. Nevertheless, in a controlled animal model, they represent an opportunity to ensure reproducible injury, better quantify the degree of injury, and assess for ongoing muscle damage.

Anesthesia and analgesia

Anesthetic agents and analgesia are components of CS animal model research that warrant further consideration, as they may influence key physiologic parameters relevant to the research. One major challenge in animal research is the assessment of baseline blood pressure, which is often impractical before anesthesia, leading most studies to rely on postinduction diastolic pressure to calculate ∆P. However, many anesthetic agents, including isoflurane, the most common agent in this review, are known to depress diastolic pressure,107 introducing variability in this key diagnostic threshold for CS. Beyond their impact on blood pressure, anesthetic agents may also suppress cell-mediated immune responses and alter inflammatory pathways, highlighting the importance of considering anesthetic effects when designing studies and interpreting findings in CS research.108 109

This review provides an overview of the current animal species, induction methods, and diagnostic modalities used in preclinical CS research. While swine represent the most physiologically compatible non-primate species, there are a variety of validated species which can be considered depending on the research objectives. Greater emphasis on comprehensive model validation and clinically relevant or combined induction methods is essential for advancing diagnostic and therapeutic approaches for CS.
