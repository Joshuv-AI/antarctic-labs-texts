Analysis of risk factors for post-replantation necrosis in complete multi-finger amputation
Authors: Xingang Pang, Peng Xu, Yongjing Huang, Chen Liu, Yong Shen, Zhihai Zhang, Haifeng Shi, Ke Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study aims to summarize the risk factors affecting the survival rate of multiple fingers after replantation.MethodA retrospective analysis was conducted on cases of completely amputated multiple fingers that underwent multi-finger replantation. Patients were divided into survival, partial survival, and necrosis groups based on the replantation outcome. The replanted fingers were categorized into survival and necrosis groups (fingers). The impact of factors on the survival rates of replantation was analyzed.ResultsA total of 201 patients with 650 fingers were included in the study. Univariate analysis showed that the surgery duration was significantly longer in the partial survival group (381.4 min) and the necrosis group (401.9 min) compared to the survival group (321.5 min). The survival rate of replantation in the Tamai zone 3 (84.2 %) was higher than in zone 4 (77.1 %). Multivariate logistic analysis revealed that surgery duration and the amputation plane significantly affected the survival rates of replantation. The rate of vein graft in daytime surgery (29.8 %) was significantly higher than in nighttime surgery (25.8 %).ConclusionIn patients with complete multi-finger amputation, surgery duration and the amputation plane are risk factors affecting the survival rate of multi-finger replantation. Under well-rested conditions, surgeons tend to choose more aggressive finger preservation strategies.

Introduction

The replantation of completely amputated multiple fingers poses a significant challenge for both patients and surgeons.1 Numerous studies have previously focused on the risk factors for replantation necrosis, covering mechanisms of injury, age, ischemia time, and amputation plane, etc.2, 3, 4 However, most of these studies have focused on single-digit amputations without clearly distinguishing between complete and incomplete amputations, or between single- and multi-digit amputations. This study will conduct a retrospective analysis of cases with completely amputated multiple fingers to explore the risk factors for necrosis in multi-finger replantation.

Method

A retrospective analysis was conducted on cases of completely amputated multiple fingers that underwent multi-finger replantation at our center from January 2023 to October 2024. Inclusion criteria: 1) Complete multi-finger amputation, 2) Replantation of two or more fingers; Exclusion criteria: 1) Incomplete multi-finger amputation, 2) Complete multi-finger amputation combined with incomplete amputation of one or more fingers, 3) Only single-finger replantation for complete multi-finger amputation, 4) Single-finger amputation, 5) Combined with severe other limb trauma such as hand or arm amputation. Replantation surgeries were performed by experienced surgeons. Two hand surgeons, including one chief surgeon and one assistant, completed the entire operation For cases with artery defects, superficial palmar veins were harvested intraoperatively for repairing arteries. Depending on the condition of the amputated fingers and their type, heterotopic replantation was performed when necessary.

The data was collected through medical records, anesthesia records, nursing records and test reports. Statistical analysis included the age, gender, mechanism of injury, finger type, amputation plane (Tamai zone), ischemia time, surgery duration, number of vascular anastomoses, whether vein graft(repairing arteries with superficial palmar veins) was performed, whether the surgery was conducted at night, number of amputated fingers, number of replanted fingers, number of necrotic fingers, ratio of replanted fingers to amputated fingers, and whether the chief surgeon’s Global Rating Index for Technical Skills (GRITS)5 score was lower than 4.5. Surgery was defined as nighttime if more than half of the procedure was performed between 20:00 and 08:00.

The mean and standard deviation of the measurement data were calculated and analyzed using ANOVA; the frequency of categorical data was statistically analyzed using the chi-square test. Finally, variables were included in binary or polytomous logistic multivariate regression analysis to calculate the odds ratios (OR) and their 95 % confidence intervals, with a significance level of 0.05. Data were calculated using IBM SPSS statistic 27.

This study received approval from the institutional ethics committee.

Results

From January 2023 to October 2024, our center treated 201 patients with complete multi-finger amputation who underwent multi-finger replantation, including 6 cases with bilateral multi-finger amputations; there were 148 males and 53 females (Figure 1).Figure 1Flowchart of the study participation.Figure 1

Of the patients, 131 had all replanted fingers survive, 52 had one or more digits surviving (at least one finger necrotize with at least one surviving), and 18 had all replanted fingers necrotize.

A total of 650 fingers were amputated, with 538 replanted and 117 necrotic; heterotopic replantation was performed in 7 cases, which included 2 cases of left ring finger to left index finger, 1 case of left index finger to left ring finger, 1 case of right index finger to left middle finger, 1 case of right middle and ring fingers to right thumb and index finger, and 2 cases of left middle finger to left index finger.

Among the replanted fingers, there were 35 thumbs, 143 index fingers, 177 middle fingers, 128 ring fingers, and 55 little fingers; Tamai zone 1 had 11 fingers, zone 2 had 117 fingers, zone 3 had 158 fingers, zone 4 had 205 fingers, and zone 5 had 47 fingers.

Based on the survival of the replanted fingers, the 201 patients were divided into a complete survival group of 131 cases, a partial survival group of 52 cases, and a necrosis group of 18 cases. ANOVA analysis was conducted on age, ischemia time, surgery duration, and the average number of vascular anastomoses per finger among the three groups. Chi-square tests were performed on gender, mechanism of injury, whether vein graft was performed, whether the surgery was at night, the number of replantation, the ratio of replanted fingers to amputated fingers, and whether the chief surgeon’s Global Rating Index for Technical Skills (GRITS) score was lower than 4.5 among the three groups.

Discussion

Multi-finger amputation is considered a difficult-to-treat and disabling hand injury with a poor prognosis,6 and it is also an absolute indication for replantation surgery. Summarizing the risk factors for necrosis in multi-finger replantation can help in the formulation of replantation strategies and the improvement of survival rates.

Advanced age is considered a relative contraindication for amputated finger replantation. The traditional view is that replantation surgery should be carefully selected for elderly patients over 60 years old.7 However, studies by Kwon2 and Barzin8 have shown that there is no significant difference in survival rates and complication rates between patients over 60 and younger patients. This study included 14 patients over 60 years old, including 1 patient over 70 years old, and the research also did not suggest that age affects the replantation outcome. This indicates that age is not an independent risk factor for the survival of replanted fingers.

It is generally believed that the mechanism of injury can impact the replantation outcome9; Kobayashi et al. found in a retrospective analysis of 292 patients that the survival rate of replanted fingers with avulsion/crushing injuries was significantly lower than that of cut-off injuries.10 However, Kaneshiro et al. categorized mechanisms of injury into sharp cuts, blunt cuts, avulsions, and crushes, and found no significant impact of these four mechanisms of injury on replantation outcomes.1 Our study categorized the mechanisms of injury into crushing, avulsion, incision, and bending injuries based on patients' descriptions of the trauma, and neither univariate nor multivariate analyses suggested that the mechanism of injury affected the survival rate of amputated fingers.

Ischemia time was once considered a key factor affecting the survival of amputated fingers. The traditional view is that warm ischemia exceeding 6 h and cold ischemia exceeding 12 h reduce the survival rate of replanted fingers. However, a series of successful delayed replantation cases have called this view into question11, 12, 13, 14; This study also analyzed the impact of ischemia time on replantation in both univariate and multivariate analyses and found no effect on replantation outcomes.

Regarding the impact of the amputation plane on survival rates of replantation, Dec et al.9 suggested that the survival rate of distal phalanx replantation is significantly lower than that of other planes, while Ercin3 found no relationship between the amputation plane and survival rates of replantation. We noted significant differences in survival rates at different amputation planes. Multivariate analysis suggested that the amputation plane is a risk factor for replantation necrosis. The possible reason is that, in cases of multi-digit amputation and replantation, those with replantation of the distal part are often cases with relatively minor injuries and relatively intact severed fingers. For cases with severe injuries, such as combined long-segment arterial injury, severe skin contusion, and comminuted fractures, in the proximal fingers, we can obtain healthy arteries and soft tissues by shortening the phalanges (5–10 mm), but this is difficult to achieve in the distal fingers. In the other words, the distal finger is more likely to lose the condition for replantation under the same degree of injury. This implies that in the enrolled cases, the degree of injury at the distal fingers is milder than that at the proximal and middle fingers, and thus the success rate of proximal replantation is lower in the retrospective analysis.

In our study, both univariate and multivariate analyses showed that surgery duration is an influential factor in the survival of replanted fingers. This may be because the surgery duration is related to the severity of the injury and the difficulty of the surgery. Unlike single-finger amputations, the severity of injury in multi-finger cases cannot be simply distinguished by the mechanism of injury. In cases where the level of replantation skill of the surgeon is similar, the surgery duration directly reflects the difficulty of the replantation.

The surgeon's experience and technical skill will have a decisive impact on the outcome of the operation. Evaluating surgeon’s skill should focus on achieving competencies over simply relying on academic rank, surgical volume, surgical specialty or year of surgery. This study use the Global Index for Technical Skills (GRITS) scale, a valid and reliable tool in the assessment of orthopedic surgical skills15 to quantify the level of surgeons. No significant differences were found in the survival rate of replanted digits between surgeons with a score above 4.5 and those below 4.5 in this study. This is due to the fact that the surgeons who have surgical authority to perform multi-fingers replantation surgery are experienced doctors at our center . Before that, the surgeon needs to practice anastomosis and learn to replant single finger. On the one hand, this reflects the consistency of the surgeon level in this study. On the other hand, this also reveals the shortcomings of this study: the impact of surgical level in reimplantation cannot be fully explored. Nighttime surgery can lead to fatigue in surgeons, which may prompt them to choose simpler and shorter surgical programs. Studies have indicated that nighttime surgery is associated with a higher incidence of adverse events in patients.16, 17, 18 Our analysis showed that the proportion of patients undergoing vascular grafts was higher in daytime surgery, which could be because doctors choose more aggressive replantation strategies when they are well-rested.

Adequate capillary perfusion pressures are necessary for tissue and adequate venous drainage to prevent venous congestion and subsequent edema, increased interstitial space pressures, arterial insufficiency, and the accumulation of metabolites. Anastomosing as many arteries and veins as possible can increase the survival rate of replanted fingers.19,20 A meta-analysis21 indicates that digit replant survival rates of 50.3 % with zero arterial anastomosis, 84.2 % with one arterial anastomosis, and 90.0 % with two arterial anastomoses;61.1 % survival rates for digits replanted with zero venous anastomosis, 73 % for those with one venous anastomosis, and 92.3 % with two venous anastomoses after analyzing the pooled dataset. Chaivanichsiri22 held that survival was directly proportional to the number of vessels repaired, especially veins In this study, univariate chi-square test and univariate logistic regression analysis did not suggest that the ratio of arterial and venous anastomosis affected the result of replantation. However, multivariate regression analysis after the inclusion of replantation fingers showed that the ratio of arteries and venous anastomosis was 1:2 as a risk factor for replantation necrosis, suggesting that the ratio of arteriovenous anastomosis was related to the ratio of replantation fingers to some extent. Although some scholars23 held that two vein could accommodate the outflow from two arterial repairs, we recommend 2:3 artery to vein ratio for arteriovenous repair when conditions permit considering the fact that salvage from reoperation for venous occlusion it was only 7 %24

There are some limitations in this study. Firstly, it is a retrospective study; secondly, we lack a classification of the injury severity of amputated fingers, which prevents us from analyzing the impact of injury severity on replantation survival; thirdly, for some statistical results, we cannot accurately interpret them, such as the impact of the mechanism of injury on the number of replantation and vessel anastomoses, and we cannot rule out selection bias; fourthly, the study only focuses on the survival of replanted fingers and does not address functional recovery after replantation.

Conclusion

We conducted a retrospective study on cases of completely amputated multiple fingers that underwent multi-finger replantation. The results indicate that the duration of the replantation surgery and the amputation plane are risk factors affecting the survival of replanted fingers. Compared to nighttime surgery, daytime surgery showed no difference in survival rates of replantation, but the proportion of vascular grafts was significantly higher in daytime surgery, suggesting that under well-rested conditions, there is a tendency to choose more aggressive finger preservation strategies during surgery.
