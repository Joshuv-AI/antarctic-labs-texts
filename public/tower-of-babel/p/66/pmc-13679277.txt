Interventions to prevent sexual harassment in universities: an umbrella review of systematic reviews
Authors: Francesca Dal Maso, Valeria Donisi, Lisa Anegg, Maria Grazia Lourdes Monaco, Giulia Turrini, Michela Nosè, Antonio Lasalvia, Angela Carta, Silvia Ferro, Ottavia Santarelli, Nicoletta Zerman, Vanesa Pérez-Martinez, Carmen Vives-Cases, Stefano Porru, Lidia Del Piccolo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis umbrella review analyzes existing literature on sexual harassment (SH) prevention in universities, including approaches that address SH within broader gender-based violence (GBV) forms. Conducted as part of the Uni4Equity project (CERV-2022-DAPHNE Programme), it aims to identify key trends, limitations and gaps in current prevention strategies.MethodA systematic search of four databases (PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and PsycINFO) was conducted to identify relevant systematic reviews evaluating interventions aimed at preventing SH (and related GBV forms) in university settings. Reviews were selected if they (1) demonstrated methodological requirements (e.g., detailed search strategies, pre-registered protocol), (2) were published in English between 2000 and 2024, (3) involved adult populations (students, staff).ResultsWe identified 17 systematic reviews meeting these criteria, whose methodological quality was rated as “critically low” according to the AMSTAR-2. Notably, none of the included reviews focused specifically on SH, and the available evidence derived from interventions targeting broader forms of GBV. The most consistent results came from bystander intervention programs, which effectively improved knowledge, attitudes, and self-efficacy, though they had limited impact on actual behavior change. Primary prevention programs addressing sexual and relationship violence had mixed results, while self-defense and risk reduction programs showed limited evidence of effectiveness. Social minorities, including LGBTQ + populations, were notably underrepresented in the studies reviewed.ConclusionsFindings highlight the need for comprehensive, campus-wide strategies that go beyond individual-focused interventions. These strategies should integrate education, policy reforms, awareness campaigns, and staff training. Moreover, they must be inclusive and intersectional, addressing the different layers of gender relations—social, institutional, and interpersonal. Methodological rigor is also essential to develop sustainable and culturally relevant solutions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28435-5.

Introduction

Sexual harassment (SH) can be defined as any unwanted verbal, non-verbal, physical, or digital conduct of a sexual nature that has the purpose or effect of violating a person’s dignity, particularly when it creates an intimidating, hostile, degrading, humiliating, or offensive environment. [1]. In university settings, SH may include unwanted sexual comments, jokes, gestures, messages, images, repeated sexual advances, inappropriate touching, coercive requests, or abuses of power linked to academic or professional roles. These behaviors, whether occurring face-to-face or through digital channels, are recognized as abusive and perpetuate societal power imbalances, disproportionately affecting women [2]. Often, it is difficult to define the boundary between SH and broader forms of violence such as gender-based violence, sexual abuse, sexual assault, and psychological harassment. This conceptual and terminological ambiguity complicates theoretical understanding as well as legal, research, and prevention efforts [3–5].

Within this broader framework, SH could be conceptualized as part of the continuum of sexual violence (SV) [6], representing its earlier and less explicit manifestations [7–9].

In university settings, SH is a pressing concern. In the United States, an estimated 20–25% of women and 7–8% of men experience SH during their university education, with especially high rates among women under 30 [10, 11]. This period is sometimes referred to as the "Red Zone" to emphasize the heightened risk [12]. In Europe, prevalence rates are similar, with approximately 23% of women reporting experiences of SH or abuse during their studies [13]. Although research rarely provides staff-specific prevalence data [14–16], some evidence shows that they are also affected: for example, at the Complutense University of Madrid, 11.2% of administrative employees and 12.7% of teachers reported SH—higher rates than those observed among students (5.7%) [17]. Studies also indicate that a significant proportion of men engage in such behaviors, underscoring the need for targeted preventive measures addressing this group [18]. University environments are characterized by power dynamics—often shaped by hierarchical relationships between students, faculty and administrative staff—that can create conditions conducive to SH [19–21]. These imbalances are reinforced by cultural and contextual factors such as male-dominated environments, sexist attitudes, and the persistence of traditional gender norms, all of which contribute to the normalization and underestimation of harassment [19, 22–24]. Moreover, the diversity of university populations increases risk exposure, particularly for individuals from marginalized groups based on gender identity, sexual orientation, ethnicity, or disability [19, 25–27]. Minority groups—especially LGBTQ + individuals and ethnic minorities—face disproportionately high risks due to intersecting forms of discrimination and social exclusion [28–33]. For instance, LGBTQ + individuals are up to three times more likely to experience SH than their heterosexual peers [34]. Preventing SH and other GBV forms is a complex challenge with far-reaching consequences for victims’ physical, mental, social and economic well-being, as well as for institutional climate and academic performance. Psychological impacts include depression, anxiety, and post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) alongside potential physical health consequences such as sexually transmitted infections [35, 36]. Academically, harassment can result in concentration difficulties, delayed graduation, or university withdrawal [37]. Among university staff, SH is often linked to burnout, absenteeism, and turnover, which undermine well-being and productivity [38–40]. In response, various policies and measures have been implemented at both international and national levels. In the United States, Title IX prohibits gender discrimination in educational institutions receiving public funding, while the Campus Sexual Violence Elimination (SaVE) Act [41] introduced additional provisions to prevent and address sexual violence on university campuses. In Europe, many universities have adopted similar policies, though regulations vary considerably across countries [15, 42]. Despite these efforts, identifying effective and sustainable long-term solutions remains challenging. The literature on violence prevention is extensive, encompassing numerous studies and reviews [43, 44]. In line with the conceptual overlap outlined above, many interventions addressing sexual harassment are embedded within broader programs targeting gender-based violence (GBV), SV, sexual assault (SA) and intimate partner violence (IPV), often focusing on awareness, consent, and active bystander strategies. This reflects the current state of the literature, in which SH prevention is rarely examined independently, and interventions are often developed and evaluated within broader frameworks addressing different form of GBV. To the best of our knowledge, no universally accepted classification system for these interventions exists. Overall, they can be grouped by the target populations involved (e.g., athletes, potential perpetrators, potential victims, general bystanders) and intended outcomes (e.g., improving consent-related behaviors, enhancing self-defense skills, promoting active bystander strategies, etc.) [45]. While some approaches are promising, findings remain heterogeneous and often contradictory [46], leaving open questions about which models are the most effective in specific contexts.

Given this complexity, the aim of this umbrella review was to collect, integrate, and summarize the evidence on strategies to prevent and manage SH in university settings, drawing on systematic reviews and meta-analyses, while highlighting areas of consensus and disagreement and evidence gaps in current strategies.

Methods

This umbrella review was preregistered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO; Registration Number: CRD42024597243) and conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [47]. The review was conducted as part of the Uni4Equity project (CERV-2022-DAPHNE Programme, European Union), which provided a broader framework and context for the study [48].

Eligibility criteria and outcomes of interest

Systematic reviews, with or without meta-analysis, as well as scoping reviews including any type of preventive intervention—whether primary, secondary or tertiary prevention—targeting potential victims, bystanders or perpetrators of different forms of GBV were eligible for inclusion. This because SH in higher education is rarely studied in isolation and interventions frequently target different forms of violence (GBV, SV, IPV and SH). Information sources and searching strategy

A systematic search of the literature was conducted up to February 2025 to identify systematic reviews of studies evaluating interventions aimed at preventing SH in universities. To ensure a comprehensive evidence base by preventing the omission of critical data, an inclusive search strategy considering also SH-related domains was adopted. Accordingly, a comprehensive search (comprising any type of intervention, including prevention) of four databases (PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science and PsycINFO) was conducted using a combination of controlled vocabulary (MeSH terms) and free-text keywords related to SH (e.g., “sexual harassment”, “sexual violence”, “gender-based violence”), academic environments (e.g., “university”, “college”, “campus”, “academia”). The search was limited to reviews published in English between 2000 and 2024. Full search strings are provided in the Appendix (see Table 3 in the Appendix). Reference lists of all eligible review articles were also hand-searched to identify additional relevant systematic reviews. The selection process was documented in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [47].

Selection process and data extraction

Two review authors (LA and GT) independently screened titles and abstracts for inclusion using Rayyan [49]. Articles rated as possible candidates by either reviewer were added to a preliminary list, and their full texts were retrieved. Working independently and in duplicate, the two review authors then assessed the full texts for eligibility. Discrepancies were resolved by consensus or, if necessary, through discussion with a third researcher (VD). Two review authors (FDM and LA) independently extracted data, with disagreements resolved by a third reviewer (VD). For each included review, the following information was collected: geographical and temporal focus (see Table 4 in the Appendix), the type of review (e.g., systematic review, meta-analysis), number of included studies, types of interventions (e.g., distinguishing between “Bystander Programs”, “Primary prevention Programs on Sexual and Relationship Violence”, “Self-Defense & Risk Reduction Programs”), sample characteristics (e.g., population type/role, age range, ethnicity, specific groups composition), delivery format and duration of interventions, outcome, main findings (see Table 5 in the Appendix) and assessment tools implemented (see Table 2). To facilitate systematic comparison across studies, both interventions and outcomes were categorized using a structured framework. Interventions were grouped into three main types: (1) bystander programs; (2) prevention programs on sexual and relationship; and (3) self-defense and risk reduction programs. Where possible, outcomes were classified into seven domains based on the construct assessed: (1) knowledge; (2) attitudes and beliefs; (3) intentions; (4) self-efficacy; (5) behaviors; (6) victimization; and (7) perpetration. This classification was used to organize the summary tables and to guide the descriptive synthesis of findings (see Table 1 and Table 5 in the Appendix). Consistent with the evidence-mapping purpose of this umbrella review, the results section is intended primarily to provide a descriptive synthesis of intervention characteristics, targeted outcomes, and recurring patterns across reviews.

Methodological quality of systematic reviews

The methodological quality of the included reviews was independently assessed by two reviewers using the AMSTAR-2 tool [50]. This instrument comprises 16 items, including 7 considered critical domains: prior registration of a protocol, comprehensiveness of the literature search, justification for the exclusion of studies, assessment of risk of bias in included studies, appropriateness of meta-analytic methods, consideration of the risk of bias when interpreting the findings and evaluation of the presence and potential impact of publication bias. Each review was rated as high, moderate, low, or critically low quality by two independent reviewers (FDM and LA). The quality appraisal also took into account relevant methodological limitations highlighted by Puljak et al. [51].

Primary studies overlap assessment

To ensure the independence and robustness of the synthesized findings, we assessed the potential risk of data distortion arising from the inclusion of the same primary studies across multiple systematic reviews (the "double-counting" bias). Following standard methodological guidelines for umbrella reviews, we constructed a comprehensive citation matrix mapping all included systematic reviews against their respective primary studies. Furthermore, we quantitatively evaluated the degree of overlap by calculating the Corrected Covered Area (CCA) proposed by Pieper et al. [52]. The resulting CCA percentage was interpreted according to the thresholds established by Pieper et al. [52]: slight overlap (0–5%), moderate overlap (6–10%), high overlap (11–15%), and very high overlap (> 15%).

Results

Review and primary study selection

As shown in the PRISMA flow diagram (see Fig. 1), the electronic search identified 1131 records across four databases: PsycINFO (n = 29), PubMed (n = 134), Scopus (n = 509), and Web of Science (n = 459). After removing 282 duplicates using Rayyan, 849 records remained for title and abstract screening. Of these, 754 were excluded for not meeting eligibility criteria. The full texts of 95 articles were then assessed for eligibility, resulting in the exclusion of 78 articles for the following reasons: inappropriate publication type (n = 10), unsuitable target population (n = 21; e.g., adult participants not affiliated with the university context), or irrelevant outcomes (n = 47; e.g., focusing on mobbing or bullying). The final sample included in this umbrella review comprises 17 reviews.Fig. 1PRISMA flow diagram. Articles selection process

Review characteristics

Most of the included reviews were systematic reviews (n = 13) [45, 46, 53–64], several of which were accompanied by meta-analyses (n = 8) [46, 53–59]; four were systematic scoping reviews [61, 65–67]. The included reviews allowed for the following study design: randomized controlled trials (RCTs, n = 12), quasi-experimental designs (QEDs, n = 11), and other designs (n = 8). Overall, the 17 reviews included a substantial number of primary studies, ranging from 7 to 106 per review, with an average of approximately 32 articles per review. Geographically, the primary studies were predominantly conducted in North America, particularly the United States, although some reviews incorporated data from other international contexts, such as Vietnam and South Korea [65] and Europe [56]. Regarding publication trends, most studies were concentrated in two periods: 2018–2020 (n = 5, 21%) and 2023–2024 (n = 12, 71%), despite the present search covering publications from the year 2000 onward. Further details are provided in Table 4 in the Appendix.

Methodological quality of the systematic reviews

All included reviews—both with and without meta-analysis—were rated as “critically low quality” according to the AMSTAR-2 tool. The full assessment is presented in Fig. 2. The most recurrent weaknesses were: (1) lack of justification for excluded studies (Item 7); (2) absence or inadequate reporting of risk of bias assessments for included studies (Item 9); (3) failure to report funding sources of primary studies (Item 10); and (4) lack of integration of risk of bias considerations when interpreting results (Item 13). Nonetheless, several reviews demonstrated strengths, including: (1) clearly formulated research questions (Item 1); (2) adequately describing study selection and data extraction methods (Items 5 and 6) and, (4) in some cases, comprehensive searches across multiple databases (Item 4).Fig. 2AMSTAR-2 Characteristics of systematic reviews and meta-analyses of included reviews

Overlap analysis and corrected covered area

The 17 included systematic reviews (c = 17) contained a total of 538 primary study citations (N = 538). After removing duplicates and cross-checking the references, a total of 366 unique primary studies (r = 366) were identified. The full mapping of these primary studies across the included systematic reviews is presented in the Citation Matrix (see Supplementary Material). The calculated Corrected Covered Area (CCA) for this umbrella review was 0.029 (or 2.94%). According to the criteria outlined by Pieper et al. (2014), this value falls well within the 0–0.05 range, indicating a slight overlap among the primary evidence base. This objective metric demonstrates that the systematic reviews included in this study target largely independent bodies of literature, thereby confirming that the risk of bias or artificial inflation of findings due to double-counting is negligible.

Characteristics of the Target Population

The 17 included systematic reviews focused primarily on university student populations, only one review [54] included a small number of studies conducted in secondary school settings (2 out of 14 studies), which were not considered in the present synthesis. Zara et al. [64] included studies addressing trainees and academic staff in addition to students. In most primary studies, participants had a mean age between 18 and 25 years and were predominantly cisgender and heterosexual, with limited representation of sexual and gender minorities, international students or ethnic minorities. Several reviews also considered high-risk or particularly relevant subgroups, such as fraternity/sorority members (n = 7), college athletes (n = 6) and single-sex groups (male or female; n = 9). However, reporting of socio-demographic characteristics—such as ethnicity, socioeconomic status, and sexual orientation—was often incomplete or missing. The main characteristics of the included populations are summarized in Table 5 in the Appendix.

Types of interventions and targeted outcomes

As previously described, interventions were categorized according to their core objectives and theoretical orientation into three main groups: (1) bystander programs (n = 15); (2) primary prevention programs on sexuality and relationships (n = 13) and (3) self-defense and risk reduction programs (n = 5). For each intervention category, outcomes were systematically analyzed across the eight identified domains: knowledge (n = 9), attitudes/beliefs (n = 11), intentions (n = 8), self-efficacy (n = 9), behaviors (n = 11), victimization (n = 6), and perpetration (n = 9).

Since several interventions included in this review addressed broader forms of GBV (e.g., SV, SA, IPV), findings are reported using the terminology adopted by the original reviews, reflecting the primary GBV-related concepts on which they focused. Bystander programs. This category includes interventions designed to promote prosocial behaviors and a sense of collective responsibility among peers, encouraging them to effectively recognize and respond to high-risk situations associated with sexual violence and harassment. These programs are grounded in situational models of intervention [68, 69] (e.g., the five-step model of bystander action: noticing the event, interpreting it as a problem, assuming responsibility, knowing how to help, and implementing help) that conceptually distinguish from more traditional prevention approaches by their explicit shift from an individual victim–perpetrator framework to a community-oriented model of responsibility, framing gender-based violence prevention as a collective task and positioning peers as active agents in creating a social environment that discourages and interrupts harmful behaviors. Fifteen of the seventeen reviews included in this umbrella review reported evidence on this type of intervention, and ten of these provided sufficient information on the impact for at least one of the outcomes listed in Table 1. The specific programs and their related findings are detailed in Table 5 in the Appendix.

These interventions primarily targeted university students aged between 18 and 26, including high-risk groups such as fraternity members and athletes. Program duration ranged from brief 24-min sessions to multi-week interventions. A substantial proportion of interventions included interactive and skill-based components designed to translate awareness into behavioral competence, such as structured role-playing exercises based on realistic scenarios (e.g., party-related risk situations, peer-perpetrated sexist comments, …), guided rehearsal of intervention strategies (e.g., the “5D” model), peer-led training sessions, and group problem-solving activities. Some programs also use theatre-based skits (e.g., SCREAM) or video modeling (e.g., TakeCARE) to simulate intervention opportunities within realistic social contexts. Discussion

The objective of this umbrella review was to map and synthesize the evidence on the available interventions to prevent SH in universities, including interventions that address SH and related constructs within broader gender-based violence forms. Although the search covered a broad time span (2000–2024), the scientific output is concentrated in two main periods: 2018–2020 (21%) and, more prominently, 2023–2024 (71%). This pattern indicates rapid growth. This recent surge can be linked to policy developments and cultural shifts that have brought sexual misconduct in higher education to the forefront. In particular – in the USA—the reinforcement of Title IX regulations, enactment of the 2013 Campus Sexual Violence Elimination Act and the proactive stance of the Obama administration laid important groundwork [70]. At the same time, social movements such as #MeToo and #TimeIsUp heightened public awareness and institutional accountability, creating a favorable climate for research and intervention development [15, 42]. The seventeen review studies included in this umbrella review provide a broad overview of the international landscape on SH and related constructs of SV, GBV, and IPV prevention interventions in university settings. The evidence base is heavily concentrated in North America—particularly in the United States. This geographic concentration implies that the reported results may be closely tied to the specific socio-legal framework of U.S. universities.

None of the included reviews focused exclusively on SH; instead, SH might be considered situated within a broader continuum of abusive behaviors. This aligns with the perspective highlighted by Zara et al. [64] that various forms of GBV are interconnected and rooted in power dynamics and shared socio-cultural norms also reproduced within academic environments [71]. As a result, many SV, GBV, and IPV prevention strategies were included and described in the present review as relevant also to SH prevention. This is based on the assumption that they may also address earlier or less overt manifestations of abuse [8].

Importantly, all included systematic reviews were rated as “critically low” quality according to AMSTAR-2. This has important implications for the interpretation of the findings presented in this review. While recurring patterns across intervention types can be identified, the underlying evidence base is methodologically limited, and conclusions regarding effectiveness should therefore be interpreted with caution.

Finally, the reviews examined reported frequent use of validated instruments such as the Illinois Rape Myth Acceptance Scale (IRMA) [72], the Bystander Efficacy Scale [73] and the Sexual Experiences Survey [74]. However, the marked heterogeneity of measurement instruments and the prevalence of self-report measures limit the comparability of findings across studies and introduce potential social desirability bias [54].

Bystander programs remain among the most widespread and promising strategies. Grounded in the Bystander Effect model [68], they aim to counteract the diffusion of responsibility by fostering early risk recognition, personal responsibility, and the ability to act. In academic settings, this approach is intended to foster prosocial behavior and the development of collective responsibility [64, 75] framing gender-based violence forms as a community issue to be addressed through the active involvement of the entire university population [65, 76], rather than as a problem exclusively concerning victims and perpetrators. Evidence suggests consistent improvements in knowledge, attitudes, self-efficacy and intervention intentions, although the effects on actual behavior remain weak and tend to diminish over time [45, 53, 56, 60, 62, 64]. Furthermore, these programs predominantly target men-students, largely excluding women, despite their potential value as active bystanders [59, 73, 77–81]. Effectiveness appears to depend largely on the degree of interactivity, the duration of the intervention, and the alignment between program content and contextual relevance [50, 53, 64]. Interventions incorporating experiential and skill-based components—such as role-playing activities, realistic campus scenarios, peer-led discussions and rehearsal of intervention strategies— seem to be more likely to strengthen behavioral readiness and intervention confidence [46, 53, 54, 65, 66].

Prevention programs on sexual and relationship violence generally seek to shift attitudes and cultural norms by increasing awareness of consent, healthy relationships, empathy toward victims, while also challenging rape myths. Knowledge gains are the most consistently reported outcomes [58], whereas evidence regarding effects on intentions, self-efficacy, and behaviors remain mixed [56, 63]. As with bystander interventions, these programs are primarily delivered to students and often focus specifically on men populations (e.g., “The Men’s Program, Coaching Boys into Men”), thereby offering only a partial view of the range of possible prevention strategies.

Self-defense and risk exposure/perpetration reduction programs—though less common— have roots in feminist movements and focus primarily on women’s capacity to recognize and respond to risky situations through assertiveness, situational awareness and resistance strategies. Content typically includes personal safety, assertiveness, defensive techniques and responsible alcohol use. Positive effects have been observed mainly in the reduction of female victimization, while impacts on perpetration are limited or absent [55, 59]. However, these outcomes are often underexplored and based on self-report measures, which may have contributed to underestimation of effects in this area. Regarding the delivery of content related to personal safety and responsible alcohol use, some authors have highlighted potential risks inherent in this approach: placing excessive emphasis on women’s individual responsibility may inadvertently reinforce rape myths, contribute to victim-blaming and discourage the engagement of others in prevention efforts [55]. Moreover, they appear to be less effective in producing behavioral change and remain poorly inclusive of students with non-binary gender identities or male survivors of violence [55].

General considerations on the interventions and outcomes

Across all intervention types, a recurring issue is the persistent heteronormative and binary framing that positions GBV forms primarily as male-perpetrated and female-experienced. While this reflects prevalence data indicating that women are disproportionately affected [10, 11, 13], it may unintentionally overlook other experiences —such as the victimization of men. The reliance on relatively homogeneous populations in the current evidence base—primarily cisgender, heterosexual students—raises critical questions regarding the transferability of intervention outcomes to LGBTQ + individuals, who also face heightened risk [34]. Because these programs have been evaluated on narrow samples, their effectiveness in more diverse contexts remains insufficiently established. A more intersectional approach therefore appears both urgent and necessary [48, 82], to avoid the risk of implicit marginalization of group-specific pathways.

Another key limitation is the modest medium- to long-term impact of interventions on concrete behavioral outcomes such as perpetration or victimization. Most studies are based on pre–post evaluations and lack long-term follow-up, limiting the ability to assess the durability of observed effects and to determine whether participants had real opportunities to apply the knowledge they acquired in real-world contexts [57, 65]. Outcomes that show the most consistent improvement tend to be cognitive or motivational in nature (e.g., knowledge, attitudes, self-efficacy), while behavioral effects are often weak, inconsistent or supported by methodologically fragile evidence [46, 58, 59, 62]. This pattern may reflect not only limitations in intervention effectiveness, but also structural weaknesses in study design and outcome measurement. This gap may partly reflect the distinction highlighted by McCall et al. [83]: “changing understanding is one thing, changing behavior is another”. In this context, the apparent effectiveness of interventions on intermediate outcomes (such as knowledge, attitudes, and bystander intentions) should not be interpreted as conclusive evidence of real-world behavioral impact, but rather as an indication of potential trajectories that require confirmation through higher-quality primary studies and more robust synthesis designs. Nevertheless, raising awareness of the problem and how to prevent it remains a critical step toward fostering cultural change and impacting the broader campus environment. Importantly, such knowledge must be translated into practical skills, as intervening without adequate preparation may lead to inappropriate or even counterproductive behaviors [65, 84]. Evidence suggests that the quality and depth of learning—defined as the integration of knowledge and skills—are key to enhancing both intention and effectiveness in bystander intervention. In this sense, bystander-based programs would represent a notable exception, as they combine cognitive/motivational components with practical behavioral training, and thus appear to lead to comparatively stronger and more consistent effects on actual bystander behaviors. Program structure also influences outcomes. More effective interventions tend to have a longer duration [53], incorporate interactive components (e.g., role-playing, realistic scenarios, peer-led discussions, rehearsal of intervention strategies) [56, 67] and could be reinforced by complementary strategies such as social campaigns, visual materials or follow-up sessions [53]. In contrast, brief online programs without practical experiential elements tend to yield primarily superficial, knowledge-based outcomes without significantly strengthening participants’ capacity to act [56, 85]. These findings suggest that repeated exposure and ongoing reinforcement may be necessary to counteract the natural decay of cognitive gains and support the consolidation of long-term behavioral change.

Moreover, these results may also be interpreted through a socio-ecological perspective. As suggested in prior work [65, 86], increases in individual responsibility and prosocial readiness may be undermined in contexts where explicit institutional support is perceived as insufficient or inconsistent, and where clear policies for victims-bystanders protection, reporting procedures, and perpetrator accountability are lacking. In such conditions, educational efforts risk operating in a structural vacuum that may inadvertently constrain rather than facilitate action, as participants may not perceive the organizational legitimacy or safety necessary to translate intentions into behavior [64, 86, 87]. This may help explain why many individual-level interventions are effective in improving knowledge, attitudes, and intentions, yet produce more limited effects on sustained behavioral change. When the structural drivers of harassment remain unchanged, individual gains may be insufficient to alter behaviors. Therefore, integrating prevention interventions into multi-layered, multidimensional strategies consistent with the socio-ecological model is essential [64]. This integration requires moving beyond isolated approaches and developing programs that synergistically combine educational efforts, institutional policies, targeted awareness campaigns, and ongoing training for university staff [64]. Notably, only one of the reviews included in this umbrella analysis [64] specifically addressed interventions for university staff, despite clear evidence that faculty and administrative personnel also experience SH. This gap underscores the importance of designing and implementing prevention strategies that actively involve staff alongside students [48]. While the transition toward a “whole-campus” model [64, 75] is increasingly advocated as a systemic ideal to involve the entire university community, it currently remains a primarily theoretical framework; its empirical validation is still constrained by a literature that focuses almost exclusively on interventions targeting exclusively undergraduate populations. Nevertheless, recent scholarship and policy initiatives increasingly emphasize the need for comprehensive institutional responses that actively engage all members of the university community—including students, staff, and institutional leadership—and address not only individual behaviors but also the structural and cultural conditions that enable SH within universities [88–90]. In line with this integrated approach, notable European initiatives have already begun addressing the prevalence and prevention of SH in university contexts. Two such examples include the recently concluded UniSAFE project (https://unisafe-gbv.euhttps://unisafe-gbv.eu) and Uni4Equity project (a project funded by the CERV-2022-DAPHNE Programme of the European Union) [48]. Taken together, future prevention efforts must prioritize theoretical coherence, inclusivity, and sustainability. Achieving effective and lasting outcomes requires an intersectional and holistic approach that engages the entire academic community [89, 91](such as: Rectorate, Parity Committee for Equal Opportunities, Confidential Counsellor, Disciplinary Committee, Occupational Health Service, Psychological services, Human Resources, etc.) and is grounded in robust empirical evidence. Only through such an approach will it be possible to implement equitable, culturally sensitive and transformative interventions that can be adapted to diverse educational contexts [91].

Limitations of this review

Several limitations of the present umbrella review should be acknowledged. A significant limitation is the lack of systematic reviews focusing exclusively on SH. In the available literature, SH is typically examined alongside broader and conceptually overlapping constructs such as SV, GBV, and IPV, and considered within broader GBV interventions. This has important implications for the interpretation of the current findings, as conclusions regarding SH-specific prevention strategies are based indirectly on evidence generated from broader GBV forms prevention frameworks and should therefore be interpreted with caution. Future research should more clearly distinguish SH from related forms of gender-based violence and develop and evaluate SH-specific prevention interventions to determine whether they produce different outcomes than broader violence prevention programs.

A further limitation concerns the lack of an integrative synthesis of the mechanisms underlying intervention effectiveness. Due to the substantial heterogeneity of intervention models, theoretical frameworks, and outcome measures, as well as variability in the way potential mechanisms of change were described across the included reviews, makes it difficult to identify which mechanisms are most likely to promote behavioral change. Future research would benefit from more theory-driven analyses to better clarify these processes.

From a methodological perspective, the overall quality of the included reviews was rated as "critically low" according to the AMSTAR-2 checklist [50], primarily due to inadequate justification for excluded studies, lack of systematic bias assessment, limited transparency about funding sources and insufficient integration of risk of bias into results interpretation. Moreover, as highlighted by Puljak et al. [51], the inclusion of non-comparative primary studies represents an additional methodological weakness, further limiting adherence to PICO criteria and AMSTAR-2 standards. Importantly, these limitations reflect broader structural weaknesses in the current evidence base on SH and related GBV forms of prevention, rather than limitations of the present umbrella review alone. Although we applied predefined methodological criteria to ensure a minimum level of transparency and reproducibility, these criteria do not capture several critical domains assessed by AMSTAR-2. As a result, the available body of systematic reviews remains affected by substantial methodological shortcomings that constrain the strength and reliability of the conclusions that can be drawn. Taken together, these limitations suggest that the apparent effectiveness of several interventions, should not be interpreted as conclusive evidence, but rather as indicative of emerging trends that require confirmation through higher-quality primary studies and more rigorous systematic reviews. Additionally, the available evidence is skewed toward U.S. contexts, with study populations that are not very diverse—primarily cisgender, heterosexual, white students residing on campus. This geographic and demographic concentration is largely driven by specific U.S. regulatory frameworks, such as Title IX, which mandate and fund prevention efforts, thereby limiting the direct transferability of these findings to different international academic or legal systems. The experiences of LGBTQ + students, international students, ethnic minorities and academic staff are rarely considered [55, 59]. Moreover, reporting of socio-demographic variables—such as ethnicity, socioeconomic status and sexual orientation—was frequently incomplete or entirely absent, hindering a nuanced understanding of intervention effectiveness across diverse or minority groups. This overall lack of representativeness significantly limits the transferability of findings to broader cultural and institutional contexts.

Strengths of this review

The review offers also significant contributions. Its systematic approach, adhering to PRISMA guidelines, ensures rigorous data extraction and synthesis of the available literature. A major strength of this umbrella review lies in the objective verification of data independence through the calculation of the Corrected Covered Area (CCA). The observed value confirms that the risk of bias from double-counting primary studies is negligible, reinforcing—within the inherent limitations of the included literature—the reliability of the synthesized evidence. By providing a comprehensive synthesis of the available evidence, this umbrella may serve as a resource for researchers, educators, and university policymakers, offering a foundation for developing best practices and implementing increasingly effective prevention programs across diverse international academic settings.

Conclusion

This umbrella review underscores the urgent need to move beyond fragmented and short-lived initiatives to prevent SH—and related GBV forms—toward comprehensive, multidimensional strategies consistent with the socio-ecological model. Effective prevention in university settings requires integration of educational programs, institutional policies, awareness campaigns, and staff training, embedded within a whole-campus approach that actively involves all members of the academic community.

Inclusivity and intersectionality must be prioritized to overcome the persistent heteronormative and binary framing of interventions and to ensure responsiveness to the experiences of marginalized groups. Future prevention efforts should be guided by greater methodological rigor, theoretical coherence, and international collaboration, in order to develop culturally sensitive and sustainable interventions capable of producing lasting change.
