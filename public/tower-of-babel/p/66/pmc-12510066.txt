Discordant phenotypic outcomes in monozygotic twins conceived via IVF: A case report involving Hirschsprung's disease, tricuspid and pulmonary atresia, and orofacial clefts without detectable genetic mutation
Authors: Sonia Gayete-Lafuente, Umar Mian, Elizabeth Choong, David H Barad, Norbert Gleicher
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis report concerns the incidental finding of discordant congenital anomalies in twins diagnosed as monozygotic in a private, academically-affiliated fertility center in the United States. A 40-year-old woman conceived monozygotic dichorionic twins via in vitro fertilization (IVF). One twin presented with multiple congenital anomalies, including tricuspid and pulmonary atresia, cleft lip/palate, Hirschsprung's disease, facial skin tags, and a benign ocular tumor, while the other twin presented none of those abnormalities and appeared entirely healthy. Genetic testing, including amniocentesis and whole-exome sequencing, confirmed no identifiable mutations. This case emphasizes the potential role of postzygotic mosaicism, epigenetic dysregulation, and/or environmental disruption of neural crest-derived development, highlighting the complexity of embryologic development.Keywords: Monozygotic twins, IVF, Congenital anomalies, Case report

Introduction

Monozygotic (MZ) twins originate from a single fertilized zygote and are typically considered genetically identical. However, phenotypic discordance, including congenital anomalies, has been well documented [1]. Such discordance is more common in anomalies involving neural crest cell derivatives, suggesting a developmental rather than purely genetic origin [2].

Assisted reproductive technology (ART), especially in vitro fertilization (IVF), is associated with a higher incidence of MZ twinning, possibly due to extended embryo culture, zona pellucida manipulation during intracytoplasmic sperm insemination (ICSI), or changes in the in vitro microenvironment [3,4]. Though rare, MZ dichorionic (DC) twinning is increasingly observed in IVF and likely results from early embryo cleavage. Phenotypic discordance in ART-conceived twins, especially without identifiable genetic mutations, suggests roles for epigenetic alterations, somatic mosaicism, and environmental stressors [5]. This case highlights MZ DC twins conceived via IVF, with one twin developing a constellation of congenital anomalies involving neural crest tissues, while the other was unaffected.

Case Presentation

A 40-year-old woman (G8P2) with a history of suspected immune-mediated pregnancy loss underwent IVF. Seven of eight retrieved oocytes were mature and were subjected to ICSI, resulting in five cleavage-stage embryos. Three day-3 embryos (grades 8A4, 8B3, 6B3) were transferred fresh after initiation of an immune-suppressive protocol that included low-dose (81 mg) aspirin, low-dose prednisone (varying levels between 10 and 40 mg/day), and 40 g intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG).

Early obstetric (OB) ultrasounds showed a DC twin pregnancy. Noninvasive prenatal testing (NIPT) at 13 weeks of gestation later confirmed that the twins were MZ, and the patient was discharged into OB care by a maternal fetal medicine unit. At 18 weeks, fetal echocardiography and advanced OB ultrasound revealed that twin B had severe structural anomalies, including tricuspid atresia, pulmonary atresia, cleft lip and palate, and intrauterine growth restriction while twin A was anatomically normal - a surprising disparity considering their monozygosity, where identical twin phenotypes would be expected.

Amniocentesis and whole-exome sequencing for both fetuses identified no pathogenic mutations or copy number variants. At 37 weeks, the twins were delivered via C-section. Twin A was 6 lb. and entirely healthy. Twin B was 4 lb. 9 oz. and exhibited multiple anomalies: facial skin tags, a benign ocular tumor, and a meconium plug causing abdominal distention. Twin B underwent surgical correction of his intestinal obstruction, which confirmed Hirschsprung's disease (histopathological absence of ganglion cells in the distal colon). Twin B required three surgeries during the neonatal period and was initially managed in the cardiac intensive care unit (CICU), pending a planned second open-heart repair. Twin B has since been transferred to the cardiac step-down unit following a successful second cardiac surgery. Current management focuses on optimizing enteral feeding tolerance in anticipation of subsequent procedures, including cleft lip and palate revision, excision of a limbal dermoid, and gastrostomy tube (G-tube) placement. In contrast, twin A continues to demonstrate normal growth and neurodevelopment without complications.

Discussion

Phenotypic discordance in monozygotic twins, especially following ART, presents a biological paradox: how can genetically identical individuals develop such divergent outcomes? The affected twin in this case presented with anomalies derived from neural crest cells, including the cardiac outflow tract, enteric ganglia, facial mesenchyme, and melanocytes. These anomalies raise the possibility of a disturbance in neural crest cell migration or differentiation during early embryogenesis [2,6]. Particularly in the context of congenital anomalies monozygosity does not guarantee identical outcomes. Multiple mechanisms have been proposed to explain this discordance, including postzygotic mutations, epigenetic divergence of somatic mosaicism, and differences in the intrauterine environment [1]. In fact, a very recent large multicenter study of 132,386 pregnant women from 50 study sites demonstrated that multifetal pregnancies significantly increased the risk of birth defects in ART [7].

IVF and ICSI have been associated with an increased risk of MZ twinning and congenital anomalies. This may be due to manipulation of the zona pellucida, prolonged embryo culture, or suboptimal epigenetic reprogramming in vitro [3,4,8]. Although a causal link has so far not been established, studies suggest that IVF pregnancies may be at higher risk for epigenetic syndromes (e.g., Beckwith-Wiedemann, Angelman) due to imprinting defects [9]. Though no such syndrome was identified in this case, subtle or novel epigenetic abnormalities may, however, still play a role.

The absence of detectable mutations on whole-exome sequencing supports the theory of postzygotic somatic mosaicism, where a de novo mutation occurs in a subset of cells after zygotic splitting [10]. Alternatively, epigenetic dysregulation may have selectively impaired developmental programs in one embryo while sparing the other [6]. Given that the anomalies in twin B involved neural crest-derived tissues, a localized or temporally specific disruption in neural crest cell migration, differentiation, or survival may underlie the observed phenotype. In addition to genetic and epigenetic mechanisms, the uterine environment and placental architecture can play significant roles. Unequal placental sharing, disparities in vascular perfusion, and subtle forms of twin-to-twin transfusion syndrome can also result in differences in nutrient and oxygen delivery, thereby impacting organogenesis and fetal growth. In this case, such differences could lead to the kind of congenital abnormalities seen in one of these twins [11].

This case contributes to a growing body of literature emphasizing that monozygosity does not imply phenotypic equivalence. Discordance should not be assumed to result from experimental error or diagnostic oversight but must, rather, be recognized as a biologically plausible outcome.

Conclusions

This case of MZ DC twins conceived via IVF, in which one twin exhibited Hirschsprung's disease, congenital heart defects, and orofacial clefts while the other was unaffected, illustrates the limitations of genetic determinism. Despite identical genomes, monozygotic twins can exhibit markedly different phenotypes, reflecting the influence of postzygotic events, epigenetic divergence, and environmental factors. These findings support a broader developmental framework incorporating postzygotic mosaicism, epigenetics, and ART-associated perturbations.

For clinicians, embryologists, and genetic counselors, this case demonstrates the importance of recognizing that monozygosity does not ensure identical outcomes. Discordant congenital anomalies in MZ twins, particularly in the context of IVF, should be approached not as anomalies in diagnostic accuracy but as biologically plausible and increasingly documented phenomena.
