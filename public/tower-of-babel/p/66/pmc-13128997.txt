Preparing for Tuberculosis Vaccine M72/AS01E Implementation: A Global Demand Forecast and Key Influential Factors
Authors: Judith Kallenberg, Dominique Milea, Thomas Breuer, Agathe Philippot
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionTuberculosis (TB) affects over 10 million people worldwide annually, is difficult to treat, and is a major cause of death. The M72/AS01E candidate vaccine is the first vaccine in over 100 years with the potential to protect adolescents/adults. To prepare for the introduction of the vaccine and ensure adequate manufacturing capacity, global vaccine demand must be carefully estimated in advance.MethodsA global demand forecasting model was developed that considered demand factors such as TB burden, external funding, anticipated interest, and country vaccine adoption capabilities, which determined assumptions for the timing and implementation approach of vaccine introduction. Assuming initial M72/AS01E regulatory approval in 2029, demand for routine vaccination and catch-up campaigns was forecasted through to 2045.ResultsAdoption of M72/AS01E was projected to begin in South Africa in 2029, expanding to 89 countries by 2039. In the base-case forecast, demand reached a steady state of around 56 million doses per year by 2036, and decreased to around 43 million doses per year following licensure of another global TB vaccine, assumed in 2037. Seven countries accounted for 61% of demand, and approximately half of all demand was from Gavi-eligible countries (Gavi, the Vaccine Alliance). In scenario analyses, demand increased by 43% (peaking at 79 million in 2036), assuming higher efficacy and more available funding, and decreased by 34%, assuming narrower, delayed policy recommendations and less available funding. Key drivers of global demand included adoption timing, target population, and coverage.ConclusionsM72/AS01E has the potential to reduce the TB burden worldwide. Successful vaccine introduction requires early and coordinated planning, with detailed demand forecasting refined over time. The model will be updated as new data and country implementation plans emerge. To avoid access delays, continued collaboration among key stakeholders, including vaccine suppliers, and early visibility on policy recommendations and domestic and donor funding availability will be critical in the coming years.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40121-026-01319-2.Keywords: Adolescents; Demand; Forecasting; Gavi, the Vaccine Alliance; Global health; Immunization; M72/AS01; Models (theoretic); Tuberculosis; Vaccine

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB) disease affects over 10 million people worldwide each year. Although TB can be treated and cured if managed well, it remains the single largest cause of death from infectious disease, only briefly overtaken by COVID-19 during the pandemic [1]. There were an estimated 1.3 million deaths from TB worldwide in 2023. Infection caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis can lead to latent/inactive TB with no symptoms or risk of transmission, however the bacteria can become active, causing TB disease. The lungs are the most common site of infection, leading to pulmonary TB, which can be transmitted through coughing. In addition, individuals with weakened immune systems, such as people living with human immunodeficiency virus (PLHIV), are at increased risk of severe TB [2].

TB can be cured with a lengthy course (4–6 months, or > 9 months for drug-resistant TB) of multiple anti-TB drugs (e.g., potent antibiotics such as rifampicin), however, if left untreated, around 50% of people will die. Treatment regimens to prevent latent TB from progressing to TB disease also require long-term medication courses (1–6 months) [1]. Adequate diagnostic and treatment options are not universally available, and TB treatments can have serious side effects, impacting patient well-being as well as overall treatment compliance [3]. An important threat to public health is the emergence of multidrug-resistant (MDR) TB, which is accelerated by treatment interruptions or failure to complete the regimen, and has an increased mortality risk [4–6]. The vast majority of global TB cases (87%) occur across 30 high-burden countries—the top five include India, Indonesia, China, the Philippines, and Pakistan, which also have a significant proportion of MDR TB [1]. Around 50% of people affected by TB face personal catastrophic costs (> 20% of annual income) due to medical and non-medical expenses and loss of income due to inability to work [1].

The only licensed vaccine for the prevention of TB disease is the bacille Calmette-Guérin (BCG) vaccine for newborns, which was developed more than 100 years ago. While BCG prevents severe forms of TB in young children, it offers limited protection against pulmonary TB in adolescents and adults, where transmission rates are the highest [7]. Around 90% of TB disease occurs in adults, but there are currently no licensed vaccines effective in preventing TB disease in adults, either before or after exposure to TB infection [8].

TB’s continued threat, compounded by the rise of MDR TB, and several high-profile international meetings focused on TB in recent years, have spurred increased political interest to end the TB epidemic, including through the development of effective vaccines. The United Nations’ Sustainable Development Goals have set a target to end the global TB epidemic by 2030 [9]. Gavi, the Vaccine Alliance’s Vaccine Investment Strategy (VIS)—endorsed in 2024—highlighted TB vaccines for adolescents and adults as a priority for its future vaccine portfolio [10]. In 2023, the World Health Organization (WHO) launched the ‘TB Vaccine Accelerator Council’ to prepare for rapid and effective vaccination implementation and equitable access. Its mandate includes identifying financial solutions, market solutions, and advocacy for TB vaccines [7, 11].

The M72/ AS01E vaccine represents a significant scientific breakthrough, as it is the first candidate TB vaccine in over 100 years that meets the WHO Target Product Profile (TPP) for a TB vaccine to protect adults, as shown in a phase 2b study [12]. M72/AS01E was developed to align with WHO Preferred Product Characteristics (PPC) [12]. The vaccine was developed by GSK and AERAS through to phase 2b, and out-licensed to the Gates Medical Research Institute (MRI) for further development and commercialization in lower-income countries with a high TB burden. In the phase 2b trial with 3575 participants, M72/AS01E demonstrated a significant protective effect against TB disease, with 49.7% (95% confidence interval [CI] 2.1–74.2%) efficacy in adults aged 18–50 years, with protection lasting at least 3 years [13]. The population included people infected with TB, i.e., those who tested positive on the Interferon Gamma Release Assay (IGRA+). In a separate study, the MESA-TB trial, Gates MRI demonstrated the safety and immunogenicity of M72/AS01E in PLHIV, a population with a high TB prevalence [14, 15]. In 2024, Gates MRI announced the start of phase 3 trials, currently ongoing in South Africa, Kenya, Malawi, Zambia, and Indonesia, to assess the efficacy of M72/AS01E against active pulmonary TB disease in adolescents and adults aged 15–44 years [16]. Two additional cohorts will be evaluated for safety and immunogenicity of M72/AS01E in PLHIV, and in people not infected with TB (IGRA-). The M72/AS01E vaccine requires two doses administered 1 month apart. Vaccine components are the M72 protein, currently manufactured by Gates MRI and partners, and the AS01E adjuvant, manufactured by GSK. The vaccine is being developed as a two-dose vial.

To prepare for an effective launch and rapid, equitable access to M72/AS01E, it is critical to understand potential future global demand. Several demand forecasting exercises have been undertaken to date. For example, the Wellcome Trust commissioned a demand forecast model across 13 high-burden countries, assuming routine vaccination of 15-year-olds and pregnant women, and a catch-up program for risk groups (e.g., PLHIV, health workers) [17]. Another demand forecast by Gavi for its VIS 2024 analyses [18] assumed routine vaccination of all 15-year-olds, with a catch-up program for all 16 to 18-year-olds, in 45 countries eligible for Gavi funding and in 46 middle-income countries (MICs). In October 2025, Gavi published an updated vaccine-agnostic demand forecast with 52 countries introducing a vaccine by 2040 in wide-ranging scenarios from ‘low’ to ‘maximum public health need’ with gradually increasing levels of routine and catch-up immunization [19].

Establishing a detailed understanding of likely global demand in the context of financial and operational constraints is essential to ensure adequate manufacturing capacity and timely supply, while avoiding over-supply to minimize vaccine costs. In this regard, the existing models have important limitations for supply planning, as they were developed for a somewhat different purpose (e.g., to inform investment decisions, to demonstrate different levels of ‘programmatic ambition’, or to inform market shaping activities) and/or had a limited geographic scope. GSK, therefore, developed a global demand forecast model for M72/AS01E, designed to estimate (unconstrained) vaccine volumes needed to meet projected demand for M72/AS01E in the context of known and projected financial and operational constraints, and, importantly, to identify key drivers of demand uncertainty. This model integrates country-specific data, with assumptions based on documented experience with earlier vaccine introductions and based on a limited number of consultations with national and international immunization stakeholders and with GSK experts with extensive knowledge of vaccine introduction and market access challenges.

By taking into account epidemiologic and implementation-related variables across diverse settings, as well as historical experience with new vaccine introduction, the aim was to develop a realistic demand estimate to inform supply planning and support preparatory efforts by key stakeholders—including policymakers, national health authorities, funders, and vaccine manufacturers—to enable timely and equitable vaccine access. The forecast will be adapted as new evidence becomes available.

Methods

The M72/AS01E demand forecasting model commissioned by GSK was developed in collaboration with Boston Consulting Group in early 2025, using published country-level data, insights from GSK’s experience with new vaccine introductions in lower-income countries, and from a limited number of external stakeholders. Interviews were conducted with select vaccine and TB experts from various organizations (WHO, Gates Foundation, Gates MRI, Gavi, Wellcome Trust), and with academics, Ministry of Health officials, and experts from countries with a high TB burden (Indonesia, South Africa, Malawi, Kenya, China, India), to obtain insights and inputs for model assumption development. Experts from GSK included those involved in M72/AS01E clinical development, supply, and introduction planning for the malaria vaccine RTS,S/AS01, global health epidemiology, regulatory affairs, and vaccine policy. It is important to note that interviewees in countries affected by TB generally highlighted that it was too early to make decisions or definitive statements on likely vaccine introduction timing and specific implementation approaches. The assumptions included in this forecast for order, timing, and implementation approach of vaccine introductions by individual countries were made purely for modeling purposes. Actual decisions on if, when, and how to introduce the vaccine will be made by government authorities in the future, and are currently not yet known, which requires modeling. None of the assumptions reflects a preference or prioritization by GSK or other (global) entities involved with preparing for vaccine access.

Ethical Approval

This article is based on previously conducted studies and published data, and does not contain any new studies with human participants or animals performed by any of the authors.

Forecasting Approach

Global demand for public-sector immunization programs was projected through to 2045 and assumed no supply constraints for both vaccine components. Demand was estimated based on multiple vaccination strategies, including routine vaccination of 15-year-olds and risk groups (e.g., PLHIV and healthcare workers [HCWs]), catch-up campaigns, and a booster dose after 10 years.

Eight countries have the potential to drive a significant share of global demand because of their high TB burden and large population size. For these countries (i.e., Bangladesh, China, India, Indonesia, Nigeria, Pakistan, the Philippines, and South Africa), unique, tailored assumptions were developed. For modeling purposes, all other countries were classified into five archetypes based on their TB burden (high, medium, or low), likely funding model (Gavi-eligible or not), and expected adoption speed (fast, for countries participating in M72/AS01E phase 3 trials; medium, for countries with a robust immunization program based on diphtheria, tetanus toxoid and pertussis third dose [DTP3] coverage ≥ 80%; or slow, for DTP3 coverage < 80% [20]) (Table S1).

Model Inputs

The following data inputs were compiled for each country. Population and demographic data included the 15-year-old population in 2023 and other age-specific population sizes [21]. Epidemiology data included TB incidence in 2023; HIV prevalence and incidence in the population-aged ≥ 15 years old; and data on household contacts (HHCs) aged ≥ 15 years old of people diagnosed with TB per year [22, 23]. 2024 Human papillomavirus (HPV) vaccination coverage in adolescents was used as an analogue for routine coverage with M72/AS01E in adolescents and at-risk groups. When no routine adolescent vaccination was assumed, antiretroviral therapy (ART) coverage was used as an analogue for routine vaccination coverage in PLHIV and other at-risk groups [24–26]. For modeling purposes, eligibility for Gavi funding was set based on projected eligibility in 2030 for each country.

General Assumptions (Base Case)

The following assumptions pertain to the base case, assuming that M72/AS01E vaccine efficacy in IGRA+ patients was at least 50%. The base case also assumed that TB vaccines would remain a priority for Gavi, with funding support for routine introduction in Gavi-eligible countries and limited support for campaigns in at-risk populations in some countries, and implementation of broader (self-funded) catch-up campaigns in non-Gavi-eligible countries. Rapid regulatory approvals and dissemination of global policy recommendations were also assumed. WHO policy recommendations were assumed to include IGRA− and HIV+ persons in the target groups for immunization. Alternative upside and downside scenarios were modelled, as described below.

It was assumed that the phase 3 readout for M72/AS01E would be available by early 2028. Dossier submissions to a stringent regulatory authority (SRA, e.g., the European Medicines Agency, EMA) and to the National Regulatory Authority in South Africa were assumed to follow phase 3 results in 2028, for approvals in early 2029. In parallel, the WHO was assumed to issue a global policy recommendation in 2029, with WHO Prequalification (PQ) following in early 2030. These assumptions reflect an accelerated process compared with historical regulatory timelines [27].

For all countries, coverage of the second dose was assumed to be 20% lower than the first dose, based on global averages for HPV second dose coverage [28, 29]. Higher-income settings tend to have lower drop-off rates than lower-income settings. However, since the time between doses for M72/AS01E is shorter than for HPV (i.e., 1 month versus 6 months), which normally leads to higher compliance, this was assumed to offset potential differences. Other general assumptions included requiring 25% buffer stock in countries (in line with Gavi’s VIS assumptions [18]), and an expected wastage rate of 10% (in line with WHO indicative rates for a 2–5 dose vial [30]). For countries with a phased rollout strategy (e.g., initially restricted to a sub-national region), it was assumed that 15% of the target population for routine vaccination would be vaccinated over 2 years, before scaling up nationally. The expected market share of M72/AS01E was assumed to be 100% until 2037, dropping to 50% by 2040 following the approval of an alternative TB vaccine. We further assumed a booster dose would be administered in 20% of the initially vaccinated population, 10 years after initial vaccination (based on WHO PPC of 10+ years vaccine protection [12]) (Table 1).

Country-Specific Assumptions

The year of M72/AS01E introduction in individual countries was estimated considering TB burden, participation in the phase 3 trial, eligibility for Gavi funding, and immunization program strength based on DTP3 coverage (Figure S1). The implementation strategy per country was assumed to be either at-scale (nationwide) or phased (i.e., initially restricted to a sub-national region). Routine vaccination was assumed to target 15-year-olds via school-based programs and at-risk populations of new TB HHCs and newly diagnosed PLHIV. Catch-up campaigns for other at-risk groups were modelled for PLHIV, HCWs, and incarcerated individuals, as they represent known risk groups for which epidemiological data exist. Peak or steady-state coverage rates for routine vaccination of adolescents and at-risk groups, such as newly diagnosed PLHIV, were based on HPV coverage (note: for modeling purposes, and given the relatively small numbers, HPV coverage analogues were used for the full routine immunization cohort of adolescents and at-risk populations). In countries with routine vaccination of at-risk groups only, ART coverage was used as an analogue. For countries without HPV coverage data, the global average HPV coverage rate was adjusted by country-specific DTP3 coverage data to account for country variations. The average HPV coverage rate, based on 2024 WHO data, was 62% in all countries that have introduced HPV and are forecasted to introduce the vaccine, and 72% in the seven large, high-burden countries with tailored assumptions. The time to reach routine vaccination steady-state coverage was assumed to be 2, 4, or 6 years, based on uptake curves for other vaccines, country size, the country’s previous experience with vaccine introduction, and Gavi historical data. Campaign duration was based on the type of campaign (faster implementation for at-risk versus hot spot campaigns) and country size (longer duration in large countries such as Pakistan and Nigeria). Catch-up campaign coverage was based on ART coverage for all at-risk groups and was assumed to be 50% for campaigns targeting TB hot spots.

Tailored Assumptions for Eight Countries

Table 2 presents vaccine introduction assumptions for the base-case forecast in eight countries with unique tailored assumptions.

South Africa and Indonesia were assumed to be the first countries to introduce the vaccine following regulatory approval. South Africa was assumed to introduce M72/AS01E at-scale in 2029, due to its high TB burden; participation in the phase 2 MESA trial and phase 3 trial; and anticipated strong interest in the vaccine, i.e., the TB program is a key public health priority with an estimated 80% of funding from domestic sources. South Africa’s National Regulatory Authority was assumed to rapidly review the regulatory dossier prior to/in parallel with SRA review. A school-based HPV vaccination program for girls aged 9–14 years and community outreach programs for people at risk of TB are already in place. Nationwide routine M72/AS01E vaccination of 15-year-olds in school, newly diagnosed PLHIV, and new HHCs was assumed, with additional vaccination campaigns in TB hotspots representing 15% of the population ≥ 16-year-olds.

Indonesia was assumed to introduce the vaccine in 2030, with a nationwide rollout. The same target population for routine vaccination was assumed as for South Africa, with catch-up campaigns focused on high TB burden districts assumed to cover 10% of the population ≥ 16-year-olds.

Bangladesh, Pakistan, and the Philippines were assumed to introduce the vaccine in the early years (2032 assumed in the model). Bangladesh was assumed to introduce routine vaccination at scale, based on the country’s robust immunization program, high TB burden, and potential access to limited Gavi funding following its expected transition in 2030 from Gavi eligibility; and a catch-up campaign in at-risk populations. Pakistan and the Philippines were assumed to introduce M72/AS01E with a Gavi-supported phased rollout of routine vaccination.

Nigeria was assumed to introduce M72/AS01E around 5 years following initial regulatory approval, with a phased rollout of routine vaccination, potentially benefiting from a limited amount of Gavi funding following its expected transition from Gavi eligibility in the coming years.

Significant uncertainties exist regarding the future use of the M72/AS01E vaccine in public-sector immunization programs in India and China, for example, due to potential requirements for generation of local data and potential preferences for domestic vaccine candidates. Conservative assumptions were therefore included for these two countries, with limited uptake. India was assumed to introduce routine M72/AS01E vaccination around 2034 for at-risk groups only, and China was assumed to adopt limited routine vaccination in the upside scenario only.

All vaccine introduction assumptions described here were developed for modeling purposes; decisions on vaccine introduction rest with national governments.

Specific Assumptions for Five Country Archetypes

Table 2 presents the base-case assumptions by archetype. Archetype 5 included high-burden countries, not eligible for Gavi funding. Without Gavi support, a phased and slower rollout was assumed. Routine vaccination of 15-year-olds and new at-risk populations was assumed, with catch-up campaigns for PLHIV, HCWs, and incarcerated individuals. Archetype 4 included high-burden countries eligible for Gavi funding, and assumed an at-scale rollout. Archetype 3 included medium-burden countries without Gavi support. Routine vaccination (at-scale) was assumed only for at-risk populations, with no catch-up campaigns, given the lower burden and absence of Gavi funding. Archetype 2 included countries with a medium TB burden and Gavi funding. Routine vaccination with at-scale rollout was assumed for 15-year-olds and at-risk populations, without catch-up campaigns. Archetype 1 included countries with a low TB burden that were assumed not to introduce a public-sector TB vaccination program (Table 2).

Alternative Scenario Analyses

An ‘upside’ scenario was characterized with higher vaccine efficacy and increased availability of global funding (from Gavi and other sources), resulting in broader demand for M72/AS01E and a faster rollout compared with the base case. Higher efficacy was defined as greater than 70%, and, consistent with the base case, good safety and immunological results in the two sub-cohorts (IGRA− and HIV+).

As a result of these more favorable circumstances, all countries were assumed to adopt earlier, and a rollout at-scale (rather than phased introductions starting in smaller areas) was assumed in all countries except India and Nigeria. Further, additional catch-up campaigns were assumed alongside routine immunization, enabled by greater access to external funding compared with the base case.

In addition, in China, limited adoption of routine immunization of 15-year-olds and at-risk groups was assumed in targeted geographies. Additional routine immunization (of 15-year-olds) and catch-up campaigns in medium-burden, self-funding countries, and wider campaigns in high-burden countries (covering 10–25% of ≥ 16-years-olds in TB hotspots) were also assumed (Table S2).

A ‘downside’ scenario was characterized with delayed global policy recommendations for a smaller target population and reduced global funding. It was assumed that, due to a lack of specific efficacy data in PLHIV, policy advisory bodies (e.g., the WHO SAGE) would not recommend a broader campaign strategy for PLHIV. As such, no catch-up campaigns with M72/AS01E in PLHIV were modeled in this scenario. Based on experience with the RTS,S/AS01 malaria vaccine, the downside scenario also assumed a delay in the publication of WHO recommendations and WHO PQ following phase 3 results. As a result, adoption was assumed to be delayed by 2 years in all Gavi-eligible countries, and in countries with tailored assumptions with a recent transition to self-funding status, such as Nigeria and Bangladesh, assuming these would still receive some Gavi support. A phased rollout was assumed in all countries, i.e., starting with small-scale implementation before national scale-up.

With global funding reduced, Gavi was assumed to prioritize routine immunization and not offer funding support for catch-up campaigns. In this scenario, India did not introduce the M72/AS01E vaccine in any population groups, in anticipation of future domestic vaccines (Table S3).

Results

Base-Case Demand Forecast

In the base case, forecasted demand started at 4 million vaccine doses in the first year, increasing to around 56 million doses per year by 2036. By then, 74 countries had introduced M72/AS01E, and by 2038, all 89 countries in scope for vaccine adoption had done so. Demand decreased after 2040, following the assumed entry of an alternative TB vaccine in 2037 (Fig. 1).

Demand forecast by number of doses (in millions) over time and in seven countries with tailored assumptions

Around half of all demand in this scenario (through to 2045) was from Gavi-eligible countries; around 36% in the first few years, then increasing and stabilizing at around 54%. Routine vaccination accounted for around 85% of demand, with the remainder from catch-up campaigns and booster doses. Seven of the countries with tailored assumptions (excluding China, where no adoption was assumed in this forecast) accounted for 73% of demand in the first 7 years, and around 56% from 2036 onwards (Fig. 1). Demand was highest in Indonesia, Pakistan, and Bangladesh linked to the high TB burden and large population size in these countries. Demand from India was limited as routine vaccination was only modeled for at-risk groups, assuming that India would wait for a domestically produced TB vaccine for broad, routine introduction.

Upside Scenario

In the upside scenario, overall demand was 43% higher compared with the base case. Demand initially peaked around 2031, which can be attributed to the large number of countries introducing and implementing catch-up campaigns in the first 3 years, with a second peak observed after most countries had introduced the vaccine, reaching peak volumes of around 79 million by 2036. Following the market entry of another TB vaccine, demand decreased and stabilized at around 52 million doses by 2042 (Fig. 2). Gavi-eligible countries accounted for 46% of demand on average after the first 5 years (2036–2045).

Downside Scenario

In the downside scenario, policy and funding limitations resulted in a 34% decrease in demand for M72/AS01E over the period compared with the base case. The assumed delay in availability of global policy guidelines, absent recommendations for catch-up campaigns in PLHIV, and more limited global funding led to slower adoption, and fewer, more focused catch-up campaigns. Demand peaked at 42 million doses in 2040 versus 56 million in the base case (Fig. 2).

Discussion

Global (public-sector) demand for the M72/AS01E vaccine was forecasted from the first anticipated introduction in 2029 through to 2045, without supply constraints. In the base case, demand stabilized at 55–56 million doses per year after 8 years, decreasing to around 43 million per year following the entry of another TB vaccine. By 2035, 6 years after the initial launch, 53 countries were forecasted to have introduced the vaccine, and around 73% of demand in this period was from seven high-burden countries: Bangladesh, India, Indonesia, Nigeria, Pakistan, the Philippines, and South Africa. Between 2029 and 2045, around 47% of overall demand was from countries eligible for Gavi funding (including Bangladesh, Nigeria, and Pakistan). Routine vaccination of 15-year-olds and at-risk groups (mainly newly diagnosed PLHIV) accounted for 85% of demand on average, with the remainder from catch-up campaigns in at-risk groups (mainly PLHIV) and booster doses (buffer stock and wastage volumes are included across vaccination strategies). In the upside scenario, assuming higher vaccine efficacy and more available funding would lead to faster and broader vaccine adoption across countries, demand was 43% higher compared with the base case (peaking at 79 million in 2036). In the downside scenario, with more restricted and delayed global vaccine policy recommendations coupled with more limited funding, demand was 34% lower than in the base case.

The forecast provides a global perspective, taking into account various factors that influence demand, such as disease burden, external funding, and immunization program performance. Model inputs, such as analogue coverage and assumptions for target populations, were determined based on publicly available data, insights from a select number of external stakeholders, and GSK’s experience with novel vaccine introduction. For instance, the timing of M72/AS01E adoption in a country was estimated based on factors such as TB burden, participation in the phase 3 trial (as a predictor of interest and readiness to introduce the vaccine), eligibility for Gavi support, and immunization program performance. Similar factors informed assumptions for the program implementation strategy deployed, e.g., national ‘at-scale’ or phased rollout, broad or narrow target populations, routine immunization with or without catch-up campaigns, and time needed to reach peak coverage. Routine immunization was assumed to be implemented for 15-year-olds (most likely through school-based programs) and new at-risk groups, with coverage estimates based on HPV analogues, incorporating differences in coverage rates by country. Where routine vaccination was only modeled for at-risk groups, ART coverage was used as an analogue. Catch-up campaigns require additional resourcing for vaccine costs and operational expenses. Therefore, Gavi eligibility was assumed to be an important factor influencing the likelihood of catch-up campaigns being deployed in addition to routine vaccination programs. Significant uncertainties exist regarding future use of the M72/AS01E vaccine in public-sector immunization programs in India and China, for example, due to potential requirements for the generation of local data and potential preferences for domestically manufactured vaccine candidates. Hence, conservative assumptions for India and China were applied. It will be important to monitor interest in the M72/AS01E vaccine in these two countries, as changes could result in a significant increase in forecasted global demand due to their large population sizes. Unique tailored assumptions were made for eight high-burden countries with potential high-volume demand, while a systematic approach classified other countries into archetypes with standardized assumptions. The order and timing of adoption by individual countries, as well as implementation approaches, were model assumptions. National governments will take actual decisions as more information becomes available in the coming years.

Previous demand forecasts for TB vaccines have provided high-level estimates to inform investment decisions [18], or focused on projecting demand in a small number of high TB burden countries [17]. For example, compared with our model, the higher routine vaccination demand in the 2024 Gavi-VIS analysis [18] (e.g., annually 90–100 million doses in Gavi-eligible countries and 100–110 million doses in MICs) was driven by faster adoption assumptions across countries, larger target populations (15-year-olds in all countries, including India), and higher coverage rates achieved (90%). The Wellcome Trust model in 13 high-burden countries (including India and China) assumed routine vaccination of 15-year-olds and pregnant women (15–44 years old), catch-up campaigns (in at-risk populations or in all 15–44-year-olds), and steady-state coverage achieved after 3 years in all countries [17]. No results from this model were published. In a more recent product-agnostic Gavi forecast, published in October 2025 [19], peak demand during 2030–2040 ranged from 15 to 401 million regimens per year, dependent on the scope of target populations and introduction approaches across four scenarios. The description of the ‘medium scenario’ appears closest to the base-case methodology described in this paper, with a focus on adolescent routine immunization and limited catch-up campaigns in high-risk groups. However, the resulting demand in this scenario is higher, peaking at 70 million regimens (i.e., fully vaccinated persons) per year, compared with peak volumes of 53 million doses per year for a two-dose schedule in the base-case scenario in this paper. One key difference is the assumption of broad vaccination in India, constituting 50% of demand in the first 5 years of the Gavi forecast. The difference may be further explained by the broader implementation of catch-up campaigns across countries, and by coverage assumptions. No full details on model assumptions for this forecast were available at the time of writing to enable a full comparison.

Routine immunization coverage is a key swing factor that could significantly increase vaccine supply requirements, especially in the early years. HPV coverage rates, which were used as an analogue for M72/AS01E coverage in the model described in this paper, are relatively low in low- and middle-income countries (62% on average in all countries forecasted to introduce the vaccine, and 72% in the seven large, high-burden countries with tailored assumptions). It is uncertain to what extent HPV coverage represents an underestimation of future TB vaccine coverage, and the role that factors such as disease stigma may play in TB vaccine acceptance. The strength of immunization programs, available funding for introduction planning, and community sensitization may be important factors influencing coverage.

Rapid regulatory approvals, timely WHO policy recommendations for M72/AS01E use, WHO PQ, and the opening of a Gavi funding window for TB vaccines will be critical to minimize adoption delays following favorable phase 3 results. The rollout of the RTS,S/AS01 malaria vaccine constitutes an interesting case study in this regard. After 5 years of phase 3 studies in seven sub-Saharan African countries in which 16,000 children participated, a “positive scientific opinion” was granted by the European Medicines Agency (EMA) in 2015 [32], a pre-requisite for a WHO policy recommendation for use of the vaccine. The EMA scientific opinion was based on their assessment of the quality, safety, and efficacy of the vaccine as well as its risk–benefit balance. Later in 2015, the WHO's Strategic Advisory Group of Experts on Immunization and the Malaria Policy Advisory Committee recommended further evaluation of the safety, impact, and operational feasibility of the vaccine. Following this advice, in January 2016, the WHO published its recommendation for further evaluation of RTS,S/AS01 in a series of pilot implementations before considering wider country-level introduction, noting gaps in knowledge [33]. Following the completion of these pilot implementations in three African countries, in 2021, the WHO recommended that the RTS,S/AS01 malaria vaccine should be used in children living in regions with moderate to high malaria transmission [34], enabling the opening of a Gavi funding window for the vaccine, and paving the way for broad-scale use across Sub-Saharan Africa. In early 2024, nearly 10 years after the conclusion of phase 3 trials, additional countries, beyond the three that participated in pilot implementations, were able to start introducing the vaccine with support from Gavi [31].

This experience shows that novel vaccines face significant uncertainty in the pathway to policy recommendations, a critical enabler for broad (Gavi-supported) access [35]. Our downside scenario showed that even a 2-year delay in WHO vaccine policy recommendations, combined with more conservative recommendations (i.e., no campaigns targeting PLHIV), had a major impact on demand, reducing vaccine supply requirements by 34% in the initial years. The more optimistic base-case assumptions would require advanced and coordinated planning for regulatory and policy processes, including proactive evidence generation to address relevant evidence gaps, building on the important lessons learned with RTS,S/AS01.

Beyond regulatory and policy requirements, access to external funding is a critical enabler for new vaccine introduction in lower-income countries. Half of all demand in this forecast came from countries expected to be eligible for some form of Gavi support linked to their gross national income levels, and likely dependent upon Gavi for M72/AS01E introduction. Thus, declining donor support to Gavi and other organizations supporting immunization creates uncertainty regarding the scope of future financial support for the M72/AS01E vaccine [36]. Given the important public health potential of M72/AS01E and prioritization of the vaccine in Gavi’s VIS, our base case assumed that Gavi would support routine vaccine introduction in Gavi-eligible countries. Catch-up campaigns in broader population groups are expected to significantly increase vaccine impact, however, they can be resource-intensive and disruptive for public health programs, e.g., requiring additional vaccine supply, stock management, operational and logistical efforts to reach larger population groups, and updates to HCW practices [37, 38]. Our base case assumed that Gavi would only provide limited funding for catch-up campaigns in at-risk populations, as well as limited implementation of these campaigns in self-funding countries. The upside scenario showed that faster adoption and larger catch-up campaigns could have a major impact on overall supply requirements.

As GSK will supply the adjuvant for the vaccine, this forecast helps to inform planning for manufacturing network readiness to meet demand. Based on experience with other new vaccine introductions and considering financial and operational constraints (as also described in the recent Gavi publication [19]), we believe the base case provides a realistic, current estimate of future demand for the M72/AS01E TB vaccine. GSK will update this forecast with the latest evidence on country adoption plans, funding scenarios, development of alternative vaccine candidates, and other new data and insights as they become available. The read-out of phase 3 results, expected in early 2028, will be a critical milestone, given their potential to influence demand and accelerate country decisions on vaccine introduction plans. Learnings from malaria vaccine introductions, as well as other infectious disease product launches in low- and lower-middle-income countries [27], highlight the importance of early collaboration between policymakers, manufacturers, funders, regulators, and other national and international stakeholders to minimize delays on the critical path towards vaccine adoption.

Although a variety of experts were consulted from all major international organizations involved with planning for the M72/AS01E TB vaccine introduction, consultations with representatives from high-burden countries were limited in scope. The methods and results presented here can serve as a reference point for discussion in the TB and vaccines stakeholder community preparing for M72/AS01E introduction, to drive alignment around demand assumptions, forecast scenarios, uncertainties, and key swing factors. In addition to supporting supply planning, the forecast provides insights on key factors that could delay or limit uptake, and highlights areas where collective efforts and investments are needed to minimize access delays.

Conclusions

The M72/AS01E TB vaccine, assuming successful phase 3 results, presents a formidable opportunity to change the global trajectory of TB. Historical experience with new vaccine introductions in lower-income countries has shown the importance of early, coordinated planning among national and international stakeholders. Demand forecasting is a critical part of this process, as it informs planning for future supply and for program readiness, while bringing visibility to areas where additional preparation, investment or coordination can accelerate access. In particular, it is critical to have early visibility on requirements for policy recommendations, including potential perceived gaps in the current planned evidence package, and on domestic and donor funding availability. The coming years will prove if endemic countries and the global health community are ready to act on the potential of the M72/AS01E vaccine to protect people at risk of TB.
