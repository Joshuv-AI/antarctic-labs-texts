Level of eye healthcare utilisation and associated factors in Gamo and Gofa Zones, Southern Ethiopia: a community-based, cross-sectional study
Authors: Tsegaye Alemu, Dawit Seyum, Mulate Gebre, Alemayehu Sisay, Mende Mensa Sorato
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectiveAbout 90% of eye health problems are preventable or curable with existing cost-effective interventions. There is limited evidence on eye healthcare utilisation (ECU) and the factors influencing it in the study area. This study aimed to determine the level of ECU and the associated factors in Southern Ethiopia.DesignA community-based, cross-sectional study was conducted in Southern Ethiopia from 1 June to 15 July 2023. Data were analysed using SPSS V.27.SettingTwo selected districts (Dita and Uba Debretsehay) in Southern Ethiopia.ParticipantsPermanent residents living in randomly selected kebeles.Outcome measureThe level of ECU was the outcome measure. A binary logistic regression model was used to check the association between explanatory variables and the dependent variable. To avoid many variables and unstable estimates and to control for possible confounders in the subsequent model, only variables that reached a p value of less than 0.25 on binary analysis were used in the multivariate logistic regression analysis to identify factors independently associated with ECU.Results992 respondents participated in this study, with a response rate of 95.4%. The prevalence of ECU within the past 2 years was 21.6% (95% CI 19.0, 24.3). The reported barriers to ECU were lack of awareness (123, 40.6%) and lack of money (58, 19.2%). Age <30 years (adjusted OR (AOR)=2.7; 95% CI 1.5, 4.6), being male (AOR=2.2; 95% CI 1.4, 3.6), shorter travel time (AOR=2.0; 95% CI 1.3, 3.1), undergoing regular vision check-ups (AOR=5.4; 95% CI 3.47, 8.37), history of eye diseases (AOR=4.8; 95% CI 2.5, 9.1) and having trichiasis (AOR=1.5; 95% CI 1.1, 2.3) were positively associated with eye care service utilisation. Low wealth quantile (AOR=0.62; 95% CI 0.39, 0.98) and poor knowledge (AOR=0.26; 95% CI 0.16, 0.44) were negatively associated with eye care service utilisation.ConclusionsThe ECU within the past 2 years was a quarter. Socioeconomic factors, disease-related factors and respondent knowledge are independently associated with ECU. Designing strategies that can improve the capacity of healthcare facilities and patient awareness is important in order to reduce barriers and improve service utilisation. Researchers interested in working in the area should focus on community-specific approaches that can improve community health literacy by using stronger study designs.Keywords: opthalmology, medical ophthalmology, neglected diseases, cataract and refractive surgery

Introduction

Blindness and visual impairment are serious public health problems particularly in poor and middle-income nations. Around 2.2 billion individuals worldwide suffer from visual impairment.1 According to current findings, a substantial number of persons afflicted (90%) with visual impairments reside in low-income and middle-income countries (LMICs) due to fewer alternatives to accessing the most basic eye care treatments.2 If current extremely cost-effective eye care treatment had been provided, visual impairment in almost 90% of people in LMICs might have been prevented or cured.1 3 Cataracts, refractive error, age-related macular degeneration, glaucoma and diabetic retinopathy are the primary causes of visual impairment or blindness.4 According to a study conducted in Ethiopia, the prevalence of blindness and low vision was 1.6% and 3.7%, respectively. Cataracts and trachomatous corneal opacity are the leading causes of blindness. Childhood blindness accounts for more than 6% of the overall blindness burden in Ethiopia, with a prevalence rate of 0.1%.5

Eye care service use refers to when people use eye care services to prevent, treat or cure eye problems, promote eye healthcare, or find out about existing eye health condition and prognosis.6 According to a recent research published in The Lancet, eye health has a wide range of significant impacts on health, life, development and economic outcomes.3 However, limited access to quality and reasonable eye care services contributes to the suffering of patients, families and communities. This may force these people to live with visual impairment or blindness.3 Evidence from developing countries showed discrepancies in eye care utilisation. For example, 21.1% of individuals in Northern Ethiopia,7 23.8% in Southern Ethiopia,8 73.4% in South Africa,9 18% in Nigeria10 and 35.5% in rural South India11 never had an eye examination.

The documented studies have shown that various barriers can hamper the utilisation of eye care services and lead to delayed diagnosis and treatment of eye conditions, vision loss or other eye complications.12 Therefore, cost-effective and feasible interventions are important in order to identify barriers to optimal eye care utilisation.13

Utilisation of eye health services is crucial to managing visual impairment.14 15 Moreover, existing research evidence shows that expanding access and lowering obstacles to children’s and women’s healthcare services are critical to minimising visual impairments and blindness.16 WHO also recommends various strategies to address eye access barriers that may increase eye care service utilisation.1 To the best of our knowledge, there has been no study or little is known about eye health service utilisation at the community level in developing countries, including in the context of this current study. Therefore, this study aimed to determine eye health service utilisation and the associated factors in two zones (Gamo and Gofa) of Southern Ethiopia.

Methods

Study design and setting

A community-based, cross-sectional study was conducted from 1 June to 15 July 2023 in Dita District and Uba Debretsehay District in Southern Ethiopia. Dita District is one of the districts in Gamo Zone and is located 563 km south of Addis Ababa. According to the Central Statistical Agency’s (CSA) projection, there were 121 247 (48.9% male) residents in the district in 2022. There were 24 781 households in 25 rural and 2 urban kebeles in the district. Five health centres are providing eye care services in the district. The district has 95% and 49% basic latrine and water coverage, respectively.17 Uba Debretsehay District is one of the districts in Gofa Zone and is located 555 km south of Addis Ababa. Based on the CSA’s projections, approximately 98 314 people lived in 17 rural kebeles in 2022. There were 20 064 households in the district. The district has three health centres providing eye care services. The district has 24% water and 64% latrine coverage, respectively.18

Population

The source population consisted of all permanent residents living in Gamo and Gofa Zones. All permanent residents living in the randomly selected kebeles comprised the study population.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Permanent residents who were 18 years or older were included. People with mental illness or who had severe illness during data collection were excluded from the study.

Sample size determination

The researchers considered a single population proportion for sample size determination in this study, assuming a 95% level of confidence, 4% margin of error and a design effect of 2, and considering an eye health utilisation rate of 29% from a previous study,19 a 5% non-response rate and a design effect of 2 since a multistage sampling process was used.

n = (Za/2) × P (1-P)/d2

n = (1.96)2 × 0.29 (0.71)/0.042 = 0.790985/0.0016 = 495

Considering a 5% non-response rate, the following were calculated: 495+(495×0.05)=495+24.75=520. After adjusting for the design effect, we arrived at a final sample size of 1040.

Sampling technique

We used a multistage cluster sampling technique. We randomly selected one district from each zone. The number of clusters in each district was evenly distributed (figure 1). We selected 18 clusters from each district by simple random sample procedure. Finally, 30 randomly selected households from each cluster were included in the study.

Clustered multistage sampling technique used to select participants with eye health service utilisation and associated factor assessment in Gamo and Gofa Zones, Southern Ethiopia, 2023.

Data collection procedures

The interviewer-administered structured questionnaire (Eye Care Utilization Assessment Tool; online supplemental file) was prepared from different literature reviews. This data collection tool was pretested in a nearby district similar to the study area to ensure clarity and consistency. Necessary adjustments were made based on the findings of the pretest study. One day of training was given to six data collectors (BSc degree holders with work experience in eye healthcare) and two supervisors with master’s degrees and previous experience in supervision. Data were collected using Open Data Kit (ODK) with security patterns. Data concerning participant knowledge, attitude and practice of eye care service utilisation were collected using tools adapted from similar studies.14 20 Participants were determined to have good knowledge if they scored 75% and above, average knowledge if they scored 50%–75% and poor if they scored below 50% on the knowledge questionnaire. Similarly, participants were determined to have positive attitude if they responded ‘agree’ or ‘strongly agree’ and negative attitude if they responded ‘strongly disagree’, ‘disagree’ or ‘neutral’ to the attitude questionnaire. Finally, participants who scored the mean (50%) or above on the practice questionnaire had good practice, and bad practice otherwise.

Variables

Dependent variable

The dependent variable is eye care utilisation.

Independent variables

Sex, age, marital status, residence, occupation, income, educational status, language barrier and social belief were the sociodemographic factors included. Infrastructure factors included the means of transportation, distance from health facility, source of information, access to health facility and cost of treatment. Medical illness history included history of hypertension and history of diabetes mellitus. Eye illness history included previous eye illness, surgery and treatment. Awareness factors included lack of knowledge and lack of awareness about eye care. Eye care provider factors included lack of trained eye care providers, lack of training and supervision.

Data quality assurance

To ensure data quality, the prepared data were pretested near Daneba Gofa woreda Sasega kebele, which is similar to the actual study setting. Some modifications were carried out based on the findings. One-day training on data collection tools was given to the data collectors and supervisors. Daily close supervision was conducted during the data collection process. Furthermore, data were collected using the ODK smartphone data collection software to improve data quality. ODK is a digital data collection software that can be used with mobile Android devices to collect and aggregate data. Moreover, feedback was provided based on the data submitted to the server.

Data analysis and management

Data were collected using ODK and then exported to Excel, SPSS label and imported to SPSS V.27. Descriptive data analysis was conducted using mean, median, SD and range. The results of the study are presented with graphs and tables. A multicollinearity test was done and all variables had a variance inflation factor of less than 10. A binary logistic regression model was used to check the association between explanatory variables and the dependent variable. To avoid many variables and unstable estimates and to control for possible confounders in the subsequent model, only variables that reached a p value of less than 0.25 on binary analysis were used in the multivariate logistic analysis to identify factors independently associated with eye healthcare utilisation. Model fitness was tested by the Hosmer-Lemeshow test. The results of the final model were expressed in terms of adjusted OR (AOR) and 95% CI. Statistical significance was declared at a p value less than 0.05.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics

992 participants participated in this study, with a response rate of 95.4%. The respondents had a median (IQR) age of 40 (+18) years, ranging from 20 to 85 years. Majority of the respondents (795, 80.1%) were female and 968 (97.6%) were married. Majority (926, 93.3%) of the respondents were rural dwellers. Regarding decision-making power in the household, 839 (84.6%) reported household decisions being made by the husband. About 4 out of 10 (397, 40.02%) were Protestant by religion. Regarding family size, the mean family size was 6.36±2.463, ranging from 1 to 12 members. More than half (579, 58.4%) of the participants had a family size of 6–10, followed by 4–5 members (243, 24.5%). Majority (878, 88.5%) were enrolled in a community-based health insurance (CBHI). Concerning wealth status, households were classified into three wealth quantiles, namely low, middle and high. About 4 out of 10 (397, 40.0%) were in the low wealth quantile, 202 (20.4%) in the middle quantile and 393 (39.6%) in the high quantile (table 1).

Eye diseases and eye care service availability

About 6 out of 10 (593, 59.8%) participants reported that they knew someone with eye disease in their vicinity, suggesting reported prevalence of the disease in the community. Of the participants, only 214 (21.6%; 95% CI 19.0, 24.3) visited eye care facilities in the last 2 years before the data collection period. More than half (594, 59.9%) of the respondents reported the availability of facilities delivering eye care services near the community. The mean (SD) walking distance from home to the nearby eye care centre and back to home was 15.7 (+71.0) km. The median (IQR) time to reach nearby health facilities was 60.0 (+70.0) min. More than two-thirds (66.8%) of the participants had transportation access. The mean (SD) cost of transportation from home to a nearby eye care facility and back to home was 62.0 (+62.4) Ethiopian birr, ranging from 0 to 350 Ethiopian birr. Regarding the availability of trained eye care professionals, 347 (35%) reported the presence of trained eye care service providers near health facilities. Concerning information about eye healthcare, 397 (40.0%) have been informed about eye healthcare, while 207 (20.9%) have not heard about eye healthcare. Regarding the source of information, 164 (41.2%) respondents heard about eye health information from health extension workers, 143 (36%) from health development armies, 59 (14.8%) from health professionals, 24 (6.0%) from community and peers and 8 (2%) from television/radio. Of the participants, 120 (56.1%) and 34 (15.9%) reported that the service charge was less costly and costly, respectively, while 60 (28.0%) participants cannot remember. Close to three out of five (59.4%) participants stated that there was lack of adequate diagnostics and treatment at their visited facilities, while 65 (30.5%) reported the facilities were fully equipped with diagnostics and treatment materials (table 2). Concerning gaps in the eye health facility, about two-thirds (139, 65.0%) reported lack of eye care professionals, followed by lack of medicine in the health facility (127, 59.3%), lack of medical equipment and supplies (127, 59.3%) and lack of good management system (104, 48.6%) (table 2).

Knowledge, attitude and practice of respondents towards eye healthcare

In terms of knowledge about eye healthcare utilisation, the respondents’ mean (SD) score on knowledge about eye health was 9.9 (±2.8). About one-third (330, 33.3%) had poor knowledge, 337 (34.0%) had moderate knowledge and 325 (32.8%) had good knowledge about eye health.

Regarding practice towards eye healthcare utilisation, the mean (SD) score of the respondents was 7.9 (±2.6). About 6 out of 10 (585, 59.0%) respondents had poor eye health practices, while 407 (41.0%) had relatively good eye health practices. Concerning attitude towards eye healthcare utilisation, the median (IQR) score on attitude towards eye health was 2.5 (±0.6). Of the total respondents, 556 (56.0%) had a negative attitude towards eye healthcare, while 436 (44.0%) had a positive attitude (table 2).

Eye health conditions and barriers to eye healthcare utilisation

More than a quarter (302, 30.4%) of the respondents had an eye health problem during the data collection. Majority (889, 89.6%) did not get their eyesight checked regularly, while only 103 (10.4%) had their vision checked regularly. Furthermore, a great majority of respondents (724, 73%) were eager to seek eye care services in the future, whereas only 268 (27%) were unwilling to seek eye care services in the future.

Of the participants, 225 (22.7%) had ocular discharge, 109 (11.0%) sought care for flies on their children’s eyes, 87 (8.8%) had both ocular and nasal discharge, 37 (3.7%) had nasal discharge and 534 (53.8%) had no abnormalities (table 3).

Concerning barriers to eye healthcare utilisation, majority of the respondents (123, 40.6%) reported lack of awareness about healthcare services, followed by lack of money (58, 19.2%), no transportation available to visit eye care facilities (47, 15.6%) and no family or relatives to help them during eye treatment (18, 6.1%). In addition to this, bad surgical outcome in the past (19, 6.3%), fear of having surgery (12, 3.8%), cultural or language barrier (4, 1.3%), no eye health concern (9, 3.1%), lack of medication at a health facility for CBHI (3, 1.0%) and others (8, 2.8%) were also reported as barriers (table 3).

Factors associated with eye health service utilisation

In the bivariate logistic regression analysis, age, sex, wealth quantile, time to reach eye health facility, regular vision check-up, history of eye disease, knowledge of eye health, practice of eye care, cost of transportation, being informed about eye healthcare and walking distance were the variables associated with increased or decreased likelihood of eye healthcare utilisation (p<0.25). After adjusting for potential confounders using multivariate logistic regression, the <30 years age group was 2.7 times (AOR=2.7; 95% CI 1.5, 4.6; p<0.001) more likely to use eye care service than those aged 48–85 years. The odds of eye health service utilisation among men were 2.2 times (AOR=2.2; 95% CI 1.4, 3.6; p<0.001) higher than women. Respondents walking less than 60 min to reach health facilities were two times (AOR=2.0; 95% CI 1.3, 3.1; p<0.001) more likely to use eye care services than their counterparts. Similarly, respondents having regular vision check-ups were 5.4 times (AOR=5.4; 95% CI 3.47, 8.37; p<0.001) and respondents with eye disease history were 4.8 times (AOR=4.8; 95% CI 2.5, 9.1; p<0.001) more likely to use eye care services compared with their counterparts. Respondents with eyelashes touching the eyeball were 1.5 times (AOR=1.5; 95% CI 1.1, 2.3; p=0.02) more likely to use eye healthcare services than their counterparts. However, respondents in the low wealth quantile were 62% (AOR=0.62, 95% CI 0.39, 0.98; p=0.04) less likely to use eye healthcare services compared with their counterparts. Respondents with poor knowledge were 26% (AOR=0.26; 95% CI 0.16, 0.44; p<0.001) less likely to use eye healthcare services compared with those having good knowledge (table 4).

Discussion

In this community-based study, we evaluated the level of and the factors associated with eye healthcare utilisation among 992 adult respondents at Gamo and Gofa Zones in Southern Ethiopia. The reported rate of eye care service utilisation was 21.6% (n=214; 95% CI 19.0, 24.3) within the past 2 years during the data collection. This evidence is comparable to the study carried out in Hawassa (23.8%)14 and slightly lower than a similar study conducted in Southern Ethiopia (29%).19 This finding is higher than the previous studies conducted in Nigeria, at 18%10 and 19%,22 but lower than the study conducted in South Africa, at 26.6%.9 Possible reasons for the disparity could be geographical location, study participants, study methodology and socioeconomic characteristics.

Regarding gaps in eye health facilities, about two-thirds (139, 65.0%) reported lack of trained eye care professionals as a major gap, followed by lack of medicine in the health facility (127, 59.3%), lack of medical equipment and supplies (127, 59.3%) and lack of good management system (104, 48.6%). This is supported by evidence from a study conducted in Nigeria among 48 health facilities showing lack of national policy specifically for primary eye care, lack of medication for eye conditions, inadequate service coverage and poor data handling as major gaps in facilities providing eye health services.23 Another mixed method study in Nigeria showed presence of fragmented eye care policies, limited health promotion activities to access care provided in facilities, limited health education in communities, poor supervision of health promotion activities and limited transportation to remote facilities as major gaps in facilities delivering eye healthcare services.24

Concerning barriers to eye health service utilisation, lack of awareness about healthcare services (123, 40.6%) was a major barrier to eye care utilisation in the study area, followed by lack of money (58, 19.2%) and lack of transportation to visit eye care facilities (47, 15.6%). This is supported by studies conducted in Cambodia,25 South Central Virginia26 and Mozambique,12 as well as a systematic review on improving eye care.27 Similarly, lack of family or relatives to help during eye treatment (18, 6.1%), bad surgical outcome in the past (19, 6.3%), fear of having surgery (12, 3.8%) and cultural or language barrier (4, 1.3%) were barriers to eye care service utilisation. This finding is comparable to prior evidence stating that patients consider previous eye service utilisation as a reference to judge for the next visit,28 and evidence from a Mozambique study showing that those using traditional medication had no interest in seeking eye care services.12

In this study, respondents in the <30 years age group were 2.7 times more likely to use eye care services compared with the older age group (AOR=2.7; 95% CI 1.5, 4.6, p<0.001). This is comparable to a previous study conducted in South Africa.9 However, this is contrary to the findings from a study conducted in Hawassa, Ethiopia showing that individuals of older age (>65 years) were five times more likely to use eye services,14 a study conducted in Meskan, Southern Ethiopia showing older age (participants aged 65 and above) was positively associated with eye care service utilisation19 and a study in South Western Nigeria showing individuals 70 years and above were 1.7 times more likely to use eye care services.22 These variations could be due to differences in age of the participants included in the studies. For example, the study conducted in Hawassa used the elderly population, while the study conducted in Meskan used participants 40 years of age and above. In the present study, adults aged 20 years and above were included. In addition, sociodemographic factors in the study area could also contribute to differences in eye care service utilisation (ie, in the rural area, access to information for the elderly is limited). In addition to this, elderly people may lack support from their families to bring them to an eye care facility, as evidenced by this study showing 62=1 (6.1%) reported having no family or relatives to help them or take care of them during eye treatment.

Male respondents were 2.2 times (AOR=2.2; 95% CI 1.4, 3.6; p<0.001) more likely to use eye care services compared with female respondents. This finding is comparable to a study conducted in Western Kenya29 and South Western Nigeria.22 However, a previous study contradicts this current finding stating that men had lower rates of eye exams than women,13 as well as another study showing that women were more likely to get eye exams than men.9 Possible justification could be that majority (839, 84.6%) of household decisions in this study area were made by men. These could make men more economically empowered and thus seek eye care services more than women.

In this study, the respondents in the low wealth quantiles were 62% (AOR=0.62; 95% CI 0.39, 0.98; p=0.04) less inclined to use eye care services compared with those in the richest quantile. This finding is supported by a study conducted in South Africa,9 Ethiopia14 19 and Peru.15 Evidence showed that participants having greater income were more likely to have had an eye exam within the previous year. This is because wealthier individuals are more likely to seek out eye care screening and treatments as they may be better able to pay for them.13

Our findings revealed that respondents with poor knowledge were 26% (AOR=0.26; 95% CI 0.16, 0.44; p<0.001) less likely to use eye care services compared with their counterparts. This finding is consistent with previously published literature, including a systematic review of barriers to eye care service utilisation in African countries,30 other studies conducted in developing countries22 and a study conducted in Meskan, Southern Ethiopia showing that having awareness about regular check-ups was positively associated with eye care service utilisation.19 Those with good knowledge about cataracts or other eye health problems were more inclined to participate in eye health screening and treatment.31

According to the findings of the present study, those who had regular vision check-up were 5.4 times (AOR=5.4; 95% CI 3.47, 8.37; p<0.001) more likely to use eye care services compared with those who had no regular vision check-up. This finding is supported by a study conducted in Ethiopia14 and Meskan, Southern Ethiopia.19 Regular vision check-ups are crucial to early detection and treatment of vision problems.20 32

The odds of using eye care service were almost 4.8 times more likely among individuals with a history of eye disease compared with those who had no eye disease history (AOR=4.8; 95% CI 2.5, 9.1; p<0.001). This finding supports the study conducted among adults in Hawassa City14 and Meskan, Southern Ethiopia.19 Furthermore, the current finding is supported by another study done in South Africa indicating that having a healthcare assessment within 5 years increases the likely utilisation of eye care services.9 Individuals with a history of eye disease had a higher likelihood of attending an eye care facility. This could be attributed to having health education and being exposed to information about eye care services, increasing the probability of using eye care services.

The odds of eye health service utilisation were 2.0 times higher among respondents who had less than 60 min of travel time to reach eye health facilities (AOR=2.0; 95% CI 1.3, 3.1; p<0.001) compared with their counterparts. This is in line with a previous study that showed living close to an eye care facility increases the utilisation of eye care services, supporting the current finding.22 Another study also indicated that the distance of the health facility is a factor that hinders the utilisation of eye care services.26 Therefore, individuals residing close to eye health facilities have an increased probability of accessing eye services because they may not have infrastructure problems such as transportation to health facilities.

The current study found that those who had eyelashes touching the eyeball were 1.5 times (AOR=1.5; 95% CI 1.1, 2.3; p=0.02) more likely to use eye care services compared with those presenting with eyelashes not touching the eyeball. This is supported by a study conducted in Hossana, Ethiopia33 showing that those who had post-trachoma trichiasis were more likely to visit eye health facilities, and another study conducted in Tigray showing that individuals with a history of TT surgery were more likely to visit eye health facilities.34 Possible reasons for this could be patients seeking eye care due to severe eye pain, progressive vision loss and for cosmetic purposes.

A key strength of our study was the use of an adequate and representative sample size to answer the research question. Another strength is the use of a digital form with a geographical map for data collection. However, our study also has some limitations. In view of the cross-sectional study design, no temporal relationships could be identified. Additionally, the high prevalence of illiteracy among the respondents might have impacted the level of recall bias.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the eye care utilisation was below a quarter. Gender, waiting time, regular eye care check-ups and information about eye care were positively associated with eye care utilisation, while low wealth quantile and poor knowledge were negatively associated. Therefore, different strategies and efforts are necessary to alleviate the barriers that hinder eye care utilisation and that may increase optimal eye care utilisation. To increase awareness and ensure robust and consistent eye health education, advocacy and promotion of utilisation of eye care services, an extensive and ongoing campaign should be done to reach remote and hard-to-reach communities. In addition, designing strategies that can improve the capacity of healthcare facilities (technically and materially) and increasing public awareness are critical success factors, as evidenced by the lack of trained eye care professionals and the lack of medicines and medical supplies being major barriers to service utilisation. Researchers willing to study similar areas should focus on community-specific approaches that can improve community health literacy by using stronger study designs.
