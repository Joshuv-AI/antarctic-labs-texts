A combination of ketones and NAD+ precursor preserves white matter integrity in mild cognitive impairment
Authors: Maggie Roy, Mélanie Fortier, Valérie St‐Pierre, Marie‐Christine Morin, Arnaud Boré, Manon Edde, Camille Vandenberghe, Karine Groulx, Étienne Croteau, Gabriel Richard, Stanislas Thoumyre, Tamas Fulop, Christian Bocti, François Rheault, Bernard Cuenoud, Maxime Descoteaux, Stephen C. Cunnane
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONBrain glucose metabolism declines and myelin deteriorates as Alzheimer's disease (AD) develops. Adequate energy supply to white matter (WM) is critical to maintain myelin integrity and axonal function. An exogenous source of ketones bypasses the glucose‑specific brain energy deficit and improves cognitive outcomes in mild cognitive impairment (MCI). The BREAK‐AD (BRain Energy Activation with Ketones in AD) trial tested a ketone salt and nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+) precursor mixture to compensate for reduced brain glucose uptake in MCI.METHODSParticipants were randomized to a placebo (n = 15) or active supplement (β‐hydroxybutyrate salts + nicotinamide riboside (NR); n = 15). Brain ketone and glucose metabolism (quantified by positron emission tomography [PET]), and cognitive performance were assessed before and at the end of the 6‐month intervention. For WM analysis, seven tracts of interest were extracted using diffusion magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and myelin density measures were derived from magnetization transfer (MT) imaging.RESULTSTotal gray matter ketone uptake increased by 2.4‐fold (p < 0.001) in the active group, with no change in gray matter glucose uptake in either group. In WM, ketone uptake increased in the active group by 3.1–3.6‐fold across all seven tracts of interest (p < 0.001). In the placebo group, myelin density declined by up to 10% in specific regions of the fornix (p = 0.027), with no change in the active group. Improved processing speed was significantly associated with post‐intervention change in myelin density (r = −0.39 to −0.59; p = 0.002–0.046) and ketone uptake (r = −0.40 to −0.52; p = 0.010–0.046) in WM tracts. Ketone uptake in specific WM tracts (fornix, uncinate and arcuate fasciculi), as well as in the composite of all tracts of interest was strongly associated with myelin density.DISCUSSIONThis study shows for the first time that improved myelin density may help explain the positive association between increased WM ketone uptake and improved processing speed in MCI after a ketone salt and NAD+ precursor supplementation.

BACKGROUND

A temporoparietal pattern of deteriorating brain glucose metabolism has been extensively reported in Alzheimer's disease (AD) and mild cognitive impairment (MCI). 1 , 2 1 8F‐fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG)‐positron emission tomography (PET) detects this hypometabolism early, often preceding volumetric changes observed with magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). 3 FDG‐PET predicts cognitive phenotype, specifically in the posterior cingulate 4 , and may potentially outperform amyloid‐PET as an indicator of cognitive performance. 5 While reduction in FDG uptake is most commonly reported in gray matter, white matter (WM) hypometabolism is also evident in both MCI 6 and AD. 7 , 8 WM energy supply is critical to maintain long‐term axonal integrity and signal transmission through metabolic coupling between oligodendrocytes and myelinated axons, the two main components of WM. Oligodendrocytes have high energy needs for the synthesis and dynamic remodeling of myelin lipids throughout life 9 and they transfer lactate or pyruvate to axons to sustain conduction and ATP homeostasis. 10 Disruption of WM microstructure assessed by diffusion MRI in MCI and AD 11 , 12 is associated with cognitive impairment. 13 An energetic deficit in WM may thus contribute to impaired brain function associated with the onset of AD.

Ketone bodies (or ketones: β‐hydroxybutyrate [BHB] and acetoacetate [AcAc]) are the main alternative energy substrates to glucose for the adult brain. Ketones are generated endogenously when intake of carbohydrate is low, such as during fasting or while on a low carbohydrate diet. Unlike glucose, brain ketone uptake is preserved both in MCI and AD. 2 , 6 , 14 Increasing ketone availability can compensate for reduced brain glucose uptake during aging‐associated cognitive decline and has been shown to improve cognitive performance in several randomized trials 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 . Ketones also exert neuroprotective effects: they reduce oxidative stress and neuroinflammation and preserve neurotransmission 19 .

Nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+) is a critical co‐factor closely linked to energy metabolism. NAD+ declines with aging and in neurodegenerative diseases 20 and the brain NAD+/NADH redox ratio also decreases during aging 21 but can be increased by ketone administration 22 . Restoration of brain NAD+ homeostasis has been demonstrated using the precursor nicotinamide riboside (NR) 23 . These findings suggest that a supplement combining a ketone salt and NAD+ precursor (BHB+NR) may provide synergistic benefits by correcting more completely energy deficits and neuronal dysfunction in MCI.

The primary aim of the randomized, double‐blind, placebo‐controlled BREAK‐AD (BRain Energy Activation with Ketones in AD) pilot study was to evaluate the effect of BHB+NR on brain ketone and glucose uptake, as measured by PET imaging, when administered over 6 months in MCI participants, compared with an isocaloric maltodextrin placebo. Secondary objectives included assessing the impact of BHB+NR on brain microstructure measured with MRI, cognitive performance, and overall tolerability.

METHODS

Participants

The BREAK‐AD trial (NCT04466735) was approved by our institutional ethical committee (CIUSSS de l'Estrie–CHUS, Sherbrooke) and the Health Canada Natural Health Products Directorate. The inclusion criteria were: men or women aged 55–80 years and MCI defined by subjective memory complaint, score between 20 and 25 on the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) and preserved functional autonomy (Pfeffer Functional Activities Questionnaire score < 9/30). Exclusion criteria are described in Supplementary Information.

Study design

MCI participants were randomly assigned (1:1) to the active or placebo product, which they consumed for 6 months. The active supplement (BHB+NR) consisted of a powdered mixture of sodium, calcium and magnesium salts of BHB providing a total 12 g BHB plus 550 mg NR chloride per sachet. The isocaloric placebo was composed primarily of maltodextrin, calcium and magnesium chloride (see Supplementary Tables A.1‐2 for complete products composition). Both products were manufactured by Nestlé Health Science in ISO‐certified facilities. Participants consumed two sachets/day taken at breakfast and dinner, providing 24 g BHB/day, each dissolved in 150 mL of water. During the first week, only 12 g BHB/day was taken to help gastrointestinal adaptation.

Participants underwent brain MRI and PET acquisitions following 4 to 6 hours of fasting, plus a neurocognitive battery in a separated visit, before and at the end of 6 months of intervention. At post‐intervention visits, participants consumed one sachet of their product 30 to 60 minutes before the first PET acquisition. A light breakfast was also taken before cognitive tests. Participants also attended monthly visits for safety laboratory tests (after overnight fasting), blood pressure measurement and assessment of adverse events. Plasma metabolites were analyzed as previously described 14 .

Cognitive assessment

The cognitive battery lasted approximately 2 hours. Cognitive testing at the end of the intervention started approximately 45 minutes post‐supplement to be in the time window where plasma ketones are maximal. Tests were performed in the same order for every participant. The Logical Memory II test from the Wechsler Memory Scale and the French version of the 16‐item free and cued word learning and recall test (RL/RI‐16) were used to measure episodic memory. The Trail‐Making, Stroop, and Verbal Fluency tests from the Delis‐Kaplan Executive Function System assessed processing speed, attention and executive function, respectively. The Digit Symbol Substitution Tests from the Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale provided information on processing speed and working memory. The Boston Naming Test was used for language ability. General wellness and cognitive complaint questionnaires were also included in the battery, as well as the Geriatric Depression Scale (30‐items).

Image acquisition

The MRI protocol was acquired on a 3 Tesla Philips Ingenia system and included a: (1) 3D T1‐weighted 1.0 mm isotropic sequence (duration = 4 minutes), (2) multi‐shell diffusion MRI protocol (duration = 9 minutes, 2.0 mm isotropic voxels) as previously described 24 , (3) magnetization transfer (MT) imaging protocol acquired using a 3D segmented‐echo planar imaging (EPI) gradient‐echo sequence (duration = 6 minutes) previously described, 24 and (4) 3D T2‐fluid attenuation inversion recovery (T2‐FLAIR) sequence (duration = 4 minutes).

RESEARCH IN CONTEXT

Systematic review: All peer‐reviewed articles available on PubMed reporting ketogenic interventions in mild cognitive impairment (MCI) or Alzheimer's disease (AD) were reviewed. No published studies giving a supplement combining ketones and nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+) precursors in MCI were found and no other group has assessed myelin measures in this context. Interpretation: This 6‐month randomized controlled trial in MCI demonstrates that a ketone salt+NAD+ precursor supplement preserved myelin density in the fornix, which was a direct function of white matter (WM) ketone supply. WM ketone uptake and myelin measures were both associated with processing speed. This study shows for the first time that improved myelin density may help explain the positive association between WM ketone uptake and processing speed in MCI. Future directions: A larger sample size is required to obtain definitive results for the cognitive outcomes measured in this study. However, other ketogenic supplements, such as medium chain triglycerides, may induce fewer adverse events than ketone salts thereby increasing participant retention for future trials.

The MRI session was followed by a dual‐tracer PET protocol acquired on a PET/CT system (Siemens Biograph Vision 600) in dynamic list mode as previously described 7 , 14 . The ketone PET images were acquired first, after injection of 11C‐AcAc (acquisition time of 20 minutes) and followed by a 1‐hour wash‐out period. FDG was then injected; acquisition started 30 minutes post‐injection and lasted for 30 minutes.

Image analysis

PET

PET images were automatically co‐registered to each participant's T1‐weighted image using a fusion tool implemented in PMOD 3.8 as previously described 7 . Co‐registered PET images were corrected for partial‐volume effect using the modified Müller‐Gartner method. The Patlak method 25 and image‐derived input functions were used to quantify brain net influx rate constant for both tracers (Ki; min−1) and their respective brain metabolic rates (MRAcAc, MRFDG; µmol/100 g/min; referred hereafter as “uptake”; Figure 1). Uptake voxel‐wise maps were generated for both tracers and co‐registered to diffusion MRI data using ANTs as previously described 7 . In parallel, the reversible 1‐tissue model was also used for 11C‐AcAc image analysis to determine the direct ketone transport rate (K1; ml/g/min) and metabolite washout (k2; min−1) constants.

Major steps in image processing pipelines. (1) Whole brain tractograms were reconstructed from diffusion MRI using the TractoFlow Atlas‐Based Segmentation pipeline. (2) Free‐water‐corrected DTI measures, fixel‐based AFD, FW and myelin‐sensitive maps were computed. (3) Ketone (AcAc) and glucose (FDG) brain metabolic rates were quantified from PET images using PXMOD. (4) Automated extraction tools (RecobundlesX) were used to extract the seven WM tracts of interest. Imaging measures were quantified for all WM tracts of interest individually, as well as in the composite of these seven tracts (referred to as AD_tracts). (5) Multi‐tract measures were computed using a tractometry pipeline (Tractometry_flow). Each tract measure was computed as (A) whole tract average and (B) tract‐profiles separated along its length into five sections to provide further spatial insight and tract‐profiling of imaging markers along the tracts of interest. 3T MRI, 3 Tesla magnetic resonance imaging; AcAc, 11C‐acetoacetate; AFD, apparent fiber density; FDG, 18F‐flurodeoxyglucose; FW, free‐water index; MTI, magnetization transfer imaging; MTR, magnetization transfer ratio; PET/CT, positron emission tomography/computed tomography; RDT, tissue‐based radial diffusivity; WM, white matter.

Diffusion MRI

Preprocessing and processing of diffusion‐weighted images were performed as previously described 6 , 7 using TractoFlow pipeline 26 with the Atlas Based Segmentation profile (https://github.com/scilus/tractoflow) and detailed in Supplementary Information. The free‐water index (FW) was computed to capture isotropic extra‐cellular water diffusion and used as an index of inflammation 27 . Diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) measures were free‐water‐corrected to reduce partial volume effects of the cerebrospinal fluid associated with aging. Fixel‐based apparent fiber density (AFD) 28 was extracted from the fiber orientation distribution functions and used as a marker of axonal integrity.

TractoFlow‐ABS performs local probabilistic tractography in lesion‐corrected WM masks to reconstruct whole brain tractograms. Seven tracts of interest, generally reported with microstructural alteration in AD 7 , 11 , were extracted. RecobundlesX pipeline (https://github.com/scilus/rbx_flow) was used to extract the posterior cingulum (CG_pos), arcuate (AF), inferior fronto‐occipital (IFOF), inferior longitudinal (ILF), and uncinate fasciculi (UF). Due to its location next to lateral ventricles contaminating voxels with cerebrospinal fluid, the fornix was reconstructed using a separate automated tract‐specific approach 29 . Finally, WM query language was used to extract the parahippocampal segment of the cingulum (PHC) 7 .

MT imaging

MT imaging (MTI) is sensitive to changes in WM myelin content 30 . MT images were processed as previously described 24 (https://github.com/scilus/ihmt_flow), generating two myelin‐sensitive maps: MT ratio (MTR; referred hereafter as “myelin density”) and MT saturation 31 . These maps were registered to T1‐weighted images in diffusion MRI space using nonlinear symmetric diffeomorphic normalization from ANTs.

WM hyperintensities

SHIVA, a deep‐learning‐based automatic tool 32 , was used to segment WM hyperintensities (WMH) with T2‐FLAIR and T1‐weighted images as inputs (https://github.com/pboutinaud/SHIVA_WMH).

Tractometry

PET, diffusion MRI, and MTI measures were quantified in specific WM tracts using a tractometry pipeline (https://github.com/scilus/tractometry_flow) 24 and computed as (1) whole tract average and (2) tract‐profiles. To generate tract‐profiles, each tract was resampled into five equidistant sections 7 (Figure 1). The imaging measures were quantified for WM tracts individually as well as for the composite of the seven tracts of interest, which is referred to as AD_tracts (for WM tracts that are vulnerable to change in AD; Figure 1).

Statistical analysis

An initial sample size of 40 participants (n = 20/group), based on the primary outcome (change in brain ketone uptake after supplementation), was calculated to be sufficient to detect an effect size of 1.1, with an alpha risk of 5% and 80% power (G*Power 3.1.9.2). Anticipating a 30% drop‐out during the 6‐month intervention 14 , we planned to recruit 57 participants in total.

Because assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variances for the two groups were not met at enrollment, the nonparametric Mann‐Whitney test was used for baseline intergroup comparison (placebo vs active). Imaging measures in the seven WM tracts of interest (tract averages) were all normally distributed and a general linear model was used for intergroup comparison of changes (post − pre), with sex and total WMH volume as covariates. Partial ƞ2 values of 0.01, 0.06, and 0.14 were considered to be small, moderate, or large effect sizes, respectively. For tract‐profiles, ketone uptake and MTR in each tract section were set as repeated measures using a general linear model to assess the effect of the intervention along each of the seven tracts of interest, where sphericity across data was not assumed and the Geisser–Greenhouse correction was used. Intergroup analyses of cognitive data and gray matter PET measures were analyzed with a general linear model, with sex as a covariate. Correlations were performed to assess a possible association between changes in ketone uptake or MTR in WM tracts and processing speed (Trail Making Test).

RESULTS

Clinical data

A total of 30 participants (n = 15/group) completed the interventions (Supplementary Figure A.1). The most prevalent drug classes used by the participants were statins and non‐anticholinergic antidepressants. The number of participants taking statins at enrollment was equal in both groups (three participants/group) and the number taking non‐anticholinergic antidepressants was similar between both groups (four in the placebo group and five in the active group). All completers were protocol‐compliant, that is, they consumed a mean of 92 ± 6% of the planned daily dose of the supplements, as measured by return sachet count. Adverse events (mostly gastrointestinal) are presented in Supplementary Table A.3. No serious or severe adverse events were reported. At baseline, both groups were of similar age and education (Table 1). The ratio of males/females was significantly different between both groups and there was a trend towards higher total WMH volume in the active group. Compared to baseline values, at the end of the study, plasma AcAc and BHB were increased by 3.6 and 3.7‐fold, respectively, in the active group (Supplementary Table A.4). No clinically significant changes were observed in plasma lipids (Supplementary Table A.5) and in the measured blood safety markers except for an increase in creatinine (Supplementary Table A.6).

Cognition

At baseline, MoCA, PHQ‐9, and the functional autonomy scores were not significantly different between the two groups (Table 1). The active group showed preserved episodic memory versus placebo after controlling for sex, with a large effect size for total recall (RL/RI‐16 trial 3; p = 0.039; partial ƞ 2 = 0.15), and a trend for immediate recall (p = 0.055; partial ƞ 2 = 0.13) (Supplementary Table A.7). Score on the cognitive complaint scale also improved in the active versus placebo group (p = 0.014; partial ƞ 2 = 0.21). There was no significant difference between groups for the other neurocognitive tests (Supplementary Table A.7).

PET measures in gray matter

After the 6‐month intervention, whole brain and total gray matter ketone uptake (MRAcAc) increased by 2.4‐fold (p < 0.001) in the active group but did not change significantly on placebo (Supplementary Figure A.2). There was a statistically larger increase in total gray matter ketone uptake in the active versus placebo group, with a large effect size after controlling for sex (p = 0.001; partial ƞ 2 = 0.36). In the active group, ketone uptake was increased by 2.4 to 2.5‐fold across the main gray matter regions when analyzed individually (all p < 0.001; Supplementary Table A.4). There was a statistically larger increase in whole brain and regional ketone transport rate constant (K1) in the placebo versus active group (Supplementary Table A.8), with no intergroup difference for ketone metabolite washout constant (k2). There was no change in total gray matter or regional glucose uptake (MRFDG) in either group (data not reported).

PET measures in WM tracts

When averaged for whole tracts, ketone uptake was increased in the active group by 3.1‐ to 3.6‐fold in the seven tracts of interest (p < 0.001; Supplementary Table A.9). Change in mean tract ketone uptake were statistically greater in the active versus placebo group with large effect sizes after controlling for sex and WMH (p < 0.001 to 0.002; partial ƞ 2 = 0.34 to 0.63), with the highest effect size in the fornix. Ketone uptake along WM tract‐profiles was significantly increased in the active group for all seven tracts of interest (p < 0.001; Figure 2).

DISCUSSION

The BREAK‐AD trial showed that a 6‐month supplementation with a ketone salt and NAD+ precursor (BHB+NR) improved whole brain energy supply in MCI, with a 2.4‐fold increase in ketone uptake (Supplementary Figure A.2), and no significant effect on brain glucose uptake. The supplement increased plasma ketones and WM tract ketone supply by more than 3.5‐fold, which was more than the 2.4‐fold increase in gray matter. BHB+NR improved myelin density in the fornix, which is one of the most age‐vulnerable brain regions, and as a direct function of improved WM ketone supply. Among the WM tracts analyzed, the increase in ketone uptake in the fornix had the highest effect size (0.63). Hence, BHB+NR appears to help maintain WM integrity. Improved WM ketone supply and myelin measures were associated with improved processing speed. This study indicates for the first time that myelin density may explain the positive association between increased brain ketones and processing speed in older individuals previously observed 6 .

Compared to the BENEFIC trial also conducted in MCI with the same 6‐month supplementation period but with medium chain triglycerides 14 , BHB+NR increased plasma ketones ∼60% more than medium chain triglycerides and brain ketone uptake in WM tracts ∼20% more (Figure 2) 6 . The current results confirm the BENEFIC trial results that improved processing speed in MCI is strongly associated with increased brain WM ketone uptake 6 (Figure 4) and that ketones improve measures of episodic memory (free and cued recall) 15 . However, the percentage of participants with adverse events, precisely diarrhea, in the active group was higher with BHB+NR than with medium chain triglycerides (49 vs 11%, respectively; Supplementary Table A.3). Drop‐outs in the active group were also higher in the present study than in the BENEFIC trial (51 vs 30%, respectively). Although medium chain triglycerides are less ketogenic than ketone salts, they may induce fewer adverse events thereby increasing participant retention in future trials.

Functional and cognitive benefits of ketogenic interventions in patients with cognitive decline 15 , 16 , 17 , 33 , 34 can be attributed to improved brain energy status as measured in the present study, since ketones are a key alternative energy substrate for neurons, compensating the chronic glucose deficit in brain cells 2 , 14 . In addition to benefitting brain energy metabolism, a neuroprotective role of ketones may also be implicated. Indeed, ketones increase brain‐derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) 35 , 36 and cerebral blood flow 36 in healthy individuals, which could both have an impact on cognition during aging‐associated cognitive decline. Preservation of cognitive measures (episodic memory) in the present study may also be attributed in part to the combination of BHB+NR, which would be expected to help restore the age‐associated decrease in NAD+ and may help preserve mitochondrial function 20 .

Supplementation with BHB+NR for 6 months prevented the decline of myelin density seen in the placebo group in which MTR declined by up to 10% in the fornix (Figure 3), a structure highly implicated in memory retention. This agrees with the fact that episodic memory (total recall) was the only cognitive measure showing an intergroup difference, after controlling for sex (Supplementary Table A.7). MTR results in the fornix were also confirmed with a second myelin‐sensitive measure, MT saturation, showing the value of tractography to detect local microstructural changes in the brain. The fornix is particularly vulnerable to microstructural deterioration associated with aging, which occurs presymptomatically 37 , even before hippocampal atrophy 38 , 39 . In this study, we detected significant changes in MTR over 6 months, which agrees with previous studies showing lower MTR values in the medial temporal lobe in MCI 40 and throughout the brain in AD 41 .

Demyelination during aging contributes to cognitive dysfunction and the development of AD by impairing connectivity of brain networks 42 . Myelination enables fast and reliable information processing and continuous myelin sheath remodeling and lipid turnover throughout life help adjust conduction speed which is crucial for optimal motor skills and cognitive function. Oligodendrocytes have a relatively high energy requirement due to their role in synthesis and remodeling of myelin 43 . Indeed, in the present study, the increase in ketone uptake was ∼40% higher in WM tracts than in gray matter regions. Their high energy need make oligodendrocytes more vulnerable to impaired glucose metabolism (glycolysis) in AD 44 . In addition, the apolipoprotein E4 isoform, an important risk factor for the development of AD, reduces the population of mature and actively myelinating oligodendrocytes 45 . Overall, this demonstrates a potential link between glucose hypometabolism and demyelination in AD pathophysiology. The therapeutic potential of BHB+NR in AD therefore seems linked to fueling oligodendrocytes and neurons despite impaired glucose metabolism but also as substrates for cholesterol synthesis and remyelination 46 .

Improved processing speed was strongly associated with ketone uptake in WM tracts (Figure 4), a link which may have been due to better preserved myelin (Figure 5). Processing speed is directly linked to myelin integrity because the speed of conduction of action potentials is proportional to the thickness of the myelin sheath 47 , 48 .

The BREAK‐AD trial had several limitations. Enrollment was challenging because of slow recruitment and difficulty for retention of participants during coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID‐19) pandemic, leading to a smaller sample size than planned (30 completed instead of 40). Although this study was underpowered relative to the initial plan, the between‐group difference in the primary outcome (brain ketone uptake) and plasma ketones showed a larger effect size than anticipated. However, smaller effects across brain glucose uptake (second aspect of the primary endpoint) and secondary outcomes (MRI or cognition) may have gone undetected due to the smaller sample size and statistical power than planned. The high proportion of drop‐outs was unexpected and appears to have been due to gastrointestinal discomfort and sour taste of the active supplement, as reported elsewhere 49 . Daily intake of a relatively high amount of magnesium (0.52 g/day) may underly osmotic diarrhea, which is a common side effect from magnesium supplementation at doses > 0.35 g/day. 50 Gastrointestinal discomfort may also have been due in part to elevated blood ketones. There was an increase in plasma creatinine, potentially linked to salts contained in the active supplement. Although group means stayed inside the normal ranges, two participants were withdrawn from the study because of elevated creatinine and thus it warrants caution if this supplement is further developed for longer‐term studies in humans. The ratio of males/females was also unbalanced between placebo and active groups due mainly to unbalanced drop‐outs. Stratified or adaptive randomization (minimization), instead of block randomization would have helped prevent this imbalance, especially with a small sample size.

In conclusion, in the present randomized controlled trial, a supplement combining a ketone salt and NAD+ precursor (BHB+NR) improved whole brain energy supply in MCI, especially in WM tracts. BHB+NR preserved myelin density in the fornix, one of the most age‐vulnerable brain regions, which was a direct function of improved WM energy status. Improved WM ketone supply and myelin measures were both associated with improved processing speed. These results support a key role of WM in processing speed for which ketones and NAD+ may act as an important structural and energy substrate. Ketogenic supplementation may prove beneficial for patients suffering from other WM and demyelinating diseases.
