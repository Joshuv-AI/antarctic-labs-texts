Early Identification and Management of Patients with Rash on Apalutamide
Authors: Alison J Birtle, Luigi Formisano, Vincent Descamps, Peter Weisenseel, Antoni Vilaseca
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractApalutamide is a selective androgen receptor signalling inhibitor that is used in the treatment of prostate cancer. Skin rash is one of the most common adverse events with apalutamide. Although the majority of rash events are grade 1 and 2, the appearance of skin rash during treatment can lead to dose reduction, a pause in treatment or even treatment discontinuation, especially if patients present late when the rash has become severe. This in turn can result in a significant delay or even a permanent discontinuation in the patient’s treatment of prostate cancer. As apalutamide is a generally well tolerated and an effective treatment for many men with advanced prostate cancer, it is extremely important to make attempts to prevent skin problems or to manage them at the earliest stage possible. We therefore have developed practical guidance for the management of apalutamide-related rash, including an infographic with recommendations for rash management by grade. Central to this approach is patient education and awareness. Encouraging patients to proactively care for their skin from the start of treatment and informing them of the risk of rash with apalutamide therapy are essential. If the patient observes any skin changes, they should be advised to report it straight away to their cancer care team. Adopting this simple, proactive approach of patient education and increased vigilance from the care team is expected to lead to early identification of rash and subsequent intervention to allow for quicker resolution and enable patients to continue their cancer treatment with a drug that can delay disease progression and increase survival in patients with prostate cancer.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40487-024-00290-7.Keywords: Adverse event, Apalutamide, Cutaneous side effects, Dermatology, Oncology, Prostate cancer, Skin rash, Safety, Therapy management

Introduction

Apalutamide is an orally administered, selective androgen receptor (AR) signalling inhibitor used in combination with androgen deprivation therapy (ADT) for the treatment of adult men with prostate cancer. In Europe, it is approved as treatment for patients with non-metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer (nmCRPC) at high risk of developing metastatic disease, or with metastatic hormone-sensitive prostate cancer (mHSPC). Apalutamide binds directly to the ligand-binding domain of the AR to prevent AR nuclear translocation, inhibit DNA binding and impede AR-mediated transcription, thus enhancing its antitumour activity by reducing tumour cell proliferation and by promoting apoptosis [1, 2]. The efficacy and safety of apalutamide has been established in two phase 3, randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled studies, the TITAN [1] and SPARTAN [2] trials. In the TITAN trial, 1052 patients with mHSPC were randomised to receive either 240 mg of oral apalutamide (N = 525) or placebo (N = 527), both once daily, and all patients received concomitant ADT with gonadotropin-releasing hormone analogue (GnRHa) or had prior bilateral orchiectomy [1]. Compared to the patients receiving placebo + ADT, patients treated with apalutamide + ADT had a statistically significant improvement in radiographic progression–free survival (rPFS) (68.2% vs. 47.5%; hazard ratio [HR] 0.48; 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.39–0.60; P < 0.001) and a significantly reduced risk of death by 35% in the intention-to treat population (median overall survival [OS] not reached vs. 52.2 months; HR 0.65, 95% CI 0.53–0.79; P < 0.0001) and by 48% after adjustment for crossover from placebo to apalutamide (HR 0.52, 95% CI 0.42–0.64; P < 0.0001) [1, 3]. In the SPARTAN trial, 1207 men with nmCRPC were randomised 2:1 to receive either 240 mg oral apalutamide in combination with ADT or placebo + ADT, both once daily [2]. Compared to the placebo arm, apalutamide significantly reduced the risk of metastasis or death by > 70% and improved the median metastasis-free survival (MFS) by > 2 years (40.5 vs. 16.2 months; HR 0.28; 95% CI 0.23–0.35; P < 0.001) [2].

Safety Profile of Apalutamide—with a Focus on Dermatologic Adverse Events

Skin rash is one of the most common adverse events (AEs) associated with apalutamide [1–4]. In the TITAN and SPARTAN trials, treatment-related skin rash was more frequent in patients in the apalutamide arm (27.1% and 23.8%, respectively) versus those in the placebo arm (8.5% and 5.5%, respectively) and was commonly described as macular or maculo-papular [1, 2]. The majority of reported rash events were grade 1 and 2, while grade 3 rashes, defined as rashes covering > 30% body surface area (BSA), were reported in 6.3% and 5.2% of patients treated with apalutamide compared with 0.6% and 0.3% in the placebo arm, respectively [1, 2]. In the TITAN study, skin rash led to dose interruption in 44 patients (8.4%), dose reduction in 28 (5.3%) patients and treatment discontinuation in 12 patients (2.3%) [1]. In this study, median time to onset of rash was 81 days in the apalutamide group and 141 days in the placebo group [1]. In the final analysis of TITAN, after unblinding and placebo-to-apalutamide crossover, 29.2% of patients in the apalutamide arm experienced skin rash of any grade, compared to 9.3% of patients in the placebo group; most skin rash events were grade ≤ 2, with the incidence of rash reaching a plateau after about 6 months [3]. Similarly, in the SPARTAN trial, skin rash led to treatment discontinuation in 19 patients (2.4%), dose reduction in 22 (2.7%) patients and dose interruption in 55 (6.8%) patients [2]. The onset of skin rash occurred at a median of 82 days of treatment and resolved within a median of 60 days for 80.6% of patients [2]. There were no reported events of toxic epidermal necrolysis (TEN) or Stevens-Johnson syndrome (SJS) in either trial [1, 2]. The safety findings of these phase 3 trials have been confirmed in a real-world study which found that the majority (88.7%) of dermatological AEs were grade 1 or 2 and there were no cases of SJS, TEN or grade 4 AEs [5].

Management of Rash as per the Summary of Product Characteristics

According to the manufacturer’s Summary of Product Characteristics for apalutamide, when an AE ≥ grade 3 occurs, treatment should be paused rather than permanently discontinued until symptoms improve to at least grade 1 or return to the initial grade, followed by the resumption of apalutamide treatment at the same or a reduced dose (180 mg or 120 mg) if warranted. Patients should be advised about the signs and symptoms suggestive of a drug reaction with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms (DRESS), SJS or TEN, and advised to stop apalutamide and seek immediate medical attention. No cases of DRESS, SJS or TEN were reported in the SPARTAN and TITAN trials [1, 2]; however, severe cutaneous adverse reactions (SCARs) have been reported in the literature with the use of apalutamide, primarily among Asian patients. [6, 7]. DRESS can appear as a widespread rash, fever and enlarged lymph nodes. Initially, SJS/TEN can appear on the trunk as some reddish target-like spots or circular patches, often with central blisters. Furthermore, skin peeling and/or ulcers in the mouth, throat, nose and genitals can occur, and the eyes can be red and swollen. These serious skin rashes are often preceded by fever and/or flu-like symptoms. The rashes may progress to widespread skin peeling and, in rare cases, to life-threatening complications. In case of DRESS, SJS/TEN or grade 4 AEs, apalutamide must be permanently discontinued.

Rash Management in the Clinical Trials

In the TITAN and SPARTAN trials, rash was managed by grade according to the study protocol, as shown in Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM) Table 1 [1–4]. For grade 1 rash, apalutamide could be continued at the current dose and the rash treated with a topical steroid and oral antihistamines. For grade 2 or symptomatic grade 1 rashes, apalutamide was paused for up to 28 days, with the patients receiving the same treatment as for grade 1 rash. For grade ≥ 3 rash, apalutamide treatment was paused for up to 28 days and the patient treated as described above with the addition of a short course of oral corticosteroids if needed. If the rash improved to grade ≤ 1, apalutamide treatment could be re-initiated at a one-dose-level reduction (i.e., 180 or 120 mg).

Experience of Apalutamide-Associated Rash in Clinical Practice

While the appearance of rash with apalutamide treatment was identified in the clinical trials as described in the preceding section, initially there was limited experience in recognising and managing it in clinical practice. In our early experience with apalutamide, patients reported rash to their physician at a late stage, often when the rash had already become severe. In these patients, management of grade 3 rash required treatment with high-dose oral and topical steroids, and sometimes required hospital admission. This treatment can lead to a significant delay—or even a permanent discontinuation—in the prostate cancer treatment of the affected patient, which may have been working well from an oncological viewpoint. This experience highlighted the need to establish a much more proactive approach to the management of patients starting apalutamide, in order to reduce the risk of serious events by identifying and treating patients experiencing treatment-related skin rash early and thereby enabling their cancer treatment to be continued. As apalutamide is a generally well-tolerated and effective treatment for many men with advanced prostate cancer, it is extremely important to try and prevent skin problems or to manage them at the earliest stage possible. Even a grade 1 reaction can impact the quality of life of these patients and affect compliance with life-extending treatment for prostate cancer.

Practical Guidance on the Identification and Management of Apalutamide-Related Rash—A Proactive Approach for Clinical Practice

Practical guidance was developed by a group of oncologists, urologists and dermatologists to bring together their joint experience in treating cancer and managing skin reactions. The aim is to identify apalutamide-related rash at the earliest opportunity in order to minimise its impact on cancer treatment. In principle, this includes: (1) patient education to increase awareness and facilitate early recognition and presentation in the event of a rash developing; (2) advising patients to adopt gentle skin care from the start of treatment; and (3) recommendations for the management of rash according to its severity. Patients starting treatment with apalutamide should be educated on what to look for, encouraged to proactively care for their skin (see Fig. 1) and be closely monitored for any changes. The importance of skin care and rash reporting should be highlighted at every appointment. Importantly, patients should contact their cancer care team at the first sign of a rash or skin changes so that the team can intervene early when the rash is at grade 1 or 2, thereby enabling apalutamide treatment to be continued, even if a temporary dose reduction is needed. It is imperative to inform patients about the risk of rash development and explain what to look for by showing pictures of the typical apalutamide-related rash. In clinical trials as well as in current practice, the cutaneous manifestation is often described as macular or maculo-papular, characterised by itchy macules (flat) and papules (elevated) that spread centripetally from the upper trunk (see Fig. 2).

While maculo-papular rash is the most common type of presentation, clinicians should be aware that other possible manifestations can occur with apalutamide, including exfoliative rash, and that rash can affect various areas of the body, including the mouth, eyes and genitals. Oral or eye mucosal involvement should always trigger urgent dermatology referral. It is important to explain to the patient that although the rash can look unpleasant, it is rarely dangerous and usually resolves with appropriate interventions. Patients should be made aware of the warning signs of a serious AE, such as mucosal or eye involvement, and advised to stop apalutamide and seek immediate medical attention if this happens. Any systemic symptoms, such as a high temperature, pain or blistering, or a rash that covers > 30% of the body would be signs that the patient should be urgently reviewed the dermatologist. The rash may also be a manifestation of a more severe systemic syndrome, such as DRESS with organ involvement, especially of the liver. A simple total blood count to check for abnormal leukocyte count (atypical lymphocytes and/or eosinophilia) is recommended for making or excluding a diagnosis of DRESS, which is not limited to the severity of the rash but linked to visceral manifestations, in particular, liver involvement. It is also important to educate the patient’s caregiver in this process so they can support them and help with skin care and monitoring.

At the start of treatment, clinicians should ask the patient about pre-existing skin problems and examine their skin, including the soles of the feet. At each follow-up appointment—including in-person, online and phone consultations—the patient should be asked proactively if they have noticed any skin rash or changes. If the patient reports a rash or other skin changes, they should be assessed in person as soon as possible to establish whether the rash is related to apalutamide or has another cause, such as an inflammatory skin disorder, drug interaction, infection, allergy or insect bite. Clinicians should ask about concomitant medications, especially antibiotics, as well as the patient’s travel history or if anything has changed in their environment (see Table 1). Clinicians should consider the criteria for DRESS, such as fever, facial oedema, lymph node enlargement and abnormal leukocyte count, and those for SJS/TEN, such as mucous membrane involvement or blistering. If the precise cause of the rash is unclear, clinicians should consider referring the patient to dermatology for consultation. Skin biopsies could be useful in establishing the cause of the rash but should only be needed for grade 2 or 3 or if the patient has an abnormal leukocyte count. It should be noted that, in contrast to other drug reactions, there can be a significant delay in the development of the rash and that this delay does not rule out a drug reaction, especially for DRESS when a delay of several weeks is possible. If the rash is considered to be not related to apalutamide, the patient should be evaluated and treated according to the aetiology and symptoms. A diagnosis of adverse drug reaction (ADR) can be made when other possible causes have been ruled out, and its severity graded in accordance with the National Cancer Institute’s Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (NCI-CTCAE) Version 5.0, as shown in Table 2 [8].

Management of Rash by Grade

Grade 1 and 2 toxicities can usually be managed by the cancer care team but consideration should be given to referring the patient for a dermatological consultation if the skin toxicity is worsening despite intervention after a week of treatment, if > 30% of the skin’s surface is involved or if there is any sign of swelling or pain. All patients who experience skin toxicity ≥ 3 should be referred for dermatological consultation. For all grades, clinicians should ensure that the patient understands the need for good skin care with regular emollients/moisturisers containing ingredients such as urea ≤ 5% and/or glycerine (which should have been started prior to the initiation of apalutamide treatment). For topical treatments, some patients prefer ointments as they adhere to the skin, but runnier lotions may be preferable for covering large surface areas. Interventions could include topical steroids to control inflammation, oral antihistamines every 12 h, using non-sedating during the day and a sedating one at night when the itch can be more troublesome, and oral steroids for more severe reactions (see Fig. 1). If corticosteroids are needed, the patient should receive education on their appropriate use, as some patients may have concerns, as well as an explanation of how much to use: for example, one phalanx of a digit is enough to cover one hand.

If the rash does not improve after 5–7 days of treatment and other symptoms, including pain, blistering erythroderma, facial swelling or other signs of DRESS, appear, a complete blood count with blood smear and liver test is necessary to look for atypical lymphocytes, eosinophilia and hepatitis, and the patient should be referred for dermatological consultation and apalutamide treatment paused. In very severe cases, admitting the patient to hospital should be considered. Once the rash has improved to at least grade 1, corticosteroids can be gradually discontinued to avoid the rash flaring up, and treatment with apalutamide can be restarted at the same or a reduced dose (180 mg or 120 mg) if warranted. Apalutamide must not be restarted in patients who have experienced DRESS or SJS/TEN, and an alternative treatment should be considered.

Discussion

Finding the correct balance between efficacy and toxicity is fundamental for every oncological treatment. Identifying an emerging drug-related rash would help to facilitate early intervention and could prevent more severe reactions. Adopting a proactive approach, as described in this article, should enable any interruption to apalutamide treatment due to AEs to be avoided, thereby enabling treatment with apalutamide to continue. A similar protocol has been evaluated in a phase 2, open-label study of apalutamide + ADT enrolling 108 treatment-naïve patients with high-risk localised prostate cancer who underwent radical prostatectomy (ClinicalTrials.gov Identifier: NCT04523207) [9]. In this study, rash management included proactive patient education on appropriate skin care, rash monitoring and frequent follow-up with the aim of reducing dermatologic AEs [9]. In the event of a grade 1 or 2 rash, the protocol recommended continuing apalutamide at the same dose and considering treatment with topical steroids and oral antihistamines. Grade 3 rashes were managed by pausing apalutamide and were treated as described above for grade 1 or 2 rash with the addition of oral steroids. With this protocol, fewer patients experienced any-grade rash with apalutamide (21.3%) compared to those in the SPARTAN and TITAN trials (28.3% and 33.3%, respectively), as shown in Fig. 3 [9]. In patients who experienced a rash, it was more likely to be a lower grade skin event compared rash observed in the TITAN and SPARTAN studies (see Fig. 4) [9]. There was a high rate of resolution (95.7%) and a shorter median time to recovery at 45.5 days versus SPARTAN (60.0 days) and TITAN (142.0 days). Furthermore, this approach resulted in no patients discontinuing apalutamide treatment, whereas in TITAN and SPARTAN discontinuation rates were 9.5% and 6.3%, respectively [9]. Of the 23 patients presenting with a rash, 11 required treatment: ten with topical corticosteroids, five with oral antihistamine and three with systemic corticosteroids [9].

While further research is needed, this study provides initial evidence that a proactive and patient-empowered approach can result in a lower overall incidence of apalutamide-related skin rash, and for those who do experience rash, a reduced severity, shortened time to resolution and lower rates of dose reduction and treatment interruption or discontinuation [9]. It is anticipated that following the simple, practical guidance outlined here can similarly reduce the incidence and severity of rash, and thereby reduce the need to discontinue or lower the dose of apalutamide. Central to this approach is patient education and awareness. Encouraging patients to proactively care for their skin from the start of treatment and informing them of the risk of rash with apalutamide and what to look out for, including appropriate photos to illustrate the rash, is essential. If the patient observes any skin changes, they should be advised to report it straight away to their oncology team and not wait until the rash is severe. This, coupled with increased vigilance from the care team, is expected to lead to early identification of rash and subsequent intervention to allow for quicker resolution and enable patients to continue their cancer treatment with a drug that can delay disease progression and increase survival in patients with prostate cancer.
