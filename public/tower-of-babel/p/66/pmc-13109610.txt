Multi‐Factorial and Multi‐Component Fall Prevention Interventions Initiated From the Emergency Department: A Systemic Review and Meta‐Analysis
Authors: Lauren T Southerland, Fabrice I Mowbray, Ian A Tarnovsky, Alexander X Lo, Sangil Lee, Kristie Harper, Suzanne V Ryer, Charles L Maddow, Christopher R Carpenter, Aaron J Malsch, Luna Ragsdale, Shan W Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundFall risk screening and prevention interventions initiated from the Emergency Department (ED) are endorsed by current national guidelines. We aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of ED‐based multi‐factorial and multi‐component interventions to prevent falls.MethodsWe conducted a systematic review and meta‐analysis of interventions for fall prevention initiated in the ED for older patients (age ≥ 60 years). Multi‐component and multi‐factorial interventions were included. We excluded studies without a control or comparison group. The published literature was searched from 2019 to May 2024. Risk of bias was assessed with the Newcastle Ottawa tool for observation studies and the Cochrane Risk of Bias v2 for randomized trials. A meta‐analysis was completed for the outcomes with multiple studies.ResultsThe search resulted in 6312 abstracts with 2571 duplicates, for 3741 unique citations. A total of 18 studies were included in the systematic review; 5 were rated as high risk of bias/low quality. The articles were heterogenous in the intervention type (8 multi‐factorial and 8 multi‐component), setting (ED focused vs. outpatient), intervention components (i.e., nurses, physicians, therapists), and size (103–1435 participants). The interventions did not decrease risk of falls at 3 months (risk difference 0.05 95% CI [0.00; 0.09]), 6 months (0.07 [−0.04; 0.18]) or 12 months (−0.02 [−0.11; 0.07]). ED revisits at 1 month (−0.01 [−0.03; 0.00]), 3 months (−0.04 [−0.14; 0.06]), and 12 months (0.02 [−0.05; 0.25]) were also unchanged. Mortality and hospitalization rates were also unaffected. Improvement in functional status was noted in 4 of 5 studies reporting this outcome.ConclusionsMulti‐factorial and multi‐component fall prevention interventions initiated from the ED did not decrease falls or recurrent healthcare use. These interventions may improve functional status in older adults at fall risk. Comparisons are limited by the heterogeneity in types of interventions, intervention compliance, and timing of outcomes.Keywords: emergency department, fall prevention, meta‐analysis

Introduction

Older adults (adults aged 60 years and older) are at high risk for falls and fall‐related injuries [1]. Every 11 s, an older American is treated in the Emergency Department (ED) for a fall and 2.8 million per year are injured from their fall [2, 3]. The estimated direct medical cost of fall‐related injuries in the United States (U.S.) is $50 billion annually and increasing each year [4]. Despite the high morbidity and mortality associated with falls, multi‐factorial fall risk assessment and the initiation of fall prevention interventions are not routine practice within the ED setting [5, 6, 7]. This omission neglects unique opportunities to prevent future falls and injuries [8, 9].

Multidisciplinary national and international guidelines encourage a comprehensive approach to address the needs of patients presenting after a fall [10, 11, 12]. The 2014 Geriatric Emergency Department Guidelines recommend an ED workup that consists of “not only a thorough assessment for traumatic injuries, but also an assessment of the cause of the fall, and an estimation of future fall risk” [11]. The European Task Force guidelines also recommend a focus on maintaining mobility and avoiding falls as part of comprehensive geriatric assessment in the ED.

Some barriers to performing fall risk assessment in the ED are the time needed for additional screening and interventions, lack of training, and the around‐the‐clock nature of emergency care [5, 6]. For example, specialty multidisciplinary team members such as physical therapists (PT) and pharmacists are often only available during business hours [13]. Another barrier is the heterogeneity of fall prevention interventions and a general lack of implementation strategies to introduce the paradigm shift of prevention into ED operational flow [14, 15]. Given the time and staffing constraints in EDs, it is necessary to determine which possible interventions after a fall are most effective in preventing future falls and injury. In 2021, Harper and colleagues performed a systematic review which found that multi‐factorial interventions reduced the rate of falls and subsequent hospital admissions among ED patients [16].

Mindful that additional research in this area has been published, the objective of our systematic review and meta‐analysis was to synthesize and evaluate the current evidence surrounding fall risk prevention interventions from the ED. Our study was conducted to support the Geriatric ED Guidelines 2.0 project to update the 2014 Geriatric ED consensus guidelines using systematic reviews of evidence and transparent decision‐making and reporting [17]. We hypothesized that multi‐factorial and multi‐component fall prevention strategies geared towards older ED patients would decrease falls and fall‐related injuries.

Methods

Design

We conducted a systematic review and meta‐analysis of multi‐factorial or multi‐component fall interventions among patients attending the ED for a fall or who presented at high risk for falls. An international, interdisciplinary group of emergency medicine physicians, a nurse researcher, and a physical therapist from the U.S., Canada, and Australia met monthly to decide on the clinically pertinent patient question and methods. The proposed question was then reviewed by the Geriatric ED Guidelines 2.0 steering committee (Supporting Information Data A: Steering committee list).

The team chose to update an existing systematic review by Harper et al., which uses the sub‐categories of complex interventions (multi‐component and multi‐factorial) [16]. We included all the papers as found by the Harper et al. study, except one where the participant population also included non‐ED settings (Tan et al. [18]) Specifically, we classified multi‐component interventions as a set combination of interventions provided to each patient and multi‐factorial interventions as the matching of intervention components to distinct patient fall risk factors. In multi‐component interventions, every patient receives a standardized intervention bundle, whereas in multi‐factorial interventions, patients receive only the intervention components most relevant and beneficial to them (i.e., patient‐tailored). We defined ED‐based interventions as those in which an assessment or initiation of care occurred in the ED. Interventions which identified patients after the ED visit were excluded. Studies that recruited from non‐ED or short stay unit settings were also excluded. Methodology reporting adheres to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyzes (PRISMA) guidelines (Supporting Information Data B: PRISMA guidelines worksheet) [19]. We registered the study protocol with the PROSPERO systematic review (2022; CRD42022351462).

Research Question

In adults (> 60 years) who present to the ED, are ED‐initiated multi‐factorial and multi‐component interventions focused on fall prevention effective at improving health outcomes when compared to standard practice?

Outcomes

The primary outcome of interest was falls after discharge from the ED. Additional outcomes collected were pre‐determined by the study team to include: injurious falls, functional status, quality of life (QOL), unscheduled healthcare use (defined as ED visits and/or hospitalizations), new need for skilled care at discharge, and cost effectiveness.

Data Sources and Search Strategy

Our search strategy was developed with the support of a medical librarian and was limited to studies conducted in 2019 or later, as we were updating a prior systematic review. We included a combination of evidence‐informed search and MESH terms, including but not limited to (elderly OR aged OR older adults) AND (emergency department OR hospitalized OR discharge) AND (falls or fall risk assessment tools) AND (intervention OR program OR multi‐factorial OR physiotherapy OR occupational therapy OR telemedicine OR follow‐up) (Supporting Information Data C: Search String). We conducted our preliminary test search in PubMed and EMBASE. The results were evaluated, and the search terms were adjusted. The initial search was conducted in June 2022 and updated in May 2024, incorporating the following databases: Ovid MEDLINE, EMBASE, PubMed, CINAHL, Scopus, APA PsycInfo, PEDro, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials, and clinicaltrials.gov. We conducted citation tracking on all eligible studies and reviewed prior systematic reviews in this area to ensure that any qualifying studies were identified.

Study Selection and Screening

Our search was restricted to adults and non‐animal studies. We included conference proceedings and abstracts through to full text evaluation. If the study would fit criteria, we either found the corresponding full text article or contacted the authors to determine if a full manuscript had been published. We did not exclude studies based on the language of print or sample size.

We included interventional study designs (randomized and quasi‐randomized) that employed a multi‐component or multi‐factorial intervention geared towards fall prevention. We included interventions initiated in the ED setting, such as identifying intervention participants, conducting initial screening during the ED visit, or initiating the intervention during the ED visit. Studies also recruiting populations outside of the ED setting were excluded. We required studies to provide an explicit definition of their multi‐component/multi‐factorial interventions to be considered for inclusion. We excluded studies that lacked a comparison group (such as case series).

Abstract and full‐text reviewers were trained on a series of articles. Reviewers were emergency medicine physicians, a physical therapist (PT), a nurse researcher, and a medical student. Reviewers were not allowed to screen or vote on any citation in which they authored or supported the work. Inter‐rater reliability (IRR) was assessed using a Cohen's Kappa value. We screened titles, abstracts, and full texts using Covidence software [20] with at least two independent reviewers. Cases of disagreement were brought back to the full committee at monthly meetings for adjudication by the group. To maintain group objectivity, the initial reviewers of an article were not allowed to vote during the group adjudication.

Data Abstraction

Full text data extraction was performed independently and in duplicate by trained abstractors, with any discrepancies resolved by an independent third abstractor. We created a pre‐determined data abstraction sheet using REDCap, an online research database tool that allows for branching logic and multiple outcomes [21]. Data elements included: first author, year of publication, study design, intervention design (multi‐component and multi‐factorial), age, sex, intervention location, funding status, prior falls, provider of the intervention (e.g., physician, physiotherapist, nurse, etc.), sample sizes (overall, intervention, and control groups), confounders of interest (age, sex, prior falls), reported outcomes, timing of outcome reporting (in months), length of follow‐up, unit of change for continuous predictors, classification for categorical predictors, the unadjusted and adjusted point estimates of risk alongside measures of variance (e.g., confidence intervals, standard deviations, etc). All quantitative outcomes were collected at all timepoints provided by each study. Any disagreements between the two initial reviewers were resolved by a third independent reviewer.

Data Synthesis and Analysis

We synthesized data using R (The R Foundation, https://www.r‐project.org/) and the ‘meta’ package. Data were analyzed by a trained research methodologist (FM). A random‐effects model was used for all statistical pooling, mindful that models of care, patient populations, and outcomes were likely to vary between institutions and regions. Study results were pooled to ensure outcomes and timing were measured homogenously. For the primary analysis, we present the estimates for all studies, irrespective of the intervention design. Where the data allowed, we also conducted a subgroup analysis of intervention effectiveness between interventions that were multi‐component and multi‐factorial. We assessed statistical heterogeneity by visualizing forest plots, evaluating the I 2 statistics (≤ 40%), and conducting the chi‐square test for homogeneity (p ≥ 0.05). We were able to obtain all missing data on effect estimates by contacting the corresponding authors.

Pre‐planned subgroup analyzes included (1) multifactorial vs. multicomponent studies (which were done by the parent systematic review), (2) ED‐based interventions, and (3) studies including persons with cognitive impairment. Due to the variability in study outcomes, subgroup analysis #3 was not completed. We did perform a sensitivity analysis including only randomized controlled trials (RCTs).

Results

Search Results

A total of 6312 results were generated from our original search, with 2571 duplicates removed (Figure 1). In addition to the database search strategy, we identified 32 articles through citation tracking and an additional three articles published prior to 2019, which were not included in the prior review completed by Harper et al. (Supporting Information Data E). Of the original 11 articles included by Harper et al., one was excluded because it recruited from clinics, the community, and the ED [18]. Two others were excluded for being single‐component interventions (fall prevention education [30]; occupational therapy home visits [31]).

PRISMA flow diagram of articles in the systematic review of Emergency Department fall prevention interventions.

Inter‐Rater Reliability

For abstract screening, the agreement between the two initial reviewers was 95.7% and the Cohen's kappa was 0.26. The IRR was low due to the high number of rejected abstracts (n = 3574) causing a high probability of agreement by chance. For full text review, the initial agreement was 83.6% and the IRR by Cohen's kappa was 0.38. Excluded articles at full text review and the reason for exclusion are available in Supporting Information Data F.

Included Articles

Full text review resulted in 22 included articles. Four were abstracts only without any associated full manuscripts and so were excluded from the final meta‐analysis [32, 33, 34, 35]. The final study population included 16 distinct patient populations and 18 published manuscripts (Table 1), as two studies had more than one publication on the same dataset. There were 14 randomized controlled trials, 1 quasi‐randomized trial, and three cohort studies. Studies were performed in the United Kingdom (n = 4) [36, 37, 38, 39], Australia (n = 4) [40, 41, 42, 43], United States (n = 3) [44, 45, 46, 47], Singapore (n = 3) [48, 49, 50, 51], Finland (n = 1) [52] and the Netherlands (n = 1) [53]. All studies but one were financed with government funding [46]; Greenberg et al. and Shaw et al. received funding from foundations [39, 46].

Risk of Bias

Of the 12 randomized controlled trials, five studies were deemed to have low risk, four had some concerns, and two were judged to have high risk (Figure 2). Almost all had some risk of bias due to deviation from the intended interventions. For the observation and quasi‐experimental studies, one study was rated as good quality and the three others as poor quality (Figure 2).

Risk of bias was assessed for 12 randomized controlled trials using the Cochrane risk of bias v2 (A) and in 4 cohort studies using the Newcastle‐Ottawa risk of bias for nonrandomized studies (B). For Newcastle‐Ottawa evaluation, a study can receive one star maximum for each category except for comparability of cohorts which can receive up to two stars. Overall quality rankings (good, fair, poor) are pre‐determined based on total stars and quality in specific categories.

Types of Interventions

Most interventions screened patients in the ED and then initiated the intervention in the outpatient setting (n = 10). Five interventions were completed in the ED or an ED observation/short stay unit [37, 45, 46, 48, 52]. Interventions were diverse, with care teams led by physiotherapists (n = 8), occupational therapists (n = 6), researcher staff (n = 5), geriatric or emergency nurses (n = 4), and geriatrician or geriatric services (n = 3). Other team members included physicians, podiatrist, dieticians, and pharmacists. Half of the study interventions (n = 8) were classified as multi‐factorial [38, 39, 40, 42, 43, 46, 48, 51]. Fall risk prevention for the control group was also heterogeneous, with some including lower‐intensity interventions such as education on fall risk, referral to a general practitioner for a fall risk evaluation, or geriatrician nurse evaluation as part of their standard care/control group.

Study Population

Population sizes ranged from 110–712 patients, with a mean of 367 patients included per study. The average age of study samples was 78.3 years, and most studies recruited a greater proportion of females (64.9%). The majority of studies followed patients for 1 year post‐intervention (n = 9). Prior falls were reported for participants in 10 studies. Only five studies included participants with cognitive impairment (Table 1) [39, 42, 45, 46, 54].

Outcomes

Our primary pre‐planned outcome was falls at 90 days, but because of the wide variety of outcomes and timeframes in these studies, we included all reported outcomes up through 1 year in our analyzes. Table 1 displays the outcomes reported by study. Falls were the most frequently reported outcome (n = 12), followed by hospitalizations (n = 11), repeat ED visits (n = 11), mortality (n = 9), functional status (n = 5), and quality of life (n = 3). Reporting time frames also varied from 7 days to 12 months.

The primary outcome of falls after the ED visit was not decreased at 3, 6, or 12 months (Figure 3). Heterogeneity in this outcome was high, I 2 of 71.8% at 6 months and 90.2% at 12 months. Certainty in the evidence is Very Low at 3 months due to only having observational studies. Certainty in the evidence at 6 months is Low due to observational studies and the risk of bias in the two randomized trials. Certainty in the evidence at 12 months is Moderate due to inconsistency and risk of bias. A subgroup evaluation comparing multi‐factorial vs. multi‐component interventions found no difference (Supporting Information Data G) in the rate of falls. Injurious falls were reported in 4 studies and were not significantly decreased by the intervention (RD −0.09 [−0.22; 0.05], Figure 4) [36, 41, 42, 53]. Heterogeneity for injurious falls at 12 months was high (I 2 91%). Certainty in the evidence for a reduction in the injurious falls is Low due to inconsistency and risk of bias.

Among seven studies, hospitalization rates were unchanged by the interventions at 12 months (Supporting Information Data G). Heterogeneity in this outcome was high (I 2 93.5%), and several studies had high risk of bias or poor quality determinations; thus, certainty in the evidence is low. There was no difference in effectiveness for ED revisits at 12 months between multi‐factorial and multi‐component studies (Supporting Information Data G).

Mortality was reported in 9 studies; six reported mortality rates at 12 months. The pooled RD was 0.00 [−0.02; 0.01], indicating that these interventions had no effect on mortality risk at 12 months (Supporting Information Data G). Heterogeneity was moderate (I 2 47.7%) and certainty of evidence is moderate due to some observational studies and risk of bias, but overall a narrow effect estimate and moderate heterogeneity.

Patient‐reported outcomes included QOL and functional status. Three studies examined as secondary outcomes the effect of fall‐prevention interventions on QOL for community‐dwelling older adults presenting to the ED as a consequence of a fall [41, 52, 53]. Different measures were used to assess QOL, which did not allow for a meta‐analysis. However, no differences in QOL at 1 year were noted in any of the 3 studies. Certainty of evidence is Low. Five studies examined functional status as an outcome [36, 38, 48, 53, 54]. One reported outcomes at 3 months, two at 6 months, and three at 1 year. Meta‐analysis was not possible due to the multiple timeframes and the 4 different measurement scales used. Four of these studies [36, 38, 48, 54] reported a positive effect on functional status in the intervention group, either by improvement from baseline or a decrease in decline in functional status compared to control. One article found no effect on functional status [53]. Certainty of evidence in an improvement in functional status would be Moderate due to the one high‐quality RCT, but as we could not assess heterogeneity or effect size, the certainty was downgraded to Low.

Only 1 study analyzed cost‐effectiveness [49]. This study from Singapore evaluated the incremental cost‐effectiveness ratio over 9 months based on quality‐adjusted life years. Overall, the program was not cost‐effective; however, effectiveness was increased in the subgroup with fewer comorbidities (the healthier subgroup), who also derived the most benefit from the program. Certainty in the evidence is moderate with only one RCT which had a low risk of bias.

The subgroup of studies in which the intervention occurred fully in the ED or ED observation unit (n = 4) all included physical therapy evaluation during the ED visit [37, 45, 48, 52]. A meta‐analysis of this ED subgroup could not be performed due to differing outcomes and timeframes. However, all 3 studies which reported fall‐related outcomes (subsequent falls [37, 48] and fall‐related ED visits [45]) reported a statistically significant decrease in these outcomes. The fourth study found no effect on the primary outcome of hospital admissions [52].

A sensitivity analysis of only RCTs was done (Supporting Information Data G). This sensitivity analysis found a change in only two outcomes. The proportion of patients with falls at 6 months was reported by 2 studies and found a pooled RD of 0.08 [−0.26; 0.41] with high heterogeneity (I 2 91.5%). At 12 months, the 7 reporting RCTs demonstrated no significant change in proportion of patients with a fall (pooled RD −0.05 [−0.13; 0.02], heterogeneity high at I 2 72.8%).

Discussion

This systematic review of ED‐based interventions to prevent falls identified and analyzed 16 study populations. We found that ED‐based multi‐factorial/multi‐component interventions overall did not significantly reduce the incidence of falls or injurious falls, our primary outcome. Among secondary outcomes, the interventions which reported functional status mostly reported positive effects. Additionally, interventions initiated in the ED (rather than risk assessment in the ED and referral for outpatient care) showed a trend towards decreased injurious falls and ED revisits; however, this evidence is not robust due to differing outcomes and timeframes reported.

Differences in the type of and the penetration of the interventions may explain some of the outcome differences. The interventions were heterogeneous, with different staff participating and different rates of intervention adoption/adherence. For example, the two‐component study by Goldberg et al. had 98% completion of the pharmacist intervention and 84% completion of the PT intervention. This study was effective at reducing falls. In comparison, a home assessment and referral program by Russell et al. was performed after the ED visit and had limited penetration, as patient adherence ranged from 7% to 69%. In Greenberg et al., a multi‐factorial program with participant choice, adherence was best for completing an eye exam (100%) and worse for completing an exercise program (69%, n = 25/36 of those who chose that intervention, but only 27%, n = 25/93 of the total intervention group completed an exercise intervention). This demonstrates the importance of reporting adherence to these complex bundled interventions to differentiate whether an intervention is delivered with fidelity. Future investigators should report outcomes for subsets with complete adherence compared with partial adherence to bundled interventions. Exploring predictors of non‐adherence would also be helpful to stakeholders for future scale‐up of bundled interventions to optimize outcomes.

Another potential bias in these studies is the long lag times for outpatient interventions. In Russell et al., the median time to evaluation in a follow‐up fall risk clinic was 4 months from the index ED visit. In Hendricks et al., the initial study visit took 5–10 weeks, and results were sent to the general practitioner team on average 3.5 months after the ED visit. This long lag time until intervention initiation could bias the results towards the null, as the impact of ED referral and intervention is potentially diluted by other healthcare interventions over time. On the other hand, these studies report pragmatic outcomes of program applications in current health systems, which are more generalizable than those conducted with research infrastructure.

Other confounding factors were study crossover and the differing definitions of a control intervention. Several studies used standard care or a letter to the primary care team about the patient's fall risk, leading to control patients receiving the same interventions. In Russell et al., 30% of controls received a physical therapy evaluation. The intervention in Whitehead et al. was also a risk assessment and referral to outpatient programs, and 48% of the control group adopted at least one recommended fall prevention strategy. In Chong et al. 67% of the intervention group received standard care geriatric emergency nurse assessment, which is known to decrease hospitalizations and ED revisits [54]. In Greenberg et al. the control and intervention groups each completed a median of 1 fall prevention intervention (non‐significant difference between the groups) [46]. The differences in what constituted the control group and the level of additional fall prevention intervention they received must be considered, as these high levels of contamination/cross over are likely to reduce the observed effect size.

While our analysis did not identify whether one particular intervention effectively reduced injurious falls, these results should not be interpreted as a signal of therapeutic nihilism for ED falls prevention [15]. Significant opportunities exist to improve the transparency, reproducibility, and overall quality of ED‐initiated falls prevention. First, interventions performed in the ED setting may have higher compliance with intervention completion and should be prioritized pragmatically [24]. Second, future investigators should routinely report time to intervention and compliance to help differentiate the effects of delays from the incident ED visit and fidelity of patient engagement on observed outcomes. Third, future researchers should strive to assess pre‐intervention comparability of fall risk between the intervention and control cohorts using validated risk assessment instruments. Fourth, the ED falls prevention community would benefit from a standardized approach to evaluating outcomes of incident falls and fall injuries, QOL, functional status, and cost‐effectiveness in order to assure comparability across studies in the future [24]. Finally, efforts to contemplate components of implementation science and learning health systems science as ED fall prevention efficacy studies continue should accelerate subsequent scale‐up once an effective intervention is identified.

Potential limitations of our review include the characterization of study interventions and the risk of missing studies due to the search strings used. Our conclusions are also limited by the evidence available on this topic.

Conclusion

Multi‐component and multi‐factorial fall prevention programs from the ED did not significantly reduce falls and ED revisits. While these programs may be helpful to some patients if the interventions are completed, patient compliance, lag time to intervention initiation, and clinical crossover in the control groups limit the certainty of the existing evidence. Some ED‐based interventions may reduce functional decline and ED revisits, but the certainty of evidence is low.
