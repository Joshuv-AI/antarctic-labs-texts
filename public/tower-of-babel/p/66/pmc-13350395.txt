Transcatheter Management of Severe Aortic Stenosis, Acute Pulmonary Embolism, and Gastrointestinal Bleeding
Authors: Zhouming Wang, Taotao Xie, Xin Ding, Yanbin Su, Yajun Liu, Na Zhang, Zhenwen Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe concurrent presentation of severe aortic stenosis (AS), acute pulmonary embolism (PE), and gastrointestinal bleeding creates a clinical dilemma where treating one condition may exacerbate another.Case SummaryA 72-year-old woman presented with fatigue, palpitations, and melena. She was found to have severe AS, bilateral acute PE, and a bleeding duodenal ulcer. After initial stabilization with transfusion, we used a staged multidisciplinary approach: catheter-directed thrombectomy with adjunctive balloon angioplasty for residual stenosis, followed by transfemoral transcatheter aortic valve replacement.DiscussionThis case illustrates the challenges of coexisting AS, gastrointestinal bleeding, and PE. Although Heyde syndrome was considered a possible unifying mechanism, absent angiodysplasia and von Willebrand factor testing preclude definitive diagnosis. A staged, physiology-driven strategy prioritized immediate threats and enabled safe definitive therapy.Take-Home MessagesIn patients with competing thrombotic and bleeding pathologies, a staged, physiology-first approach prioritizing the most immediate threat enables safe sequential intervention. Percutaneous techniques minimize cumulative risk in high-risk patients.

Past Medical History

Her medical history was significant for a 10-year history of hypertension and a 1-year history of gradually declining exercise tolerance. She was not on any anticoagulant or antiplatelet therapy.

Physical Examination

On admission, she was hemodynamically stable with a blood pressure of 144/64 mm Hg and a heart rate of 101 beats/min. Respiratory rate was 18 breaths/min, temperature was 36.8 °C, and oxygen saturation was 94% on room air (improved to 98% on 5 L of oxygen) (Table 1). Notable findings included pale conjunctivae, a biphasic murmur heard best over the right second intercostal space, and bilateral lower extremity edema. Auscultation of the lungs revealed wet rales at the left base.

Investigations

Initial laboratory investigations revealed severe anemia (hemoglobin: 54 g/L, hematocrit: 17.4%), an elevated B-type natriuretic peptide (1,270 pg/mL), and a positive fecal occult blood test. Coagulation profile was notable for a D-dimer of 1.5 μg/mL. Arterial blood gas on 5 L of oxygen showed hypoxemia (Po2: 66.8 mm Hg).

An emergency esophagogastroduodenoscopy identified a bleeding duodenal bulb ulcer with stigmata of recent hemorrhage (Figure 1). Bedside transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated severe aortic stenosis (AS) (aortic valve area: 0.9 cm2, mean gradient: 40 mm Hg) with preserved left ventricular ejection fraction (57%) and elevated pulmonary artery systolic pressure (43 mm Hg).Figure 1Emergency Gastroscopy Revealing a Duodenal Bulb Ulcer With an Adherent Clot (Stellate Sign of Recent Hemorrhage)

PE Severity Assessment

The Simplified PESI (Pulmonary Embolism Severity Index) score was 0 (low risk) if using the most conservative parameters, but the patient's transient tachycardia, hypoxemia (Pao2: 66.8 mm Hg on 5 L), and elevated B-type natriuretic peptide (1,270 pg/mL) suggested at least intermediate-risk physiology. Echocardiography showed no right ventricular dilation or hypokinesis, but pulmonary artery systolic pressure was elevated (43 mm Hg). Given the contraindication to systemic thrombolysis (recent gastrointestinal bleeding [GIB]) and the patient's high bleeding risk, the heart team judged that catheter-directed therapy (thrombectomy) offered a favorable risk-benefit profile compared with anticoagulation alone or systemic thrombolysis. The decision was not based solely on clot burden but on the convergence of the following: 1) the need to clear the thrombus before transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) to minimize periprocedural risk; 2) contraindication to full-dose anticoagulation because of recent GIB; and 3) failure of low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH) trial to achieve rapid thrombus resolution (D-dimer remained elevated).

Acute PE

After initial resuscitation with 4 U of packed red blood cells, hemoglobin improved to 97 g/L. However, a repeat D-dimer had risen sharply to >5 μg/mL. Computed tomography pulmonary angiography was performed and revealed multiple filling defects, consistent with acute PE.

Differential Diagnosis

The differential diagnosis for this patient's complex presentation included the following:•Heyde syndrome: the triad of severe AS, acquired von Willebrand factor deficiency, and gastrointestinal angiodysplasia. Although our patient had a duodenal ulcer rather than classic angiodysplasia, the coexistence of AS and GIB raised this possibility. Definitive diagnosis would have required von Willebrand factor multimer analysis, which was not performed in the acute setting.•Malignancy: occult malignancy could present with both hypercoagulability (provoking PE) and bleeding (from tumor erosion). Age-appropriate cancer screening was pursued postdischarge and was negative.•Primary hypercoagulable state: conditions such as antiphospholipid syndrome or inherited thrombophilia could predispose to unprovoked PE; however, no prior thromboembolic events were reported.

Heart Team Discussion and Decision-Making

A multidisciplinary heart team comprising interventional cardiology, cardiothoracic surgery, gastroenterology, hematology, and anesthesiology convened to devise a management strategy. The central challenge was the mutually contradictory therapeutic imperatives: the PE required anticoagulation, the GIB demanded hemostasis, and the severe AS necessitated definitive valve intervention.

Final Decision and Rationale

The heart team unanimously selected the staged percutaneous approach. The decision was based on the following principles:1.Physiology-driven sequencing: active bleeding was the most immediate threat; stabilization was mandatory before any prothrombotic or anticoagulant therapy could be safely administered.2.Risk mitigation: catheter-directed therapy for PE avoided systemic thrombolysis and required only minimal anticoagulation, which could be deferred until GIB was controlled.3.Definitive valve therapy as the final step: TAVR provided a minimally invasive solution for AS without the need for cardiopulmonary bypass. Moreover, if Heyde syndrome was contributing to the bleeding, TAVR would correct the high-shear state and potentially resolve the acquired von Willebrand factor deficiency, reducing future bleeding risk

Antithrombotic Management in Stage 2 (PE Phase)

Key decisions

After GIB stabilization (hemoglobin: 97 g/L, no active bleeding), a 7-day trial of LMWH (nadroparin 1 mg/kg subcutaneously twice daily) was initiated on day 14. No rebleeding occurred. On day 24, catheter-directed mechanical thrombectomy was performed with intraprocedural unfractionated heparin (target activated clotting time: 250-300 seconds). After the procedure, LMWH was continued until TAVR.

Follow-Up

The patient's postoperative course was uneventful. At 1-week follow-up, she was asymptomatic, with a normally functioning bioprosthetic aortic valve on transthoracic echocardiography (mean gradient: 11 mm Hg, no paravalvular leak). Repeat transthoracic echocardiography also demonstrated normalized pulmonary artery systolic pressure (28 mm Hg). There was no recurrence of GIB. Follow-up at 1 month (by telephone interview) confirmed sustained symptom improvement and absence of rebleeding or thrombotic events. No routine imaging for PE recurrence was performed in the absence of symptoms.

Discussion

We report a rare and complex case of a patient presenting with the triad of severe AS, acute PE, and acute GIB. This clinical scenario presents a significant management paradox because therapies for thrombosis (anticoagulation) directly contradict those for bleeding. The successful outcome hinged on a staged, minimally invasive interventional strategy.

The co-occurrence of AS and GIB in this patient prompted consideration of Heyde syndrome, a triad of AS, acquired type 2A von Willebrand syndrome, and gastrointestinal angiodysplasia.1,2 The proposed mechanism involves high shear stress across the stenotic valve, causing proteolysis of high-molecular-weight von Willebrand factor multimers.3 However, several limitations preclude a definitive diagnosis in this case. First, the patient presented with a bleeding duodenal ulcer rather than angiodysplasia, the classic vascular lesion in Heyde syndrome. Second, von Willebrand factor multimer analysis was not performed in the acute setting. Third, no endoscopic evidence of angiodysplasia was documented. Although the resolution of bleeding after TAVR is consistent with Heyde physiology, it does not establish causality because ulcer healing may have occurred independently with proton pump inhibitor therapy and hemodynamic stabilization. Therefore, we can only speculate on a Heyde-like physiology rather than claim a clinically established diagnosis. Future cases would benefit from prospective von Willebrand factor testing and systematic endoscopic evaluation to clarify this relationship.4, 5, 6, 7

The addition of acute PE to this picture is exceptionally rare and adds another layer of complexity. We hypothesize that in the setting of possible Heyde-like physiology, the patient's relative immobility and perhaps a hypercoagulable state triggered by the acute bleed or underlying factors led to de novo thrombosis. The initially mildly elevated D-dimer, which rose dramatically after resuscitation, points to a dynamic thrombotic process. A systematic evaluation for deep vein thrombosis (lower extremity compression ultrasound) was performed and was negative. Malignancy screening (age-appropriate) was pursued postdischarge and was negative. No inherited thrombophilia testing was performed given the presence of provoking factors (immobilization and acute illness).

This case underscores the importance of a systematic, stepwise approach. Our strategy was guided by 2 principles. First, prioritize the immediate life threat. The GIB was stabilized first because active hemorrhage would have precluded any anticoagulation or intervention for the PE. Second, remove obstacles to definitive therapy. The acute PE significantly increased the procedural risk of TAVR by complicating periprocedural anticoagulation and hemodynamic management, a judgment reached by our multidisciplinary heart team based on the patient's individual physiology. By clearing the pulmonary thrombus percutaneously, we not only treated an emergent condition but also created a safe window for valve replacement, which ultimately addressed the root cause of the patient's bleeding diathesis.

Adjunctive Balloon Angioplasty for Residual Stenosis After Mechanical Thrombectomy

In this case, after mechanical aspiration thrombectomy, angiography revealed persistent, flow-limiting stenosis in segmental pulmonary artery branches. The residual lesions had smooth borders without intraluminal filling defects, suggesting vessel recoil or underlying chronic fibrotic changes rather than acute thrombus. Given the incomplete thrombus debulking and the patient's contraindication to systemic thrombolysis, we performed low-pressure, incremental balloon inflation (2.5- × 20-mm and 3.5- × 15-mm balloons) to improve distal flow. BPA is primarily established for chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension; its use in acute PE is unconventional and should be reserved for highly selected scenarios where residual mechanical obstruction persists after thrombectomy and alternative therapies are limited. In this patient, the decision was made jointly by interventional cardiology and pulmonary hypertension specialists, with careful attention to balloon sizing and inflation pressure to avoid reperfusion injury or vessel rupture. This approach is not standard and should not be generalized; it is reported here to document the full procedural details in a complex, resource-limited context.

Single-stage vs staged management: a comparative perspective

A single-stage surgical approach (eg, surgical aortic valve replacement with pulmonary embolectomy) was considered but ultimately rejected because of several factors: the need for cardiopulmonary bypass with full anticoagulation in a patient with recent GIB, the surgical trauma that might exacerbate coagulopathy, and the patient's overall risk profile. In contrast, the staged percutaneous strategy offered the following: 1) minimized bleeding risk: catheter-based interventions avoided systemic anticoagulation except during brief intraprocedural periods; 2) physiological titration: the response to each stage (hemostasis and then PE intervention) could be assessed before proceeding to the next; and 3) definitive correction: TAVR addressed the root cause of the bleeding diathesis (Heyde syndrome) while providing durable valve function.

Anticoagulation and antiplatelet strategy evolved in parallel with the staged interventions

In phase 1 (GIB stabilization), no anticoagulation was used. Phase 2 (PE intervention) used LMWH trial to assess for rebleeding, followed by intraprocedural heparin during thrombectomy/BPA, and brief postprocedural anticoagulation. Phase 3 (TAVR) included aspirin monotherapy and avoiding dual antiplatelet therapy or long-term anticoagulation given the absence of atrial fibrillation and the patient's bleeding history.

This individualized, dynamic approach allowed us to treat thrombosis while minimizing hemorrhagic risk.

Conclusions

The simultaneous presentation of severe AS, acute PE, and GIB is a rare and life-threatening condition. In this single case, a staged management strategy that first addresses active bleeding, then intervenes on acute thrombosis, and finally corrects the valvular pathology with TAVR was associated with a favorable outcome at 1-month follow-up. This case illustrates one potential approach to navigating conflicting clinical priorities, demonstrating the importance of a multidisciplinary team and the utility of percutaneous techniques. However, the optimal management strategy for such rare presentations remains uncertain, and decisions must be individualized based on local expertise, bleeding risk, thrombus burden, and hemodynamic status.
