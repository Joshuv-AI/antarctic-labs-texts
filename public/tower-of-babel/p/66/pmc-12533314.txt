Association of triglycerides with bone mineral density in children and adolescents: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Shang-An Si, Rui-Ning Lu, Gui-Ju Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTriglycerides (TGs) are the major lipids in the human body and have an impact on bone development. However, evidence on the relationship between TG and bone mineral density (BMD) in pediatric populations remains sparse. This study systematically addresses this epidemiologic knowledge gap through a rigorous analysis of nationally representative data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES).MethodThe present study examined a total of 3818 participants from NHANES spanning the years 2011 to 2018. The independent variable examined in this study was TG levels, while the dependent variable measured was BMD. The covariates were selected based on previous studies and clinical practice and included demographic information, laboratory testing, and pertinent physical examinations. The intrinsic association between TG and BMD was analyzed using weighted multiple linear regression and meticulous subgroup assessment. The smooth curve fitting visually demonstrates the linear correlation between them.ResultsThe study ultimately included a total of 3,818 participants, consisting of 2,056 men and 1,762 women. After adjusting for all covariates, the weighted multiple linear regression model revealed a significant positive association between TG and pelvic BMD (β = 0.047, 95% CI: 0.032–0.062, P < 0.001). TG levels also exhibited a positive correlation with trunk BMD (β = 0.015, 95% CI: 0.006–0.025, P = 0.002). A correlation between pelvic BMD was observed only in the third and fourth quartiles of TG levels among the groups. After adjusting for all variables, TG demonstrated a significant positive association with lumbar BMD at the Q3 level (0.869–1.276) (β = 0.006, 95% CI: -0.007-0.018, P < 0.05). The findings indicated a lack of a statistically significant association between TG level and head BMD (P > 0.05).ConclusionsThe impact of TG levels on BMD in the population of children and adolescents in the United States exhibits concentration-dependent variations and site-specific effects. It is recommended that children with low TG concentrations undergo regular testing for BMD. More research is required to further investigate the dual effects of TG on BMD and identify the optimal serum TG concentration for promoting bone development.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12891-025-08518-x.

Introduction

Bone mineral density (BMD) is an important indicator of bone strength, which can be used to predict the possibility of fracture. The principle is to analyze the mineral content and density of human bone by dual energy X-ray absorptiometry. Osteoporosis, a prevalent bone disease, is characterized by reduced BMD and has garnered significant attention due to its pathological features of diminished bone quality and increased susceptibility to fractures. BMD rapidly increases during childhood and adolescence, reaching its peak at approximately the age of 20 [1]. The prevalence of osteoporosis is not only greater among elderly and postmenopausal women but also significantly impacts the bone health of children [2]. Healthy bone status in childhood reduces the incidence of osteoporosis in adulthood [3]. The relevant survey data reveal a global increase in expenditure on osteoporosis-related diseases, with projected treatment costs for these conditions expected to more than double by 2050 based on current statistics [4, 5]. Bone growth and increases in BMD are mainly concentrated in childhood and adolescence, when bone itself has a strong capacity for remodeling and recovery. The optimal period for intervention and treatment of osteoporosis is prior to the age of 20 [6].

The presence of TG in the bloodstream is integral to lipid composition. TG in the appropriate range of levels promotes essential energy supply, hormone synthesis, immune system enhancement, and internal organ protection. Elevated TG levels are a prominent predisposing factor for metabolic disorders as well as cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases [7]. The current discourse surrounding the relevance of TGs to bone health is characterized by numerous discussions and controversies. TG levels and whole-body BMD were found to be negatively associated with both male and female sex in a cross-sectional study of older adults in the United States [8]. Yang XL et al. demonstrated that an increase in serum low density lipoprotein (LDL) and TGs may lead to a decrease in BMD [9]. Long-term osteoporosis can also lead to the accumulation of TG in the bone microenvironment. Meanwhile, during bone mineralization, stearoyl-coa saturase and sphingomyelin organization increase, which further adversely affects the activity of osteocytes [10]. However, contrasting findings have been reported. In a study conducted on young women, it was observed that TG was beneficial for improving the BMD of the spine and femur [11]. This finding was also observed in individuals aged 50 years and above, but it was only present at low levels of TG, and a significant positive association was found between lumbar BMD and TG alone [12].

However, to date, there are no studies on the relationship between tg levels and bone health in children and adolescents. At the same time, when examining the relationship between TG levels and BMD in different skeletal anatomical regions, previous studies have demonstrated different results. Understanding the effect of TG status on BMD can provide important information for the prevention and treatment of osteoporosis in the future. The present study aimed to conduct a cross-sectional investigation utilizing data from the NHANES spanning from 2011 to 2018 to provide relevant epidemiological data.

Methods

Study population

The data utilized in this study were obtained from the NHANES database, which is a nationally representative cross-sectional survey. The NHANES collects information on the lifestyle habits and physical characteristics of the US population through questionnaires, laboratory tests, and related examinations. The study initially involved a total of 39,156 participants over the period spanning from 2011 to 2018. The lack of TG data for subjects younger than 12 years of age in the database resulted in the selection of individuals between 12 and 19 years of age at age screening, and 8,621 children and adolescents were obtained. The study included a total of 3,818 subjects after excluding those with missing TG and BMD data. Figure 1 depicts the screening process for participants.

Measurement of TG

Refrigerated serum was collected following an 8–12 h fast. Subsequently, lipoprotein lipase completely hydrolyzed serum TGs into glycerol, which was then oxidized to generate hydrogen peroxide in the presence of peroxidase through the Trinder end-point reaction. Finally, photometry was employed to measure the results, which are reported in integer units of mg/dL. Low results were reported as < 9 mg/dL. More detailed data standards are available at https://wwwn.cdc.gov/Nchs/Nhanes/2015-2016/BIOPRO_J.htm.

The source of the bone mineral density

The BMD data included the head BMD, lumbar BMD, pelvic BMD and trunk BMD. The unit of measurement was gm/cm2. BMD was measured by dual energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA). Trained and certified radiological technicians administer the DXA examinations. The DXA protocol is described in the Body Composition procedure manual on the NHANES website. To obtain detailed address in https://wwwn.cdc.gov/Nchs/Data/Nhanes/Public/2013/DataFiles/DXX_H.htm.

Other covariates

Potential covariates associated with BMD in children and adolescents were identified for this study based on previous research findings and clinical experience [13, 14]. The continuous variables included the ratio of family income to poverty (PIR), albumin levels (g/dL), total calcium levels (mmol/L), creatinine levels (µmol/L), cholesterol levels (mmol/L), phosphorus levels (mmol/L), high density lipoprotein (HDL, mmol/L), LDL (mmol/L), body mass index (BMI, kg/m2), uric acid levels (µmol/L) and age in years. The classification variables included sex and race.

Statistical analysis

After considering the NHANES analysis sample weight as per the guidelines, it is crucial to incorporate weights in the data analysis. The utilization of weights allows for a more nuanced examination of variables that may have subtle influences, thereby ensuring that the results accurately reflect the characteristics of the U.S. population [15, 16]. The participants were categorized based on their TG quartile level. Weighted t tests were employed to analyze continuous variables, and the results are presented as the means and standard deviations of these variables. The chi-square test was used for categorical variables, and the results are presented as the number and percentage of subjects [17]. Subsequently, we employed weighted multiple linear regression to examine the association between TG levels and BMD in three models. Model 1 did not account for any covariates, while Model 2 adjusted for age, sex, and race only. In contrast, Model 3 incorporated adjustments for all relevant covariates. Finally, the significance results were stratified to examine the influence of different subgroups on the outcomes. Additionally, smooth curve fitting was used to compare TG levels and BMD. A P value less than 0.05 indicated statistical significance. All the aforementioned statistical analyses were conducted using R software and EmpowerStats.

Discussion

The results showed that TG levels in children were positively correlated with pelvic BMD. TG and lumbar bone density are correlated within a specific range of TG levels. However, the relationships between TG and other aspects of BMD are not clear. Subgroup stratification analysis revealed a consistent association between TG levels and pelvic BMD.

The presence of lipids is crucial for bone development, and lipids serve as vital constituents of mineralized bone tissue [18]. The most prevalent lipid in the human body is TG, and it also dominates the lipid composition of human bone tissue, accounting for 70-90% of the total lipid content [19]. Osteoblasts contain stored lipids, although these lipids do not provide the energy needed for osteoblasts [20]. The enhanced uptake of chylomicrons by osteoblasts compared to bone marrow cells also implies that the lipids obtained by osteoblasts are not solely derived from adipocytes within the bone [21]. There was a significant correlation between osteoblasts and lipids. Relevant research has demonstrated that the differentiation and functionality of osteoblasts are intricately linked to signaling interactions, such as those involving Notch, with the activity of the Notch protein being regulated through lipid control [22, 23]. The involvement of WNT/β-catenin signaling pathway in the interaction between osteoblasts and lipids has been observed in other studies [24]. The key WNT-β signaling pathways are primarily involved in embryonic development and regulation of bone, synovial membrane, and cartilage [25].

TG has garnered significant attention from individuals with a high level of numerical concern. Although there is currently a paucity of research on the impact of TG on bone health in the pediatric population, several studies have demonstrated a positive correlation between TG levels and BMD [26]. A growing body of evidence suggests a strong positive correlation between TG and lumbar and partial tuberosity BMD, although the study population was limited to women [27, 28]. After the disruption of CPT2 expression in osteoblasts, sex-specific differences in the bone status of the mice were observed during the experiments. Male mice exhibited a transient decrease in trabecular volume only at week 6, while female mice showed trabecular bone volume defects in both femurs and vertebrae at both 6 and 12 weeks on a normal diet [29]. A study conducted by van Gastel et al. revealed that fatty acids significantly promote the process of fracture healing [30]. Our findings demonstrated a nonlinear relationship between TG and pelvic BMD in female adolescents, as depicted in Fig. 3. This observation may be attributed to the potential impact of estrogen and elevated testosterone levels on bone density reduction and compromised skeletal health among women [31]. TG had a similar positive association with BMD in males and adolescents, and the results became nonsignificant when body fat or BMI was taken into account [32]. However, other studies have reported a positive association between TG and BMD, which persists even after correction for body fat [33]. It has been argued that obesity, weight, and BMI are positively correlated with BMD [34], and TG is also positively correlated with obesity, so it is natural to find a positive correlation between TG and BMD. However, this positive association persisted in our analysis after adjustment for BMI. There was a significant inverse association between serum triglycerides and BMD in the elderly population [35]. In the middle-aged and elderly population, triglyceride level was found to be positively correlated with lumbar BMD when triglyceride level was lower than 2.597 (mmol/L). Above this level, however, a negative association is shown [36]. This relationship is thought to be related to vitamin D status in older adults. An interesting finding in African American (AA) patients with type 2 diabetes is that this population is more likely to be vitamin D deficient but has higher BMD levels while having TG levels within the normal range [37]. This suggests a new hypothesis that vitamin D level expressed as TG (VitD /TG) does not indicate vitamin D deficiency in AA population. Whether this quantitative relationship is valuable in studying the relationship between TG and bone mineral density needs further discussion.

However, the negative effects of TGs on bone are worth considering. Other studies have demonstrated that the free fatty acids liberated from TGs can exert a direct impact on bone [38]. The presence of adipocytes hinders the proliferation and development of osteoblasts [39]. The presence of octanoic acid in TGs simultaneously contributes to the deterioration of the trabecular bone microstructure and accelerates the degradation of bone quality [40]. A significant inverse correlation was observed between the triglyceride-glucose index and BMD, particularly at the lumbar spine, in a cross-sectional study of adults [41]. Previous studies have indicated that TG is a significant risk factor for the development of the calcaneal bone and mineral density in both pediatric and geriatric populations [42, 43]. In the elderly population, high TG levels are clearly a danger signal of systemic osteoporosis [8]. Although we found that TG levels were beneficial for some bones, it is crucial not to overlook the significance of TG as a risk factor for BMD. Furthermore, even after adjusting for the influences of LDL and HDL, TG remains a major risk factor for cardiovascular disease [44]. The findings suggest that TG exhibits distinct dose-dependent effects on bone and the cardiovascular system, with a potential beneficial impact on bone within a certain range of TG levels. However, elevated TG levels may contribute to an increased risk of cardiovascular disease, thereby counteracting these positive effects. Consequently, it is crucial to determine the optimal concentration of serum TG during the period of bone development and identify the threshold at which TG influences BMD.

Strengths and limitations of the study

The study involved a total of 3,682 participants. This extensive dataset provides robust evidence to substantiate the association between TG levels and BMD. Moreover, acknowledging this correlation can facilitate a more comprehensive evaluation of skeletal health status in this population. Moreover, a comprehensive selection of bone sites was incorporated into this study to facilitate a more intricate analysis of the effects of TG on various skeletal locations. However, there are still certain limitations. First, due to the lack of prior research, some of our references are relatively outdated. Second, it is imperative to further investigate the dual impact of TG on BMD during adolescence, considering this unique period of growth and development. Finally, as our findings were specific to children and adolescents in the United States, their applicability to other populations may be limited. Therefore, the next step involves expanding the study population and conducting prospective studies.

Conclusion

This study revealed a significant association between TG levels and BMD in a pediatric population. TG levels demonstrated a significant positive correlation with pelvic BMD. Additionally, a positive correlation between TG levels and lumbar BMD was found in those with moderate TG levels. These findings underscore the importance of considering the potential impact of low TG levels on BMD when monitoring skeletal health in children.
