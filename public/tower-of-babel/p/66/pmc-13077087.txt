Effect of temperature on 2D terahertz plasmons in AlGaN/GaN heterostructures
Authors: M. Dub, P. Sai, D. Yavorskiy, Y. Ivonyak, A. Seweryn, M. Godlewski, P. Prystawko, R. Kucharski, G. Cywiński, W. Knap, S. Rumyantsev
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The effect of temperature on delocalized and localized plasmonic modes in large-area AlGaN/GaN plasmonic crystals formed by grating-gates and by ungated arrays of disks with two-dimensional electron gas was studied experimentally. The temperature dependences of the plasmon frequency was characterized by hysteresis and high dispersion of the experimental data across different plasmonic crystals, which was attributed to the uncertainty of the surface states on the open surface of AlGaN and the corresponding uncertainty of the electron concentration in the ungated parts. The redshift of the plasmon frequency with temperature increase was explained by the combined effect of temperature dependence of the electron concentration in the ungated parts and the electron effective mass. The temperature dependence of the electron effective mass in GaN was analyzed based on the results of the cyclotron resonance measurements using the simplified approach and taking losses into account. It was demonstrated that indeed, the electron effective mass increases (1.5–2) times at temperatures from T = 70 K to T = 290 K. The strong temperature dependence of such a basic parameter should be taken into account when designing GaN-based electronic, optoelectronic, and terahertz plasmonic devices.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-41524-0.

Introduction

Collective oscillations of the charge carriers in semiconductors (plasmons) have been of great interest for over 50 years. Two-dimensional (2D) plasmons excited in semiconductor quantum wells (QWs) or 2D materials, like graphene, are of special interest because in the case of high 2D charge carrier density (∼ 1013 cm−2) and for the devices with lateral dimensions in the micrometer range, their frequency lies in the terahertz (THz) frequency band.

One of the ways to study 2D plasmons is to measure the optical properties of semiconductor structures of large area with a periodic charge density profile. These kinds of structures, known as plasmonic crystals (PCs) can be formed, for example, by integration of metal gratings with inversion layers in semiconductors, heterostructures with QWs, and 2D materials. Theoretical and experimental studies of these grating-gate structures started in the 1980s using Si inversion layers and currently continue using AlGaAs, AlGaN, and graphene-based devices (see ref.1 and refs. therein). It was shown experimentally and by numerical simulations that periodic structures behave as PCs and their properties depend on the carriers’ concentration profile and PC period1–3. Although all details of PC behavior can be obtained only by rigorous electrodynamic simulations, the basic properties of plasmons can be found from an analytical model that describes well the experiments3,4. The wide spectrum of PC properties and ease of fabrication make PCs promising for devices like detectors, tunable filters, amplifiers, and generators operating at THz frequencies. It was already demonstrated experimentally that PCs can be used as different kinds of passive devices, like detectors and filters5–11.

Depending on the gate voltage, two types of plasmon modes in PCs can be distinguished. When the gate voltage is such that the 2D concentrations in gated and ungated parts of a PC are comparable, the delocalized mode can be observed1. In this case, the plasmon properties are determined by the interacting gated and ungated parts of the PC. When the concentration under the gate tends to zero, ungated parts play a major role, and a so-called localized mode of the PC is formed1. The localized mode (also called unscreened or ungated mode) has the advantage of higher frequency for the same characteristic dimensions. The frequency of both the delocalized and localized modes can be adjusted by the gate voltage but the physical phenomena responsible for the tuning are different. As it was shown in refs.1,12, the frequency of delocalized modes is tuned by carrier density under the gated regions of the PC, whereas the localized mode tuning is related to the effect of shrinking length of the ungated parts (side gate effect) or by additional back gate.

It was demonstrated already that the plasmon resonant frequency of the delocalized mode depends on temperature, and this dependence was explained by the strong temperature dependence of the electron effective mass13.

Here, we studied the temperature dependence of both delocalized and localized plasmon modes and performed the cyclotron resonance (CR) measurements, which indeed demonstrated the strong temperature dependence of the effective mass. However, we found that in addition to the temperature dependence of the electron effective mass, the temperature dependence of the electron concentration in the ungated parts of the PC contributes to the effect of the plasmon frequency temperature dependence.

The transmittance spectra of the plasmonic crystal were studied using a Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) vacuum spectrometer (Vertex 80v, Bruker, Billerica, MA, USA) equipped with a continuous-flow liquid helium or nitrogen cryostat (Optistat CF-V, Oxford Instruments, Abingdon, UK) and a mercury lamp as a source. The temperature controller (MERCURY-ITC-1, Oxford Instruments, Abingdon, UK) was synchronized with an electrical heater and a temperature sensor placed near the sample on the cold finger inside the cryostat. During temperature-dependent measurements, an additional delay of 5 min was applied before the start of the measurement to ensure thermal equilibrium and minimize temperature fluctuations. At each temperature point, the measurement was performed twice to verify temperature stabilization by ensuring the absence of changes in the spectral characteristics. Fabry-Pérot oscillations from the sample substrate were eliminated using Fourier transformation with a step size of 4 cm−1 (0.12 THz).

The FTIR spectrometer was equipped with a cryogenically cooled silicon bolometer (General Purpose 4.2 K Bolometer System, IRLabs, Tucson, AZ, USA), a solid-state silicon beam splitter, and an external water-cooled high-power mercury lamp as the radiation source.. To prevent oversaturation of the Si-bolometer, a 2 mm aperture was positioned directly after the sample, restricting electromagnetic radiation transmission to the size of the plasmonic crystal active region. The spectrum of the mercury lamp measured with this open aperture was used as a reference. Electrical control of the plasmonic crystal and DC measurements within the FTIR spectrometer were performed using a Keysight B2902A Precision Source/Measure Unit. Fig. S1 in the supplementary materials shows the schematic of the FTIR experimental setup.

Cyclotron resonance experiments were performed using an untreated AlGaN/GaN wafer mounted in a variable-temperature insert positioned at the center of a superconducting magnet (Cryogenic Ltd., UK), with the 2DEG oriented perpendicular to the applied magnetic field. The sample temperature was controlled in the range from 10 to 290 K using a programmable PID temperature controller (Lake Shore Model 336, USA). Monochromatic THz radiation at 0.66 THz was generated using an electronic source (Virginia Diodes Inc., USA). The THz radiation was guided to the sample via a cylindrical stainless-steel waveguide with an internal diameter of 8 mm, whose termination was positioned approximately 5 mm from the sample surface. The transmitted radiation was collected by a waveguide located outside the cryostat, and its intensity was detected using a pyroelectric detector (Edinburgh Instruments Ltd., UK) in combination with a lock-in amplification technique (Signal Recovery, Model 7270, USA).

Results and discussions

The effect of temperature on the resonant frequency of 2D delocalized plasmons in AlGaN/GaN QWs was found in a few earlier publications. First, it can be seen in the experimental data of ref.16, where it was not discussed yet. A more detailed study of the plasmon temperature dependence for the first harmonic of the delocalized mode in AlGaN/GaN grating-gate structures is presented in ref.13.

Figure 3 and Fig. S2 (in the supplementary materials) show the transmittance spectra for S7, S2, and S3 PCs at different temperatures and at VG = 0 (Fig. S3 in the supplementary materials shows the spectra for S7 PC in absolute values). First and second harmonics of the delocalized mode are clearly seen in Fig. 3, which redshift with the temperature increase. The evolution of the plasmon resonances with the gate voltage for several PCs is shown in Fig. S4 of the supplementary materials. Up to five harmonics of delocalized mode can be seen for some structures. As expected and discussed in several publications, the resonant frequency of delocalized plasmons redshifts with the negative gate voltage increase due to the decrease of electron concentration under the gate (see ref.1 and references therein). For the single cavity approach, the frequency of 2D screened (gated) and unscreened (ungated)plasmons can be easily found analytically17,18. However, PCs represent periodic structures with gated and ungated interacting cavities with different plasmon velocities. Therefore single cavity approach cannot be used for the PCs analysis1.

It was shown in ref.4 that the analytical model of the one-dimensional plasmonic crystal, proposed in refs.2,3, describes well the PCs’ basic behavior. In this model, the angular plasmon resonant frequencies, ωp, (delocalized mode) can be found as the solutions of the equation:2\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$cos\frac{{\omega }_{p}{L}_{G}}{{s}_{G}}cos\frac{{\omega }_{p}{L}_{UG}}{{s}_{UG}}-\frac{{s}_{G}^{2}+{s}_{UG}^{2}}{2{s}_{G}{s}_{UG}}sin\frac{{\omega }_{p}{L}_{G}}{{s}_{G}}sin\frac{{\omega }_{p}{L}_{UG}}{{s}_{UG}}=1,$$\end{document}where LG and LUG are the lengths of the gated and ungated cavities, respectively. The gated, sG, and ungated, sUG, plasmon velocity can be found as17,18:3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{s}_{G}=\sqrt{\frac{4\pi {e}^{2}{n}_{sG}d}{{m}^{*}\varepsilon }}}; {s}_{UG}=\sqrt{\frac{4{e}^{2}{n}_{sUG}{L}_{UG}}{\left(\varepsilon +1\right){m}^{*}}},$$\end{document}where ε is the dielectric permittivity, d is the barrier thickness, and nsG, nsUG are the 2DEG concentrations in the gated and ungated parts of a PC, respectively. These velocities determine the plasmon frequency and its dependence on electron concentration, effective mass, and dielectric permittivity, which all might be temperature-dependent. Dash-dotted line in Fig. 3 shows the frequency of the delocalized plasmon fDL = ωp/2π calculated using Eq. (3) for m* = 0.23 me (me is the free electron mass). Next, we have to find which parameters in Eq. (3) are temperature-dependent.

As seen in Fig. 2, the threshold voltage of these grating-gate structures does not depend on temperature. Therefore, we can conclude that concentration, nsG, does not depend on temperature as well. Therefore, the temperature dependence of concentration nsG cannot explain the decrease of the plasmon frequency with the temperature increase.

It is known that dielectric permittivity, ε is only weakly temperature dependent in semiconductors. For example, for relatively wide-band gap semiconductors, GaAs and GaP dielectric permittivity increases only ~ 3–4% when temperature is changed from 4 to 300 K19. We are not aware of the publications where the temperature dependence of GaN low-frequency dielectric permittivity was studied. However, the temperature dependence of GaN optical dielectric permittivity measured at high temperatures is also weak20. The same as in other semiconductors, the change in the GaN dielectric permittivity does not exceed a few percent over a 300 K temperature range. Having in mind that the plasmon frequency is proportional to the square root of ε, we can conclude that the temperature dependence of dielectric permittivity gives a negligible correction to the plasmon frequency.

One more mechanism of the plasmon properties temperature dependence is the effect of the electron concentration in the ungated parts of the PC. The plasmon resonant frequency in PC depends on the plasmon velocities in two parts of the structure with two different electron concentrations. We already discussed that the concentration under the gate can be easily estimated from the transistor threshold voltage and showed that it does not depend on temperature. However, this might not be the case for the ungated region, which is exposed to the environment. Due to the effect of temperature on the surface states, the electron concentration in the ungated parts might depend on temperature.

In order to check it, we measured the plasmon transmittance spectra for the localized mode when the concentration under the gate is virtually zero (large negative gate voltage). In this mode, the plasmon frequency is determined only by the concentration in the ungated part of the PCs. The temperature dependences of the transmittance spectra in the 2D1 and 2D2 PCs were also measured. Since these are two-dimensional PCs, Eq. (2) cannot be used to calculate the plasmon frequency precisely in this case. However, we assume that the general trend of the resonant plasmon frequency dependence on the electron concentration and effective mass is still the same as that given by Eq. (2) for one-dimensional PCs.

To further confirm the importance of the surface states, we measured transmittance spectra under ultraviolet (UV) illumination and in the PCs where the surface was modified by the HfO2 deposition. We used unfocused light from the light-emitting diode (LED) with λ = 270 nm peak wavelength (ProLight Opto PB2D-1CLA-TC). The quanta energy for this wavelength is above the Al0.25Ga0.75N barrier layer bandgap at 70 K. Therefore, we can expect effective generation of electron–hole pairs in AlGaN and an increase of the electron concentration in the QW. The estimate for the UV optical power using the nominal power of LED and the ratio of the PC area to the area of the actual light spot yields a small value of the incoming radiation power of P ≈ 50 μW.

Figure 6 shows the temperature dependences of the resonant plasmon frequency for 2D2 PC in the dark, under UV illumination, and for the part of PC covered with 50 nm thick HfO2 (Fig. S7 in the supplementary materials shows the corresponding spectra at different temperatures). The effect of light on the plasmon frequency is due to the two main processes: direct generation of electrons in GaN and change of the surface states under UV illumination. As seen in Fig. 6, UV light not only increases the plasmon frequency but also reduces the frequency jump at T ≈ 220 K. HfO2 deposited on the top of the structure shifts the frequency jump to a lower temperature, and also reduces its amplitude. Overall, the change of the frequency in the PCs covered with HfO2 is due to the high dielectric permittivity of HfO2 and due to the protection of the surface from the ambient and the corresponding change of the surface charge. The last effect should be the dominant because the HfO2 thickness is orders of magnitude smaller than the terahertz frequency wavelength (50 nm of HfO2 versus ~ 300 μm wavelength at 1 THz). These results further confirm the importance of the surface states in defining the frequency of localized plasmons.Fig. 6Temperature dependences of the resonant plasmon frequency for 2D2 PC in the dark, under UV illumination, and for the part of the PC covered with HfO2 (heating cycle).

The temperature dependence of 2DEG concentration and mobility can be easily obtained from the Hall measurements. Figure 7 shows the results of Hall measurements on the ungated Hall bar fabricated on the same SL 3665 wafer as S7 PC. One can see an expected decrease of the mobility with the temperature increase and a minor decrease of the electron concentration, which should contribute to the plasmon frequency temperature dependence. These result agrees with other Hall measurements of the AlGaN/GaN heterostructures, indicating a weak temperature dependence of electron concentration21–24. However, the increase of the 2DEG concentration at T ≈ 200 K, which may lead to the sudden increase of the plasmon frequency is not seen in Fig. 7. We assume that the effect of the frequency jump at T ≈ 200 K can be explained by the defects located close to the gate edges where the electric field is high when plasmon oscillations are excited (see Figs, 12, 13 in ref.1). The change of the electron states in these regions under the combined effects of temperature and electric field may lead not only to the local electron concentration change but also to the change of the effective resonance cavity length.Fig. 7Temperature dependence of Hall mobility and 2DEG concentration for SL 3665 wafer (SweGaN).

Anyway, we can conclude that the localized plasmon frequency is strongly affected by the temperature dependence of the surface states. In general, this effect can affect the frequency of delocalized plasmons as well, because their frequency is determined by the plasmon velocities in both parts of the structure, i.e., gated and ungated.

As seen in Fig. 8, the experimental data for the temperature dependence of the plasmon resonant frequency are characterized by high dispersion. The same conclusion can be made by analyzing the temperature dependence of the higher plasmon harmonics (see the supplementary materials for more details). Ungated parts of the PCs are open to the environment and are subject to different humidity, illumination, and temperature, providing high dispersion of the experimental data between different samples.

The most straightforward way to find the effective mass in semiconductors is the cyclotron resonance (CR) measurements. This method was used several times at low temperatures25–27 but we are not aware of CR measurements in GaN as a function of temperature up to T = 300 K. As seen, the absorption lines shift to higher magnetic fields and become wider with the temperature increase. Shift of the absorption lines to higher magnetic fields is an indication of the effective mass increase. Widening of the spectral lines is a result of the mobility decrease with the temperature increase, i.e. increasing losses. (Interestingly, CR measurements in AlGaAs/GaAs QWs in ref.28 also showed the substantial increase in the effective mass with the temperature increase.)

With the temperature increase from T = 70 K to T = 300 K, the cyclotron effective mass increases by a factor of 1.5–2. Similar results were obtained by optical Hall measurements24,29–31, from temperature dependences of plasmon frequency in refs.13, and by THz spectroscopy of Drude conductivity32. As seen in Fig. 8, our measurements of the temperature dependence of the plasmon frequency yield high dispersion of data for different samples, which is explained by the different surface states in different samples. This is due to the variation of the technology and atmospheric environment during the sample storage.

The temperature dependence of the effective mass in GaN and AlGaN/GaN heterostructures was discussed in a few recent papers24,29–32. In the majority of the experiments, the increase of the effective mass with temperature was observed. In principle, electron wavefunction hybridization due to the penetration to the barrier layer, conduction band non-parabolicity, and polaronic effects may contribute to the effective mass temperature dependence. However, all these effects cannot explain the strong effective mass increase at high temperature (see discussion and references in ref.31). Although the physical mechanism of the strong temperature dependence of the effective mass is still unknown, along with the temperature dependence of the surface state in the ungated parts of the PCs, it contributes to the temperature dependence of the plasmon resonant frequency.

Conclusions

The temperature dependence of both delocalized and localized modes of plasmons in large-area AlGaN/GaN PCs was studied experimentally. Two types of PCs were fabricated and studied: grating-gate FETs with different grating geometries and 2D arrays of disks with 2DEG. It was found that with an increase in temperature, the resonant frequency of both localized and delocalized plasmon modes decreases. However, the temperature dependence of the plasmon frequency was characterized by hysteresis and high dispersion of the experimental data for different PCs. The resonant plasmon frequency depends on the PC’s geometry, electron concentration in the gated and ungated parts of the PC, dielectric permittivity, and electron effective mass. Since we found that the threshold voltage of grating-gate FETs does not depend on temperature, we concluded that the electron concentration in the gated parts does not depend on temperature. Dielectric permittivity is known to be almost temperature independent in semiconductors. Therefore, we concluded that high dispersion of the experimental data and hysteresis of the temperature dependences are due to the uncertainty of the surface states on the open surface of AlGaN and the corresponding uncertainty of the electron concentration in ungated parts. This conclusion was confirmed by the measurements of the temperature dependence of the localized plasmon frequency of 2D disc arrays and grating-gate FETs at high negative gate voltage when electron concentration under the gates is virtually zero. The electron effective mass temperature dependence can also contribute to the plasmon frequency temperature dependence. However, the high dispersion of the plasmon frequency experimental data makes it impossible to estimate the electron effective mass from plasmon resonant frequency measurements, as was proposed earlier.

To find the temperature dependence of the effective mass, we performed the cyclotron resonance measurements, which are not sensitive to surface charge. The effective mass was evaluated using the simplified approach and taking losses into account. Although the broadening of the cyclotron resonance lines at high temperature makes the accuracy of the effective mass estimate low, the (1.5–2) times increase in the effective mass with the temperature increase from 70 to 290 K can still be justified. The electron effective mass is an important characteristic of semiconductor materials, which determines virtually all characteristics of electronic and optoelectronic devices. Its strong temperature dependence should be taken into account when designing GaN-based devices like field effect transistors, light sources, plasmonic detectors, and emitters.
