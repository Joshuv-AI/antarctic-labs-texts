Illuminating the black box of reservoir computing
Authors: Claus Metzner, Thomas Kinfe, Andreas Maier, Achim Schilling, Patrick Krauss
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractReservoir computers, using recurrent neural networks with fixed random connections, are known to perform a wide range of information-processing tasks. Yet the transformations taking place within the reservoir, the interaction between input matrix, reservoir, and readout layer, and the influence of key design parameters remain insufficiently understood. Here, we shift the focus from performance maximization to the identification of minimal computational requirements for different model tasks. We investigate how many neurons and how much nonlinearity are needed to solve specific tasks, including cases with non-sigmoidal activation functions. Our results show that the division of labor between input matrix, reservoir, and readout layer depends strongly on the task. In many cases, a weakly coupled and only minimally nonlinear reservoir proves sufficient. In addition, features often considered secondary, such as the structure of the input matrix or the steepness of the activation functions, can become decisive for performance.Subject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing, Neuroscience, Physics

Introduction

In recent years, deep learning has advanced rapidly1,2, driven in part by the development of large language models3. These models predominantly rely on feedforward architectures, in which data propagates in a single direction from input to output. By contrast, recurrent neural networks (RNNs) incorporate feedback loops, allowing them to operate as self-sustaining dynamical systems4 that maintain internal activity independently of ongoing input.

RNNs are known for several universal capabilities, including their theoretical power to approximate arbitrary mappings5 and emulate general dynamical systems6. Such properties, combined with practical strengths, have sparked ongoing interest in the internal workings of these networks. For instance, RNNs can integrate information across extended time periods7–12, and they are capable of forming compact yet expressive internal representations by balancing dimensional compression and expansion13.

Another important direction of research has focused on how RNN dynamics can be shaped and stabilized, particularly under the influence of noise – both internal and external14–21. RNNs have also gained attention as computational models of brain-like processing22. Especially sparse RNNs with relatively few connections per unit, echoing the architecture of biological neural circuits23 have shown advantages in memory retention and representational efficiency24–27.

In our previous work, we initiated a systematic study of how structural features influence RNN dynamics, starting from small motifs of three neurons28. Building on this, we demonstrated how large autonomous networks can be shaped through three key parameters: the weight distribution width w, the connection density d, and the excitation/inhibition balance b21,29. Additionally, we analyzed how such systems respond to noise, revealing resonance-like effects and stochastic stabilization phenomena19,20,30–34.

In our more recent studies, we examined how specific dynamical properties and structural regularities affect the performance of reservoir computing systems.

First35, we systematically varied the degree of neuronal nonlinearity and the strength of recurrent coupling to explore their impact on classification accuracy in synthetic tasks. Surprisingly, even ’calm’ reservoirs with minimal internal dynamics and very weak nonlinearity proved capable of generating linearly separable representations for the readout layer, as long as subtle high-dimensional state features were exploited. While task performance generally decreased under strongly chaotic dynamics, accuracy often peaked near the transition zones between chaotic and oscillatory or fix-point regimes, supporting the “edge of chaos” hypothesis.

Second36, we analyzed how biologically inspired structural regularities - such as Dale’s principle1, reciprocal symmetry (as in Hopfield networks39), and brain-like modularity40,41 - shape both the autonomous dynamics of RNNs and their functional performance in reservoir computing. We found that Dale-conform output weights and modular network structure tend to enhance task accuracy, likely by supporting stable and flexible internal representations. In contrast, strong reciprocal symmetry was detrimental, often driving the system into premature saturation and reducing its capacity to differentiate inputs. Together, these findings highlight the potential benefits of incorporating organizational structure into randomly connected networks.

In the present work, we examine in greater detail the interplay between the input matrix, the reservoir, and the readout layer in reservoir computing, with particular attention to parameter settings that are often regarded as secondary. To this end, we introduce a set of artificial prototypical tasks designed primarily for diagnostic purposes, each emphasizing different key aspects of recurrent data processing. For each task, we systematically vary design features and parameter settings to analyze how the components interact and contribute to performance.

A fundamental problem in reservoir computing, discussed for a long time in the context of the echo state7 and fading memory42,43 properties, is that residual state traces from earlier processing can carry over into the present episode. Results

Dynamical effects of reservoir coupling strength

The dynamical state of the reservoir is monitored using four measures, each derived from the time series of neural activations (see Methods section for details). The first is the fluctuation F, a normalized value between 0 and 1 that captures the average amplitude of neural activity. Closely related is the nonlinearity , which ranges from –1 in the linear regime to +1 in the digital regime. We also compute two unnormalized correlations, and , corresponding to lag times and , respectively. Both range from –1 in oscillatory regimes to +1 in fixed-point regimes. Together, these four simple metrics provide a useful, compact characterization of the reservoir’s dynamical state35.

In earlier work35, we primarily varied the balance parameter b to steer the reservoir dynamics from oscillatory (), through chaotic (), to fixed-point behavior (). In this study, we instead fix the balance at and focus on tuning the reservoir coupling strength w. This approach allows us to revisit and extend previous findings suggesting the existence of a favorable, ’quiescent’ regime at low w, in which the reservoir remains free of spontaneous fluctuations yet still provides sufficient nonlinearity and mixing for most computational tasks.

To examine the effect of w on the dynamics of the standard reservoir under realistic input conditions, the first 10 neurons receive independent random inputs at time step of each episode. These input values are uniformly drawn from the interval . Immediately before this, at , all 50 reservoir neurons are initialized to zero. After the random input vector is applied, the reservoir is allowed to evolve freely for 10 time steps before the next episode begins with a fresh initialization.

We begin with the standard reservoir setting (Fig.1(c)) and observe that the reservoir consistently relaxes to the same global fixed-point state toward the end of each episode. This state is characterized by low-amplitude activations that differ between neurons but remain constant over time. Its precise form is determined by the reservoir’s internal weights and biases. Notably, the weakly coupled reservoir converges to this resting state extremely rapidly - typically within just 1–2 time steps after the input vector is applied. The dynamical measures (Fig.1(b)) confirm that at , the system operates in a quiescent regime: the fluctuation amplitude F is very small, and indicates strongly linear behavior. The temporal correlations and are both close to zero.

Increasing the coupling strength to (Fig.1(d)), we observe that the reservoir begins to exhibit sustained fluctuations of larger amplitude following each input injection (see also Fig.1(b)). Unlike in the quiescent regime, these fluctuations persist throughout the entire episode and do not decay. The final reservoir states at the end of each episode remain similar across trials but are no longer identical. The dynamical measures indicate that marks the onset of a new dynamical regime, characterized by a rapid rise in both fluctuation amplitude and nonlinearity.

Finally, we increase the coupling strength to (Fig.1(e)). This leads to a pronounced increase in fluctuations, and the nonlinearity measure indicates that the neurons now operate in the saturated, digital regime for a substantial portion of the time. The temporal evolution of the reservoir states appears more chaotic; however, due to the consistent reset at the beginning of each episode, the dynamics remain fully determined by the injected input vector.

Sequence memorization task

In a typical reservoir computing system, the readout layer generates its output based solely on the reservoir’s current state. To allow the system to process and integrate temporally sequential data, the reservoir must therefore retain distinct traces of past inputs over an extended period. While it is relatively easy for the readout to reconstruct an input vector presented just one time step earlier, this task becomes increasingly difficult with each additional time step. The recurrent internal dynamics of the reservoir tends to mix and obscure the influence of earlier inputs, progressively degrading the separability of their traces. This challenge is further amplified when new inputs are continuously injected, each one potentially masking the lingering effects of its predecessors.

We test the reservoir’s ability to retain past inputs using a Sequence Memorization Task. In this task, the reservoir computer operates analogously to an autoencoder: a sequence of input vectors is injected into the reservoir, and after a fixed delay , the system is required to reconstruct the original input sequence with minimal error.

As an initial test, we inject a sequence of four input vectors, each consisting of five random components, into the standard reservoir of 50 neurons. The input matrix is sparse, targeting only the first five reservoir neurons with the incoming signals. To ensure consistent initial conditions, the reservoir is reset at the beginning of each episode, prior to presenting the first input. The delay is set so that reconstruction of the first input vector begins exactly one time step after the final input has been fed into the system (Fig.2).

Formally, this autoencoder task can be seen as a mapping from a -dimensional input sequence to an output sequence of the same shape. However, the mapping is implemented temporally, in a sliding-window fashion: The readout layer receives only the current activations of the 50 reservoir neurons and attempts to reconstruct from this state, instantaneously, the input vector that was presented time steps earlier. Although the delay is constant across all reconstructed vectors, the first input in each episode may be easier to retrieve, as it follows immediately after a reservoir reset to a well-defined initial state.

An independent test set of equal size is then used to evaluate performance by computing the root-mean-square error between the actual and target outputs. Patches classification task

In general, we view reservoir computers as trainable sequence-to-sequence mappings (see Methods, section Reservoir computer as a sequence-to-sequence map, and Fig.1(a)). Within this framework, classification tasks represent a special case in which the output “sequence” consists of a single discrete class label c per episode, while the input sequence may have both spatial width M and temporal depth .

Classification tasks involving temporal structure of the input require the reservoir to retain information over time. Since this memory capacity has already been assessed separately via the Sequence Memorization Task, we now deliberately focus on a non-temporal setting in which each episode consists of a single M-dimensional input vector.

When the data contains K distinct classes, the readout layer comprises one linear output unit per class, corresponding to a readout matrix of size , where N is the number of reservoir neurons. The K continuous outputs of the readout matrix are interpreted as class scores and converted into a single discrete label c via the argmax function.

In this setting, the primary role of the reservoir is to transform the input data into a representation that is linearly separable by the K readout units. In principle, this transformation could require several recurrent updates. However, in simpler cases, the nonlinearity of each neuron, combined with heterogeneous biases and input weights, may already suffice to disentangle the classes without any temporal processing.

Many real-world classification problems involve high-dimensional input vectors, such as the -pixel images in the MNIST dataset. However, high input dimensionality does not necessarily imply high classification complexity. For instance, compact Gaussian clusters are easily separable even in very high-dimensional spaces, without requiring nonlinear transformation by the reservoir. Conversely, classification can be difficult even in low-dimensional settings if the class boundaries are highly curved or fragmented.

Cellular automaton (CA) prediction task

We next consider a prediction task in which the reservoir computer must map a given state of a complex deterministic system to its successor state . Candidate systems for such a task include chaotic iterated maps, such as the logistic equation. However, in the chaotic regime, their extreme sensitivity to initial conditions demands exceptionally high numerical precision - requirements for which reservoir computers are generally not well suited.

Sequence generation task

We next consider a task in which the reservoir computer must reliably reproduce a predefined target sequence of output values . The following consideration shows that this should indeed be possible under certain conditions:

The target sequence can be collected into a column vector . At the beginning of each episode, our reservoir is reset to the same initial state, so that the recurrent network thereafter runs deterministically through a sequence of activation vectors . Stacking these as row vectors yields the state matrix . A single linear perceptron in the readout layer computes the dot product between each reservoir state and a fixed weight vector , thus generating an output sequence of values. To reproduce the target sequence exactly, the readout weights must satisfy the linear system . If the target values are independent random numbers, this is possible in general, provided that the reservoir dimension N is at least as large as the sequence length , and that the subsequent state vectors are linearly independent.

Note that the trajectory of the reservoir through state space, and thus also the output sequence , depend on the initial state used for the reset at the beginning of each episode. If the reservoir does not operate in a chaotic regime, small deviations in are expected to cause only small errors in the output sequence , rather than being exponentially amplified.

Conversely, if we initialize the reservoir with a completely different state , this will in general produce a different output sequence. In principle, the same reservoir computer could thus be used to generate any one of distinct output sequences, provided it is reset to a corresponding set of distinct initial states. In such a scenario, larger reservoirs are generally required, since a single readout layer has now to accommodate multiple target sequences simultaneously and for this purpose needs sufficiently rich reservoir states.

In our practical implementation, the reservoir is always reset to zero neural activations in time step of each episode. In time step , an M-dimensional input vector is injected into the first M reservoir neurons, thereby moving the reservoir to a different region of its state space. The subsequent reservoir state vectors are then passed to the readout to generate the output sequence. Rather than prescribing a simple list of scalar values , the target is defined as a sequence of output vectors, each of dimension K. Accordingly, the readout layer comprises K independent linear perceptrons, each with its own bias term.

Discussion

In this work, we systematically examined the interplay of input matrix, reservoir, and readout layer in reservoir computing, focusing on simplification rather than performance maximization. We found that reservoirs with very weak internal coupling–the “quiescent” regime–can still support accurate computation by providing fading memory and weak nonlinearity, without requiring strong spontaneous dynamics. Design aspects often considered secondary–such as the structure of the input matrix, the steepness of activation functions, or the choice of non-standard nonlinearities–proved to be decisive determinants of performance in several tasks.

Two of the investigated tasks, Patches Classification and Cellular Automaton Prediction, were non-temporal. As they did not require repeated recursive updating of the reservoir, the system effectively reduced to a feed-forward network with untrained hidden layers, which is more typical of extreme learning machines44. In contrast, the temporal tasks Sequence Memorization and Sequence Generation made use of the specific strengths of recurrent neural networks.

In the following, we summarize the impact of these design features across the different tasks.

Coupling strength w

The coupling strength w between reservoir neurons is a crucial parameter that directly shapes the system’s dynamics. Beyond a critical threshold, neuronal fluctuations increase strongly and drive the network into saturation and chaotic regimes (Fig.1(b) and (c-e)).

For most tasks, good performance requires staying below this threshold so that the reservoir remains in a quiescent regime, where input-induced perturbations do not develop into irregular, unpredictable fluctuations. Once the chaotic regime is entered, accuracy quickly deteriorates in the Sequence Memorization Task (SMT, Fig.2(b)) and in the Sequence Generation Task (SGT, Fig.5(f,g)).

At the same time, these two temporal tasks also require a minimal coupling strength. In the SMT, inter-neuron coupling ensures that information from the directly stimulated neurons propagates to other parts of the reservoir before it is overwritten by subsequent inputs. In the SGT, a certain degree of nonlinearity is needed to generate a dynamical landscape with sufficiently long transients into the resting-state attractor.

In the CA Prediction Task (CPT), relatively strong coupling can even be beneficial, as it enables neurons to generate binary outputs that match the predicted successor states (Fig.4(b)).

The Patches Classification Task (PCT) is an exception, as it does not rely on recurrent coupling at all and therefore exhibits accuracy that is independent of w (Fig.3(a)).

A natural point of reference for these findings is the foundational work by Jaeger7, who introduced the echo state property (ESP) as a key condition for reliable computation in recurrent networks. In this framework, the influence of initial conditions must vanish over time, such that the network state is uniquely determined by the input history. Jaeger related this requirement to the strength of recurrent coupling and identified a transition from stable (contracting) to unstable dynamics as coupling increases.

As the coupling strength increases, the system transitions to regimes with sustained fluctuations and increasing nonlinearity. This can be interpreted as approaching the boundary of the ESP, where contraction weakens and sensitivity to perturbations grows. While such regimes are often associated with richer dynamics, our results show that they also introduce variability across trials and reduced reproducibility of trajectories.

For even stronger coupling, the system enters a highly nonlinear regime with large fluctuations. From the perspective of the ESP, this lies beyond the classical stability boundary, where internal dynamics can dominate over input-driven behavior. Consistently, we observe more chaotic and less structured dynamics, which can impair task performance.

Overall, our findings place the ESP into a continuum of dynamical regimes. Rather than a binary condition, coupling strength shapes a spectrum from quiescent, reproducible dynamics to strongly nonlinear, fluctuation-dominated behavior. Importantly, optimal performance can occur well within the strongly contractive regime, suggesting that coupling primarily controls the balance between stability, nonlinearity, and input sensitivity.

In the Sequence Memorization Task, a central requirement is the ability of the reservoir to retain information about past inputs over multiple time steps. Again, this directly connects our results to the classical concept of the echo state property (ESP)7, and to subsequent analyses of memory traces in dynamical systems45.

In Jaeger’s framework, reliable short-term memory arises in regimes where the reservoir dynamics are contractive, such that past inputs leave fading but still recoverable traces in the current network state. Our results are fully consistent with this picture: optimal performance is achieved in a weakly coupled, quiescent regime, where the dynamics are strongly damped and spontaneous fluctuations are minimal. In this regime, input signals are neither immediately lost nor overwhelmed by internally generated activity, but instead leave structured, decaying traces that can be linearly extracted by the readout layer.

From the perspective of Ganguli, memory in recurrent systems can be understood as a trade-off between signal propagation and dynamical stability. Very weak coupling prevents the propagation of information through the network, while strong coupling leads to chaotic mixing and loss of decodable structure. Our results closely mirror this theoretical prediction: for very small coupling strengths, memory performance remains at baseline because input signals do not spread beyond the directly stimulated neurons. As coupling increases, performance improves due to enhanced propagation and mixing. However, beyond an optimal range, increasing fluctuations and nonlinearity degrade memory, consistent with the idea that excessive dynamical complexity destroys recoverable memory traces.

Importantly, our findings refine this classical view by showing that high memory performance does not require strongly nonlinear or “edge-of-chaos” dynamics. Instead, near-optimal performance is achieved well within a strongly contractive, weakly nonlinear regime. In this sense, the reservoir primarily serves as a linear or weakly nonlinear fading-memory system, while the readout performs most of the reconstruction. This observation complements the theoretical results of Jaeger and Ganguli et al., by emphasizing that practical reservoir computing systems may operate most effectively in regimes that prioritize stability and reproducibility over maximal dynamical richness.

Scaling factor s

The steepness of the sigmoidal neural response function in our study is controlled by the scaling factor s, which in other models is often interpreted as an inverse temperature. Across most of the tasks considered, accuracy exhibits a unimodal dependence on s, with a single peak at an optimal value (Fig.2(c), (Fig.3(c,d), Fig.4(c)). This indicates that a certain degree of nonlinearity is beneficial, whereas overly steep activation functions push the reservoir into harmful large-amplitude fluctuations, resembling the instability observed at large coupling strengths w.

In the case of the PCT, we demonstrated in detail how the scaling factor s controls the deformability of decision boundaries in the reservoir computer’s input space, allowing them to align with hard class boundaries (Fig.3(e-h)).

Reservoir size N

We found that accuracy increases monotonically with the number of reservoir neurons N across all tasks in which this parameter was varied (Fig.2(e), Fig.3(b), Fig.4(d,e)). For large reservoirs, performance reached a perfect level of in some tasks (Fig.3(b), Fig.4(d)), whereas in others accuracy saturated at suboptimal levels (Fig.2(e), Fig.4(e)).

Type of nonlinearity

While the standard reservoir employed the tanh activation function, we also tested alternative types in two of the tasks.

In the PCT, nonlinearity is essential: tanh, sin, and Gaussian activations perform comparably well, whereas linear neurons fail completely (Fig.3(d)). Interestingly, Heaviside activations achieve only relatively low accuracy, suggesting that continuously deformable decision boundaries are more effective in this task, despite the presence of sharp class boundaries.

In the SMT, tanh and sin activations again perform comparably (Fig.2(f)), but Gaussian activations perform very poorly, and Heaviside functions fail completely. In contrast, linear neurons reach perfect accuracy, indicating that old input vectors are best retrieved when reservoir states are transformed in an affine-linear way at each time step.

Division of labor

In reservoir computing, the nonlinear recurrent network was long regarded as the central component. The reservoir is assumed to provide the key functions of memory and data transformation, while the readout layer merely selects useful features from the reservoir’s state vector. Some of the tasks considered in this work, however, challenge this primacy of the reservoir.

An extreme case is the PCT. Although the class distribution in the two-dimensional input plane is highly complex (Fig.3(e)), the task relies solely on the input matrix and the nonlinearity of the activation functions. Recursive updates are not required, such that the reservoir neurons can even be left completely unconnected. In this setup, the readout layer receives only tanh-transformed linear combinations of input signals (generated by the dense input matrix) and can still separate the classes effectively.

The SGT provides another example where the main workload lies in the readout layer. To generate input-dependent predefined sequences of output states, the reservoir only needs to produce any deterministic, input-dependent state sequence, as long as subsequent states are sufficiently distinct (i.e., not linearly dependent) and do not overlap between input classes (Fig.5(c,f)). The readout layer can then transform such an arbitrary state sequence into the desired target sequence. This is mathematically straightforward, yet it illustrates the remarkable power of affine-linear transformations optimized via the pseudo-inverse method.

In the CPT, the workload is more balanced: both the soft nonlinearity of the activation functions and the recurrent updates are required to predict the next cellular automaton state (Fig.4). Nevertheless, it remains the task of the pseudo-inverse–trained readout layer to extract the local update rules of the cellular automaton from the complex reservoir state.

Taken together, our results suggest that reservoir computing does not rely on a single dominant mechanism but instead on a flexible distribution of functions across input, reservoir, and readout components. Depending on the task, the reservoir may serve as a source of memory traces, as a nonlinear feature generator, or merely as a passive conduit of transformed inputs. This perspective shifts the focus away from optimizing reservoir dynamics alone and toward understanding how different architectural elements share computational roles. In future work, it will be important to explore how these task-dependent divisions of labor extend to more complex benchmarks, and whether biologically inspired principles such as modularity, sparsity, or plasticity can be harnessed to design more efficient and interpretable reservoir systems.

Connection to recent trends in reservoir computing

Our result that, depending on the task, the division of labor between input matrix, reservoir, and readout layer can vary substantially - and that in some extreme cases the reservoir plays only a minimal role - is closely aligned with evolving perspectives in reservoir computing.

The field was originally introduced from a dynamical systems perspective in which the reservoir is understood as a driven system that gradually forgets its initial state. This idea is captured by the echo state property7, which ensures that the internal state of the reservoir becomes uniquely determined by the input signal after a sufficient transient period. Closely related to this is the notion of fading memory42,43: past inputs continue to influence the present state, but their impact decays over time. Together, these concepts guarantee that the system behaves in a stable and reproducible way, independent of how it was initialized.

A more modern interpretation reframes this behavior in terms of generalized synchronization. Instead of emphasizing the loss of initial conditions, this view highlights the emergence of a functional relationship between the input signal and the reservoir state. The reservoir effectively synchronizes with the driving input, meaning that its trajectory becomes a consistent transformation of the input dynamics. From this perspective, reproducibility arises not simply from forgetting the past, but from being continuously aligned with the structure of the incoming signal. This interpretation has been explored in particular in the context of nonlinear dynamical systems, where reservoir computing can be used to reconstruct and predict chaotic attractors directly from data46,47. More recently, it has been shown that this connection can be formalized rigorously by viewing the reservoir as a high-dimensional embedding of the underlying dynamical system, combining ideas from Takens embedding theory with the echo state property48.

In parallel to this dynamical systems perspective, a complementary line of work has shifted attention toward the role of the readout layer. It has been shown that memory can be introduced explicitly by providing the readout with delayed versions of reservoir states or of the input signal itself. In this view, the temporal context required for prediction is not stored implicitly in the reservoir dynamics, but constructed directly as part of the feature representation. This idea has been explored from different angles, including systematic studies of time-shifted readouts, which demonstrate that delayed features can significantly enhance performance and reduce the need for large reservoirs49, as well as more general analyses showing that delayed variables provide a simple and effective extension closely related to fading memory and synchronization concepts50.

Taken to its logical extreme, this perspective leads to approaches in which the dynamical reservoir is no longer required at all. In so-called next generation reservoir computing, the reservoir is replaced by an explicit nonlinear feature expansion of delayed input variables, resulting in a nonlinear vector autoregressive model that is often more efficient and interpretable while achieving comparable performance51. From this point of view, reservoir computing, classical time series models, and modern machine learning approaches can be understood within a common framework in which the main differences lie in how features are constructed and how the readout is designed52.

Together, these developments suggest a shift in perspective: while classical reservoir computing emphasizes the role of internal dynamics as a fading memory system, more recent work highlights that memory can be implemented explicitly and that the reservoir may primarily serve as a nonlinear feature generator. This unifying view provides a natural bridge between dynamical systems theory and modern machine learning approaches, and offers a broader conceptual framework in which different implementations of reservoir computing can be understood as variations along a continuum between implicit and explicit representations of temporal structure.

Footnotes

Dale’s principle, originally formulated by Henry Hallett Dale (1935)37, posits that a neuron exerts the same chemical action at all of its synaptic terminals. In modern neuroscience this is usually interpreted as meaning that a neuron releases the same set of neurotransmitters at all of its outputs. Within computational neuroscience, this is often simplified to the assumption that neurons are functionally homogeneous in their postsynaptic effects–that is, a neuron is either purely excitatory or purely inhibitory. While this idealization is helpful for network modeling, it is an oversimplification: some neurons co-release multiple transmitters, and modulatory effects (e.g. GABA being excitatory early in development) complicate the picture. Nevertheless, the excitatory/inhibitory dichotomy derived from Dale’s principle remains a foundational assumption in many theoretical models of neural circuits38.
