Left Bundle Branch Area Pacing After Subaortic Membrane Resection
Authors: Eyad Alhaj, Layan Alhaj, Dema Alhaj, Ali Alhaj

LBBAP may reverse postoperative left bundle branch block–associated cardiomyopathy.

Left bundle branch area pacing (LBBAP) has emerged as an alternative to biventricular pacing for cardiac resynchronization therapy (CRT), particularly in patients with unfavorable coronary sinus anatomy or failed left ventricular (LV) lead placement.1,2 To our knowledge, LBBAP after surgical resection of a subaortic membrane has not previously been reported. We present a case of successful LBBAP in a patient who developed left bundle branch block (LBBB) and cardiomyopathy after subaortic membrane resection.Take-Home Messages•LBBAP can achieve effective resynchronization and functional recovery in patients with postoperative LBBB and cardiomyopathy following subaortic membrane resection.•When conventional CRT is not feasible owing to unfavorable coronary sinus anatomy, conduction system pacing represents a viable and effective alternative.

Case Presentation

A 44-year-old man with hypertension and dyslipidemia presented with progressive dyspnea on exertion, fatigue, and exercise intolerance. Baseline electrocardiogram showed normal sinus rhythm (Figure 1). Echocardiography demonstrated preserved left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) with flow acceleration in the LV outflow tract. Transesophageal echocardiography confirmed a subaortic membrane, with a normal trileaflet aortic valve and no stenosis or regurgitation.Figure 1Baseline 12-Lead Electrocardiogram

Baseline 12-Lead Electrocardiogram

Cardiac catheterization revealed myocardial bridging of the mid–left anterior descending artery (LAD) and a significant stenotic lesion in the mid-obtuse marginal branch 2 (OM2). The OM2 lesion was treated with a drug-eluting stent. Symptoms persisted, and the patient subsequently underwent surgical resection of the subaortic membrane and unroofing of the mid–left anterior descending artery bridge. Postoperatively, his symptoms improved, and echocardiography before discharge confirmed preserved LVEF.

Several weeks later, he experienced worsening dyspnea and reduced exercise tolerance. Electrocardiogram revealed a new LBBB (Figure 2), and echocardiography showed a reduced LVEF of 30% without residual LV outflow tract gradient. Repeat angiography confirmed patency of the OM2 stent and absence of significant coronary artery disease or residual myocardial bridging.Figure 212-Lead Electrocardiogram After Surgery to Remove Subaortic Membrane

The patient underwent biventricular implantable cardioverter defibrillator (ICD) implantation at another hospital. Despite 2 procedures, the LV lead repeatedly dislodged from the only suitable coronary sinus branch. Epicardial LV lead placement was offered but declined. He was transferred to our institution with an right ventricular ICD lead already in place. At this point, LVEF had been depressed for several months, raising uncertainty regarding reversibility. Although a dual-chamber pacing system might have been preferable, the ICD had already been implanted, limiting further options.

Given unfavorable coronary sinus anatomy and failed CRT attempts, conduction system pacing was pursued. Using a 3,830 lead and C315 sheath (Medtronic Inc), the sheath was positioned at the mid-lower interventricular septum to target the posterior fascicle (Figure 3, Figure 4, Figure 5). The paced QRS showed a QR pattern, narrowing with a superior axis, and a stimulus–to–R-wave peak time of 90 ms in lead I, 95 ms in lead V6, and V1–V6 interpeak delay of 47 ms. Although no left bundle branch (LBB) potential was recorded, these findings, combined with fluoroscopic positioning, were consistent with LBBAP (Figure 6). Discussion

Postoperative LBBB is a recognized complication of certain cardiac surgeries and may result in mechanical dyssynchrony, reduced ejection fraction, and symptomatic cardiomyopathy.3,4 Surgery for subaortic membrane carries a particular risk of injury to the proximal LBB trunk owing to septal scarring and distortion of the conduction system.5 In our patient, this likely contributed to the development of new-onset LBBB and LV dysfunction.

Conduction system pacing has been shown to provide superior electrical resynchronization compared with conventional CRT in patients with LBBB.6 Criteria for confirming LBB capture remain debated, and deep septal pacing can mimic conduction system pacing. In this case, QRS narrowing, R-wave peak time values, QR morphology, and, most importantly, the recovery of LVEF strongly support effective resynchronization.

Alternative strategies were limited. The OM2 lesion was stented before surgery, and surgical bypass was not pursued at that time. The patient was transferred to our institution with an ICD already implanted, restricting device selection. Coronary sinus anatomy was unfavorable for LV lead placement, as evidenced by repeated LV lead dislodgment. Thus, conduction system pacing was a reasonable and ultimately effective strategy.

Limitations of this case include the absence of LBB potential recordings, lack of intraprocedural imaging (eg, transesophageal echocardiography or intracardiac echocardiography) to guide septal positioning, and absence of lateral fluoroscopic views. These limitations are acknowledged, but the dramatic clinical and echocardiographic improvement supports the therapeutic efficacy of LBBAP in this setting. This case highlights the feasibility and effectiveness of LBBAP in patients with postsurgical LBBB and cardiomyopathy, especially when coronary sinus anatomy precludes conventional CRT.

Conclusions

In patients who develop LBBB and cardiomyopathy following subaortic membrane resection, LBBAP can restore physiological conduction and reverse LV dysfunction when conventional CRT is not feasible. To our knowledge, this is the first reported case of successful LBBAP after subaortic membrane resection.
