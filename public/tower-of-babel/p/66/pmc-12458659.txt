Implementation of patient safety monitoring systems in hospitals: a systematic review
Authors: Ghasem Alizadeh-Dizaj, Shahla Damanabi, Mohammad Esmaeil Hejazi, Samira Raoofi, Leila R Kalankesh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe significance of patient safety has been acknowledged in healthcare systems, prompting the need for effective patient safety monitoring systems (PSMSs). These systems’ endeavour is to manage patient safety data and improve overall safety within healthcare organisations. This study aims to characterise the implementation of and outputs of such systems across hospital settings.MethodsA systematic literature review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines. The review included a comprehensive search of databases such as PubMed, EMBASE, Scopus, Web of Science and Google Scholar for studies published in English up to 30 July 2024. The focus was on monitoring systems that manage patient safety in medical care, with inclusion criteria that required studies to examine the application of PSMSs and report their implementation outputs.ResultsThe literature search yielded 23 relevant studies published between 2009 and 2023. PSMSs were used in various clinical contexts, including emergency departments, radiology wards, intensive care units and operating rooms, addressing various issues such as medication safety, healthcare-associated infections, blood transfusion errors, surgical site infections, laboratory and radiology adverse events. The findings indicated positive outputs from the implementation of PSMSs. Furthermore, these systems provide valuable information and timely alerts and contribute to a culture of safety in healthcare facilities.ConclusionsPSMSs can be used for enhancing safety practices, reducing adverse events and promoting a culture of patient safety. Further research and continued implementation of PSMSs are essential to further augment patient safety standards in healthcare settings.Keywords: Health Information Management; Medical Informatics; Decision Support Systems, Management; Data Visualization

WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC

Before this study, there was a recognised need for effective patient safety monitoring systems (PSMSs) in healthcare, primarily focusing on medication safety and infection control. While numerous studies explored various types of PSMSs, there was limited synthesis of evidence regarding their applications and outcomes across diverse healthcare settings, highlighting a gap in understanding their broader impact on patient safety.

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

This systematic review characterises the use cases of PSMSs across various clinical contexts, demonstrating their positive effects on patient safety outcomes, such as improved adherence to safety protocols and reduced adverse events. The findings reveal that PSMSs enhance incident reporting and contribute to a culture of safety within healthcare organisations, providing timely alerts and valuable information that support proactive interventions.

HOW THIS STUDY MIGHT AFFECT RESEARCH, PRACTICE, OR POLICY

The study underscores the importance of implementing PSMSs to improve patient safety standards in healthcare settings. It suggests that future research should focus on developing and evaluating tailored PSMS solutions, particularly bedside monitoring systems, to optimise patient engagement and safety. Introduction

Improving the quality and safety performance of hospitals has become increasingly crucial in recent years.1 Many countries, including the USA, Canada, Australia, the UK and the Netherlands, have implemented national policies, programmes and frameworks focusing on quality and safety, introducing quality indicators to assess healthcare performance.2 Patient safety is a multifaceted issue involving various disciplines and processes, necessitating a holistic approach to enhancement. It is a critical aspect of healthcare quality, and any harm to patients goes against the principles of healthcare.1 3 4 Patient safety may be compromised as patient well-being often relies on healthcare professionals and can be influenced by system failures, inadequate organisational processes and flawed management.5 Healthcare procedures are not always risk-free, and errors and medical accidents may occur.6 A medical error is described as an action in a healthcare setting that deviates from established health standards. These errors can occur at any point in the healthcare process, jeopardising patients’ health and quality of care.7 8 In the USA, medical errors result in over 250 000 deaths annually, ranking as the third leading cause of death.9 In addition to causing physical and psychological complications, medical errors—the primary factor affecting patient safety and events that compromise it—impose significant financial burden on the healthcare system. Adverse events reportedly cost the UK healthcare system £1 to £2 million annually.10 Meaningful efforts are required to enhance patient safety and reduce medical mishaps.6 Improving quality should address unsafe medical practices.11 Consequently, concerns regarding the recurrence of medical errors and their impact have prompted the development of patient safety monitoring systems (PSMSs) for hospitals and healthcare facilities.12 13

The implementation of PSMSs and incident analysis demonstrates that effective reporting and investigation of patient safety issues can enhance patient safety and health.14 By using these systems, organisations can manage internal knowledge and establish learning mechanisms by reflecting on mistakes, ensuring optimal efficiency with minimal effort. Such systems are at the core of the learning health system notion.15 Monitoring in healthcare settings is inherently labour-intensive task and could not be effectively carried out by humans, with the vast amount of data collected posing challenges in problem detection.16 However, if health information monitoring is automated, the system can identify and highlight issues by connecting dots (examining connections and patterns), enabling professionals to intervene proactively before incidents occur.17 Healthcare organisations have implemented various tools to monitor the performance of the health system, reduce medication errors, optimise treatment, decrease infection rates and improve care quality across departments.18,20 Among these tools, PSMSs play a crucial role. Evidence shows these systems allow users to oversee hospitals’ safety performance by presenting a clear overview of necessary quality and safety indicators to meet healthcare quality objectives.21 22 The utilisation of these monitoring systems may pave the way to enhance hospital quality and safety performance, as studies suggest that their implementation results in lower infection rates, medication errors and bed falls.23,25

Numerous studies have explored various types of PSMSs concentrating on patient outcomes like medication errors, adverse events and hospital-acquired infections. Nonetheless, uncertainty and lack of evidence synthesis persist about the application and result of employing these systems across various healthcare settings and populations, with limited research on their impact. While these studies highlight the potential of PSMS to reduce adverse events, they predominantly focus on isolated technologies or narrow clinical contexts. A critical gap remains in synthesising evidence on how PSMS are operationalised across diverse hospital settings, including variations in use cases, implementation challenges and contextual factors influencing their effectiveness. Furthermore, existing reviews often lack a holistic analysis of human-system interactions and emerging use cases. This limits actionable insights for hospitals aiming to adopt or optimise PSMS. Therefore, the current systematic review aims to assess studies on different PSMSs. The main objectives of this study were to examine the implementation of PSMSs in hospital and to explore outputs of employing PSMSs on patient safety within healthcare organisations.

Methods

Design

A systematic literature review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines (PRISMA).

Search strategy and inclusion criteria

All available published studies in English were searched using five literature databases (MEDLINE via PubMed, EMBASE, Scopus, Web of Science and Google Scholar). Publications were included if they discussed a monitoring system for managing patient safety event data in the hospital setting. Given the broad range of patient safety issues, we used various relevant keywords for the search, as outlined in table 1.

Screening process

After manually removing duplicates and non-journal publications (eg, magazine articles and book chapters), two authors reviewed titles and abstracts of each remaining article or abstract and all stages of selection and screening of articles were performed independently. First, the titles of all articles were reviewed, then articles that were not consistent with the study’s objectives were excluded from the study. In the following steps, the abstract and full text of the articles were studied, respectively, to identify and exclude studies that met exclusion criteria or were not related to the study’s subject. The inclusion and exclusion criteria for studies in this review were defined as follows: Inclusion criteria: Focus on patient safety event management (eg, detection/monitoring of adverse events), hospital-based implementations (inpatient/emergency settings), peer-reviewed journal articles (to ensure methodological rigour). Exclusion criteria: Studies on non-safety care quality (eg, efficiency, cost reduction), systems limited to risk prediction without event monitoring, non-hospital settings (eg, outpatient clinics), non-empirical/non-technical literature (eg, opinion pieces). Thus, our parameters, in PICOS format, were: Population (P): hospital settings (inpatient/emergency departments), Intervention (I): PSMSs, Comparison (C): not applicable, as this review synthesises evidence on PSMS implementations rather than comparing interventions, Outcome (O): use cases, operationalisation and effectiveness of PSMS in managing patient safety events (eg, adverse events, errors). Publications were only included if they described management of patient safety data by a monitoring system. Publications that discussed only non-safety event-related aspects of care quality were excluded. Similarly, literature on monitoring systems providing risk factors to prevent patient safety events rather than events themselves (eg, intensive care screens that display a particular patient’s heart rate and oxygenation saturation or calculate a real-time risk level) was beyond the scope of this study and was excluded. We reviewed all publications potentially meeting study criteria in full. Reviewers discussed each inclusion, and disagreement regarding whether an article or abstract met criteria was resolved by consensus. If necessary, the third researcher was consulted to deal with disputes. Figure 1 displays a flow chart of the search strategy.

Quality and bias assessment

To evaluate the methodological rigour of included studies, we employed the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT) a validated tool for assessing qualitative, quantitative and mixed-methods studies. The MMAT evaluates five criteria per study type, including appropriateness of research design, sampling strategy, data collection, analysis methods and alignment between objectives and conclusions. Two reviewers independently scored each study (1=met criterion, 0=unmet), with discrepancies resolved through discussion or third-reviewer arbitration. Studies scoring ≥4/5 were classified as high quality, 3 as moderate and ≤2 as low quality. This stratification informed our evidence synthesis, with higher weight given to high-quality studies in thematic analysis.

For interventional studies, we supplemented MMAT with the Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool (RoB 2.0) to assess randomisation, allocation concealment, blinding and attrition bias. Non-randomised studies were evaluated using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale, focusing on selection, comparability and outcome measurement (maximum score: 9; studies scoring ≥7 were deemed low risk).

Data extraction

Two authors independently extracted data from each identified publication using a predefined data extraction and review form. The data extraction and review form encompasses study characteristics (ie, author, country, purpose, study type, monitoring method, objectives of PSMSs, patient safety data elements, use cases of PSMSs, outputs of using PSMSs and main results). Reviewers specifically identified (1) exact place/location of the monitoring system was used in, (2) the patient safety topic covered by the monitoring system, (3) the time period of using the monitoring system and (4) the outputs of the monitoring system identified by each publication’s authors. To evaluate the strengths and weaknesses of the evidence regarding patient safety improvements, the reviewers also examined the study and determined whether a control or other comparison group was used. Content analysis was used to analyse data and helped researchers to highlight key findings and provide a detailed understanding of how monitoring systems can help improve patient safety outcomes in healthcare settings. Analysis was performed using software that supports systematic review and data synthesis processes, such as EndNote and Excel to organise and tabulate the extracted data. To ensure accuracy in data extraction, all studies—including complex non-randomised controlled trial (RCT) designs (eg, mixed-methods, observational)—were independently extracted by two reviewers using a standardised form. Discrepancies (eg, divergent interpretations of qualitative themes or outcome metrics) were resolved through consensus or arbitration by a third reviewer. Given the heterogeneity of study designs, extraction for non-RCT studies (eg, mixed-methods, observational) was performed in duplicate, with a third reviewer resolving disagreements. Pilot testing of the extraction form on five randomly selected studies ensured consistency prior to full implementation.

Results

Our literature search identified a total of 8280 results (PubMed: 1110, EMBASE: 1834, Scopus: 3458, WoS: 1816 and Google Scholar: 60). After removing 3358 duplicates, 4922 result entries remained. Moreover, 717 records including conference papers (n=621), book or chapter (n=84), generic papers (n=11), thesis papers (n=1) were excluded. Two reviewers independently reviewed titles and abstracts of the remaining 4205 articles. Reviewers manually reviewed titles and abstracts and excluded publications that did not relate to the subject of the PSMSs as a primary or secondary focus, or their use cases in patient safety management. After excluding irrelevant papers, reviewers identified 176 publications that warranted further review of the full text. Reviewers discussed each publication, and after consensus, identified 23 final publications that warranted inclusion in the analysis. References of each publication were reviewed for additional sources but did not identify additional publications. Figure 1 displays the PRISMA diagram of the search strategy.

Quality and bias assessment

The methodological quality of the included studies varied, with 10 studies (43.5%) rated as high quality, 9 (39.1%) as moderate and 4 (17.4%) as low quality based on MMAT criteria. The only RCT25 demonstrated low risk of bias, while observational and cross-sectional studies were more susceptible to selection, detection and reporting biases. Common limitations included small sample sizes, single-centre designs, reliance on self-reported data and lack of control groups. Mixed-methods studies26 27 scored highly due to rigorous integration of qualitative and quantitative data. Basic characteristics of the studies

The systematic review encompassed studies from eight different countries, reflecting a wide range of geographical representation, with the USA contributing the highest number at 11 studies (45.8%), followed by both the UK and China each at 3 studies (12.5%). Target groups varied significantly, with physicians accounting for 40.6% (9 studies), followed by nurses at 26.1% (6 studies), and patient safety experts at 21.7% (5 studies). Methodologically, the review included 3 cross-sectional studies (13%), 4 mixed-methods studies (17.4%), one randomised controlled trial (4.3%), 10 observational studies (43.5%) and a few studies employing pilot and retrospective cohort designs (each 4.3%). Notably, 66.7% (16 studies) of the research focused on real-time monitoring approaches, facilitating immediate feedback for clinical decision-making, while 29.2% (7 studies) used non-real-time monitoring techniques. The primary objectives of PSMSs were to enhance patient safety, with 37.5% (9 studies) concentrated on medication safety and adverse drug reactions, 25% (6 studies) targeted improving adverse event reporting, 20.8% (5 studies) focused on preventing healthcare-associated infections, and 16.7% (4 studies) sought to enhance the overall quality and efficiency of patient care. Reported output of PSMSs usage

Some studies reported the use cases and output of PSMSs quantitatively while some reported them qualitatively. Alhmoud et al found that the National Early Warning Score increased from 64% (before the study) to 83% after implementing the PSM dashboard. Additionally, Griffeth et al’s research demonstrated a 48% rise in reports of patient safety incidents in the intensive care unit following the implementation of the system. Hans et al noted an increase in lab adherence among inflammatory bowel disease patients from 39% to 81%. Following the implementation of a surgical site infection (SSI) surveillance system, Kasatpibal et al reported a decrease in the overall rate of SSIs from 1.8 per 100 surgeries to 1.2 per 100 operations. According to 15 studies, PSMSs generate and provide users with useful, applicable information.21,2327 Moreover, in two studies, there was evidence of enhancing provider efficiency and saving time through using PSMS.32 36 Six of the included studies focused on how PSMSs can deliver timely and helpful alerts to assist in identifying medication errors and surgical infections.24,2630 31 39 Furthermore, several studies highlighted how these technologies contribute to and strengthen the patient safety culture in hospital environments.22 32 38 40 Numerous research efforts have examined how PSMSs are used to track and report adverse events in hospitals.2124 27,30 35 38 39 41 These studies have underscored the systems’ potential to identify errors early, track them and expedite the reporting of adverse events. They offer hospital administrators and other healthcare organisations the opportunity to accurately understand the safety environment and implement appropriate judgments, actions and guidelines. Discussion

The current systematic review highlighted the essential use cases of PSMSs towards improving patient safety within hospital settings. Notably, the majority of studies concentrated on drug safety and hospital infections accounting for 37.5% and 20.8% of the reviewed literature, respectively. This may imply a higher perceived risk and prevalence of issues in these domains,44 45 as medication errors and healthcare-associated infections are among well-documented challenges in clinical settings.46 47 Accordingly, these issues have attracted more targeted research efforts and resource allocation in patient safety.48 The relatively low coverage of other patient safety areas could arise from their lower incidence in clinical practice or inherent challenges in effectively monitoring and documenting these safety issues. One significant factor can be attributed to the comparatively lower incidence of reported cases relative to medication errors and infections.49 This can foster a perception that these issues are less critical or frequent, resulting in fewer studies and interventions being directed toward them. Additionally, challenges in monitoring and documenting these safety concerns can hinder research and systematic reviews. For instance, areas such as patient falls, retained foreign items, and postoperative kidney injury, while significant, may lack the robust surveillance mechanisms established for drug-related adverse events and infections.49 The evidence on PSMSs is predominantly moderate in quality, with only 43.5% of studies meeting high-quality criteria. Common limitations include selection bias (small/single-centre samples), reporting bias (underdocumented safety events) and detection bias (manual data entry errors). While these constraints necessitate cautious interpretation, consistent findings across higher-quality studies suggest PSMSs can enhance safety monitoring.

The primary target groups identified in these studies were healthcare professionals, primarily physicians (40.6%) and nurses (26.1%). This emphasis on providers rather than patients reflects a traditional healthcare paradigm where patients are often viewed as passive recipients of care.50 A significant gap exists in the engagement of patients in their safety efforts.51 Empowering patients through initiatives such as personal electronic health records and patient portals could enhance safety measures and treatment adherence, allowing patients to take an active role in monitoring their health outcomes and fostering a sense of responsibility and agency.52 In a study, O’Leary et al reported that the implementation of tablets with a mobile patient portal application enhanced hospital patients' understanding of their physician team members.53 When patients are empowered to participate in their care, they are more likely to communicate effectively with healthcare professionals, adhere to treatment plans and promptly report safety concerns.54 Consequently, involving patients in monitoring and decision-making can lead to improved safety outcomes and overall satisfaction with the healthcare experience.55 Transitioning towards a model that includes both healthcare providers and patients as active participants in patient safety initiatives appears to be essential for establishing a comprehensive and effective approach.56

Interestingly, the majority of PSMSs examined were hospital-wide systems, not specifically designed for bedside use. This limitation could hinder their effectiveness, as bedside interventions may offer more immediate and actionable data that can be used in real-time decision-making.57 Systems tailored to the bedside could potentially enhance patient safety by enabling direct, ongoing feedback from monitoring devices and fostering a more responsive approach to care delivery.58 The findings reveal a need for further research into the development and implementation of tailored bedside PSMSs that can be seamlessly integrated into existing workflows. Also, incorporating automated data feed capabilities into electronic health records can deliver real-time updates and alerts regarding patient status, medication schedules and potential safety concerns, thereby enabling proactive interventions by healthcare professionals.59 This integration can optimise workflow, minimise the risk of human error and ensure that healthcare providers have access to the most current and relevant information throughout the patient encounters.60 By integrating automated data, these systems can be transformed from passive data collection to a dynamic, real-time monitoring approach that enhances clinical decision-making and improves patient care outcomes.59

The duration and scope of implementing PSMSs significantly influence their effectiveness and patient safety outcomes. With implementation periods ranging from 2 months to 10 years, the average of 30 months indicates that longer integration often facilitates more comprehensive data collection and a better understanding of the systems’ impact. However, a shorter period of implementation may be accompanied by superficial integration and less impactful outcomes, especially in organisations that lack a supportive culture for patient safety.61 Such culture promotes transparency, encourages reporting and emphasises education as an essential component for maximising the benefits of PSMSs.62 Therefore, healthcare organisations must not only concentrate on the duration and sustainability of the implementation but also ensure a supportive organisational environment to enhance patient safety culture effectively.

Finally, the successful deployment of PSMSs entails a complex interplay of technical, organisational and social factors that extend beyond just technological implementation.31 Key elements include comprehensive training for healthcare staff, robust management support and the acceptance of new technologies within clinical environments, all of which contribute to cultivating a culture of safety.63 Effective training should be continuous and highlight the relevance of PSMSs, while the management team must actively advocate for safety initiatives to promote staff engagement.22 Furthermore, involving staff in the decision-making process can help alleviate resistance to change.64 Future research should investigate these interconnected dimensions across various hospital settings to identify best practices and facilitate the sustainable and effective implementation of PSMSs, ultimately enhancing patient safety and care quality.

Conclusions

Despite the recognised benefits, the review also highlights gaps in the current literature, including the necessity for increased focus on patient engagement and the development of bedside monitoring solutions. The successful deployment of PSMSs relies on a multifaceted approach that encompasses technological, organisational and social elements, necessitating active involvement from healthcare staff and robust management support. As healthcare continues to evolve, ongoing research is crucial to refine PSMS utilisation, promote a robust culture of safety, and ultimately improve patient outcomes across diverse hospital settings. For PSMSs to reach their full potential, it is essential to foster environments that prioritise transparency, reporting and continuous learning in order to address the complexities of patient safety effectively.
