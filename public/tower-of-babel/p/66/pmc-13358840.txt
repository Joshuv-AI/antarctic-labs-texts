A decade within reach-but not the destination: transcatheter aortic-valve replacement durability beyond year 10
Authors: Jeffrey H Shuhaiber
Published: 2026

The 7-year PARTNER 3 report is a real milestone, and it deserves to be treated as one. In low-risk patients with severe symptomatic aortic stenosis (AS) and a mean age of 73 years, transcatheter aortic-valve replacement (TAVR) and surgical aortic-valve replacement (SAVR) were similar at 7 years for the major composite outcomes, with mean gradients of 13.1±8.5 mmHg after TAVR and 12.1±6.3 mmHg after SAVR, and bioprosthetic valve failure (BVF) rates of 6.9% and 7.5%, respectively (1,2). The original report is careful in its conclusions: it establishes important 7-year clinical and hemodynamic reassurance in the randomized low-risk PARTNER 3 population; it does not claim to settle durability at 15, 20, or 30 years. The trial protocol also makes clear that this was a selected low-risk, transfemoral-suitable population with important anatomic and clinical exclusions (3). The purpose of this commentary is therefore not to dispute the trial, but to place its reassuring signal in the lifetime-management frame that now defines aortic valve therapy.

That distinction matters. TAVR in carefully selected septuagenarians has moved well beyond a rescue strategy for frail or inoperable patients. It now deserves to be discussed as a credible biologic valve strategy. But the stronger the first-decade data become, the more disciplined our language must be. Seven-year equivalence is not the same thing as second-decade certainty. A durable valve strategy is not defined only by survival at 7 years; it is defined by freedom from clinically meaningful structural valve deterioration (SVD), bioprosthetic valve dysfunction (BVD)/BVF, reintervention, clinically important regurgitation or stenosis, thrombosis requiring treatment, and a repeat-intervention pathway that remains anatomically and physiologically safe (4,5).

The first discipline in discussing TAVR durability is to define the endpoint before debating the result. Consensus documents have separated SVD, which refers to intrinsic and generally irreversible changes in the bioprosthesis, from non-structural dysfunction, thrombosis, endocarditis, and BVF, which is the clinically consequential state of valve-related death, reintervention, or severe hemodynamic dysfunction (4,5). This distinction is useful because a valve can look acceptable by gradient alone while still generating a clinically relevant lifetime problem through paravalvular regurgitation, pacemaker dependence, leaflet thrombosis, coronary-access difficulty, prosthesis-patient mismatch, or a poor platform for redo intervention. The PARTNER 2 durability analysis further illustrates why standardized definitions matter when comparing transcatheter and surgical bioprostheses across time and risk strata (6). In older patients, these issues may never mature into a dominant clinical problem. In younger or longer-lived patients, they may.

The second discipline is choosing the right comparator. Too much of the durability conversation is distorted by comparisons with younger SAVR cohorts in whom SVD is expected to emerge sooner and more aggressively. That is the wrong comparison for PARTNER 3. The trial population had a mean age of 73 years, was randomized, and was deliberately selected for transfemoral suitability, low operative risk, and the absence of several competing hazards, including bicuspid anatomy and marked frailty (1,3). These features are not flaws in the trial. They are the conditions under which the trial question was responsibly tested. The fair surgical reference, therefore, is not the 50-year-old tissue-valve patient being asked to live with a bioprosthesis into the third decade. It is the older SAVR population in whom biologic valve performance has historically been sufficiently durable that many patients never live long enough to experience dominant structural failure.

The older-patient SAVR literature is more reassuring than many contemporary discussions acknowledge, while also being heterogeneous. Surgical bioprosthetic durability differs by valve model, tissue treatment, stented versus stentless design, annular sizing, implantation technique, prosthesis-patient mismatch, renal disease, age, and competing mortality. In elderly recipients of the Carpentier-Edwards Perimount pericardial bioprosthesis, Mistiaen and colleagues reported long-term outcomes in a cohort with a median age of 73 years, showing acceptable thromboembolic and valve-related outcomes (7). In the 15-year multicenter Freestyle report, Bach and colleagues showed good long-term clinical outcomes, with older age associated with lower risk of reoperation and explant for SVD (8). Amabile and colleagues reported freedom from SVD of 94% at 10 years in a prospective Freestyle cohort (9). These data should not be treated as randomized comparisons with PARTNER 3. Their main message is narrower and more useful: in patients around their early 70s, surgical tissue-valve durability has generally been clinically sufficient, not biologically fragile.

The younger SAVR series still matter, but for a different reason. They remind us what the second decade can reveal when biology has more time to express itself. Bourguignon’s long-term Perimount data in patients aged 50 to 65 years showed expected durability of approximately 19 years, yet freedom from reoperation for SVD fell substantially by 20 years (10). Une and colleagues, examining the Hancock II bioprosthesis in younger patients, reported that SVD rose sharply from 10 to 20 years, particularly in those under 50 years and in those with prosthesis-patient mismatch (11). These studies do not prove that low-risk TAVR in septuagenarians will deteriorate early. They support a more modest and more important point: first-decade reassurance may coexist with meaningful second-decade uncertainty.

The third discipline is biological honesty. The early similarity of TAVR and SAVR curves should not be misread as evidence that they are identical biological pathways. They are not. In PARTNER 3, new-onset atrial fibrillation was more frequent after SAVR, whereas permanent pacemaker implantation was more common after TAVR (1). That difference matters because it clarifies the trade-off. Postoperative atrial fibrillation is part of the surgical injury and recovery footprint; conduction injury and pacemaker implantation are part of the device-tissue interaction unique to TAVR. These are not interchangeable liabilities. They are different footprints of two different therapies that currently converge on similar midterm clinical endpoints.

Leaflet thrombosis is the clearest example of an unresolved biological question. In PARTNER 3, clinically significant valve thrombosis was higher after TAVR than after SAVR at 7 years (1). The protocol did not mandate routine vitamin K antagonist (VKA) therapy after TAVR in patients without atrial fibrillation or recent stenting; antiplatelet-based regimens predominated, and the computed tomography (CT) substudy excluded patients with a planned need for anticoagulation (3). The mechanistic concern is plausible: if leaflet thrombosis is more common in transcatheter valves and if recurrent or persistent thrombosis contributes to fibrosis, calcification, impaired motion, or accelerated hemodynamic dysfunction, then durability is partly a management question rather than only a materials question. That chain, however, remains a hypothesis rather than a proven clinical pathway.

The strength of the contemporary TAVR position is that first-decade reassurance is no longer speculative. PARTNER 3 at 7 years, the PARTNER 3 echocardiographic results at 5 years, the Evolut Low Risk 5-year data, and contemporary registry data in trial-eligible low-risk patients all point in the same direction: early and midterm transcatheter performance in selected low-risk patients is real, not fragile (1,2,12). Hahn and colleagues showed stable valve hemodynamics at 5 years, although mild aortic regurgitation remained more frequent after TAVR (12).

The literature supports caution, not overstatement. In the 2015 New England Journal of Medicine report on possible subclinical leaflet thrombosis in bioprosthetic aortic valves, reduced leaflet motion was identified in both transcatheter and surgical valves and often resolved with therapeutic anticoagulation (13). In the PARTNER 3 CT substudy, subclinical leaflet thrombosis was more frequent in transcatheter than surgical bioprosthetic valves at 30 days, although the difference was not maintained at 1 year (14). Cahill and colleagues summarized the evidence by noting that oral anticoagulation prevents and treats subclinical leaflet thrombosis after TAVR, but has not shown a net clinical benefit that clearly outweighs bleeding risk (15). Thus, this editorial does not recommend routine anticoagulation after low-risk TAVR. It argues only that thrombosis biology should remain inside the durability discussion, particularly as follow-up moves beyond the first decade.

The surgical anticoagulation literature adds a related but indirect signal. In the Danish nationwide analysis by Mérie and colleagues, discontinuation of warfarin within 6 months after bioprosthetic aortic-valve replacement (AVR) was associated with increased cardiovascular death (16). In the Swedish analysis by Christersson and colleagues, warfarin exposure after surgical bioprosthetic AVR was associated with lower long-term risk of ischemic stroke and thromboembolic events, at the cost of more bleeding, with similar mortality (17). These studies do not prove that anticoagulation preserves valve durability. They do, however, reinforce the broader point that antithrombotic strategy, valve biology, and long-term outcomes cannot be separated too neatly.

Forrest and colleagues reported comparable rates of all-cause mortality or disabling stroke at 5 years in the Evolut Low Risk trial (18). Vekstein and colleagues, using the Society of Thoracic Surgeons/American College of Cardiology Transcatheter Valve Therapy (STS/ACC TVT) Registry, showed excellent real-world outcomes in trial-eligible low-risk patients, though randomized trial populations remain more selected (19). All of that supports confidence in the first decade. None of it abolishes the need for lifetime planning.

The guideline environment reflects this transition. The 2020 American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association (ACC/AHA) valve guidelines and the 2025 European Society of Cardiology/European Association for Cardio-Thoracic Surgery (ESC/EACTS) valve guidelines emphasize age, life expectancy, anatomy, patient preference, and Heart Team judgment when choosing between TAVR and SAVR (20,21). That is exactly right. Durability is not only a device property; it is a time-horizon problem. A 73-year-old patient and a 58-year-old patient may both want a less invasive therapy, but they do not bring the same biological clock, coronary-access future, redo-valve options, or tolerance for late uncertainty. Furthermore the outcome of TAVR for young patients is still limited and a work in progress (22).

The conclusion, then, should be confident but not triumphant. PARTNER 3 has given the field an important, reassuring, randomized 7-year signal. Low-risk TAVR in selected septuagenarians has earned a credible first-decade durability story and a legitimate place beside surgery. What remains unsettled is not whether TAVR works; it does. The unsettled question is how far first-decade reassurance can be extended into second-decade trust, and for which patients. The next phase of the field will be decided less by who wins the first 30 days and more by whether transcatheter leaflets remain biologically quiet, hemodynamically forgiving, and strategically useful when the first valve becomes the platform for the second. If TAVR ultimately proves truly competitive in the second decade for patients above 70 years, it will not be because it was less invasive. It will be because it behaved enough like a good surgical tissue valve when time finally had its say.
