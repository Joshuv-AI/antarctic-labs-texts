Lipid metabolism related inflammation modulates ocular growth in a high-fat diet guinea pig model
Authors: Xiaoran Zhang, Yamin Chen, Kailei Wang, Ning Zhang, Zichen Luan, Rui Hao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMyopia has become a major global public health concern, with increasing evidence linking diet and inflammation to its development. This study aimed to investigate the effects of a high-fat diet (HFD) on refractive development and myopia progression in guinea pigs.MethodsThirty-two 3-week-old pigmented guinea pigs were randomly assigned to four groups: Control, HFD, lens-induced myopia (LIM), and lens-induced myopia with high-fat diet (LIM + HFD) group. The HFD group was fed a high fat diet for 4 weeks, while myopia was induced in the right eye of LIM and LIM + HFD groups using a -10D lens. Ocular parameters, including refractive error (RE), axial length (AL), retinal thickness (RT), choroidal thickness (ChT), and choroidal blood perfusion (ChBP), were measured at baseline, 2 and 4 weeks. Serum lipids (TC, TG, HDL, LDL) were assessed using an automated biochemical analyzer and serum and retinal levels of interleukin-1β (IL-1β), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and nuclear factor kappa-B (NF-kB) were evaluated by ELISA and qPCR at baseline and 4 weeks.ResultsAfter 4 weeks, HFD and LIM + HFD groups developed dyslipidemia with elevated TC, TG, and LDL (P < 0.01). Compared with controls, the HFD group showed decreased RE, increased AL, and reduced RT, ChT, and ChBP (P < 0.0001), indicating greater susceptibility to myopia. In lens-wearing eyes, LIM + HFD did not exacerbate myopic progression compared with LIM (P > 0.05). However, in fellow eyes, LIM + HFD induced HFD-like structural and functional changes, significantly different from LIM alone (P < 0.001). Correlation analysis further demonstrated that TC and LDL levels were positively associated with AL and negatively correlated with ChT and ChBP (all P < 0.05), whereas TG showed no significant association with ocular parameters, supporting a link between metabolic dysregulation and ocular structural remodeling. ELISA and qPCR revealed elevated IL-1β, IL-6, and NF-kB in HFD and LIM groups, with the highest levels in LIM + HFD.ConclusionsA 4-week HFD increased susceptibility to myopia during normal refractive development but did not worsen lens-induced myopia. HFD was associated with enhanced systemic and retinal inflammation, suggesting a potential link between diet-induced inflammation and increased myopia susceptibility.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-026-07977-7.Keywords: High fat diet, Inflammation, Myopia, Guinea pigs, OCT

Introduction

In recent years, myopia has become a significant global public health concern, posing a significant threat to visual health. By 2050, it is projected that the global myopic population will reach 5 billion [1]. In East Asia, the prevalence of myopia among adolescents has exceeded 80%, with approximately 20% of these cases progressing to high myopia [2, 3]. Myopia not only compromises visual quality but also increases the risk of severe complications, including macular degeneration, glaucoma, and retinal detachment, all of which can lead to irreversible vision loss [3]. Consequently, understanding of the mechanisms underlying myopia development and identifying effective preventive and therapeutic strategies have become critical research priorities in visual health.

The development of myopia is generally considered to result from the interaction between genetic predisposition and environmental factors. Reduced outdoors activity and increased near work are believed to disrupt retinal defocus signaling, thereby triggering myopia onset [4, 5]. However, with the shift in modern lifestyle, increasing attention is being paid to the potential relationship between diet and myopia, with several studies emerging to explore this association.

Certain nutrients have been suggested to influence myopia progression. For instance, a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled clinical trial reported that children receiving daily crocetin supplementation for 24 weeks exhibited significantly less myopic progression compared to the placebo group [6]. Additionally, both animal and human studies have demonstrated that omega-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids, such as docosahexaenoic acid (DHA) and eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA), can slow myopia progression in experimental models and improve choroidal blood flow compromised by prolonged near work [7]. Lutein, a carotenoid with antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties, has been shown to modulate the intraocular environment, protect retinal cells, and support visual development, potentially attenuating myopia progression [8]. Recent studies have also indicated that a high-sugar diet may promote myopia onset and progression in guinea pigs by enhancing scleral glycolysis and lactate production [9]. In addition to carbohydrate, high-fat diets have also attracted attention. Several large-scale epidemiological studies have identified a significant association between obesity and high myopia in children and adolescents, particularly among females. Obese children are three to five times more likely to develop high myopia compared to their normal-weight peers [10–12], suggesting that high-fat diets—by increasing the risk of obesity—may directly or indirectly contribute to myopia onset and progression.

A high fat diet usually refers to a dietary pattern in which fat accounts for more than 30% of total energy intake and is a major factor in metabolic disorders [13, 14]. Long-term consumption of a high-fat diet can induce systemic diseases through various mechanisms, such as insulin resistance and type 2 diabetes [15, 16]. Beyond overall health, high-fat diets also have detrimental effects on the ocular tissues. Studies have found that high fat diets reduce tear secretion, thereby contributing to the development of dry eye disease [17]. Moreover, it may lead to retinal thinning and compromised retinal function [18]. In recent years, accumulating evidence has highlighted the critical role of inflammation in the onset and progression of myopia. Both clinical observations and animal studies have demonstrated that elevated levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as IL-1β and IL-6, can disrupt scleral extracellular matrix remodeling, thereby promoting axial elongation and myopia progression [19–22]. Meanwhile, NF-kB, a central regulator of inflammatory responses, not only modulates the expression of these cytokines but may also alter the retinal micro environment, further contributing to abnormal refractive development [21, 23, 24]. Notably, high-fat diets have been shown to induce a chronic low-grade inflammatory state, characterized by increased IL-1β and IL-6 expression and activation of the NF-kB signaling pathway [25, 26]. These findings suggest that high-fat diets may promote the release of inflammatory mediators, which in turn drive structural and functional alterations in ocular tissues, ultimately affecting refractive development and facilitating the onset of myopia.

Although the link between high-fat diets and various ocular diseases is becoming increasingly recognized, systematic investigations into their impact on refractive development remain limited. Therefore, exploring the effects of high fat diets on refractive development is of substantial scientific relevance. Guinea pigs share several lipid metabolic characteristics with humans, such as primarily relying on LDL as the main cholesterol carrier. Additionally, guinea pigs profile changes in response to high fat feeding that closely mirror those observed in humans, making them a suitable animal model for studying the effects of high-fat diets on refractive development [27, 28].

In the present study, we established HFD and LIM + HFD guinea pig models to investigate the effects of a high fat diet on normal refractive development and LIM, with a particular focus on the potential role of inflammatory mediators such as IL-1β, IL-6, and NF-kB activation in this process.

Methods

Animals

This study was approved by the Animal Care and Ethics Committee at the Nankai University (Tianjin, China), and the treatment and care of animals were conducted in accordance with the ARVO Statement for the Use of Animals in Ophthalmic and Vision Research. The experiments were conducted under natural daylight conditions, with a 12-hour light/12-hour dark cycle in a controlled environment. The light intensity was set at 300 lx, and the room temperature was maintained at 25℃. Guinea pigs were provided with adequate feed, water and vitamin C daily.

Establishment of LIM models and high fat diet models

Thirty-two healthy pigmented male guinea pigs (3 weeks old, 155–175 g) were randomly assigned to four experimental groups (n = 8 per group): Control, HFD, lens-induced myopia (LIM), and LIM + HFD. All guinea pigs were clinically examined before the experiment to exclude any with cataracts, conjunctivitis, spontaneous myopia, and refractive errors.

The LIM model was established using a -10D lens designed for guinea pigs and manufactured by a 3D printer. The − 10D lens was placed on the right eye, while a plano lens was placed on the left eye as an internal control. All lenses were cleaned and wiped twice daily, with replacements made if visible scratches were detected. No intervention was made in the control group.

The high-fat diet (HFD), custom-formulated by Jiangsu Xie Tong Pharmaceutical Bioengineering Co., Ltd., supplemented standard guinea pig chow with lard, sucrose, and casein, providing 45% energy from fat, 35% from carbohydrates, and 20% from protein. The control group received standard guinea pig chow without additional fat or sucrose supplementation. The guinea pigs in the control group were given ad libitum access to standard chow for 4 weeks. The LIM + HFD group received both lens-induced myopia and high-fat dietary intervention for 4 weeks.

Composition of HFD ingredients

The high-fat diet (HFD) was custom-formulated based on the standard guinea pig chow to induce metabolic dysregulation. The specific composition of the HFD consisted of guinea pig basic feed (48.671%), lard (21.3%), sucrose (5.0%), casein (12.0%), experimental animal premix (2.5%), alfalfa (10.0%), limestone flour (0.5%), and coated vitamin C (0.059%). To ensure the reproducibility of the nutritional intervention, the caloric density was calculated as 4533.33 kcal/kg. Specifically, the diet energy was provided by fats (2040.00 kcal/kg), proteins (906.65 kcal/kg) and carbohydrates (1586.69 kcal/kg). The energy distribution was strategically designed to provide 45.00% of total calories from fat, 20.00% from protein, and 35.00% from carbohydrates. The percentage of fat energy was calculated using the following formula: (grams of fat per kilogram × 9 kcal/g) / total kcal per kilogram. Lard served as the primary fat source, providing a complex profile of fatty acids consisting of approximately 40% saturated fatty acids (SFA) and 60% unsaturated fatty acids (UFA). The control group was maintained on a standard diet with a conventional energy distribution to serve as a metabolic baseline.

Blood collection and serum centrifugation

Ocular blood samples were collected from all guinea pigs before and after 4-week dietary intervention. Animals were fasted for 12 h before sampling, and all procedures were performed at the same time of day to minimize circadian variation. Anesthesia was induced using 5% isoflurane, and blood was collected via orbital venous plexus puncture at the lateral canthus of the right eye using a capillary glass tube (outer diameter: 0.9–1.1 mm) to obtain approximately 0.5 mL of blood. The puncture site was disinfected using an alcohol swab and gatifloxacin ophthalmic solution. To ensure complete clot retraction and maximize serum yield while minimizing the risk of hemolysis, blood samples were stored at 4 °C overnight. The supernatant serum was carefully aspirated using a micropipette and partitioned into specific aliquots to prevent repeated freeze-thaw cycles: 100µL was reserved for blood lipid testing and 450µL was allocated for subsequent enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) to detect inflammatory cytokines. All aliquots were immediately stored at − 80 °C until further analysis.

Lipid level testing

Serum lipid profiles were analyzed using an automated biochemistry analyzer (Model BS-360E, MINDRAY Inc) following standardized protocols. Measurements included total cholesterol (TC), triglyceride (TG), high-density lipoprotein (HDL) and low-density lipoprotein (LDL).

Refraction and biometry measurement

To assess refraction, each eye of the guinea pigs was dilated with 1% compound tropicamide, and after a 15-minute interval, the eyes were examined with a retinoscope (66 Vision Tech Co., Ltd. China). Each eye underwent three measurements, and the average was used to determine the final refractive error (RE).

Axial length (AL) was measured using an A-scan ultrasonography device (MD-1000 A/P, MEDA Co., Ltd., China). Each eye was anesthetized with one drop of 0.5% proparacaine hydrochloride (Alcon Ophthalmic Product Co., Ltd. USA). The probe tip was placed perpendicular to the corneal plane, and ten repeated measurements were taken to minimize the error.

The corneal curvature radius (CCR) was measured using a keratometer (YZ38, 66Vision Tech Co., Ltd., China). A + 8.0D lens was affixed to the anterior surface of the keratometer to facilitate accurate measurements on the guinea pigs’ steep corneas. Each eye underwent three measurements with the keratometer, and the averaged values were multiplied by 0.451 to obtain the final CCR.

Retinal thickness, choroidal thicknesses and choroidal blood perfusion measurement

Retinal and choroidal thicknesses were assessed using a 400 kHz optical coherence tomography angiography (OCTA) instrument (VG200S, Intalight Technology Co., Ltd., China). After pupil dilation with 1% compound tropicamide, the optical coherence tomography (OCT) cross-scan mode was used to center on the optic disc (shown in Fig. 1A). To ensure precise positioning, the software-provided vertical reference line was aligned with the geographical center of the optic disc (Fig. 1B). A 6 × 6 mm field of view, encompassing 512 B-scans, was scanned vertically across the peripapillary region. OCT images were exported for analysis, with the choroidal region delineated based on predefined anatomical boundaries. The retina was defined from the internal limiting membrane to the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) (Fig. 1B, upper blue line), and the choroid was defined as the outer surface of the RPE (Fig. 1B, upper blue line) to the inner surface of the sclera (Fig. 1B, lower blue line).

Image analysis was performed using ImageJ. For topographic consistency, three concentric circles with radii of 1000, 1500, and 2000 μm were manually drawn using the calibrated ‘Oval Selection’ tool, centered strictly on the optic disc. ChT and RT was measured at the intersections of these circles and the anatomical axes to provide an average value. ChBP was assessed by isolating the defined choroidal region and clearing extra-choroidal signals. The RGB images were decomposed using the ‘Color Split’ function, and the green channel was selected for quantification. A standardized global threshold of 1–75 was applied to generate binary masks of motion-positive pixels (Fig. 1E). The total number of motion-positive pixels was calculated using the ‘Measure’ command. All quantification was performed by a single observer masked to the experimental groups to ensure the reliability of the motion-pixel determination.

The content of serum inflammatory factors was detected by ELISA

The serum levels of IL-1β, IL-6, and NF-kB in guinea pigs were measured using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits (Jiubang Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Quanzhou, China), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, serum samples and standards were added to microplate wells pre-coated with capture antibodies specific for guinea pig targets, followed by incubation with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated detection antibodies. After incubation and thorough washing, tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) substrate solution was added for color development. Under the catalysis of HRP, TMB was converted to a blue product, which was subsequently transformed into yellow upon the addition of acid. The optical density was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader, and the concentrations of the samples were calculated based on the standard curve.

Real-time fluorescence qPCR

After the experiment, guinea pigs were euthanized. Eyeballs were enucleated, and an incision was made approximately 2 mm posterior to the limbus to isolate the retinal tissue, which was then preserved at − 80 °C in cryogenic tubes. Total RNA from retinal tissues was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Beijing TransGen Biotech Co., Ltd., China). cDNA was synthesized from total RNA by reverse transcription using a reverse transcription kit (Beijing TransGen Biotech Co., Ltd., China). The mRNA expression levels of IL-1β, IL-6, and NF-kB were detected by quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR). Primer sequences for each gene were designed using DNA Star software (Table 1). Relative expression levels of IL-1β, IL-6, and NF-kB in retinal tissues of guinea pigs were quantified using the 2^-ΔΔCt method.

Statistical analysis

The data of lipid levels, refractive parameters, biometric measurements, and inflammatory cytokine levels were analyzed using two-way repeated-measures ANOVA with time and treatment as factors, followed by Šídák’s multiple comparisons test (GraphPad Prism 9.0). The assumption of sphericity was assessed during repeated-measures analysis, and when violated, the Greenhouse–Geisser correction was applied to adjust the degrees of freedom. Pearson correlation analysis was performed to evaluate the relationships between refraction, AL, RT, ChT, ChBP, TC, TG and LDL. Prior to correlation analysis, the distribution of each variable was assessed using normality tests in GraphPad Prism 9.0. As the data approximately followed normal distributions and demonstrated linear relationships, no data transformation was applied. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Dyslipidemia in guinea pigs caused by HFD

Baseline serum lipid parameters, including total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), high-density lipoprotein (HDL), and low-density lipoprotein (LDL), showed no significant differences among the groups before the experiment (P > 0.05). After 4 weeks of intervention, both the HFD and LIM + HFD groups exhibited pronounced elevations in TC, TG, and LDL compared with the Control and LIM groups (Fig. 2A, B, D), whereas HDL levels showed no significant differences among the groups (Fig. 2C). These findings indicate that high-fat feeding induced clear lipid disturbances and confirm successful establishment of the high-fat diet model.

Increased susceptibility to myopia in the HFD group compared with the control group

Alterations of biological parameters in the control group and HFD group

RE, AL, and CCR were measured in guinea pigs prior to the initiation of the experiment, with no statistically significant differences observed among groups (P > 0.05). After feeding the high fat diet, RE was significantly altered at both the second and fourth weeks compared to the control group (week2: 95%CI: 0.49 to 1.90, P < 0.001, week4: 95%CI: 1.95 to 3.36, P < 0.0001, Fig. 3A). The AL in the HFD group was significantly increased compared with the NC group at the fourth week (95%CI: -0.20 to -0.07, P < 0.0001, Fig. 3B). The changes of RE and AL are shown in the Fig. 3E and F. Although CCR gradually increased with LIM duration, the differences between groups were not statistically significant (Fig. 3C). In addition, in both the HFD and control groups, RE and AL were strongly negatively correlated (r = -0.82, 95%CI: -8.22 to -6.14, P < 0.0001, Fig. 3D), indicating that eyes with higher myopia typically exhibited longer axial lengths.

Alterations of RT, ChT and ChBP in the control group and HFD group

Similarly, RT, ChT, and ChBP were measured by OCT before the start of the experiment and at 2 and 4 weeks thereafter, with no significant baseline differences (P > 0.05). After 2 weeks of high fat feeding, both RT and ChT were slightly but significantly reduced compared with the control group, while ChBP showed a marked decrease (Fig. 4A, B, C). A strong positive correlation was found between ChBP and ChT in all eyes from both the control and HFD groups (r = 0.53, 95%CI: 0.29 to 0.71, P = 0.001, Fig. 4D). ChBP and RE also exhibited a positive correlation, indicating that more hyperopic refractions were associated with higher ChBP values (r = 0.47, 95%CI: 0.22 to 0.67, P < 0.001, Fig. 4E). In addition, both RT (r = 0.52, 95%CI: 0.27 to 0.69, P < 0.001, Fig. 4F) and ChT (r = 0.49, 95%CI: 0.24 to 0.68, P < 0.001, Fig. 4F) were positively correlated with RE.

HFD did not induce significant alterations in myopia progression in the LIM-eye

Alterations of biological parameters in the LIM group and LIM + HFD group

Prior to the experimental intervention, baseline measurements of RE, AL, and CCR were performed in all guinea pigs, and no statistically significant differences were observed among the groups (P > 0.05). After two weeks of high fat diet feeding and lens induction, a slight but significant difference in AL was detected between the right and left eyes in the LIM group (Fig. 5B; Table 2). In contrast, the LIM + HFD group showed no significant interocular difference in AL, and neither RE nor CCR differed significantly among the groups. However, the magnitude of AL change at week 2 was slightly greater in the LIM group than in the LIM + HFD group (Fig. 5F). After four weeks of intervention, both RE (Fig. 5A; Table 2) and the change in refraction (Fig. 5E) showed significant differences between the LIM and LIM + HFD groups. Meanwhile, AL in both LIM and LIM + HFD eyes increased, but the difference between them was not statistically significant (P > 0.05). Notably, a significant difference emerged in their fellow eyes, consistent with the trend observed in the change of AL (Fig. 5B, F; Table 2). CCR remained comparable across groups, although a gradual increase was observed with prolonged LIM duration (P > 0.05). Furthermore, in both the LIM and LIM + HFD groups, RE was strongly negatively correlated with AL (r = − 0.88, 95%CI: -0.93 to -0.82, P < 0.0001, Fig. 5D), indicating that eyes with higher degrees of myopia tended to have longer axial lengths.

Alterations of RT, ChT and ChBP in LIM group and LIM + HFD group

RT, ChT, and ChBP were measured using OCT before the start of the experiment, and no significant differences were observed among the groups (P > 0.05). After two weeks of high fat diet feeding, the RT of LIM + HFD-Fellow eyes were thinner than that of the LIM-Fellow eyes (Fig. 6A; Table 2). Changes in ChT were more pronounced in all groups. Specifically, the LIM eyes exhibited reduced ChT compared with their fellow eyes after two weeks of myopia induction, and a similar trend was observed in the LIM + HFD group (Fig. 6B; Table 2). Regarding ChBP, the LIM eyes and LIM + HFD-Fellow eyes showed an opposite trend relative to the LIM-Fellow eyes, with significant differences detected (Fig. 6C; Table 2). After four weeks of HFD feeding, the overall trends in RT and ChT changes remained consistent across groups, although the magnitude varied. The LIM + HFD group showed lower RT and ChT values compared with the LIM group, but the differences were not statistically significant (P > 0.05). Correlation of systemic lipid metabolism with ocular structural parameters

To evaluate the potential association between metabolic dysregulation and myopic changes, Pearson correlation analysis was performed using data from all animals at the 4-week endpoint. A correlation heatmap summarized the overall relationship between lipid profiles and ocular biometry (Fig. 8J).

The analysis revealed that systemic LDL and TC levels were significantly associated with ocular structural remodeling. Specifically, TC was significantly correlated with AL (r = 0.46, 95%CI: 0.13 to 0.70, P < 0.05, Fig. 7A), ChT (r = -0.49, 95%CI: -0.71 to -0.16, P < 0.05, Fig. 7B), and ChBP (r = -0.41, 95%CI: -0.67 to -0.07, P < 0.05; Fig. 7C). Similarly, LDL exhibited a robust positive correlation with AL (r = 0.53, 95%CI: 0.23 to 0.74, P < 0.01, Fig. 7G) and strong negative correlations with ChT (r = -0.51, 95%CI: -0.73 to -0.19, P < 0.01, Fig. 7H) and ChBP (r = -0.48, 95%CI: -0.71 to -0.15, P < 0.01, Fig. 7I). Interestingly, no significant correlations were observed between serum TG levels and any ocular parameters (AL, ChT, or ChBP; all P > 0.05, Fig. 7D-F), suggesting that TC and LDL, rather than TG, may play more predominant roles in the HFD-induced ocular changes.

Validation of internal reference gene stability

To verify the suitability of GAPDH as an internal reference gene for retinal tissue under the present experimental conditions, its Cq values were analyzed across all four experimental groups. The mean Cq values were 14.16 ± 0.05 in the Control group, 14.23 ± 0.04 in the HFD group, 14.22 ± 0.04 in the LIM group, and 14.23 ± 0.04 in the LIM + HFD group, demonstrating minimal variation among groups.

Ordinary one-way ANOVA revealed no statistically significant differences in GAPDH expression among the groups (F [3, 8] = 1.759, P = 0.2325). Furthermore, the Brown-Forsythe test confirmed homogeneity of variance (P = 0.9736). These results indicate that GAPDH expression remained stable across experimental conditions and supports its use as a reliable internal reference gene in this study.

HFD and LIM elevate systemic inflammatory cytokine levels

At the end of the 4-week experiment, serum levels of inflammatory cytokines showed significant alterations among the four groups. For IL-1β, the HFD, LIM, and LIM + HFD groups all exhibited markedly higher concentrations than the Control group, with no significant differences among the three experimental groups (Fig. 8A). IL-6 levels were also significantly elevated in the HFD, LIM, and LIM + HFD groups relative to the Control group, and the LIM + HFD group displayed a further significant increase compared with the LIM group (Fig. 8B). Similarly, serum NF-kB levels were significantly higher in the HFD and LIM + HFD groups than in the Control group, and were further elevated in the LIM + HFD group compared with both the HFD and LIM groups (Fig. 8C).

Collectively, these results indicate that both high-fat diet and lens-induced myopia independently activate systemic inflammatory responses, and their combination exerts a synergistic effect, particularly on NF-kB signaling.

Discussion

This study demonstrates that short-term exposure to a high-fat diet (HFD) is sufficient to induce systemic dyslipidemia and low-grade inflammation, disrupt normal emmetropization, and increase susceptibility to myopia-related structural changes in guinea pigs. Importantly, these effects occurred in the absence of visual deprivation or optical defocus, indicating that metabolic inflammation alone can modulate ocular growth during critical postnatal developmental windows. These findings expand the current understanding of myopia pathogenesis by identifying diet-induced metabolic dysfunction as a systemic, non-visual contributor to refractive development, with potential relevance to the parallel rise of pediatric obesity and myopia worldwide.

In this study, guinea pigs fed a high-fat diet supplying 45% of total energy from fat for 4 weeks exhibited significantly elevated serum TC, TG, and LDL levels compared with controls, consistent with previous reports of diet-induced dyslipidemia in guinea pigs [29, 30]. In contrast, high-density lipoprotein (HDL) levels remained unchanged. This pattern likely reflects species-specific lipid metabolism rather than an absence of metabolic disturbance [31–34].

This stability of HDL levels may reflect the unique species-specific lipid metabolism of guinea pigs. Unlike commonly used murine models, which rely predominantly on HDL as the main cholesterol carrier [35, 36], guinea pigs, similar to humans, exhibit high CETP (cholesteryl ester transfer protein) activity and primarily transport cholesterol via LDL [37, 38]. Moreover, guinea pigs naturally have low baseline HDL concentrations, and dietary interventions often exert minimal effect on HDL levels, with only intensive exercise reported to induce significant increases [27, 39]. Therefore, the selective elevation of LDL and TC without a corresponding change in HDL observed here further supports the suitability of the guinea pig as a translational model for human-like dyslipidemia and its associated ocular complications.

Pediatric obesity is increasingly recognized as a state of chronic low-grade systemic inflammation, often referred to as metabolic inflammation, characterized by dyslipidemia and elevated circulating pro-inflammatory cytokines [40–42]. Clinical studies have shown that lipid abnormalities and inflammatory burden are closely linked to metabolic complications in obese children [42, 43]. The metabolic phenotype induced by HFD in our model-elevated TC, TG and LDL levels accompanied by increased inflammatory markers-therefore recapitulates key features of pediatric obesity-related inflammatory, supporting the translational relevance of this experimental paradigm.

We further demonstrate that HFD exposure significantly disrupts normal emmetropization, manifested as a reduction in hyperopic reserve and excessive axial elongation. These refractive and structural changes were accompanied by retinal and choroidal thinning as well as reduced choroidal blood perfusion, consistent with prior evidence linking impaired ocular perfusion to accelerated axial growth [44–46]. Notably, these myopic shifts occurred independently of visual manipulation, challenging the traditional paradigm that emphasizes visual environmental factors as the dominant drivers of myopia. Instead, our findings highlight systemic metabolic dysfunction as an independent regulator of ocular development during early life.

From a clinical perspective, although the magnitude of refractive change observed in animal models cannot be directly extrapolated to humans, epidemiological studies indicates that once myopia develops, children typically experience progression rates of approximately − 0.30 to − 0.70 diopters per year [47, 48], with faster progression observed in younger children aged 6–12 years, and those with earlier onset or higher baseline myopia [49]. In this context, metabolic-driven reductions in hyperopic reserve or acceleration of axial elongation could plausibly increase the risk of myopia onset and subsequent progression. While animal models do not quantitatively replicate human refractive trajectories, they provide essential mechanistic insight into biologically meaningful modulators of refractive development. refractive development.

Interestingly, HFD exposure did not further aggravate LIM in the treated eye but significantly influenced the contralateral untreated eye, suggesting differential roles of systemic and local regulatory in ocular growth. HFD is known to induce chronic low-grade systemic inflammation characterized by increased circulating cytokines, oxidative stress, and metabolic dysregulation [50, 51]. These systemic alterations may affect ocular tissues by activating inflammatory pathways that promote scleral extracellular matrix remodeling, a key process in axial elongation [52, 53]. Therefore, HFD may provide a systemic pro-myopic background that influences refractive development. However, ocular growth is primarily regulated by local retinal visual feedback. Visual defocus triggers retina-to-sclera signaling cascades that directly control axial elongation and are largely independent of systemic physiological changes [54, 55]. In the LIM eye, the strong defocus stimulus likely acts as the dominant driver of ocular growth [56], potentially masking additional modulatory effects induced by systemic inflammation. In contrast, the contralateral eye may be more susceptible to systemic metabolic and inflammatory alterations. These findings support a dual regulatory model in which local visual signaling predominates in experimentally induced myopia, while systemic inflammation acts as a secondary modulator of refractive development, particularly in untreated eyes.

Correlation analysis further highlighted a potential cholesterol-mediated pathogenic axis underlying HFD-induced ocular remodeling. Serum TC and LDL levels were significantly correlated with AL, ChT, and ChBP, whereas TG showed no such associations (all P > 0.05). While TG primarily reflects short-term dietary energy storage [57], LDL is a major contributor to endothelial dysfunction [58]. Elevated LDL may therefore promote choroidal microvascular impairment, leading to hypoxia and subsequent structural remodeling. However, it is important to emphasize that correlation analyses identify statistical associations rather than causal relationships. Although these findings suggest mechanistic links between cholesterol metabolism, inflammation, and ocular remodeling, causal pathways require confirmation through targeted interventional or mechanistic studies.

At the molecular level, the observed elevated IL-1β, IL-6, and NF-kB in both serum and retinal tissue provide a molecular bridge between metabolic inflammation and ocular growth. Inflammatory cytokines may increase myopia susceptibility through at least two specific targets: the choroidal vasculature and the scleral extracellular matrix. Pro-inflammatory mediators such as TNF-α and IL-6 can induce endothelial dysfunction [59, 60], consistent with the reduced ChBP observed in this study. In parallel, systemic inflammation is known to activate matrix metalloproteinases, particularly MMP-2, in the sclera [52], accelerating collagen degradation and ECM remodeling, thereby weakening scleral biomechanical integrity and facilitating axial elongation.

The markedly elevated cytokine levels observed in the LIM + HFD group further suggest a potential synergistic interaction between systemic metabolic inflammation and locally induced inflammatory signaling, possibly mediated by NF-kB-related pathways. Although these enhanced inflammatory responses did not translate into additional refractive or structural changes within the experimental timeframe, they may represent an early sensitization state precedingdetectable morphological alterations.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the 4-week HFD exposure represents a relatively short-term metabolic challenge and may not fully capture the cumulative effects of chronic metabolic inflammation on long-term refractive outcomes. Second, the study was conducted exclusively in young guinea pigs (3 weeks old), and age-dependent susceptibility to metabolic influences on ocular growth remains to be determined. Third, only male guinea pigs were included. Given established sex differences in metabolic regulation and inflammatory responses [61, 62], these findings may not fully generalize to females. Future studies incorporating longer-term interventions, multiple developmental stages, both sexes, and targeted anti-inflammatory or dietary strategies will be essential to clarify causal relationships and to evaluate potential preventive approaches.

From a preventive medicine and public health perspective, the coexistence of increasing childhood obesity and the global rise in myopia prevalence raises important concerns regarding potential shared pathogenic pathways. Our findings suggest that metabolic health and dietary patterns may influence ocular development through inflammatory and vascular mechanisms. These results highlight the possibility that early lifestyle interventions, including dietary modification and metabolic control, could serve as complementary strategies in myopia prevention programs. Integrating metabolic health management into vision care strategies may provide additional benefits in reducing the long-term burden of myopia-related visual impairment.

Overall, this study provides evidence that the HFD-induced dyslipidemia and metabolic inflammation increase susceptibility to myopia through cholesterol associated inflammatory pathways. These findings offer new insights into the systemic regulation of refractive development and underscore the importance of considering metabolic health as a modifiable factor in myopia prevention and early intervention.
