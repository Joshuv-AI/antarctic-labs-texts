Influence of graphene quantum dots on the aging properties of polyvinyl butyral as a relics adhesive
Authors: Xuwen Xie, Siyu Zhang, Zichen Zhao, Shulei Li, Qing Niu, Chen Wu, Xin Liu, Yan Wu, Xing Zhao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Polyvinyl butyral (PVB) is commonly used as an adhesive for cultural relics. However, PVB suffers from poor stability, yellowing, and decreased adhesion performance after aging. Here, graphene quantum dots (GQDs) were used as stabiliser materials to modify PVB and obtain GQDs@PVB that was improved in anti-UV/thermal aging. The yellowness index, adhesive strength, chemical structure, and thermal properties of PVB and GQDs@PVB, before and after aging, are characterized using a benchtop spectrophotometer, universal testing machine, Fourier-transform infrared spectrometer, and simultaneous thermal analyzer. The results indicate that during the aging process, GQDs@PVB shows a lower yellowness index and higher adhesive strength than that of PVB consistently. Meanwhile, it exhibits a lower intensity of carbonyl peaks generated by thermal aging with a thermal decomposition temperature increased by 19 °C. The addition of GQDs improves the photothermal stability of PVB and holds promise for the stability research of materials in relics conservation.

Introduction

Polyvinyl butyral (PVB) is a condensation product obtained by the reaction of polyvinyl acetate (PVAc) or polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) with butyraldehyde under catalysis, as shown in Fig. 1 with its chemical molecular formula. Polyvinyl butyral is a commonly used adhesive in the field of relics conservation1,2. It is soluble in alcohols, ethers, and esters, exhibiting high transparency, impact resistance, tensile strength, and good adhesive properties3–5. Due to its moderate post-curing adhesive strength and cost-effectiveness, PVB has been widely used in bonding fragile ceramic artifacts and small ceramic items, as well as in the process of detaching wall paintings.

However, the hydroxyl, carbonyl, and acetal ring groups present in PVB exhibit poor stability6, which can lead to phenomena such as yellowing and brittleness upon aging7, resulting in deteriorative damage to conserved relics. To address the application challenges of PVB materials in cultural heritage preservation, modification can be achieved by introducing nano-particle stabilizers into PVB materials to prepare organic-inorganic nano-composites. Danial Harandi et al.8 incorporated nanocrystalline cellulose (CNCs) and zinc oxide nanoparticles into a PVB solution to produce PVB/CNCs/ZnO nano-composite materials, significantly enhancing their photo-stability and surface hydrophobicity. Huaqing Liu et al.9 modified PVB using cinnamic acid-functionalized ZnO nanoparticles (CNA-ZnO), resulting in PVB/CNA-ZnO composite materials. These composites maintain good transparency while enhancing UV shielding capability and tensile strength, although exhibiting a slight decrease in thermal stability.

Graphene has been used in cultural heritage conservation for applications such as nanomaterials、surface coatings and sensor fabrication10–13. The high specific surface area and unique two-dimensional structure of graphene make it an excellent support for the preparation of nanomaterials. By encapsulating substances with protective properties on the surface of graphene, the protective effect can be enhanced while minimising potential damage during use. GQDs are zero-dimensional graphene materials with sizes smaller than 100 nm and thicknesses within 10 layers. While retaining the highly crystalline structure of single or few-layer graphene, they exhibit stronger quantum confinement effects and edge effects14,15. GQDs demonstrate excellent ultraviolet absorption capabilities, biocompatibility, water dispersibility, and chemical stability. Additionally, due to their non-toxicity and biological inertness, they are considered environmentally friendly materials16,17. GQDs can be synthesized through various methods such as hydrothermal synthesis, microwave-assisted cleavage, and are widely used in areas including fluorescent polymers, UV-blocking coatings, bioimaging, photocatalysis, and sensors18–20. Research on GQDs/polyimide composite films indicates that GQDs can significantly improve the stability of the substrate21.

In this study, GQDs were used as stabiliser materials to modify PVB and obtain GQDs@PVB. PVB and GQDs@PVB were subjected to UV aging and thermal aging tests to investigate the effect of GQDs on the aging behaviour of PVB and to explore the aging mechanism of GQDs@PVB. This research provides new insights into the stability study of materials for relics conservation.

Preparation of samples

Preparation of GQDs@PVB samples

The fabrication process of GQDs@PVB is shown in Fig. 2. Add 1.5 mg of GQDs aqueous solution to a centrifuge tube, supplement with anhydrous ethanol to a solvent mass of 7 g, mix thoroughly, and sonicate in ice water for 40 min. Weigh 3 g of PVB powder, add it to the mixed solvent after ultrasonic dispersion, sonicate until no significant bubbles are seen in the adhesive (The loading amount of GQDs is 0.05%). Transfer 2.5 mL of the adhesive to the centrifuge tube, dissolve in anhydrous ethanol to a final volume of 15 mL, and sonicate in ice water for 20 min. Place 5 mL of the solution in a 25 × 75 mm silicone paper box and let it cure at room temperature for 24 h.

Preparation of GQDs@PVB adhesive strength test samples

Ceramic tiles are used as simulated specimens to test the adhesive strength of the adhesive. The preparation of these simulated specimens and the subsequent mechanical performance testing can be followed according to the guidelines of ISO 4587:2003, IDT. The specific methods and test conditions were as follows: The adhesive was extracted using a syringe, dripped onto a ceramic plate, and then uniformly coated on one side of a 100 × 25 × 3 mm ceramic plate using a coating bar with a thickness of 200 μm. The two ceramic plates were overlapped, pressed together, fixed, and left to cure. After curing, the length and width of the overlap area were measured to calculate the bonding area.

Preparation of infrared spectroscopy samples

Grind potassium bromide (KBr) powder in a mortar and then press it into a mold. Take a small amount of GQDs@PVB material, add anhydrous ethanol, prepare a 5% solution, sonicate to disperse, drop it onto the surface of the KBr pellet, and allow the solution to fully cure.

Aging methods

UV aging

The GQDs@PVB samples were subjected to accelerated aging in a UV aging chamber. The distance between the sample surface and the bottom of the lamp tube was set at 20 cm. The UV irradiance of the UVC lamp used was 29.9 ± 0.4 W/m².

Thermal aging

The GQDs@PVB samples were placed in a forced-air drying oven and subjected to thermal aging at a temperature of 130 °C.

The impact of GQDs on PVB photodegradation

When the molecules or groups that constitute polymer materials absorb light energy, they enter an excited state. This process leads to the partial breakage of some molecular chains. The resulting free radicals and unstable excited state molecules further react with hydrogen peroxide and carbonyl groups generated through oxidation reactions, triggering photochemical reactions. In this process, polymer materials undergo repeated breakages and oxygen absorption, leading to an increasing number of photosensitive sites. This results in the automatic oxidation of polymers, which is the aging process23. Among the numerous factors influencing the photoaging of polymer materials, ultraviolet (UV) light is one of the primary causes. Although UV light accounts for only 4% of sunlight in nature, due to its shorter wavelength and higher energy, its radiation energy can reach 7–8% of the total solar radiation energy. UV light can damage side chain groups, leading to the decomposition of the polymer material structure and a decline in material properties24. The UVC band, with its shortest wavelength (254 nm used in this study) and highest energy, can effectively break most organic material chains directly. Therefore, it is utilized in laboratory accelerated aging experiments to provide an efficient testing environment for the rapid screening and evaluation of material photostability within a limited time frame.

Yellowness index

Yellowness is a manifestation of aging degradation in polymeric materials25. Yellowness of adhesives can adversely affect the original appearance of cultural relics, which is contrary to the principles of relics conservation. By observing Fig. 4a, it can be seen that the yellowness index of the PVB material continuously increases with the extension of time during 200 h of UV aging. Simultaneously, the material becomes brittle. At the 200th hour of UV aging, the PVB film broke, rendering it impossible to continue measuring the chromaticity. Under the influence of ultraviolet light and oxygen, the aging process of PVB is primarily characterized by crosslinking reactions. Crosslinking reactions render PVB brittle, ultimately leading to film fracture26,27. In the ultraviolet aging test, the yellowness index of GQDs@PVB materials remains lower than that of PVB materials, with only a slight change. The PVB film does not break after 200 h. In Fig. 4b, the PVB film without the addition of GQDs exhibits good transmittance in both UV and visible light regions. After the addition of GQDs, the transmittance of PVB at 200–380 nm decreases. Within the range of 200–400 nm, the UV transmittance of PVB is 60.50%, while that of GQDs@PVB is 56.51%, showing a decrease of 6.60%. This decrease is attributed to graphene’s ability to absorb UV light, acting as a shield against UV radiation28–31, thus preventing polymer aging. The introduction of GQDs absorbs a portion of the UV light. The incorporation of GQDs absorbs some UV light, delaying crosslinking-induced yellowing and fragmentation of materials due to photooxidation and maintaining the performance of PVB. In the visible light region, GQDs@PVB exhibits higher light transmittance compared to PVB. In the field of cultural heritage preservation, this difference does not affect the observation of surface information on cultural relics.

The impact of GQDs on the thermal aging of PVB

As an adhesive for relics, PVB was primarily affected by light exposure at the bonded joints. However, thermal aging has an extensive and unavoidable effect on its properties. To better understand the effects of thermal aging, infrared spectroscopy tests were conducted on PVB and GQDs@PVB before and after thermal aging. The detailed results and analysis of these tests are presented in Sect. Infrared spectroscopy.

Yellowness index

Figure 6 shows the yellowness index changes of PVB and GQDs@PVB during thermal oxygen aging process. As the thermal aging time increased, the yellowness index of both material films showed an increasing trend. Before 120 min of thermal aging, the yellowness index of the PVB film had already shown significant changes, while the yellowness index of the GQDs@PVB film remained almost unchanged. After 120 min, the yellowness index of the GQDs@PVB film gradually increased, and at around 180 min, it began to increase rapidly, consistently showing smaller variations compared to the PVB. Under the influence of heat and oxygen, PVB undergoes hydroxyl oxidation, ester decomposition, and the thermal ring-opening of acetal rings to generate alkoxyl radicals, which further lead to the formation of ketones, aldehydes, and these newly formed chromophoric groups such as carbonyl groups cause the yellowing of PVB32. The presence of unpaired electrons in GQDs due to defects, alongside their π-conjugated properties, enhances their electron storage and charge transfer capabilities, giving them radical scavenging properties33–35. During the thermal aging process of GQDs@PVB, GQDs may act as radical scavengers, capturing reactive oxygen species (ROS) that attack PVB molecular chains. This reduces the formation of alkoxy radicals generated due to ROS attacks, thereby delaying the production of chromophoric groups.

Figure 8 shows the infrared spectra of PVB and GQDs@PVB. In the FTIR spectrum of PVB, the characteristic absorption peak at 3460 cm-1 corresponding to -OH shifts to a lower wavenumber of 3440 cm-1 after the addition of GQDs, and the intensity of the peak also increases. Thermogravimetric analysis

Due to the presence of flexible molecular chains, PVB exhibited poor heat resistance. Its thermal decomposition mainly occured in the range of 300–450 °C37. From Fig. 10a, it was observed that both GQDs@PVB and PVB undergo thermal decomposition in the range of 300–450 °C. They showed a similar decomposition trend characterised by a single step reaction. This indicated that the mass loss in the composite is mainly due to the thermal decomposition of PVB. According to the thermogravimetric analysis results, the initial decomposition temperature of PVB material was 359 °C, while that of GQDs@PVB was 378 °C. During the entire decomposition process, GQDs@PVB exhibited a higher mass retention rate and a slower mass loss rate compared to PVB, indicating the enhanced thermal stability provided by the incorporation of GQDs into the PVB matrix. In Fig. 10b it was observed that both materials exhibit small weight loss peaks attributed to moisture removal between 60 and 70 °C. During this stage, GQDs@PVB demonstrated a lower mass loss rate, and the peak was shifted towards higher temperatures. In the range of 300–450 °C, both materials undergo rapid thermal decomposition, and a prominent peak was observed around 400 °C, which was associated with the decomposition of butyraldehyde groups38–41. From Fig. 10c, it could be seen that GQDs@PVB reached its maximum weight loss rate at around 412 °C, while PVB exhibited a peak near 406 °C. At this stage, PVB undergoes complete decomposition at approximately 433 °C, whereas GQDs@PVB reached its complete decomposition temperature at 511 °C. After 450 °C, the weight loss rates of both materials approached zero, indicating that the materials undergo slow decomposition and carbonization of residual substances after rapid pyrolysis. Combining thermal analysis of the two materials reveals that GQDs enhance the thermal stability of PVB material. On one hand, van der Waals forces and hydrogen bonding interactions between PVB and GQDs reduce polymer chain mobility at high temperatures, improving thermal stability. On the other hand, GQDs may act as radical scavengers, delaying degradation temperatures. Additionally, incorporating GQDs into PVB can achieve a “tortuous path” effect, effectively preventing oxygen permeation42. This delays the decomposition rate of the base PVB material, shifting the decomposition temperature of PVB towards higher values.

Conclusions

In this study, GQDs were used to modify PVB (a commonly used adhesive for ceramic artefacts) to produce GQDs@PVB composites. We characterized the adhesive strength and yellowness index of PVB and GQDs@PVB during UV aging and thermal aging processes, as well as the chemical structure of both materials during thermal aging. The results showed that during UV aging, the yellowness index change of GQDs@PVB remained consistently lower than that of PVB, with no significant differences observed in the adhesive strength of the two materials over the aging time. In the thermal aging process, the yellowness index change of GQDs@PVB remained consistently lower than that of PVB, and differences in adhesive strength were noted, with GQDs@PVB exhibiting more stable adhesive strength compared to PVB. The carbonyl index change rate of PVB during thermal aging was 18.011, while that of GQDs@PVB was 12.691, indicating that PVB produced more degradation products within the same thermal aging time. In thermogravimetric analysis, the initial decomposition temperature of GQDs@PVB was 19 °C higher than that of PVB, and throughout the decomposition process, the mass retention rate of GQDs@PVB exceeded that of PVB material. The incorporation of GQDs improved the UV and thermal stability of PVB.

Given the limitations of artificial aging in evaluating material aging properties, further studies will examine performance changes under natural aging conditions.

Future perspectives

Based on the results obtained in this study, future research will focus on exploring the performance of GQDs@PVB under natural aging conditions. This work is crucial for confirming the long-term durability of materials in real environments and is the foundation for predicting their service life and ultimately achieving practical applications.
