Nitroxoline as an antimicrobial synergist reverses colistin resistance in multidrug-resistant Escherichia coli
Authors: Hao Wang, Jie He, Hao Li, Chengming Li, Muhammad Inam, Jianfeng Wang, Huifeng Dong, Dingkuo Liu, Fang Liu, Jingrui Chen, Yu Jia, Yunhang Gao, Lingcong Kong, Hongxia Ma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryColistin is a crucial drug for treating multidrug-resistant (MDR) Escherichia coli infections. The emergence and bacterial transmission of the colistin-resistant gene mcr-1 have substantially diminished its therapeutic efficacy. The development of colistin enhancers represents an effective strategy to counteract this resistance. This study demonstrates that the FDA-approved drug nitroxoline (NTX) synergistically enhances the antibacterial activity of colistin against mcr-1-positive MDR E. coli and effectively suppresses the development of colistin resistance. Functional assays and transcriptomic profiling demonstrate that NTX targets MCR-1, thereby potentiating the membrane-disruptive activity of colistin. Moreover, the combined administration of NTX and colistin perturbs bacterial metabolic pathways, provokes oxidative damage, and accelerates bacterial death. Furthermore, in both the Galleria mellonella and mouse peritonitis infection models, NTX improved the therapeutic effect of colistin against E. coli infections. In conclusion, NTX is identified as a promising colistin adjuvant for the treatment of colistin-resistant bacterial infections.Subject areas: Biological sciences, Microbiology, Natural sciences, Pharmacology

Introduction

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) has emerged as a severe threat to global public health.1 According to predictions by the World Health Organization, without effective interventions, the global annual death toll due to drug-resistant bacterial infections could reach as high as 10 million by 2050.2,3 Colistin (COL) is a secondary metabolite produced by Paenibacillus polymyxa that binds to the lipid A component of lipopolysaccharide (LPS), disrupting the bacterial outer membrane and ultimately leading to bacterial cell death.4 Given its potent antimicrobial activity and relatively low resistance rates, colistin is among the last lines of defense in the treatment of multidrug-resistant (MDR) Gram-negative bacteria.5 However, the emergence of mcr-1-resistant gene significantly decreased the clinical efficacy of colistin.6 The phosphoethanolamine transferase encoded by the plasmid-mediated mcr-1 gene reduces the negative charge of lipid A, thereby impairing the affinity of colistin for the bacterial cell membrane and contributing to the development of resistance.7,8 More alarmingly, the rapid spread and global prevalence of the mcr-1 gene have posed a significant challenge to global public health.9 Consequently, there is a pressing need for strategies to tackle the crisis of colistin resistance posed by mcr-1.

The development of antibiotics has progressed at a relatively slow pace.10,11 Compared with the time-consuming and expensive process of antibiotic development, identifying adjuvants from approved compounds that can restore or even enhance the efficacy of colistin is a more efficient and economical strategy.12,13 In recent years, researchers have identified a variety of synergists such as PBT2 [2-(dimethylamino)methyl-5,7-dichloro-8-hydroxyquinoline],14 chelerythrine,15 equisetin,16 and gallium nitrate,17 which can enhance the inhibitory effect of colistin on drug-resistant pathogens. Although considerable research is underway, no colistin adjuvants have yet advanced to clinical application, while the diversity and complexity of resistant pathogens continue to escalate.18 Accordingly, the continued development of colistin potentiators remains imperative for expanding the arsenal of effective therapeutic options.

Nitroxoline (NTX), a clinical drug approved by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA), exhibits multiple biological activities, including antimicrobial, antiviral, and antitumor effects.19,20,21 Since the 1960s, nitroxoline has been utilized as a first-line agent for managing both acute and recurrent urinary tract infections in adults and children across European nations.22,23 Previous studies reveal that its core antimicrobial mechanism involves chelating divalent metal ions to disrupt biofilms, thereby exhibiting significant antibacterial activity against Gram-negative pathogens.24,25,26 Given its significant pharmacological activity and anti-biofilm properties, nitroxoline has considerable potential as an antimicrobial synergist and warrants further investigation. This study systematically evaluated the potential of NTX as an adjuvant to colistin and demonstrated that NTX significantly enhances the antibacterial efficacy of colistin in both in vitro and in vivo settings. Furthermore, the molecular mechanism by which NTX reverses colistin resistance mediated by mcr-1 in Escherichia coli was elucidated. These findings establish a theoretical foundation for the development of inhibitors targeting MCR-1 and propose a strategy for restoring the clinical efficacy of colistin against MDR strains.

Results

NTX enhances the antimicrobial activity of colistin and delays the development of resistance

To evaluate the potential of NTX as an antibiotic potentiator, this study used the broth microdilution method to examine the combined effects of NTX with six antimicrobial agents (ciprofloxacin, meropenem, florfenicol, gentamicin, colistin, and doxycycline) against the MDR E. coli B2 strain. The results showed that the combination of NTX and colistin exhibited the highest synergistic antibacterial activity (Figure 1A), with a fractional inhibitory concentration index (FICI) value of 0.258. When NTX (4 μg/mL, 1/4 MIC) was used, the MIC for colistin decreased from 8 to 0.063 μg/mL, representing a 128-fold reduction, well below the clinical resistance breakpoint for colistin (2 μg/mL, according to CLSI 2021). To further validate the combined bacteriostatic effect of NTX and colistin, we conducted time-kill assays. Neither NTX nor colistin alone was sufficient to suppress the colony formation of E. coli B2. However, when NTX (4 μg/mL) was administered in combination with colistin (2 μg/mL), a pronounced synergistic bacteriostatic effect was observed (Figure 1B).

In subsequent studies, the scope was expanded to include other Gram-negative strains, focusing on investigating the potentiating effect of NTX on colistin resistance and susceptibility, including mcr-1-positive colistin-resistant E. coli and other colistin-sensitive strains. The results indicated that the potentiation of colistin by NTX (with the FICI indicating synergistic or additive effects) was similarly observed in these strains (Figures 1C and 1D). These results suggest that NTX effectively potentiates the antimicrobial activity of colistin against Gram-negative bacteria.

To investigate whether the combination of NTX and colistin prevents the development of resistance, we conducted an in vitro continuous passaging experiment with E. coli B2, treated with either colistin alone or in combination with NTX. The results demonstrated that after 30 passages with colistin alone, the MIC increased 16-fold, whereas the MIC for colistin increased only 2-fold when combined with NTX at the end of the experiment (Figure 1E). This suggests that combination therapy effectively inhibited the development of colistin resistance. The lowest concentration of the drug necessary to stop resistant mutant strains from proliferating is referred to as the MPC and is commonly used to assess the ability of antibiotics to limit the emergence of resistant populations.27 Our experimental results demonstrated that NTX decreased the MPC of colistin in a dose-dependent manner from 64 to 8 μg/mL, representing 1/8 of the original value (Figure 1F). This finding indicates that NTX significantly suppressed the accumulation of resistant mutant subpopulations and restricted the emergence of resistance mutations.

NTX restores the disruptive effect of colistin on the bacterial cell membrane and inhibits biofilm formation

NTX was shown to enhance the antibacterial efficacy of colistin. We further investigated its potential synergistic mechanism. Colistin binds to the bacterial outer membrane, altering membrane permeability and causing the leakage of cellular contents, ultimately leading to cell death.28 Resistance to colistin mainly arises from a decreased affinity between colistin and components of the bacterial outer membrane.29 Considering this, we evaluated whether NTX could intensify the disruptive action of colistin on the bacterial membrane. Bacterial morphological changes were observed using SEM. E. coli B2 treated with NTX or colistin alone exhibited partial membrane damage on the bacterial surface, while the combination treatment resulted in significant morphological changes, including surface collapse and shrinkage, indicating severe membrane damage (Figure 2A).

Additionally, we used the fluorescent dye propidium iodide (PI) to evaluate membrane permeability.30 It was demonstrated that the fluorescence intensity was greatly enhanced by the combination of NTX and colistin. Compared with colistin alone, membrane permeability was significantly enhanced (Figures 2B and 2C). Furthermore, the diffusion and transport functions of cell membranes depend on membrane fluidity.31 Next, Laurdan fluorescent probes were used to measure cell membrane fluidity. Laurdan GP values were negatively correlated with cell membrane fluidity, and the results showed that combined treatment with NTX and colistin significantly increased membrane fluidity (Figures 2D and 2E), further supporting the observation of membrane damage.

Biofilm formation enhances bacterial resistance, thus increasing the difficulty of eradicating bacteria with antimicrobial agents. Through crystal violet staining, we observed that the combination of NTX and colistin significantly inhibited the formation of E. coli B2 biofilm. Compared to colistin alone, the addition of NTX markedly enhanced the inhibitory effect (Figures 2F and 2G). These results indicate that the combination of NTX and colistin not only disrupts the structure and homeostasis of the bacterial cell membrane but also effectively inhibits biofilm formation, thus enhancing antimicrobial activity.

NTX binds to MCR-1 and interferes with the resistance mechanism it mediates

Our results indicate that NTX has a significantly greater synergistic effect on colistin in mcr-1-positive resistant E. coli than in colistin-sensitive strains. We hypothesized that the mechanism by which NTX enhances colistin activity is likely associated with the mcr-1-mediated resistance mechanism. Therefore, we further examined the expression of the mcr-1 gene in E. coli B2 exposed to NTX and colistin using reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR). The findings showed no discernible shift in the expression of the mcr-1 gene at the 6-h time point (Figure 3A), suggesting that NTX likely acts by binding to the catalytic center of MCR-1 and modulating its activity. Surface plasmon resonance (SPR) is a technique that enables real-time, label-free monitoring of intermolecular binding kinetics. In this study, SPR was employed to investigate the interaction between NTX and the MCR-1 protein. The analysis revealed a dissociation equilibrium constant (KD) of 7.6 μM for the NTX-MCR-1 complex, indicating that NTX engages in a specific interaction with MCR-1 and establishes a stable molecular complex (Figures 3B and 3C).

The possible binding mechanism of NTX to MCR-1 was investigated through molecular docking and MD simulations. The stability of the system was evaluated using the root mean square deviation (RMSD).32 During the 100 ns simulation, the protein structures of both models gradually stabilized. Notably, at 60 ns, the RMSD of the MCR-1-NTX complex was markedly lower, suggesting enhanced structural stability of the protein upon binding (Figure 3D). The radius of gyration (RG) reflects the overall compactness of the protein, and the trend was consistent with the RMSD results. The fluctuations in RG gradually leveled off, indicating that the protein gradually adapted to the binding of NTX and eventually reached equilibrium (Figure S1). Additionally, the root mean square fluctuation (RMSF) was employed to quantify local residue-level fluctuations, revealing the flexibility of the protein at various residue positions (Figure 3E). Further analysis of the 70 ns conformation in the MD simulation showed that NTX formed stable interactions with the MCR-1 protein within the cavity of its active site. Stable hydrogen bonds were formed between the hydroxyl group of the small compound and the MCR-1 residues THR-283 and SER-284, while the nitro group formed hydrogen bonds with the TYR-287 residue (Figure 3F).

Using the MM/GBSA method to determine the free energy of binding for the MCR-1-NTX complex, we found that the binding energy of NTX was −17.27 kcal/mol, suggesting a strong binding affinity between NTX and the MCR-1 binding site. Further, residue-level free energy decomposition analysis (Figure 3G) revealed that, throughout the simulation, amino acid residues were crucial for maintaining ligand binding. Specifically, GLY-282, THR-283, SER-284, TYR-287, VAL-303, PRO-481, and ASN-482 primarily stabilized the complex through van der Waals forces and electrostatic interactions. To further validate these findings and identify specific binding sites, we constructed mutant strains. In these mutants, all residues, except for TYR-287, which was mutated to phenylalanine (PHE), were mutated to alanine (ALA) (Figure S2). Subsequently, we determined the level of resistance to colistin in these mutant strains. Resistance levels in the mutant strains remained unchanged compared with the parental strain (Table S1). The checkerboard assay results demonstrated that, in contrast to the wild-type strain, the combination of colistin and NTX showed reduced synergistic efficacy against MCR-1-T283, MCR-1-S284, MCR-1-Y287, and MCR-1-N482 (Figure 3H). In summary, NTX binds to the catalytic center of MCR-1, thereby compromising the resistance mechanism it mediates.

Transcriptomic analysis of NTX combined with colistin

The synergistic effect of NTX and colistin in mcr-1-negative pathogens remains modest, and the underlying mechanisms are yet to be fully understood. To further investigate this issue, we conducted a transcriptomic analysis of E. coli B2 strains treated with either colistin alone or a combination of NTX and colistin for 6 h. Compared to colistin treatment alone, a total of 1165 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were found in the combination group (|log2[fold change]| > 0.58, p < 0.05), with 543 genes upregulated and 622 genes downregulated (Figure 4A).

Gene Ontology (GO) annotation analysis revealed the following major functional categories: biological processes (BPs), which were enriched in cellular processes, metabolic processes, and responses to stimuli. Cellular components (CC): predominantly associated with protein complexes and anatomical components of cells. Molecular functions (MFs) enriched in binding, transport, and catalytic activity (Figure S3). Significant gene enrichment was observed using Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis, which involved the ribosome biosynthesis pathway. Additionally, DEGs were primarily enriched in key metabolic pathways, including the two-component system, oxidative phosphorylation, tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, and purine metabolism (Figure 4B). Additionally, the key inhibitory pathways revealed by the above KEGG enrichment analysis are highly conserved in Gram-negative pathogens.33 Specifically, in the combination treatment group, gene expression involved in the synthesis of ribosomal 30S and 50S subunits, components of the two-component system, and oxidative phosphorylation-related genes was significantly downregulated (Figure 4C). The two-component system regulates outer membrane proteins and adhesion factors associated with biofilm formation, thereby inhibiting biofilm formation.34 These changes are consistent with the previously observed results of biofilm inhibition.

Notably, transcriptomic data revealed that the combination treatment significantly downregulated key genes involved in oxidative phosphorylation, such as adenosine triphosphate (ATP) synthase genes (atpCDEF) and cytochrome oxidase genes (cyoCDE). This suggests a disruption in the bacterial energy generation process. Meanwhile, the TCA cycle plays a central role in bacterial energy metabolism. We observed that the expression of genes involved in the TCA cycle, such as malate dehydrogenase (mdh) and fumarase (fumC), along with genes associated with Trimethylamine N-oxide (TMAO) respiration, were significantly upregulated. This suggests that bacteria may experience an imbalance in energy supply, adapting to fluctuations in energy demands by activating the TCA cycle and TMAO respiration pathways.35 In summary, transcriptomic analyses of E. coli B2 under the combined treatment of NTX and colistin reveal a pervasive suppression of energy metabolism pathways, most notably the TCA cycle and oxidative phosphorylation. These molecular perturbations provide a plausible mechanistic rationale for the observed potentiation of their antibacterial efficacy.

The combination of NTX and colistin exerts antibacterial effects by disrupting bacterial energy metabolism

According to the transcriptomic study, NTX and colistin together dramatically reduced bacterial energy metabolism, particularly affecting ATP synthesis and oxidative phosphorylation pathways. This phenomenon may represent an additional mechanism underlying the enhanced antibacterial activity observed with the combination treatment. To investigate this, we measured intracellular ATP levels in bacteria and found that, compared to colistin treatment alone, the combination treatment significantly reduced ATP levels (Figures 5A and 5B). A decrease in ATP levels typically forces bacteria to accelerate energy metabolism to maintain basic physiological functions, potentially leading to further activation of the TCA cycle.36,37 To test this hypothesis, we measured the NAD+/NADH ratio in E. coli B2. The results demonstrated that combined treatment with NTX and colistin markedly reduced the NAD+/NADH ratio (Figures 5C and 5D). This diminished ratio stimulates the TCA cycle, further supporting the notion of accelerated TCA cycle activity.38

It is noteworthy that enhanced TCA cycle activity is often accompanied by excessive accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS). We further hypothesized that the combination of NTX and colistin could increase ROS generation, exacerbating oxidative damage to bacteria. Experimental results demonstrated that, although NTX alone did not induce ROS accumulation, compared to the group receiving colistin alone, the ROS levels in the combination treatment group were noticeably greater (Figures 5E and 5F). ROS accumulation can cause oxidative damage to proteins, nucleic acids, and lipids within bacterial cells,39 thereby contributing to bacterial death.

By lowering ATP levels, the combination of NTX and colistin improves antibacterial action by activating the TCA cycle and increasing ROS production, ultimately leading to intensified oxidative damage in bacteria. This multi-layered antibacterial mechanism provides a potential strategy for treating drug-resistant bacterial infections.

NTX enhances the in vivo efficacy of colistin against MDR bacteria

To evaluate the safety and efficacy of the combined application of NTX and colistin in vivo, we conducted a mouse subacute toxicity test and an animal infection model study.33 In the subacute toxicity study, no toxic reactions were observed when comparing colistin monotherapy with the NTX-colistin combination group (Figure S4; Tables S4 and S5).

To this end, we evaluated the in vivo efficacy of the combination of NTX and colistin in animal infection models. In the Galleria mellonella infection model, neither colistin nor NTX alone effectively protected the larvae. However, the combination of NTX and colistin (5 + 2 mg/kg) significantly enhanced larval survival rates, indicating synergistic effects in vivo (Figure 6A). Compared to either colistin or NTX monotherapy, the combined treatment increased survival rates in the mouse peritonitis infection model (Figure 6B). Bacterial burden analysis of the spleen and kidneys revealed that the combination treatment significantly reduced bacterial loads compared to colistin alone (Figure 6C). This synergistic effect was further observed in the neutropenic mouse thigh muscle infection model, where combination therapy exhibited enhanced efficacy (Figures 6D–6F). In summary, the NTX-colistin combination exhibited both safety and pronounced synergistic antibacterial effects in multiple infection models.

Discussion

Bacterial resistance has become a major global public health threat, resulting in severe infections. Colistin, a critical antimicrobial agent widely used to treat resistant bacterial infections, has seen significantly reduced efficacy due to the emergence and spread of the mcr-1 resistance gene. The development of antimicrobial enhancers offers an effective approach to restore or enhance the activity of antimicrobial drugs.40 This study demonstrates that the FDA-approved drug NTX can serve as a potential enhancer for colistin. In both in vitro experiments and animal infection models, NTX significantly improved the antibacterial activity of colistin against mcr-1-positive pathogens. Moreover, the combination of NTX and colistin not only inhibited the development of resistance but also effectively prevented the emergence of resistant mutant strains.

In mechanistic investigations, this study observed that the combination of NTX and colistin exerted a pronounced synergistic antibacterial effect against MCR-1-positive strains (FICI ≤ 0.5). Given that NTX is a well-established biofilm disruptor with broad-spectrum activity, such synergy would not be expected to be restricted to a single antibiotic. This phenomenon therefore suggests that NTX may specifically target the resistance pathway mediated by MCR-1. Subsequent experiments further demonstrated that NTX can directly associate with MCR-1, thereby modulating its function and potentially attenuating its resistance-conferring capacity. Additionally, biofilm formation is an important defense mechanism for bacteria.41 Our study further found that adding NTX significantly inhibited biofilm formation. Consistent with this, A. Sobke et al. also found that NTX could reduce the formation and spread of Pseudomonas aeruginosa biofilms.26 Inhibition of biofilm formation facilitated more efficient antibiotic entry into bacterial cells, thereby further enhancing the antibacterial potency of colistin.

It is noteworthy that in mcr-1-negative bacteria, the combination of NTX and colistin exhibited an additive effect (0.5 < FICI ≤ 1.0), indicating that the mechanism of action is not limited to targeting MCR-1. Transcriptomic analysis combined with functional studies showed that the combined application of NTX and colistin significantly inhibited ribosomal biosynthesis and oxidative phosphorylation pathways. Specifically, NTX chelates divalent cations required by bacterial RNA polymerase,42 reducing protein synthesis and disrupting bacterial metabolism and growth, ultimately leading to bacterial death. Moreover, the combination of NTX and colistin also significantly inhibited bacterial energy metabolism pathways, resulting in a marked reduction in intracellular ATP levels. This change prompted bacteria to activate the TCA cycle and TMAO respiration to compensate for the energy gap through metabolic adjustments. However, due to the inhibition of oxidative phosphorylation, the overall energy balance of the bacteria remained disrupted. At the same time, NTX further enhanced the generation of ROS, and the accumulation of excess ROS caused oxidative damage to cellular components, thereby accelerating bacterial death. It is noteworthy that some of the observed changes in differentially expressed genes may reflect indirect consequences of the combined treatment on cellular metabolism and physiological states, rather than being exclusively attributable to the direct pharmacological actions of the drugs. In summary, our findings indicate that NTX targets the catalytic center of MCR-1, thereby potentiating the membrane-disruptive activity of colistin. Additionally, NTX interferes with bacterial core metabolism and redox processes, inhibits energy metabolism, and further induces oxidative stress damage, thereby significantly enhancing colistin antibacterial activity through multiple pathways.

In clinical applications, NTX has been widely used to treat uncomplicated urinary tract infections, demonstrating acceptable safety and tolerability.43 However, the clinical use of colistin is somewhat limited due to its neurotoxicity and nephrotoxicity.44 The combination of NTX and colistin can effectively reduce the required dose of colistin, thereby decreasing its toxic side effects and enhancing clinical safety. Consistent with the study by Luigi Principe et al.,45who used a G. mellonella infection model, it was demonstrated that NTX enhanced the in vivo efficacy of meropenem. Our study further confirmed in a mouse model that NTX did not induce acute toxic reactions. Moreover, in both the G. mellonella and mouse infection models, the combination of NTX and colistin exhibited significant synergistic antibacterial effects. These results further support the potential of NTX as an antimicrobial enhancer.

Based on these findings, we believe that NTX is an effective enhancer of colistin, capable of restoring and enhancing its antibacterial activity. The combination of NTX and colistin holds promise as a strategy for treating colistin-resistant bacterial infections.

Limitations of the study

This study demonstrates that NTX functions as a potentiator of colistin, exhibiting synergistic antibacterial activity against mcr-1-positive strains and providing preliminary insights into the underlying mechanism of this enhancement. Nevertheless, the scope of the present work does not encompass a broader range of clinically relevant resistant isolates, and thus the generalizability of the findings warrants further validation. In particular, additional investigations are required to determine whether the inhibitory effect of NTX arises from interference with the MCR-1-mediated phosphoethanolamine modification process or from direct suppression of its enzymatic activity, thereby furnishing more definitive evidence for its mode of action on MCR-1. Moreover, although the G. mellonella and murine infection models employed here offer valuable biological relevance, they cannot fully recapitulate the complexity of human infection. The clinical efficacy and safety of NTX consequently remain to be established through large-scale clinical trials. Future studies should therefore expand along these lines to substantiate the potential of NTX as a clinically applicable colistin adjuvant.

Experimental model and study participant details

Mice

Female BALB/c (6-8 weeks old) or KM mice (18-20 g) were purchased from Liaoning Changsheng Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Liaoning, China). Mice were housed in groups under controlled conditions (22 ± 2 °C, 12 h light/dark cycle) with ad libitum access to sterilized food and water. All animals were acclimatized for five days prior to infection. At the end of the experiment, mice were humanely euthanized by cervical dislocation.

Animal ethics statement

All animal experiments were conducted in compliance with institutional ethical guidelines and approved by the Ethics Committee of Jilin Agricultural University, China (Approval No. 20210316004). All efforts were made to minimize animal suffering.

Bacterial strains and culture medium

All bacterial strains used in this study are listed in Table S1. Unless otherwise specified, the strains were sourced from the Key Laboratory of New Veterinary Drug Research and Development, Jilin Province (Jilin, China). The MDR E. coli B2 (mcr-1) strain was generously provided by China Agricultural University and has been maintained in our laboratory.46 E. coli BL21 and E. coli DH5α strains were obtained from Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China). Mueller-Hinton broth (MHB, 11816) and agar (MHA, 10706) were purchased from QDRS Biotech, while Luria-Bertani broth (LB, A507002) and agar (LB, A507003) were provided by Sangon Biotech. All bacterial strains were cultured at 37°C in liquid media (MHB or LB broth) or on solid media (LB or MHA agar plates).

Galleria mellonella treatment model

Efficacy was evaluated in Galleria mellonella (n = 12/group, four groups). A 10 μL suspension of E. coli B2 containing 1×106 CFU was injected into the right hind leg of each larva to establish an infection model. One hour post-infection, treatments were administered: PBS, colistin (2 mg/kg), NTX (5 mg/kg), or a combination of both, which were injected into the left hind leg. The drugs were dissolved in 2% DMSO and 5% Tween-80, and the solution was sonicated. Larval survival was monitored at 37°C for 120 hours.

Mouse peritonitis treatment model

Infection was modeled with E.coli B2. Injecting a bacterial suspension with 1.5×108 CFU in 200 μL into the left peritoneal cavity of mice served as an infection model. At one hour post-infection, each group received PBS, colistin (2 mg/kg), NTX (5 mg/kg), or a combination. Treatments were administered via 200 μL intraperitoneal injections into the right peritoneal cavity, with a second dose given 12 hours later. Drug solutions contained 2% DMSO and 5% Tween-80 and were sonicated for proper solubilization. After 48 hours, mice were euthanized and organ bacterial burdens were measured using plate culture.

For the survival experiment, an infection dose of 2.5×108 CFU was administered. The treatment protocol remained the same as in the bacterial load experiment, and mouse survival was monitored for 5 days after infection.

Neutropenic mouse thigh infection model

Neutropenia was induced in mice by administering cyclophosphamide via intraperitoneal injections at doses of 150 mg/kg and 100 mg/kg on the 4th and 1st days before infection, respectively. Each mouse was injected with 100 μL of E. coli B2 suspension (1×106 CFU) into the right thigh muscle. An hour after the infection, animals were treated according to their respective experimental groups with a 200 μL intraperitoneal injection of the drug solution, followed by a second dose 12 hours later. At 48 h post-infection, thigh tissues were collected and plated after homogenization to assess bacterial burden.

In vivo toxicity test

In the subacute toxicity test, female BALB/c mice were randomly assigned to four groups (n=6): PBS, solvent, colistin (5 mg/kg), and colistin+NTX (5 + 20 mg/kg). The drugs were dissolved in a solution containing 2% DMSO and 5% Tween-80, except for the control group, in which the drugs were dissolved by sonication. Mice received one intraperitoneal injection daily for seven consecutive days, and their body weight was monitored throughout the treatment. The study concluded with the collection of blood samples for comprehensive biochemical and blood cell analyses.

Method details

Minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC)

Each antimicrobial agent was serially diluted in two-fold gradients using MHB within a 96-well plate. Each well was subsequently inoculated with a bacterial suspension that contained a final concentration of 1×105 CFU/mL. After placing the culture plates at 37°C for 18 hours, the MIC was recorded as the lowest concentration of the antimicrobial agent at which no visible bacterial growth was observed. The experiment was repeated three times to ensure data reliability.

Checkerboard assay

The checkerboard dilution method was employed to assess the synergistic activity and fractional inhibitory concentration index (FICI). The antimicrobial agent was placed along the x-axis and NTX along the y-axis of a two-dimensional gradient, with concentrations serially reduced in MHB. A bacterial suspension containing a final concentration of 1 × 105 CFU/mL was added to each well, and the plates were incubated at 37°C for 18 hours. FICI = (MIC of drug A in combination/MIC of drug A alone) + (MIC of drug B in combination/MIC of drug B alone). An FICI of ≤0.5 denotes a synergistic effect, whereas a FICI between >0.5 and ≤1.0 indicates an additive effect. An FICI ranging from >1.0 to ≤4.0 indicates no effect, while an FICI of >4 signifies an antagonistic effect.

Time-kill curve assay

After E. coli B2 was cultured to the logarithmic growth phase, the bacterial concentration was adjusted to 105 CFU/mL. The following groups were prepared: colistin (2 μg/mL), NTX (4 μg/mL), combination treatment, and the control group. Samples were collected at 0, 1, 2, 4, 6, 8, and 24 hours during incubation at 37°C. After sampling, the bacterial suspensions were resuspended in PBS, serially diluted 10-fold, and then plated onto MHA plates. After incubating the plates at 37°C overnight, the colonies were counted.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

The procedure was adapted with slight modifications from previous studies.38 E. coli B2 was cultured in MHB medium until reaching the logarithmic growth phase, harvested by centrifugation, and washed with PBS. It was then incubated overnight with NTX (4 μg/mL), colistin (2 μg/mL), or their combination. Glutaraldehyde was then used to preserve the samples at 4°C. The samples were sequentially dehydrated with ethanol solutions of increasing concentrations. Morphological alterations in the bacterial cells were then analyzed using SEM.

Membrane permeability determination

The membrane permeability was measured using propidium iodide (PI) staining. A bacterial suspension (OD600 = 0.5) was incubated with 10 nM PI at 37°C in the dark for 30 minutes. Treatment groups were established as follows: NTX (0-16 μg/mL) alone, colistin (0-8 μg/mL) alone, or a combined treatment of NTX (4 μg/mL) and colistin(0-8 μg/mL). These groups were designated as fixed-dose treatment combinations. Fluorescence intensity was measured at 535/615 nm (excitation/emission) after 2 hours of treatment.

Biofilm formation assay

E. coli B2 (OD600 = 0.1) was diluted 10-fold and inoculated into 96-well plates. The treatment groups included NTX (0-16 μg/mL), colistin (0-8 μg/mL), and the combination of NTX (4 μg/mL) with colistin(0-8 μg/mL). After incubation at 37°C for 24 hours, the samples were washed, fixed with methanol for 30 minutes, air-dried, and stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 30 minutes. Following an additional wash, the bound dye was solubilized in absolute ethanol, and absorbance was measured at 595 nm.

Membrane fluidity assay

The Laurdan was used to assess the effect of NTX on bacterial membrane fluidity. E. coli B2 (OD600 = 0.5) was incubated with 10 μM Laurdan at 37°C in the dark for 30 minutes. After washing with PBS, the cells were treated with the drug for 2 hours. Fluorescence intensities were measured at 350 nm for excitation, and 440 nm and 490 nm for emission. Membrane fluidity was quantified using the Laurdan Generalized Polarization (GP) formula: Laurdan GP = (I440 − I490)/(I440 + I490).

Resistance evolution study

References47,48 were followed. E. coli B2 (1×105 CFU) was treated with either 1/2 MIC colistin alone or in combination with 1/2 MIC NTX. MICs were determined daily, and the culture that exhibited growth at the highest drug concentration was subcultured. Drug concentrations were then adjusted according to the changes in MIC. After 30 consecutive passages, the fold increase in MIC was calculated.

LB agar plates containing gradient concentrations of colistin or its combination with NTX were prepared. E. coli B2 (1010 CFU) was plated and incubated at 37°C for 48 hours. Up to four single colonies were picked from the plates showing bacterial growth and further cultured to determine the MIC. Colonies with an MIC greater than the initial value were classified as highly resistant mutants. The minimum drug concentration that inhibited the growth of the mutants was defined as the mutant prevention concentration (MPC).

Intracellular ATP content measurement

E. coli B2 (OD600 = 0.5) was treated with the following drug combinations: NTX (0-16 μg/mL) alone, colistin (0-8 μg/mL) alone, or a combination of NTX (4 μg/mL) and colistin(0-8 μg/mL). After incubation at 37°C for 4 hours, the samples were centrifuged, washed, and then lysed to collect the supernatant. ATP content was measured following the instructions provided in the Enhanced ATP Detection Kit(#S0027, Beyotime, Shanghai).

NAD+/NADH ratio measurement

E. coli B2 (OD600 = 0.5) was treated with the following drug combinations: NTX (0-16 μg/mL) alone, colistin (0-8 μg/mL) alone, or a combination of NTX (4 μg/mL) and colistin. After incubation at 37°C for 4 hours, the samples were centrifuged, washed, and resuspended. The NAD+/NADH ratio was measured following the instructions of the NAD+/NADH detection kit(#S0175, Beyotime, Shanghai).

ROS level measurement

After treatment with the specified drug combinations, E. coli B2 (OD600 = 0.5) was incubated at 37°C for 4 hours, followed by centrifugation, washing, and collection of the bacterial pellet. Subsequently, a 10 μM DCFH-DA fluorescent probe was added to the cells, and ROS levels were measured following the instructions of the reactive oxygen species detection kit(#S0033S, Beyotime, Shanghai).

RT-qPCR analysis

E. coli B2 (OD600 = 0.5) was treated with colistin (2 μg/mL) or a combination of NTX (4 μg/mL) and colistin (2 μg/mL), and incubated at 37°C for 6 hours. After incubation, RNA was extracted, reverse-transcribed, and analyzed by qPCR. 16S rRNA served as the internal control, and relative expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method.

Surface plasmon resonance (SPR) analysis

The MCR-1 protein was diluted to 20 μg/mL and immobilized onto the chip surface at a flow rate of 10 μL/min. NTX was serially diluted in DMSO into eight concentration gradients (100-1.56 μM) and injected sequentially at a flow rate of 20 μL/min for analysis. For each concentration, A 100-second association phase was followed by a 180-second dissociation phase. The sensorgram was analyzed, and the dissociation equilibrium constant (KD) was calculated.

Molecular docking and molecular dynamics (MD) simulations

Molecular docking studies were performed using Sailvina final V1.0. The crystal structure of MCR-1 (PDB: 5GRR) was used. Prior to docking, both the protein and NTX molecules were prepared. After docking, the best conformation was selected for interaction analysis, and structural images were generated using PyMOL.

Molecular dynamics simulations were performed using GROMACS 2022.3.49,50 Briefly, the small molecules were assigned the GAFF force field using AmberTools, and RESP charges were calculated with Gaussian. The simulation conditions were set to 300 K and 1 bar, with the protein force field Amber99sb-ildn. The system underwent energy minimization, followed by 100 ps of NVT and NPT equilibration, and concluded with a 100 ns free MD simulation. The RMSD, RMSF, and RG of the protein were calculated, and the free energy was computed using the MM/GBSA method.

Plasmid construction

The mcr-1 gene was cloned from E. coli B2 using the primers listed in Table S2 and introduced into the pET28a vector by enzymatic digestion. Next, MCR-1 mutants were constructed by mutating the pET28-mcr-1 vector using the kit(C214-01/02, Vazyme, Nanjing). The accuracy of all constructed strains was verified by sequencing. Finally, the expression vectors pET28a, pET28a-MCR-1, and pET28a derivatives carrying the mcr-1 mutated alleles were transformed into E. coli BL21 (DE3), followed by MIC determination.

Transcriptomic analysis

E. coli B2 (OD600 = 0.5) was treated with colistin (2 μg/mL) or a combination of NTX (4 μg/mL) and colistin (2 μg/mL) at 37°C for 6 hours. Three biological replicates were included in each treatment group. After incubation, the bacterial cells were washed three times with PBS and centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 10 minutes to collect the cell pellets. Sample preparation and processing were performed by Guangdong Meige Gene Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Briefly, mRNA libraries were prepared using the NEBNext® Ultra™ Directional RNA Library Prep Kit and sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. Reads were aligned to the E. coli K12 reference genome. DEGs were identified using DESeq2 (v1.34.0) with P < 0.05 and ∣log2FC∣ > 0.585. GO and KEGG databases were used for functional annotation.

Quantification and statistical analysis

The statistical packages SPSS and GraphPad Prism 9 were used for data analysis. The results are presented as mean ± SD. When comparing two groups, a two-tailed unpaired t-test was used, and when comparing multiple groups, one-way ANOVA was employed. The log-rank (Mantel-Cox) or Mann-Whitney U test was used to assess the significance of survival rate and bacterial load. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05, with “ns” indicating no significant difference, ∗P < 0.05, ∗∗P < 0.01, and ∗∗∗P < 0.001.
