Dynamic contrast sensitivity during human locomotion
Authors: Brian Szekely, Paul R. MacNeilage
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Locomotion poses a challenge to clear and stable vision. Reflexive head and eye movements act to stabilize the retinal image, but these do not act perfectly, so retinal image motion is increased during walking compared with standing. We nevertheless perceive the world as clear and stable during locomotion, suggesting that the visual system is well-adapted to meet the challenges posed by locomotion. To better understand these processes, we assessed dynamic contrast sensitivity during locomotion by presenting brief (24 ms) foveal Gabor targets (6°, 11 cpd) at threshold contrast to observers walking on a treadmill in an otherwise darkened room. Head and ankle motion were tracked, and presentation time was randomized, which allowed post hoc binning of responses according to stride-cycle timing to investigate how sensitivity is impacted by head motion and stride-cycle timing. Contrast sensitivity was improved during walking compared with standing over large portions of the stride cycle, except for epochs aligned with heel strikes, which drive large and unpredictable perturbations. This resulted in periodicity in contrast sensitivity at two cycles per stride, with additional oscillations observed at four and six cycles per stride. Pupil size was found to be moderately larger during walking compared with standing and also exhibited periodic fluctuations that were phase-locked to the stride cycle. Perceptual oscillations reflect the entrainment of visual processing by active behaviors. Robust contrast sensitivity during walking may be supported by action-contingent effects of locomotion on visual cortical activity that have been observed in several animal models.

Introduction

Visual function depends critically on the ability to detect luminance edges or contours in the image, and the visibility of these edges depends on the observers sensitivity to luminance contrast at edges, often referred to simply as contrast sensitivity. Human constrast sensitivity for stationary images has been well-documented. However, under everyday circumstances, the eye is in constant motion, meaning that the image of the stationary world is constantly moving on the retina.

Small amounts of image motion that result from fixational eye movements can lead to contrast sensitivity that is improved relative to when the image is artificially stabilized on the retina, likely because modest image motion prevents so-called fading of visual responses that occurs when images are artificially stabilized (Kelly, 1979a; Rucci, Iovin, Poletti, & Santini, 2007; Ratnam, Domdei, Harmening, & Roorda, 2017). However, during locomotion and other active behaviors, image motion is likely to be significantly increased relative to the image motion that would result from fixational eye movement alone. Studies that have systemtically investigated contrast sensitivity in response to moving images (i.e., spatiotemporal contrast sensitivity) have found that contrast sensitivity is impaired in response to excessive image motion (Kelly, 1979b). This notion is at odds with the perceptual experience of viewing a clear and stable world during active behaviors. What mechanisms underlie our ability to see the world clearly despite our own movement and the associated retinal image motion?

A number of reflexive behaviors act to keep the image of the world stable at the retina despite observer movement, including the vestibulo colic reflex, the vestibulo-ocular reflex (VOR), and the optokinetic reflex. High-precision head and eye tracking during human locomotion suggests that these mechanisms succeed in stabilizing the retinal image somewhat, but residual retinal motion is nevertheless estimated to be on the order of 5 deg/s (Crane & Demer, 1997). Studies in stationary observers indicate that deficits in contrast sensitivity would be expected in response to retinal motion of 3 deg/s or more (Kelly, 1979b). Ultimately, clear and stable vision during locomotion may rely on mechanisms other than simple reflexive stabilization of the eye relative to the target.

Specifically, the neural processing of the visual stimulus may be adapted during locomotion to facilitate clear and stable vision. Evidence that locomotion may be accompanied by adaptive changes in visual processing comes from research in animal models, which have observed an increased gain of response in visual cortex when animals are walking or running compared with when they are still (Niell & Stryker, 2010; Erisken et al., 2014; Dadarlat & Stryker, 2017; Savier, Chen, & Cang, 2019). The idea that basic visual function may be mediated by motor actions is supported by recent studies suggesting that contrast sensitivity does exhibit periodic fluctuations and these are phase-locked to voluntary actions, such as reaching and button presses (Tomassini, Spinelli, Jacono, Sandini, & Morrone, 2015; Tomassini, Ambrogioni, Medendorp, & Maris, 2017; Zhang, Morrone, & Alais, 2019; Benedetto, Morrone, & Tomassini, 2020). These perceptual oscillations are thought to reflect oscillations in inhibitory neural activity in circuits that mediate perceptual sensitivity to contrast.

Locomotion is an action like these others and could, therefore, also entrain or otherwise impact contrast sensitivity (Davidson, Verstraten, & Alais, 2024). However, to date, behavioral evidence that visual processing is adapted to overcome increased retinal image motion during locomotion is sparse. Dynamic visual acuity gets worse with increasing walking speed (Hallemans et al., 2018), and contrast sensitivity is worse in the peripheral visual field (Benjamin, Wailes-Newson, Ma-Wyatt, Baker, & Wade, 2018). Oscillations in contrast sensitivity during locomotion have been reported recently (Davidson et al., 2024), but these measures were conducted using a head-mounted display and stimulus rendering would have been impacted by latency in head tracking and rendering, as well as any prediction or smoothing in the rendering pipeline. Additionally, target visibility could have been impacted by headset jitter or instability during locomotion, which would have been maximal around the time of heel strike.

In the current study, contrast sensitivity was assessed in observers walking on a treadmill viewing briefly presented targets on a projection screen in an otherwise darkened room. Surprisingly, contrast sensitivity was not impaired during walking compared with standing. Head, eye, and ankle movement were tracked in an attempt to reconstruct head and eye stabilization behaviors across the stride cycle. Targets were presented at random time points, and post hoc binning of responses based on stride-cycle timing (Szekely, Shankar, & MacNeilage, 2022; Davidson et al., 2024) allowed reconstructing pronounced oscillations in contrast sensitivity across the stride cycle at 2, 4 and 6 cycles per stride. Surprisingly, contrast sensitivity was better during walking than during standing across large potions of the stride cycle. We interpret these findings in light of several factors that could impact contrast sensitivity during locomotion including head, eye, and retinal image motion; pupil size; arousal; and action-dependent oscillation of neural processing in visual pathways. This work was previously presented in abstract form (Szekely et al., 2022).

Materials and methods

Subjects

Fifteen human subjects (25 ± 7 years old; 6 female, 9 male) with normal or corrected to normal vision participated in the current study. All participants provided written informed consent before participation and the study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the University of Nevada, Reno.

Experimental setup

An overview of the experimental setup is presented in Figure 1. Contrast discrimination targets were rendered using custom software (MATLAB2019a) and PsychToolBox 3 (Kleiner, Brainard, & Pelli, 2007). Stimuli were presented on a projection screen (Stewart Silver 5D) using a VPixx PROPixx projector operating at 120 Hz with a resolution of 1,920 × 1,080 pixels. The room was otherwise dark and all walls and the ceiling were painted black to minimize reflection of ambient light. Participants either stood or walked on a standard consumer exercise treadmill positioned in front of the screen; the projection screen was 158 × 89 cm with a typical viewing distance of 175 cm. Participants wore an Eyelink II head-mounted eye tracker operating at 500 Hz. Reflective markers were placed on the ankle, head, and the corners of the screen to allow continuous tracking of these rigid bodies using four Optitrack Prime13 W (240 Hz) infrared cameras and Optitrack Motive software version 1.22.

Experimental setup. Participants wore a head-mounted eye tracker and an ankle rigid body to track head, eye, and gait movements while viewing the Gabor stimuli on a projection screen.

Procedure

Participants donned the eye tracker with head markers and the ankle rigid body markers. Participants then completed a custom 13-point eye tracking calibration and validation routine while standing stationary on a treadmill at a viewing distance of approximately 175 cm from the projection screen. In the calibration procedure, 13 targets of 2 visual degrees each were distributed in a circular pattern with a field of view of 40°. The validation process contained the same calibration targets, where the eye tracker was evaluated for accuracy. If the average error from all 13 targets was greater than 1°, the entire calibration process was repeated.

On each trial, a Gabor patch was rendered in the center of the screen and participants indicated whether the orientation of the Gabor was tilted 45° to the left or right by pressing the left or right mouse clicker. The Gabor patch subtended 6 degrees of visual angle, had a spatial frequency of 11 cpd, and was displayed for 24 ms. This spatial frequency was chosen because we hypothesized that contrast sensitivity would be adversely affected by retinal image motion, and prior work has demonstrated that image motion causes the contrast sensitivity function to shift toward lower spatial frequencies with the most pronounced changes occurring above 10 cpd (Kelly, 1979a). Presentation time was randomized to occur between 500 and 1,500 ms after the previous response had been registered. This resulted in a roughly uniform distribution of trials across the stride cycle (Figure 2).

Distribution of stimulus onset as a function of stride cycle percentage across all participants. Stimulus presentation times were randomized relative to the stride cycle, producing an approximately uniform distribution across bins.

A preliminary block of 350 trials was collected to identify the contrast level that was just-detectable for each participant. In this block, the contrast of the Gabor patch was manipulated from trial to trial according to a three-down-one-up staircase procedure which converges to the contrast level that elicits 80% correct responses (Leek, 2001). The stopping criteria for the staircase was 20 reversals. We then fit a psychometric function to the data and selected the contrast at the 80% threshold.

During subsequent blocks, this just-detectable (80%) threshold contrast value was remeasured at the beginning of each testing session used. During the first visit of the main experiment, participants completed one block of 200 trials while standing and one block of 490 trials while walking on the treadmill at a speed of 5 km/h (Benjamin et al., 2018). The order in which blocks were completed was randomized across subjects. During the second visit of the main experiment, both standing and walking blocks were completed again, but in the reverse order to counterbalance and compensate for potential order effects.

After evaluation of the results of the main experiment, we decided to conduct two follow-up control experiments. The first was intended to control for possible differences in viewing distance between walking and standing conditions. Tracking data from the main experiment revealed that participants were on average closer to the screen when walking on the treadmill compared to standing still (see Figure 3), such that the Gabor target would have subtended a slightly larger area and would have manifest as a slightly lower spatial frequency stimulus. In this first control condition, participants first completed a block of 240 walking trials while the viewing distance to the screen was tracked. They then completed the same number of standing trials with a fixed viewing distance of 175 cm and with the spatial frequency of each target adjusted slightly (based on trial-to-trial viewing distance data) to achieve the same stimulus spatial frequencies experienced by each participant in their previous walking block.

Distribution of eye-to-screen distances during preliminary walking and standing blocks. During standing trials, participants stood a distance of 1.75 m from the screen. During preliminary walking trials, the mean distance was 1.70 ± 0.11 m.

In the final control experiment, we were concerned with measuring and comparing average pupil size during walking and standing. Each participant completed 120 trials of both walking and standing with order counterbalanced across subjects. The EyeLink II provided an estimate of pupil size in arbitrary units, which allowed examining whether pupil size differed between walking and standing using a paired t-test.

Head movement and contrast sensitivity as a function of stride cycle-timing

All head tracking data collected during walking were temporally partitioned into separate strides using the ankle tracking data (Figure 4). Strides were defined as the intervals between the peaks in vertical position (y-axis) of the ankle tracker; these peaks occur in the middle of the mid-swing phase of the step cycle for the foot wearing the marker. Data were then re-sampled such that there were 100 evenly spaced samples within each stride corresponding with 0% to 100% of the stride cycle. Resampling the data to have 100 evenly spaced samples within each stride facilitates averaging, combining, and comparing data across strides within and between participants.

Illustration of stride-cycle identification using the vertical (y) linear position of the ankle (blue trace). The shaded region indicates one complete stride, starting from the peak y-position of the ankle during midswing and extending to the peak y-position of midswing of the same foot.

Eye tracking data were collected with the aim of combining head and eye tracking data to estimate the position and motion of the eye relative to the screen and target, and thereby estimate retinal image motion. Unfortunately, eye movement measures were corrupted by jitter of the eye cameras relative to the eyes, which caused our estimates of eye movement and retinal image motion to be unreliable. Specifically, we registered artifactually high eye velocities around the time of heel strike, likely due to bouncing of the eye cameras relative to the eyes. In future studies, alternative eye tacking methods may be used to mitigate these problems (see Discussion: Head and eye stabilization during walking).

Ultimately, we were interested in whether there were systematic variations in contrast sensitivity as a function of stride-cycle timing. To evaluate this question, each trial that was presented during locomotion was labeled using a percentage value from 0 to 100 according to when the stimulus onset occurred during that specific stride cycle. Percent correct as a function of stride cycle timing was then computed separately for each percentile of the stride cycle using a moving average with a sliding window width of 10% of the stride cycle.

To estimate if the contrast sensitivity was periodic as a function of the stride cycle, we applied a fast Fourier transform (FFT) to the contrast data after they were sorted as a function of the stride cycle. The contrast data was replicated five times to increase frequency resolution of the fast Fourier transform, as well as improve signal-to-noise ratio.

Pupil analysis

Pupil size, measured in arbitrary units from the Eyelink 2 system, was collected from blocks 1 and 2. The data were smoothed using a Savitzky-Golay filter with a window length of 65 samples and a polynomial order of 2. Stride cycles were identified using the vertical position of the ankle marker. The pupil data were then segmented according to each participant’s stride cycle. Subsequently, pupil data from all strides across all participants were averaged together. We applied a custom wrapping moving average with a window length of 5% to smooth the data. To estimate pupil size variability throughout the stride cycle, we applied a wrapping moving 95% confidence interval algorithm. The smoothed pupil data was then replicated five times to increase frequency resolution and improve the signal-to-noise ratio. After this, we performed a fast Fourier transform on the replicated data.

Computing sample entropy

Between-subject variation in the regularity of locomotor movements was quantified by computing sample entropy of tracking data. Sample entropy is a measure of the irregularity of time series data, which has been used in the past to measure human gait regularity (Ahmadi, Sepehri, Wu, & Szturm, 2018). Sample entropy is calculated by first finding all subsets of the time series whose values match within a set tolerance. Then, it measures the negative natural logarithm of the conditional probability that these matching patterns will also match at the next point in the series. Signals such as white noise have high entropy compared with periodic signals, such as sinusoidal waveforms, which have low entropy. We implemented a principal component analysis to get the principal component of the 3D linear head position, and sample entropy was calculated on this principal component. To examine whether the metric of regularity is predictive of dynamic visual performance, we computed the Pearson correlation between this sample entropy and percent correct during locomotion in the main experiment.

Estimating the impact of head movement on contrast sensitivity via machine learning

Constrast sensitivity should be impacted by retinal slip, which depends on head and eye movement. To estimate which dimensions of head movement impact contrast sensitivity most strongly, we performed a machine learning analysis with XGBoost model. Head linear (x, y, z) and angular (roll, pitch, yaw) velocities were used as feature data, while responses during walking in blocks 1 and 2 were used as labels, with our classes being binary—correct or incorrect. Thus, we extracted the aforementioned features for each stimulus presentation.

The dataset had 7,200 samples initially. We removed outliers using z-score thresholding (z-score > 3). After thresholdoing, there were 6,512 samples. The features were standardized using StandardScaler and normalized using Normalizer with L2 norm from the scikit-learn library (Pedregosa et al., 2011). There was high class imbalance between the incorrect and correct classes, with the correct classes having 3,194 more samples than the incorrect class. Therefore, to mitigate the effect of high class imbalance, we used the synthetic minority over-sampling technique (SMOTE) to balance the dataset. SMOTE works by creating synthetic samples of the minority class to balance the class distribution (Wang, Dai, Shen, Hu, & Guo, 2021). The algorithm selects minority class samples and generates new synthetic samples that are along the line segments that join all the k-nearest neighbors of the minority class. This process is repeated until the minority class is balanced with the majority class. The dataset was split into training and testing, with a 75/25 training/testing split. The SMOTE algorithm was applied to the training, not the testing data.

The model we used in this binary classification task was XGBoost (Chen & Guestrin, 2016). XGBoost performs ensemble learning, which helps, to reduce overfitting, improves model generalization, and effectively handles imbalanced datasets (Chen & Guestrin, 2016; Wang, Deng, & Wang, 2021). The model configuration used a binary logistic objective function and log loss evaluation metric. In order to optimize the model performance, we used hyperparameter tuning five-fold cross-validation. The hyperparameters tuned in this study were the number of estimators, the learning rate, and the max depth. The number of estimators varied between 50 and 200 to balance the model complexity. The learning rate varied between 0.01 and 0.20 to optimize convergence speed and accuracy. Last, the maximum depth, otherwise known as tree depth, varied between 3 and 7 to allow for the model to capture complex patterns and mitigate the risk of overfitting. For the evaluation of the model, we used the F1 score so that we could balance precision and recall in our evaluation. To identify the most important features in the model’s predictions, we used normalized feature importances. These were derived from the gain values in XGBoost, which indicate how much each feature contributes to improving the classification task. The gain values were then normalized by dividing each value by the total sum of all gain values.

Results

Locomotion is a periodic behavior that poses a challenge to stable vision owing to head and eye movements that accompany locomotion and cause the image of the visual target to move on the retina. Here we show that, despite this challenge, contrast sensitivity during locomotion is maintained at levels that equal and sometimes exceed performance when stationary. We also show that periodicity and regularity in locomotor kinematic behavior is predictive of contrast sensitivity.

Challenges to visual stability during walking

Head and eye movements during locomotion pose a challenge to stable vision. To gain insight on the relationship between locomotor behavior and contrast sensitivity during walking, we divided locomotor data into strides and examine head velocity as a function of stride-cycle timing. These data are presented in Figure 5.

Average linear head velocity across the stride-cycle is shown in the top row. Lateral head velocity completes one cycle during each stride while fore–aft and vertical head velocities complete two cycles each. In other words, fore–aft and vertical head movement trajectories repeat with each step, while lateral head movement trajectories repeat with each stride (i.e., left-to-right sway owing to alternating left and right footsteps). Velocity ranges are similar for lateral and vertical movements (±0.1 m/s), but the range of velocities is much smaller for fore–aft movements (±0.03 m/s).

Average angular head velocity is shown in the second row. Roll and pitch complete two cycles per stride while yaw completes only one cycle. As noted previously (Pozzo, Berthoz, & Lefort, 1990), during locomotion the head tends to rotate in the direction opposite the linear movement to counteract perturbations introduced by linear head movement. This explains why lateral head movement (Figure 5, top left) is roughly out of phase with yaw rotation (Figure 5, bottom right), while vertical head movement (Figure 5, top middle) is roughly in phase with pitch rotation (Figure 5, bottom middle).

By combining head and eye tracking data, it is possible to compute how the eye is moving in the world and thus how the image of the target is moving on the retina (Crane & Demer, 1997; Aytekin, Victor, & Rucci, 2014). Unfortunately, jitter of the eye cameras relative to the eye caused our eye tracking data to be unreliable. Eye tracking data and estimates of retinal image motion are therefore not presented here.

However, we observed that participants were on average slightly closer to the screen during walking than they were during standing, which would result in a slightly larger target at a slightly lower spatial frequency. To investigate whether this potential confound was influencing results, we conducted a follow-up experiments in which we controlled for effects of differences in viewing distance between walking and standing conditions by changing the spatial frequency for the standing stimuli contingent on the viewing distances that were recorded during walking. Overall results from this control condition are shown in Figure 7, right; the difference in performance was no longer significant, indicating that small differences in viewing distance may have impacted results. In a further control experiment, we measured and compared pupil size during walking and standing and found that pupil size was approximately 8% larger during walking, suggesting that increased light entering the eye could possibly lead to improved contrast sensitivity when walking.

To further investigate how pupil size may have impacted contrast sensitivity during walking, we calculated the change in pupil size across the stride-cycle in a control experiment (Figure 8). Results revealed a prominent modulation at two cycles per stride with minimum pupil size around the time of heel strikes. These decreases in pupil size around the time of heel strike could very well impact contrast sensitivity in two different ways. First, a smaller pupil aperture leads to decreased luminance at the retina. Second,a smaller pupil aperture would lead to increased diffraction, which may also limit sensitivity to high spatial frequencies.

Relationship between head movement regularity and contrast sensitivity

To investigate whether contrast sensitivity during walking is impacted by the regularity (and thus predictability) of stride behavior, we computed the sample entropy of linear head movement. Lower sample entropy values correspond with greater regularity or predictability of head movement. As shown in Figure 9, sample entropy was negatively correlated with contrast sensitivity during walking, suggesting that contrast sensitivity during walking may be improved in participants with more predictable linear head movement trajectories. Owing to the small sample size, these results should be interpreted with caution until they can be corroborated with a larger dataset.

Relationship between sample entropy of linear head movement and proportion correct in the main experiment. The significant negative correlation suggests that contrast sensitivity during walking may be improved in participants with more predictable head movement trajectories.

Which dimensions of head movement best predict contrast sensitivity?

Head movement exhibits regular oscillations during locomotion, and these drive retinal slip. To better understand how these factors may impact contrast sensitivity, we used machine learning to evaluate which dimensions of head and eye velocity best predicted whether an observer would respond correctly or incorrectly on a given trial. Predictive strength is quantified using relative feature importance.

Overall, feature importance did not vary much across the investigated dimensions (Figure 10), but the analysis does allow quantification of relative differences. Vertical linear head speed showed the highest feature importance. This measure shows strong (±0.15 m/s) oscillations at 2 Hz (Figure 5), and therefore provides reliable phasic information about the step cycle, specifically about the timing of heel strikes and the associated decrease in contrast sensitivity that drives the two cycles per stride oscillation in contrast sensitivity. Lateral and fore–aft linear head movement exhibit decreased importance, perhaps due to the reduced magnitude of oscillations (Figure 5). Pitch and yaw angular head speed, which would be expected to drive retinal slip and thus reflexive angular VOR eye movements most strongly, exhibited comparable feature importance, while roll angular head speed exhibited reduced feature importance.

Discussion

Locomotion poses a challenge to clear and stable vision because the movement of the eye relative to the stationary environment causes the image of the environment to slip across the retina. To overcome this challenge, the nervous system relies in part on compensatory movements of the head and eye, which work together to improve the stabilization of the retinal image. However, this reflexive mechanical stabilization is imperfect, meaning that the retinal image will always be less stable during walking than standing.

Despite this instability, we found that contrast sensitivity was just as good during walking as during standing. This demonstrates that overall contrast sensitivity is not impaired by the retinal slip that accompanies normal walking, at least under the conditions investigated in this study.

To gain better insight into the dynamics of contrast sensitivity during walking, we used a novel technique to calculate contrast sensitivity as a function of stride cycle timing (Szekely, Shankar, & MacNeilage, 2022; Davidson et al., 2024). This analysis suggests that retinal slip does in fact lead to impaired contrast sensitivity during walking, but only around the time of heel strike. During other phases of locomotion, contrast sensitivity appears to be improved during walking compared with standing.

In the remainder of this discussion, we first place our observed head and eye stabilization behaviors in the context of previous studies. We then discuss the locomotion-dependent dynamics of contrast sensitivity and how these may be impacted by stimulus factors versus sensory processing.

Head and eye stabilization during walking

Numerous prior studies have examined joint head and eye movement during walking and found that reflexive head and eye movements work in synergy to stabilize vision. Our measurements are generally in line with these previous studies. Walking results in periodic vertical linear head movement at a rate of approximately two cycles per stride (Figure 5, top middle) as the body and head are first propelled up and over the stance leg before falling again until the heel of a swing leg strikes the ground. This vertical linear head movement is partially compensated for by pitch rotation of the head in the opposite direction (Figure 5, bottom middle), which acts to keep the head pointed at a fixed point in front of the observer (Pozzo et al., 1990; Hirasaki, Moore, Raphan, & Cohen, 1999; Cromwell, Schurter, Shelton, & Vora, 2004).

This head stabilization occurs in response to lateral linear head movement as well. As the walker transitions from left leg support to right leg support, the body and head sway from left to right and then back again at a rate of approximately one cycle per stride (Figure 5, top left). This lateral linear head movement is accompanied by yaw rotation of the head in the opposite direction (Figure 5, bottom right), which again acts to keep the head pointed at a fixed point in front of the observer (Pozzo et al., 1990; Hirasaki et al., 1999; Cromwell et al., 2004).

At the same time, reflexive eye movements including the VOR and the optokinetic reflex (sometimes referred to collectively as the visually enhanced VOR) work to stabilize the image of the fixated target on the retina. By combining head and eye tracking data it is possible to compute how the eye is moving in the world and thus how the image of the target is moving on the retina (Crane & Demer, 1997; Aytekin et al., 2014). Unfortunately, jitter of the eye cameras relative to the eye led to unrealistically high eye velocities. To date, it remains challenging to exclude such artifacts and accurately measure eye movements in active participants (Tatara, Toda, Maeda, & Handa, 2023).

In future studies, alternative eye tracking methods may be used to mitigate these issues. For example, scleral search coils avoid issues associated with camera movement during video-oculography (Crane & Demer, 1997), and modern head-mounted implementations have been explored (Eibenberger, Eibenberger, & Rucci, 2016), but search coils can be cumbersome and can lead to discomfort for participants. Using video-oculography, it may be possible to devise methods to stabilize the camera relative to the skull. In animals, cameras may be rigidly attached to the skull (Parker, Abe, Leonard, Martins, & Niell, 2022), and in humans, a bite bar or similar device may be used to mount the camera. Another promising approach is the use of head-mounted dual-Purkinje methods that would for allow extremely precise eye tracking measures (Jolly, Idrizović, Mizobuchi, Wu, & Rucci, 2025). Alternatively, video/image processing may be used to correct for artifacts resulting from camera jitter (Biswas & Lescroart, 2024).

Preliminary joint analyses of head and eye tracking data (not shown) yielded peaks in estimated retinal image motion around the time of heel strike, which could explain dips in contrast sensitivity at these time points. However, it was impossible to determine whether this motion was caused by movement of the eye, which would impact retinal image motion, or movement of the eye camera, which would not.

Similar measurements have been performed in participants walking on a treadmill with head and eye movement tracked using search coils (Crane & Demer, 1997). Unfortunately, that study did not report retinal slip as a function of stride-cycle timing. However, with viewing distance similar to ours, maximum retinal slip was calculated to be on the order of 5 deg/s during normal walking.

Contrast sensitivity when walking versus standing

Despite the mechanical stabilization mechanisms characterized above, retinal slip will be increased during walking compared with standing (Crane & Demer, 1997), and contrast sensitivity is known to be affected by the velocity of the target on the retina. Kelly (1979b) exhaustively characterized spatiotemporal contrast sensitivity using a method that allowed precise control of the retinal velocity of the stimulus by measuring and compensating for the effects of even fixational eye movements. Results show that as the retinal velocity of the target increases, the contrast sensitivity curves shift towards lower spatial frequencies.

In our experiment, we used a Gabor target with a spatial frequency of 11 cpd. The standard contrast sensitivity function exhibits a sharp decrease in sensitivity as spatial frequency increases above 11 cpd. Given that retinal image motion causes the contrast sensitivity function to shift toward lower spatial frequencies, we would expect increased retinal slip during walking to lead to substantial decreases in contrast sensitivity, even for retinal image motion as low as 3 deg/s (Kelly, 1979b). The fact that contrast sensitivity is unaffected or perhaps even improved during locomotion for most phases of the stride cycle outside of heel strike suggests that other mechanisms may be acting to improve contrast sensitivity during walking. Such mechanisms would be adaptive in terms of facilitating clear and stable vision even during locomotion.

One possible explanation is the increase in pupil area observed during walking compared to standing, measured at an 8% average increase in the current study. Increased pupil size would lead to greater luminance and decreased diffraction. However, it is unclear whether such a modest increase could explain the observed maintenance of contrast sensitivity despite increased retinal slip.

Another possibility is enhanced visual processing in the nervous system during walking compared to standing. Numerous studies have reported that locomotion leads to increased gain of responses in visual brain areas in model organism including both flies and mice (Niell & Stryker, 2010; Erisken et al., 2014; Dadarlat & Stryker, 2017; Savier et al., 2019), but the same may not be true in non-human primates (Liska et al., 2024).

Studies in humans have looked for a perceptual manifestation of this phenomenon, that is, increased visual sensitivity, with mixed results (Benjamin et al., 2018; Cao & Händel, 2019; Cao, Chen, & Haendel, 2020). Most similar to the current study, and inspired by our methods (Szekely et al., 2022; Davidson et al., 2024) report that the detection of a target, which essentially depends on contrast sensitivity, is roughly equivalent during walking and standing. Importantly, this study was conducted using a head-mounted display such that stimulus rendering could have been impacted by latency in head tracking and/or rendering, as well as prediction or smoothing in the rendering pipeline that is intended to facilitate visual stability. Presentation may have also been affected by headset jitter or instability that could arise, for example, around the time of heel strike. In our study, in contrast, retinal slip would have been more comparable to that experienced when fixating a world-fixed target under natural circumstances. The associated retinal slip, expected to be on the order of 5 deg/s on average (Crane & Demer, 1997), should have led to decreases in contrast sensitivity (Kelly, 1979b). Why was this impairment not observed over significant portions of the stride cycle?

Given imperfect mechanical stabilization by the vestibulocolic reflex and VOR, it would be adaptive for neural mechanisms to play a role in generating a stable and clear representation of the moving retinal image, especially in circumstances such as locomotion, in which significant retinal image motion is expected and predictable. An example of such a phenomenon is the observation that blurring or smearing of moving targets is less than would be expected based on the temporal integration window of the visual system (Burr, 1980). However, the short presentation time used in the present study (24 ms) suggests that a somewhat different mechanisms would have to be responsible. An alternative possibility is a kind of filling-in process based on the knowledge that natural images exhibit a 1/f spatial power spectrum. In the absence of compensatory mechanisms, retinal image motion is expected to lead to blurring that will be more consequential at higher than lower spatial frequencies. The visual system could proactively boost gain at higher spatial frequencies, in proportion to the expected motion, to maintain the expected power across spatial frequencies in the neural and/or perceptual representation, a phenomenon that has been previously suggested for stationary images (Campbell, Howell, & Johnstone, 1978) (Vince Billock, personal cummunication). Such compensation would require the nervous system to maintain and deploy an estimate of the motion to be compensated. For example, knowledge of image motion is used to achieve so-called super resolution in computer vision systems, and analogous mechanisms have previously been suggested to play a role in biological vision as well (Anderson, Ratnam, Roorda, & Olshausen, 2020). In this example, the estimate of image motion is derived from the image itself, but the estimate or prediction of image motion to be compensated could be extra-retinal as well. For example, empirical evidence suggests that extra-retinal information about fixational eye movements influences spatial perception (Zhao, Ahissar, Victor, & Rucci, 2023); similar extra-retinal information about head, eye, and/or locomotion actions could be used to predict retinal image motion during locomotion and could therefore support hypothesized deblurring mechanisms.

Alternatively, contrast sensitivity during walking may simply benefit from systemic physiological factors that are not specific to the visual system. For example, increased blood flow, blood oxygenation, or arousal that accompanies vigorous physical activity could lead to an enhancement of contrast sensitivity (Bullock, Elliott, Serences, & Giesbrecht, 2017; Dodwell, Liesefeld, Conci, Müller, & Töllner, 2021).

Phasic modulation of contrast sensitivity during walking

Our method of binning perceptual responses according to stride cycle timing (Szekely et al., 2022) allowed us to identify phasic modulations in contrast sensitivity at two, four, and six cycles per stride (Figure 6). A parsimonious hypothesis is that these modulations are driven exclusively by retinal slip, especially the two cycle per stride modulation, with troughs in sensitivity that roughly coincide with the time of heel strikes which cause head/eye perturbations that likely result in increased retinal slip.

Davidson et al. (2024) used this same method to identify phasic modulations in contrast sensitivity, but using a head-mounted rather than external display. The study found phasic modulation in performance at two cycles per stride, roughly in phase with vertical head height, meaning that performance was worst around the time of heel strikes, and best at intermediate time points, similar to our results. One possible explanation of this result is that there were perturbations, oscillations, or vibrations of the HMD relative to the eye around the time of heel strike and this led to impaired performance at those time points. However, we also observed these oscillations in the current study using a stimulus that was rendered on a world-fixed screen, suggesting that these two cycle-per-stride oscillations were not simply an artifact of headset jitter.

In the current study, we also observed higher frequency oscillations. A parsimonious and plausible explanation of the higher frequency modulations is stride–cycle–dependent variation in the direction of retinal slip, with slip that is parallel/perpendicular to the presented grating leading to decreased/increased blur and resulting in better/worse sensitivity, respectively.

In addition to retinal slip, one might reasonably expect contrast sensitivity to be impacted by stride-dependent variation in pupil size. Smaller pupil size would be expected to decrease luminance and increase diffraction, thereby reducing contrast sensitivity, especially at higher spatial frequencies. Average pupil area as a function of the stride-cycle (Figure 8) does show prominent dips at the time of heel strike, which could explain the decrease in contrast sensitivity at these time points; pupil area shows some modulation at higher frequencies as well.

A final possible explanation for the phasic modulation of contrast sensitivity is stride-cycle–dependent modulation of the efficacy of visual processing in the nervous system. In other words, oscillations in performance could be due to oscillations in neural activity that are entrained by walking. The entrainment of contrast sensitivity to voluntary actions such as reaching (Tomassini et al., 2015) or button presses (Zhang et al., 2019) has been reported previously, with oscillations in performance observed in the theta range (4–8 Hz). This entrainment is hypothesized to reflect periodic inhibition of attentional mechanisms (Tomassini et al., 2017). It is plausible that similar entrainment underlies the locomotor phase dependence of contrast sensitivity in this study as well as related studies (Davidson et al., 2024). It would make sense for inhibition and associated insensitivity to take place at time points where perturbations are expected, such as at the time of heel strike during walking or analagously, during saccades.

Related to this interpretation, we know that the nervous system relies on prediction to improve performance, and the periodicity of locomotion means that predictive mechanisms could play a role. Prior work has shown that head movement is least predictable around the time of heel strike and most predictable at intermediate time points (MacNeilage & Glasauer, 2017). If the predictability of the head, eye, or retinal image motion plays a role, we would expect performance to be worst at the time of heel strike and best at intermediate time points, as we have observed. Such a predictive mechanisms could also explain why we found that contrast sensitivity during walking is improved for participants whose head movements were the most regular, i.e., those who exhibited the lowest sample entropy (Figure 9).

Ultimately, oscillations in performance may be driven by oscillations in the visibility of the physical stimulus (e.g., owing to motion blur or pupillary luminance modulation), oscillations in neural processing, or some combination of the two. To better understand how these oscillations are related to kinematic oscillations in head and eye movement, and the associated sensory and motor signals, we used a machine learning approach to identify which dimensions of movement best predict observers’ contrast sensitivity (Figure 10). Vertical linear head speed exhibits the highest feature importance, likely because it provides good information about the timing of heel strikes and the associated, pronounced dips in contrast sensitivity. Sensation of vertical head movement is performed by the saccules of the vestibular system. If oscillations in performance are driven by changes in the visibility of the stimulus, this suggests that saccular stimulation can predict oscillations in stimulus visibility. However, to the extent that oscillations in contrast sensitivity are driven by oscillations in neural processing, saccular stimulation and/or the locomotor efference signals that drive it could conceivably play a role in mediating these neural oscillations.

Conclusions and future directions

A primary conclusion of the current study is that contrast sensitivity is not impaired during locomotion, despite the likelihood of increased retinal slip. This conclusion is, however, weakened by our inability to precisely track eye movements and thus precisely quantify retinal image motion during locomotion. In future, precise and accurate tracking of eye movements in active observes will allow for a better characterization of the challenges to vision imposed by locomotion. With challenges accurately quantified, it will then be possible to better characterize the ways in which the nervous system is adapted to overcome these challenges. Beyond improved characterization of retinal image motion, it will also be necessary to address other potential confounding factors, such as effects of pupil size and general arousal level.

Another conclusion of the current study is that there are marked oscillations in contrast sensitivity that are phase-locked to the locomotor stride-cycle. Pronounced decreases in sensitivity at the time of heel strike are likely related to the increased retinal image motion resulting from unpredictable kinematic perturbations of the head and eye, but improved eye tracking will be necessary to confirm this. However, stimulus-independent perceptual oscillations have been observed in the context of other active behaviors (Tomassini et al., 2015; Tomassini et al., 2017; Zhang et al., 2019; Benedetto et al., 2020). If stimulus-independent perceptual oscillations are present during locomotion as well, it would make sense for these perceptual and neural oscillations to be phase-locked to the locomotor stride cycle. The periodicity of behaviors such as locomotion, and associated periodicity in sensory processing demands, could play a central role in driving neural and perceptual oscillations that have been well-documented in other contexts. Once again, careful experimental control will be necessary to disentangle and isolate the various stimulus, sensory, and motor factors that mediate dynamic contrast sensitivity during locomotion.
