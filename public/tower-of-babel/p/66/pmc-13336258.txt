Bridging environmental pollution and immune dysregulation: How microplastics disrupt gut homeostasis to promote allergic diseases
Authors: Xu Zhang, Ke Liu, Zhiqiang Zhang, Xinyu Huang, Mengyuan Liu, Daoming Bai, Rui Yang, Chunping Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTParallel increases in microplastic (MP) pollution and allergic diseases suggest an environmental link in allergy pathogenesis. While intestinal barrier dysfunction and dysbiosis are hallmark features of allergic diseases, the mechanisms by which persistent MPs drive these processes remain unclear. This review proposes a mechanistic pathway where orally ingested MPs disrupt the intestinal barrier through physical and chemical toxicity, precipitating functional dysbiosis. Specifically, MPs suppress key short-chain fatty acid (SCFA)-producing commensals. The resulting SCFA deficit impairs the “gut-airway axis,” weakening respiratory immune tolerance and increasing allergen susceptibility. We further argue for integrating gut health assessments into standardized allergy diagnostics and treatments. Despite this novel perspective, significant knowledge gaps remain regarding causality, dose-response relationships, and specific biomarkers. Addressing these challenges is critical for translating scientific findings into public health policies and clinical interventions to mitigate the rising global burden of allergic diseases.

Introduction

The proliferation of plastic waste has established microplastic pollution as a formidable global health challenge. Concurrent with the exponential growth of global plastics production, which surged from just 2 million tons in 1950 to 367 million tons by 2020 [1], these plastic particles – defined as being less than 5 mm in diameter [2]—have ubiquitously penetrated every global ecosystem. This pervasive distribution facilitates their entry into the human body through various pathways, most notably via the food chain and direct ingestion of contaminated food and water. For instance, a landmark global survey found microplastics in 93% of bottled water samples [3]. The alarming reality of human exposure is no longer a matter of speculation; direct evidence has confirmed the presence of microplastics in critical human tissues and fluids, including the lungs, placenta, brain, and even circulating in the blood of healthy donors [4–7]. Animal models further underscore the potential risk, demonstrating that chronic exposure can induce tissue inflammation, oxidative stress, and gut dysbiosis [8]. Consequently, the potential impact of microplastics on human health is now an issue of significant international concern.

Parallel to the escalation of microplastic pollution, the global prevalence of allergic diseases has risen dramatically over the past several decades, affecting up to 30% of the population in industrialized nations [9]. The “hygiene hypothesis” and its more recent evolution into the “microbiota hypothesis” provide a compelling explanatory framework for this trend. This theory posits that modern lifestyles have diminished early-life exposure to a diverse range of microorganisms, thereby impairing the normal maturation of the immune system and the establishment of tolerance [10,11]. This disruption can lead to a systemic bias toward a Type 2 helper T cell (Th2) immune response, which underlies the pathophysiology of most allergic diseases. Within this context, the critical role of environmental factors is well-established, but a crucial question remains: do novel and persistent environmental contaminants like microplastics contribute to this rising burden of allergic disease?

The primary interface between such environmental exposures and the host immune system is the gastrointestinal tract. The gut is not merely a digestive organ but the body’s largest and most complex immunological hub, where the “gut-barrier-microbiota-immune” axis operates as a highly integrated network to maintain systemic homeostasis [12]. A healthy gut microbiota reinforces the integrity of the intestinal epithelial barrier and “educates” submucosal immune cells, particularly regulatory T cells (Tregs), to actively induce tolerance to harmless substances [13]. A disruption of this equilibrium – a state known as dysbiosis – can lead to increased intestinal permeability (“leaky gut”). This allows luminal antigens and microbial products to translocate into circulation, triggering a state of low-grade systemic inflammation known to affect distant organs, including the respiratory tract, thereby predisposing an individual to allergic diseases [14,15].

Based on this evidence, the central scientific question of this review is: As environmental contaminants that primarily enter the body via the digestive tract, do microplastics promote or exacerbate the development of allergic diseases by perturbing the gut-barrier-microbiota-immune axis? Preliminary evidence from animal models supports this hypothesis, demonstrating that microplastic exposure can indeed induce gut dysbiosis and impair barrier function [8]. Therefore, this review aims to systematically analyze and elucidate this potential mechanism. The article will first elaborate on the foundational principles of the intestinal barrier and allergy, then detail how microplastics disrupt this system and the gut microbiota. Subsequently, it will provide an overview of current and emerging diagnostic and therapeutic strategies based on this mechanism. Finally, it will conclude by summarizing existing challenges and proposing future research directions, with the goal of bridging basic research and clinical application in this critical interdisciplinary field.

Literature Search and Selection Criteria: To ensure a comprehensive overview, a systematic literature search was conducted across PubMed, Web of Science, and Scopus databases for articles published from inception up to January 2026. The search strategy employed a combination of Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms and free-text keywords using Boolean operators: (“microplastics” OR “nanoplastics” OR “plastic particles”) AND (“gut microbiota” OR “intestinal barrier” OR “dysbiosis”) AND (“allergic diseases” OR “asthma” OR “allergic rhinitis” OR “gut-airway axis”). We strictly included peer-reviewed original research, meta-analyses, and systematic reviews published in English. Priority was given to studies elucidating the mechanistic pathways linking oral exposure to immune regulation. Studies lacking clear toxicological data or focusing solely on marine ecology were excluded.

Finally, it is important to acknowledge the scope of this review. We primarily focus on the pathway initiated by the oral ingestion of microplastics and its subsequent impact on the gut-airway axis. While other routes of exposure, such as the direct inhalation of airborne microplastics and their localized effects on the respiratory immune system, are undoubtedly important, a detailed analysis of these mechanisms is beyond the purview of this article. The framework presented here aims to synthesize the evidence for the gut-mediated pathway, which represents a major route of human exposure.

Foundational principles of the intestinal barrier and allergic diseases

As exogenous particles, the potential in vivo risks of microplastics (MPs) begin with the critical step of crossing the intestinal barrier. This is not a simple process of physical penetration but rather a multidimensional, dynamic process governed by the two major transport pathways of intestinal epithelial cells (paracellular and transcellular), the complex regulatory interface of the mucus layer and bio-corona, and the physicochemical properties of the MPs themselves. The intestinal tract serves not only as the primary site for nutrient digestion and absorption but also as the body’s foremost defense against invading pathogens and harmful substances. The integrity of the intestinal barrier is therefore critical for maintaining host immune homeostasis, and its dysfunction is recognized as a key factor in the development of numerous immune-mediated conditions, particularly allergic diseases [12].

The integrated defense system of the intestinal barrier

The intestinal barrier is a sophisticated, multi-layered defense system whose integrity is fundamental to host health. The first line of defense is formed by a synergistic physical and chemical barrier. Structurally, a single layer of intestinal epithelial cells is sealed by apical tight junctions (TJs), where key proteins including claudins, occludin, and ZO-1 regulate the paracellular pathway, forming the primary physical gatekeeper of the system [16]. This epithelium is overlaid by a dynamic mucus layer, which acts as a physical obstacle to luminal contents [17] and is simultaneously enriched with chemical defenses, such as antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) and secretory immunoglobulin A (sIgA). These molecules neutralize toxins and pathogens, performing “immune exclusion” without inducing excessive inflammation [18]. The coalescence of this structural architecture and chemical defense constitutes the barrier’s foundational defense line.

Complementing this physical-chemical frontline is the biological barrier, composed of the vast commensal microbial community known as the gut microbiota. Through “colonization resistance,” the microbiota competes with pathogens for nutrients and adhesion sites, thereby inhibiting their engraftment [19]. However, its role is far from passive. As an active metabolic organ, the gut microbiota ferments dietary fiber to produce key signaling molecules, most notably short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs). SCFAs, such as butyrate, not only serve as a primary energy source for colonocytes but also enhance barrier function by upregulating the expression of TJ proteins [13]. Consequently, a diverse and stable microbial ecosystem is a central driver of homeostasis for the entire intestinal barrier system.

The most sophisticated layer of this defense network is the immunological barrier, managed by the gut-associated lymphoid tissue (GALT) [20]. The intestinal immune system operates in a state of delicate balance, tasked with mounting effective responses against pathogens while maintaining tolerance to innocuous antigens from food and commensal bacteria [21]. This equilibrium is critically dependent on the potent suppressive function of regulatory T cells (Tregs). By producing inhibitory cytokines like IL-10 and TGF-β, Tregs actively restrain inappropriate immune responses, thereby preventing chronic inflammation and allergy [22]. The proper functioning of this immune barrier, particularly the robustness of its tolerance mechanisms, is therefore decisive in preventing diseases of immune dysregulation.

Multi-dimensional destructive mechanisms of microplastics on the intestinal barrier

As emerging environmental contaminants, orally ingested MPs can compromise the intestinal barrier through a multi-pronged assault, encompassing direct physical and chemical damage as well as an indirect “Trojan Horse” effect.

First, MPs can inflict direct physical injury and biochemical toxicity. Larger particles may cause mechanical abrasion of the mucosal surface, whereas smaller, nano-sized particles can be internalized by intestinal epithelial cells, inducing cytotoxicity and triggering apoptosis [15]. Furthermore, MPs act as foreign bodies that elicit a potent biochemical stress response. Studies have demonstrated that MP exposure induces the overproduction of reactive oxygen species (ROS), leading to oxidative stress. This, in turn, can disrupt barrier integrity by activating signaling pathways such as NF-κB, which downregulate the expression of critical TJ proteins [23]. Concurrently, MPs can activate inflammasomes like NLRP3, promoting the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g. TNF-α, IL-1β), which not only exacerbates cellular damage but further dismantles TJ integrity, creating a vicious cycle of inflammation and permeability [8].

Second, MPs function as vectors for other harmful substances, a mechanism known as the “Trojan Horse” effect. Their large surface-area-to-volume ratio and hydrophobic nature facilitate the adsorption of environmental co-contaminants, including heavy metals and persistent organic pollutants (POPs). Upon ingestion, these MPs can release their toxic payload directly onto the gut epithelium. This effect not only ensures efficient delivery of toxicants to a vulnerable target but may also act synergistically with the intrinsic toxicity of the MPs themselves, thereby amplifying the overall damage to the intestinal barrier [24].

The distinct toxicity of nanoplastics

While microplastics pose established risks to gut health, nanoplastics (NPs, <1 μm) warrant specific attention due to their unique toxicokinetic properties, which differ fundamentally from those of larger particles. Unlike micro-sized particles that are primarily confined to the intestinal lumen or transported via M cells, NPs exhibit a superior ability to breach the intestinal epithelium [25,26]. They can readily translocate across the barrier via multiple specialized pathways, including clathrin- and caveolae-mediated endocytosis, allowing them to enter the systemic circulation and lymphatic system with greater efficiency [27]. This enhanced permeability facilitates their accumulation in distal organs such as the liver, kidney, and brain, thereby escalating the potential risk from a localized gastrointestinal disturbance to a systemic threat [27–29].

Furthermore, the biological reactivity of NPs is critically governed by their physicochemical characteristics. The exponentially larger surface-area-to-volume ratio of NPs facilitates the rapid adsorption of endogenous proteins and lipids upon contact with biological fluids, forming a “protein corona” or “eco-corona” [30]. This corona effectively redefines the biological identity of the particles, influencing their cellular uptake and subsequent interaction with immune receptors. Particle morphology also plays a decisive role; non-spherical NPs, such as fibers or fragments, often exhibit slower clearance rates and can induce “frustrated phagocytosis” in macrophages compared to their spherical counterparts, potentially triggering sustained inflammatory responses [31].

Consequently, NPs exhibit a heightened potential for immunotoxicity. Due to their dimensional similarity to viruses and large proteins, NPs are preferentially internalized by antigen-presenting cells (APCs) such as dendritic cells [31]. Once intracellular, they can destabilize lysosomal integrity and trigger the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome, leading to the release of IL-1β and other pro-inflammatory cytokines [31,32]. This capacity to deeply penetrate biological barriers and directly interact with subcellular structures suggests that NPs may act as more potent drivers of the immune dysregulation and sensitization processes discussed in this review [25].

The core pathophysiology of allergic diseases: immune imbalance and tolerance failure

Allergic diseases are fundamentally manifestations of failed immune regulation. Their core pathophysiology is defined by two interconnected events: a profound skewing of the immune response toward a T helper 2 (Th2) phenotype and a foundational defect in immune tolerance.

The hallmark of an allergic reaction is an excessive, Th2-dominated immune response to otherwise harmless antigens. In allergic individuals, the Th1/Th2 balance is disrupted in favor of Th2 cell differentiation. These cells secrete a signature cytokine profile – including Interleukin-4 (IL-4), IL-5, and IL-13—that orchestrates the allergic cascade. IL-4 and IL-13 drive B cells to switch to allergen-specific IgE production, while IL-5 is critical for the recruitment and activation of eosinophils [33]. The resulting IgE antibodies then bind to high-affinity receptors on mast cells and basophils. Upon subsequent allergen exposure, these sensitized cells rapidly degranulate, releasing histamine and other inflammatory mediators that precipitate the acute symptoms of a Type I hypersensitivity reaction [34].

This Th2 polarization, however, is a downstream manifestation of a deeper, more fundamental failure: the breakdown of immune tolerance. A healthy immune system relies on active suppression of responses to innocuous antigens, a process primarily governed by regulatory T cells (Tregs) [35]. Tregs function as the “brakes” of the immune system. In allergic diseases, a numerical or functional deficiency in Tregs removes this critical restraint on Th2 cells, permitting their unchecked expansion and effector function. This failure of tolerance is the permissive step that allows the development of allergy [22]. Disturbance of the gut microecology by microplastics and its axis effects

Exposure to MPs is a potential environmental factor that can trigger gut dysbiosis, significantly altering the structure, diversity, and metabolic function of the gut microbiota, as well as its immune interactions with the host. Through complex axis signaling networks, these effects can radiate to distant organs such as the liver, brain, and even the respiratory tract.

Structural remodeling and functional dysregulation of the gut microbiota

MP exposure first induces significant changes in the structure of the gut microbiota. At the phylum level, a common finding is an imbalance in the Firmicutes-to-Bacteroidetes (F/B) ratio and an increased abundance of the potentially pathogenic Proteobacteria, both of which are classic hallmarks of dysbiosis [36–38]. Beta-diversity analysis consistently confirms that MPs alter the overall community composition of the microbiota. In contrast, findings on α-diversity changes are inconsistent, showing either a decrease or an abnormal increase, the latter of which may be related to the invasion of opportunistic pathogens [37–39]. The ultimate outcome of these structural changes is often the loss of key anti-inflammatory bacteria (e.g. Faecalibacterium prausnitzii) and the colonization of opportunistic pathogens (e.g. Enterobacteriaceae) [38,40].

Changes in microbial structure directly led to the remodeling of its metabolic functions. MP exposure can cause intestinal and even systemic metabolic disorders, particularly in key metabolic pathways involving short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), bile acids (BAs), and tryptophan. Among these, alterations in the SCFA profile are directly correlated with changes in the abundance of SCFA-producing bacteria (e.g. Prevotella, Roseburia, Alistipes) and affect host systemic metabolism through pathways like the “gut-liver axis” [41–44]. Furthermore, MPs can severely disrupt microbiota-mediated bile acid and tryptophan metabolic pathways, inhibiting the production of secondary bile acids (e.g. DCA, LCA) and indole derivatives, which have important anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory functions [43,45–47]. Multi-omics joint analysis provides a complete chain of evidence demonstrating how MPs induce systemic metabolic remodeling in the host by disrupting the microecology [41,43,44].

Immune activation and cascade reactions

The dysbiosis and metabolic disorders induced by MPs are closely associated with the activation of the mucosal immune system and inflammatory responses [31,48]. MP particles themselves or their carried bacterial components (such as lipopolysaccharide, LPS) can act as danger signals, directly activating immune cells and upregulating key pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g. TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β). The underlying mechanisms involve the activation of signaling pathways such as NF-κB and the NLRP3 inflammasome [31,49,50]. Submucosal macrophages are key responders; upon phagocytosing MPs, they polarize toward a pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype. The T-cell balance is also affected, manifesting as a disrupted Th17/Treg cell balance or an altered IFN-γ/IL-4 ratio [25,31,50–52]. This series of immune activations ultimately synergizes to exacerbate intestinal barrier damage, including villus erosion, thinning of the mucus layer, and a significant decrease in the expression of key tight junction proteins (claudin-1, occludin, ZO-1), leading to increased intestinal permeability [25,48]. Microplastic-induced gut dysbiosis transmits toxicity to distant organs via distinct pathways: the Gut-Liver Axis (via portal vein transport of LPS), the Gut-Brain Axis (via vagus nerve signaling and BBB disruption), and the Gut-Airway Axis (via reduced SCFA availability impairing respiratory immune tolerance).

Gut-Liver Axis: The gut-liver axis is a critical functional pathway connecting the intestine and the liver via the portal vein. After MP exposure disrupts the intestinal barrier, harmful substances like LPS can more easily enter the portal circulation, disturbing the homeostasis of the gut-liver axis [53–55]. LPS and MP particles entering the liver are primarily captured by liver-resident macrophages (Kupffer cells), triggering liver inflammation through mechanisms such as activating the TLR4 signaling pathway or inducing pyroptosis [26,32,54]. Persistent inflammatory signals can further activate hepatic stellate cells, initiating and promoting the process of liver fibrosis [56,57]. Ultimately, the liver damage induced by MPs via the gut-liver axis primarily manifests as a “triad” of inflammation, oxidative stress, and lipid metabolism disorders, with features highly consistent with metabolic-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) [56,58–60].

Gut-Brain Axis: Micro(nano)plastics (MNPs) can not only directly penetrate the blood-brain barrier (BBB), but their neurotoxicity also indirectly causes nervous system dysfunction by interfering with the “microbiota-gut-brain axis” [28]. Evidence suggests that MPs can transmit abnormal signals to the brain via the vagus nerve pathway [61] and cause imbalances in key neurotransmitters within the brain (such as serotonin, GABA, and dopamine) by disrupting the microbiota [29,62,63]. Additionally, MPs and their carried inflammatory factors can directly compromise the integrity of the BBB, activate microglia, and induce neuroinflammation [27,64–66]. These disturbances in neurochemistry and the immune system ultimately manifest in animal models as cognitive and behavioral abnormalities, including impaired learning and memory, anxiety, and depression-like behaviors [61,62,64–66].

Gut-Airway Axis: Similar to the aforementioned axis effects, the health of the gut microecology is crucial for maintaining the immune homeostasis of the respiratory mucosa [67–69]. Key metabolites like SCFAs, produced by a healthy gut microbiota, can reach the respiratory tract via blood circulation, promoting the differentiation of local Treg cells and establishing immune tolerance to inhaled antigens. One of the core hypotheses of this review is that microplastics disrupt the immunomodulatory function of the “gut-airway axis” by selectively inhibiting SCFA-producing bacteria, leading to insufficient SCFA production. Lacking this tolerance-inducing signal, the respiratory mucosa is more likely to mount an excessive Th2-type immune response upon contact with allergens, ultimately manifesting as allergic rhinitis and asthma which are typically evaluated and diagnosed in clinical guidelines using standardized methods such as the skin prick test [70, 71].

Role of Extracellular Vesicles: Beyond metabolite-driven pathways, emerging evidence suggests that extracellular vesicles (EVs), particularly exosomes, play a pivotal role in mediating the systemic effects of microplastic exposure. Nanoplastics have been shown to alter the biogenesis and cargo of exosomes derived from intestinal epithelial cells. These modified exosomes can enter the circulation and transport pro-inflammatory microRNAs and proteins to the respiratory system, effectively bridging the gut-airway axis [72,73–75]. This mechanism provides a novel explanation for how gut-localized toxicity translates into respiratory immune dysregulation.

Integration of Molecular Networks in Axis Signaling: From a systems biology perspective, the toxic effects of MPs represent a multi-organ synergistic process that begins in the gut. Gut dysbiosis first affects the liver via the “gut-liver axis.” Subsequently, the pathological states of the gut and liver jointly influence brain function through various routes (e.g. circulating inflammatory factors, abnormal metabolites, neural signals), forming a “gut-liver-brain” tri-axis interaction [28,29,54,63]. Multi-omics integrated analysis clearly delineates how MPs transmit toxic effects across multiple organs by integrating metabolic, immune, and neural signaling pathways [62]. Diagnostics and biomarkers for allergic diseases and microplastic exposure

The precise diagnosis of allergic diseases and the accurate assessment of environmental exposure risks are two core pillars for implementing personalized prevention and treatment strategies and for investigating etiological mechanisms. Currently, the clinical diagnostic framework for allergic diseases is highly mature in its ability to delineate the final immunological phenotype, enabling targeted therapies. However, it lacks robust tools for evaluating the early environmental triggers of the disease, such as microplastic exposure, and the initial pathophysiological changes they induce, such as intestinal barrier dysfunction. Concurrently, while the emerging field of human microplastics exposure assessment has made progress in exposure detection, validated biomarkers that link exposure to clinical disease remain to be established. This chapter aims to analyze this disconnect between clinical practice and environmental health research, first by reviewing the current diagnostic paradigms that precisely characterize allergic outcomes, and subsequently by exploring emerging methods for assessing microplastic exposure. Ultimately, it argues for the critical importance of integrating these two fields to identify intersecting biomarkers for the future etiological diagnosis of allergic diseases.

The current diagnostic paradigm: precisely delineating the immune phenotype of allergic diseases

The primary objectives of the current diagnostic paradigm for allergic diseases are to identify the sensitizing allergen and to characterize the features of the inflammatory response. Traditional methods for allergen identification include in vivo skin prick tests (SPT) and in vitro serum specific IgE (sIgE) assays. However, both of these approaches, which are based on crude allergen extracts, share a core limitation: they cannot effectively distinguish between genuine sensitization and cross-reactivity. To overcome this challenge, Component-Resolved Diagnostics (CRD) enables the precise characterization of a patient’s “sensitization profile,” thereby significantly improving the accuracy and success rate of allergen-specific immunotherapy (AIT). Following the identification of sensitization, the second diagnostic step, particularly for heterogeneous diseases like asthma, is to determine the inflammatory phenotype or endotype. While pulmonary function tests (spirometry) remain the gold standard for diagnosing airflow limitation [76], modern management has shifted toward precision medicine driven by biomarkers. By measuring indicators such as fractional exhaled nitric oxide (FeNO) [77], blood/sputum eosinophil counts [78], and serum periostin [79], clinicians can accurately identify the majority of patients with “Type 2-high” inflammation. This classification not only predicts the patient’s response to corticosteroids but also serves as the basis for selecting targeted biologic therapies directed against T2 pathways (e.g. anti-IL-5/IL-5 R, anti-IL-4 Rα), heralding an era of precision treatment for asthma [80,81]. In summary, the existing diagnostic paradigm is highly adept at characterizing the final immunological outcomes of disease but does not directly assess the potential etiological factors that give rise to them.

Challenges and future directions: integrating exposomics with clinical diagnostics

Currently, the clinical diagnosis of allergic diseases and environmental health research on microplastic exposure exist as two largely independent fields with a lack of effective integration. Clinicians can precisely measure the final inflammatory manifestations of a disease but generally lack tools to assess the initiating factors; environmental scientists can detect pollutants in the body but struggle to link them directly to specific clinical phenotypes [82]. The core challenge and future direction lie in integrating these two domains to establish a complete evidence chain from “environmental exposure” to “mechanistic change” and finally to “clinical phenotype.” To this end, the primary challenge is the standardization of analytical methods. The current lack of globally unified and validated standard operating procedures (SOPs) for MP analysis severely limits the comparability of data across studies, making it exceptionally difficult to establish exposure-response relationships [83,84]. Secondly, there is a critical need for large-scale, long-term prospective human cohort studies. This approach will facilitate the screening and validation of clinically useful biomarker panels that can reflect the risk of MP-associated allergy. The ultimate vision is that the future diagnosis of allergic diseases will not only identify a “T2-high” label but will also be able to answer why it is T2-high – for instance, whether it is driven by MP-mediated intestinal barrier dysfunction and dysbiosis. Such knowledge would enable truly etiological prevention and more fundamental, personalized interventions for allergic diseases.

Therapeutic strategies and clinical practice

The effective management of allergic diseases requires a comprehensive consideration of their complex pathophysiological mechanisms and the adoption of multi-level therapeutic strategies. Therefore, current therapeutic approaches can be broadly divided into two categories. The first includes interventions targeting the early stages of the disease’s pathological process, such as repairing intestinal barrier dysfunction and correcting dysbiosis, which aim to fundamentally regulate the immune response. The second category comprises standardized treatments that target the final clinical manifestations of the disease, with the goal of effectively controlling the symptoms and inflammation of allergic rhinitis and asthma with drugs or biologics. This chapter will systematically review the latest advances in both types of strategies and will focus on discussing the future direction and potential of integrating gut microbiota interventions as an adjuvant therapy for allergic diseases, with the aim of providing a reference for constructing a more comprehensive therapeutic framework.

Intervention strategies targeting the intestinal barrier and microbiota

Given the initiating role of intestinal barrier dysfunction and dysbiosis in the development of allergic diseases, direct intervention in the gut micro-ecosystem to repair the barrier and restore immune tolerance has become a highly promising new direction for prevention and adjuvant therapy. Probiotics, through the supplementation of specific, clinically validated live microbial strains (such as specific Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium strains), can enhance barrier function through multiple mechanisms, including competitive exclusion of pathogens, production of short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), and direct modulation of the Treg/Th2 cell balance. Clinical meta-analyses have shown that supplementing with specific probiotic strains during early life (prenatally and/or in infancy) may reduce the risk of atopic dermatitis and allergic rhinitis. However, the effects are highly strain-specific and dose-dependent, and evidence for their efficacy in treating established allergic disease remains limited [85,86]. Prebiotics, such as fructo-oligosaccharides (FOS) and galacto-oligosaccharides (GOS), act as substrates for beneficial bacteria, selectively promoting their growth. Some studies suggest they may help mimic the microbiota composition of breastfed infants and could be beneficial in allergy prevention, though their efficacy depends on the host’s endogenous microbiota and the evidence for them as a standalone intervention is weaker than for probiotics [87].

Fecal Microbiota Transplantation (FMT) aims to comprehensively remodel a dysbiotic intestinal micro-ecosystem by transferring the entire functional microbiota from a healthy donor. While FMT has achieved revolutionary success in treating recurrent Clostridioides difficile infection, its application in allergic diseases is still at a very early, exploratory stage. Only case reports and small-scale studies have suggested a potential for symptom improvement, while its safety and efficacy are far from being established [88,89]. Safety is the primary concern, including the risk of transmitting pathogens and unknown long-term immunological consequences. Furthermore, FMT faces significant challenges related to the lack of standardization in donor screening, preparation processes, and delivery routes, as well as complex ethical and regulatory issues.

Specific nutrients are vital for maintaining intestinal barrier integrity and immune function. For instance, glutamine is the primary energy source for enterocyte renewal; zinc is directly involved in maintaining the integrity of epithelial tight junctions and cell proliferation; and vitamins A and D play key roles in modulating mucosal immunity, promoting IgA production, and supporting Treg cell function [90,91]. Although the mechanisms for these nutrients are well-defined, their clinical application in allergy is primarily as adjuvant nutritional support. There is currently a lack of high-quality clinical evidence to support their use as independent, primary therapeutic interventions for allergic diseases, and their optimal dosage and effect relationships require further investigation. Clinical management of allergic diseases: from standard treatment to precision medicine

For established allergic rhinitis and asthma, the core of clinical management is to effectively control symptoms, improve quality of life, and prevent exacerbations. For allergic rhinitis, first-line standard pharmacotherapies include second-generation oral antihistamines, intranasal corticosteroids (INCS), and leukotriene receptor antagonists (LTRAs), with INCS being the most effective control medication for moderate-to-severe persistent AR [76]. For asthma, management strictly follows the stepwise treatment approach recommended by the Global Initiative for Asthma (GINA), which is centered on the use of inhaled corticosteroid (ICS)-containing medications, with therapy stepped up or down based on symptom control and risk assessment [53].

For patients whose disease is poorly controlled with standard medications or who wish to alter the course of their disease, Allergen-Specific Immunotherapy (AIT) is currently the only disease-modifying treatment available [92]. In recent years, the greatest therapeutic breakthrough has been the development of precision targeted therapies for severe disease. By identifying the T2-high inflammatory phenotype, various biologics can precisely block key molecules in the allergic inflammatory pathway, offering unprecedented treatment options for patients with severe, refractory asthma and other allergic conditions [93–95]. Therapeutic outlook: integrating gut microbiota interventions with allergic disease management

Although targeted therapies against allergic inflammation have achieved tremendous success, they are costly and primarily act on the terminal effectors of the inflammatory cascade. A more forward-looking therapeutic philosophy involves combining interventions that repair the early stages of the pathological process, such as gut microbiota modulation, with the standardized management of clinical manifestations to create a synergistic, comprehensive strategy. Currently, exploring gut microbiota interventions as an adjuvant therapy for the management of asthma and other allergic diseases is an attractive area of research. The core rationale is that restoring the gut-airway axis, which may be damaged by environmental factors like microplastic exposure, through probiotics or prebiotics could potentially: 1) improve the efficacy of existing therapies, such as enhancing the response to AIT [98]; 2) reduce the reliance on medications like corticosteroids by strengthening endogenous immunoregulatory capacity; and 3) serve as a primary prevention strategy by intervening in high-risk infants during the critical early-life window to lower their future risk of developing allergic disease. Although clinical evidence in this area is still accumulating and faces many challenges, including strain selection and dosing regimens, it undoubtedly opens a new door to understanding and intervening in allergic diseases at a more fundamental level. The future of personalized therapy may lie in a “combination approach” based on a patient’s allergic phenotype, sensitization profile, and gut microbiota/metabolite characteristics, which includes both targeted drugs and microbiota-modulating interventions.

Core challenges and future outlook

The theoretical framework suggesting that microplastic exposure influences allergic diseases via an intestinal route offers a novel etiological perspective on their rising prevalence. However, it is crucial to recognize that current research is still in a preliminary stage. Before the associations within this “environment-gut-immunity” axis can be established as a definitive causal pathway, the field must confront and overcome a series of significant scientific and methodological challenges. This chapter aims to critically analyze these core challenges and, based on them, to propose an integrated outlook for future research.

First, the field faces the fundamental challenge of progressing from association to causation. The majority of current evidence is derived from in vitro or animal models using exposure doses that may not reflect real-world human scenarios. Meanwhile, epidemiological studies struggle to disentangle the specific effects of microplastic exposure from the complex milieu of the human exposome, which includes a vast array of chemical pollutants, dietary factors, and lifestyle variables [99]. It is also plausible that the direct inhalation of airborne microplastics acts synergistically with the gut-mediated pathway proposed here. Inhaled particles could prime the respiratory immune system locally, initiating inflammation [5,15], while gut-derived systemic signals simultaneously lower the threshold for sensitization. This “two-hit” exposure model represents a critical area for future investigation.

It is imperative to distinguish established evidence from hypotheses. While animal models have demonstrated a clear causal link between MP exposure, dysbiosis, and immune skewing [8,25,38], evidence in humans remains largely correlational [51]. Future research must bridge this gap to confirm whether the mechanistic pathways elucidated in preclinical studies – such as the specific suppression of SCFA-producing bacteria – directly translate to human allergic pathology [8,37]. Compounding this is the profound uncertainty in the dose-response relationship. Microplastics are not a monolithic entity; their toxicity is intricately linked to a diverse range of physicochemical properties, including size, shape, polymer type, and adsorbed chemical additives. This heterogeneity makes the establishment of a universal risk assessment model or a safe exposure threshold exceptionally difficult [100]. To address these issues, future research must move beyond cross-sectional studies and prioritize the launch of large-scale, long-term prospective human cohort studies, particularly those that begin in utero or early infancy. Such studies should integrate high-resolution exposomics to more accurately assess the microplastic burden and its long-term impact on the risk of allergic disease within a real-world context [46].

Second, a deeper understanding of the underlying biological mechanisms and the impact of inter-individual variability is required. At present, many molecular events within the “microplastic-microbiota-barrier-immunity” signaling pathway remain a “black box.” This is further complicated by the high degree of inter-individual variability in the human gut microbiome, which makes defining a universal standard for “dysbiosis” challenging and contributes to the inconsistent outcomes of microbiota-based interventions [11]. Therefore, future mechanistic research must shift from macroscopic descriptions to microscopic resolution. The use of advanced tools such as multi-omics technologies, gut organoid models, and humanized gnotobiotic mice is essential to precisely delineate the key molecules, receptors, and cell types involved in this pathway. The focus of such research should evolve from purely taxonomic characterizations of the microbiota to a functional analysis (e.g. assessing the capacity for SCFA production). This will not only clarify core mechanisms but also provide the scientific basis for developing personalized intervention strategies that target specific microbial functions [98].

Finally, methodological standardization and the clinical translation of research findings represent a critical bottleneck. The lack of globally unified and cross-validated standard operating procedures (SOPs) for the detection and quantification of microplastics in human biological samples severely limits the comparability of data across studies, posing a major obstacle to progress in the field [83,84]. Concurrently, the long-term efficacy and safety of promising gut-targeted interventions, such as FMT, must be rigorously evaluated in large-scale randomized controlled trials (RCTs) before they can be considered for clinical use in non-enteric diseases like allergies [101]. Consequently, future progress depends on breakthroughs in technology development to create high-throughput, standardized analytical platforms. In the realm of therapeutic innovation, a deeper mechanistic understanding should guide the development of novel interventions, such as engineered probiotics or barrier-enhancing drugs, which can then be rigorously tested for their clinical utility as adjuvants to standard allergy care.

In conclusion, while the challenges are formidable, the potential of this research avenue is immense. Future progress requires a synergistic, multi-level approach that connects precise exposure assessment, deep mechanistic investigation, large-scale human validation, innovative therapeutic development, and ultimately, public health policy. Such a systematic effort will not only elucidate the definitive role of microplastics in allergic diseases but will also pave the way for true etiological prevention and more fundamental interventions for these complex chronic conditions, holding profound strategic importance for public health.

Conclusion

Against the backdrop of the growing global burden of allergic diseases, the search for their environmental drivers has become a critical public health priority. This review has focused on microplastics to systematically construct a pathophysiological pathway through which they may act as a potential contributing factor. This pathway initiates with the physical and chemical damage to the intestinal barrier, which subsequently triggers a functional dysbiosis characterized by the diminished capacity of key commensal bacteria, particularly in producing SCFAs. This functional disturbance ultimately creates a state of susceptibility for allergic inflammation in the distal respiratory tract by disrupting the immunomodulatory balance of the “gut-airway axis.”

This body of evidence collectively points to a central viewpoint: the integrity of the intestinal barrier is not only a critical node in the internal immune dysregulation of allergic diseases but may also serve as a sensitive window for assessing the health effects of environmental pollutants. Therefore, integrating environmental exposure assessment with the monitoring of intestinal health status could offer a novel, integrated paradigm for understanding and intervening in modern chronic inflammatory diseases. Although this theoretical framework is logically consistent, several critical knowledge gaps remain. The most central challenges lie in confirming a direct causal effect for microplastics and in elucidating their dose-response relationships. Concurrently, the field still lacks validated, specific biomarkers that can reflect the biological effects of microplastic exposure. Therefore, advancing through methodological standardization, large-scale human cohort studies, and in-depth mechanistic exploration is the essential path forward to translate the scientific discoveries in this emerging field into effective public health policies and clinical intervention strategies.
