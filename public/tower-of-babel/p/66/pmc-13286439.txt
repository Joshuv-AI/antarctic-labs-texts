Association of hydrochlorothiazide exposure with all-cause mortality among US cancer survivors: A prospective cohort study
Authors: Qiang Li, Zheng-Hong Xiao, Peng-Hao Wen, Xu Zhang, Ping Li, Yan-Hua Ding, Hong Yang, Tian-Cai Wang, Tian-Yu Li, Bo Zhao, Jiang-Xia Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hydrochlorothiazide (HCTZ) is commonly prescribed to cancer patients. However, there is a paucity of research examining the effects of HCTZ exposure on the mortality risk of cancer survivors. We constructed a prospective cohort using data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey from 2003 to 2018 to investigate the associations between HCTZ exposure and the risk of all-cause mortality among cancer survivors. The participants were categorized into 2 groups based on their self-reported use of HCTZ. Multivariate-adjusted Cox proportional hazards models were used to assess the survival outcomes between groups. Multivariate-adjusted logistic regression models were conducted to investigate the association between HCTZ exposure and the risk of cardiovascular disease and cerebrovascular disease. The final cohort comprised 4202 cancer survivors, with a mean age (standard deviation) of 66.01 ± 14.35 years. Of these, 53.2% were females, and 68.4% were non-Hispanic White individuals. The medication stratification identified 3517 non-HCTZ-exposed individuals and 685 HCTZ-exposed individuals. During a mean follow-up of 6.93 years (maximum 17 years), 1337 deaths were recorded. Compared with non-HCTZ-exposed individuals, HCTZ-exposed individuals presented reduced risk of all-cause mortality (hazard ratio = 0.80, 95% confidence interval [CI] = 0.67–0.95; P = .003). Cross-sectional studies have indicated that exposure to HCTZ is associated with a lower risk of angina pectoris (odds ratio = 0.81, 95% CI = 0.72–0.93; P < .001) and heart attack (odds ratio = 0.89, 95% CI = 0.84–0.95; P = .001). HCTZ exposure is likely correlated with a lower risk of all-cause mortality in cancer survivors. However, HCTZ use among participants was assessed only once via self-report, lacking detailed information on dosage, duration of use, indication, cancer stage, and treatment regimens, precluding definitive conclusions regarding a protective effect of HCTZ. Further research is warranted to confirm these findings.

1. Introduction

Cancer remains one of the leading causes of death worldwide, imposing a substantial economic burden on global communities,[1,2] although recent advances in medical technologies have significantly extended the survival of cancer patients.[3–5] The management of comorbidities among this population has increasingly become a complex clinical challenge.[6–11] Hydrochlorothiazide (HCTZ) is a commonly used agent frequently prescribed to cancer survivors for comorbidity management.[12–14] Inappropriate use of such medications may lead to severe adverse outcomes, including death.[15,16] Notably, accumulating evidence presents conflicting perspectives on HCTZ’s effects among cancer patients. Several studies have reported associations between HCTZ use and elevated risks of keratinocyte carcinoma and melanoma,[17–19] while numerous investigations suggest that HCTZ may confer cardiovascular benefits, reduce proteinuria, and thereby improve patient prognosis.[20,21] Given this limited and inconsistent evidence regarding the impact of HCTZ on outcomes among cancer survivors, further rigorous investigation is warranted.

Our study aims to address this knowledge gap by examining the association between HCTZ use and all-cause mortality in a nationally representative US cohort of cancer survivors with adequate follow-up duration. We found that HCTZ use may be a potential protective factor against mortality risk in cancer patients. Our findings offer critical insights to inform optimal medication selection in this vulnerable population. Importantly, as our study employs an observational design, residual confounding cannot be ruled out, causal inference should be interpreted with caution.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Study population and data sources

The study cohort was derived from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), a nationally representative surveillance program. The NHANES is a publicly accessible database approved by the US National Center for Health Statistics Institutional Review Board. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants during the national survey. As this study involved secondary analysis without additional data collection, no further Institutional Review Board approval was needed. The NHANES employs a systematic multistage stratified sampling design to recruit participants across diverse geographic regions and racial/ethnic groups, ensuring population-level generalizability. The NHANES incorporate special weights which comprehensively address nonresponse bias, oversampling, and post-stratification adjustments. Firstly, the NHANES survey weights account for unequal selection probabilities, thereby mitigating the influence of oversampling certain subpopulations. Secondly, they adjust for participant nonresponse by redistributing weight to respondents within similar demographic and socioeconomic strata, ensuring representativeness despite missing data. Finally, they are calibrated through post-stratification so that weighted survey estimates align with national benchmarks from the US Census Bureau, using the household as the primary sampling unit. This ongoing cross-sectional survey series has operated biennially since 1999. Our analysis incorporated 8 consecutive NHANES cycles (2003–2018). Cancer diagnosis and histologic types were ascertained through structured interviews. Participants who reported a history of at least 1 cancer diagnosis, specifically, those who answered “Yes” to the survey question “Ever told you had cancer or malignancy?” were classified as cancer survivors and included in this study. Individuals who responded “No,” declined to answer, indicated “Don’t know,” or had missing data for this question were excluded. Our cohort includes individuals with multiple primary cancers. However, due to substantial missingness in the variable indicating multiple cancer diagnoses, this factor was not further analyzed or adjusted for in our models. The progression of cancer is estimated based on the number of years the patient has had cancer at the time of the interview, which is determined by 2 primary factors: the age of the patient at the time of the interview and the age at which the cancer was initially diagnosed. Follow-up visits for participants are conducted annually, and follow-up data were obtained through linkage with the National Death Index as of December 31, 2019 (https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/). Other variables, including age, sex, race/ethnicity, education level, smoking status, alcohol consumption, and clinical comorbidities, were extracted from NHANES cycles between 2003 and 2018. The number of cases with missing variables and follow-up data was relatively small, so we directly excluded these cases. Figure 1 shows the participant selection flowchart and exclusion criteria.

2.2. Primary drug exposure and covariates

The primary exposure to HCTZ was determined through verification of prescription medications during household interviews. Based on self-reported medication use, the participants were categorized into the HCTZ-exposed cohort and the non-HCTZ-exposed cohort. The potential covariates included age, sex, race/ethnicity (categorized as non-Hispanic White, non-Hispanic Black, Mexican American, other Hispanic, and other), body mass index (BMI), education level (<high school, high school or equivalent, college or equivalent), socioeconomic status measured by the poverty-to-income ratio, smoking status, alcohol consumption and comorbidities, which may be related to the use of HCTZ, such as congestive heart failure, liver disease, and renal insufficiency/failure. Participants who smoked fewer than 100 cigarettes were classified as “never,” whereas those who smoked more than 100 cigarettes were classified as “current/former.” Participants who consumed fewer than 12 alcoholic drinks were classified as “never,” whereas those who consumed more than 12 drinks were classified as “current/former.”

2.3. Statistical analysis

The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. As all continuous variables in this study met the assumption of normality, they are presented as mean ± standard deviation. Intergroup differences for continuous variables were evaluated using the independent samples t test. Categorical variables are reported as frequencies (counts) and corresponding percentages. Differences in the distribution of categorical variables across groups were assessed using Pearson chi-square test. We strictly adhered to the analytical guidelines of the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention and applied the full-sample survey weights provided by NHANES in all Cox regression and logistic regression analyses to generate unbiased population-level estimates for the noninstitutionalized civilian US adult population. Multivariate-adjusted weighted Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to assess the risks of all-cause mortality across groups. In our Cox proportional hazards models, the time scale is defined as “time-to-event” measured in years. Time zero (t = 0) corresponds to the baseline survey date for each participant (i.e., the NHANES interview date). The event time is defined as the interval from baseline to the occurrence of either of the following: death from a specific cause, as ascertained by the National Death Index; or the administrative censoring date of December 31, 2019. Participants who remained event-free by the end of the follow-up period were right-censored at the study cutoff date. We assessed the proportional hazards assumption for all continuous and categorical covariates included in the Cox model using Schoenfeld residuals. Specifically, we computed global Schoenfeld residuals for each covariate and performed a rank-based chi-square test. All covariates yielded P values >.05, indicating no statistically significant evidence of violation of the proportional hazards assumption. Multivariate-adjusted weighted logistic regression models were used to assess the risks of cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases. Prior to finalizing our multivariable models, we conducted comprehensive diagnostic checks. For both logistic regression and Cox proportional hazards models, we calculated variance inflation factors for all continuous covariates and dummy-coded categorical predictors included in the models. All variance inflation factor values were below 5, indicating the absence of substantial multicollinearity among the explanatory variables. In addition, we examined standardized residuals from the multivariable logistic regression models and identified no extreme outliers that would unduly influence model estimates. The partially adjusted models were corrected only for age, sex, and race. Variables demonstrating statistically significant differences between groups were incorporated as covariates within the multivariate adjustment model. An analysis was conducted to assess the mortality risk associated with exposure to HCTZ among patients with various tumor types. This analysis was undertaken to validate the robustness of the study findings. This study did not prespecify subgroup analyses a priori. Given that small subgroup sizes could amplify the influence of random variation on outcome estimates, we conducted sensitivity analyses restricted to cancer types self-reported by more than 200 participants. However, due to the high heterogeneity in reported cancer types and substantial missing data regarding cancer diagnosis in the study population, subgroup analyses across specific cancer types lacked sufficient robustness to support definitive conclusions. Accordingly, we supplemented our analysis by reporting interaction P values between the primary exposure (HCTZ use) and each covariate included in the multivariable Cox proportional hazards model. For the interaction analyses, continuous variables such as age and BMI were dichotomized using clinically meaningful thresholds: BMI was stratified at 28 kg/m2, and age at 65 years. Binary covariates were stratified according to their original categories. For race/ethnicity, which is a multinomial variable in NHANES, the number of participants in all categories other than non-Hispanic White was considerably smaller. To ensure adequate statistical power and model stability, we collapsed all non-Hispanic White groups into a single category, thereby converting race into a binary variable (non-Hispanic White vs other) for interaction testing. Two-sided tests were used for statistical significance, with a threshold of P < .05. All data analyses were conducted via R software (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, version 4.4.2). Multivariate-adjusted weighted Cox and logistic regression analyses were conducted using the “survey” package, this software package is specifically designed for the analysis of complex survey data and is recommended by Centers for Disease Control and Prevention for NHANES data analysis. Data visualization was achieved through the forestploter package.

3. Results

3.1. Baseline characteristics

The analytical cohort included 4202 cancer survivors, representing a weighted population of 21,676,631 individuals at the national level. After weighting, the mean age (standard deviation) of the participants was 66.01 (14.35) years, with 53.2% identified as female. The majority of participants (2733 individuals, 68.4%) were non-Hispanic White. The other racial/ethnic groups included 600 non-Hispanic Black individuals (14.3%), 281 Mexican Americans (6.7%), 230 other Hispanic individuals (5.5%), and 215 individuals from other racial groups (5.1%). Among the participants, 3517 were never exposed to HCTZ, and 685 were exposed to HCTZ (Table 1). With a mean follow-up of 6.93 years (83.17 months; range: 0.1–17 years), 1337 all-cause deaths were observed.

3.3. Associations of HCTZ exposure with the risk of cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases

Cross-sectional studies examining exposure to HCTZ in relation to cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases indicate that, compared with individuals never exposed to HCTZ, those exposed to HCTZ were associated with a reduced risk of angina pectoris (odds ratio = 0.81, 95% CI = 0.72–0.93, P < .001) and heart attack (odds ratio = 0.89, 95% CI = 0.84–0.95, P = .001). No association was found between HCTZ exposure and coronary heart disease or stroke (Table 3). However, these findings are derived from a cross-sectional study, therefore, the temporal relationship between the exposure and the outcome remains unclear. Moreover, the limited number of cases precludes any definitive inference regarding a causal association between the exposure and the observed events.

3.4. Sensitivity analysis

Consistent with the main findings of this study, decreased risks of all-cause mortality were observed with HCTZ exposure in subgroups of breast cancer, prostate cancer, melanoma, and nonmelanoma skin cancer patients (Fig. 2). HCTZ-exposed cervical cancer patients did not exhibit a survival advantage. This may be attributable to the limited number of individual cases observed within the HCTZ exposure group. We also compared cancer survivors who used HCTZ with those who did not use HCTZ but were treated with furosemide. The analysis revealed that HCTZ users had a lower risk of all-cause mortality, consistent with the potential protective association observed in our primary analysis (Table 4). Furthermore, we examined potential interactions between HCTZ exposure and key covariates (Table 5). Our analysis identified BMI as a significant effect modifier, with a statistically significant interaction (P = .002). Specifically, among individuals with BMI < 28 kg/m2, HCTZ exposure was associated with a significantly lower risk of all-cause mortality (HR = 0.66, 95% CI = 0.52–0.91, P = .007). In contrast, among those with BMI ≥ 28 kg/m2, HCTZ exposure was associated with an increased mortality risk (HR = 1.22, 95% CI = 1.09–1.41, P = .029).

4. Discussion

This study explored the relationships between HCTZ exposure and the hazards of all-cause mortality among cancer survivors. Prior research on diuretics in patients with cancer has focused largely on cancer incidence after drug exposure. To our knowledge, no studies have assessed how HCTZ exposure influences the risk of all-cause mortality among cancer survivors. In a nationally representative cohort of US cancer survivors, we applied survey-weighted Cox proportional hazards models reflecting the complex survey design and observed that, compared with individuals with no HCTZ exposure, HCTZ exposure was associated with a lower hazard of all-cause mortality. However, as our study is observational in nature, the findings may be subject to unmeasured residual confounding and indication bias. These methodological challenges substantially limit our ability to establish a causal relationship between HCTZ exposure and all-cause mortality risk among cancer survivors, necessitating cautious interpretation of the results.

In this study, participants with missing data on exposure, covariates, or survival outcomes were excluded from the analysis. The number of cases with missing exposure or survival information was minimal and thus unlikely to materially affect our findings. However, a relatively larger number of participants had missing covariate data. Since most covariates were collected via interview, missingness primarily resulted from participant refusal to answer or unclear responses. Notably, older individuals and those with a higher burden of comorbidities were more likely to be unable to provide reliable answers due to cognitive or memory difficulties. Consequently, our complete-case analysis may have introduced selection bias against these vulnerable subgroups, potentially limiting the internal validity of our estimates and undermining the generalizability of our findings to the broader population of cancer survivors. To address this methodological limitation, we conducted sensitivity analyses aimed at assessing the robustness of our results. While these efforts enhance the reliability and validity of our conclusions, residual bias due to selective exclusion of certain subpopulations cannot be entirely ruled out.

Several studies have linked HCTZ use to a higher incidence of skin cancer, lip cancer, and breast cancer.[17,22,23] In contrast, a German cohort study revealed no such pattern for other diuretics.[24] However, the prognostic implications of HCTZ exposure among cancer survivors remain unexplored.

HCTZ shares an unsubstituted sulfonamide moiety with carbonic anhydrase inhibitors. While it retains modest carbonic anhydrase–inhibitory activity, its diuretic action reflects additional, more complex mechanisms.[25] Specifically, HCTZ inhibits the apical electroneutral Na+-Cl− cotransporter in the early distal convoluted tubule, thereby blocking sodium reabsorption at this site, increasing sodium delivery to the collecting duct, promoting natriuresis, and enhancing K+ and Mg2+ exchange with consequent urinary losses of these ions. Compared with furosemide, HCTZ has a longer duration of action, although the basis for this persistence has not been fully elucidated. Evidence suggests that vasodilation occurs via potassium channel activation.[26] Initiation of therapy reduces plasma volume and cardiac output.[27,28] With chronic use, continued low-grade diuresis and mild volume contraction lower systemic vascular resistance and improve hemodynamics, with plasma volume and cardiac output returning to baseline.[29] These hemodynamic benefits may underlie the influence of HCTZ on the prognosis of patients with cancer. Moreover, thiazides are more effective than furosemide at reducing proteinuria, which may further contribute to improved outcomes in this population.[20] Prior studies indicate that HCTZ lowers the risk of cardiovascular events.[21] Consistent with this, our cross-sectional analysis revealed lower risks of acute cardiovascular events, including angina pectoris and heart attack, among individuals with HCTZ exposure. This suggests that the reduction in all-cause mortality risk associated with HCTZ may be linked to a decrease in the incidence of acute cardiovascular events.

This study, based on the NHANES database, represents the first investigation into the association between HCTZ exposure and all-cause mortality risk among cancer survivors. Overall, HCTZ use was associated with a reduced risk of all-cause mortality. However, interaction analyses revealed an important effect modifier: BMI. Specifically, among individuals with BMI < 28 kg/m2, HCTZ exposure was associated with lower mortality risk, whereas in those with BMI ≥ 28 kg/m2, HCTZ exposure was paradoxically associated with higher mortality risk. This suggests that BMI may substantially alter the long-term biological effects or risk–benefit profile of HCTZ in cancer survivors. Several potential mechanisms may underlie this interaction. HCTZ is a hydrophilic drug whose volume of distribution is influenced by body composition. Individuals with obesity (BMI ≥ 28 kg/m2) typically have a higher proportion of adipose tissue and altered lean body mass, which may affect the drug’s distribution, metabolism, and clearance, potentially leading to differences in systemic or tissue-level drug exposure and thereby modifying its long-term effects. Moreover, HCTZ is commonly prescribed for hypertension, a condition strongly linked to increased cardiovascular mortality in cancer survivors. In leaner individuals (BMI < 28 kg/m2), the cardiovascular benefits of blood pressure control may predominate. In contrast, among those with obesity, the adverse metabolic effects of HCTZ, such as hypokalemia and hyperuricemia, may outweigh its antihypertensive benefits, resulting in a net increase in risk. This finding underscores the critical importance of considering body composition and metabolic status when evaluating the long-term impact of medications in cancer survivors. It further suggests that clinicians should exercise caution when prescribing HCTZ to obese cancer survivors and consider close monitoring of metabolic parameters. Nevertheless, it must be emphasized that, as an observational study, our findings are subject to residual confounding and cannot establish causality. Future research should validate this interaction in prospective cohorts or randomized controlled trials and explore the underlying molecular and physiological mechanisms.

Our study offers several novel contributions. We leveraged data from a US health survey with national representativeness, yielding a large, population-based sample. We applied survey-weighted multivariable Cox proportional hazards models and incorporated a broad set of potential confounders, including demographic and socioeconomic factors, comorbidity profiles, and time since cancer diagnosis. The findings of this study suggest that HCTZ has the potential to enhance the prognosis of cancer survivors, thereby providing insights to support the optimization of medication choices for this vulnerable population.

However, our study also has several limitations that cannot be ignored. As an observational study, we were unable to include all potential factors that affect patient survival. Cancer characteristics in this study were based solely on self-report, precluding access to critical clinical details such as tumor stage and treatment regimens. Similarly, exposure data relied on participant recall, resulting in the absence of essential pharmacological information, including drug dosage, duration of use, and the primary indication for HCTZ prescription (e.g., hypertension, heart failure). Furthermore, the observational design and reliance on retrospective self-reported data introduce the possibility of survivor bias, as individuals who survived long enough to participate in NHANES may differ systematically from those who died earlier. Together with unmeasured or residual confounding, particularly indication bias, these limitations significantly constrain our ability to infer a causal protective effect of HCTZ on all-cause mortality among cancer survivors. In addition, the observed associations between HCTZ use and specific cardiovascular or cerebrovascular events stem from cross-sectional analyses, which cannot establish temporal sequence or causality. Future studies with prospective designs, larger sample sizes, detailed clinical and medication records, and longitudinal follow-up are needed to rigorously evaluate the relationship between HCTZ use and long-term outcomes in cancer survivors.

5. Conclusion

This nationwide cohort study suggests a potential association between HCTZ exposure and survival benefit among cancer survivors. However, our findings represent an observational association that may be influenced by residual confounding, and therefore cannot be used to establish a causal relationship. Future research employing more rigorous study designs and larger sample sizes is warranted to confirm the association between HCTZ use and all-cause mortality risk in cancer survivors.
