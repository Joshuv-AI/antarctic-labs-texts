Background

Non-communicable diseases (NCDs), such as cardiovascular disease and type 2 diabetes, are the leading cause of mortality in Europe, accounting for approximately 87% of all deaths among individuals aged 30 to 70 years [1]. These diseases are largely preventable through the reduction of modifiable risk factors, including poor diet, physical inactivity, harmful alcohol consumption, and smoking. However, NCDs disproportionately impact specific populations, with socioeconomic inequalities playing a pivotal role in shaping both risk exposure and health outcomes [2, 3]. Communities experiencing disadvantage often face systemic barriers to health, such as limited access to quality healthcare, lower health literacy, fewer opportunities for healthy living, and greater exposure to environmental and behavioural risk factors [1].

Rapid urbanisation across Europe has further intensified these health inequities [4]. While cities offer opportunities for improved services and infrastructure, they also concentrate risk factors that disproportionately affect people experiencing disadvantage, such as ethnic minorities or low income populations. Risk factors include air pollution, sedentary lifestyles, inadequate housing, unhealthy food environments, and social isolation [4]. In this context, the intersection of urbanization and social disadvantage creates a compounded risk for the development of NCDs, making prevention efforts more complex and urgent.

Community-based interventions have emerged as a critical strategy in addressing these challenges and promoting health equity [5]. By engaging local communities and tailoring interventions to their specific needs, these programmes have the potential to reduce health inequities, empower individuals, create supportive environments, and enhance access to preventive services. However, evidence regarding the effectiveness of those interventions, particularly in urban settings with disadvantaged communities across European contexts, remains limited.

The aim of this paper is to present the evaluation framework for the Health Outcomes from Raised Urban Settings (HORUS) programme. The HORUS programme is a community-based programme designed to reduce key modifiable NCD risk factors and will be implemented in disadvantaged communities across three European cities.

HORUS

The HORUS evaluation study will implement and evaluate a community-based programme, based on the social-ecological model, to promote healthier lifestyles and reduce key modifiable NCD risk factors across three European cities: Rijeka, Croatia; Rotterdam, The Netherlands, and Valencia, Spain. The HORUS study sites reflect diverse urban contexts and welfare and healthcare system models. HORUS was established in response to the HORIZON EUROPE call “Interventions in city environments to reduce risk of non-communicable disease’’, funded under grant agreement No 101136516.

Objectives

The primary objective of this study is to evaluate the effectiveness and implementation of the HORUS programme. Specifically, the study aims to: (1) assess the impact of the HORUS programme on lifestyle indicators associated with the prevention of non-communicable diseases (NCDs); and (2) evaluate key implementation outcomes, including acceptability, appropriateness, feasibility, adoption, penetration, implementation costs, and sustainability.

Hypotheses

Our hypothesis is that persons participating in the HORUS programme report healthier diets, higher physical activity, lower alcohol use and reduced smoking compared to those in the control condition.

Methods

This study protocol adhered to the SPIRIT guidelines for clinical trial protocol [6].

Study design

The evaluation of HORUS is designed as a convergent parallel mixed-methods, controlled study, comprising an impact evaluation and an evaluation of implementation outcomes. The study includes an intervention group receiving the HORUS programme and a control group that continues their usual activities. The quantitative component involves measurements collected at baseline (T0) and after 9 months (T1), corresponding to the end of the intervention period, in both groups. Additionally, administrative records will be used to measure implementation outcomes. The qualitative component consists of focus group discussions at baseline, after 4 months and after 9 months, with participants in the intervention group, aiming to explore their experiences and perceptions of the programme. Quantitative and qualitative data will be collected and analysed concurrently, with integration during interpretation to provide a comprehensive understanding of outcome measures. In each of the three European cities (Rijeka, Croatia; Rotterdam, The Netherlands, and Valencia, Spain), intervention and control sites are chosen. Baseline data collection is scheduled to begin in the summer of 2025.

Study population

All participants who provide informed consent will be enrolled in the study. The target is to recruit a total of 900 participants. Each study site will allocate 150 participants to the intervention condition and 150 participants to the control condition. Randomisation may be applied at either the individual or cluster level to assign participants to the intervention or control groups. Study participants will be eligible if they meet the following criteria: (i) are aged 18 years or older, (ii) are at risk for non-communicable diseases (NCDs) or have a diagnosis of type 2 diabetes and/or cardiovascular disease (CVD), (iii) reside in selected neighbourhoods within one of the pilot sites (Rijeka, Rotterdam, or Valencia), (iv) are capable of providing informed consent, and (v) have comprehension of the local language. Neighbourhoods were selected based on elevated prevalence of NCDs, lower household income, lower educational attainment, lower walkability, access to services, and proximity to green spaces, relative to other neighbourhoods. Participants will be excluded from the study if they: (i) reside outside the selected neighbourhoods, (ii) are unable to adequately engage with the intervention activities proposed at the study site due to factors such as severe physical and intellectual disabilities, and language barriers, or (iii) are unwilling to provide informed consent.

Recruitment of study participants and baseline data collection is scheduled to begin in the summer of 2025. Potential participants residing within the catchment area of an intervention or control site will be provided with information about the study and an invitation to provide informed consent to participate. The study site team will facilitate this process by directly engaging with potential participants. In addition to direct outreach, recruitment could be supported through collaboration with local stakeholders (e.g., community centres, residents’ clubs, religious organisations), health and social care providers, as well as through the use of posters, advertisements, and recruitment events. These efforts aim to raise awareness of the study and encourage participation from individuals who meet the eligibility criteria. To encourage participation, study sites may offer incentives to intervention and control group participants, such as financial compensation.

Intervention condition

Participants assigned to the intervention condition will receive the HORUS programme. The HORUS programme is grounded in the theoretical framework of the social-ecological model [7]. This model distinguishes between factors, such as age, sex, and genetics, which by definition are not modifiable, and a range of potentially modifiable factors organised into multiple layers of influence. These layers encompass individual lifestyle choices, as well as physical and social surroundings, along with broader socio-economic, cultural, and environmental conditions. The social-ecological model illustrates how individuals, communities, and their physical, social, and political environments interact with and impact health and wellbeing. Based on the theoretical framework a general implementation framework is developed. Each pilot site has the possibility to adapt the programme to its local context. Core elements of the HORUS programme include: an assessment, Motivational Interviewing sessions, and the Wakamola support app.

The HORUS programme starts with a structured assessment, using self-reported questionnaires to evaluate lifestyle, self-efficacy, health outcomes, and sense of belonging to the neighbourhood. The purpose of this assessment is to explore the individual’s health and lifestyle. The assessment informs the development of tailored health and lifestyle support strategies using Motivational Interviewing sessions [8].

Following the assessment, participants will take part in Motivational Interviewing sessions aimed at enhancing intrinsic motivation for adopting a healthier lifestyle. The number of sessions depends on the participant’s motivation and progress. A minimum of three sessions is expected to take place during the intervention period. Sessions are delivered by health and social professionals, with the first session preferably scheduled within two weeks following completion of the assessment.

Based on the outcomes of both the assessment and the initial Motivational Interview, a personalised plan will be jointly developed by the participant and the professional. This co-produced plan will be reviewed periodically during follow-up sessions and adjusted as needed to address participant’s evolving health and wellbeing needs.

The personal plan will include pathways designed to support a healthy lifestyle. Each participant’s plan will incorporate at least two core pathways: diet and physical activity. Additionally, the plan can be supplemented with activities addressing alcohol consumption, smoking, and sleep. Each pathway comprises motivating messages, referrals to local activities and/or referral to relevant prevention and care professionals. Specific activities at each pilot site will be selected based on local relevance and resource availability. Examples of such activities include walking groups, yoga classes, and cooking workshops.

The HORUS technical solution supports the delivery of the pathways. It includes a mobile application (the Wakamola app) [9, 10], specifically designed to enhance citizen engagement and collect users’ data related to their daily activity. The app is connected to a secure, web-based application (the Wakamola-HORUS dashboard) and serves as a communication channel to send motivational messages, announce local lifestyle-related events, and provide users with insights into their own health, particularly regarding physical activity and nutrition, by making use of self-reported questionnaires. These questionnaires are also used to monitor participants’ progress and personalise support [11].

With informed consent, health and social care professionals can access the Wakamola-HORUS dashboard, enabling them to track participants’ progress over time and tailor the programme as needed.

Control condition

In the control condition, participants continue their usual activities, and no intervention measures are implemented.

Data collection

Data will be collected using a complementary, mixed-methods approach. Self-reported questionnaires will be used to assess lifestyle, self-efficacy, health outcomes, and sense of belonging to the neighbourhood. Sensor-based data will be gathered to capture objective indicators relevant to the programme. In addition, qualitative data on participants’ lived experiences will be collected to provide contextual insights into the programme’s implementation and perceived impact. The Wakamola-HORUS dashboard will support both the collection and integration of data from multiple sources, facilitating comprehensive analysis across methods.

Impact evaluation

Data will be collected through self-reported questionnaires filled in by participants from both study groups at baseline and after 9 months. The questionnaires can be completed on paper or digitally through the Wakamola-HORUS dashboard. Assistance to fill in the questionnaire will be provided by the research team, if necessary. For instruments lacking validated translations, a standardized forward–backward translation procedure will be employed. Impact outcome measures and instruments

The primary outcome is lifestyle, measured across four domains: diet, physical activity, alcohol consumption and smoking. This is measured by the Rapid Prime Diet Quality Score Screener (rPDQS) [12], the International Physical Activity Questionnaire Short Form (IPAQ-SF) [13], the Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test (AUDIT-C) [14], and 4 items on smoking status, including the use of vapes.

Secondary outcomes measured across all three pilot sites, include health-related self-efficacy, Body Mass Index (BMI), chronic conditions, health-related quality of life (HRQoL), and neighbourhood cohesion. Depending on the study site, additional data may be collected on sleep quality, motivation, hip and waist circumference, medication use, and environmental perceptions. Methods and instruments were selected based on validation, and comprehensiveness, as well as their suitability for self-administration.

Additionally, with permission of the participant, sensor-based data will be collected from their smartphones, using the Wakamola app [11]. Sensor-based data are collected to optimise the app’s functionalities and provide a more comprehensive understanding of participants’ daily activities (digital phenotyping). Respondents retain full control over sensor permissions, which can be managed at any time via their device settings. These permissions remain adjustable throughout the study period, allowing users to enable or disable access according to their preferences. Evaluation of implementation outcomes

The implementation outcome evaluation is guided by the taxonomy proposed by Proctor et al. [24], encompassing the following key domains: acceptability, appropriateness, feasibility, adoption, penetration, implementation costs, and sustainability. A mixed-methods approach is employed to assess these outcomes, integrating data from multiple sources. These include a self-reported follow-up questionnaire administered after 9 months (end of the intervention period), focus group discussions with participants and stakeholders, and routinely collected administrative data from the pilot sites. This multi-source strategy allows for a comprehensive assessment of both perceived and actual implementation processes and impacts. Implementation outcome measures and instruments

Adopting a realist evaluation approach, focus groups will be conducted at three time points: the start of the intervention, after 4 months, and at the end of the 9-month intervention period. At each study site, separate focus groups will be held with citizens (n = 6–9) and stakeholders (n = 6–9). These sessions aim to identify and refine the underlying mechanisms through which the intervention is expected to produce outcomes, while also accounting for the specific contextual factors of each pilot site. In addition, the focus groups after 4 months and after 9 months will assess the implementation process in two key domains: (1) the appropriateness of the intervention, by exploring its relevance and perceived alignment with participants’ needs and contexts, and (2) its feasibility, by examining practical barriers and facilitators to the integration and daily use of intervention components such as the Wakamola app.

Power consideration

In each of the three sites, 150 participants will be included in the intervention group and 150 participants in the control group. Thus, we aim to include a total of 900 participants in the pilot sites in the three countries combined (for numbers per pilot site, see Table 3). Assuming a 20% participant loss [25] to follow up between T0 and T1 (e.g., due to disability, rehousing, mortality, study withdrawal) we expect to get complete data from 360 participants in the intervention group and 360 participants in the control group of all sites at follow-up; in total n = 720 study participants. We assume equal standard deviations in the intervention group and the control group, alpha of 0.05 and power of 0.80. Thus, given three participating pilot sites with each an intervention group and control group, applying a correction factor to account for the clustered design, assuming an average cluster size of 120 citizens (720/6) and an intra class correlation coefficient of 0.02. For this expected overall sample size and assumptions, with regards to the continuous outcome measures a difference of 0.23 SD (standard deviation) between the intervention and the control group can be detected at follow-up. This means that both at the European level, and in addition in each pilot separately, small differences regarding the outcomes in the intervention group compared to the control group can be shown [26].

Data management and analysis

Data from all study sites will be pseudo-anonymised and integrated into a secured web-based application managed by Universitat Politècnica de València (Spain). Erasmus University Medical Center holds responsibility for data analysis and reporting. Given the collection of health-related data, particular attention is paid to the roles of each partner in accordance with the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), specifically regarding responsibilities as data controllers or processors. The data management plan outlines procedures to uphold high data quality standards, adhering to the FAIR principles: Findability, Accessibility, Interoperability, and Reusability [27]. All data will be treated with strict confidentiality, and scientific data will be stored in a pseudo-anonymised format.

Descriptive statistics will be used to summarise participant characteristics and implementation outcomes in each study site and in the total study population. Changes between baseline (T0) and follow-up (T1) measurements are evaluated using multilevel linear regression analyses for continuous outcome variables and multilevel logistic regression analyses for dichotomous outcome variables. Interaction terms for age, gender, living situation, education level, and income will be included to explore subgroup effects. All analyses will be replicated separately for each study site to account for contextual differences. A p-value of ≤ 0.05 will be considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses will be conducted according to the intention-to-treat (ITT) principle.

Sensor-based data will be processed as time-series signals, and appropriate feature extraction techniques will be employed to identify meaningful temporal patterns and behavioural signatures. The primary analytical goal is to examine the relationship between these objective sensor-based measures and participants’ self-reported data obtained through questionnaires, with the aim of uncovering potential correlations that may inform and enhance digital phenotyping. To capture non-linear, high-dimensional relationships within the data, a multimodal analytical framework will be implemented, encompassing both traditional statistical approaches (e.g., linear and logistic regression) and more complex machine learning models, including deep neural networks.

Qualitative analysis will be conducted on focus group data to explore participants’ experiences with the intervention, including perceived impacts on health and well-being, as well as contextual factors shaping implementation. All focus group discussions will be digitally recorded, transcribed, and—where necessary—translated for analysis. Data will be managed and coded using NVivo or comparable software. A thematic content analysis approach will be applied to identify key patterns, meanings, and mechanisms across the data [28]. The analysis seeks to generate both site-specific insights and cross-contextual understanding of the lived experiences of citizens participating in the intervention across Europe. An iterative approach will be employed, allowing analysis after each round of data collection. This facilitates timely reflection and the adaptation of intervention strategies at the local level. The analyses are led by Erasmus University Medical Center, in collaboration with Universitat Politècnica de València and research partners from each study site.

Dissemination

A targeted dissemination and communication strategy is implemented to maximize the project’s impact. Scientific results are shared through peer-reviewed journals and international conferences, with oversight by a Scientific Committee. Authorship follows ICMJE guidelines. Dissemination to stakeholders - such as local authorities, health and social care professionals and community centres - is carried out via the project website (https://horus-urbanhealth.eu), professional publications, events, and capacity-building workshops. Relevant networks on health, disadvantaged communities, and urban environments are engaged to strengthen outreach and uptake.

Discussion

This study aims to evaluate the potential benefits of the HORUS programme on indicators of lifestyle, including diet, physical activity, alcohol consumption and smoking. The programme provides a comprehensive intervention targeted at the participant’s motivation to change lifestyle, community-based activities to promote healthier lifestyles, and the use of urban environments. The HORUS evaluation study uses a mixed-methods, controlled design and is implemented across three European cities: Rijeka, Croatia; Rotterdam, The Netherlands, and Valencia, Spain.

This study has several notable strengths. The HORUS evaluation study aims to implement a comprehensive community-based programme addressing the main risk factors for NCDs across diverse European contexts. The mixed-methods evaluation approach and the breadth of data collection represent a unique opportunity to generate a rich, high-quality dataset on the relationship between lifestyle and use of the urban environment [29]. The study can provide important evidence on the effectiveness of community-based interventions and increase awareness on the urban environment’s potential for health promotion. Implementation across multiple countries enhances understanding of the feasibility and transferability of the intervention in different sociopolitical and environmental settings. In addition, the use of a standardised questionnaire and uniform measurement tools ensures methodological consistency, allowing for meaningful cross-country comparisons. Furthermore, the study’s mixed-methods design, grounded in the social-ecological model, enables in-depth analysis of how specific interventions affect different population groups, offering nuanced insights into what works, for whom, and under what conditions. Similarly, the data collected via the Wakamola app—from both self-reported questionnaires and objective mobile sensor inputs—will support advanced analyses to uncover participants’ behavioural patterns. These insights will inform the development of digital phenotyping profiles, enabling the research team to tailor the HORUS programme to individual needs. Additionally, this data will be cross-referenced with neighbourhood-level information to examine how environmental factors shape health-related behaviours, with the overarching goal of preventing chronic conditions [30].

We also expect to encounter some challenges. Engaging with hard-to-reach populations requires a focus on overcoming challenges such as fear of disclosing immigration status, small population sizes, reluctance to discuss one’s health, mistrust in research, and low digital literacy [31]. Our recruitment strategy seeks to develop community partnerships to establish trust and obtain reliable data. Previous studies showed that communicating in the participant’s preferred language, providing assistance with reading and responding to study questions, and recruiting in locations where the populations of interest live are effective engagement strategies [31, 32]. In addition, training activities are anticipated to support the effective implementation of the intervention and promote the uptake of the new technology, thereby enhancing engagement with the intervention and encouraging sustained use of the technical solutions among the target populations.
