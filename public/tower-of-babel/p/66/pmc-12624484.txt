Isolation, Identification and Typing of Nine Strains of Brucella in Arun Banner
Authors: Na Ta, Jing Gao, Ke‐Ming Zuo, Shuang‐Li Niu, Hong‐Ming Lin, Yong‐Jun Wen, Hai Jiang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveTo better understand the molecular biological characteristics and strain types of Brucella in Hulunbuir, Arun Banner, Inner Mongolia Autonomous Region, through isolation, biochemical identification, AMOS‐PCR, and multiple locus sequence typing (MLST) of Brucella strains from human brucellosis patients, and to provide a reference for the scientific prevention and control of brucellosis in this region.MethodThe clinical and epidemiological data of patients with human brucellosis in Arun Banner, Inner Mongolia, were collected and analysed. Aseptically collected samples included venous blood, midstream urine and fresh diseased tissues (cerebrospinal fluid, synovial fluid, bone marrow, lymph node aspirates and abscess puncture fluids). Gram staining, serum agglutination, dye inhibition and phage lysis tests were used for the identification of Brucella species and biovars. Molecular identification and typing of Brucella were performed using AMOS polymerase chain reaction and multiple locus sequence typing (MLST).ResultsNine Brucella strains were isolated, all being B. melitensis: 2 biovar 1, 4 biovar 2, 2 biovar 3, and 1 variant. All infected individuals (9/9) were herdspersons, with a male‐to‐female ratio of 7:2 and an onset age of 30–63 years (mean 44). Among the cases, 88.89% became ill following contact with sheep, 66.67% were engaged in breeding work and 33.33% had direct contact with cattle and sheep. In MLST typing, seven strains were sequence type (ST) 8, one was a newly identified allele and one was ST12. Phylogenetic analysis using the neighbour‐joining method showed that all nine isolates clustered with B. melitensis reference strains (M5, M28, 16 M, Rev.1), consistent with biochemical and PCR identification results.ConclusionPeople involved in raising and trafficking sheep constitute a high‐risk group for brucellosis. The strains isolated were B. melitensis, with ST8 as the predominant type identified by MLST typing. Attention should also be given to the discovery of new alleles.Keywords: biochemical identification, brucellosis, epidemiological investigation, multiple locus sequence typing

Introduction

Brucellosis is one of the zoonotic infectious diseases caused by Brucella infection (Zhongming 2001). It is classified as a Class B infectious disease under the Law of the People's Republic of China on Preventing and Treating Infectious Diseases. The disease poses a serious threat to human health and impedes the development of animal husbandry and the wider economy (Xu et al. 2022; Shakir 2021). Sheep, cattle, pigs, deer and dogs are the main sources of infection for animal brucellosis. Brucella is highly pathogenic and can invade the body through the surface mucosa as well as the digestive and respiratory tracts. The Chinese population is generally susceptible to brucellosis, which is clinically characterised by fever, sweating, fatigue and systemic migratory pain in muscles and joints (Staff 2019; Zheng et al. 2018).

As a historically endemic area, the brucellosis epidemic in Inner Mongolia was effectively controlled in the 1980s. However, since the late 1990s, particularly after 2000, the epidemic has resurged rapidly due to the vigorous development of the animal husbandry industry (Liang et al. 2021; Ta et al. 2022). Arun Banner is located in the southern foothills of the Daxing'an Ridge. It serves as the southern gateway to Hulunbuir and borders the three northeastern provinces: Zalantun across the river to the west, the Zagtun Mountain Range and Molidawa Daur Autonomous Banner to the east, Orunchun Banner to the north, Yakeshi to the northwest and Gannan County (Heilongjiang Province) to the south, separated by the Jinjie Trench. As one of China's main animal husbandry bases, Arun Banner was projected to have 955,000 livestock in 2022, including 850,000 sheep and 105,000 cattle (Földi et al. 2020).

Historically, Arun Banner has been a high‐incidence area of brucellosis. In the 1990s, the region had effectively controlled the disease to a stable state. However, with the rapid development of animal husbandry, the epidemic began to rise; by 2005, there was an outbreak, with 1093 cases reported in the banner (Jingchuan et al. 2013). In 2022, a total of 267 cases were reported in the banner, and 804 cases of Class A and B infectious diseases were reported in the entire banner, accounting for 33.21% of the total number of cases (Whatmore et al. 2007). The outbreak remains at a high level and prevention and control efforts remain intense, highlighting one of the region's most serious public health problems.

Current research on Brucella variants highlights their genetic distinctiveness and potential epidemiological importance. A study analysing Brucella melitensis isolates in China using MLST and other molecular typing methods revealed substantial genetic variation among strains, with some exhibiting unique profiles distinct from typical strains, suggesting independent evolutionary trajectories and potential differences in pathogenicity (Li et al. 2020). Another study reporting a novel Brucella variant isolated from cattle in Croatia, identified through whole‐genome sequencing and multiple typing methods, found that it displayed unique phylogenetic characteristics, growth properties on dyes and bacteriophage typing results compared with common strains. These findings indicated region‐specific emergence, possibly linked to local disease prevalence, host interactions and control measures (Spicic et al. 2021). Together, these results underscore the need for further research into the genetic basis and epidemiological implications of the variant identified in our study.

Multiple locus sequence typing (MLST) is widely used for international strain comparison and evolutionary analysis of Brucella, as it enables cross‐regional and cross‐temporal genetic comparison. Globally, MLST has become a mainstream method for Brucella strain characterisation, providing a standardised tool for studying genetic diversity (Truong et al. 2022). In China, relevant studies have mainly focused on the northwest and northern regions. Cao et al. (2018) genotyped 66 Brucella strains from northwest China using MLST and found that ST8 was the dominant prevalent strain, consistent with the regional distribution pattern. However, data on strain diversity in Hulunbuir, Inner Mongolia—an important pastoral area—remain limited, particularly regarding the genetic relationships between strains and their epidemiological links. This study thus aims to fill this gap by isolating and typing Brucella strains in Arun Banner, enriching local genotyping data and supporting targeted prevention and control strategies.

Materials and Methods

Sample Collection

A convenient sampling method was used to select nine cases of brucellosis in Arun Banner, Hulunbuir City, Inner Mongolia Autonomous Region, between January 2022 and January 2023. These cases were provided by the Comprehensive Disease Prevention and Control Centre of the Inner Mongolia Autonomous Region. In this study, blood samples, urine samples and fresh diseased tissues were aseptically collected from the patients. Blood samples (3–5 mL venous blood) were used for bacterial culture, deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) extraction and serum agglutination tests. Urine samples (3–5 mL midstream urine) were used for bacterial culture. Lesion samples, including cerebrospinal fluid (1 mL), synovial fluid (1 mL), bone marrow, lymph node aspirates and abscess puncture fluids, were used for bacterial isolation, biochemical identification (e.g. Gram staining and phage lysis tests) and DNA extraction for molecular typing. All samples were cryopreserved and sent to the laboratory within 24 h.

Aseptically collected samples included 3–5 mL of venous blood or urine and 1 mL each of cerebrospinal fluid and synovial fluid from confirmed and suspected patients. The centre also supplied a standard reference Brucella sheep strain (16 M). This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Inner Mongolia Autonomous Region Centre for Disease Control and Prevention. All participants were informed about the study and provided signed informed consent. During sample collection, we also collected demographic information of all participants, including gender, age, occupation, and history of contact with livestock (e.g., sheep, cattle), to support the subsequent epidemiological analysis.

Main Instruments and Reagents

The following instruments were used: a constant temperature incubator (Shanghai Yiheng Scientific Instrument Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China), cryogenic centrifuge (Thermo Fisher Scientific Co., Ltd., Waltham, MA, USA), bacterial McClicity turbidity meter (Hangzhou Huaduan Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Hangzhou, China), Class II biological safety cabinet (NUAIRE Corporation, Plymouth, MN, USA), automatic medical polymerase chain reaction (PCR) analysis system (Shanghai Hongshi Medical Technology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) and an upright microscope (Nikon Instruments Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan).

The following reagents were used: a deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) extraction kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany), 2×Es Taq MasterMix (Jiangsu Kangwei Century Biological Co., Ltd., Jiangsu, China), pansy‐red test strip, Brucella Tb bacteriophage, Brucella Bk2 bacteriophage, Brucella monophasic specific serum A/M and a Brucella AMOS PCR kit (purchased from Qingdao Zhongchuanghuike Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Qingdao, China). The primers were synthesised by Shenggong Bioengineering Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China.

Isolation of Bacteria and Deoxyribonucleic Acid Extraction

Suspected Brucella blood cultures were inoculated onto Brucella agar medium and incubated at 37°C for 48 h under anaerobic conditions. After incubation, a single colony was selected and inoculated onto Brinell‐inclined medium to enhance bacterial growth, which was then used for biochemical identification. Inactivation and DNA extraction were performed concurrently, and the extracted DNA was stored at −20°C.

The AMOS Polymerase Chain Reaction Analysis

The AMOS‐PCR kit was used to detect B. melitensis, B. abortus, B. suis and B. canis. The reaction system (25 µL) included 12.5 µL 2×Es Taq MasterMix, 1 µL DNA template, 1 µL each of forward and reverse primers (10 µmol/L) and 9.5 µL deionised water. The reaction conditions were as follows: pre‐denaturation at 95°C for 5 min; 35 cycles of denaturation at 95°C for 30 s, annealing at 58°C for 30 s and extension at 72°C for 45 s; followed by a final extension at 72°C for 10 min. Reporter dye Cy5 was positively identified as B. abortus, FAM as B. canis, HEX as B. melitensis and Texas Red as B. suis; a positive result was defined as a Ct value of <36.

Biological Identification of Brucella

The primary culture was purified by streaking on Brucella agar medium, and colony morphology (size, shape and colour) was observed after 48 h of incubation.

Gram staining: Smears were prepared from single colonies, stained with crystal violet (1 min), iodine solution (1 min), decolourised with 95% ethanol (30 s) and counterstained with safranin (30 s). Gram‐negative coccobacilli were observed under a microscope.

Serum agglutination test: 0.5 mL of A/M monospecific serum (1:20 dilution) was mixed with 0.5 mL bacterial suspension (McFarland 0.5), incubated at 37°C for 2 h. Agglutination ≥1:20 was considered positive.

Dye inhibition test: The bacterial suspension was inoculated onto Brucella agar containing basic fuchsin (1:50,000) and thionin (1:25,000) and incubated at 37°C for 48 h. Growth inhibition was recorded. The procedure was based on the Manual for the Diagnosis, Treatment and Prevention and Control of Brucellosis (Whatmore et al. 2007).

Multiple Locus Sequence Typing: Nine Gene Amplification

Seven housekeeping genes (dnaK, gyrB, trpE, aroA, cobQ, gap, glk), one outer membrane protein gene (omp25) and one intergenic region (int‐hyp) were selected as target genes for MLST (Liu et al. 2022). Primers were designed and synthesised using standard sequences from the PubMLST database (https://pubmlst.org/). The primer sequences and expected amplification lengths are shown in Table 1. PCR products were analysed by 1.5% agarose gel electrophoresis to determine whether the expected amplicons had been obtained. The amplification products were then sent to Beijing Tianyi Huiyuan Biological Company for sequencing.

Multiple Locus Sequence Typing and Phylogenetic Tree Construction

For the raw data generated by sequencing, low‐quality sequences were removed, and the remaining sequences were assembled to obtain complete sequences. The gene sequences of nine reference strains, including B. melitensis, B. abortus, B. suis and B. canis, were downloaded from the National Center for Biotechnology Information. All sequence results were uploaded to the PubMLST database to obtain the allele numbers and MLST strain types for the nine loci.

The MEGA11 software package was used to concatenate the alleles of each strain, and the neighbour‐joining (NJ) method was used to construct the phylogenetic tree. The PHYLOViZ online analysis platform (https://online.phyloviz.net/index) was used to construct an MLST minimum‐spanning tree to determine the relationships between different ST types.

Statistical Analysis Method

In this study, Excel 2016 and SPSS 26.0 software (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA) were used for data analysis. Measurement data (e.g. age) were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, and normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Count data (e.g. gender ratio, occupation distribution, contact history) were expressed as the number of cases and percentages. Comparisons between groups were performed using the chi‐square test. A p‐value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Among the nine cases, herdspeople accounted for 100% of the strains (9/9). The male‐to‐female ratio was 7:2. The age of onset ranged from 30 to 63 years, with a mean age of 44 years. Evolutionary Tree Construction

The gene sequences were concatenated using MEGA11 software, and the NJ method was used to construct the phylogenetic tree. The results of the tree construction are shown in Figure 2. All nine Arongqi isolates clustered on the same evolutionary branch as the four B. melitensis reference strains (M5, M28, 16 M and Rev.1), consistent with the biochemical identification and quantitative PCR typing results. In contrast, the remaining B. abortus, B. suis and B. canis reference strains (S19, bv.2, 104 M, S2 and canis) formed a separate branch.

The nine Arongqi isolates could be divided into two evolutionary branches. Among them, ARQ‐001, ARQ‐002, ARQ‐011, ARQ‐022, ARQ‐032, ARQ‐034, ARQ‐045 and ARQ‐066 were highly homologous with the vaccine strain M5 and the highly virulent Brucella strain M28. ARQ‐069 showed high homology with the 16 M and Rev.1 standard strains of B. melitensis.

The PHYLOViZ online analysis software was used to construct an MLST minimum‐spanning tree for the different ST types. The results are shown in Figure 3. According to the minimum‐spanning tree, the ST types could be grouped into three categories: ST7, ST8 and ST12. The NEW type clustered with these into a single group, encompassing all ST types of the Arongqi isolates. ST14, ST15 and ST20 also formed a separate cluster, whereas ST5 was categorised independently.

Discussion

This study isolated nine Brucella strains from herdspeople in Arun Banner, all identified as B. melitensis (biovars 1, 2, 3 and one variant) via biochemical and AMOS‐PCR tests. Multiple locus sequence typing revealed seven ST8 strains, one ST12 (first reported in China) and one novel allele. Epidemiologically, all cases involved herdspeople with direct sheep contact, predominantly male (7:2), aged 30–63 years. These results highlight the dominance of ST8 B. melitensis in this region and underscore sheep‐related occupations as high‐risk factors.

As a genotyping tool for assessing genetic diversity and relationships, MLST is widely used for identifying and analysing bacterial diversity and epidemiological characterisation. It is also useful for distinguishing Brucella species or biological variant levels and provides effective strategies for investigating the molecular association between Brucella strains in clinical isolation (Hai et al. 2018, 2020; Zilberman et al. 2022; Shome et al. 2016). In this study, nine strains of Brucella were analysed via biochemical identification, AMOS‐PCR and MLST classification. The results showed that seven of the nine Brucella strains were ST8, consistent with the findings of Cao et al. (2018).

Notably, the dominance of B. melitensis in our study aligns with findings from other pastoral regions in China. For example, a study by Yang et al. (2024) investigating Brucella isolates from the Xinjiang Uygur Autonomous Region also reported B. melitensis as the primary species (89.1% of isolates), with ST8 being the most prevalent ST (61.5%). This consistency suggests that B. melitensis, particularly ST8, may exhibit broad ecological adaptation to intensive sheep‐raising systems across northern China's pastoral areas. In contrast, a study by Li et al. (2024) in eastern China (Shandong Province) found a higher proportion of B. abortus (38.2%) and a lower frequency of ST8 (22.7%), which they attributed to the region's mixed farming systems integrating cattle and pig rearing alongside sheep. This difference highlights how livestock composition shapes local Brucella species and ST distribution, reinforcing that our findings in Arun Banner reflect its sheep‐dominated pastoral economy.

This indicates that ST8 is a widely endemic strain in north‐western China and also widely distributed both domestically and internationally, particularly in the eastern Mediterranean region, where brucellosis is thought to have originated (Zilberman et al. 2022). In addition, the study by Cao et al. noted that the main isolated STs in north‐western China were ST1, ST3, ST7, ST8 and ST14. Through a review of Chinese literature and the PubMLST database, this study determined that the isolated ST12 type represents the first report of this ST in China.

The global distribution of the ST12 Brucella strain was analysed using the PubMLST database, identifying 65 isolation sites. The results showed that ST12 strains mainly occurred in eastern Africa, northern Europe and the north‐western South Asian subcontinent. Countries with more frequent reports included Somalia (30.77%), Tanzania (15.38%), Norway (10.77%) and Ethiopia (9.23%). The specific distribution information is shown in Figure 4.

Geographical distribution of Brucella ST12.

According to host statistics for the 65 ST12 strains, 58 were detected in human hosts (89.23%). Two strains each were detected in cattle and sheep (3.08%), one in a goat (1.54%) and two in unknown hosts (3.08%). This suggests that the ST12 strain may infect humans and other susceptible animals through cattle, goats, sheep and their livestock products. Accordingly, the monitoring of livestock such as cattle, sheep and goats—and their products—should be strengthened.

A new ST, ARQ‐002, was generated by a novel allele combination that has not yet been numbered. The specific sequence information was submitted to the PubMLST database. According to the flow survey data, the patient was mainly engaged in livestock trafficking and may have been exposed to infection in workplaces such as slaughterhouses, breeding plants or during transportation, greatly increasing the probability and complexity of infection. This indicates that additional attention should be paid to quarantine measures during livestock trafficking in future and that controlling transmission routes is important for the prevention and control of brucellosis (Sun et al. 2020).

Furthermore, in the genetic evolution analysis of the nine Brucella isolates, the results of the phylogenetic tree construction and minimum‐spanning tree analysis showed a high degree of homology, suggesting that no foreign strains introduced via transport routes were present in the Arun Banner area.

Different subtypes showed typical symptoms of brucellosis, such as pain, fever, sweating and fatigue (Colmenero et al. 1996; Pappas et al. 2006). The pathogenicity of Brucella depends on its strong adaptability and invasiveness, and each type of Brucella has strong pathogenic potential. If contact history and epidemiological data are not considered, early clinical diagnosis of brucellosis can be difficult. Recognising it from other diseases may also be problematic (Corbel 2006). Therefore, the diagnosis of brucellosis in non‐epidemic regions often occurs a few months or even years after disease onset. The main reasons for this are as follows: (1) the incubation period of brucellosis fluctuates greatly, with persistent pathogenicity and strong infectivity, typically lasting 1–4 weeks; (2) the clinical symptoms and signs of the disease are diverse (Gorvel 2008), with the former including non‐specific fever syndromes (periodic or intermittent fever, muscle pain, back pain, sweating and fatigue); (3) because the symptoms resemble those of other infectious and non‐infectious diseases, brucellosis may lead to varying clinical diagnoses and misdiagnoses; and (4) the course of brucellosis varies (acute, subacute or chronic), involving multiple organs, tissues and focal lesions, which makes diagnosis difficult.

Some studies (An et al. 2021; Dadar et al. 2022) indicate that the ST8 Brucella strain has strong pathogenicity. In this study, nine patients had a history of direct contact with sheep and their products or pollutants. This suggests that sheep may be the main reservoir of Brucella ST8. Although the brucellosis outbreak is more serious, the species structure is relatively singular, with all cases caused by B. melitensis. Based on the typing experiment conducted in this study, it is more feasible to formulate prevention and control strategies suitable for a specific region, which can serve as an example for comprehensively controlling brucellosis in humans. Concurrently, this approach can further deepen the understanding of the pathogenesis, infection mechanism and microevolution of Brucella in China, enrich the country's MLST database and provide a stronger basis for preventing and controlling brucellosis in Inner Mongolia. This may help provide effective technical support for subsequent discussion of the genetic diversity and geographical distribution characteristics of Brucella in China, enhance understanding of the genetics involved and the variation and evolution of Brucella in other parts of China and support the implementation of animal brucellosis prevention and control measures and eradication strategies.

The current study has some limitations. First, the sample was small and may not reflect all the characteristics of a Brucella epidemic in Inner Mongolia. In addition, the study period was short, and changes among the isolated strains were not analysed over an extended timeframe. Finally, as the included population represented a single occupational group, this may have limited the observed genetic diversity. Therefore, more long‐term and comprehensive strain monitoring studies should be conducted in future.

Conclusion

People involved in raising and trafficking sheep are a high‐risk group for contracting brucellosis infections. This study focused on B. melitensis via MLST typing, with ST8 identified as the primary strain. However, further attention is needed to identify new alleles.
