CD14 Blockade, A Next-Generation, Anti-Inflammatory Biological for Post-MI Remodeling
Authors: Douglas G Tilley, Jop H van Berlo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Corresponding AuthorsKey Words: CD14, heart, monocyte, macrophage, STEMI

ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) remains a prevalent form of acute coronary syndrome, with over 800,000 occurrences annually in the United States and billions of dollars in associated health care costs.1 Although percutaneous reperfusion has significantly lowered mortality rates via restoration of coronary blood flow to improve the viability of the peri-infarct region, reperfusion acutely promotes further oxidative damage and inflammation that can paradoxically promote cardiac cell death. Thus, strategies that dampen postreperfusion damage responses may lead to better preservation of myocardial tissue viability and repair that ultimately improve long-term cardiac structure and function. Following ischemic injury, release of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) from dying/stressed cardiac cells activates nearby surviving cells to secrete chemokines, particularly CCL2 (chemokine [C-C motif] ligand 2)/MCP-1 (monocyte chemoattractant protein-1) to quickly recruit CCR2+ (C-C chemokine receptor 2) monocytes to the heart where they can exert proinflammatory effects and differentiate into macrophages to replace those lost to injury.2 Although different monocyte subsets exist, commonly identified via their combinatorial expression of CD14 (cluster of differentiation 14), CD16, and CCR2, CD14+CD16−CCR2+ classical monocytes predominate in humans and infiltrate the injured heart in large numbers. These monocytes, and the macrophages derived from them, participate in several processes including phagocytosis, generation of reactive oxygen species, and secretion of mediators of both inflammation and repair.2

Attenuation of the acute inflammatory response following cardiac injury has been demonstrated experimentally to accelerate transition to the reparative phase, thereby limiting long-term infarct expansion, cardiac dysfunction, and pathological remodeling, including fibrosis,3 suggesting that therapeutic strategies preventing monocyte recruitment during the early inflammatory phase could be clinically beneficial. Indeed, inhibition of CCR2-mediated recruitment of monocytes to the heart following injury improves outcomes in several mouse models. However, CCR2+ macrophages have also been recognized to mediate several beneficial effects in the heart,2 thus targeting CCR2 therapeutically may be challenging. Although studies specifically investigating the efficacy of CCR2 inhibition in preventing or ameliorating cardiovascular diseases via disruption of monocyte trafficking have not advanced clinically,4 several studies in humans are focused on utilizing CCR2 detection to develop imaging-based approaches for disease diagnoses.

Alternatively, targeting CD14+ monocytes/macrophages could offer an approach to attenuate their responses to ischemic injury, not by preventing their recruitment, but rather by dampening CD14-dependent proinflammatory signaling regardless of their location. CD14, a glycoprotein receptor that exists in either a secreted soluble form or in a membrane-bound form expressed particularly on monocytes and macrophages, binds pathogen-associated molecular pattern–type ligands during infection or DAMPs released because of tissue injury to initiate proinflammatory signaling. Although several molecular mechanisms can contribute to proximal CD14-mediated signaling, including acting as a coreceptor for Toll-like receptors, pathogen-associated molecular pattern or DAMP ligand binding to CD14 leads to activation of downstream proinflammatory responses.5 As such, inhibition of CD14-dependent signaling may decrease the pathogenesis of diseases involving innate immune cell-mediated inflammation, a concept that has begun to be tested with the anti-CD14 monoclonal antibody atibuclimab in randomized clinical trials for a variety of inflammatory diseases.6 With respect to cardiac diseases, current phase 1 clinical studies include those for acute decompensated heart failure (NCT06556810), arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy (NCT06275893), and STEMI (NCT06678074).4 As clinical studies trials ramp up for testing the safety and clinical efficacy of atibuclimab in the treatment of cardiac diseases, understanding its specific impact on cardiac monocyte/macrophage populations that regulate remodeling after injury will be helpful to inform its use and contextual mechanism(s) of action.

Toward this goal, in this issue of JACC: Basic to Translational Science, D’Elia et al6 tested the hypothesis that interventional CD14 blockade with a murine analogue of atibuclimab would prevent secondary immunological exacerbation of myocardial injury in a translational mouse model of reperfused ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (R/STEMI). A major strength of the study is the rigorous use of the author’s verified R/STEMI mouse model to investigate the effectiveness of systemic administration of the anti-CD14 antibody at the time of reperfusion and at subsequent set intervals as a therapeutic strategy. The study provides comprehensive phenotyping via echocardiography, pressure-volume hemodynamics, and immunohistochemistry to demonstrate that anti-CD14 antibody treatment, vs saline or isotype control antibody injection, at reperfusion and 24 hours after surgery improves cardiac function and decreases infarct size and left ventricular fibrosis at 7 days post-R/STEMI. Further, weekly anti-CD14 antibody administration yielded functional improvements at 21 and 28 days post-R/STEMI, assessed via cardiac magnetic resonance and echocardiography, respectively. Although the authors determined that anti-CD14 antibody treatment did not impact initial injury parameters including area-at-risk and overall immune cell accumulation in response to injury, bulk RNAseq analysis of general CD11b+CD64+CD45+ cells sorted from the heart 3 days post-R/STEMI demonstrated decreased expression of transcripts encoding proinflammatory mediators and protrafficking cytokines and increased expression of anti-inflammatory mediators and extracellular matrix modulators. Notably, via scRNASeq on the same population of 3-day post-R/STEMI sorted cardiac cells, subsets of monocyte/macrophage populations were responsive to anti-CD14 antibody treatment. In particular, the authors observed an increase in cluster 0, defined as mature polarized CCR2lo macrophages, and a decrease in cluster 1, defined as Ly6c2hi and CCR2hi monocytes/macrophages. These changes in cluster proportions suggest that the long-term post-R/STEMI improvements in cardiac structure and function with anti-CD14 antibody treatment may result from an early shift in balance of the proinflammatory and proreparative monocyte/macrophage subpopulations.

From an academic standpoint, these findings are intriguing and warrant further future investigation into the cellular mechanism(s) by which ant-CD14 antibody relays these effects. For instance, the tissue resident macrophage (TRM) subset most conserved between mice and humans has been defined as Timd4+Lyve1+Folr2+(TLF+), CCR2−, and MHC-IIlo, which are known to promote adaptive remodeling processes, dampen inflammatory responses to stress, and decrease in abundance within the infarcted region after ischemic injury.2 Conversely, TLR−CCR2+Ly6Chigh monocyte-derived macrophages expand to replace the resident macrophages lost to injury and typically promote inflammation and pathologic cardiac remodeling. Although not defined as such in their study, the cluster 0 cardiac cell population identified by D’Elia et al6 at 3 days post-R/STEMI are suggestive of TLF+CCR2− macrophages and the Cluster 1 population of TLR−CCR2+ monocyte-derived macrophages. Because the abundance of general CD11b+CD64+CD45+ macrophages was not different at this timepoint, but the proportions of the macrophage subsets were altered, particularly clusters 0 and 1, their study suggests that anti-CD14 antibody treatment may be capable of promoting preservation of TRM, decreasing the influx or differentiation of peripheral CCR2+ monocytes into macrophages, or some combination thereof. It would be interesting to know more about these anti-CD14 antibody-sensitive cell clusters; how they align with TLR+ TRM, CCR2+ monocyte-derived macrophages, and other subtypes already defined in the literature; and whether the cluster 0 cells are preserved TRM or derived from peripheral monocytes but differentiated into less inflammatory state. As the authors suggest in their discussion, straightforward lineage tracing experiments would help define their origin.

From a therapeutic standpoint, it would be valuable to know the minimal effective treatment strategy of the anti-CD14 antibody required to promote its beneficial functional outcomes. For instance, because % ejection fraction was significantly better in the anti-CD14 antibody treatment group vs the control groups at day 7 post-R/STEMI, but did not improve beyond that at the later timepoints, whether the additional weekly anti-CD14 antibody treatments are necessary is unclear vs limiting treatment to just time of reperfusion and/or within the first week postreperfusion. The effects of the anti-CD14 antibody therapy at a cellular level at day 3 postinjury would suggest that antagonizing CD14-mediated actions on specific monocyte/macrophage subsets during this acute phase of injury would likely be sufficient to produce the long-term beneficial effects observed, if mediated solely by manipulating these cell types. With a phase 1 pilot study underway for use of a single administration of atibuclimab to STEMI patients treated with percutaneous intervention (NCT06678074), such results may be informative for interpretation of potential clinical efficacy. Regardless, the advancement of therapeutics that can dampen the proinflammatory environment post–cardiac injury is exciting and will hopefully result in a novel class of drugs available to treat cardiovascular diseases in the near future to improve the outcome for patients.
