How Defining ‘Disadvantaged’ Shaped Bipartisan Infrastructure Law Drinking Water Funding
Authors: Samyukta Shrivatsa, Derek Ouyang, Daniel E Ho, Khalid K Osman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe 2021 Bipartisan Infrastructure Law (BIL) directed $30.7 billion specifically towards safe drinking water programs, representing the single-largest investment into water infrastructure in United States history. The law mandates that 49% of funds be provided as grants and forgivable loans to “disadvantaged communities.” Notably, the definition of disadvantaged communities has been left to the discretion of states, allowing variability in how different states identify and prioritize these communities. This study examines how states redefined disadvantaged communities after the BIL, maps their spatial distribution, and analyzes disparities in drinking water funding from 2019 to 2023. Our results suggest that, despite widespread updates to disadvantaged community definitions, racial and ethnic minorities continue to encounter barriers to accessing funding. If policies are aimed to address historical disparities in drinking water investment, definitional delegation appears to fall short, and ongoing engagement with underserved communities may be needed for more equitable infrastructure investment.Subject terms: Water resources, Climate-change policy

Introduction

Drinking water violations have been found to disproportionately affect communities with high proportions of people of color, low-income individuals, non-native English speakers, and those living in crowded conditions or with limited access to transportation1. Predominantly Black and Hispanic communities experience higher rates of health complications from poor drinking water, including elevated levels of per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS) and higher water lead levels, as well as increased emergency room visits for gastrointestinal issues linked to water quality2–4. Communities with high proportions of Black, Hispanic, and American Indian and Alaskan Native populations are also at greater risk for exposure to contaminants, including arsenic, uranium, lead, nitrate, and fluoride5–7. These water systems are also more likely to remain out of compliance with drinking water quality standards for longer periods of time than those in predominantly White communities8.

Given these disparities, the allocation of water infrastructure funding presents an opportunity to address inequities in drinking water access and related public health outcomes. The Safe Drinking Water Act (SDWA) was originally passed in 1974 to empower the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) to set minimum standards for tap water quality and oversee water suppliers in complying with these public health standards9. The Drinking Water State Revolving Fund (DWSRF) was established in 1996 Amendments to the SDWA to assist states in meeting the objectives of the SDWA10. The DWSRF offers low-interest loans for water infrastructure projects such as replacing lead pipes, tackling emerging contaminants in small and underserved communities, and assisting rural water initiatives11. States match 20% of federal grants and may allocate up to 31% of funds for program administration and technical assistance. Loan repayments revolve back into the fund, creating resources for future projects. Types of assistance include loans, refinancing, and guarantees10.

Signed in 2021, the BIL allocated $50 billion to strengthen water and wastewater infrastructure, with $30.7 billion directed specifically towards safe drinking water programs through the DWSRF12, representing the single largest investment in water infrastructure in United States history. The BIL also mandates that 49% of funds provided through the DWSRF General Supplemental Funding and DWSRF Lead Service Line Replacement Funding must be provided via principal forgiveness, debt restructuring, or grants to state-defined disadvantaged communities13. To meet these requirements, states are expected to “evaluate and revise, as needed, [their] DWSRF disadvantaged community definition[s],” moving beyond “problematic” definitions that do not consider affordability12. States must annually prepare an Intended Use Plan, detailing the projects to be assisted, the criteria for fund distribution, and the fund’s financial status. The EPA requires that plans prioritize projects addressing serious health risks, ensuring compliance with regulatory requirements, and assisting the most financially needy systems14.

Existing literature indicates that states with definitions based solely on affordability or population, or stipulations with a low cap on financial assistance, often fail to sufficiently meet the needs of low-income or underrepresented communities15. In Mississippi’s DWSRF, disadvantaged community stipulations prior to the BIL, lack of flexible loan options, and $500,000 cap on principal forgiveness contributed to a concentration of funding in a narrow band of low-income communities (below $29,999), inadvertently restricting support for areas with high need, leaving many underserved communities unable to access adequate resources, and also limiting support for larger-scale projects16. Texas’s and Michigan’s disadvantaged community definitions have been criticized for being too narrow to capture all disadvantaged communities and too broad in their application, as they offer the same level of principal forgiveness to all disadvantaged communities, regardless of need17,18.

As for the specific guidance the EPA provides to states for updating their disadvantaged community definitions, we underscore three dimensions of potential cross-state variation, providing a framework for our quantitative results. First, departing from a historical focus of DWSRF programs on small rural communities19, the EPA instead expects states to revise their criteria such that they “capture both urban and rural disadvantaged communities”12. Second, the EPA provides nearly a dozen example criteria that states may consider incorporating into their revised definitions, ranging from income-based measures to utilization of welfare benefits and the social vulnerability index (SVI) calculated by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC)20,21. But, as has been much deliberated in policy and academia22, explicit measures of racial and ethnic diversity are not included aside from proxies for race, such as the SVI. Third, differences in rurality and racial or ethnic composition are also closely linked to predictable patterns in political preferences across communities. Since the BIL was promoted as a bipartisan effort, one way to evaluate that claim is by examining whether funding patterns shifted across communities with different majority political affiliations.

As a baseline, disparities in drinking water funding existed prior to the passage of the BIL. Small water systems and water systems serving predominantly communities of color have received disproportionately low numbers of DWSRF awards. Eligibility has also far exceeded actual receipt of funding; although 66,552 out of 140,000 public water systems in the United States were eligible to apply for DWSRF assistance between 2011 and 2020, only 7.1% of eligible disadvantaged communities received assistance in this period23. These baseline gaps suggest that past funding mechanisms have been insufficient in addressing inequities in water infrastructure investment. Despite the BIL’s directive to establish affordability criteria to define disadvantaged communities, there is no statutory federal definition of what factors must be included in these criteria13. Other than receiving encouragement to consider factors beyond affordability and population, states have considerable discretion in setting criteria for what constitutes a disadvantaged community, influencing how funds are distributed and whether they reach the communities most in need.

In this study, we contribute the following. First, we provide the most comprehensive documentation to-date of qualitative changes to disadvantaged community definitions adopted by all 50 states after the implementation of the BIL. Second, for a subset of 10 states where DWSRF funding data were available, we characterize the spatial and demographic distributions of disadvantaged communities before and after the BIL. Third, we analyze the distribution of actual funds received by communities that qualify as disadvantaged, focusing on the urban-rural divide, racial and ethnic trends, and variations across congressional districts. By analyzing changes in the distribution of both eligibility status and actual fund disbursement across various demographics and geographical areas, we investigate whether the revised criteria appear consistent with funding outcomes that actually reach historically underserved communities.

Results

Qualitative changes to disadvantaged community definitions

Twenty-seven states have modified their disadvantaged community definitions since the BIL was signed. This includes four states (Connecticut, North Dakota, Rhode Island, Vermont) that did not have disadvantaged community programs or for which no data could be found pre-BIL. The categorization of definitions post-BIL is detailed in Figs. 1 and 2.

Spatial distribution of definition criteria post-BIL and states that modified definitions.

Definitions were grouped under seven broad criteria categories as defined by the EPA: socioeconomic, demographic, financial, public health, environmental justice, and other special communities such as colonias or rural areas15. We added a category, “multi-criteria metrics,” to cover states that either adopted disadvantaged communities as defined by the Climate and Economic Justice Screening Tool or EJScreen45, or defined custom metrics that integrated multiple predefined categories.

Qualitative changes to disadvantaged community criteria pre- and post-BIL.

Twenty-seven states modified or expanded their disadvantaged community criteria post-BIL. The most common indicators added were related to water affordability (10 states), population trends (9 states), employment rates (8 states), and environmental justice metrics (7 states).

As seen in Fig. 2, in keeping with the BIL’s stipulation to shift away from definitions based solely on affordability or population, several states started including a variety of additional criteria in their definitions, most commonly related to water affordability (10 states), employment rates (8 states), and environmental justice metrics (7 states). For example, Maine and New Hampshire specifically began to target water systems affected by environmental pollution, naturally occurring contaminants, or lead in service line materials24,25. Nevada completely removed the median household income criterion from their definition, focusing entirely on systems experiencing “economic, environmental, or health issues”26. New Jersey and Washington adopted more quantitative approaches to define environmental justice communities. New Jersey began to define a disadvantaged community (officially referred to as an “overburdened community”) as a block group with 35% low-income households, 40% of the residents identifying as underrepresented or as members of a State-recognized tribal community, or 40% of the households having limited English proficiency27. Washington began to assign communities an Environmental Health Disparities Rank, which comprises scores given for environmental exposures to PM2.5 and ozone, environmental effects of lead exposure, hazardous waste treatment and disposal, superfund sites, and wastewater discharge, and sensitive populations due to cardiovascular disease and low birth weight28. On the other hand, while California also has an existing state-level score-based index for identifying disadvantaged communities called CalEnviroScreen29, the state has not begun to make use of this existing tool for the purpose of its DWSRF disadvantaged community eligibility.

Some states have elected to integrate federal tools, such as the EPA’s Climate and Economic Justice Screening Tool (CEJST) and EJSCREEN and the CDC’s SVI, into their assessments. For instance, Alabama, Hawaii, and Virginia began to use CEJST to identify disadvantaged communities. Delaware began to utilize the EPA’s EJSCREEN tool, applying it to communities scoring at the 90th percentile or higher for Environmental Justice Indices or classified as “Low Income” in the Socioeconomic Indices30. In Washington, project areas began to be categorized as disadvantaged if 50% or more of the proposed project is located within a census tract that has an SVI rank of 7 or higher28.

The use of varying criteria has enabled some vulnerable communities to attain disadvantaged status. Salient examples of such communities located in the states of West Virginia and Arizona are detailed in Supplementary Fig. 1 to illustrate the effects of definitional changes.

Disadvantaged versus non-disadvantaged communities before and after the BIL

We explored the extent to which modifications in disadvantaged community criteria have translated into tangible differences in eligibility and funding across the aforementioned dimensions of rurality, race/ethnicity, and political affiliation. Given the data available at the time of this study, specifically the combination of eligibility and funding information, we narrowed our analysis to 10 states: 5 of which updated their eligibility criteria (California, Connecticut, Florida, Washington, and West Virginia), and 5 of which did not (Indiana, Maine, Michigan, Nebraska, and Texas).

As shown in Table 1, before the BIL was enacted, there were baseline differences between (1) non-disadvantaged communities (n = 8815) and (2) disadvantaged communities (n = 5191) across various demographic and socioeconomic indicators. Two-sided Welch’s t-tests show that, on average, disadvantaged communities had significantly higher percentages of Black (5.66% vs. 4.03%, p < 0.001) and American Indian and Alaska Native residents (0.93% vs. 0.57%, p < 0.001) compared to non-disadvantaged communities. They also had lower percentages of Asian residents (1.33% vs. 3.24%, p < 0.001), compared to non-disadvantaged communities. Differences in Hispanic representation were small and not statistically significant. In addition, disadvantaged communities tended to be situated in areas with a lower proportion of urban residents (37.29% vs. 44.14%, p < 0.001). They experienced a higher number of health-based violations in the period between 2019 and 2023 (mean difference = 0.49 violations, p < 0.001) and cumulative count of all recorded water quality or regulatory infractions (mean difference = 5.04 violations, p < 0.001). Disadvantaged communities also had lower median household incomes ($53,400 vs. $85,800, p < 0.001) and lower populations (2530 vs. 11,600, p < 0.001). Water systems in Republican districts were also more likely to qualify as disadvantaged (79.18% vs. 59.80%, p < 0.001).

We also observed significant differences between (2) the communities of water systems that were considered disadvantaged both before and after BIL, and (3) a new marginal cohort of communities that gained disadvantaged community status post-BIL (n = 605). Communities that gained disadvantaged status post-BIL have significantly higher percentages of White (70.28% vs. 64.56%, p < 0.001), American Indian and Alaska Native (2.08% vs. 0.93%, p < 0.001), and Asian populations (2.55% vs. 1.33%, p < 0.001), and lower percentages of Hispanic (14.24% vs. 19.20%, p < 0.001) and Black populations (4.28% vs. 5.66%, p < 0.05). Newly eligible communities also exhibit higher median household incomes ($72,600 vs. $53,400, p < 0.001), higher urban percentages (50.52% vs. 37.29%, p < 0.001), and tend to report fewer rates of health-based violations (mean difference = −1.82 violations, p < 0.01). These communities also tend to serve larger populations (7,250 vs. 2,530, p < 0.01). In contrast to the comparison of (1) and (2), they were less likely to be situated in Republican districts (35.54% vs. 79.18%, p < 0.001). There was no significant difference in the cumulative count of water quality or regulatory infractions between the two groups. The full t-test results for the selected states are included in Supplementary Tables 1 and 2.

We found 453 communities that lost disadvantaged community status post-BIL, located in Florida and Washington. Florida added an income criterion that requires communities to have a median household income below the state median to its pre-existing population threshold of under 10,000 residents. As a result, no new communities qualified under the revised definition31,32.

These results were fairly consistent among the larger subset of 28 states for which we could determine disadvantaged community status (but not necessarily funding allocations). Some differences included the significantly higher percentage of White residents in (1) as compared to (2), and the insignificance of differences in health-based violations between (2) and (3). The full results can be found in Supplementary Table 3.

Funding disparities by rurality, race/ethnicity, and congressional district

As seen in the descriptive statistics (Table 2), in 2023, states allocated an average of 34.90% of their DWSRF funding to subsidies for eligible communities of water systems via principal forgiveness. While the BIL mandates that 49% of funds provided through the DWSRF General Supplemental Funding go to disadvantaged communities, achievement of this specific mandate could not be precisely verified, as states did not consistently publicly report the breakdown between funding from the base grant and the new BIL allocation. Despite overall DWSRF amounts being higher on average during the 2019–2022 period compared to 2023, this marks an increase in the proportion of principal forgiveness allocations to disadvantaged communities from the pre-BIL average of 12.90%. Our analysis is limited by the fact that most states only had one year of post-BIL data (i.e., 2023). Hence, our averages and confidence intervals for post-BIL data should be viewed as suggestive of ongoing trends. We also used a subset of 10 states that had available data, which are not necessarily representative of general national trends. Where possible, we expanded our analyses to the largest subset of states with available data to evaluate the robustness of our findings. We anticipate that, as appropriations for the DWSRF have been approved through FY 2026, the availability of more data will enable a comprehensive assessment of the long-term effects of these changes on funding distributions in the future12.

For ease and consistency of analysis, we assumed demographic factors from the 2020 Decennial Census and 2018–2022 American Census Survey 5-year estimates were constant for the period between 2019 and 2023. Additionally, only California, Connecticut, Illinois, Texas, and Washington had publicly available explicit water system boundaries. For the remaining states, we use water system boundaries that were estimated either by matching addresses to census boundaries, or by modeling an estimated circular boundary around a point33. This approach may introduce some spatial inaccuracies, potentially impacting the precision of funding allocations and demographic associations.

For a number of states, disadvantaged community criteria could only be partially modeled. Before the BIL was signed, 21 states used water bill amounts and water affordability data to determine disadvantaged community status, for which data was not publicly available. This number increased to 28 after the BIL. Other water system-specific factors, such as operating ratio (Louisiana) and number of connections (North Carolina) were also omitted from the model. Public water systems in states including North Carolina and Vermont had variable disadvantaged community status based on the cost of the proposed DWSRF project, and a subsequent increase in per-connection charges was included in their criteria. Arizona used the fiscal capacity points determined for individual projects based on the DWSRF Project Priority List. Some states had criteria that were not specified to the public. Tennessee, for instance, uses an Ability to Pay Index based on a weighting of nine factors: median household income, unemployment, food stamp dependence, families in poverty, community assets, revenues, debt, expenditures, and change in population. Texas utilizes a Household Cost Factor that considers income, unemployment rates, and population trends. In both cases, the relative weighting of individual factors was unknown and was hence left out of the analysis.

Discussion

Our analysis documents a variety of ways in which states are expanding their criteria for designating disadvantaged communities, most commonly incorporating water affordability, population trends, employment rate, and environmental justice metrics. This represents a move towards a more comprehensive understanding of disadvantage, acknowledging the interplay between economic hardship, environmental risk, and social vulnerability. However, examining 605 newly eligible disadvantaged communities in 10 states, as well as the actual recipients of funding in these states pre- and post-BIL, we find that the impacts of definitional changes on funding trends for vulnerable communities have been ambiguous.

The findings of this study have three major implications. First, this work complicates any effort to frame a simple and clear narrative about whether the BIL has furthered environmental justice goals. Perhaps the only surety is that the legislation increased the overall flow of funding into drinking water infrastructure improvements through principal forgiveness for state-defined disadvantaged communities. The DWSRF program appears to have already appropriately targeted its funding likelihood and levels towards communities with greater environmental exposure, as measured by existing health violations. However, BIL’s relation to broader conceptions of environmental justice is less clear. Newly eligible communities based on post-BIL disadvantage definitions have a third as many health violations on average. We found no evidence that the BIL changed either the likelihood of receiving funding or the amount of funding awarded to low-income or racial minority-serving systems. Although newly eligible communities appear to reflect EPA guidance for post-BIL definitions to increase urban representation, rural communities remain significantly more likely to receive funding. Similar to the trends observed by other researchers in clean energy investments, our results indicate that DWSRF investments in the study states predominantly benefit public water systems in Republican-held districts34, which also tend to be communities that are more rural and less diverse. Overall, these results indicate that financial outcomes need to be paired with consistent capacity-building and a critical evaluation of the outcomes of state revolving funds, particularly in disadvantaged communities.

Second, our findings rely on the careful compilation of decentralized state-level data and represent a preliminary analysis, constrained by both delays in implementation (with 2023 as the first meaningful post-BIL year) and limitations in data availability and consistency. As noted, we cannot fully reproduce state eligibility definitions, collect service boundaries for all PWSs, or obtain detailed data on all funded communities. These gaps stem partly from decentralized definitions and environmental federalism, which in turn limit the ability of federal agencies and researchers to assess the equity impacts of that same decentralization. Some states provide far greater transparency than others; for instance, in 2023, California published a breakdown of the racial composition, median household income, and poverty level of projects that received DWSRF funding35. This level of disclosure and disaggregation clarifies which communities benefit from DWSRF support and helps states identify funding gaps across racial and economic groups.

Data availability and transparency also matter to disadvantaged communities themselves. Water systems have previously identified the complexity and the cost of completing an SRF application as key barriers to applying for funding, a problem that is likely to be exacerbated with more complex criteria36. For instance, rural and tribal water systems often lack the data necessary to measure water affordability, making it harder for them to demonstrate eligibility for funding or assistance programs37. Applying multiple criteria also presents challenges in states where disadvantaged status is determined by water systems through self-reported survey data. Data collection challenges can lead to underreporting and inconsistencies, potentially excluding communities most in need of support38. Additionally, incorporating more data sources, such as environmental metrics and health indicators, can increase model sensitivity and uncertainty, as these harder-to-measure indicators introduce more variability in the model outputs39.

The use of a centralized tool with state-managed data could alleviate data collection burdens, ensuring consistency and comprehensive coverage. A centralized tool using state-managed data could ease data collection and improve consistency. Some states, like Washington28 and Iowa40, have developed assessment tools combining social, economic, and environmental indicators. Other states have opted to rely solely on CEJST, the EPA’s nationwide index, for their approach. However, research shows CEJST may miss key racial-ethnic disparities41. Moreover, the recent rollback of federal environmental justice initiatives has led to the removal of CEJST and other tools, creating additional uncertainty around how disadvantaged status is defined and applied across states42.

Third, we highlight the importance of linking changes in eligibility with actual funding outcomes. Our findings suggest that expanded eligibility does not always lead to increased investment, possibly due to informational, technical, or political barriers. If future research shows alignment between eligible and funded communities, focus may remain on refining definitions. But if a disconnect persists, attention may need to shift downstream to how funding decisions are made. Overall, our study underscores the considerable discretion states hold post-BIL in both defining disadvantage and assigning it real financial weight.

As of the time of this writing, the EPA retained the power to move ahead with disbursement of the BIL-appropriated SRF dollars, despite other funding streams through the BIL being paused by an Executive Order “Unleashing American Energy” signed on January 20, 202543. However, as previously noted, the rollback of federal environmental justice initiatives has introduced uncertainty in how disadvantaged status is defined across states, potentially complicating the equitable allocation of remaining SRF resources.

Taken together, our findings suggest that states are expanding definitions of disadvantaged communities to capture a wider range of vulnerabilities, including sociodemographic and environmental factors. However, these changes have yielded only incremental improvements in funding equity. In some cases, disparities persist or are exacerbated, particularly along racial lines. Structural barriers related to technical, financial, and managerial capacity, as well as political dynamics influencing allocation decisions, continue to limit the ability of under-resourced systems to fully benefit from expanded funding. Addressing these constraints, alongside improving data transparency and consistency, will be critical to ensuring that DWSRF investments more effectively reach the communities most in need.

Methods

We manually tabulated the disadvantaged community definitions of all 50 states as delineated in their Intended Use Plans from before 2020 and after the implementation of the BIL44. These definitions were grouped under seven broad criteria categories as defined by the EPA: socioeconomic, demographic, financial, public health, environmental justice, and other special communities such as colonias or rural areas15. We added a category, “multi-criteria metrics”, to cover states that either adopted disadvantaged communities as defined by the Climate and Economic Justice Screening Tool or EJScreen45, or defined custom metrics that integrated multiple predefined categories. The full definitions for all states can be accessed via Github46.

The boundaries for public water systems were obtained for 45,973 community water systems delivering tap water to 307.7 million people47. This dataset has been utilized in prior environmental justice research48. Explicit water system boundaries were available in the dataset only for California, Connecticut, Illinois, Texas, and Washington. For all other states, boundaries were estimated by the data providers using one of two methods: either by geocoding system addresses to align with census boundaries, or by generating an estimated circular boundary around each system’s central location based on a regression model using system-level characteristics47. For the purposes of our analysis, we defined a community as the population being served by a given water system. Census data for race (Hispanic or Latinx, and Not Hispanic or Latinx by Race) and urban and rural population counts (Demographic and Housing Characteristics, Urban and Rural) were queried at the block level from the 2020 Decennial Census, while income rates (Median Household Income in the Past 12 Months (in 2023 Inflation-Adjusted Dollars)) were queried at the census block group level from the American Community Survey 2018–2022 Estimates. Drinking water quality violations for each public water system were extracted from the EPA’s Safe Drinking Water Information System for the period between 2019 and 2023. The categories of violations include Treatment Technique Violations, Maximum Residual Disinfectant Level Violations, Maximum Contaminant Level Violations, Monitoring and Reporting Violations, and other violations49.

For each unique public water system ID, a union of geometries was created using the queried data. Public water system areas were then intersected with census geometries to determine the extent of spatial overlap between them, and the percentage of area overlap was calculated. The population demographics for each water system were calculated based on the percentage area overlap with census blocks or block groups to account for population distribution within the intersecting regions. Water systems were assigned to congressional districts that overlapped with the majority of the service area, using the boundaries of the 118th Congressional Districts50. For the analysis, we were able to fully model the spatial distribution of disadvantaged communities based on individual states’ definitions in 25 states pre-BIL and 37 states post-BIL (Fig. 3). Using approximations of average water bills for two states (Washington and West Virginia), and including Connecticut as a state that did not have a disadvantaged community program prior to BIL, we had 28 states for which both pre- and post-BIL status could be determined.

States used in the analysis.

States modeled pre-BIL and post-BIL, and the subset of states used for financial analysis.

We compiled funding trends for the case study states from Annual Reports published by their respective state departments between the years of 2019 and 2023. We defined the post-BIL period as the year 2023, as this was the first year in which definition and funding changes were implemented and active for all the states in our sample set, and were explicitly mentioned in their respective Annual Reports. Funding data for the post-BIL period were available for 10 states at the time of analysis: California, Connecticut, Florida, Indiana, Maine, Michigan, Nebraska, Texas, Washington, and West Virginia. The descriptive statistics for water systems that were classified as non-disadvantaged and disadvantaged throughout the study period, as well as those that gained disadvantaged community status post-BIL, were used to analyze trends and demographic shifts in qualification. We conducted two-sided Welch’s t-tests to compare the means of key demographic and system-level characteristics between non-disadvantaged and disadvantaged public water systems before the BIL, as well as between public water systems that gained disadvantaged community status only after the BIL and those that maintained disadvantaged status both before and after the BIL.

To evaluate factors influencing funding receipt and magnitude, we employed a two-part modeling strategy. First, we employed a fixed-effects logistic regression model to estimate the probability (Pit) that water system (i) in state (s) receives funding in year (t). Second, we utilized a linear fixed-effects regression to analyze the impact of community characteristics on the log-transformed total volume of financial awards (yit). among water systems with non-zero funding (2). Both models used cluster-robust standard errors at the water system level to account for within-system correlation and unobserved spatial heterogeneity51. We included state-fixed effects to control for regional policy variations and fiscal priorities that might otherwise bias the estimated impact of community-level demographics. This dual approach allowed us to capture both the binary nature of funding selection and the distributional nuances of funding amounts. The full regression specifications and equations are detailed below:

where

μs: State-fixed effects to account for time-invariant state policy environments.

BILt: A binary indicator for the post-Bipartisan Infrastructure Law period (year 2023).

Χi: A vector of time-invariant community characteristics, including log-transformed population, racial minority-serving status (1 if the percentage of White residents was lower than the state average), low income status (1 if median household income was less than 75% of the state median), rural status (1 if 50% or more of the population was in a rural area), and high risk for health-based violations (1 if a system had health-based Safe Drinking Water Act violations in or above the 90th percentile).

εit: Error term for water system i in year t

Key independent variables included population size (log-transformed continuous variable), racial composition (categorized as racial minority-serving if the percentage of White residents was lower than the state average), income (categorized as low-income if median household income was less than 75% of the state median), rurality (categorized as rural if 50% or more of the population was in a rural area), and health-based violations (binary indicator for whether a system had health-based Safe Drinking Water Act violations in or above the 90th percentile). We incorporated interaction terms between a post-BIL indicator (the year 2023) and all other factors to test for any differential effects post-BIL.

Statistics and reproducibility

All statistical analyses were conducted using R (version 4.3.1). Two-sided Welch’s t-tests and fixed-effects regression models were applied as described above. No statistical method was used to predetermine sample size. The study included all available public water systems and states for which relevant data were accessible.

No data were excluded from the analyses. All observations meeting the inclusion criteria were retained. The study is observational and did not involve randomized experiments. All data used in this study are publicly available from federal sources, and the compiled dataset of state definitions is accessible via GitHub
