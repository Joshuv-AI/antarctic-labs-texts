Pretest-posttest trial of a lung cancer screening decision aid for individuals with multimorbidity from a primary care population
Authors: Kyle M. Koster, Francesca Minardi, Abigail Feinberg, Mara Schonberg, Julie B. Schnur, Juan P. Wisnivesky, Michael A. Diefenbach, Minal S. Kale
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study pilot tested a novel Decision Aid (DA) that clarifies the impact of multimorbidity on Lung Cancer Screening (LCS) risks and benefits. Outcomes of interest include LCS knowledge, disposition to complete LCS, decisional conflict and confidence.MethodsEvaluation scales were administered before and after DA exposure to 50 patients with smoking history; 70% of them having moderate or severe multimorbidity.ResultsLCS Knowledge Measure scores improved (median 3 vs. 5 on scale 0–12, p < 0.0001). Scores of Decisional Conflict subscale related to feeling uninformed improved (median 3.5 vs. 3 on scale 0–15, p = 0.03). Overall Decisional Conflict (median 16 vs. 16 on scale 0–80), Stage of Decision Making (median 4 vs. 4 on scale 1–4), and share of participants disposed to complete LCS (86% vs. 84%) were similar.ConclusionExposure to a DA tailored to individuals with multimorbidity was associated with improved knowledge of LCS. Further investigation is needed to evaluate the DA efficacy in patients with higher baseline decisional conflict and lower baseline confidence.InnovationA novel DA presenting information on the impact of comorbidities on LCS risks and benefits was found to hold potential for better supporting the decision-making of patients with multimorbidity.

Background

Lung cancer is the leading cause of cancer mortality in the United States [1]. Lung cancer screening (LCS) with annual low dose computed tomography (LDCT) reduces lung cancer mortality and is recommended by the United States Preventive Services Task Force (USPSTF) for patients aged 50–80 years who currently smoke or who have quit within 15 years [2]. Despite the population-level benefits of LCS, individuals' decision to initiate annual LCS with LDCT is considered preference-sensitive due to risk of screening- and treatment-related complications in a population with a higher-than-average prevalence of comorbid diseases. Shared decision-making (SDM) is therefore important when initiating screening [3].

SDM for LCS is also important because uptake of LCS is markedly low compared to screening for other cancers [4], [5]. Moreover, uptake since implementation of LCS recommendations in 2013 has not been equitable [6]. The unique SDM requirement by Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS) for providers to conduct and document SDM prior to LDCT coverage [7] may also pose unique challenges to LCS uptake [8].

Despite the substantial benefits of SDM for initiating LCS, SDM is underutilized due to inadequate time to conduct high-quality SDM and a dearth of best practices for conducting SDM [9]. This creates an environment in which pre-existing knowledge, preferences and biases in both providers and individuals undergoing screening strongly influence the decision to initiate screening [10]. Individuals undergoing screening often have limited understanding of the indication for LDCT or trade-offs inherent in the decision to initiate screening yet tend to overweight the benefits of screening relative to risks [11]. In contrast, providers may overestimate competing risk of non-lung cancer death in some patients, such as older patients and those with multimorbidity [[10], [12]].

Due to limited time, asymmetric knowledge between providers and screening candidates, and biases in both parties, decision aids (DA) are an attractive tool to facilitate and standardize SDM. Over 20 patient DAs have been evaluated for LCS [[13], [14]]; most include digital or multiple components, few are print-only, and only one is self-administered and printed [15]. Furthermore, no studies have evaluated the role of DAs in supporting SDM in LCS candidates with multimorbidity. Therefore, we set out to evaluate the efficacy of printed DA for individuals with multimorbidity in a diverse, medically vulnerable population.

Methods

Decision aid

The lung cancer screening brochure DA was iteratively developed by the research team with external health communications experts to promote user-centered design methods, in concordance with DA standards, and based on qualitative interviews with patients and providers. The DA included standard elements regarding the components of screening, benefits, harms, the potential need for biopsies, and bar charts with impact of comorbidity on life years gained from screening (see Appendix A), informed by quantitative modeling performed by the study team [16].

Study design

We conducted a pretest-posttest trial to evaluate the efficacy of a printed DA designed for individuals aged 50–80 years with a smoking history consistent with USPSTF guidelines for LCS. We recruited participants from a large, hospital-based, academic primary care practice in the Mount Sinai Hospital System in New York. The Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai's Institutional Review Board approved this study (protocol #19–02795).

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria included: attending a visit at a primary care practice in the previous 18 months, 50–80 years old, minimum 20 pack-year smoking history, currently smoking or having quit within the past 15 years, and ability to speak and read English. We relied on the electronic medical record's (Epic) hard-coded tobacco history section to identify candidates, acknowledging the potential to miss eligible participants due to inadequate documentation, as we recently reported elsewhere [17]. We excluded individuals who were deceased, hospitalized, residing in long-term care facilities, who had a chart diagnosis of dementia or cognitive impairment, and who did not have chart-documented smoking history consistent with LCS criteria. We also excluded individuals with 2 out of 6 incorrect responses to a cognitive screener adapted from the Mini Mental Status Exam [18].

Recruitment

We utilized the electronic health record to identify potential participants who met inclusion criteria. After obtaining approval from their primary care provider, we contacted potential participants by email or mail to inform them of their potential eligibility for our study. We then telephoned potential participants to confirm eligibility requirements, including tobacco history, and to assess willingness to participate. If the participant was eligible and willing during this telephone call, the participant was then scheduled for an in-person research appointment at their primary care practice, where they met a Clinical Research Coordinator (CRC) and informed consent was obtained. Participants were then asked to complete the pre-test survey, read the DA (which takes approximately 5–10 min), and complete the posttest survey. The CRC was present throughout the visit and available for questions about the surveys and DA. Participants were offered a $50 honorarium for participation in the study.

Data collection

The pre-test survey assessed knowledge, perceptions of decision-making, and sociodemographics. The post-test survey first assessed DA acceptability, then reassessed knowledge and perceptions of decision-making. Both surveys were administered during the same in-person visit at which the DA was administered, after eligibility was confirmed by telephone and informed consent was obtained upon arrival to the appointment.

Measures

We assessed numeracy with the Subjective Numeracy Scale 3 [19], an 8-item questionnaire developed from 42 numeracy questions from 3 separate studies. Answers highly correlate to objective numeracy scales but have several advantages over such scales, including requiring lower cognitive load, taking less time to complete, and being better received by participants. We assessed literacy with the Rapid Estimate of Adult Literacy in Medicine Short Form [20], a widely used estimate of literacy that requires the participant to read and verbalize 7 words related to medical topics. We assessed comorbidities with the Charlson Comorbidity Index, a widely used prognosticator of mortality [21]. We assessed participants' approach to healthcare using the Medical Maximizer-Minimizer Scale, a 10-item measure that estimates whether someone is predisposed to taking medical action [22]. Scores are significantly correlated with predisposition to pursue both low-benefit and high-benefit care.

The LCS-12 [23] Knowledge Measure, Decisional Conflict Scale [24], Stage of Decision Making measure [25], and Choice Predisposition/Decision measure [26] were administered pre- and post DA exposure. The LCS-12 is a brief measure consisting of 12 multiple-choice questions objectively assessing LCS knowledge, reported on a scale of 0–12 with one point for each question. It was developed with input from multiple expert stakeholders and testing on individuals with ≥30 pack-year history recruited from a tobacco treatment program. The LCS-12 has been validated based on performance before and after exposure to a video-based DA,

although the population was primarily White and had less than college-level education [27]. The Decisional Conflict Scale measures 5 dimensions of decision making: feeling uncertain, feeling uninformed, feeling unclear about values, feeling unsupported, and ineffective decision making. It is reported on a scale 0–80 with each domain reported on subscales of 0–15. Higher scores indicate more decisional conflict and inversely correlate with knowledge measures. Stage of Decision Making measures a patient's readiness for decision-making across four stages (range 1–4). Lower scores suggest the individual is earlier in the decision-making process and may benefit more from DA exposure. Choice Predisposition Decision measures the participants' self-reported propensity to undergo the test on a 1–15 scale and their choice before and after exposure to the intervention. At data analysis, patient scores were categorized as “no screening” (1–5), “uncertain about screening” (6–10), or “get screening” (11–15).

DA acceptability was evaluated after DA exposure, prior to repeating the four outcome measures. The acceptability measure was adapted from a standardized set of questions, including eight multiple-choice questions scored on a scale 10–40 and two open-ended questions for positive and constructive feedback [[28], [29]].

Statistical analyses

Descriptive statistics were calculated for the study participants. Scores on LCS-12 [23], Decisional Conflict Scale, Stage of Decision Making, and Choice Predisposition/Decision scales were compared pre- and post-DA exposure using Wilcoxon tests. All scales were ordinal, and the median [interquartile range (IQR)] are reported unless noted otherwise. We pre-defined a p-value less than 0.05 as statistically significant. Analyses were conducted using SAS version 9.4.

Results

Participants

Out of 4632 patients with any smoking history based on chart abstraction, 1850 patients were potentially eligible based on age and smoking history after manual chart review by the study team. Of these, the majority (1549) were excluded because the primary care provider refused or did not respond to a request for their assent to recruitment; 8 were hospitalized or in long-term care facilities; and 4 were deceased prior to outreach. We attempted to contact the remaining 289 potential participants by phone after receiving permission from their primary care provider. Of those, 111 declined and 80 were unable to be reached. After successfully contacting 98 potential participants, another 41 were deemed ineligible after reviewing their smoking history during the screening call, 4 declined to participate after successful screening, and 3 were unable to schedule the in-person appointment. Ultimately, we enrolled 50 participants (Fig. 1). Our recruited sample (Table 1) had a mean age of 65 years, similar frequency of men (46%) and women (54%), and diverse self-reported racial/ethnic identities (40% Hispanic, 38% non-Hispanic Black, 18% non- Hispanic White). Most indicated English as their native language (90%), made $3000 per month or less (80%), were not employed (67%), and had some insurance (98%). Participants had a high smoking burden (mean pack-years = 40) but most had quit (70%). Most participants (84%) had previously completed screening for at least one cancer, and 62% had previously completed LCS. Acceptability of decision aid

Participants rate the DA highly on the acceptability measure (acceptability mean [SD]: 32.3.

[4.9] on scale 10–40). Most participants rated the DA as having “just right” length (86%), “just right” amount of information (82%), and comprehensibility (86%). Most also rated the DA as having enough information to make a screening decision (84%). Participants were split on whether the DA was balanced (58%) or slanted toward getting an LDCT (42%), while none reported it as slanted away from LDCT.

In the open-ended questions soliciting positive and constructive feedback, 98% found it useful for making a screening decision while 2% stated “no opinion.” Positive feedback emphasized conciseness, clarity, layout, and design choices. 72% also left constructive feedback while 28% stated the DA was acceptable as-is. Constructive feedback was varied. 4% suggested avoiding the word “harms”, one of whom suggested replacing it with “potential negatives”. 8% suggested more detailed description and/or depiction of the LDCT procedure. 14% suggested more explanation of co-morbidities/chronic diseases. Particularly insightful comments included: “weighing your options section is scary” and “give these pamphlets [out] more; lung cancer is not discussed like other cancers”, which might be indicative of the complexity for patients to reach a preference-concordant LCS decision.

Discussion and conclusion

Discussion

Overall, our findings support a role for printed DA in LCS while raising important questions about the effectiveness of printed DAs for improving preference-concordant LCS uptake and their applications for individuals with multimorbidity.

Our findings of improved LCS knowledge after DA exposure and overall acceptance of the DA align with prior research on the role of DAs and strengthen the case for printed DAs.

Most DAs require patients to interact with multistep web-based interfaces; others require the patient to watch videos over 5 min long. Both approaches present challenges in many primary care settings lacking the means (including staff or provider time required to set up or assist with video DAs, in addition to the need for adequate digital infrastructure and equipment) to deliver video-based education to patients who are disadvantaged by low levels of digital literacy and multiple topics needing the attention of their treating physicians during brief appointments [[14], [15]]. Printed DAs therefore offer an opportunity to support SDM in resource-limited settings, reach individuals with low digital literacy, and engage patients outside the exam room such as waiting areas, lobbies, and community outreach, allowing scarce time in the exam room to focus on provider-patient discussion. In the sole study to evaluate a printed DA for LCS, the population had higher baseline decisional conflict (Decisional Conflict Scale median: 28.9), yet decisional conflict was only reduced modestly before and after DA exposure (28.9 vs. 25) [15]. Our study adds to prior research by observing similarly equivocal changes in individuals with multimorbidity while also observing an improvement in LCS knowledge. In our study, baseline decisional conflict was lower (median: 16), the distribution of DCS scores shifted downward non-significantly, and the median was unchanged. This result aligns with a recent randomized controlled trial in a veteran population of a novel, web-based LCS DA: the trial reported improved LCS knowledge and uptake in the intervention arm compared to the attention-control group receiving general education on cancer risk reduction, but no difference in the Decisional Conflict Scale [30]. Therefore, it is unclear whether a reduction in decisional conflict is required for a DA to be effective at improving overall uptake.

We did not observe an improvement in overall decisional conflict or choice disposition. It is possible that differences were not observed because participants' high baseline decisional readiness and low decisional conflict (Stage of Decision Making median: 4; Choice Predisposition/Decision median: 3) resulted in a ceiling effect. In light of prior research, it is also possible that digital DA are superior to printed DA, perhaps due to their interactive nature; however, given publication bias and a low number of studies reporting on printed DAs, qualitative research and randomized controlled trials directly comparing these methods may be useful to determine optimal strategies for specific applications or populations. These trials should also consider real-world implementation, such as time and resources needed for administration in a typical clinical setting by non-research clinical personnel, and should be powered to assess LCS uptake as a primary outcome.

Strengths of this study include prospective within-subjects design, diverse participants, high prevalence of serious comorbidities, and evaluation of multiple scales of decisional conflict and readiness. Furthermore, high acceptability of the DA and the printed format allow for immediate dissemination. Nevertheless, our study has several limitations. Our study was not powered to compare the DA to an alternative intervention nor to assess LCS test completion. The sample size was small and may have been subject to selection bias. Participants had high numeracy and literacy, characteristics that may be related to high baseline decisional confidence and low baseline decisional conflict, which may limit generalizability. Because the study sample was enriched for participants with multimorbidity and relatively few participants had low morbidity burden, our study was not designed to evaluate the impact of comorbidities on DA outcomes.

Innovation

Together with a pool of patients, primary care providers, and professional communicators, our research team has co-developed a printed DA to support the LCS decision-making of patients living with multimorbidity. The novel DA innovates on existing DAs by providing specific information on the impact of comorbidities on LCS risks and benefits.

In this pretest-posttest trial of the novel comorbidity-focused LCS DA delivered to participants recruited from a diverse primary care population, exposure to the DA was associated with improved knowledge regarding LCS and decisional certainty related to feeling informed about the decision. These findings indicate that the novel DA – considered highly acceptable by study participants – holds potential for more appropriately addressing the specific information needs of vulnerable patients living with one or more comorbidities.

Conclusion

This pretest-posttest trial of a novel decision aid provides preliminary evidence supporting the use of printed DAs to enhance knowledge in LCS decision-making for patients living with multimorbidity. Our study is the first study to evaluate an LCS DA specifically in this population, and many questions remain. Given improved knowledge in our study and improved LCS uptake in another trial [30], both without a change in decisional conflict, future studies should employ qualitative research methods to elucidate unexamined variables contributing to decision-making and include sufficiently large sample sizes of participants both with and without multimorbidity to understand potential differences between these populations. Are required to clarify the role of DAs in this population. Ultimately, given numerous small pretest-posttest DA trials with heterogeneous methods, alongside a paucity of evidence for effective strategies in patients with multimorbidity, randomized controlled trials are necessary to determine optimal strategies to improve preference-concordant LCS uptake for patients with or without multimorbidity.
