Concurrent Appearance of Inverted Follicular Keratosis with Epidermal Cyst: Is there any Association?
Authors: Shree Dhanani, Shubhangi Hirma, Keyur Patel, Pragya A. Nair
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

A 30-year-old male presented to the outpatient department of dermatology with non-itchy and painless elevated lesions on the neck for the past 5 years. The lesions gradually increased in size and number, with occasional pus discharge. The patient had a history of consuming unpasteurized milk and exposure to domestic animals since childhood. He was a chronic tobacco chewer. No other significant past or family history was noted.

On cutaneous examination, a few skin-coloured to erythematous, dome-shaped, non-tender papules to nodules of firm consistency, approximately 1 × 1 × 1 to 1 × 1 × 2 cm in size, with central depression, were present over the right side of the neck [Figure 1]. Hair, nails, genitals, and oral cavity were normal. No lymphadenopathy was present. All routine investigations, including histogram, random blood glucose, serum creatinine, and serum electrolytes, were normal. HIV, Hepatitis b surface antigen, Hepatitis c antibody was negative. Pus culture showed no organisms after 48 hours of aerobic incubation.

Few skin-coloured to erythematous, dome-shaped, firm, non-tender papules and nodules with a central depression over the right side of the neck

Dermoscopy (Dermlite DL-4) with 100x magnification showed a keratin plug with central whitish to yellowish amorphous areas and a few linear vessels and red dots surrounding it [Figure 2]. A skin biopsy was taken, keeping a differential diagnosis of molluscum contagiosum, scrofuloderma, sarcoidosis, hidradenitis suppurative, inverted follicular keratosis (IFK), seborrheic keratosis (SK), squamous cell carcinoma (SCC), histoplasmosis, actinomycotic mycetoma, and syringocystadenoma. Histopathology showed well-circumscribed lobules and finger like endophytic proliferation of squamous epithelium with cyst formation containing keratin material extending into the dermis. In some areas, irregular clefts with cyst formation were seen. Occasional squamous eddies and focal acantholysis of basal cells at the periphery with neutrophilic infiltration seen [Figure 3a and b]. This favoured the diagnosis of IFK.

A therapeutic surgical excision was conducted [Figure 4] and sent for biopsy, which revealed a subepidermal cyst lined by stratified squamous epithelium with a granular layer, containing keratin flakes and inflammatory infiltrates comprised of lymphocytes, plasma cells, and neutrophils [Figure 5].

IFK is an asymptomatic, rare, benign tumour of the follicular infundibulum characterized by endophytic or exophytic growth. It typically presents as a solitary, skin- coloured to reddish, elevated, slow-growing lesion over the head and neck in middle-aged to elderly males, but multiple IFK have been reported in patients with Cowden syndrome.[1]

The etiopathogenesis of IFK is not well-known. It represents a distinct entity related to viral wart, SK,[2] or a neoplasm of follicular origin.

The dermoscopic features of IFK have been described in only a few cases. The white structureless area corresponds with the amorphous central area in the presence of large tumour lobules composed of basaloid cells. The vascular patterns seen are hairpin vessels surrounded by a whitish halo or polymorphic blood vessels.[3]

Histopathology is the gold standard for diagnosis. It is characterized by endophytic proliferation of basaloid cells with a variable degree of squamous differentiation. Whorled squamous eddies and copious eosinophilic cytoplasm in the form of protruding squamous metaplasia is seen.

Treatment includes 5% imiquimod, which modulates and upregulates the immune system and has antitumoral and antiviral activity.[4] Complete surgical excision prevents invasive growth or metastasis.[5] Our patient was treated with wide surgical excision. The specimen was sent for biopsy, and it revealed an epidermal cyst.

Epidermal cysts are small, slow-growing, non-tender, dome-shaped lesions over hair-bearing skin areas. Cysts on the acral surfaces of the skin are considered to arise from the implantation of epidermis into the dermis through trauma. These cysts usually grow through the accumulation of epithelial and keratinous debris.[5] The simultaneous presence of an epidermal cyst might have some correlation with IFK. Also, no such cases have been previously reported. Chronic trauma due to shaving may be reason for simultaneous development of both conditions in our case.
