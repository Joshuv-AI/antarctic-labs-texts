Tissue‐Specific Developmental Changes in Lignin Deposition in Model Plants
Authors: Weiwei Zhu, Jaime Barros
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTLignin is a plant cell wall phenolic polymer and the largest renewable source of aromatic carbon in nature. While lignin is essential for plant survival, little work has been carried out to understand its variation across plant tissues and developmental stages. Here, we combined microscopy, spectrophotometry, and mass spectrometry to compare lignin deposition in roots, stems, and leaves of the model plants Arabidopsis thaliana and Brachypodium distachyon across four developmental stages. Brachypodium accumulated more lignin and exhibited higher syringyl‐to‐guaiacyl (S/G) ratios than Arabidopsis in all stages and tissues. Lignin deposition increased across all tissues over development and was maintained during senescence, with stems and roots showing the largest lignin content and leaves contributing more substantially at senescence. Furthermore, lignification began with the deposition of G‐units, followed by the accumulation of S‐units, which became more predominant at later developmental stages in all tissues. Brachypodium contained more p‐hydroxyphenyl (H) lignin than Arabidopsis, with the highest levels observed in roots compared to stems and leaves. Interestingly, while the S/G ratio in stems plateaued at maturity (R3 stage), roots of both species continued accumulating S‐lignin during senescence (S4 stage). These results show that herbaceous monocots and dicots have different content and chemical compositions of lignin depending on the time of harvest, with implications for both biomass utilization and biological carbon sequestration.Keywords: CO2 storage, monolignol biosynthesis, phenylpropanoids metabolism, secondary cell wall

Introduction

Lignin is a phenolic polymer found in the secondary cell walls of vascular plants, where it provides mechanical support and hydrophobicity critical for efficient water transport and plant growth. It also serves as a physical and chemical barrier against pests and pathogens and contributes to plant tolerance to abiotic stress (Peracchi et al. 2024). As one of the most abundant biopolymers on Earth, second only to cellulose, lignin accounts for up to 35% of the dry weight of plant biomass (Shorey et al. 2024). Due to its abundance, high carbon content, and chemically stable structure, lignin plays a central role in terrestrial carbon cycling and is considered a valuable renewable feedstock for the production of biobased fuels and materials (Dixon et al. 2024).

Lignin is synthesized via the phenylpropanoid pathway, which produces the building blocks of lignin (or monolignols): p‐coumaryl, coniferyl, and sinapyl alcohols. During lignin deposition, monolignols are synthesized in the cytosol and exported across the plasma membrane into the cell wall (apoplast), where they polymerize via free radical coupling, producing the three major types of lignin units: H (p‐hydroxyphenyl), G (guaiacyl), and S (syringyl) lignin, respectively (Vanholme et al. 2019). The content and relative proportion of each lignin subunit vary not only between plant taxa (Weng and Chapple 2010), but also among tissues and cell types (Ménard et al. 2022), and in response to specific environmental and developmental changes (Rogers and Campbell 2004). This natural variation in the composition of the lignin polymer has practical implications for biomass utilization. For example, plant tissues with higher syringyl‐to‐guaiacyl (S/G) ratios contain lignins rich in carbon–oxygen bonds, which are more susceptible to chemical and enzymatic degradation (Luo et al. 2024). The S/G ratio is by far the most extensively studied lignin characteristic, mainly due to its well‐established impact on wood pulping efficiency during paper production (Biermann 1996). It is considered a determining factor in pulpwood quality and has been used as a selection trait in tree breeding programs (Vanholme et al. 2013). Lignin composition has also been widely studied in herbaceous species, particularly grasses and legumes, due to its influence on forage digestibility and animal nutrition (Jung 1989; Rønn et al. 2022). More recently, lignin has gained renewed attention in the context of lignocellulosic biorefineries, mainly driven by its potential as a high‐value co‐product for the production of biofuels, bioproducts, and green chemicals (Ragauskas et al. 2014; Palumbo et al. 2024).

Despite its biological and industrial relevance, few studies have investigated the variation in lignin deposition in different tissues in the same plant or in tissues at different stages of development. Several studies comparing forage grasses and legumes at early and mature stages have reported significant increases in lignin content with plant maturity (Abiven et al. 2011; Wallsten and Hatfield 2016; Bhorade et al. 2023). However, lignification after physiological maturity and during senescence has been less studied, even though this stage is key for defining the optimal harvest timing for forage and biomass utilization (Liu et al. 2023). Lignin composition also differs between plant taxa. For example, angiosperms synthesize S‐units that are absent in gymnosperms (Wagner et al. 2015). There are also differences within angiosperms. For instance, wild‐type herbaceous dicots, such as the model plant A. thaliana , typically have low S‐lignin (S/G ~0.1–0.5), while woody angiosperms like Populus species show higher S/G > 1.5 (Meyer et al. 1998; Lourenço and Pereira 2018). Similarly, among monocot grasses, B. distachyon and maize stems accumulate a higher proportion of S‐units (S/G ~1.5) (Barros et al. 2022; Oliveira et al. 2025), whereas others like sorghum (S/G ~0.5) or rice (S/G ~0.3) have higher proportions of G‐units (Tetreault et al. 2020; Afifi et al. 2022). Changes in lignin composition with growth have not been extensively studied. Rencoret et al. (2011), reported an increase in S‐lignin deposition during wood maturation in Eucalyptus. Similarly, Mann et al. (2009) and He et al. (2018) reported higher lignin content and S/G ratios in more mature internode stems of switchgrass and maize. Differences in lignin content and composition have also been reported between plant parts. For example, monocot roots often contain higher total lignin content and S/G ratios compared to stems and leaves (Abiven et al. 2011; Yang et al. 2024). However, lignification patterns in belowground tissues are still understudied. While these general trends have been observed, the available information is limited to a few plant species, and no single study has performed a systematic analysis of the spatial and temporal patterns of lignin deposition during plant development in model plants.

To address this gap, we combined histochemical staining, fluorescence microscopy, and chemical and mass spectrometry methods to compare lignin content and composition in roots, stems, and leaves of Arabidopsis thaliana and Brachypodium distachyon across four developmental stages: one vegetative (V1), two reproductive (R2 and R3), and one senescent (S4). We selected Arabidopsis and Brachypodium because they are established and widely used genetic models for investigating lignin biosynthesis in plants. Specifically, we asked two main questions: (1) Does lignification continue after physiological maturity?; (2) If so, does it occur uniformly across tissues, and do above‐ and below‐ground tissues show similar changes in lignin composition? The implications of the spatial and temporal differences in lignin deposition observed between monocot and dicot plants for both biomass utilization and biological carbon sequestration are discussed.

Results

Selection of Developmental Stages

We selected four developmental stages in A. thaliana (Col‐0) and B. distachyon (Bd21‐3) to analyze lignin content and composition in root, stem, and leaf tissues, using phenological data. In Arabidopsis, Vegetative stage 1 (V1) was defined at 3 days after bolting (DAB), when flowers on the main stem began to show petals, lateral branches had not yet elongated, and average plant height was 9.2 cm. Reproductive stage 2 (R2) occurred at 7 DAB, when the main stem began to form siliques, and the average plant height was 28.3 cm. Late reproductive stage 3 (R3) was defined at 20 DAB, when flowering of the main stem and lateral branches was nearly complete, with most siliques developed, with an average plant height of 50.1 cm. Senescent stage 4 (S4) was defined at 60 DAB, when all siliques had matured, plants had turned brown and naturally dried, and the average plant height was 53.8 cm (Figure 1A,C). Similarly in Brachypodium, V1 stage was defined at 25 days after planting (DAP), coinciding with flowering initiation, and an average plant height of 14.2 cm; R2 stage was set at 35 DAP, when the main stem began to form seeds and plants began tillering, with an average plant height of 21.3 cm; R3 stage occurred at 45 DAP, when tillering was complete and seeds entered the grain‐filling stage, with plants reaching an average height of 28.9 cm. Stage S4 represented senescence at 90 DAP, when seeds were fully matured, plants had turned brown and dried naturally, and the average plant height was 32.1 cm (Figure 1B,D). These four stages capture the main phases in plant development, enabling a systematic comparison of lignin deposition across tissues in both species.

Histochemical Analysis of Lignin Deposition During Development

We first performed microscopic analysis of basal stem cross‐sections at each developmental stage using fluorescence microscopy and multiple staining methods, including Wiesner (phloroglucinol‐HCl), Mäule, and toluidine blue (Figure S1). These methods provide complementary information on lignin content and composition. UV excitation enables visualization of lignin autofluorescence but can generate non‐specific signals from other aromatic compounds. Alternatively, phloroglucinol (Wiesner) staining detects coniferaldehyde and p‐coumaraldehyde, showing G and H but not S‐lignin subunits; whereas the Mäule reaction specifically stains S‐lignin, and toluidine blue differentiates lignin (blue) from pectin‐rich (purple) cell walls. The UV‐microscopy results showed that in both Arabidopsis and Brachypodium, lignin autofluorescence gradually increased during plant development, with this trend being particularly pronounced in Arabidopsis (Figure 2A,B). Stronger fluorescence signals were observed in the stems at later developmental stages (Figure 2A). In addition, the histochemical staining results with phloroglucinol‐HCl, Mäule, and toluidine blue were consistent with these findings (Figure 2A,B). In both Arabidopsis and Brachypodium, tissue staining became progressively more intense during plant development, particularly in the interfascicular region, indicating that sclerenchyma fiber cells accumulate higher lignin levels during maturation. Mäule staining revealed that S‐unit deposition became more prominent between R2 and R3 stages, suggesting that S‐lignin contributes to later phases of secondary cell wall formation. Toluidine blue staining revealed pink colored cells in Arabidopsis, indicative of pectin‐rich type I cell walls, while Brachypodium showed mainly blue staining, characteristic of type II cell walls with high lignin levels. Therefore, in the stems of both plants, lignification intensifies with plant maturity, mainly driven by fiber cell wall thickening and S‐lignin deposition.

Developmental Changes in Lignin Content

To study how lignin deposition varies among tissues over time, we quantified the lignin content in roots, stems, and leaves at each developmental stage in Arabidopsis and Brachypodium using the acetyl bromide method (Figure S1). Lignin content increased gradually during plant development in all plant parts of both species, with stems showing the largest absolute increase (Figure 2C,D). In Brachypodium, however, leaves exhibited the largest relative (%) increase from V1 to S4. In Arabidopsis, stem lignin content increased from 55 mg/g CWR (5.5 wt%) at stage V1 to 28.7 wt% at stage S4, whereas in Brachypodium, stem lignin increased from 19.3 to 35.1 wt% (Figure 2C,D). Lignin content in Arabidopsis stems doubled from 11.4 to 23.8 wt% between stages R2 and R3 (7 to 20 DAB). After physiological maturity, between R3 and S4 stages, lignin content continued to increase in all organs of both species, with Arabidopsis and Brachypodium leaves showing the largest increases (77% and 47%, respectively). Together, these data show that lignin deposition increases progressively during plant development and continues beyond physiological maturity into late senescence.

Lignin Content in Different Plant Parts

Brachypodium accumulated more lignin than Arabidopsis across all tissues and developmental stages (Figure 2C,D). At senescence (S4), stems reached the highest overall lignin content in both species, with Brachypodium reaching 35.1 wt% and Arabidopsis 28.7 wt%. Similarly, root lignin levels at S4 were substantially higher in Brachypodium (33.5 wt%) than in Arabidopsis (24.5 wt%). Leaves had the lowest lignin content in both species, with Brachypodium showing over twice as much lignin as Arabidopsis (26.7 vs. 11.3 wt%). These results show similar tissue‐specific lignification patterns (i.e., stems > roots > leaves) in both species, with Brachypodium exhibiting higher overall lignin levels across all plant parts.

Brachypodium and Arabidopsis showed different patterns of lignin allocation across plant tissues (Figure S2). In both species, roots dominated lignin deposition at early stages (V1), but the patterns of tissue allocation diverged over time. For instance, by senescence (S4), lignin allocation in Brachypodium was more evenly distributed among roots, stems, and leaves, whereas Arabidopsis displayed a rapid increase in stem lignification, with stems dominating the total lignin pool at S4. In leaves, lignin allocation remained low during development in Arabidopsis, but increased moderately in Brachypodium (Figure S2). These data show developmental differences in lignin allocation across plant tissues in both Arabidopsis (dicot) and Brachypodium (monocot).

Changes in Lignin Composition During Plant Growth

To investigate the changes in lignin composition across different plant tissues during development, we used thioacidolysis followed by GC–MS analysis (Figure S1). The results show that lignin composition changed during development across all tissues, with a consistent decrease in G‐lignin and a corresponding increase in the proportion of S‐lignin (Figure 3A,B). These findings suggest that S‐lignin deposition occurs later than G‐lignin and gradually becomes more predominant as development proceeds. In addition, the stems showed the strongest decrease in the relative proportion of G‐lignin and the most pronounced increase in the percentage of S‐lignin among plant tissues, especially during the transition from the R2 to R3 stages (Figure 3A,B). This result was conserved in both Arabidopsis and Brachypodium. These changes in G‐ and S‐lignin deposition led to a significant increase in the S/G ratio across all tissues, with the most marked shift occurring in the stem during the transition from R2 to R3 stage (Figure 3C,D). Interestingly, while the S/G ratio in stems plateaued at maturity (R3 stage), roots continued to accumulate S‐lignin during senescence (S4 stage), with S/G ratios increasing by 62% in Arabidopsis and 39% in Brachypodium (Figure 3C,D). Overall, these results show that dimethoxylated S units are incorporated into the lignin polymer later than monomethoxylated G units (Figure 3E) and continue to accumulate during senescence, particularly in root tissues.

Differences Between Tissues in Lignin Composition

Across all developmental stages and tissues, Brachypodium accumulated higher H‐lignin than Arabidopsis, with the largest difference observed in stems (~5‐fold) and roots (2‐fold) (Figure 3A,B). Roots had the highest H‐lignin content in Brachypodium, where values were larger than 10% until S4. H‐lignin in stems decreased with development in both species, while levels in leaves remained similar between Arabidopsis and Brachypodium. On average across all developmental stages, Brachypodium had a 6.7‐fold higher S/G ratio in leaves, a 4.7‐fold increase in stems, and a 9.4‐fold increase in roots compared to Arabidopsis (Figures 3C,D and S3). Overall, our dataset shows that lignification is a tissue‐ and species‐specific plant trait, with Brachypodium showing a more pronounced accumulation of lignin‐rich in S‐units across all tissues. In both species: (1) roots showed the highest lignin content at early stages (V1–R2); (2) stems displayed the strongest developmental increases; and (3) leaves had the lowest lignin levels and S/G ratios (Figure S4 and Table S1).

Changes in Cell Wall‐Bound Phenolics in Brachypodium

The cell walls of Brachypodium, like other grasses, contain significant levels of cell wall‐bound p‐hydroxycinnamates. p‐Coumaric acid (pCA) is mainly acylated to the S‐lignin subunits and is thought to facilitate radical transfer during polymerization, whereas ferulic acid (FA) reinforces the wall by cross‐linking glucuronoarabinoxylans and lignin (Ralph 2010; Rancour et al. 2012). In leaves, we found both pCA and FA increased steadily throughout development. The pCA content increased from 5.9 mg/g at the V1 stage to 9.7 mg/g at the S4 stage, while the FA content rose from 4.7 mg/g at the V1 stage to 7.2 mg/g at the S4 stage (Figure S5). In stems, pCA levels were highest at R2 and declined slightly thereafter, while FA remained relatively stable across developmental stages. In roots, pCA levels remained high across development and showed a marked increase at S4, reaching the highest absolute values observed in any tissue (13.6 mg pCA g−1 CWR). By contrast, FA levels declined progressively from V1 to S4. Overall, pCA levels were higher than FA in all tissues and developmental stages, with particularly pronounced differences in senescent roots (Figure S5).

Discussion

One of the main goals of this study was to determine whether lignification persists after physiological maturity, as lignification at late developmental stages could impact biomass processing in bioenergy crops or reduce silage digestibility in forage crops. While plant height plateaued at R3 stages, marking the end of active vegetative and reproductive growth (Figure 1), lignin continued to accumulate in all tissues, indicating that lignification persists during senescence (Figure 2). This sustained deposition of lignin during late developmental stages may result from residual metabolic activity, enabling the polymerization of monolignols. Additionally, lignin deposition at the S4 stage could be facilitated by increased levels of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which commonly accumulate during cell death (Pennell and Lamb 1997). ROS can act as substrates for cell wall peroxidases and laccases, which catalyze the oxidation of monolignols, facilitating their polymerization into lignin (Tobimatsu and Schuetz 2019). Supporting this hypothesis, peroxidase and laccase activities have been detected in decaying wood (Větrovský et al. 2011; van der Wal et al. 2016), suggesting that lignin polymerization may occur during late senescence, even in dead tissues. Our results align with previous observations in Brachypodium, showing that stem lignification during late development is localized in the interfascicular region, with minimal changes in vascular bundles (Matos et al. 2013). This suggests that the spatial pattern of lignification during senescence could be conserved in monocots and dicot species, and that lignification in the interfascicular fibers may contribute to the plant's structural integrity at this stage. Further work is needed to elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying post‐harvest lignification, with particular attention to interfascicular fiber cells.

Lignin is often cited as representing between 20% and 35% of the cell wall dry weight of plants at maturity (McKendry 2002; Shorey et al. 2024). Our findings support this range, but also reveal substantial variation in lignin content across species, organs, and developmental stages (Figure 2C,D). For example, in Arabidopsis, leaf lignin content varied from 6 to 11 wt%, stems from 6 to 29 wt%, and roots from 13 to 25 wt%, whereas in Brachypodium, leaf lignin content varied from 12 to 27 wt%, stems from 19 to 35 wt%, and roots from 26 to 34 wt% of the cell wall. Across all experimental samples, lignin content ranged from 5 to 35 wt% with S/G ratios between 0.02 and 1.57 (Figure S4). Our results show that Brachypodium accumulated nearly twice as much lignin as Arabidopsis across all tissues and developmental stages. One possible explanation for this difference is the presence of bifunctional phenylalanine/tyrosine ammonia‐lyase (PTAL) in grasses, enabling monolignol biosynthesis from either phenylalanine or tyrosine (Barros et al. 2016; Takeda‐Kimura et al. 2024). Unlike dicots such as Arabidopsis, which use only phenylalanine as the entry point to the phenylpropanoid pathway, grasses possess PTAL enzymes that also convert tyrosine into p‐coumaric acid (Barros et al. 2016). We speculate that the ability of PTAL to use both substrates may increase flux into the monolignol biosynthetic pathway and could thereby contribute to the higher lignin content observed in Brachypodium. Access to both phenylalanine and tyrosine pools may also enable grasses to modulate lignification in response to environmental stress (Dixon and Barros 2019). Considering that lignin is mainly deposited in interfascicular fibers during late developmental stages, it would be interesting to test whether PTAL plays a more prominent role in these cell types compared to vascular bundles.

The most pronounced changes in lignin content occurred during reproductive development (R2–R3), coinciding with seed development. During this phase, carbon is remobilized from vegetative tissues toward developing seeds, primarily as proteins and carbohydrates (Gebbing and Schnyder 1999). In contrast, lignin cannot be remobilized once deposited into the cell wall, which likely contributes to its increased relative levels observed during senescence. Additionally, the increase in lignin may reflect not only polysaccharide remobilization but also active lignification after cell death (Pesquet et al. 2013). Among plant organs, roots contained the highest lignin levels during most developmental stages, with stems only surpassing roots at the S4 stage in both Arabidopsis and Brachypodium, a pattern consistent with previous findings (Abiven et al. 2011; Yang et al. 2024). The high lignin content in roots is particularly relevant for carbon storage in soils, as higher lignin deposition is generally linked to slower root decomposition rates (Sun et al. 2018). Notably, while stem S/G ratios plateaued after maturity, roots continued to accumulate S‐lignin during senescence. The underlying cause of this observation is unclear, but it may reflect root‐specific roles related to interactions with the soil microbiome. Lignin biosynthesis shares upstream precursors with flavonoids, which are known to modulate microbial activity in the root zone (Bais et al. 2006), and both pathways use biosynthetic enzymes such as caffeic acid O‐methyltransferase (COMT), involved in the biosynthesis of S‐lignin and the flavonoid tricin (Eudes et al. 2017). Recent work in Brachypodium has shown that comt mutants alter the composition of root‐associated microbiomes (Priya et al. 2025). Similarly, the observed increase in cell wall‐bound pCA in Brachypodium roots at the S4 stage may indicate a role in root‐microbe interactions, as pCA is a common root exudate that can influence the composition of the rhizosphere microbial communities (Zhou et al. 2018; Novak et al. 2025). Together, these observations suggest that S‐lignin and pCA deposition during root senescence may contribute to plant‐microbe interactions in the rhizosphere. A better understanding of how lignin is deposited and degraded in senescent roots, and the impact that this process has on soil carbon sequestration, could be applied to maximize the benefits of food and bioenergy crops, while also promoting soil health (Moinet et al. 2023).

In summary, there is a clear transition from reproductive to senescent lignification in both Arabidopsis and Brachypodium (Figure 4). On average, Brachypodium exhibited nearly twofold higher lignin content than Arabidopsis, with earlier and higher deposition of S‐lignin. In contrast, Arabidopsis exhibited a more pronounced transition in stem lignification, with a steep increase in S/G after bolting. In both dicot and monocot model plants, lignin levels peaked at senescence in stems, followed by roots and leaves. Leaves showed the most modest S/G ratio changes, along with a substantial increase in total lignin deposition at senescence. Lignification continued well after physiological maturity across all tissues, both above and below ground. While S/G in stems plateaued at maturity, roots continued to accumulate S‐lignin during senescence in both model plants. Collectively, our results show species‐ and tissue‐specific variation in developmental lignification that extends well into late senescence, with implications for animal feed production, biomass bioprocessing, and carbon capture in plants.

Materials and Methods

Plant Material

Arabidopsis thaliana (Col‐0) and Brachypodium distachyon (Bd21‐3) were grown in a controlled growth chamber in plastic pots (9 × 9 cm) filled with Berger BM7 soil. Plants were maintained under a 16‐h light/8‐h dark photoperiod at 22°C–25°C, with a light intensity of ~300 μmol m−2 s−1 and a relative humidity of 60%. For each developmental stage, whole root systems were washed free of soil and separated from the shoots at the root collar. Basal stem segments were collected for microscopy, and the remaining stem tissue was used for lignin content and composition analyses, as described below. Leaf sampling included only rosette leaves in Arabidopsis, whereas in Brachypodium, all leaves were collected. Following harvest, all tissues were ground in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C until further analysis.

Histochemical Staining and Microscopy

Basal stem segments (~2 cm) from different developmental stages of A. thaliana and B. distachyon were hand‐trimmed and sectioned into 70 μm‐thick transverse slices using a Leica VT1000S vibrating blade microtome. To visualize lignin, three histochemical staining methods were used. Phloroglucinol–HCl (Wiesner) staining was conducted with a 2% (w/v) phloroglucinol solution in ethanol, mixed with 12 N HCl in a 2:1 ratio (Mitra and Loqué 2014). This method highlights cinnamaldehyde residues in lignin. For Mäule staining, the slices were immersed in 1% w/v KMnO4 for 5 min, rinsed twice with water, incubated in 12% HCl for 5 min, rinsed twice with water, and observed after adding 1.5% NaHCO3 solution. This staining detects syringyl (S) lignin subunits, indicated by a reddish coloration. Toluidine blue staining, a polychromatic method particularly useful for detecting pectins (pink‐purple) and lignin (blue), was performed using 0.02% toluidine blue O solution. Light and lignin UV‐autofluorescence images were taken with an Echo Revolve R4 microscope equipped with a DAPI LED light cube (excitation: 380/30 nm; emission: 450/50 nm; dichroic mirror: 425 nm).

Lignin Content and Composition Analyses

Cell wall residues (CWRs) were prepared by sequential extractions of fresh leaf, stem, and root tissues with methanol, chloroform/methanol (2:1), and Milli‐Q water, followed by overnight lyophilization. Total lignin content was determined spectrophotometrically using the acetyl bromide method (Moreira‐Vilar et al. 2014). Briefly, 1.90–2.10 mg of each CWR sample was incubated with 0.5 mL of 25% (v/v) acetyl bromide in glacial acetic acid at 70°C for 30 min. After chilling on ice for 10 min and centrifuging, 20 μL of supernatant was mixed with 380 μL of a quenching solution composed of 2 M NaOH, 5 M hydroxylamine‐HCl, and glacial acetic acid in a ratio of 0.9:0.1:6 (v/v/v) and transferred to a quartz cuvette. Absorbance was measured at 280 nm with a UV–Vis spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Lignin concentration was quantified based on a standard curve generated from alkali lignin (Sigma, CAS 8068‐05‐1) processed in parallel.

Lignin composition (H%, G%, S%, and S/G ratio) was determined by thioacidolysis followed by GC–MS analysis, following established protocols (Barros et al. 2019). Briefly, 2.90–3.10 mg of each CWR sample was incubated with 2 mL of thioacidolysis reagent, consisting of dioxane:ethanethiol:boron trifluoride etherate in an 87.5:10:2.5 ratio for 3 h at 103°C. Following incubation, 2.7 mL of Milli‐Q water, 0.57 mL of saturated NaHCO3, 20 μL of 0.3% (w/v) docosane (internal standard), and 2 mL of chloromethane were sequentially added to each sample. One milliliter of organic phase was transferred to a glass vial and dried under a stream of nitrogen gas. Samples were derivatized with 100 μL of pyridine‐BSTFA containing 1% TMCS (1:1 v/v) and analyzed by GC–MS (Agilent 8890/5977B GC–MS). Lignin monomers were separated on an Agilent HP‐5MS column (30 m × 0.25 mm, 0.25 μm film thickness) and identified using their characteristic mass‐to‐charge ratios: 239 m/z for H units, 269 m/z for G units, 299 m/z for S units, and 57 m/z for the internal standard (docosane).

Quantification of Cell Wall‐Linked Phenolics

Approximately 4.90–5.10 mg of each CWR sample was used for analysis. Samples were subjected to low‐temperature alkaline hydrolysis (0.5 mL 2 M NaOH, 37°C, 12 h) to release esterified cell wall‐bound ferulate (FA) and p‐coumarate (pCA). After acidification to pH 2.0 with 6 M HCl, phenolics were extracted three times with ethyl acetate (0.5 mL each). The pooled extracts were dried under nitrogen and derivatized with 100 μL of BSTFA/pyridine (1:1, v/v) for GC–MS analysis using the protocol described above for lignin composition analysis. Quantification was based on calibration curves generated from pure FA and pCA standards processed in parallel.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis of the results was performed using two‐way ANOVA with Bonferroni's post‐test. A 95% confidence interval was used for statistics and p < 0.05 was considered significant. All statistical tests were performed using GraphPad Prism 10 software.

Figure S1: Overview of lignin analyses in this study. Cell walls were isolated from crude dried Brachypodium and Arabidopsis plant material (root, stems and leaves). The cell wall material was then weighted into aliquots and subdivided for the acetyl bromide and GC–MS analysis. Samples were treated with acetyl bromide and the solubilized lignin quantified by UV‐spectroscopy (Abs: 280 nm). For the determination of lignin composition, plant material was subjected to thioacidolysis. The solubilized phenolics underwent TMS derivatization and were separated and quantified by GC–MS analysis based on their characteristic mass‐to‐charge (m/z) ratios: m/z 299 (S, syringyl unit), 269 (G, guaiacyl unit), and 239 (H, p‐hydroxyphenyl unit). The basal internodes of the inflorescence stem were used for UV lignin autofluorescence, as well as phloroglucinol (Wiesner), Mäule, and Toluidine blue staining.
