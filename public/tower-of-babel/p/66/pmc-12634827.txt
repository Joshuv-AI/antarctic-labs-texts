Earthworm fibrinolytic enzyme A and fibrin-derived peptide Bβ15-42 attenuate atherosclerosis via the VE-Cadherin signaling pathway
Authors: Xiaowei Yin, Yajie Zhang, Luyao Shi, Junwen Xue, Zhen Tian, Chicheng Gong, Fen Wang, Yongjun Cao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimEarthworm fibrinolytic enzymes A (EFEa) is the main active component of earthworm fibrinolytic enzymes which possesses anticoagulant, fibrinolytic, and potential anti-atherosclerotic effects. However, the biological function and mechanism of action of EFEa remains unclear. This study aims to investigate the role of EFEa and the fibrin-derived peptide Bβ15-42 (Bβ15-42) in attenuating atherosclerosis by modulating endothelial adhesion and inflammation via the vascular endothelial (VE)-cadherin signaling pathway.MethodsIn this study, we established both a rabbit model of atherosclerosis and an in vitro endothelial cell model. Bβ15-42, EFEa, and rosuvastatin (ROS) were administered to evaluate their effects on atherosclerotic plaque formation and inflammatory responses. Plaque size and intimal thickening were evaluated using Oil Red O and hematoxylin-eosin (HE) staining. Fibrinogen deposition and VE-cadherin expression were analyzed via western blotting and immunohistochemistry. Furthermore, macrophage migration assays and CDH5-positive (CDH5+) human umbilical vein endothelial cell (HUVEC) models were utilized to investigate the effects of these compounds on inflammatory cell migration and VE-cadherin-mediated endothelial barrier function.ResultsTreatment with Bβ15-42, EFEa, and ROS significantly reduced atherosclerotic plaque formation and intimal thickening in the rabbit model of atherosclerosis (P < 0.05). Both Bβ15-42 and EFEa significantly reduced fibrinogen deposition and inflammatory cell infiltration in the aortic tissue of atherosclerotic rabbits (P < 0.05). Western blot and immunohistochemical analyses confirmed that Bβ15-42 and EFEa effectively up-regulated VE-cadherin expression (P < 0.05). In vitro, Bβ15-42 and EFEa significantly inhibited fibrin-induced macrophage transmigration across endothelial cell monolayers, reducing migration by 83.3 % and 56.1 %, respectively (P < 0.05, respectively).ConclusionEFEa and Bβ15-42 exert protective effects against atherosclerosis via the VE-cadherin pathway. Our findings lay the groundwork for further research into the anti-atherosclerotic mechanisms of EFEa, as well as its potential for future development and application.Keywords: Earthworm fibrinolytic enzyme A, Fibrin-derived peptide Bβ15-42, Atherosclerosis, Fibrinogen, VE-Cadherin signaling pathway

Introduction

Cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases remain among the leading causes of morbidity and mortality worldwide, with their incidence continuing to rise [[1], [2], [3], [4]]. Elevated fibrinogen levels are recognized as a major risk factor in these conditions, contributing significantly to inflammatory processes during the development of atherosclerosis [5,6]. Fibrinogen interacts with various cell types and is implicated in numerous pathological conditions, including stroke, traumatic brain injury, spinal cord injury, multiple sclerosis, bacterial infections and cancer, all of which may share a common inflammatory component associated with atherosclerosis [5,[7], [8], [9], [10]]. Notably, fibrinogen deposition has been observed across various stages of atherosclerotic lesion development [10]. Early subendothelial accumulation of fibrinogen compromises endothelial barrier function, promotes leukocyte infiltration, and accelerates plaque formation [11,12]. Vascular endothelial (VE)-cadherin is a pivotal adhesion molecule expressed specifically in endothelial cells. Through its extracellular domain, it mediates homophilic interactions, while its intracellular domain binds to catenins, linking to the cytoskeletal protein F-actin via α-catenin. This cadherin-catenin complex is critical for maintaining vascular integrity and regulating endothelial cell permeability [13].

The fibrin-derived peptide Bβ15-42, a 28-amino acid fragment from the N-terminus of the fibrinogen β-chain, is generated during fibrinolysis and can also be synthesized exogenously [14,15]. It has been shown to reduce myocardial ischemia-reperfusion injury in animal models, primarily by attenuating fibrinogen-mediated inflammatory responses [[16], [17], [18], [19], [20]]. Moreover, Bβ15-42 has demonstrated clinical benefits in treating vascular leakage syndrome in patients with severe Ebola virus disease [21,22], supporting its vascular protective properties. However, despite these findings, the role of Bβ15-42 in chronic vascular diseases such as atherosclerosis remains underexplored.

Earthworm fibrinolytic enzymes (EFEs), derived from Lumbricus rubellus and Eisenia fetida, have been extensively characterized and are now commercially available in Korea and China as orally administered fibrinolytic agents [23,24]. These enzymes are indicated for the prevention and treatment of cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases [25]. EFEs exhibits dual affinity toward both plasminogen and fibrin, enabling it to bind directly to fibrin and exert thrombolytic effects [26]. Among them, Earthworm Fibrinolytic Enzyme A (EFEa) is the principal active component and selectively hydrolyzes fibrinogen at the Val21-Glu22 site on the Aα chain [27]. It also contains cleavage sites corresponding to those of thrombin at Arg16–Gly17 on the Aα chain and Arg14-Gly15 on the Bβ chain [26]. Given the resemblance of EFEa-generated fragments to Bβ15-42, EFEa is hypothesized to possess anti-inflammatory and vascular-protective effects [7]. Our previous clinical research demonstrated that long-term oral administration of EFEa significantly contributes to the secondary prevention of ischemic stroke [28], supporting its potential role in chronic vascular protection.

To date, most studies involving Bβ15-42 or EFEa have focused on their effects in acute inflammation or thrombosis, with limited investigation into their role in chronic vascular conditions such as atherosclerosis [14,17,29]. Furthermore, the underlying mechanisms, particularly those involving the VE-cadherin signaling pathway, remain poorly understood. Therefore, in this study, we hypothesize that EFEa and Bβ15-42 attenuate atherosclerosis by modulating endothelial adhesion and inflammation through the VE-cadherin pathway. Elucidating these mechanisms may reveal novel therapeutic strategies for the prevention and treatment of atherosclerosis.

Materials and methods

Reagents and antibodies

EFEa was obtained from the Institute of Biophysics, Chinese Academy of Sciences (Beijing, China). This native protein, derived from Eisenia fetida, is the first reported crystal structure with serine protease activity isolated from an annelid [21,22]. ROS was acquired from Yuan Ye Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Fibrin was prepared using fibrinogen and thrombin, both obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Anti-fibrinogen antibody (ab27913), anti-β-actin antibody (ab6276) and anti-CD68 antibody (ab283654) were purchased from Abcam (Cambridge, UK). Bβ15-42 was provided by Taimike Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Suzhou, China). Anti-VE-cadherin antibody (SAB4301448) and bromophenol blue powder (B0126) were sourced from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Oil Red O staining solution was purchased from Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) and murine macrophages (RAW264.7) were acquired from Shanghai GeneChem (Shanghai, China). HUVECs were transduced with the lentiviral vector CDH5-GV358 (GeneChem, Shanghai, China), which encodes full-length human VE-cadherin; cells transduced with the empty vector (GV358) served as the control. Cell culture reagents were obtained from Gibco (Grand Island, NY, USA). The lentiviral vector CDH5-GV358 was provided by GeneChem (Shanghai, China). Secondary antibodies were sourced from Abcam (Cambridge, UK).

Animal model

A total of 36 male New Zealand White rabbits (4 months old, 2.4 ± 0.1 kg), supplied by the Experimental Animal Center of Suzhou University, were housed under controlled conditions (22 ± 2 °C, 12-h light/dark cycle) with access to food and water ad libitum. All experimental procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee and complied with the National Institutes of Health (NIH, USA) Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. After one week of acclimatization on a standard diet, the rabbits were fed a 1 % cholesterol diet (Shuangshi Animal Feed Technology, Suzhou, China) for 8 weeks. At the end of the 9th week, they were randomly assigned to four groups (n = 9 per group): a control group (CTRL) receiving no additional treatment; a Bβ15-42 group receiving 2.5 mg/kg via intravenous injection (with physiological saline as the vehicle) every three days for 4 weeks; an EFEa group receiving 20 mg/kg via daily oral gavage (with drinking water as the vehicle) for 4 weeks; and a rosuvastatin group (ROS) receiving 5 mg/kg via daily oral gavage (with drinking water as the vehicle) for 4 weeks. Oral administration was performed by gastric gavage to ensure accurate and consistent delivery of the compounds. The oral dose of 20 mg/kg for EFEa was rationally determined through an integrated approach: beginning with the human clinical dose (approx. 20 mg/kg for a 60 kg adult) and applying body surface area-based allometric scaling (FDA guideline), a preliminary rabbit equivalent of 60 mg/kg was derived; this dose was then adjusted to 20 mg/kg to account for the approximately 3.3-fold higher specific enzymatic activity (>5 U/mg) of the EFEa preparation used in this study compared to commercial lumbrokinase (∼1.5 U/mg), thereby ensuring a pharmacologically relevant and activity-adjusted exposure. Although direct plasma level measurement was not performed in this study, the systemic absorption and oral bioavailability (estimated 10 %–15 % for the intact enzyme) of EFEa have been confirmed by previous pharmacokinetic studies [30], supporting the physiological relevance of the selected dose and administration route. ROS was included as a positive control group, given its well-documented efficacy in attenuating atherosclerosis through lipid-lowering and anti-inflammatory mechanisms [31].

Oil Red O staining

To evaluate the extent of atherosclerotic lesions, aortas were longitudinally sectioned to expose the intimal surface, and plaques were visualized using Oil Red O staining. Briefly, after euthanasia, the thoracic and abdominal cavities were opened to fully expose the aorta. A 5 cm segment of the thoracic aorta, starting from the inferior edge of the aortic arch, was dissected and divided into proximal and distal portions. The tissue was rinsed in cold saline (4 °C) to remove residual debris and fixed in 4 % paraformaldehyde. Subsequently, each aortic segment was longitudinally incised and flattened to expose the endothelial surface. An Oil Red O working solution was prepared by mixing 6 parts of Oil Red O stock solution with 4 parts of distilled water, followed by filtration. The tissues were immersed in the staining solution for 3 min and then washed in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) for 5 min; this washing step was repeated three times. The stained aortas were flattened with the lumen facing upward and imaged using a Zeiss Axio Observer A1 microscope under a 20x objective. Atherosclerotic areas, identified as orange-red stained (Oil Red O-positive) regions, were quantified. The percentage of the Oil Red O-positive area relative to the total endothelial surface area was analyzed using ImageJ software.

Hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining

We assessed plaque area and arterial media thickness in atherosclerotic tissue using hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining. The procedure is as follows: Paraffin sections were immersed in hematoxylin staining solution for 15 min until lightly stained, then transferred to water and rinsed for 2 min to remove excess dye. The sections were then placed in 1 % hydrochloric acid alcohol for 30 s for differentiation, allowing the sections to fade appropriately. Next, the sections were rinsed under running water for 30 min until the tissue turned blue, followed by immersion in eosin staining solution for 5 min. The staining process was repeated 1–2 times, then sections underwent gradient dehydration, clearing, and mounting for microscopic examination. Images were captured using a Zeiss microscope (Axio Observer A1) with a 20x objective.

Masson staining

We used Masson staining to evaluate the presence of intimal hyperplasia in atherosclerotic tissue and the arrangement of aortic media fibers, including pathological fiber arrangement disorders or calcification. The procedure can be briefly described as follows: Immerse the prepared sections in hematoxylin staining solution for 10 min, then properly wash off the excess dye. After that, move the slides into Masson's trichrome stain, specifically the Ponceau-acid fuchsine solution, for another 10 min. Subsequently, transfer the slides to 0.2 % glacial acetic acid aqueous solution and 1 % phosphomolybdic acid aqueous solution for differentiation, lasting 3 min each. Finally, after counterstaining, washing, gradient dehydration, clearing, and mounting, the tissue morphology was observed. All sections were imaged under a Zeiss Axio Observer A1 microscope using a 20x objective for subsequent morphological analysis.

Cell culture and treatment

Human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) and murine RAW264.7 macrophages were cultured in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle's Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 3.7 g/L NaHCO3, 10 % (v/v) fetal bovine serum (FBS), and 1 % (v/v) penicillin-streptomycin solution at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5 % CO2. Bβ15-42 was applied at a final concentration of 0.6 mg/mL, and EFEa was used at 2 μg/mL; both compounds were diluted in serum-free DMEM. To generate a HUVEC line stably overexpressing VE-cadherin (HUVEC-CDH5+), cells were transduced with the lentiviral vector CDH5-GV358 (GeneChem, Shanghai, China) encoding full-length human VE-cadherin for 48 h, followed by selection with puromycin. Control cells were transduced with the empty vector (GV358) and used in parallel throughout the study.

Western blot analysis

We use aortic lysate to extract total protein. Samples were washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and subsequently homogenized in ice-cold lysis buffer containing 150 mmol/L NaCl, 25 mmol/L Tris-HCl, 5 mmol/L EDTA, and 1 % Nonidet P-40 at pH 7.5, supplemented with protease inhibitor cocktail tablets (Roche Diagnostics, Penzberg, Germany). Protein concentrations were quantified using bicinchoninic acid (BCA) protein assay kits (Pierce, Rockford, IL, USA). Protein samples were resolved on 8 % sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gels and electrotransferred onto nitrocellulose membranes. Membranes were blocked for 1 h at room temperature in 5 % (w/v) non-fat dry milk powder dissolved in 0.1 % Tris-buffered saline/Tween 20 (TBST), followed by incubation with anti- VE-cadherin primary antibodies (SAB4301448, Sigma-Aldrich; 1:500), anti-fibrinogen (ab27913, Abcam; 1:500), anti-β-actin antibody (ab6276, Abcam; 1:10000) overnight at 4 °C. After washing with TBST, membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (SA00001-2, Proteintech, 1:5000) and HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse secondary antibody (SA00001-1, Proteintech, 1:5000) for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using ECL chemiluminescence reagents (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, USA). The band densities were quantified using ImageJ software by an observer blinded to the experimental conditions.

Immunohistochemical and immunofluorescence staining

For immunohistochemical staining, antigen-retrieved tissue sections were placed in a humidified chamber. Endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched by treating the sections with 3 % hydrogen peroxide for 15 min, followed by three 5-min washes in PBS. The sections were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with rabbit anti-fibrinogen (ab27913, Abcam; 1:500) and rabbit anti-CD68 (ab283654, Abcam; 1:500). After thorough washing with PBS, the sections were incubated for 1 h at room temperature with HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (SA00001-2, Proteintech; 1:5000). Following additional washes, chromogenic detection was carried out using a 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB) substrate for 3 min at room temperature. The reaction was terminated by rinsing with distilled water. Nuclei were counterstained with hematoxylin for 15 s, followed by blueing in running tap water for 10 min. Finally, all immunohistochemically stained sections were dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, cleared in xylene, and mounted with neutral balsam.

For immunofluorescence staining, samples were incubated overnight at 4 °C with rabbit anti–VE-cadherin (SAB4301448, Sigma-Aldrich; 1:500) and mouse anti-F-actin antibody (ab205, Abcam; 1:500). After washing with PBS, samples were incubated for 1 h at room temperature in the dark with the following fluorescent secondary antibodies: CoraLite® 594-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (SA00013-4, Proteintech; 1:500) and CoraLite® 488-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (SA00013-1, Proteintech; 1:500). Following a final series of PBS washes, nuclei were counterstained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI), and samples were mounted using anti-fade mounting medium. All images were acquired under consistent lighting conditions using Zeiss Axio Observer A1 microscope.

Cell migration assay

A transendothelial migration assay was performed using a Transwell system (6.5 mm diameter insert, 8 μm pore size). HUVEC-CDH5+ cells were digested, resuspended, and seeded into the upper chambers at a density of 5 × 104 cells/mL in 200 μL medium. The lower chambers were filled with 500 μL fresh medium. Concurrently, 200 μL of the same cell suspension was added to a 96-well plate to monitor confluence. After 24 h of culture (37 °C, 5 % CO2), when HUVECs reached full confluence, the lower chamber medium was replaced with serum-free DMEM containing different interventions for 24 h. Raw264.7 murine macrophages were then digested, resuspended in serum-free DMEM, and added to the upper chambers at a density of 3 × 105 cells/mL in 200 μL. The lower chambers received 50 μM fMLP as a chemoattractant. After 24 h of incubation, non-migrated cells on the upper membrane surface were removed with a cotton swab. The membrane was excised, fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde, and subjected to immunofluorescence staining. Migrated cells on the lower surface were imaged and counted under an upright microscope using three random fields per membrane. Experiments were repeated at least three times independently, and data are presented as the mean number of migrated cells per field.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from endothelial cells using Trizol reagent (Invitrogen) following the manufacturer's protocol. RNA concentration and purity were assessed with a Nanodrop 2000 spectrophotometer. cDNA was synthesized from 1000 ng of total RNA using Oligo (dT) primers and RevertAid Reverse Transcriptase. Quantitative real-time PCR was performed using SYBR Green master mix on a real-time PCR system. The following primer sequences were used: VE-cadherin Forward: 5′-CGCAATAGACAAGGACAT-3′, Reverse: 5′-GCCGTGTTATCGTGATTA-3’; 18S rRNA – Forward: 5′-GTAACCCGTTGAACCCCATT-3′, Reverse: 5′-CCATCCAATCGGTAGTAGCG-3’. The 18S rRNA gene served as an internal control. Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2–ΔΔCt method.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (Version 9.0). Data were presented as mean ± SEM. Statistical significance was assessed using Student's t-tests or one-way ANOVA followed by post-hoc Tukey's test when appropriate. Differences were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05.

Results

EFEa and Bβ15-42 decreased plaque formation in a rabbit model of atherosclerosis

Oil Red O and HE staining suggested that Bβ15-42, EFEa, and ROS reduced the area of atherosclerotic plaques and intimal thickening caused by atherosclerosis (P < 0.05, Fig. 1A–E). Aortic Masson staining, which marked collagen fibers (blue) and muscle fibers (red), showed that after atherosclerosis, there was hyperplasia of the arterial intima, but the fiber arrangement in the arterial media remained normal, with no significant hyperplasia, migration, or calcification of smooth muscle. After intervention with EFEa, Bβ15-42, and ROS, there was a slight reduction in arterial intimal hyperplasia, without pathological fiber arrangement disorder or calcification (P < 0.05, Fig. 1F).

EFEa and Bβ15-42 decreased fibrinogen deposition and inflammatory cell infiltration in a rabbit model of atherosclerosis

Fibrinogen staining revealed patterns consistent with aortic atherosclerotic lesions. Compared to the control group, EFEa and ROS treatments significantly reduced fibrinogen deposition in histological sections (P < 0.05, Fig. 2A). However, Bβ15-42 treatment did not result in a statistically significant reduction in fibrinogen deposition compared to the control group (P > 0.05, Fig. 2A). Fibrinogen protein expression in the aorta was significantly decreased in all drug intervention groups relative to the control group (P < 0.05, Fig. 2B). Immunohistochemical staining for CD68, a macrophage marker, revealed extensive inflammatory cell infiltration within the atherosclerotic lesions of the CTRL group. CD68-positive cells were predominantly localized in the subendothelial space and the core of the plaques, with a morphology indicative of activated macrophages (Fig. 2F). Quantitative analysis demonstrated a high density of CD68+ cells (85.3 ± 6.4 cells per field) in the CTRL group. Treatments significantly attenuated this infiltration. The number of CD68+ cells was markedly reduced in the Bβ15-42 group (78.2 ± 5.1, P < 0.05), EFEa group (40.7 ± 4.8, P < 0.05), and ROS group (35.6 ± 3.9, P < 0.05) (Fig. 2E). These results indicate that Bβ15-42, EFEa, and ROS effectively suppressed the macrophage-driven inflammatory response in the aortic wall. Based on these findings, we concluded that fibrinogen deposition and inflammatory cell infiltration were predominantly observed in regions of atherosclerotic tissue within the aorta. Treatment with EFEa and ROS effectively attenuated the progression of atherosclerosis and decreased the inflammatory response in aortic tissue caused by atherosclerosis.

Establishment of a HUVEC cell line with overexpressed VE-cadherin

Given the variability in cadherin expression levels across different cell types, investigating the mechanisms of action ex vivo presented substantial challenges. To address these challenges, we established a CDH5-positive human endothelial cell line (HUVEC-CDH5+) via lentiviral transduction, which consistently expresses high levels of VE-cadherin at both the protein and mRNA levels (Fig. 3A and B). Compared to conventional HUVECs, HUVEC-CDH5+ cells exhibit tighter junctions and are more prone to form a stable endothelial monolayer that closely mimics the vascular intima (Fig. 3C, D).

Fibrinogen decreases VE-cadherin expression in HUVEC-CDH5+ cells and promotes inflammatory cell migration In vitro

Serum-free medium supplemented with 6 mg/mL fibrinogen or fibrin was incubated with HUVEC-CDH5+ cells for 3, 6, 12, or 24 h. Western blot analysis revealed that fibrinogen treatment progressively decreased total VE-cadherin protein levels in a time-dependent manner, with statistical significance achieved at 24 h compared to the control group (Fig. 4A–C). Similarly, fibrin significantly reduced total VE-cadherin protein levels after 12 and 24 h of exposure relative to untreated controls (Fig. 4B–D). To further investigate concentration-dependent effects, HUVEC-CDH5+ cells were exposed to fibrinogen and fibrin at concentrations of 1, 3, and 6 mg/mL for 24 h. The results showed that fibrinogen significantly reduced VE-cadherin protein expression only at the highest concentration of 6 mg/mL, whereas fibrin demonstrated significant effects even at the lowest concentration of 1 mg/mL (Fig. 3F–H). Immunofluorescence staining revealed structural alterations in fibrin-treated cells, characterized by shortened and thickened F-actin filaments, and cell separation due to diminished VE-cadherin expression (Fig. 4I–K). Based on these findings, we demonstrate that fibrin, rather than fibrinogen, significantly promotes inflammatory cell migration across the endothelial monolayer into the lower chamber (Fig. 4L and M).

EFEa and Bβ15-42 exert protective effects on endothelial cells via the VE-cadherin pathway and inhibit macrophage migration

Immunofluorescence experiments revealed that Bβ15-42 (0.6 mg/mL) and EFEa (2 μg/mL) significantly alleviated cytoskeletal rearrangements and maintained VE-cadherin expression, thereby preserving the integrity of endothelial cell monolayers (Fig. 5A–C). Western blot analysis confirmed these findings, showing an increase in VE-cadherin protein levels following treatment with Bβ15-42 and EFEa (Fig. 5B–D). Additionally, cell migration assays demonstrated that Bβ15-42 and EFEa substantially inhibited fibrin-induced macrophage transmigration through endothelial cell layers, reducing it by 83.3 % and 56.1 %, respectively (Fig. 5E).

Discussion

Fibrinogen is widely recognized as a crucial factor in the progression of atherosclerosis [9]. Previous studies have shown that Bβ15-42 can inhibit leukocyte accumulation, preserve endothelial function, and reduce inflammatory infiltration [16,18]. EFEa, an enzyme that cleaves fibrinogen, is also suggested to have clinical potential similar to Bβ15-42, particularly for lowering fibrinogen levels and exhibiting anti-tumor, antiviral, and anti-inflammatory properties [26]. This study aimed to elucidate the anti-atherosclerotic mechanisms of EFEa and Bβ15-42.

The effects of EFEa appear to be mediated through molecular antagonism, akin to Bβ15-42, rather than by directly altering fibrinogen expression. Previous research has indicated that fibrinogen predominantly accumulates in early-stage atherosclerotic tissues, contributing to endothelial cell damage and lesion progression [10,11]. As a result, treatment with Bβ15-42 or EFEa reduces fibrinogen deposition and alleviates the overall atherosclerotic burden. We found that both Bβ15-42 and EFEa treatments led to a significant decrease in the area of aortic atherosclerotic lesions and the thickness of the intimal-medial layer in atherosclerotic aortas, demonstrating their potent anti-atherosclerotic effects.

Local inflammation and cell activation are key mechanisms underlying atherosclerosis. Macrophage accumulation is markedly elevated in atherosclerotic plaques and closely correlates with the severity of plaque formation, progression, and rupture [32]. We found that both EFEa and Bβ15-42 significantly reduced the number of macrophages in the aorta, suggesting that these compounds play a substantial role in mitigating the inflammatory response, as evidenced by a reduction in macrophage infiltration.

Despite these findings, the precise mechanisms of action for Bβ15-42 and EFEa remain largely undefined. Petzelbauer et al. initially demonstrated that the fibrin fragment N-terminal disulfide knot (NDSK)-II promotes the migration of human peripheral blood mononuclear cells across HUVEC monolayers in vitro [17]. Consequently, we hypothesize that the Bβ15-42/VE-cadherin pathway functions as a "molecular bridge," facilitating leukocyte transmigration across endothelial cell monolayers. To test this hypothesis, we employed Western blot and immunofluorescence to confirm VE-cadherin expression in HUVECs following successful lentiviral vector-mediated overexpression of VE-cadherin (HUVEC-CDH5+). The HUVEC-CDH5+ cells exhibited robust tight junctions, providing an ideal model to simulate the in vivo state of vascular endothelium.

To further explore the effects of fibrinogen and fibrin on VE-cadherin expression, we exposed HUVEC-CDH5+ cells to varying concentrations of fibrinogen and fibrin over different time periods. High-dose fibrinogen treatment (6 mg/mL for 24 h) significantly reduced VE-cadherin protein expression. This finding aligns with previous reports by Lominadze et al. [33], who showed that high concentrations of fibrinogen (4 mg/mL) decrease VE-cadherin expression in cerebral vascular endothelial cells, leading to dysfunction in the cerebral vasculature. In MMP9 knockout mice, ICAM-1 function-blocking antibodies were able to counteract the fibrinogen-induced reduction in VE-cadherin expression, suggesting that ICAM-1 plays a key role in the interaction between fibrinogen and endothelial cells. The reduction in VE-cadherin levels following fibrinogen injection in vivo has also been associated with MMP9 activity [8]. Our results suggest that fibrin exerts an even more pronounced effect on reducing VE-cadherin expression, with low-dose fibrin (1 mg/mL) treatment significantly decreasing VE-cadherin levels, while high-dose fibrin (6 mg/mL) treatment resulted in a greater reduction after 12 h compared to 24-h fibrinogen treatment [34]. Thus, we propose that fibrin deposition within blood vessels, or exposure of fibrinogen to the β15-42 sequence, leads to reduced VE-cadherin expression, compromising endothelial integrity and accelerating atherosclerosis.

We used immunofluorescence to investigate the distribution of VE-cadherin and F-actin in HUVEC-CDH5+ cells exposed to high concentrations of fibrinogen or fibrin. Treatment with high doses of fibrinogen and fibrin resulted in a decrease in VE-cadherin expression, which became aggregated, while the F-actin structure became disorganized, leading to reduced co-localization of F-actin with VE-cadherin at cell junctions. Higher concentrations of fibrinogen (4 mg/mL) increased the formation of endothelial cytoskeletal protein F-actin, thereby enhancing intercellular permeability. Our results demonstrate that fibrin has a more pronounced effect on VE-cadherin redistribution and F-actin morphological changes compared to fibrinogen.

The central E region of fibrin comprises α17-78, β15-122, and γ1-62. Its homologue, NDSK-II (α17-51, β15-118, γ1-78), facilitates leukocyte transmigration through the endothelium in vitro [17]. To elucidate the role of migration across endothelial monolayers, we stimulated HUVEC-CDH5+ cells with fibrin and observed a significant increase in macrophage transmigration across the endothelial barrier, while fibrinogen did not elicit this response. These findings suggest that the α17-and β15-terminal exposure of fibrin is essential for promoting inflammation and facilitating cell migration. Subsequently, we used Bβ15-42 and EFEa to interfere with fibrin-induced macrophage migration. Both compounds effectively inhibited fibrin-induced cell transmigration through endothelial monolayers and preserved VE-cadherin integrity. Several studies have shown that Bβ15-42 mitigates ischemia-reperfusion injury and protects vascular endothelial integrity. The primary mechanism likely involves Bβ15-42 mimicking the amino-terminal sequence of the fibrin β-chain, thereby competitively binding to VE-cadherin and preventing leukocyte transmigration. Additionally, Bβ15-42 induces Fyn release, inhibits RhoA activity, prevents cytoskeletal rearrangement, maintains intercellular VE-cadherin continuity, and reduces inflammatory cell infiltration [17,18]. EFEa exhibits trypsin-like activity and has multiple hydrolytic sites for plasminogen and fibrin, enabling it to hydrolyze fibrinogen [35]. Our findings suggest that the Bβ15-42/VE-cadherin "molecular bridge" connects the amino terminus of leukocyte integrins CD11c and CD18, and that Bβ15-42 exerts an antagonistic effect on fibrin-mediated pro-inflammatory cell migration.

In conclusion, our results demonstrate that fibrinogen plays a pivotal role in the pathogenesis and progression of atherosclerosis. Fibrinogen facilitates macrophage migration through endothelial cells by downregulating VE-cadherin expression and reorganizing F-actin filaments. EFEa and Bβ15-42 preserve endothelial function, reduce local fibrinogen deposition, and inhibit inflammatory cell infiltration in the aorta, thereby exerting anti-atherosclerotic effects. This process may be mediated through the regulation of the VE-cadherin pathway.

Despite our findings, this study has several limitations. First, the use of the Raw264.7 murine macrophage cell line, while standard, may not fully recapitulate the behavior of primary human macrophages or the complexity of the in vivo environment. Second, although we demonstrated the involvement of the VE-cadherin pathway, other signaling mechanisms might also contribute to the observed effects and warrant further investigation. Finally, our current data are derived from in vitro and ex vivo models; future studies utilizing animal disease models will be crucial to validate the therapeutic potential of Bβ15-42 and EFEa in a more physiologically relevant context.
