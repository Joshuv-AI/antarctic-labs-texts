Impact of clinical pharmacy services on patient outcomes in Sudan: a systematic review of interventional studies
Authors: Yousif B Hamadalneel, Safaa Badi, Mohammed Elmadani, Kannan O Ahmed, Ahmed A A Omer
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundClinical pharmacy services have demonstrated positive impacts on patient outcomes in several countries, but data from Sudan are still scarce. Therefore, this systematic review aimed to evaluate the impact of clinical pharmacy services on patient outcomes in Sudan.MethodA systematic literature search was performed in accordance with PRISMA guidelines. The PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, MEDLINE, and Embase databases, as well as Google Scholar, were searched from their commencement to December 31, 2024. Eligible studies included both randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and quasi-experimental designs. Data extraction and qualitative analysis were subsequently performed. The risk of bias for RCTs was assessed via the ROB-2 tool, while non-randomized studies were assessed via the ROBINS-I V2 tool.ResultsAmong the 150 retrieved studies, 14 met the inclusion criteria, with a total of 3,895 participants; (6 out of 14, 42.9%) were RCTs, and (8 out of 14, 57.1%) were quasi-experimental studies. Clinical pharmacy services had a positive impact on therapeutic (7 out of 8, 87.5%, p value < 0.05), humanistic (6 out of 7, 85.7%, p value < 0.05), safety (2 out of 14, 14.3%, p value < 0.05), and economic (1 out of 1, 100%, p value < 0.05) outcomes. The majority of studies were conducted in hospital settings (12 out of 14, 85.8%) and focused on chronic conditions such as heart failure, diabetes, and asthma. Services included medication optimization, patient education, medication reconciliation, and protocol implementation.ConclusionClinical pharmacy services in Sudan had a positive impact on humanistic, therapeutic, economic, and safety outcomes, although evidence for the economic and safety impacts remains limited. There is an urgent need for national policy implementation and wider integration of clinical pharmacists across healthcare settings to enhance care quality and efficiency. Further research, particularly in high-risk populations, safety, and economic evaluations, is warranted.Keywords: Clinical pharmacy, Clinical pharmacist, Intervention, Service, Outcomes, Sudan

Introduction

Clinical pharmacy practice incorporates the philosophy of pharmaceutical care, combining a care orientation with expert therapeutic knowledge, experience, and judgment to achieve the best possible patient outcomes [1]. Additionally, it is committed to generating new knowledge that enhances health and improves quality of life (QoL) [1].

Clinical pharmacists play pivotal roles in healthcare innovation, establishing pharmacist-managed clinics, and improving patient satisfaction, treatment outcomes, and access to care [2]. In addition, clinical pharmacists have a role in preventing drug-related morbidity by ensuring safe medication use, patient education, and collaborative care, ultimately improving healthcare quality [3]. Furthermore, clinical pharmacists play a role in appropriate prescribing and optimizing medication management and QoL [4].

Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and various studies have confirmed that clinical pharmacists enhance diabetes management through interprofessional collaboration [5–11] and play a vital role in intensive care units by lowering mortality, preventing medication errors, and reducing costs [12, 13]. In addition, clinical pharmacists have a substantial impact on anticoagulant management [14]. Multiple systematic reviews and meta-analyses have consistently demonstrated that clinical pharmacists significantly improve therapeutic, safety, humanistic, and economic outcomes through interventions such as medication therapy management and collaborative care, with evidence from Pakistan [15], China [16], Arab countries [17], and South Asia [18]. However, variations in cost assessments make their broader economic impact less certain [18], highlighting the importance of clinical pharmacists, particularly in resource-limited environments [19].

Developed countries have well-established clinical pharmacy systems that are characterized by advanced infrastructure, highly trained clinical pharmacists, and robust protocols ensuring comprehensive pharmaceutical care [20]. In contrast, those systems in developing countries such as Sudan reflect a distinct context where the integration of clinical pharmacy services is still in its early stages but holds significant potential to enhance patient care and optimize healthcare delivery. The clinical pharmacy field in Sudan is evolving but faces substantial challenges, which may arise from other health practitioners’ willingness, practice setups, and attitudes toward clinical pharmacists [21]. In addition, clinical pharmacists, together with pharmacy-regulating bodies in Sudan, need to set standards and job specifications for practices that suit patients and the healthcare system of the country [22]. However, despite these challenges, progress is being made as their role extends beyond traditional dispensing functions to encompass active participation in patient care teams, ensuring safe and effective medication use [23]. Although the impact of clinical pharmacy services is well established in developed countries, systematic evidence in developing countries, particularly in Sudan, is lacking. Hence, this systematic review is the first to bridge this gap by integrating existing data on the impact of clinical pharmacy services on patient outcomes in Sudan.

Methods

Study design

This was a systematic review conducted according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [24]. This review was registered in advance on PROSPERO (CRD420251006945).

Search strategy and keywords

A systematic literature search of published studies was performed using the following electronic bibliographic databases from their commencement to December 31, 2024: PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, MEDLINE, and Embase (Supplementary file). Additionally, Google Scholar was used as a citation index. The PICO information consisted of population (P): patients receiving clinical pharmacy services; intervention (I): clinical pharmacy intervention or services; comparison (C): usual or standard care or other health care provider interventions without pharmacist involvement; and outcome (O): any outcome of clinical pharmacy intervention or services. The search strategy used a combination of keywords and Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms related to the impact of clinical pharmacy services on patient outcomes in Sudan (Supplementary file). The specific keywords used were (“clinical pharmacist” OR “clinical pharmacist-Led” OR “pharmacist intervention” OR “pharmacist’s” OR “pharmacist” OR “hospital pharmacy-based” OR “medication therapy management” OR “counseling” OR “led-educational intervention” OR “medication reconciliation” OR “pharmaceutical care”) AND (“health outcomes” OR “clinical outcomes” OR “medication adherence” OR “medication titration” OR “hospital readmissions” OR “adverse drug events” OR “drug‒drug interaction” OR “mortality” OR “cost-effectiveness” OR “cost” OR “patient satisfaction” OR “treatment satisfaction” OR “complications” OR “disease management” OR “patient’s outcome” OR “quality of life”) AND (“Sudan” OR “sudanese patients” OR “sudanese” OR “healthcare in Sudan”) (Supplementary file).

Eligibility criteria

Studies documenting the impact of clinical pharmacy intervention or services on patient outcomes in Sudan were included if they met the following criteria: involved a pharmacist as the main investigator in providing the service or intervention where it was compared with another group, such as standard care or non-pharmacist intervention for all types of patient-related outcomes; the studies were carried out in hospital and/or primary health care settings; the design of the studies was quantitative, including RCTs; and the quasi-experimental study designs included studies published in English and conducted in Sudan.

The exclusion criteria included observational studies, case series, case reports, abstracts or conference proceedings, editorials, systematic reviews, meta-analyses or other types of reviews, or articles not available in full-text format, pharmacy-based interventions, and studies published in languages other than English. We chose these exclusion criteria to ensure that the included studies were relevant to our research question.

Screening and study selection process

All curated studies were imported into Mendeley software to identify and remove duplicate records. To ensure optimal study selection, two independent reviewers (YBH and AAAO) screened the titles and abstracts of all identified articles for eligibility. Any disagreements were resolved by a third reviewer (ME) if needed. The full-text articles were then reviewed by the same two independent reviewers (YBH and AAAO), and any disagreements were resolved by a third reviewer (ME) if needed. The included studies were cross-checked and compiled by (ME). Discrepancies were resolved through discussion and consensus. Covidence software was used for the screening process.

Data extraction

The author (YBH) independently extracted the data using a standardized template in Microsoft Office Excel 2019, and the author (AAAO) reviewed the extracted data for accuracy and consistency. The extracted data were summarized descriptively, and the outcomes were analyzed thematically. The data extracted included author name, year of publication, city/setting, study design, follow-up duration, patient characteristics, descriptions of the intervention and comparator, measurement metrics, number of participants, and main outcomes.

Operational definition

Clinical pharmacy services refer to “patient-centered services provided by pharmacists that are aimed at optimizing medication therapy and promoting health, wellness, and disease prevention” [1]. These services include activities such as applying scientific research, clinical guidelines, and advanced technologies to ensure safe and effective medication use; medication therapy management; therapeutic drug monitoring; patient counseling; and participation in multidisciplinary healthcare teams to ensure safe and effective use of medications [1].

Therapeutic outcomes, reflecting measurable health improvements such as symptom relief or disease control; humanistic outcomes, capturing quality of life, knowledge, attitude, medication adherence, and patient satisfaction; safety outcomes, focusing on the prevention of harm and adverse events; and economic outcomes, assessing the cost-effectiveness of interventions in terms of both costs and health benefits [15, 25].

Measures of effect

The measure of effect was improvement in patient outcomes. The extent of the difference between the clinical pharmacy services or intervention and standard care, or the comparator, was expressed according to the study’s aim. The outcomes were categorized as positive impact (a statistically significant difference in the desired outcome(s) supporting pharmacist-delivered care or intervention) and negative impact (a statistically significant difference supporting standard care or the comparator), whereas outcomes with no statistically significant differences were categorized as neutral [17].

Risk of bias assessment

Two reviewers (YBH and ME) independently assessed the risk of bias (RoB) in the results of the RCTs via the Cochrane risk of bias tool 2 (ROB 2) [26]. The risk of bias in nonrandomized studies of interventions, version 2 (ROBINS-I V2), was used to assess the risk of bias in the results of the quasi-experimental studies [27]. Any disagreements were resolved by a third reviewer if needed (AAAO).

Data synthesis

A qualitative synthesis of the selected studies was conducted rather than a meta-analysis because the included studies were quite diverse in terms of the nature of the intervention, measurement metrics, outcomes, and follow-up duration. Therefore, pooling the data for meta-analysis was considered inappropriate.

Results

Study selection

A total of 150 studies were identified, including 143 from electronic databases. After deduplication via Mendeley, 94 articles remained for title and abstract screening, with 86 excluded because they did not meet the inclusion criteria. Eight full-text articles were reviewed, one of which was excluded for not involving a pharmacist intervention. Seven additional articles were identified through Google Scholar citation tracking, all of which were included after full-text review. No further studies were found through bibliography screening. Ultimately, 14 studies met the inclusion criteria and were included in this systematic review. The search and screening process is detailed in the PRISMA flowchart (Fig. 1) [24].

Study participants and duration

The included studies involved 3,895 participants, with sample sizes ranging from 62 to 700; of these, 1,484 (38.1%) were RCTs. The studies were published between 2008 and 2024, with the majority (10 out of 14; 71.4%) published in the last five years [9–11, 28–34] (Table 1).

Study design and settings

Among the 14 included studies, 8 (57.1%) were quasi-experimental [11, 30–36], and 6 (42.9%) were RCTs [9, 10, 28, 29, 37, 38]. The studies were conducted across five Sudanese cities, with the majority in Khartoum city (6 studies, 42.9%) [29, 31–33, 35, 37], followed by Omdurman (3 studies, 21.4%) [9–11], Nyala and Wad Medani (2 studies each, 14.3%) [28, 30, 36, 38], and one in Elqutainah (7.1%) [34]. Most studies (12 out of 14, 85.8%) were conducted in hospitals [9–11, 28–30, 32–35, 37, 38], with one in a primary care center [36] and one in a specialized center [31] (Table 1).

Studies’ clinical conditions

The included studies covered various clinical conditions, with diabetes mellitus (DM) examined most frequently in six studies (42.9%) [9–11, 31, 36, 38]. Heart failure (HF) [32, 33], asthma [28, 37], and surgical prophylaxis [29, 34] were each addressed in two studies (14.3%), whereas medication reconciliation [30], blood pressure (BP) [36], and anticoagulant management [35] were each covered in one study (7.1%) (Table 1).

Risk of bias assessment

Among the included RCTs, three (21.4%) had a low ROB [9, 29, 38], one had an unclear ROB (7.1%) [10], and two (14.3%) had a high ROB [28, 37]. All studies showed low reporting bias, and two raised concerns about the randomization process [28, 37]. Regarding outcome measurement, two studies had some concerns [10, 37], and one had a high ROB due to unblinded assessors [28]. Regarding bias due to deviation from intended interventions, one study reported a high ROB due to over 20% loss to follow-up [37]. Outcome measurement metrics

Metrics were varied across studies, including HbA1c, fasting blood glucose, international normalized ratio (INR), BP, HF medication target dose achievement, reduced asthma attacks, symptoms, and β2 agonist use, and SSIs for evaluating therapeutic outcomes [29, 31, 33, 35–38]. Adherence was evaluated via validated tools such as the Medication Adherence Rating Scale (MARS) and Morisky 4 Medication Adherence Scale (MMAS-4) [9, 32], whereas humanistic outcomes included treatment satisfaction and QoL assessed through Likert scales and the Short Form-36 Health Survey (SF-36) [10, 36]. Safety outcomes focused on bleeding events, hospitalizations, and medication reconciliation [30, 35]. Economic outcomes were assessed through cost-saving analyses [34] (Table 1).

Clinical pharmacy services

Clinical pharmacy services varied across the studies and consist of one or more interventions, which can be grouped into; {1} Patient education, (10 of 14, 71.4%) [9–11, 28, 30, 32, 35–38], by counseling, written materials, and campaigns to improve adherence, self-care, disease understanding, medication use, proper administration techniques, and lifestyle modifications, {2} Protocol implementation, which was applied in (2 out of 14, 14.3%) studies, where clinical pharmacists developed and/or implemented antimicrobial prophylaxis for surgery and elective cesarean Sects [29, 34], {3} Medication optimization, which was applied in (2 out of 14, 14.3%), included modifying medication to reach target doses or therapeutic, such as HF drug titration to targeted dose [33] or altering warfarin dose according to INR level [35], {4} Medication reconciliation, which was applied in (1 out of 14, 7.1%) study [30], and {5} Drug clinical outcome evaluation, which was applied in (1 out of 14, 7.1%) study [31] (Table 1).

Therapeutic outcomes

Therapeutic outcomes were documented in (8 out of 14, 57.1%) of the studies [9, 29, 31, 33, 35–38]. Overall, almost all the studies reported positive impacts (7 of 8, 87.5%), and one reported a neutral impact [31].

In HF patients, Ahmed et al. reported that clinical pharmacist intervention significantly increased the proportion reaching target doses of key medications: angiotensin converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEIs) (0 before vs. 77.4% after; p value 0.001) and beta blocker (BBs) (8.6% before vs. 66.1% after; p value 0.001) [33].

In diabetic patients, three studies reported significant HbA1c reductions following clinical pharmacist interventions; Badi et al. (8.7 ± 2.2 before vs. 6.8 ± 0.8 after; p value 0.028) [9], Ahmad et al. (9.3 ± 1.7 before vs. 7.8 ± 1.9 after; p value 0.001) [38], and Ahmed et al. (10.5 ± 1.2 before vs. 7.6 ± 0.2 after) [36], while Suliman et al. observed a no significant reduction (10.8 ± 2.1 before vs. 10.1 ± 2.5 after; p value 0.199) [31].

In high BP patients, Ahmed et al. reported significant improvements after clinical pharmacist intervention: SBP decreased from 135.7 to 123.3 mmHg and DBP from 81.8 to 77.6 mmHg (p value 0.001) [36].

In anticoagulant therapy, Ahmed et al. reported a significant improvement in INR control after clinical pharmacist intervention (51.5% before vs. to 68.3% after; P value < 0.001) [35].

In asthma management, Abdelhamid et al. reported significant improvements after pharmacist intervention, including reductions in acute attacks (mean 1.91 vs. 1.0; p value 0.03) and inhaled β2-agonist use (mean 19.9 vs. 3.3; p value 0.01) [37].

In surgical prophylaxis, Elnour et al. reported a significant reduction in surgical site infections (SSIs) after clinical pharmacist intervention (intervention: 42.5% to 25.7% vs. control: 57.5% to 44.2%; p value 0.001) [29].

Humanistic outcomes

Humanistic outcomes were reported in (7 out of 14, 50%) of the studies, with six showing positive effects (85.7%). One study reported mixed findings: positive for treatment satisfaction and neutral for QoL [10].

Medication adherence was the most frequently assessed outcome (3 out of 7, 42.8%) [9, 32, 34]. Ahmed et al. reported a significant improvement in heart failure medication adherence following clinical pharmacist interventions (mean MMAS-4 score 2.64 ± 1.1 before vs. 0.67 ± 0.9 after; p value 0.001) [32]. Similarly, Badi et al. found that adherence in diabetic patients increased significantly after pharmacist intervention (6.8 ± 1.7 before vs. 7.4 ± 1.5 after; p value < 0.001) [9], and Suliman et al. demonstrated a significant improvement in adherence to prophylactic antibiotic guidelines [34].

QoL was evaluated in (2 out of 7, 28.6%) of studies [10, 36]. Badi et al. reported no statistical significant (28.3 (± 3.2) before vs. 33.7 (± 5.7) after; p value 0.157) [10], while Ahmed et al., using the SF-36 reported a significant improvement in domains such as physical functioning [36].

Knowledge and attitudes, treatment satisfaction, and medication administration were each evaluated in one study (14.3%) [10, 11, 28]. Badi et al. reported significant improvement in knowledge (14.8% before vs. 28.5% after; p values < 0.001) and attitude (The mean score was increased by 1.7 (± 0.2) from baseline; p value < 0.001) [11], as well as treatment satisfaction (26.1 (± 5.8) before vs. 27.1(± 4.5) after; p value < 0.001) [10]. Khaled et al. showed significant improvement in inhaler techniques score in both dry powder inhaler techniques (6.83 ± 0.44 before vs. 3.74 ± 2.09 after; p value < 0001) and pressurized metered-dose inhalers (3.51 ± 1.29 before vs. 6.78 ± 0.515 after; p value < 0001) [28].

Safety outcomes

This outcome was evaluated in (2 out of 14, 14.3%) studies, both of which demonstrated positive outcomes [30, 35]. In anticoagulant management, Ahmed et al. reported significant reductions in bleeding events (39.2% before vs. 27.4% after; p value < 0.05) and warfarin-related hospitalizations (10.4% before vs. 3.7% after; p value < 0.001) after pharmacist intervention [35]. In medication reconciliation, Elamin et al. found pharmacist-led reconciliation was effective in detecting and resolving discrepancies (OR = 4.558; 95% CI; p value 0.001) [30].

Economic outcomes

The economic outcome was evaluated in (1 out of 14, 7.1%), which reported a 31% cost-saving (p value < 0.01) after clinical pharmacist intervention [34].

Discussions

This is the first systematic review that comprehensively compiles evidence on the impact of clinical pharmacy services on patient outcomes in Sudan. The findings revealed that these services have a positive impact on therapeutic, humanistic, safety, and economic outcomes, echoing global evidence from countries such as Pakistan across the Arab region and China [15, 17, 39]. Similar benefits have also been reported among high-risk groups, including cancer, HIV/AIDS, and pediatric patients [40–42]. The alignment of evidence from various healthcare systems and patient demographics enhances the general applicability of the findings noted in the Sudanese setting. By utilizing the proven benefits of clinical pharmacist, the healthcare decision-makers in Sudan can improve healthcare quality, minimize avoidable medication related problem, and enhance general health metrics, especially given the constraints of limited resources and systemic difficulties in low income countries such as Sudan.

Therapeutic outcomes

Aligned with global research, clinical pharmacy services in Sudan had a positive impact on 87.5% of the included studies and were especially evident in the management of chronic conditions such as DM [9, 36, 38], HF [33], and BP [36]. For example, three studies focusing on HbA1c reported significant reductions post-intervention [9, 36, 38], and one study reporting neutral outcome [31], reinforcing findings from comparable global systematic review studies that highlight how pharmacist participation enhances glycemic management [15, 17, 43, 44], while the neutral impact reported in one study could be attributed to the low sample size in this study which was 31 diabetic patients [31]. In HF management, pharmacist-led interventions significantly increased the proportion of patients reaching target medication doses [33]. This result was consistent with systematic review conducted in arab countries where pharmacists are crucial in enhancing medication plans and adjustments [17]. In patients with high BP, a one study showed a significant improvement in BP control after clinical pharmacist interventions [36], reinforcing findings from comparable global systematic review studies that highlight how clinical pharmacist interventions enhances BP control [15, 17, 43, 44]. Additionally, one asthma-focused study reported a significant reduction in attack frequency and β2 agonist use [37]. This finding supported by global umbrella review revealed that pharmacist intervention in management of asthma patients improves the asthma control, quality of life, lung capacity, inhaler technique and therapeutic adherence [45]. The findings of therapeutic outcomes in this review align with global evidence showing that clinical pharmacy services play a critical role in optimizing the management of chronic diseases. The consistency with international systematic reviews reinforces the value of pharmacist involvement in improving therapeutic outcomes in sudan and similar resource limiting setting.

Humanistic outcomes

This was addressed in 50% of the included studies [9–11, 28, 32, 34, 36], with most showing improvements in medication adherence, treatment satisfaction, and knowledge. Adherence was the most frequently studied outcome, with all related studies reporting statistically significant improvement following pharmacist interventions [9, 32, 34]. One study reported significant improvements in knowledge and attitudes following clinical pharmacist intervention [11]. Another study found improved treatment satisfaction and QoL, with no statistical significant [10]. A third study using the SF-36 reported significant improvements in domains such as physical functioning [36]. These findings supports by different global and regional systematic and umbrella review studies [15, 17, 40, 41, 46–48], while the neutral impact in QoL could be attributed to the challenges in assessing QoL, which can be affected by various clinical and non-clinical elements. These findings highlight the necessity of more incorporating clinical pharmacists into patient education, follow-up and counseling. Enhancing these humanistic aspects of care can improve outcomes of long term treatment, decrease the health system pressures from non-adherence, and improve the overall quality of healthcare services in Sudan.

Safety outcomes

This was evaluated in two studies. In anticoagulant management, pharmacist interventions were associated with significant reductions in bleeding events and warfarin-related hospitalizations [35], reinforcing findings from comparable global scoping review study that highlight a significant effect of pharmacist interventions on the adherence, readmission, and bleeding rates of anticoagulant medications [14]. Which emphasizes the essential role of clinical pharmacists in enhancing adherence, minimizing the risk of bleeding, and decreasing readmission rates linked to anticoagulant. In medication reconciliation, successful resolution of medication inconsistencies were resolved by clinical pharmacist interventions [30]. This finding supported by global systematic review highlights the important role of clinical pharmacist in the process of medication review [49]. Although the current evidence is derived from a limited number of studies, its consistency with international findings emphasizes the value of integrating clinical pharmacists into patient safety initiatives within Sudan’s healthcare system. Implementation efforts should prioritize enhancing pharmacist involvement in the management of high-risk medications and in medication reconciliation, particularly during patient admission and discharge.

Economic outcomes

One study reported a 31% cost savings following the implementation of pharmacist-led antibiotic prophylaxis guidelines for elective cesarean Sect [34]. This finding is supported by systematic reviews from China, globally, and in Arab countries, which reported cost savings through reduced hospital stays, readmissions, medication costs, and INR testing [16, 17, 50, 51]. However, one global systematic review found no significant economic impact [43], highlighting possible variation across healthcare system and challenges of clinical pharmacy services implementation. Despite limited local data, evidence suggests that incorporating clinical pharmacists in Sudan could provide economic benefits. Future studies should include detailed economic evaluations, and policymakers should consider their integration into cost-reduction strategies, particularly for chronic diseases, surgical care, and older adults.

Policy, practice implications and future research directions

This review highlights strong evidence supporting greater integration of clinical pharmacists into Sudan’s healthcare system, particularly in managing chronic conditions such as asthma, hypertension, HF, and DM. Expanding the role of pharmacists into primary care, developing national guidelines for pharmacist-led interventions, and investing in structured training programs are essential steps in enhancing healthcare delivery. Integrating clinical pharmacists into national health strategies and prioritizing their involvement in patient safety initiatives, such as high-risk medication management and medication reconciliation, can improve care quality and reduce healthcare costs. Clinical pharmacy services should also be leveraged in national cost-reduction plans, especially in chronic disease management, antibiotic stewardship, and elderly care. Notably, most Sudanese physicians report comfort and positive experiences working with clinical pharmacists, underscoring the potential for effective collaboration [23]. Future research should focus on priority populations, including oncology, nephrology, pediatric, and geriatric populations; adopt standardized outcome measures; and incorporate cost-effectiveness analyses, as well as safety outcome. Additionally, multicenter randomized trials and qualitative studies explore barriers and support wider implementation of clinical pharmacy services in Sudan.

Study strengths and limitations

In terms of strength, this systematic review addressed all published studies conducted in Sudan, incorporating both database searches and manual searches using Google Scholar as a citation index. The majority of studies reported low to moderate ROB. The review incorporated hospital and primary care health settings and diverse outcomes.

This systematic review has some limitations. The included studies were highly heterogeneous in terms of interventions, outcome measures, and follow-up durations, making a meta-analysis impractical. As a result, no formal sensitivity analysis was conducted; instead, the potential influence of high-risk studies was considered narratively during interpretation. Importantly, (2 out of 14, 14.3%) included studies were assessed as having a high risk of bias, which may reduce the certainty of our conclusions and highlights the need for future systematic reviews with more homogeneous data that permit quantitative sensitivity analyses to more rigorously assess robustness. Most of the studies were localized in the center of Sudan, with minimal representation from rural and peripheral regions. The economic and safety outcomes were marginally addressed, emphasizing a notable deficiency in pharmacoeconomic and safety assessments. The impacts of clinical pharmacy services on very specific and high-risk patient groups, such as nephrology, oncology, or pediatric patients, were not covered. The review excluded unpublished studies and gray literature. Therefore, the results of this systematic review must be interpreted with care.

Conclusion

This systematic review demonstrated that clinical pharmacy services had a positive impact on patient outcomes, especially in the management of chronic conditions. To maximize these benefits, urgent national policies are needed to formally integrate clinical pharmacists across healthcare settings, including primary care and rural areas. Expanding services, investing in workforce development, and establishing standardized guidelines are essential to enhance care quality, safety, and cost-effectiveness. Further research, particularly on high-risk populations, economic, and safety impacts, is crucial to guide sustainable policy and practice reforms.
