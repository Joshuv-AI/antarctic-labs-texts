Ki67 in cytological specimens of pancreatic neuroendocrine tumors: A literature review and validation of automated quantification
Authors: Sahar Narimani, Sophie Pirenne, Birgit Weynand
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe Ki67 proliferation index is mandatory for grading, prognostication, and clinical decision-making in pancreatic neuroendocrine tumors (PanNETs). Automatic Ki67 quantification on cytology has been shown to be at least as accurate, less time-consuming, and more consistent than the current gold-standard manual determination. After a thorough literature review, we aimed to validate the Visiopharm image analysis software for automatic Ki67 quantification on diagnostic cell block material from PanNETs.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective study and assembled a cohort of 69 PanNETs from clinical routine with available endoscopic ultrasound fine needle aspiration cell block, Ki67, and synaptophysin immunostained slides. The manual Ki67 index, if available, was obtained from the original pathology report. Otherwise, a manual count was performed by a pathologist using a cell counter. The automatic Ki67 index was quantified through four consecutive algorithms from the Visiopharm Image Analysis software on aligned serial sections.ResultsAutomatic Ki67 quantification showed a strong correlation with manual counting based on the non-parametric Spearman correlation coefficients of r = 0.786 [95% confidence interval (CI): 0.650–0.873, p < 0.001] and r = 0.721 (95% CI: 0.558–0.830, p < 0.001]), for absolute Ki67 values and grades, respectively. Grade concordance showed excellent agreement for Grade 1 and Grade 3 tumors (91.89% and 83.3%) and rather moderate agreement for Grade 2 lesions (59.09%) due to underestimation. Bland–Altman analysis obtained excellent results, with a mean underestimation of digital versus manual quantification of 0.2265%.ConclusionOur findings show accurate assessment of the proliferation index from PanNETs using the Visiopharm software for digital Ki67 quantification and provide a prevalidation framework for the implementation of this technique in pathology practice. Discrepancies were mainly seen in Grade 2 tumors due to tumor heterogeneity of Grade 2 lesions. To this end, future research should seek refinement of the digital algorithms and examine the reliability of prognosis and clinical endpoints based on this technique.Keywords: Pancreatic neuroendocrine tumor, Cytological grade, Ki67 proliferation index, Digital image analysis, Digital pathology, Cell block

Introduction

Representing merely 1–2% of all pancreatic neoplasms,1,2 pancreatic neuroendocrine tumors (PanNETs) are relatively uncommon. Yet, they are the second most frequent neoplastic lesion in the pancreas,1 and their occurrence has risen considerably in the past few decades.2,3 PanNETs usually occur in patients between 40 and 60 years old.1 Classification of these tumors as functional or non-functional is based on the presence or absence of hormone (i.e., insulin, gastrin) hypersecretion.1,2 Compared to pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC), PanNETs usually display a favorable prognosis reflecting an indolent behavior.2 Nevertheless, PanNETs can be highly lethal.1 As they fall under the classification of malignant epithelial neoplasms, their 10-year survival following surgical resection is limited to 40–50%.4 Parameters such as Ki67 index, tumor grade, tumor size, perineural invasion, and presence of lymph node metastasis have been linked to an increased risk of tumor recurrence, which in turn reduces the survival.5 These factors are essential for prognostication and treatment decisions,6 which makes an accurate preoperative grading of PanNETs particularly important.3 As a consequence, the most recent World Health Organization (WHO) classification for pancreatic neuroendocrine neoplasms (PanNENs) from 2017, adopted a proliferation rate-based grading (Table 1).1,3,7 This proliferation rate is established either by the “mitotic activity index” (MAI) or by the “Ki67 index”. The latter involves measuring the proportion of Ki67-positive tumor cells in a tumor hotspot, enclosing at least 500–2000 tumor cells.8 When considering prognostication and overall survival, we and others have shown that the Ki67 index is more accurate than MAI, even on diagnostic material (i.e., endoscopic ultrasound fine needle aspiration (EUS-FNA)).1,9

Currently, a manual count in a tumor hotspot is considered the gold-standard technique.10,11 Despite their accuracy, manual methods for this purpose are challenging because of their time-consuming nature,1,11 as well as their high susceptibility to inter-reader variability, which leads to low reproducibility among pathologists.5 As a result, in routine practice, pathologists more often rely on an eyeball estimate of the Ki67 as a more efficient and time-saving approach.11 Unfortunately, research has proven that eyeball estimation is inaccurate and unreliable for grading PanNETs.11

To overcome the above-mentioned issues, several authors suggested automated counting methods using digital image analysis (DIA), as they are faster and more accurate.6,11 Following previous studies, the use of digital pathology linked to machine learning for the evaluation of Ki67 in NETs is highly accurate and reproducible.10 Our group also showed that automated digital count based on aligned synaptophysin and Ki67 slides (rather than double stained slides) with hotspot detection performs as well as the manual count.1 In conclusion, automated Ki67 quantification generates accurate and more reproductible results in a reduced time interval contributing to the standardization of the proliferation index (PI) quantification, and to a better comparison between studies attempting to optimize WHO classification.1,9,10

The subject of automatic Ki67 determination has been studied in other tumors, for instance, in breast carcinoma,12, 13, 14 pulmonary neuroendocrine tumors,15 and ovarian carcinoid tumors10,16 and has already been implemented in routine clinical practice.12, 13, 14,17 For PanNETs, the current guidelines still recommend manual assessment of Ki67,10 due to the absence of large-scale comparative studies, especially on cytological material.

Therefore, we review in this study the latest literature on automated Ki67 quantification in PanNETs, and validate the use of Visiopharm (Oncotopix Discovery, Visiopharm, Hoersholm, Denmark) for the Ki67 assessment on cytological material of PanNETs (3. Validation of Visiopharm).

Literature review

Artificial intelligence (AI) is gaining increasing popularity and holds significant potential to enhance healthcare. Its versatility, reliability, and reproducibility suggest that it could eventually be integrated in routine medical practice. Recognizing this, the WHO published “Recommendations on digital interventions for health system strengthening” in 2019.10,18 In particular, the field of pathology has seen growing adoption of AI-based techniques over the past decade due to the introduction of digital pathology.8,10 Among others, automated image analysis for automatic Ki67 quantification in PanNETs is winning territory.8 Here, we offer an overview of the latest evolutions and today's challenges on which we present various points of view and solutions by critically reviewing the latest literature on this topic.

Study selection

We included all comparative literature on manual versus digital Ki67 assessment in PanNETs published between 2018 and 2024. Literature before 2018 has already been reviewed and summarized in our previous work.1 Studies were selected based on reporting data that compared digital analysis (AI-based or automatic quantification) of Ki67 and manual count. We focused on studies that examined PanNETs. Studies involving PanNECs or mixed neoplasms were excluded unless PanNETs were included.

Consequently, the exclusion criteria were: the absence of PanNETs in the analysis, literature published before 2018, and non-English-language studies. Due to the limited literature available, no criterion was set on the number of included cases of PanNETs nor on the type of material: studies on cytology (cell blocks), resection specimens, and biopsies were all considered.

We searched PubMed using the following keywords, each time supplemented by “[Title/Abstract]”, and mutually combined in multiple ways: “digital”, “digital pathology”, “digital quantification”, “image analysis”, “automated”, “semiautomated”, “semi-automated”, “artificial intelligence”, “AI”, “Ki67”, “Ki-67”, “proliferation”, “proliferation index”, “neuroendocrine”, “pancreas”, “gastro-enteropancreatic”, “neuroendocrine tumor”, “neuroendocrine tumour’.

The following MesH terms were used: “neuro-endocrine tumors” [MeSH] AND “pancreas” [MeSH]; “neuro-endocrine tumors” [MeSH] AND “pancreas” [MeSH] AND “Ki-67 Antigen” [MeSH]; “Neuroendocrine Tumors” [Mesh]) AND “Pancreas” [Mesh] AND “Artificial Intelligence” [Mesh]; “Pancreatic Neoplasms” [Mesh] AND “Artificial Intelligence” [Mesh]; “Pancreatic Neoplasms” [Mesh] AND “Ki-67 Antigen”; “Gastro-enteropancreatic neuroendocrine tumor” [Supplementary Concept]) AND “Ki-67 Antigen” [Mesh].

Identified articles were screened in full text according to the defined criteria.

Key findings and gaps in current literature

When looking at the overall accuracy of digital Ki67 quantification, recent literature shows promising results (Table 2). However, we consistently find similar shortcomings in the available recent literature, namely the cost of digital analysis, the underestimation of digital analysis due to tumor heterogeneity, the overestimation of digital analysis due to background contamination, challenges regarding the use of continuous variables, the lack of studies in cytology, and deficiencies in statistical analysis. These limitations reflect the practical challenges for implementing digital Ki67 in routine pathology. In what follows, each of these challenges and potential solutions is discussed.

Cost

The inaccessibility of the digital counting methods caused by high cost and maintenance requirements has been repeatedly mentioned as one of the primary issues, as it requires an expensive high-capacity whole-slide scanner.1,19, 20, 21 This specific financial issue is even more appropriate in the context of countries with low socioeconomic status. Dere et al.21 investigated the accuracy of a simple software, which loads captured images from the microscope directly to the computer and calculates the Ki67 index, circumventing the need for a scanner. Nonetheless, due to overlapping of the cells and tumor heterogeneity, the agreement reached by this technique is insufficient to consider clinical applications. In another attempt to reduce the costs of the digital assessment of proliferation, Satturwar et al.6 used augmented reality microscopy (ARM). When linked to DIA software, ARM quantifies Ki67 directly from cytology slides, avoiding the necessity to photograph and digitalize the slides first.10 An additional way to lower the expenses of digital imaging methods is open-source software without extra costs. For example, “ImageJ” software has proven to be much faster, as it does not require a slide scanner, yet it is equally accurate as manual counting methods.22 Finally, Zehra et al.23 investigated the open-source software and AI deep learning model “DeepLIIF” and managed to demonstrate good reliability between manual counting and the digital analysis.

Although these options are interesting, decreasing costs and wider implementation of scanners make digital pathology increasingly accessible.1,8,10 The combination of technological improvements during the past decade and the fall in implementation costs ensured that the availability of whole-slide scanners and digital cameras became increasingly standard in pathology labs, making the adaptation of image analysis and AI more feasible and amenable to employ.1,8,10,24

Underestimation of the Ki67 index and tumor grade due to EUS-FNA material and tumor heterogeneity

Several authors have found that grading of PanNETs based on EUS-FNA material tends to underestimate tumor grade. This issue is of significance as preoperative grading based on cytology has a diagnostic and interventional role: PanNETs show a spectrum of aggressiveness, and their biological behavior is highly unpredictable.22 It is particularly important in small lesions (≤2 cm), where treatment decisions rely heavily on Ki67-based grading.5 In our previous studies, we confirmed this cytological tendency to underestimate tumor grade compared to matched surgical resection specimens, mainly in Grade 2 cases.1,25, 26, 27 Several authors corroborated these findings.3,4,10,28 In most of these studies, Ki67 is quantified using manual counting method. For example, Abi-Raad and colleagues reported undergrading of all Grade 2 tumors on cytological material compared to surgical specimens, when using manual counting methods on cell blocks with less than 1000 cells. In this respect, the study accomplished by Hwang et al.4 is of particular interest, as they determined the Ki67 index using DIA, yet found similar results.

This cytological underestimation is largely due to intratumoral heterogeneity in Ki67 expression.4,25,26 This was investigated more extensively by Zhang et al.,29 who studied the different parameters to map intratumoral heterogeneity and spatial distribution of Ki67 within gastro-enteropancreatic neuroendocrine neoplasms (GEP-NENs). Their results show that high-grade lesions (G3 neuroendocrine tumors (NETs) and neuroendocrine carcinomas (NECs)) have significantly lower intratumoral heterogeneity, compared with low-grade tumors (G1, G2), which show more variability and less spatial uniformity of Ki67.

Efforts to target proliferation hotspots by EUS-FNA can reduce this underestimation. This theory correlates with our findings that grade underestimation is reduced, and cytological grading demonstrates good accuracy, when the tumor size is small (≤2 cm), and a hotspot with at least 2000 tumor cells is counted.25, 26, 27 Such findings are in accordance with other sources, for instance, Fujimori et al.28 and Hasegawa et al.,30 who confirmed a higher agreement in tumor grading in cases with a higher count of tumor cells. A concordance rate of even 90% was reported by Hasegawa et al.,30 when using the hotspot technique and excluding samples with fewer than 2000 tumor cells.3,4,27,30 Furthermore, Abi-Raad et al.3 discovered that counting hotspots rather than the whole cell block provides a stronger correlation. On the contrary, Hwang et al.4 failed to demonstrate such a tendency. However, this could be explained by the different Ki67 counting methods, whereby background contamination and sources of bias including double immunostaining were not considered when digital Ki67 quantification was applied.3 A different perspective is provided by Satturwar et al.6 investigating the use of ARM coupled with an image analysis software for digital Ki67 quantification on cytology material.10 ARM and other DIA methods have shown to have a potential for improving grading on cell blocks. In conclusion, accurate grading from EUS-FNA cell blocks is achievable with hotspot-based sampling and sufficient tumor cell count.

Overestimation of the Ki67 index and tumor grade due to background contamination

The most frequent pitfall encountered with digital analysis is the overestimation of the PI due to background contamination.1,6,9,10,22,31,32 This is caused by proliferating non-tumoral cells (e.g., lymphocytes, endothelial cells) and pigmented contaminants like hemosiderin.1,10 Especially Grade 1 lesions near cut-off values are vulnerable to such misclassification. In such cases, miscounting erroneously increases the overall tumor grade, resulting in misdiagnosis and, eventually, mistreatment.1,9,10,33 Several solutions have emerged from literature, whether or not complimentary.

Because Ki67 is not a lineage-specific marker,31,33 algorithms must distinguish tumor cells from stromal or inflammatory cells.10 Studies using such exclusion algorithms show good correlation with manual counts.1,9,22 Conversely, poor concordance often results from lacking such exclusion.9,33 Algorithms based on double synaptophysin-Ki67 staining have exhibited strong potential for routine application,1,10,27 using either double-stained slides or aligned serial sections. In our prior study, double-stained slides led to inconsistencies in nearly half of cases, partly due to overestimation.1 Aligned slides, where Ki67 and synaptophysin stains are digitally matched across serial sections, yielded near-perfect correlation.1

The difference in results in our previous study can be attributed to the software's failure to detect many Ki67-negative nuclei with double staining and the loss of synaptophysin staining in high-grade lesions. Hacking et al.16,33 also reported promising results using this innovative technique.

Adding hotspot detection further improves accuracy by focusing on areas with tumor cells only. In our experience, combining DIA algorithms with hotspot detection on aligned slides yielded excellent results.1 Moreover, digital hotspot detection is more precise than manual methods, as it allows repeated measurements or comparison of multiple regions, potentially increasing accuracy. Tools such as gradient map visualization, as explored by Saetiew et al.,34 can enhance hotspot identification. This may even result in superior accuracy compared to manual counting.1,8 This theory explains the minor discrepancies observed by Lea et al.9 when both a DIA algorithm and hotspot detection were applied, possibly due to the superior sensitivity of the automated method.

As the above-mentioned illustrates, background correction is essential for validation of DIA methods. For instance, Hacking et al.33 applied morphological separation of tumor and non-tumor cells but without an AI algorithm using aligned slides, resulting in suboptimal correlation (36%) despite the adequate number of casus (n = 50) and hotspot detection. Expert-level visual distinction can be prone to interpretative variability, fatigue, and printer resolution effects, whereas DIA corrects for this inter- and intra-reader variability and provides consistency and standardization.20

Hotspot size also plays a role. Larger hotspots dilute local variability. Volynskaya et al.32 described this “concentration-dilution effect”, where increasing hotspot size reduces the Ki67 index. Owens et al.8 found optimal correlation when DIA used 2000 tumor cells in a hotspot versus 1000 for manual count. The improved correlation with the digital 2000-cell hotspot likely stems from a dilution effect that both compensates for intratumoral heterogeneity and mitigates the inherent overestimation of the Ki67 index by DIA caused by background contamination.32 Similarly, in our study, using 2000-cell hotspots obtained very satisfactory results.1 Several authors used smaller hotspot sizes (e.g., 500–1000 cells), in accordance with the most recent 2019 WHO recommendations regarding grading of NETs,7,35 yet still reported decent accuracy.9,20,22,33,36 However, we and others have shown that 2000-cell hotspots are essential for accurate digital Ki67 quantification in PanNETs, correcting tumor heterogeneity and background contamination.1,8 Finally, it should be noted that in several studies, the analyzed hotspots were manually selected by a pathologist.8,19,28 First, this goes against the primary goal of DIA: efficiency. Manual hotspot identification is an intensive, time-consuming, and therefore highly inefficient task, abolishing the time savings achieved through automatic Ki67 quantification. Furthermore, as mentioned above, automatic hotspot detection is at least equivalent to manual assessment, which leaves room for the superiority of digital techniques. However, as nowadays image analysis tools become increasingly sophisticated, these automated hotspot detection algorithms applied in our previous study become standard practice techniques.1,9

An alternative approach is deep learning. Niazi et al.31 studied this method for background exclusion. While promising results, their cohort is small, and further large-scale studies are needed.

In conclusion, background contamination is the principal issue encountered with DIA for Ki67. Digital algorithms using aligned slides and a 2000 cells hotspot detection are promising solutions.1,8, 9, 10,22,32,33

Challenges of classifying a continuous variable

Grading of PanNETs based on Ki67 presents a conceptual limitation, as it reduces a continuous biological parameter to fixed categories (Grades 1, 2, 3).1,10,20 The WHO 2017 classification defined these cut-offs based on clinical–pathological correlations, including tumor behavior, patient survival, aggressiveness, and risk of progression. Whereas practical for diagnosis and prognosis, this simplification does not reflect the full prognostic spectrum, because increasing Ki67 values correlate linearly with tumor progression and mortality risk20: not solely the Ki67 index itself is continuous but its clinical correlate and prognostic significance in PanNETS are continuous as well. Furthermore, Klöppel et al.37 argue that this applies to all GEP-NENs.1

Another issue with fixed tumor categories stands that they vary over time. For instance, several authors advocate for a 5% Grade 2 cut-off instead of 3%.1,28,38

We therefore recommend reporting the exact Ki67 percentage on cytological material, rather than assigning a grade. This preserves nuance for prognostic and therapeutic purposes. In research contexts, comparing continuous values rather than categories is more informative.1

Lack of studies on cytological material

Even though cytology plays a crucial role in diagnosis and management of PanNETs, very few studies focus on Ki67 quantification in cytology specimens. As shown in Table 2, only two larger studies on cell block material have been published in recent years. The first by Satturwar et al.,6 investigated 39 PanNETs using DIA, and surprisingly found eyeball estimation outperformed digital and manual methods. These findings contradict previous research and are likely due to lack of correction for background contamination and limited resection specimen comparison.6 The second, by Shaker et al.,39 examined 66 PanNENs and found a strong agreement (92.4%) between manual and digital count. However, they used a suboptimal hotspot size of 100 tumor cells which may have led to overgrading of G1 tumors due to background interference. Overall, these studies underscore both the importance and the challenges of applying DIA to cytological material, highlighting the need for further validation.

Shortcomings in statistical analysis

Whereas most studies report correlation coefficients to compare digital and manual Ki67 assessment, this alone is insufficient to evaluate agreement. Additional statistical methods such as Bland–Altman analysis or Passing–Bablok regression provide more insight into systematic bias and proportional differences. These complementary analyses help determine whether DIA results are interchangeable with manual counting and whether discrepancies are clinically acceptable. Including such methods would strengthen the validation of DIA and increase confidence in its implementation.

In conclusion, with this literature study, we were able to identify several shortcomings we want to address in the current validation study.

Validation of Visiopharm

Materials and methods

Case collection

This is a retrospective study analyzing 69 clinical routine cases with a confirmed diagnosis of PanNETs between 2010 and 2022, which were retrieved from the Pathology Department archives of UZ Leuven (n = 61) and Institut Jules Bordet (n = 8). Inclusion criteria for this study were cytological EUS-FNA obtained specimens from PanNET, for which satisfactory cell block was available as well as the original Ki67 and synaptophysin immunostained slides. All cases were classified into Grade 1, 2, or 3 based on their manual Ki67 PI, according to the 2017 WHO classification.7

Immunocytochemistry

As mentioned earlier, staining of Ki67 and synaptophysin was already performed during the initial diagnosis and the available sections were retrieved from the pathology archives of UZ Leuven and Institut Jules Bordet. Immunostained slides originated from formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded cell blocks. Antibodies used for Ki67 and synaptophysin staining were, respectively, the MIB-1 clone (clone MIB-1, RTU, Dako, Glostrup, Denmark) and the DAK-SYNAP clone (clone DAK-SYNAP, RTU, Dako, Glostrup, Denmark). Immunohistochemistry was performed using standard lab procedures.

Manual Ki67 measurement

Ki67 PI was defined as the number of Ki67-positive tumor cells, divided by the total number of tumor cells, within a hotspot. Manual Ki67 PI was conducted either at the time of diagnosis and obtained from the original cytology report, or if not available, manually counted during the study inclusion period. The manual count was performed by two pathologists using the same method. The manual counting method involves a pathologist looking through a microscope, counting all Ki67 positive nuclei in hotspot areas, using a cell counter. Between 500 and 2000 tumor cells were counted per case. A hotspot is defined as an area of the highest nuclear labeling.

DIA-based Ki67 measurement using Visiopharm

All slides were digitalized at ×40 magnification using a Philips scanner (Ultra-Fast Scanner, Philips, Best, The Netherlands). Slides were studied using the image analysis software from Visiopharm (Oncotopix Discovery, Visiopharm, Hoersholm, Denmark). We used a customized configuration of Visiopharm applications tailored to the study's specific needs. These include the following applications and serial numbers: “sYN VDS, Tumor detection” (Serial nr: 931DFFE5-B8CF-4B23-A1CA-A136A25EC6BB), “Ki-67 APP, eus-FNA MRA” (Serial nr: E7FFCCE5-3F2F-4B10-8450-2FD2A7EC6DB8), “Hotspot detection” (Serial nr: AA767776-B4F8-4AD8-996D-9BF1324BAA93), and “Nuclei Quantification” (Serial nr: B120AE84-D1FF-4042-8749-7BB7D2D5342A). The Ki67 and synaptophysin stained serial sections, with a thickness of 4 μm, were aligned with Tissuealign (Oncotopix Discovery, Visiopharm, Hoersholm, Denmark). For analysis, four sequential algorithms were applied using custom Visiopharm configurations.

First, tumor detection was based on synaptophysin staining and began with the application of a median filter to the aligned slides. This filter preserved nuclear and cytoplasmic edge detail while reducing background noise by replacing each pixel with the median value of its neighboring pixels. Following this, a pixel intensity threshold (range 0–255) was applied to differentiate synaptophysin-positive tumor regions from background or non-tumor tissue. Pixel intensity above a certain value (e.g., >150) are classified as tumor and those below as non-tumor. This step was performed using the “sYN VDS, Tumor detection” application (Serial nr: 931DFFE5-B8CF-4B23-A1CA-A136A25EC6BB).

Next, nuclear detection and classification based on Ki67 staining were performed using a large series of advanced image features and threshold filters to distinguish Ki67-positive and -negative nuclei. Several parameters included are nuclear size, staining intensity, nuclear contour, and the presence of a brown nuclear border. A number of clean-up steps were incorporated to improve accuracy and minimize noise, including the removal of overlapping or fragmented nuclei. The analysis was strictly limited to the tumor regions of interest (ROIs) previously identified in step 1. This analysis was conducted using the “Ki-67 APP, eus-FNA MRA” (Serial nr: E7FFCCE5-3F2F-4B10-8450-2FD2A7EC6DB8).

Hotspot detection was then performed using the “Hotspot detection” application (Serial nr: AA767776-B4F8-4AD8-996D-9BF1324BAA93), which generated a heatmap based on the local positive fraction of Ki67-positive nuclei, using a cluster radius of 50 μm. This allowed the algorithm to automatically identify up to five hotspot areas within the tumor ROIs, each containing approximately 2000 synaptophysin-positive tumor cells and representing regions with the highest proliferative activity.

Finally, Ki67 quantification was conducted within the previously selected hotspot regions. The same nuclear detection algorithm described in step 2 was reapplied, now restricted to the boundaries of the individual hotspot ROIs. Within each hotspot, the number of positive and negative nuclei was counted and a PI was calculated as: (positive nuclei/total nuclei) × 100. This quantification step used the “Nuclei Quantification” application (Serial nr: B120AE84-D1FF-4042-8749-7BB7D2D5342A).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using the IBM SPSS Statistics for Macintosh, software version 28.0.1.1. (SPSS Inc. Armonk, NY). For correlation between manual and digital count, absolute Ki67 values as well as grades were considered. The correlation was evaluated using the non-parametrical Spearman correlation coefficient. A correlation coefficient of 1 indicated a perfect correlation, whereas a correlation coefficient of 0 indicated no correlation. Correlation is considered statistically significant if the p-value associated with the Spearman correlation coefficient is lower than 0.05. Correlations are presented with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Bland–Altman analysis was used to plot the disparity between the manual and DIA Ki67 measurement against the average value.

An extensive overview of the data and performed statistics are provided in the Supplementary Materials. Analysis of the two outliers showed that the overestimation of DIA in case no. 46 was due to the contaminating hemorrhagic background in the slide. This is an example of background contamination: the large area of densely packed red blood cells caused the software to misinterpret their red-brown color as synaptophysin and Ki67 positivity leading to significant overestimation of the Ki67 index. Case no. 62 involved a largely dedifferentiated tumor and consequently weak synaptophysin expression, which greatly complicated the recognition of the tumor by the software. As outliers can have an effect on correlation analysis by over- or underestimating the true relationship between two variables, we excluded them. It shows that a visual check of the results is necessary to detect extreme values potentially reflecting an error in digital execution.

In one case (no. 28), the software initially failed to accomplish the analysis due the presence of non-cytological regions on the digitalized glass slide. We realigned the slides and selected a ROI consisting of only cytological material. After selecting this ROI, digital analysis was successful. In five cases (no. 13, 44, 47, 53, and 60), digital analysis generated a PI of 0.0%. Therefore, the subsequent algorithm was not able to generate a hotspot PI. Further investigation showed that all these cases contained less than 2000 tumor cells and a very low number of Ki67 positive cells. This combination can result in failure of the software to detect any positive nuclei, resulting in a PI of 0.0%, and thus in the impossibility to delineate a proliferative hotspot and subsequently calculate a hotspot PI. In these cases, the PI of 0.0% was accepted as the digital Ki67 index. In nine cases (8, 12, 21, 22, 56, 58, 63, and 66), hotspot detection failed because of poor alignment, as a result of which a hotspot PI was not generated. Once again, the overall Ki67 index was interpreted as the digital PI.

Discussion

Ki67 PI plays a critical role in the grading of PanNETs and is considered an essential part of the clinical management of these tumors. Grading is based on tumor's proliferative activity, most accurately represented by the Ki67 labeling index. We and others showed that this is a robust and accurate way to assess the prognosis of PanNETs, even on diagnostic cytological material. Given the persistence of inter-laboratory and inter-reader variability associated with the time-consuming manual technique,10 Ki67 quantification remains a topic of debate. Quicker counting methods, such as eyeballing, are considered inaccurate and are not recommended.1 Digital Ki67 quantification has shown to be a promising alternative, yet there is a lack of large-scale studies on cell block material. Additionally, each DIA software requires individual validation before implementation in pathology practice.

Here, we conducted a literature review concerning automatic Ki67 quantification and some of its pitfalls and potential solutions. Then, we performed a retrospective comparative study on 65 cases of PanNETs, using the Visiopharm image analysis software. At the same time, we took all the current pitfalls into account that have been seen as prohibitive for routine use of digital Ki67 quantification. To the best of our knowledge, this is one of the largest comparative studies on diagnostic material since our previous report in 2018,1 investigating the accuracy of digital versus manual Ki67 quantification in diagnostic cell block PanNET material. Our main objective was to validate the Visiopharm image analysis software for automatic Ki67 quantification of EUS-FNA obtained PanNET material.

This study shows that digital analysis is particularly accurate when considering absolute values. Spearman correlation coefficient for absolute Ki67 values suggested a strong, yet not excellent, level of agreement with r = 0.786 (p < 0.001) and a slightly disappointing, moderate correlation for grade agreement with r = 0.721 (p < 0.001) and overall concordance rate 80%. These values are consistent with those reported in the literature. For instance, a comparable study accomplished by Satturwar et al.6 showed a concordance of 73.7%. Yet, many studies, including our previous report, reveal a much stronger, correlation.1,8,9,20,22 However, comparisons with the other studies should be interpreted with caution, given the differences in the number of cases, organ localization, material type, and digital analysis software. Surprisingly, we found a higher correlation when considering absolute values. Nevertheless, given grade cut-offs and classifications change over time, especially for cytological material, grade concordance is subordinate to absolute correlation. Also, when Ki67 values are close to cut-offs, relatively small absolute differences in the Ki67 index can quickly push a tumor from Grade 1 to Grade 2, without necessarily indicating an inherent quantification error of DIA. Therefore, we advocate the application of absolute Ki67 indices in reporting and clinical decision-making.

Further analyses revealed inaccuracies mainly for grade transitions between Grade 1 and Grade 2, with especially underestimation of Grade 2 lesions by DIA. Grade concordances for Grade 1 and Grade 3 were almost perfect. Analogously, the Bland–Altman analysis revealed an average underestimation of the DIA Ki67 of 0.2% compared to manual count. Hasegawa et al.30 and Volynskaya et al.32 reported a similar underestimation of the Ki67 index when using DIA. They attributed these variations in Ki67 labeling to, among others, known tumor heterogeneity, which is greatest in Grade 2 tumors.

This increased susceptibility of Grade 2 tumors to underestimation has been consistently described in the literature and is largely explained by their inherently heterogeneous nature.4,25,26,29 Unlike high-grade lesions, which tend to exhibit uniform and diffusely elevated Ki67 expression, Grade 2 PanNETs show a patchy distribution with both low and moderately proliferative areas.29 This variability complicates hotspot identification and increases the risk of undersampling areas of highest proliferation, particularly in limited cytology specimens.

Even in digital methods with heatmap-based hotspot detection, the final region selected for quantification often uses a standardized field size or fixed number of tumor cells. As a result, a large standardized field size may under-represent focal areas of high proliferation, leading to lower average Ki67 indices and undergrading. This effect is especially relevant in Grade 2 tumors, where minor variations in Ki67 can lead to a shift in grade.

In our study, discordant Grade 2 cases frequently shared such histological and technical risk factors: high intratumoral heterogeneity, borderline hotspot cellularity, and low overall cellularity (<2000 tumor cells). Furthermore, suboptimal immunostaining intensity can make weakly positive nuclei more difficult to detect for AI algorithms. Hence, both biological (patchy proliferation) and technical (low cell count, weak staining, alignment issues, and scanning artifacts) features may serve as early predictors of potential DIA unreliability in these cases.

We noticed similar heterogeneity in certain cell blocks as a potential source of discrepancies based on hotspot selection errors within one slide. This implies that although the use of digital counting methods may provide more consistency, one can acquire distinctive outcomes depending on hotspot selection.10 In addition, the discrepancy between the digital hotspot size of a minimum of 2000 tumor cells versus manual hotspot size of 500–2000 cells is a fixed source of disagreement through a dilution–concentration effect, which has already been described by other authors.8, 9, 10,32 This is in fact a known problem of lack of standardization. The WHO and European Neuroendocrine Tumor Society criteria describe a region of at least 500 cells in an area of the highest nuclear labeling for Ki67,7,41 which has been met in our study. However, we have already shown in item 2 (literature review, section 2.2) of this manuscript, that it is more accurate to use a minimum of 2000 cells. This might be time-consuming with manual counting methods, but with automatic tools, this is no longer a stumbling block. Furthermore, the use of serial sections and aligned slides, successfully minimized the occurrence of background contamination given that we could only withhold one case where a hemorrhagic background caused overestimation. Such cases are great candidates for preliminary selection of a ROI, after which the software can calculate the PI accurately. Finally, we have confirmed significant variation of the Ki67 index based on technical issues such as slide processing, scanning, and alignment. In a few cases, the immunostained slides were found to be non-sequential, making correct alignment challenging. Poor alignment led to reduced tumor cell recognition and variations in PI. In addition, loss of contrast of Ki76 staining in some cases led to under-recognition of faintly positive staining cells. Analogously, the lack of an intense and diffuse synaptophysin staining played an essential role in misclassification of one case, by preventing the software from recognizing the tumor. DIA is not suitable to such cases which should be picked out by pathologist supervision for manual review. These cases further emphasize the requirement of excellent technical quality for accurate digital quantification. Finally, 4 out of 13 discordant cases involved Ki67 values very close to the grade cut-off of 3%.

In light of these vulnerabilities, clear criteria are needed to identify when pathologist intervention is warranted to override the digital output in clinical workflows. Based on our findings and those of others,1,3,4,6,8, 9, 10,22,27,29,32,33 such cases include: (1) tumors with low overall cellularity or hotspot areas containing fewer than 2000 tumor cells; (2) Ki67 indices falling near the grading cut-offs, particularly around 3%; (3) evidence of strong intratumoral heterogeneity, as shown by uneven Ki67 distribution; (4) suboptimal immunostaining or weak nuclear contrast, impeding proper detection of positive cells; (5) presence of extensive background contamination such as hemorrhage or stromal cells, which may falsely elevate the index. As previously demonstrated in our literature review, several studies have emphasized the need for hotspot-based sampling and tumor cell exclusion algorithms to achieve accurate grading.1,3,4,8, 9, 10,22,27,29,32,33 These findings highlight that not all samples are equally suitable for fully automated analysis and that certain predefined histological and technical conditions should trigger mandatory manual verification. Therefore, we recommend incorporating a decision-support system in which digital results are flagged for manual review when any of these histological or technical red flags are detected. This hybrid approach, combining algorithmic efficiency with expert oversight, ensures safe and reliable implementation of DIA in routine pathology practice.

The strengths of our study are the high number of cases subjected to complete digital and manual analysis (n = 67) and the successful handling of non-tumoral proliferating cells, one of the major challenges of DIA.

Our study has some limitations that are also potential sources of bias. First, we revealed imperfections of the DIA method that require further refinement of the software and algorithms. Samples with low cellularity and the presence of heterogeneity in the sections are still challenging for automatic counting and caused discrepancies based on an underestimation of Grade 2 lesions.

Second, some technical factors played a role. Weak Ki67 staining intensity and suboptimal alignment due to non-consecutive and/or poorly processed sections, affected automatic and manual count, and contributed to inconsistencies. This represents a potential source of measurement bias, especially in cases with a low PI.

Finally, the manual control count was performed by two different pathologists who applied the same counting method. Although this represents a possible source of interobserver variation, no systematic clustering of cases was observed between the two reviewer (Fig. 2). Moreover, we have already proven in a previous study27 that interobserver variability is limited when a consistent manual methods applied, thus minimizing the risk of interobserver bias in this regard.

Our results encourage the implementation of automatic counting systems in routine pathology practice. Nevertheless, DIA supervision by pathologists is of utmost importance, and the proposed grade should be interpreted taking into account other clinicopathological data of each individual patient. Pancreatic lesions provide additional complexity, given their heterogeneity and the consequent need for hotspot selection. This is one of the advantages of the Visiopharm system with automatic hotspot detection.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study is one of the largest cohorts comparing manual and digital Ki67 PI on cell block material in PanNETs, with the primary aim of clinically validating the automatic counting system Visiopharm software. The results demonstrate comparability in terms of accuracy, with a strong correlation coefficient of r = 0.786. Upon reviewing selected cases, to ascertain the reasons for the limited discrepancies, it was noted that non-tumor cell contamination was quite rare. Grading errors persisted due to sample heterogeneity, cases near grade cut-offs, and technical issues. These inaccuracies stress the importance of high technical quality and highlight the need for overall pathologist supervision to assure the results generated through automated platforms. In any case, automated analysis offers pathologists stronger consistency than manual count for grading PanNETs on diagnostic material. Based on our findings, we suggest a potential validation framework for digital Ki67 measurements in PanNETs. Given that therapeutic strategies in PanNETs rely significantly on the cytological Ki67 index, the next logical step in validating Visiopharm-based automatic Ki67 counting would be to optimize the minor inaccuracies in the algorithms, address the technical challenges, and investigate prognosis and clinical endpoints.
