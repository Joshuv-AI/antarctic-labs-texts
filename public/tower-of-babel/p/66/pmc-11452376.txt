Identification of semen traces at a crime scene through Raman spectroscopy and machine learning
Authors: Alexey V Borisov, Mikhail S Snegerev, Sonivette Colón-Rodríguez, Marisia A Fikiet, Igor K Lednev, Yury V Kistenev
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBiological fluid stains can be instrumental in solving crimes. Identification of semen can help reconstruct events in sexual assault cases and identify suspects via DNA profiling. Current methods for semen identification suffer from limitations, including destruction of the sample and potential false positives. One of the main unsolved issues is the elimination of underlying substrate interference. In this paper, chemometric approaches were developed to isolate and identify a biofluid stain on interfering substrates using Raman spectroscopy. The first approach, called Multivariate Curve Resolution with the Addition Method, combines the standard addition method with multivariate curve resolution. The second one uses a criterion based on reducing the spectrum complexity when a spectral component is removed from a Raman spectrum of a multi-component sample entirely. The results demonstrate the superiority of the first approach relative to the second for both small volume fraction of the fluid stain compared to the substrate and random noise.Subject terms: Imaging and sensing, Data mining, Cheminformatics

Introduction

Biological fluid stains play a significant role in modern practical forensics since they are used for reconstructing events at a crime scene and identifying suspects via DNA profiling1. Semen, the male reproductive fluid, can be especially important as evidence in sexual assault cases. Therefore, its identification at the scene and later extraction for DNA is of utmost importance. Current methods for semen stain identification rely on proteins like prostate-specific antigen (PSA) and semenogelin I and II (SgI/II) as well as sperm, the most unique part of semen2–7. Sperm cells are not present in other body fluid, and so their presence is the most reliable means of semen identification. However, sperm cells are not present in all semen, or they can be in low concentrations, or degraded and therefore hard to find8,9. Other methods involve the identification of human semen proteins using immunochromatographic methods. The protein PSA is used in the ABAcard® p30 immunochromatography cartridge, while RSID™-Semen uses SgI/II. It was thought that PSA and SgI/II were unique to semen, but both have been found in other tissues and organs2,4,5,10. Also, existing methods necessitate the destruction of evidence for testing. Therefore, an abundance of research has been conducted on alternative methods of body fluid identification that do not rely on non-specific proteins and are not destructive in nature.

Raman spectroscopy based on the registration of inelastic scattering of light with a spectral shift corresponding by molecule’s vibrational modes that allows identifying a studied sample composition. Raman spectroscopic methods have the advantage of being non-destructive, rich in information on chemical content, and need only several picograms for analysis1,11–18. While Raman-based methods have their advantages, the current largest hurdle is the interference from the substrate on which a body fluid is deposited. The substrate can produce Raman scattering, i.e. orders of magnitude stronger than the body fluid to be identified19–21. Therefore, the vital problem here is the identification of characteristics of a body fluid placed on a substrate. Conventional regression methods such as partial least squares regression, principal component regression, partial least-squares (PLS), Hierarchical PLS Analysis, multivariate curve resolution alternating least squares, and classical least squares considering an additive contribution of a specific substance in total Raman spectrum of a sample are actively and successfully used for spectral data analysis to determine the concentrations of individual components of mixtures22–27. However, all these approaches have common disadvantages, especially when applied to forensic science because, to apply these methods, it is necessary to carefully train and calibrate the model on a training sample that includes all possible substrate-body fluid combinations. It is important to note that the amount of possible substrates dealt with at a crime scene that could have body fluids on them is almost infinite. This means that the training set for a regression model that would deconvolute and quantify a body fluid on a substrate would have to be impractically large. It is especially true if the rule of 5 is employed. As demonstrated in Shoeb Alahmad et al.24. , for the quantitative analysis of a three-component mixture by the PLS method, fifteen mixtures (3 times 5) were used in the calibration set. Therefore, the vital problem here is identification of characteristics of a biofluid placed on a substrate so that a training set for all the possible substrates does not have to be made.

It is for this reason that methods based on deconvoluting that are calibrated with spectra of pure body fluids have shown the most promise. Calibration of these types of methods are carried out only with the help of a spectrum of pure biological fluid. McLaughlin et al. developed Hypothetical Addition Multivariate Analysis with Numerical Differentiation (HAMAND) based on Multivariate Curve Resolution and demonstrated that body fluid spectral contributions could be identified and extracted even when significant interference from substrates was seen28. Kistenev et al. compared Multivariate Curve Resolution with the Addition Method (MCRAD) and Reducing Spectrum Complexity (RSC). MCRAD is based on artificial extending the experimental dataset by numerical adding target component Raman spectrum with definite concentrations to experimental Raman spectrum of this component and other unknown component mixture. RSC considers fact that a spectrum complexity is reduced when we remove a definite spectral component from a mixture entirely. It was demonstrated that while both methods were able to extract body fluid traces from substrate interference, RSC works better even when the spectrum of the body fluid has similar features to that of the substrate29.

Current research into this area has used various chemometric and machine learning-based methods to help overcome substrate interference. In this study, two approaches based on RSC and MCRAD were used and compared to separate the spectra of semen from those of polyester and glass substrates. These two substrates exhibit a significant interference but in different ways, with polyester having a large Raman signal with many peaks throughout the spectra and glass having a very large fluorescence signal that masks most Raman signals.

Regardless of the reason behind the complexity, RSC was able to separate the semen signal from the substrate signal in a joint Raman spectrum without using a reference Raman spectrum of the substrate.

The peculiarity of both RSC and MCRAD is that they need to know a reference Raman spectrum of a studied fluid sample. The difference between the reference and experimental Raman spectra caused by person-to-person chemical variability of biofluids and external conditions is a source of possible inaccuracy of these methods. One of the ways to overcome this limitation is to use a decomposition of a biofluid Raman spectrum on principal chemical substances or abstract features like principal components.

1 Materials and methods

1.1 Data description

Semen samples used in this study were purchased from Lee Biosolutions, Inc. Samples were collected from anonymous donors in accordance with the approved protocol and a signed informed consent form. A small 10 µL drop of semen fluid was placed on a substrate and dried completely under room conditions overnight.

A Renishaw inVia confocal Raman spectrometer equipped with a research-grade Leica microscope, 20 long-range lens (numerical aperture of 0.35), and WiRE 2.0 software was used. A 785-nm laser light was utilized for excitation. The laser power on the dried samples was about 115 mW, and the spot size of the excitation beam in standard confocal mode was about 5 μm wide. Accumulation time was 10 s for each scan. The spectral resolution was about 3.5 cm−1, and peak accuracy was assured by calibration with a silicon standard. For the automatic mapping, the lower plate of a Nanonics AFM MultiView 1000 system was set up under the microscope, and measurements were taken using Quartz II and QuartzSpec software. The used Raman spectra data set is presented in Table 1. The processing of the received data was carried out with MATLAB software.

Conclusion

Typically, Raman spectra biofluid stains at a crime scene are influenced by an underlying substrate with an unknown Raman signature. We conducted a comparative analysis of two approaches enabling a decomposition of Raman spectrum of a biofluid on a substrate using a predefined reference Raman spectrum of the biofluid. The MCRAD combines the standard addition method with multivariate curve resolution. The RSC method uses a criterion based on a spectrum complexity minimizing, when we totally remove a component spectrum from a total spectrum.

Results of numerical simulation, using a set of synthesized Raman spectra of a semen on a polyester or a glass substrate, demonstrated the superiority of MCRAD relative RSC for both small VFs and small random noise. The opposite situation takes place for non-small VFs and (or) non-small random noise. The reason is that RSC is not an iterative method like MCRAD. Therefore, the accuracy of RSC is defined mainly by matching between a reference and actual Raman spectra of the studied biofluid.

Application of the RSC and MCRAD methods for the experimental Raman spectra of a semen fluid drop dried on a polyester or a glass substrate showed the following. When we restore semen FV in a certain spatial point on a substrate, MCRAD drawbacks are that the corresponding distribution function support has a negative part with no physical sense and that this distribution is more widespread compared to the RSC application results. In total, RSC provides better results than MCRAD.

The necessary condition for both methods (RSC and MCRAD) implementation is to have good etalon Raman spectrum of a target substance. Therefore, we can use both methods for analysis of a single Raman spectrum of target biofluid stain on a substrate. The test dataset volume defines only statistical stability of estimations of these methods efficiency. To demonstrate this explicitly, we mixed randomly selected Raman spectra of polyester and glass substrates with two etalon Raman spectra of semen (on Al substrate) with =0.04 and =0.01. The more suitable the etalon spectrum is, the better is result of target component VF value restoration. In any case, both method are quite robust regarding bias between etalon spectrum and spectrum presented in a mixture “a biofluid sample + a substrate”. Note that RSC is less sensitive to the variability of the etalon spectrum compared to MCRAD. Therefore, the principal limitation is that the accuracy of RSC/MCRAD is defined by matching between a reference and real Raman spectra of the target component. The individual variability is important for biofluids. Also, spatial position of analyzed point on substrate surface is factor, which should be keeping in mind. Of course, we can reduce possible errors by collecting more data of the reference Raman spectra. Another way is to use a decomposition of a biofluid Raman spectrum on principal chemical substances or abstract features like principal components. Also, a simple but effective criterion of a Raman spectrum correct decomposition is that the estimations of a biofluid stain VF in various spatial points on a substrate surface have a low variation.
