Assessment of the Efficacy of Alstonia scholaris, Calotropis procera, and Chlorhexidine Mouthrinses on Oral Health of Children Aged 9-12 Years: A Randomized Clinical Trial
Authors: Parnaja Sanjay Valke, Ashveeta J Shetty, Shilpa S Naik, Sanjana R Kodical, Priya Gore, Pranali Sandip Band
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAim and backgroundChlorhexidine (CHX) is a gold-standard chemotherapeutic agent for preventing gingival and periodontal diseases. However, its drawbacks, such as metallic taste, burning sensation, and staining of teeth, make its acceptance questionable in children. To address this, the study aimed at the formulation of mouthrinses using herbal extracts of Alstonia scholaris and Calotropis procera plants, and evaluating and comparing its efficacy with CHX.MethodologyThe study was registered at CTRI (CTRI/2024/06/068874), which involved formulation of mouthrinses using the extracts, followed by a double-blinded clinical trial with 60 children aged 9-12 years, randomly assigned to three groups: A. scholaris, C. procera, and CHX. Parents were directed to dispense 10 mL of mouthrinse to their children two times a day for 14 days. The children were assessed at baseline and 14 days for plaque index, gingival index, salivary pH, and taste acceptability.ResultsThe intragroup comparison showed a statistically significant reduction in mean plaque scores across all three groups over 14 days. Additionally, the C. procera group exhibited a significant increase in salivary pH over 14 days (p < 0.05). The intergroup comparison revealed no statistically significant differences in mean plaque and gingival scores (p < 0.05). However, the C. procera group showed an increase in salivary pH compared to the other two groups (p < 0.05). Furthermore, the taste acceptability of the C. procera group was significantly better than that of the other groups (p < 0.05).ConclusionThe two herbal extracts were as effective as CHX in maintaining the oral health of children, with better taste acceptance, and can be considered as an alternative to CHX.Clinical significanceMouthrinses formulated with A. scholaris and C. procera extracts can be safely and effectively used for children.How to cite this articleValke PS, Shetty AJ, Naik SS, et al. Introduction

Dental plaque is a biofilm on oral surfaces implicated in the etiology of dental caries and gingival disease.1 The implementation of both mechanical and chemical plaque control strategies is essential for the prevention and management of dental caries and periodontal diseases.2 Dexterity, motivation, and cognitive development are three important characteristics that influence brushing efficiency, especially in children.3,4 Furthermore, less accessible parts of the oral cavity, such as the subgingival and interproximal regions, need adjuncts like chemotherapeutic medications for plaque control.2 The chemotherapeutic agents in mouthrinse, in addition to mechanical usage of toothbrushes, prove helpful in improving gingival health and prevention of dental caries by inhibiting plaque formation.5 Mouthrinses serve as an effective medium for delivering chemical agents and are valued for their convenience, ability to reduce plaque, and breath-freshening properties.6 Chlorhexidine (CHX), a gold standard in today's date, has established itself as a credible mouthrinse and is available in different variants in the market. However, prolonged use of this chemical can lead to a metallic taste, staining of teeth, and a burning sensation. Due to these reasons, the acceptability of CHX in children becomes questionable. In an attempt to overcome these pitfalls, herbal mouthwashes can be used.8

Ayurveda has a rich 5000-year history in India and is used for the treatment of many medical ailments. Many herbal medicines, such as triphala, tulsi patra, jyestiamadh, neem, clove oil, pudina, ajwain, aloe vera, peppermint, tea tree essential oil, fennel seeds, sesame seeds, etc., have been documented in the literature and employed in dental formulations in one form or another.9–18 Two such herbs, Alstonia scholaris (A. scholaris) from the Apocynaceae family and Calortropis procera (C. procera) from the Asclepiadaceae family, are recognized for their antimicrobial and therapeutic properties within the oral cavity. However, to the best of our knowledge, these two extracts have not been used in any dental formulations; hence, this study was conducted to formulate mouthrinses using these two herbal extracts and to evaluate their effect on the gingival health, plaque index, and pH of the saliva of children.

Materials and Methods

The present study was conducted in the Department of Pediatric and Preventive Dentistry of the institute. Prior to initiating the study, ethical clearance was secured from the Institutional Review Board (IREB/2024/PEDO/18). The study was conducted in two phases. Phase I involved the in vitro formulation of the mouthrinses using the two herbal extracts, and phase II involved the in vivo evaluation of the prepared mouthrinses on the plaque index, gingival index, pH of saliva, and taste acceptability of children aged 9-12 years.

Phase I: Formulation of Mouthrinses

Formulation of mouthrinses was done using the cold maceration technique.19 The extracts were weighed into 25 gm using an electronic weighing machine and put into a sterile container to which 500 mL of sterile deionized distilled water was added. Subsequently, the mixture was passed through a Whatman No. 4 filter paper for filtration. Following the overnight filtration, a sweetening agent (0.05 gm% saccharin) and preservative (0.01% sodium benzoate, code 211) were added. A total of 5600 mL of A. scholaris and C. procera mouthrinse was prepared considering the sample size and stored in opaque plastic containers in a cool, dry place.

Phase II: Clinical Trial

Study Design

This study was designed as a double-blind, randomized parallel-arm clinical trial, conducted in accordance with Consolidated Standards for Reporting Trials (CONSORT) guidelines (Fig. 1), and registered with the Clinical Trial Registry of India (CTRI/2024/06/068874).

Sample Size

The sample size was calculated using the G*Power software 3.1. (Universitat Kiel, Kiel, Germany) at an alpha of 0.05, power of 0.8000, and confidence interval at 95%. The sample size obtained was 17 per group. Considering 10% attrition, the minimum sample size was 60, which is 20 participants in each group.

Procedure

The randomization of the participants was carried out using a lottery technique of randomization by the primary investigator into the following three groups:

Group I: Children receiving mouthrinse formulated from A. scholaris.

Group I: Children receiving mouthrinse formulated from A. scholaris.

Group II: Children receiving mouthrinse formulated from C. procera.

Group II: Children receiving mouthrinse formulated from C. procera.

Group III: Children receiving CHX mouthrinse.

Group III: Children receiving CHX mouthrinse.

Allocation concealment was ensured through the use of sealed, opaque envelopes that were sequentially numbered. An independent observer dispensed the bottles to the parents. Parents were directed to dispense 10 mL of the mouthrinse to their child, to swish and rinse in the oral cavity for 1 minute, and then spit, once in the morning and once at night after tooth brushing under parental supervision for the next 14 days. Participants were instructed to maintain their usual dietary habits and oral hygiene routines throughout the trial. A calendar was provided to record the frequency and specific days of mouthrinse usage. Every participant was provided with 280 mL of their respective mouthrinse.

Statistical Analysis

The statistical analysis was performed using the IBM SPSS version 21 (IBM Corp., Armonk, New York, United States) software. The comparative statistics for the age of the participants were performed using analysis of variance (ANOVA), and the sex of the participants using the Chi-square test. The intragroup comparison for all three groups was performed using a paired t -test. The intergroup comparison for all three groups was performed using the ANOVA test.

Discussion

The ingredients used in the mouthrinse were A. scholaris and C. procera extracts, distilled water, 0.05 gm% saccharin, and 0.01% sodium benzoate. Saccharin, a Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved noncaloric artificial sweetener, is known to reduce extracellular polysaccharide formation, thereby showing anticariogenic properties.23 As per the World Health Organization (WHO)/Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) Joint Expert Committee on Food Additives (1993), an acceptable daily intake of saccharin is 5 mg/kg body weight, and in the present study, it was used at 0.05 gm% concentration.24 Sodium benzoate was added at 0.01%, which is an FDA-approved antibacterial and antifungal preservation with a permissible limit of 0-5 mg/kg body weight.25 No flavoring agent was added to the formulations because it would be a confounding factor.

A parallel-group design was chosen over a crossover-group design because some effects of the mouthrinse used may persist and alter the response observed in the later treatment periods.26 In the present study, the age-group of 9-14 years was selected because the erupting teeth report greater plaque accumulation, leading to an increase in the occurrence of dental caries.27 According to Jean Paget, children above the age of 7 years develop a sense of semi-logical reasoning to infer cause-and-effect relationships, and hence, good compliance could be expected at this age.28

To evaluate the effectiveness of the mouthrinses in removing existing plaque and inhibiting its recurrence, oral prophylaxis was intentionally omitted prior to the initiation of the study. This was in accordance with the studies previously conducted by Charles et al.,29 Sharma et al.,30 and Gupta et al.31 Plaque accumulation is an initial step of caries formation, and the amount of plaque accumulated is directly proportional to the caries experience in the child.32 The assessment of plaque accumulation was done using the Silness and Loe index, which requires simple diagnostic instruments and does not require a disclosing agent for assessment.5 The results obtained suggest that A. scholaris and C. procera extracts have an antiplaque accumulation property comparable to CHX. However, to our best knowledge, there is no literature on the use of these two herbal extracts previously as antiplaque agents.

The worldwide prevalence of plaque-induced gingivitis is 73% in children aged 6-12 years.33 A peak increase in gingivitis is also seen in adolescents due to an increase in the circulating sex hormones.34 The results obtained suggest that A. scholaris and C. procera extracts have an antigingivitis effect in the oral cavity comparable to CHX. However, to our best knowledge, there is no literature on the use of these two herbal extracts previously as antigingivitis agents.

Salivary pH alterations influence the pathogenesis of caries, and it is seen that higher salivary pH values reduce the risk of enamel demineralization.17 In the present study, Indikrom paper strips were used for the assessment of the pH of saliva. This was in accordance with the studies done by Balappanavar et al.35 and Dandekar et al.4 The two herbal extracts are known to increase the pH of the gut,36 but to the best of our knowledge, there is no available literature on the effectiveness of these extracts in increasing the pH of saliva.

It was observed that the taste acceptability was better for both herbal extracts than for CHX. CHX is known to have a bitter taste,37,38 and hence, was not liked by the participants. Since these extracts have never been used before for the formulation of mouthrinses, there is no available literature on their taste acceptability.

The limitations of the study were that since the study was conducted on the patients visiting the outpatient department of Pediatric and Preventive Dentistry, uniformity in the dietary habits was not possible as against the studies which were conducted in a residential setting with a fixed dietary schedule and the study could have included a longer follow-up after the withdrawal of mouthrinses, to evaluate the long-term effects of the mouthrinses.

Conclusion

From the results obtained, it can be concluded that the two herbal mouthrinses were equally effective as CHX in maintaining the oral health of children. There was a reduction in the plaque and gingival index over 14 days and an increase in the pH of saliva. The taste of herbal mouthrinses was more acceptable to the children than CHX. Fewer side effects were observed in both the herbal mouthrinse groups as compared to CHX, and hence, the prepared herbal mouthrinses can be considered as an alternative to CHX in children.

Clinical Significance

Mouthrinses formulated with A. scholaris and C. procera extracts can be safely and effectively used for children.
