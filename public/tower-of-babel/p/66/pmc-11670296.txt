Durable Muscle Extracellular Matrix Engineered with Adhesive Phenolic Moieties for Effective Skeletal Muscle Regeneration in Muscle Atrophy
Authors: Soojeong Choi, Mi Jeong Lee, Moohyun Kim, Yunsu Bae, Jang‐Ung Park, Seung‐Woo Cho
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractMuscle atrophy detrimentally impacts health and exacerbates physical disability, leading to increased mortality. In particular, sarcopenia, aging‐related degenerative muscle loss, necessitates urgent remedies. Current approaches for treating muscle atrophy include exercise and nutrition, while drug exploration remains in its early stages. Cell therapy, focusing on satellite cells, faces significant challenge due to poor engraftment, safety issue, and high cost. Cell‐free approach using extracellular matrix (ECM) shows a regenerative potential, but a lack of mechanical and adhesive properties hinders prolonged efficacy of ECM therapy. Here, durable muscle ECM (MEM) hydrogels for muscle atrophy by fortifying MEM with adhesive phenolic moieties including catechol and pyrogallol are demonstrated. The resultant phenolic MEM hydrogels exhibit enhanced mechanical and adhesive properties and provide sustained muscle‐like microenvironments to address muscle atrophy. No local and systemic toxicities are observed after phenolic MEM injection into tibialis anterior muscle. Notably, these engineered MEM hydrogels, devoid of cells or drugs, induce tissue rejuvenation by promoting muscle protein synthesis and facilitating functional muscle recovery in mouse models of disuse‐ and age‐induced atrophy. This study introduces cell‐free, ECM‐based therapeutics with translational potential for muscle atrophy by reversing muscle loss and restoring function.

Introduction

Skeletal muscles, composing 40%–50% of total body mass, play a fundamental role in maintaining upright posture, facilitating movement, and are involved in various physiological processes such as thermogenesis, metabolism, and the secretion of peptides for inter‐tissue communication.[ 1 , 2 ] The reduction of skeletal muscle mass, known as muscle atrophy, can be caused by immobility, aging, malfunctions, medications, or diseases affecting the musculoskeletal or nervous system, which is highly detrimental to the human body.[ 3 , 4 , 5 ] This results in a combination of excessive protein degradation and insufficient protein synthesis. Muscle atrophy not only worsens existing diseases but also impairs the efficacy of many different therapeutic treatments, leading to increased morbidity and mortality. In addition, sarcopenia, characterized by the progressive loss of muscle mass and strength with age, is estimated to affect 10%–16% of the elderly population worldwide.[ 6 , 7 ] Recently, classified as a disease, there is a significant urgency to develop cures for sarcopenia.

Current treatment strategies for muscle atrophy primarily involve physical exercise and nutritional supplementation.[ 8 , 9 ] While the investigation of potential drugs for muscle atrophy, including drug repositioning, is in its early stages, no therapeutic drug has yet been approved for clinical use. Stem cell therapy particularly focuses on satellite cells because the functions or the number of these stem cells are significantly reduced in patients with muscle atrophy.[ 10 ] Several studies have actively explored increasing the number of satellite cells or enhancing their function by injecting stem cells to treat muscle atrophy.[ 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 ] However, the use of stem cells faces significant challenge such as poor engraftment, safety issues, and high cost.[ 16 , 17 ] In another approach, electrical stimulation or mechanically active materials have been employed to induce muscle contraction to alleviate muscle atrophy.[ 18 , 19 , 20 ] Despite a promise of electrical and mechanical stimulation, the practicality of these strategies is rather limited in a clinical setting due to the insufficient therapeutic efficacy of standalone use and nonspecific target site. To address these limitations, extensive research has been conducted on the development of biomaterials for muscle regeneration, particularly decellularized tissue‐derived extracellular matrix (ECM) hydrogel.[ 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 ] Decellularized muscle ECM hydrogels have demonstrated therapeutic efficacy in regenerating skeletal and cardiac muscles.[ 21 , 22 , 23 , 25 , 26 ] Importantly, Traverse et al. assessed the clinical feasibility of decellularized porcine myocardium‐derived ECM hydrogel in patients with left ventricular dysfunction in both early and late myocardial infarction.[ 26 ] While muscle ECM hydrogel contains muscle‐specific growth factors and structural ECM proteins that can improve cell infiltration and subsequent differentiation within muscle defects, they often lack the essential mechanical and adhesive properties required for functional restoration of skeletal muscle, resulting in failure in sustained regenerative efficacy. Accordingly, durable ECM‐based biomaterials should be developed for improving muscle tissue regeneration to address muscle atrophy. There is currently no effective strategy for muscle tissue regeneration to address muscle atrophy.

Herein, we propose a novel cell‐free therapeutic strategy for muscle atrophy. We endowed muscle extracellular matrix (MEM) with mechanically reinforced, muscle adhesive properties by modifying MEM with bioinspired phenolic moieties (catechol: CA and pyrogallol: PG). Phenolic modification of MEM can prolong the effects of MEM in vivo to induce various biological processes involved in muscle protein synthesis and muscle development. These engineered MEM hydrogels are highly biocompatible and marginally immunogenic in both local and systemic locations. Intramuscular administration of MEM hydrogels modified with CA (MEM‐CA) and PG (MEM‐PG) more effectively promoted synthesis of muscle proteins (e.g., dystrophin, laminin) and increased muscle mass in a mouse model of disuse‐induced muscle atrophy. Unmodified MEM and decellularized non‐muscle tissue (skin)‐derived ECM hydrogels modified with phenolic moieties did not exhibit such regenerative effects for muscle regeneration. More importantly, MEM‐CA and MEM‐PG hydrogels demonstrated muscle regeneration and functional improvement in an age‐induced muscle atrophy mouse model, which was mainly attributed to a prolonged muscle‐like microenvironment with rejuvenating effects, elevating protein synthesis, activating muscle developments, and mitigating oxidative stresses. These outcomes highlight the potential of our engineered MEM hydrogels for translational therapeutic applications in restoring muscle mass and function in muscle atrophy.

Results and Discussion

Fabrication and Characterization of MEM

In this study, MEM was fabricated through decellularization of porcine femoris skeletal muscle and subsequent lyophilization (Figure 1a). Decellularization was verified by a significant reduction in double‐stranded DNA (dsDNA) content in MEM (8.53 ± 0.73 ng mg−1 of dry tissue) compared to native muscle tissue (1544.18 ± 40.81 ng mg−1) (Figure 1b). MEM contained dsDNA below 50 ng mg−1, which meets the criterion for effective decellularization suitable for clinical applications.[ 27 ] In addition, quantification of principal ECM components, glycosaminoglycan (GAG) and collagen, revealed that GAG and collagen were well retained in MEM after decellularization, exhibiting significantly higher levels in MEM (GAG: 6.70 ± 0.42 µg mg−1 and collagen: 29.43 ± 0.88 µg mg−1) compared to those in native muscle tissue (GAG: 3.81 ± 0.27 µg mg−1 and collagen: 5.22 ± 0.29 µg mg−1) (Figure 1c,d). The concentrations of dsDNA, GAG, and collagen after decellularization were measured based on the weight of the lyophilized MEM. Post decellularization process, the elevated levels of GAG and collagen are attributed to the removal of cytoplasmic components, which leads to the compaction of ECM components, thereby concentrating GAG and collagen in MEM.[ 28 , 29 ] This affirmed that the decellularization process did not compromise the integrity of ECM components in MEM. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining showed the absence of nuclei in MEM, while Masson's trichrome (MT) and toluidine blue (TB) staining demonstrated preserved collagen and GAG, respectively (Figure 1e). Overall, these data confirmed complete removal of DNA and cellular materials in muscle tissue while major ECM components were well‐preserved.

ECM profiles in MEM were investigated with mass spectrometric analysis. According to the Matrisome Project database,[ 30 ] proteins in MEM were categorized as matrisome and non‐matrisome proteins. MEM was found to be composed of core matrisome and matrisome‐associated proteins in the following distribution: 63.90% collagens, 3.23% glycoproteins, 32.76% proteoglycans, and 0.11% ECM regulators (Figure 1f). Similar to the native muscle ECM,[ 31 ] the main ECM component of MEM is collagen (Figure 1f, g). Further investigation on the core matrisome proteins of MEM elucidated additional insights into the essential proteins in MEM (Figure 1g). ECM proteins pivotal for muscle function and regeneration were included in MEM, such as collagen type 6 alpha 1, 2, and 3 (COL6A1, COL6A2, and COL6A3) and biglycan (BGN).[ 32 , 33 ] Decorin (DCN) and dermatopontin (DPT) aid in myogenesis.[ 33 , 34 ] Lumican (LUM) has been noted to provide protection against muscle atrophy, thus emerging as a novel therapeutic target for sarcopenia.[ 35 ] Core matrisome proteins, including collagen type 4 alpha 2 (COL4A2), collagen type 12 alpha 1 (COL12A1), laminin alpha 2 (LAMA2), laminin gamma 1 (LAMC1), periostin (POSTN), tubulointerstitial nephritis antigen‐like 1 (TINAGL1), and fibromodulin (FMOD), are involved in integral muscle processes like myoblast differentiation, muscle development, muscle regeneration, muscle stability and strength.[ 31 , 32 , 36 , 37 , 38 , 39 , 40 , 41 ] Thus, we could conclude that MEM is comprised of various matrisome proteins that can potentially contribute to muscle tissue development, growth, and function.

For a comprehensive understanding of MEM, functional annotation of proteins in MEM was performed with Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis. Matrisome proteins in MEM were involved in biological processes like ECM organization, collagen fibril organization, and cell adhesion (Figure 1h). GO biological processes of non‐matrisome proteins were also explored, and muscle system processes, muscle contraction, and muscle structure development were significantly enriched (Figure 1i). Furthermore, the protein–protein interaction (PPI) of MEM was examined with STRING database. Based on the PPI network analysis, three distinct functional clusters were found in MEM. Cluster 1 (blue), 2 (purple), and 3 (red) were associated in biological processes: muscle processes, ECM organization, and metabolic processes, respectively (Figure 1j). As suggested by GO enrichment and PPI network analyses, MEM can provide structural support as a scaffold and proteins related to muscle‐specific function and development to promote muscle regeneration. In addition, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways revealed enrichment in the phosphoinositide 3‐kinase (PI3K)/Akt signaling pathway, calcium signaling pathway, and amino acid biosynthesis in MEM (Figure 1k). The PI3K/Akt signaling is a well‐known signaling pathway associated with the regulation of muscle size during tissue regeneration.[ 42 ] The calcium signaling pathway is critical in the regulation of muscle development, homeostasis, and regeneration.[ 43 ] Amino acid biosynthesis is fundamental as amino acids are involved in protein synthesis in skeletal muscle for muscle mass growth.[ 44 ] Collectively, the interplay of ECM proteins in MEM is associated with various pathways essentially involved in muscle development and regeneration.

To investigate the similarity between porcine‐derived MEM and human skeletal muscle, the muscle‐enriched proteins from the Human Protein Atlas (proteins expressed fourfold higher in muscle compared to the other tissues) were compared between MEM and human skeletal muscle.[ 45 , 46 ] All 51 muscle‐enriched proteins identified in MEM were commonly shared with muscle‐enriched proteins in human muscle tissues, and all 28 matrisome proteins in MEM were also present in matrisome proteins of human muscle (Figure 1l). In sum, our proteomic data suggests that the porcine‐derived MEM has resemblance to native human muscle tissues and can recapitulate structural and functional properties of native muscle tissues.

Characterization of MEM‐CA and MEM‐PG

The MEM‐CA and MEM‐PG were synthesized by conjugating dopamine hydrochloride (catechol; CA) and 5′‐hydroxydopamine hydrochloride (pyrogallol; PG) to carboxyl groups of ECM elements in MEM, respectively, using 1‐ethyl‐3‐(3‐dimethylaminopropyl) carbodiimide (EDC)/N‐hydroxysuccinimide (NHS) chemistry (Figure 2a). Proton nuclear magnetic resonance (1H‐NMR) spectroscopy of MEM‐CA and MEM‐PG conjugates demonstrated newly detected aromatic proton peaks at around 7 ppm (Figure S1, Supporting Information), indicating the conjugation of CA and PG to MEM. The MEM‐CA and MEM‐PG can be crosslinked to form the hydrogels via oxidation under physiological or weak basic conditions. To determine whether the proteins in MEM are preserved after CA and PG conjugation, we performed a proteomic assessment of MEM, MEM‐CA, and MEM‐PG hydrogels. Comparison of matrisome protein composition revealed no significant alteration in the contents of collagens, glycoproteins, proteoglycans, and ECM regulators in MEM‐CA and MEM‐PG hydrogels compared to the MEM hydrogel (Figure 2b). In all hydrogel types, collagens were the most abundant type of ECM component, constituting 51.40% in MEM hydrogel, 59.72% in MEM‐CA hydrogel, and 51.85% in MEM‐PG hydrogel, while ECM regulators were the least abundant type in all hydrogels, containing 7.48% in MEM hydrogel, 5.56% in MEM‐CA hydrogel, and 4.94% in MEM‐PG hydrogel. Although the profiles of glycoproteins and proteoglycans slightly differed, the overall composition was highly similar in MEM, MEM‐CA, and MEM‐PG hydrogels. In MEM hydrogel, 27 matrisome proteins were identified, and out of those 27 proteins, 20 and 21 were ubiquitously present in MEM‐CA and MEM‐PG hydrogels, respectively (Figure 2c). Moreover, among the 10 most abundant matrisome proteins, 9 proteins (asporin (ASPN), BGN, collagen type 1 alpha 1 and 2 (COL1A1, COL1A2), collagen type 3 alpha 1 (COL3A1), collagen type 4 alpha 1 (COL4A1), COL6A2, COL6A3, elastin (ELN)) demonstrated an overlap across all three hydrogels (MEM, MEM‐CA, MEM‐PG) (Figure 2d, left). Similarly, among the 10 most amply present muscle‐enriched proteins in MEM, MEM‐CA, and MEM‐PG hydrogels, 8 proteins (aldolase, fructose‐bisphosphate A (ALDOA), desmin (DES), four‐and‐a‐half LIM domain protein 1 (FHL1), myosin heavy chain 1 (MYH1), MYH4, MYH7, myosin light chain 1 (MYL1), and MYL11) were consistently shared in all hydrogel types (Figure 2d, right). These proteins play crucial roles in muscle development, function, and homeostasis.[ 33 , 47 , 48 , 49 , 50 ] With the results above, we confirmed the preservation of major matrisome and muscle‐elevated proteins that can provide both biochemical and physical cues for effective muscle regeneration.

A minimally invasive procedure in treating muscle atrophy is required for significantly reduced recovery time and the prevention of recurring muscle loss. The injectability, specifically the extrusion force, of MEM‐CA and MEM‐PG hydrogels would be a crucial factor for effective muscle regeneration in muscular atrophy. Given that these hydrogels can undergo in situ gelation within tissue through autoxidation, the pre‐gel solutions of the MEM‐CA and MEM‐PG can be conveniently injected into muscle tissue. To evaluate the injectability, we measured the force required to push the pre‐gel solution through a 26 G needle (Figure 2e). The syringe loaded with each pre‐gel solution or phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS) was secured on the universal testing machine (UTM), and the extrusion force was recorded while the syringe plunger was pushed down. Importantly, the extrusion forces of the 1% and 2% (w/v) pre‐gel solution of MEM‐CA and MEM‐PG were not significantly different from that of PBS (Figure 2f,g). In addition, as the concentration of the MEM‐CA and MEM‐PG increased, the viscosity tended to increase, requiring more force to inject. Consequently, concentrations of MEM‐CA and MEM‐PG exceeding 2% (w/v) were excluded.

Although swelling is an inherent characteristic of hydrogels,[ 51 ] the MEM, MEM‐CA, and MEM‐PG hydrogels, regardless of their concentration, exhibited minimal swelling (swelling ratio < 3% at all timepoints) during 7 d of incubation under physiological condition (in PBS at 37 °C) (Figure 2h). This indicates the formation of internal networks through the self‐assembly of collagen fibrils via thermal incubation. Moreover, the MEM‐CA and MEM‐PG hydrogels are further crosslinked through covalent bonding (via Michael addition or Schiff‐base formation) and noncovalent bonding (hydrogen bond, π–π interaction, hydrophobic interaction) owing to the presence of phenolic moieties,[ 52 ] which is advantageous for maintaining hydrogel structure, eventually leading to long‐lasting and effective therapeutic efficacy. When the MEM, MEM‐CA, and MEM‐PG hydrogels were exposed to enzymatic treatment of collagenase (50 µg mL−1), 1% and 2% (w/v) MEM hydrogels completely degraded within 48 and 60 h, respectively, whereas 1% and 2% (w/v) MEM‐CA and MEM‐PG hydrogels exhibited more prolonged degradation profiles over 72 h (Figure 2i). We could speculate that the dual networks in the MEM‐CA and MEM‐PG hydrogels delay degradation under enzymatic hydrolysis compared to the unmodified MEM hydrogel with only single type of crosslinking via self‐assembled collagen fibrils.

Next, we compared the mechanical strength of each hydrogel by measuring storage modulus (G′) and loss modulus (G″) with a rheometer at a frequency sweep mode (frequency range of 0.1–10 Hz). Values of G′ were consistently higher than that of G″ in all hydrogels, indicating stable formation of polymeric networks in all hydrogels (Figure 2j). Elastic moduli of 2% (w/v) MEM‐CA (2020.83 ± 333.55 Pa) and 1% and 2% (w/v) MEM‐PG (1240.08 ± 461.38 and 2628.43 ± 351.54 Pa, respectively) hydrogels were significantly higher than those of 1% and 2% (w/v) MEM (103.29 ± 9.6 and 190.15 ± 14.07 Pa, respectively) (Figure 2k). These data suggest that the mechanical property of MEM‐CA and MEM‐PG hydrogels could be reinforced through dual networks of thermally induced assembly of collagen fibrils and oxidative crosslinking of phenolic moieties, resulting in long‐lasting hydrogels with structural integrity and durability compared to the MEM hydrogel with only thermal fibrillar assembly. In addition, the mechanical strength of the hydrogels could be further improved with increasing concentration. Based on these results, we chose to use 2% (w/v) for all hydrogels for further studies.

To determine the tissue adhesive property of MEM‐CA and MEM‐PG hydrogels, tissue adhesion to porcine skeletal muscle was measured using a rheometer in a tack test mode (Figure 2l). The maximum tissue adhesion forces of MEM‐CA and MEM‐PG hydrogels (1.79 ± 0.53 and 1.92 ± 0.11 kPa, respectively) were significantly higher than that of the MEM hydrogel (0.35 ± 0.19 kPa) (Figure 2m), underscoring the superior tissue adhesive property conferred by CA or PG conjugation. This implies that upon injection, the MEM‐CA and MEM‐PG hydrogels could adhere to muscle and maintain their position even during muscle movement (extension and flexion). As a result, these adhesive MEM hydrogels offer prolonged retention of muscle‐specific ECM to facilitate muscle regeneration at the targeted site, compared to the unmodified MEM hydrogel. In addition, we investigated the internal structure of the hydrogels including porosity and pore size using a scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and porosimeter. We found that all MEM, MEM‐CA, and MEM‐PG hydrogels had nanofibrillar architectures with >93% porosity, while exhibited different pore size distributions and morphology (Figure 2n,o). This indicates that internal structures of MEM‐CA and MEM‐PG hydrogels are formed via additional crosslinking induced by phenolic chemistry.

Oxidative stress elevates reactive oxygen species (ROS) formation, which causes impairment of neurotransmitter release and neuronal degeneration, leading to the loss of muscle mass and strength.[ 53 ] Moreover, sarcopenia is possibly caused by an increase in ROS formation in skeletal muscle since aging is known to be related to increasing ROS levels.[ 53 , 54 ] To investigate the ROS scavenging capability of MEM‐CA and MEM‐PG hydrogels, hydroxyl radical‐ and 2,2‐azino‐bis‐3‐ethylbenzothiazoline‐6‐sulfonic acid (ABTS) radical‐scavenging assays were conducted using Fenton reaction and colored ABTS radicals, respectively. The hydroxyl‐ and ABTS‐radical scavenging effects of MEM‐CA and MEM‐PG hydrogels were comparable to that of ascorbic acid, a positive control, whereas MEM hydrogel exhibited limited hydroxyl‐ and ABTS‐radical scavenging (Figure 2p,q). These results underline the exceptional antioxidant capacity of MEM‐CA and MEM‐PG hydrogels, capable of mitigating tissue damage and inflammation, thereby facilitating tissue regeneration. Altogether, these data suggest that the MEM‐CA and MEM‐PG hydrogels can offer potent therapeutic strategy for muscle atrophy through ease of injection with low extrusion force, enhanced mechanical and tissue adhesive properties, and ROS scavenging capability, along with providing muscle‐specific ECM cues.

Biocompatibility of MEM‐CA and MEM‐PG Hydrogels

For evaluation of in vitro biocompatibility, murine macrophage RAW 264.7 cells were co‐cultured with the hydrogels for 24 h, and the secreted levels of pro‐inflammatory cytokines, tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF‐α) and interleukin‐6 (IL‐6), were examined. Macrophages co‐cultured with MEM, MEM‐CA, and MEM‐PG hydrogels exhibited minimal immunoreactivity with TNF‐α and IL‐6 levels comparable to those of no treatment group (Figure S2a,b, Supporting Information). In contrast, macrophages treated with lipopolysaccharides (LPS), a well‐established pro‐inflammatory activator, demonstrated significantly elevated secretion levels of TNF‐α and IL‐6. In addition, the endotoxin level of the MEM‐CA and MEM‐PG hydrogels was 0.15 ± 0.02 and 0.24 ± 0.02 EU mL−1, respectively, which were below the threshold established by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for medical devices (Figure S2c, Supporting Information).[ 55 ]

To validate in vivo biocompatibility, the hydrogels were directly injected into the tibialis anterior (TA) muscle of mice, and subsequently monitored at intervals of 3, 7, and 14 d. Histological assessment using H&E and TB staining revealed no indication of tissue necrosis, fibrosis, or substantial damage to the skeletal muscle across all timepoints (Figure 3a). Three days post‐injection, a slight inflammatory response was detected in all groups. This response was attributed to cell recruitment at the injection site, likely due to the puncturing during the injection procedure. However, the recruited cells were noticeably diminished within 14 d post‐injection around the local area in the PBS, MEM, MEM‐CA, and MEM‐PG groups. In TB staining, mast cells were not detected around the site of injection. Remarkably, the MEM hydrogel within muscle tissue degraded over 14 d, while the MEM‐CA and MEM‐PG hydrogels were well‐maintained within muscle fibers, confirming their enhanced structural integrity and durability compared to the MEM hydrogel.

To further explore the immune modulation induced by MEM‐based hydrogels, the effect of MEM, MEM‐CA, and MEM‐PG hydrogels on macrophage polarization was evaluated in vitro by treating bone marrow‐derived naïve macrophages with these hydrogels. After 48 h of the hydrogel treatments, we observed a notable change in cell morphology, characterized by cell elongation and an increase in cell area (Figure S3a–c, Supporting Information), which are typically associated with M2 macrophage morphology. Furthermore, a significant increase in the expression of the M2 macrophage marker (CD206) was observed following the treatments with MEM, MEM‐CA, and MEM‐PG hydrogels (Figure S3a,d, Supporting Information), confirming the naïve macrophage polarization towards M2 phenotype. To confirm the M2 polarization in vivo, the immunostaining of macrophages at the injection site was performed. Macrophages were distinguished by the following markers: F4/80 for pan‐macrophage, inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) for M1 macrophage, and CD206 for M2 macrophage. Three days post‐injection, the recruited M1 macrophages (F4/80+ iNOS+) were notably observed in the MEM, MEM‐CA, MEM‐PG hydrogel groups, and PBS group (Figure 3b). The macrophages were recruited to the injection site due to a host's innate immune response against the exogenous materials (Figure 3b,c). The number of M1 macrophages was significantly reduced in all treated groups at 7 and 14 d post‐injection compared to those at day 3. Interestingly, the number of recruited M2 macrophages (F4/80+ CD206+) was rarely observed on day 3. However, over the course of the 14 d, the number of M2 macrophages in MEM, MEM‐CA, and MEM‐PG hydrogel groups substantially increased compared to those on day 3, displaying the inverse trend in the number of M1 and M2 macrophages at the injection site, which indicates the shift from a pro‐inflammatory to an anti‐inflammatory macrophage phenotype (Figure 3b,c). Even though the introduction of the hydrogels elicited a mild inflammatory response at early timepoints, it did not develop into severe inflammation that can possibly cause detrimental effects on the muscle tissue. In addition, we examined the level of key pro‐regenerative protein secreted from M2 macrophages, interleukin 10 (IL‐10) (Figure S4, Supporting Information). On day 14 post‐injection of PBS, MEM, MEM‐CA, MEM‐PG hydrogels, a significant increase in the level of IL‐10 was observed in MEM‐CA and MEM‐PG groups compared to PBS group. These results suggest that MEM‐CA and MEM‐PG hydrogels promoted polarization of macrophages towards M2 phenotype and these M2 macrophages secreted pro‐regenerative factors like IL‐10 to facilitate muscle regeneration. Overall, the polarization of pro‐inflammatory M1 macrophages into anti‐inflammatory M2 macrophages by phenolic MEM hydrogels suggests a transition of the inflammatory environment towards establishing a pro‐regenerative environment,[ 56 ] which would promote effective muscle regeneration.

Evaluation of Systemic Toxicity of MEM‐CA and MEM‐PG Hydrogels

In addition to the immune response at the local injection site, potential systemic toxicity induced by the MEM‐CA and MEM‐PG hydrogels was investigated. Histological assessment of major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) was performed after intramuscular injection of the hydrogels (Figure 4a). No signs of tissue damage or systemic inflammatory response within the organs were detected at 14 d post‐injection of MEM, MEM‐CA, and MEM‐PG hydrogels. The morphological features of these organs in the MEM, MEM‐CA, and MEM‐PG groups remained consistent with those of normal group, suggesting no adverse impact of the hydrogels on the major organs throughout the experimental periods. To examine any potential additional systemic effects of the hydrogels, hematological analysis was also conducted including complete blood count (CBC) and serum biochemical analysis (basic and comprehensive metabolic panels). At 14 d post‐injection, all components of CBC and blood chemistry panels for the MEM, MEM‐CA, and MEM‐PG groups showed no significant changes compared to those of normal healthy mice at all timepoints (Figure 4b). Specifically, the counts of white blood cells (WBC) and lymphocytes, the indicators of inflammation, in the PBS, MEM, MEM‐CA, and MEM‐PG groups, were comparable to those of normal mice. These results suggest that no detectable systemic inflammation was triggered by hydrogel injection. The examination of other hematological parameters, such as red blood cells (RBC), hemoglobin (HGB), hematocrit (HCT), mean corpuscular volume (MCV), mean corpuscular hemoglobin (MCH), mean corpuscular hemoglobin concentration (MCHC), and mean platelet volume (MPV), demonstrated no indication of hematological toxicity by hydrogel injection. In addition, no significant differences among all groups at all timepoint in the analysis of creatinine (CRE), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), glucose (GLU), and total protein (TP) levels confirmed that the hydrogels did not cause any impairment in kidney and liver functions. Altogether, in vitro and in vivo safety evaluations of MEM‐CA and MEM‐PG hydrogels confirmed high biocompatibility and low immunogenicity of the hydrogels, supported by evidence of negligible levels of inflammation and the absence of systemic and hematological toxicity, promising the application of MEM‐CA and MEM‐PG hydrogels as clinical treatment.

Muscle Regeneration by MEM‐CA and MEM‐PG Hydrogels in Disuse‐Induced Muscle Atrophy

Disuse (immobility)‐induced muscle atrophy, a common type of muscle atrophy, frequently occurs in clinical settings following limb immobilization, bed rest, spinal cord injury, and partial/complete peripheral nerve damage, resulting in significant loss of muscle mass and force production.[ 57 , 58 ] The therapeutic efficacy of MEM‐CA and MEM‐PG hydrogels was evaluated in a mouse model of skeletal muscle atrophy prepared by immobilization of the ankle flexor (TA muscle) of the hindlimb (Figure 5a).[ 59 ] Three weeks after hindlimb immobilization using surgical staples, the pre‐gel solutions of MEM, MEM‐CA, and MEM‐PG were intramuscularly injected into the TA muscles of mice. Subsequently, TA muscles were harvested at two weeks post‐injection for histological analysis (Figure 5b). The wet weights of TA muscles of MEM‐CA and MEM‐PG groups were significantly greater than that of the PBS group (Figure 5c), indicating that the MEM‐CA and MEM‐PG hydrogels increased muscle mass. Similarly, H&E staining revealed that the cross‐sectional area (CSA) of muscle fibers in the MEM‐PG group was significantly greater than those of the PBS and MEM groups (Figure 5b,d), and the muscles treated with MEM‐PG showed regeneration comparable to the normal muscle. Though not statistically significant, the CSA of MEM‐CA group was also greater than those of PBS and MEM groups. In addition, the fiber sizes with central nuclei were measured by quantifying the CSA of muscle fibers with centralized nuclei, based on H&E‐stained images. (Figure S5, Supporting Information). Fibers with centralized nuclei showed a significant increase in the MEM‐PG group compared to the PBS group. This result indicates that the administration of MEM‐PG hydrogel accelerated muscle regeneration process in muscle atrophy. Immunostaining against dystrophin, myosin heavy chain (MYH), and laminin (LM) revealed that the MEM‐CA and MEM‐PG hydrogels promoted muscle regeneration with mature myofibers and correct laminin deposition, suggesting that the structural and functional integrity of the regenerated muscle was significantly enhanced by MEM‐CA and MEM‐PG hydrogels compared to the PBS and MEM groups (Figure 5b,e,f). In addition, to ensure that the components remaining in the MEM did not contribute directly to the increase in MYH, we evaluated the expression of MYH in mouse muscle tissues injected with MEM‐CA and MEM‐PG hydrogels (Figure S6, Supporting Information). Immunofluorescence staining revealed no detectable expression of MYH within the hydrogels. As MEM‐CA and MEM‐PG are synthesized following 48 hours of pepsin digestion of MEM, the MYH is likely to degrade into smaller fragments.[ 60 ] Therefore, the elevated level of MYH within the harvested muscle is assumed to be induced with muscle regeneration rather than the presence of components in MEM hydrogels. MEM‐CA and MEM‐PG groups also showed significantly higher expression of acetylcholine receptors (AchR) compared to the PBS group (Figure 5g), indicating improved innervation of muscle fibers for functional restoration in MEM‐CA and MEM‐PG groups. Interestingly, even though the MEM hydrogel provided muscle‐specific ECM, the MEM hydrogel without modification was unable to induce significant muscle regeneration possibly due to its rapid degradation.

To confirm muscle regeneration by MEM‐CA and MEM‐PG hydrogels, quantitative real‐time polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) analysis was performed to evaluate the mRNA expression levels of myogenic differentiation 1 (MyoD1), a marker of early myogenic differentiation, and neonatal myosin heavy chain (Myh8), which is expressed during muscle development and regeneration,[ 61 ] in the disuse‐induced muscle atrophy model (Figure S7, Supporting Information). The qPCR results indicated that the expression level of MyoD1 was significantly lower in the MEM, MEM‐CA, and MEM‐PG hydrogel groups than in the PBS group, suggesting that these MEM hydrogel groups have progressed past the early stage of muscle regeneration.[ 62 , 63 ] In contrast, the mRNA expression level of Myh8, a marker associated with the later stage of muscle regeneration,[ 64 ] was significantly elevated in the MEM‐CA and MEM‐PG hydrogel groups. These results suggest that the MEM‐CA and MEM‐PG groups have entered the later stage of muscle regeneration, characterized by the increase in muscle fiber size, mature myofiber, and correct laminin deposition shown in Figure 5b–g.

To further validate these findings, the levels of oxidative phosphorylation (OxPhos) proteins were evaluated using Western blot analysis in TA muscle two weeks after injection of MEM‐based hydrogels into a mouse model of disuse‐induced muscle atrophy. As shown in Figure S8 (Supporting Information), we observed an overall trend of increased expression in OxPhos proteins tested—ATP synthase subunit F1 alpha (ATP5A), mitochondrial cytochrome c oxidase subunit 1 (MTCO1), succinate dehydrogenase subunit B (SDHB), and NADH oxidoreductase subunit B8 (NDUFB8)—across the MEM, MEM‐CA, and MEM‐PG groups, with significant increases in ATP5A and MTCO1 in the MEM‐PG group compared to the PBS group. Specifically, the significant increase in ATP5A, a key component of the ATP synthase complex, suggests improved ATP production and restored mitochondrial energy metabolism in the MEM‐PG treated muscles.[ 65 , 66 ] The increase in MTCO1, a critical subunit of Complex IV, indicates enhanced oxidative phosphorylation and mitochondrial respiration following MEM‐PG injection. While the increases in SDHB (Complex II) and NDUFB8 (Complex I) were not statistically significant, the upregulated trends in their expression suggest that the MEM‐CA and MEM‐PG hydrogels may contribute to the recovery of overall mitochondrial function. Our results demonstrate that MEM‐PG has a more pronounced effect on the recovery of mitochondrial function, particularly by enhancing ATP synthesis and Complex IV activity. MEM‐CA also showed positive trends, although the effects were not as strong as MEM‐PG. The observed increases in these key mitochondrial proteins support the therapeutic potential of both hydrogels in improving mitochondrial function and promoting recovery in disuse‐induced muscle atrophy. In sum, we conclude that MEM‐CA and MEM‐PG hydrogels promoted muscle regeneration to address disuse‐induced muscle atrophy by establishing a persistent muscle ECM microenvironment supplemented with antioxidant capacity at the targeted site.

Muscle‐Specific MEM‐CA and MEM‐PG Hydrogels for Muscle Regeneration in Disuse‐Induced Muscle Atrophy

Given the distinct biophysical and biochemical characteristics of tissue‐specific ECM, we compared the tissue‐specificity of MEM and skin extracellular matrix (SkEM) in terms of proteomic profiles and muscle regeneration efficacy in a mouse model of disuse‐induced muscle atrophy.[ 67 , 68 ] A comprehensive proteomic exploration of MEM and SkEM revealed substantial variation in ECM types and protein abundance (Figure 6a). Particularly, SkEM was predominantly comprised of collagen type 1, along with other collagen types, such as collagen type 3, 4, 5, and 6, which mimics the native skin ECM (Figure S9a, Supporting Information).[ 69 ] In MEM, 27 matrisome proteins and 47 muscle‐enriched proteins were detected, while 16 matrisome proteins were identified with the absence of any muscle‐enriched proteins in SkEM (Figure 6b,c). A principal component analysis (PCA) further highlighted the substantial variability in biochemical composition between MEM and SkEM (Figure 6d). These findings suggest that when compared with SkEM, MEM offers a greater diversity of matrisome proteins, specifically collagens and glycoproteins, and various muscle‐specific proteins that assist in accurately recapitulating native muscle ECM microenvironment.

Further proteomic exploration of MEM revealed 114 exclusively expressed proteins in MEM, which were not present in SkEM (Figure 6e). The GO enrichment analysis of the 114 exclusively expressed proteins showed that they are associated with various key muscle processes such as muscle system process, muscle contraction, muscle development, and muscle cell differentiation (Figure 6f), suggesting that MEM encompasses muscle‐specific proteins fundamental for muscle growth and function. On the other hand, 22 proteins solely found in SkEM had no enriched GO biological process related to muscle function. When differentially expressed proteins in MEM and SkEM were identified using a threshold of p < 0.05 and fold change < 0.5 or > 2, 4 upregulated proteins and 14 downregulated proteins were identified in MEM (Figure 6g and Figure S9b, Supporting Information). Notably, BGN and LUM, which are crucial in muscle regeneration and protection, were significantly upregulated in MEM compared with SkEM. Furthermore, the GO term associated with muscle tissue development (GO:0060537) indicates that MEM has significantly more enriched proteins involved in muscle development than SkEM (Figure 6h). Altogether, these results suggest that MEM encompasses a variety of both matrisome and non‐matrisome proteins crucial for muscle development and functionality, providing muscle‐specific biochemical and biophysical cues.

We further examined therapeutic efficacy of CA‐ and PG‐functionalized SkEM hydrogels (SkEM‐CA and SkEM‐PG, respectively) and MEM‐CA and MEM‐PG hydrogels in a mouse model of disuse‐induced muscle atrophy. Upon intramuscular injection of the hydrogels, we found that the MEM‐PG group exhibited significantly greater weight of TA muscle and larger CSA of muscle fibers than PBS and SkEM‐CA groups (Figure 6i–k). MEM‐CA group exhibited significantly greater CSA of muscle fibers compared to PBS group (Figure 6i–k). Furthermore, the expression of MYH was significantly elevated in both the MEM‐CA and MEM‐PG groups compared to the PBS, SkEM‐CA, and SkEM‐PG groups (Figure 6i,l). Similarly, the expression of LM was notably higher in the MEM‐CA and MEM‐PG groups than in the PBS and SkEM‐CA groups (Figure 6i,m). Remarkably, the presence of mature myofibers was exclusively observed in the MEM‐CA and MEM‐PG groups. Taken together, these findings demonstrate that MEM‐CA and MEM‐PG hydrogels possess tissue‐specificity, which is crucial to promote muscle synthesis, and can offer a unique therapeutic hydrogel platform for treating various muscle loss‐related conditions.

Muscle Regeneration by MEM‐CA and MEM‐PG Hydrogels in Age‐Induced Muscle Atrophy

To evaluate therapeutic efficacy of MEM‐CA and MEM‐PG in sarcopenia, the pre‐gel solutions of MEM, MEM‐CA, and MEM‐PG were intramuscularly injected into the TA muscles of 18‐month‐old mice. This age was chosen because indicators associated with aging (e.g., muscle volume, muscle mass, muscle strength, and endurance) are primarily detected, typically after at least 18 months.[ 70 , 71 , 72 , 73 ] Then, the functional analysis of recording electromyography (EMG) of the TA muscles was performed weekly, and the TA muscles were retrieved at 8 weeks post‐injection for histological analysis (Figure 7a). H&E and immunohistochemical staining revealed that the muscle fibers in Saline group exhibited disrupted muscle fiber integrity due to abundant connective tissue surrounding the fibers (indicated with black arrows) (Figure 7b). The CSA values of muscle fibers in MEM‐CA and MEM‐PG groups were significantly larger than those in both the Saline and MEM groups (Figure 7c). In addition, the CSA distribution in the MEM‐CA and MEM‐PG groups showed a tendency to have more instances of larger CSA values compared to the Saline and MEM groups (Figure 7d). Immunostaining against dystrophin, MYH, and LM revealed that MEM‐CA and MEM‐PG hydrogels remarkably promoted mature muscle regeneration (Figure 7b,e,f). The expression of desmin (DES), which is a key subunit of the intermediate filament in skeletal muscles, was significantly higher in MEM‐CA and MEM‐PG groups than in the Saline and MEM groups (Figure 7b,g). These results suggest that sarcopenic muscles treated with MEM‐CA and MEM‐PG hydrogels exhibited differentiated myotubes of mature muscle. Moreover, the MEM‐PG group showed significantly higher expression of AchR compared to all other groups (Figure 7h), indicating effective re‐innervation of muscle fibers in the MEM‐PG group. Interestingly, the expressions of DES and AchR were significantly higher in MEM‐PG group than in MEM‐CA group, in which we speculate that the retention of the MEM‐CA hydrogel might be less persistent in the loose muscle bundles of sarcopenic muscles due to spreading, as compared with MEM‐PG hydrogel.

To conduct EMG recording of the TA muscles, a non‐invasive 3D‐liquid‐metal electrode was applied to the muscle,[ 74 ] and EMG signals were recorded while hydrogel‐treated hindlimb was moved to have flexion and extension (Figure 7i). The EMG signals in all groups diminished over time, yet those in the MEM‐CA and MEM‐PG groups persisted longer duration (Figure 7j). This prolonged signal duration indicates the promotion of muscle regeneration by MEM‐CA and MEM‐PG hydrogels. One week post‐injection, the peak‐to‐peak amplitude of the EMG signal from the MEM, MEM‐CA, and MEM‐PG groups was significantly higher than that from the Saline group, maintaining this difference for up to the three weeks (Figure 7k). Both the MEM‐CA and MEM‐PG groups exhibited significantly higher peak‐to‐peak amplitudes of the EMG signal compared to the Saline and MEM groups from 3 to 6 weeks. Similarly, the root‐mean‐square (RMS) of the EMG signal from the MEM‐CA and MEM‐PG groups was significantly higher than that from the Saline and MEM groups from 3‐week post‐injection, suggesting notable improvement in functional muscle performance (Figure 7l). Such improvement could be attributed to the successful re‐innervation of muscle fibers following MEM‐CA and MEM‐PG hydrogels injection.

The peak in muscle force observed at 3‐week post‐injection is likely associated with the optimal phase of hydrogel retention and their initial effects on muscle regeneration. At this stage, the hydrogels effectively support muscle repair by enhancing fiber size and muscle mass, leading to higher functional force output. However, gradual degradation of the hydrogels over time results in a reduction in their regenerative impact. This degradation of the hydrogels, combined with the inherent challenges of aging, may contribute to the observed decline in muscle force over time, despite the relative increase in fiber size at 8 weeks. To address the transient nature of these effects, future studies should explore the administration of multiple hydrogel injections at 3‐week intervals to sustain the peak functional force over a longer period. Alternatively, developing novel formulations of MEM‐CA and MEM‐PG with enhanced stability could prolong their regenerative effects, potentially mitigating the decline in muscle function observed in the later stages.

At 8 weeks, hematological (serum biochemical panels; BUN, AST, TP, and GLU) and histological (H&E staining of retrieved major organs; heart, liver, spleen, kidney, and lung) analyses demonstrated no detectable inflammatory response in the MEM, MEM‐CA, and MEM‐PG groups (Figure S10a,b, Supporting Information). In conclusion, we find that the MEM‐PG hydrogel is highly effective for promoting muscle regeneration in sarcopenia by providing a long‐lasting muscle ECM microenvironment at the targeted site.

Proteomics Analysis of Regenerated Muscles in Age‐Induced Muscle Atrophy

To elucidate underlying mechanisms on therapeutic efficacy of MEM‐CA and MEM‐PG hydrogels in sarcopenia model, we performed an extensive proteomics analysis on the regenerated muscles treated with MEM‐CA and MEM‐PG hydrogels. Using the PANTHER classification system, the TA muscle proteomes from all groups were classified into 19 distinct protein classes (Figure 8a). In all groups, the proteins were predominantly characterized as metabolite interconversion enzymes, comprising 35%, 35.3%, 35.3%, and 33% in the Saline, MEM, MEM‐CA, and MEM‐PG groups, respectively. The dominant proportion of these enzymes reflects the crucial function of skeletal muscle in energy metabolism and maintenance of metabolic homeostasis, including glucose and lipid metabolism.[ 75 , 76 , 77 ] Following metabolite interconversion enzymes, cytoskeletal proteins, contributing to mechanical integrity and contractile properties of skeletal muscles,[ 78 ] constituted the second most abundant category, making up 10% in the Saline, 12% in MEM, 11.4% in MEM‐CA, and 11.7% in MEM‐PG groups. Interestingly, translational proteins showed relatively higher abundance in the MEM‐CA (5.2%) and MEM‐PG (5.2%) groups compared to the Saline (3.9%) and MEM (2%) groups, indicating variation in protein functional classes in MEM‐CA and MEM‐PG groups. In addition, PCA revealed that the proteomes of Saline and MEM groups were closely clustered, while the MEM‐CA and MEM‐PG groups were distinct from both the Saline and MEM groups (Figure 8b). This observation implies greater proteomic similarity between the MEM‐CA and MEM‐PG groups compared to the other groups. These findings suggest that MEM‐CA and MEM‐PG injections may induce protein alterations in the aged muscles, leading to distinct proteomic profiles from those of the Saline and MEM groups. We then identified differentially expressed proteins between the Saline group and the hydrogel (MEM, MEM‐CA, and MEM‐PG) injection groups (Figure 8c). Comparing the MEM and MEM‐CA groups with the Saline group, five and four differentially expressed proteins were identified, respectively. However, these proteins did not show any statistically significant functions. Regarding the MEM‐PG group in comparison to the Saline group, six differentially expressed proteins were identified. Intriguingly, GO enrichment analysis of the four upregulated proteins indicated a strong association with skeletal muscle contraction and the muscle system process (Figure 8c). Aging often leads to muscle system deterioration due to various factors such as changes in muscle mass, composition, fiber types, and dysfunctional motor units and neuromuscular junctions.[ 79 ] These results confirm that MEM‐PG injection in aged muscles contributes to the rejuvenation of muscle protein components and enhanced muscle function in sarcopenia.

Then, we searched for the uniquely expressed proteins in each hydrogel group compared to the Saline group and analyzed with GO enrichment analysis. In the MEM group, the uniquely expressed proteins did not entail any biological processes associated with muscle development or function. This finding is consistent with the analysis of differentially expressed proteins of the MEM group (Figure 8c), which also revealed no functional relevance to muscle development. This suggests minimal effects induced by MEM after 8 weeks likely due to its rapid degradation. In contrast, the MEM‐CA group exhibited exclusive proteins that demonstrate a strong association with muscle cell differentiation, muscle cell development, protein synthesis, and cytoplasmic translation (Figure 8d). As for the MEM‐PG group, the exclusively identified proteins were highly involved in muscle cell development and peptide biosynthesis (Figure 8e). Based on these comparative analyses, we verified that both MEM‐CA and MEM‐PG groups were significantly enriched in muscle cell development and protein/peptide biosynthesis, displaying superior muscle regeneration effects compared to the MEM group. The elevated protein synthesis and muscle cell development in the MEM‐CA and MEM‐PG groups could contribute to the alleviation of muscle loss and rejuvenation of muscle tissue.

To ascertain superior muscle regenerative ability of the MEM‐PG hydrogel to other hydrogels as shown in Figure 7, the exclusive proteins in MEM‐PG group were assessed against the MEM and MEM‐CA groups. Uniquely expressed proteins in the MEM‐PG group compared to the MEM group were involved with various key biological processes integral to muscle regeneration, including muscle structure development, cytoplasmic translation, striated muscle cell differentiation, and peptide biosynthesis (Figure S11a, Supporting Information). Proteins only expressed in the MEM‐PG group compared to the MEM‐CA group were also similarly enriched in cytoplasmic translation and peptide biosynthesis (Figure S11b, Supporting Information). Based on these findings above, we further scrutinized the proteins exclusively present in the MEM‐PG group among all aged mice and identified 42 distinct proteins, and the functional annotation of these proteins with GO enrichment analysis revealed that these were predominantly associated with peptide biosynthesis and protein synthesis (Figure S11c, Supporting Information). In alignment with all comparisons, the MEM‐PG group consistently demonstrated notable enrichment in protein synthesis, a biological process vital for muscle recovery in sarcopenia. Although the MEM‐CA group also demonstrated active muscle regeneration, the MEM‐PG group exhibited remarkable muscle recovery compared to both MEM and MEM‐CA groups. These findings highlight the critical role of the MEM‐PG hydrogel in accelerating protein synthesis, which may substantially contribute to skeletal muscle regeneration.

Finally, we evaluated the enrichment level of various GO biological processes that are essential in facilitating muscle regeneration in all groups. The GO biological processes revealed that muscle cell and tissue development and differentiation biological processes were relatively more enriched in the MEM‐CA group and especially in the MEM‐PG group compared to the Saline group, while the MEM group was similar to the Saline group (Figure 8f). This result points out that the MEM‐CA and MEM‐PG groups were more actively involved in overall muscle cell and tissue development with prominent enrichment in muscle development processes such as muscle cell development and differentiation, muscle structure development, muscle tissue and organ development, and muscle cell cellular homeostasis, which are commonly found in young muscle tissues undergoing regeneration (Figure 8f). Thus, we postulate that functional proteins associated with those processes in the muscle‐specific ECM contribute to effective muscle regeneration in aged muscles. Furthermore, the biosynthetic processes of proteins and peptides showed notable overrepresentation in the MEM‐CA and MEM‐PG groups, further supporting augmented protein synthesis following the injection of MEM‐CA and MEM‐PG hydrogels. Finally, GO enrichment of cellular response to ROS and response to oxidative stress among all groups revealed that such cellular responses were relatively overrepresented in the Saline and MEM groups compared to the MEM‐CA and MEM‐PG groups (Figure 8f), suggesting antioxidant effects of MEM‐CA and MEM‐PG hydrogels. Based on these results, we propose that the MEM‐CA and MEM‐PG hydrogels promoted muscle regeneration in sarcopenia by elevating protein synthesis, activating muscle‐related developments, and mitigating oxidative stresses.

After 6–8 weeks of saline and hydrogel injections, histological, functional, and proteomic analyses demonstrated that the MEM group had no significant regenerative effects, comparable to the Saline group. This outcome may be attributed to the rapid degradation of the MEM hydrogel within a few days, thereby limiting sustained regenerative effects. In contrast, MEM‐CA and MEM‐PG hydrogels with reinforced mechanical integrity and tissue adhesive properties promoted and extended functional muscle regeneration, evident through histological, functional, and proteomic analyses (Figures 7 and 8). Collectively, our work highlights that MEM‐CA and MEM‐PG hydrogels establish a prolonged muscle‐like microenvironment, with substantial retention of muscle ECM proteins to rejuvenate muscle tissue and facilitate muscle repair without the need for cells or drugs. While this study employed a one‐time injection, adjusting parameters like repeated injections or incorporating stem cells or drugs could potentially enhance therapeutic efficacy. This emphasizes the potential of these hydrogels as therapeutic applications for recovering from the loss of muscle mass and function associated with sarcopenia.

Conclusion

In this study, we successfully developed adhesive MEM‐based hydrogels, MEM‐CA and MEM‐PG. These engineered ECM hydrogels provide muscle‐specific biochemical and biophysical signals as well as muscle‐like microenvironment to promote muscle regeneration. Remarkably, both MEM‐CA and MEM‐PG hydrogels exhibited great mechanical robustness and tissue adhesion, thereby achieving localized and controlled manner over an extended duration. With high biosafety and biocompatibility, MEM‐CA and MEM‐PG hydrogels effectively induced muscle regeneration with functional improvement in mouse models of disuse‐ and age‐induced muscle atrophy. Here, we present evidence of their potential for muscle regeneration and functional recovery demonstrating great promise for translational applications in the realm of muscle loss‐related diseases.

Experimental Section

Materials

NHS, PG, and CA were purchased from Sigma‐Aldrich (St. Louis, MO). EDC was obtained from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA). All other chemicals used were of analytical grade.

Preparation of Muscle Extracellular Matrix

To prepare MEM, femoris muscle from a four‐month‐old female Yorkshire pig (purchased from XP BIO Inc., Anseong, Korea) was used. Briefly, the femoris muscle was diced into small pieces and washed with triple deionized water (TDW) to remove any blood. Then, the muscle tissue underwent a series of treatments: 0.2% (v/v) trypsin‐ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA; Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 3 h, 0.5% (v/v) Triton X‐100 (Sigma‐Aldrich) for 12 h, and a mixed solution of 1% (v/v) Triton X‐100 and 0.2% (w/v) sodium deoxycholate (Sigma‐Aldrich) for 22 h. The decellularized muscle tissues were then thoroughly rinsed with TDW and sterilized with 1% (v/v) penicillin‐streptomycin (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 2 h. The decellularization process was conducted at a constant temperature of 4 °C with continuous mechanical agitation, except during the trypsin‐EDTA treatment, which occurred at 37 °C. Finally, the sterilized MEM was lyophilized and stored at 4 °C until further use.

Confirmation of Decellularization

All biochemical assessments for confirmation of decellularization were conducted using standard comparative analyses between native and decellularized tissue matrices as commonly performed in the research of decellularization.[ 67 , 80 , 81 ] The dsDNA present in both native muscle tissues and MEM was extracted using TaKaRa MiniBEST Universal Genomic DNA extraction kit (Takara, Shiga, Japan) and quantified using the Quant‐iT PicoGreen dsDNA Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) following the manufacturer's instructions. The GAG contents in both native tissues and MEM were evaluated with 1,9‐dimethyl‐methylene blue (DMMB, Sigma‐Aldrich) assay. Both tissues were solubilized with phosphate buffered EDTA (PBE) buffer containing papain from papaya latex (Sigma‐Aldrich) and N‐Acetyl‐l‐cysteine (Sigma‐Aldrich) at 60 °C overnight. Chondroitin sulfate (Sigma‐Aldrich) was used as a reference standard. DMMB was added and the absorbance at 525 nm was measured using a Tecan microplate reader (Männedorf, Switzerland). Collagen contents in both native tissues and MEM were determined using Sircol Soluble Collagen Assay (Biocolor, Carrickfergus, UK) according to the manufacturer's guidelines. Histological analyses of the native muscle tissues and MEM were also conducted. Both native tissues and MEM were fixed with 10% neutral buffered formalin (Sigma‐Aldrich) and embedded in paraffin blocks. The tissue‐embedded blocks were sectioned into 6 µm and subjected to H&E, MT, and TB staining.

Synthesis of MEM‐CA and MEM‐PG

All MEM materials were synthesized from the one porcine donor to minimize the batch‐to‐batch variability. CA (or PG) was conjugated to MEM using EDC/NHS chemistry. Briefly, MEM was dissolved in a pepsin/hydrochloric acid (HCl) solution at a concentration of 10 mg mL−1 overnight, and added to 0.1 m 2‐(N‐morpholino)ethanesulfonic acid (MES) buffer dropwise. The pH of the MEM solution was adjusted to 3.5. EDC and NHS were then added to the MEM solution at EDC:NHS:CA (or PG) molar ratio of 1:1:1, while CA (or PG) was added to the MEM solution at a weight ratio of 5:1. The resulting mixture was dialyzed to remove excess chemicals using a membrane with a molecular weight cut‐off of 6–8 kDa (Membrane Filtration Products Inc., Seguin, TX). The products were lyophilized and stored at −20 °C. The conjugation of CA and PG to MEM was confirmed by 1H‐NMR spectroscopy (Bruker, Billerica, MA).

Proteomics Analysis of ECMs, ECM Hydrogels, and Tissues

To evaluate the protein composition and its abundance in the samples, the protein intensity‐based absolute quantification (iBAQ) was converted into relative intensity‐based absolute quantification (riBAQ). The riBAQ of a protein was obtained by dividing the iBAQ of a protein over the total iBAQ of all proteins within the sample, representing the relative abundance of the protein in the sample. The proteomes of human muscle tissue and SkEM were derived from publicly available datasets and utilized for comparative analysis with porcine MEM.[ 67 , 82 ] Matrisome proteins and muscle‐enriched proteins, which were expressed fourfold higher compared to other tissues, were categorized using the MIT Matrisome Project database and the Human Protein Atlas, respectively.[ 30 , 46 , 83 ] Protein functional classes were classified with the PANTHER Classification system.[ 84 , 85 ] Functional annotation of proteins was performed using GO enrichment analysis, using the PANTHER Overrepresentation test to identify significant GO biological processes.[ 84 , 86 , 87 ]

For the tissue ECMs and hydrogels (MEM, MEM‐CA, and MEM‐PG hydrogels), GO biological processes were determined by referencing Sus scrofa using GO Ontology database released on 2021.11.16 [https://zenodo.org/record/5725227]. Regarding aged mouse muscle tissues, GO biological processes were identified by searching against Mus musculus using GO Ontology database released on 2022.07.01 [https://zenodo.org/records/6799722] and 2023.05.10 [https://zenodo.org/records/7942786]. For both enrichment tests, Fisher's exact statistical test was performed with false discovery rate (FDR) correction, and a threshold of FDR less than 0.05 was considered indicative of significantly enriched GO biological processes.

To investigate protein–protein interactions and protein networks, Cytoscape and STRING were employed, respectively.[ 88 , 89 ] GO terms were searched with QuickGO.[ 90 ] The summarization of GO biological processes was visualized using GOfigure, and a heatmap was generated using seaborn library.[ 91 , 92 ] PCA was carried out with scikit‐learn library.[ 93 ]

In Vivo Biocompatibility Test of MEM, MEM‐CA, and MEM‐PG Hydrogels

In vivo biocompatibility of MEM, MEM‐CA, and MEM‐PG hydrogels was evaluated with the approval of Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Yonsei University with the permit number IACUC‐A‐202207‐1496‐02. Male BALB/c mice (20 g, 6 weeks old; Orient Bio, Seongnam, Korea) were randomly distributed into four groups as follows: PBS, MEM, MEM‐CA, and MEM‐PG. Prior to hydrogel implantation, mice were anesthetized via intramuscular injection of ketamine (100 mg kg−1; Yuhan, Seoul, Korea) and xylazine (20 mg kg−1; Bayer Korea, Ansan Korea). Hair on their legs was removed, and then 50 µL of PBS and pre‐gel solution of 2% (w/v) MEM, 2% (w/v) MEM‐CA, and 2% (w/v) MEM‐PG were injected into the TA muscle. On days 3, 7, and 14, mice from each group were sacrificed by CO2 inhalation. Subsequently, their TA muscles, livers, kidneys, hearts, spleens, and lungs were harvested and immediately fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin (Sigma‐Aldrich) for histological and immunohistochemical analyses. Blood samples were also collected via cardiac puncture for hematological and serum biochemical analyses. For hematological analysis, a CBC was conducted using HemaVet 950 (Drew Scientific, Miami Lakes, FL), and serum biochemical profiles, including BUN, CRE, AST, ALT, GLU, and TP, were evaluated using the DRI‐CHEM 4000i (Fuji Film, Tokyo, Japan).

Upon fixation in 10% neutral buffered formalin (Sigma‐Aldrich), the tissues were subjected to dehydration, followed by embedding in paraffin blocks. The paraffin embedded specimens were cut cross‐sectionally at a thickness of 6 µm. For immunohistochemical staining, tissue sections on slides were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and treated with citrate buffer (Sigma‐Aldrich) for antigen retrieval. Then, the slides were permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X‐100 (Sigma‐Aldrich) for 30 min and blocked using a solution containing 2% horse serum (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and 2% bovine serum albumin (BSA; MP Biomedicals, Irvine, CA) for 90 min. The slides were subsequently incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies: anti‐F4/80 rat monoclonal antibody (Abcam, Cambridge, UK), anti‐CD206 rabbit polyclonal antibody (Abcam), and anti‐iNOS mouse monoclonal antibody (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Dallas, TX), and anti‐IL‐10 rat monoclonal antibody (Abcam). After the overnight incubation, the slides were washed three times with PBS and then stained with secondary antibodies: Alexa fluor 488 goat anti‐rat (Thermo Fisher Scientific), Alexa fluor 594 goat anti‐mouse (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and Alexa fluor 594 goat anti‐rabbit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 1 h at room temperature. After washing three times with PBS, the slides were counterstained with 2‐(4‐amidinophenyl) indole‐6‐carboxamidine dihydrochloride (DAPI, TCI, Tokyo, Japan) for nuclei, mounted with Vectashield mounting medium (Vector Laboratories, Newark, CA), and visualized using LSM900 confocal microscopy (Carl Zeiss, Jena, Germany). The number of F4/80+ iNOS+ and F4/80+ CD206+ cells in the muscle fibers was quantified using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD).

Mouse Model Study of Disuse‐Induced Muscle Atrophy

The therapeutic efficacy of MEM‐CA and MEM‐PG hydrogels was evaluated in a mouse model of muscle loss induced by joint immobilization, resulting muscle atrophy. The immobilization of the TA muscle was achieved by restricting joint movement. All protocols were approved by the IACUC of Yonsei University under permit number IACUC‐A‐202107‐1288‐01, IACUC‐A‐202203‐1440‐01, and IACUC‐202407‐1875‐02. In brief, male six‐week‐old C57BL/6 mice were anesthetized and the hair on the left leg was removed. Then, the left foot exploiting normal dorsotibial flexion was stapled using a 35 W skin stapler (Teleflex, Morrisville, NC). The other hindlimb was used as the control. After three weeks of immobilization, pre‐gel solutions of MEM, MEM‐CA, and MEM‐PG were intramuscularly injected into the TA muscle. Two weeks post‐injection, TA muscle was harvested and weighed. Then, TA muscle tissues were fixed and embedded in paraffin blocks. For histological evaluation, tissue sections were stained with H&E. For histological quantification, four images were randomly selected from different regions of the tissue for each animal, ensuring a representative assessment of the entire tissue cross‐section. All images were analyzed using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health). To accurately separate the background from specific staining, the color deconvolution tool in ImageJ was used, which allowed for selective analysis of specific stains. CSA measurement was conducted by manually outlining the boundaries of the muscle fibers in the H&E‐stained histological images. The size of muscle fiber with central nuclei was quantified by measuring the cross‐sectional areas of muscle fibers with centralized nuclei using ImageJ software, based on H&E‐stained images. Tissue sections for immunohistochemical analysis were stained with anti‐dystrophin rabbit polyclonal antibody (Abcam), anti‐myosin heavy chain (MYH) mouse monoclonal antibody (Developmental Studies Hybridoma Bank, Iowa City, IA), anti‐laminin (LM) rabbit polyclonal antibody (Sigma‐Aldrich), and anti‐nicotinic acetylcholine receptor alpha/CHRNA (AchR) mouse monoclonal antibody (Santa Cruz Biotechnology). Then, the slides were stained with secondary antibodies as follows: Alexa fluor 488 goat anti‐mouse (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and Alexa fluor 594 goat anti‐rabbit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The cell nuclei were counterstained using DAPI (Vector Laboratories). Stained tissue sections were visualized through LSM900 confocal microscopy (Carl Zeiss). MYH‐ and AchR‐positive regions within the muscle fibers were quantified using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health).

Mouse Model Study of Age‐Induced Muscle Atrophy

The therapeutic efficacy of MEM‐CA and MEM‐PG hydrogels was evaluated in a mouse model of sarcopenia. Sarcopenia, characterized by muscle loss, becomes prominent in mice around 60 weeks of age.[ 94 ] In this study, female 72‐week‐old mice that exhibited significant muscle loss were used. All protocols were approved by the IACUC of Yonsei University under permit number IACUC‐A‐202206‐1491‐01. Briefly, female 72‐week‐old C57BL/6 mice were anesthetized and the hair on their left leg was removed. The pre‐gel solutions of 2% (w/v) MEM, MEM‐CA, and MEM‐PG were then injected into the TA muscle. EMG signals from the TA muscles were recorded on a weekly basis for a duration of eight weeks post‐injection. Eight weeks post‐injection, TA muscles were harvested and processed for histological analysis (H&E and immunohistochemical analyses), following the same preparation steps as previously established. Tissue sections for immunohistochemical analysis were stained with anti‐MYH mouse monoclonal antibody (Developmental Studies Hybridoma Bank), LM rabbit polyclonal antibody (Sigma‐Aldrich), anti‐desmin (DES) rabbit polyclonal antibody (Abcam), and anti‐AchR mouse monoclonal antibody (Santa Cruz Biotechnology). Then, the slides were stained with secondary antibodies as follows: Alexa fluor 488 goat anti‐mouse (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and Alexa fluor 594 goat anti‐rabbit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The cell nuclei were counterstained using DAPI (Vector Laboratories). Stained tissue sections were visualized through LSM900 confocal microscopy (Carl Zeiss). MYH‐, LM‐, DES‐, and AchR‐positive regions within the muscle fibers were quantified using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health).

Statistical Analysis

All values are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation. Quantitative data are presented with statistical significance determined by the Student's t‐test or one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with a post hoc Tukey's multiple comparison test using GraphPad Prism 9 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA). Values of p < 0.01 and p < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.
