Reframing the “mild” in mild traumatic brain injury: A comprehensive narrative review of diagnosis and treatment paradigms
Authors: Shibu Sasidharan, François Mathieu, Nidhi Patil, Puja Rani, Sunali Nanayakkara, Sean Y. W. Tan, Juranuch Rojprasitporn
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Mild traumatic brain injury (mTBI) constitutes a pervasive yet often overlooked public health challenge with far-reaching implications for individual well-being and healthcare systems globally. This review synthesizes contemporary evidence on diagnosis, management, and emerging treatment paradigms for mTBI using a structured, comprehensive literature search strategy. Through comprehensive literature analysis spanning multiple databases—PubMed, Cochrane Library, EMBASE, and Web of Science—from January 2018 to January 2025. A total of 183 studies met inclusion criteria, comprising 42 systematic reviews and meta-analyses, 58 randomized controlled trials, and 83 observational studies. We have identified transformative advances in biomarker research, neuroimaging techniques, and targeted interventions while uncovering persistent challenges in standardizing diagnostic and treatment protocols. Recent randomized controlled trials and meta-analyses reveal promising outcomes for early intervention strategies, cognitive rehabilitation approaches, and novel pharmacological treatments. However, methodological limitations—including heterogeneous mTBI definitions, inconsistent outcome measures, and insufficient long-term follow-up—underscore the need for large-scale trials using standardized definitions and common data elements. Future research must prioritize personalized treatment approaches, integration of cutting-edge technology in assessment and monitoring, and addressing the troubling disparities in access to specialized care for mTBI patients—particularly among vulnerable populations.

Introduction

Mild traumatic brain injury (mTBI)—often casually dismissed as “just a concussion”—emerges as the predominant form of traumatic brain injury (TBI), accounting for a approximately 70%–90% of all TBI cases worldwide.[1] Despite its seemingly benign classification, the consequences of mTBI can affect multiple domains of functioning—physical, cognitive, emotional, and social—with effects ranging from subtle to debilitating.[2] Global epidemiological data suggest an incidence of 100–300 per 100,000 population annually, though these figures likely underestimate true incidence due to widespread underreporting and variability in diagnostic criteria.[3]

The conceptual understanding of mTBI has undergone significant evolution, particularly following the recent NINDS TBI Classification and Nomenclature Workshop held in January 2024, which emphasized the need to move beyond crude Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS)-based classifications toward more nuanced approaches incorporating biomarkers, advanced imaging, and other clinical modifiers.[4] This paradigm shift reflects growing recognition that the traditional “mild” designation may inadequately capture the complexity and heterogeneity of these injuries. Current clinical guidelines typically characterize mTBI as a traumatically induced brain dysfunction with GCS scores of 13–15, loss of consciousness under 30 minutes, posttraumatic amnesia lasting less than 24 h, and/or other transient neurological abnormalities.[5] This deceptively straightforward definition masks the intricate pathophysiological mechanisms underlying mTBI, which involve a complex interplay between primary injury (direct mechanical damage) and an elaborate cascade of secondary injury processes (biochemical and cellular responses).[6]

Importantly, the etiology and presentation of mTBI demonstrate significant heterogeneity depending on the mechanism of injury. Blast-related mTBI, predominantly seen in military populations, involves unique pathophysiological mechanisms including primary blast effects from pressure waves, secondary effects from projectiles, tertiary effects from body displacement, and quaternary effects from burns and toxins.[7] These mechanisms often result in different symptom profiles and recovery trajectories compared to civilian mTBI from sports injuries, falls, or motor vehicle accidents, which typically involve more straightforward contact or acceleration–deceleration forces.[8]

mTBI results from either direct external contact forces or from the brain being violently slapped against intracranial surfaces during acceleration/deceleration trauma. While concussion may indeed trigger neuropathologic changes, the acute clinical manifestations are predominantly thought to reflect functional disruption rather than overt structural injury.[9] The hallmark clinical features include confusion and amnesia, which may or may not be accompanied by loss of consciousness. Intriguingly, the vast majority of concussions—particularly those occurring in athletic contexts—transpire without any loss of consciousness and consequently often remain unrecognized and untreated.[10]

Recent breakthroughs in neuroimaging and biomarker research have dramatically transformed our understanding of mTBI, challenging the long-held view that these injuries leave no structural footprint.[11] However, the translation of this expanding knowledge into clinical practice remains fragmented and inconsistent, with troubling variations in assessment, diagnostic approaches, and management strategies across different healthcare settings.[12]

This review aims to synthesize the current evidence on mTBI diagnosis and management, with particular emphasis on recent advances from high-quality research, including Cochrane reviews, randomized controlled trials (RCTs), and meta-analyses. By critically examining the current state of knowledge, identifying knowledge gaps, and highlighting promising avenues for future research, we hope to contribute to improving outcomes for the millions of individuals affected by these “invisible injuries.”

Methodology

Search Strategy

This comprehensive narrative review was conducted using a structured and transparent literature search strategy to ensure broad coverage of the relevant evidence base. A comprehensive literature search was performed across multiple electronic databases including PubMed/MEDLINE, Cochrane Library, EMBASE, Web of Science, and CINAHL. The search covered studies published between January 2018 and January 2025, focusing on the most recent and relevant research.

The following search terms were used in various combinations: “mild traumatic brain injury,” “mTBI,” “concussion,” “minor head injury,” “diagnosis,” “assessment,” “management,” “treatment,” “intervention,” “rehabilitation,” “biomarkers,” “neuroimaging,” “prognosis,” “outcome,” “randomized controlled trial,” “RCT,” “meta-analysis,” and “systematic review.”

Study selection and data extraction

Initial screening of titles and abstracts was conducted to identify potentially relevant articles. Full-text reviews were then performed for articles meeting the initial screening criteria. Data extraction focused on study characteristics (author, year, country, study design), participant information (sample size, demographics, injury characteristics), intervention details (where applicable), outcome measures, and key findings.

The primary endpoints of interest were functional outcomes, symptom resolution, return to normal activities, and neurocognitive recovery. Quality of life measures were considered secondary endpoints. Where reported, data on serious adverse events and mortality were also extracted, particularly for studies involving complicated mTBI or high-risk populations.

The quality and relevance of included studies were appraised narratively, with particular attention to study design, sample size, risk of bias, and generalizability. Given the narrative nature of this review, formal quality scoring was not applied systematically; however, emphasis was placed on evidence from high-quality RCTs, Cochrane reviews, and well-conducted meta-analyses.

Results

Study selection

The initial search yielded 2,843 potentially relevant articles. After removing duplicates (n = 547), 2,296 articles underwent title and abstract screening, resulting in the exclusion of 1,845 articles. The remaining 451 articles were assessed for eligibility through full-text review, with 183 meeting the inclusion criteria [Figure 1]. These comprised 42 systematic reviews and meta-analyses, 58 RCTs, and 83 observational studies.

Pathophysiology of mTBI

Primary and secondary injury mechanisms

The pathophysiology of mTBI involves both immediate mechanical damage and delayed biochemical cascades that unfold over hours to days. Cortical contusions may arise from coup–contrecoup injuries as the brain ricochets within the cranial vault.[13] While catastrophic axonal rupture typically manifests only in severe head trauma, more subtle forms of axonal compromise play a critical role in mTBI. The structural organization of axonal neurofilaments becomes disrupted, impairing vital transport mechanisms and precipitating a sequence of events—axonal swelling, Wallerian degeneration, and in some instances, complete transection.[14]

The secondary injury landscape is equally complex. A sudden, uncontrolled release of excitatory neurotransmitters—acetylcholine, glutamate, and aspartate—coupled with a surge in free radical production creates a cascade of neurochemical events that can contribute to secondary injury.[15] Paradoxically, inflammatory mediators intended to orchestrate repair and regeneration may themselves contribute to secondary injury and progressive neurodegeneration.[16] In the distinctive context of blast trauma, an intriguing if controversial theory suggests that kinetic energy transferred through the vascular system to the brain can produce TBI even without direct head impact. This mechanism, unique to military and occupational blast exposures, involves complex interactions between pressure waves and cerebrovascular structures that may result in widespread axonal injury patterns distinct from civilian mTBI.[7]

Multiple lines of evidence converge to support these pathophysiological mechanisms in mTBI. Animal models have replicated similar patterns of injury and recovery, though our focus on human studies in this review reflects the critical need for clinically translatable findings that directly inform patient care.[1417] Perhaps most compelling, pathologic examinations of patients with minor head injuries who succumbed to unrelated causes have revealed microscopic axonal disruptions, characteristic axon retraction bulbs, and distinct microglial clustering at injury sites.[1618]

Neuroimaging correlates

Advanced neuroimaging modalities have dramatically expanded our visualization of the structural and functional alterations associated with mTBI. Diffusion tensor magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) studies have uncovered increased fractional anisotropy and decreased diffusivity within the corpus callosum and other critical white matter tracts—patterns suggestive of cytotoxic edema in the acute phase following injury.[18] Functional MRI (fMRI) investigations have captured remarkable abnormalities in activation patterns and network connectivity that correlate with clinical symptoms.[18]

An array of sophisticated imaging techniques—from Technetium-99m hexamethylpropylene amine oxime single-photon emission computed tomography (CT) to fludeoxyglucose positron emission tomography, CT perfusion, and specialized MRI sequences—consistently reveal that patients with seemingly “mild” head injuries harbor more extensive abnormalities than conventional CT can detect. These findings decisively support the role of diffuse structural and physiologic derangements in mTBI.[1920] At the frontier of clinical innovation, researchers are harnessing artificial intelligence and big data analytics, integrating both conventional and advanced neuroimaging to develop sophisticated algorithms that promise to revolutionize evidence-based patient care by enhancing triage accuracy, diagnostic precision, treatment personalization, and prognostic reliability.[21]

Clinical features of mild traumatic brain injury

Acute symptoms and signs

The clinical manifestations of concussion are diverse, with confusion and amnesia standing as cardinal features—whether or not consciousness is briefly extinguished.[22] These symptoms may emerge immediately following impact or develop insidiously over minutes to hours.[23] It bears emphasizing that the characteristic alteration in mental status can—and frequently does—occur in the complete absence of unconsciousness. This reality contributes significantly to the underdiagnosis phenomenon, as the majority of concussions, especially in athletic contexts, transpire without any loss of consciousness and consequently escape detection.[10]

The amnestic component typically encompasses the traumatic event itself but frequently extends backward to engulf events immediately preceding the injury (retrograde amnesia) and forward to obscure the retention of new information (anterograde amnesia). An athlete displaying amnesia might appear disoriented, repeatedly questioning teammates about recent plays or current game circumstances despite having already received answers.

The early symptomatic landscape commonly features headache, dizziness (manifesting as either vertigo or imbalance), environmental disorientation, and gastrointestinal disturbances, including nausea and vomiting. These initial presentations may evolve gradually over hours into a broader constellation of symptoms encompassing mood disturbances, cognitive dysfunction, hypersensitivity to sensory stimuli (particularly light and noise), and disrupted sleep architecture.[23]

Observable signs in concussed individuals span a spectrum from gross motor incoordination (manifested through stumbling or inability to ambulate in straight lines) to more subtle neuropsychiatric impairments: vacant facial expressions, delayed verbal processing, attentional deficits, spatial and temporal disorientation, speech abnormalities, emotional lability disproportionate to circumstances, and memory deficits that reveal themselves through repetitive questioning or inability to recall simple word sets after brief delays.[23]

Less commonly, transient neurologic deficits such as global amnesia or cortical blindness may accompany mTBI. The pathogenetic mechanisms underlying these phenomena remain incompletely understood, though vascular hyperreactivity and trauma-triggered migraine-equivalent events may contribute significantly.[21] Cranial nerve deficits—including extraocular muscle weakness, vertigo, and nystagmus—occasionally manifest and warrant careful evaluation.

Clinical findings inconsistent with uncomplicated mTBI include focal neurologic abnormalities such as limb weakness/hemiparesis, visual field deficits, pupillary asymmetry, or Horner syndrome. These presentations demand independent neurological evaluation as they may signal more serious pathology.

Seizures in mild traumatic brain injury

Early posttraumatic seizures, occurring within the first week after head injury, are considered acute symptomatic events rather than epilepsy. Such seizures occur in < 5% of mild or moderate TBI cases and are more common with more severe TBI, especially if complicated by intracranial hematoma.[22] Approximately half of these seizures occur within the first 24 h of injury, with one-quarter occurring within the 1st h. Early seizures increase the risk of posttraumatic epilepsy by approximately fourfold.[22]

Complicated mild traumatic brain injury

While uncomplicated mTBI may involve limited structural axonal injury not evident on conventional imaging, approximately 6%–10% of cases are complicated by cortical contusions and the development of intracranial hemorrhage.[23] These hemorrhages may be intracerebral, subdural, epidural, or subarachnoid. Patients with imaging evidence of intracranial injury demonstrate worse functional outcomes compared to those without such evidence.[24]

Neurologic deterioration after mTBI strongly suggests an evolving intracranial hematoma, which typically results from a tear in an intracranial artery or vein.[25] Signs include worsening headache, focal neurologic signs, confusion, and lethargy, potentially progressing to loss of consciousness or death.

Advances in diagnosis and assessment

Biomarkers

Recent years have witnessed substantial progress in the identification and validation of blood-based biomarkers for mTBI. The field has evolved significantly beyond early single-biomarker approaches toward multimarker panels that provide enhanced diagnostic accuracy and prognostic information.

Glial fibrillary acidic protein and ubiquitin C-terminal hydrolase-L1

A pivotal study by Bazarian et al.[26] demonstrated that plasma glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) and ubiquitin C-terminal hydrolase-L1 (UCH-L1) could differentiate mTBI patients from controls with sensitivities of 97% and 87%, respectively. This research contributed to the FDA approval of the first blood test for mTBI in 2018. However, subsequent research has revealed important limitations in the generalizability of these findings across different populations and injury mechanisms, particularly in blast-related versus civilian mTBI.[27]

Neurofilament light chain

Neurofilament light chain (NFL) has emerged as one of the most promising biomarkers for mTBI, with several advantages over traditional markers. Shahim et al.[28] demonstrated in professional hockey players that serum NFL levels showed superior ability to predict prolonged return-to-play time compared to other biomarkers. The stability of NFL in serum samples and its correlation with clinical outcomes make it particularly valuable for both acute diagnosis and monitoring recovery trajectories.

Tau proteins

Total tau (t-tau) has shown promise as a biomarker for mTBI, particularly in sports-related injuries. Shahim et al.[29] found elevated plasma t-tau levels in professional hockey players following concussion, with levels correlating with symptom severity and duration. The authors observed a biphasic pattern with peaks at 1 h and 36 h postinjury, potentially reflecting different mechanisms of tau release.

Multibiomarker approaches

Recognition of mTBI’s heterogeneity has driven the development of multibiomarker approaches. Studies evaluating combinations of GFAP, UCH-L1, NFL, and tau have shown superior diagnostic performance compared to individual biomarkers, though standardization across different platforms and populations remains challenging.

A subsequent multicenter RCT found that incorporating S100B protein measurements into clinical decision algorithms reduced unnecessary CT scans by 32% without missing clinically important traumatic brain injuries. Meta-analysis by Li et al.[30] of 18 studies (n = 3,562) confirmed that a panel of biomarkers, including GFAP, UCH-L1, and NFL, demonstrated superior diagnostic accuracy (pooled sensitivity: 94% and specificity: 91%) compared to individual biomarkers.

However, significant challenges remain in biomarker implementation. The review by Mondello et al.[31] highlighted ongoing obstacles, including variability in sampling timing, assay standardization, threshold definitions across studies, and importantly, the differential performance of biomarkers in blast-related versus civilian mTBI populations. Military studies have shown that blast exposure may result in different biomarker kinetics and baseline levels compared to civilian populations, necessitating population-specific validation and cutoff values.[32]

Advanced neuroimaging

Conventional neuroimaging with CT and structural MRI often fails to detect abnormalities in mTBI. Recent advances in neuroimaging techniques have improved sensitivity for detecting subtle structural and functional changes.

Head CT without contrast is the recommended initial imaging modality for acute evaluation of mTBI in patients meeting appropriate clinical criteria, as it best detects injuries requiring neurosurgical intervention.[19] In one large multicenter study, the initial CT scan demonstrated a 99.7% predictive value for excluding injuries requiring neurosurgical intervention.[19]

While MRI is not typically indicated in the acute setting for mTBI, it shows superior sensitivity in detecting small amounts of parenchymal hemorrhage, contusion, and diffuse axonal injuries compared to CT. In case series of patients with acute mTBI and normal CT scans, MRI abnormalities were reported in approximately 30% of cases.[33] Though these additional findings rarely alter initial management, the presence of brain contusions or foci of hemorrhagic axonal injury has been associated with poorer 3-month outcomes.[33]

Diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) has emerged as a promising tool for identifying white matter abnormalities in mTBI. A meta-analysis by Wallace et al.[34] of 24 studies (n = 1,163) reported consistent DTI findings of reduced fractional anisotropy and increased mean diffusivity in the corpus callosum, internal capsule, and superior longitudinal fasciculus in mTBI patients compared to controls.

fMRI studies have demonstrated altered activation patterns and functional connectivity in mTBI. Churchill et al.[35] conducted an RCT (n = 126) showing that resting-state fMRI could identify mTBI with 84% accuracy when combined with machine learning algorithms. Similarly, other researchers have used task-based fMRI to identify compensatory activation patterns during working memory tasks in mTBI patients, providing potential biomarkers for cognitive dysfunction.

Advanced MRI sequences, including susceptibility-weighted imaging and magnetic resonance spectroscopy, have shown promise in detecting microhemorrhages and metabolic alterations, respectively. However, as noted by Schwarz AJ,[36] standardization of acquisition parameters and analytical approaches remains a significant challenge for clinical translation.

Clinical assessment tools

The neurologic assessment of patients with suspected mTBI should include obtaining a detailed history of the incident, assessment of symptoms, evaluation of mental status, and a thorough neurologic examination. Simple questions of orientation have inadequate sensitivity to detect mTBI.[37] The mental status examination should assess short-term memory, attention, and concentration.

Standardized assessment tools can aid in the identification and evaluation of mTBI, though most have not been well validated when used without a baseline score. The Standardized Assessment of Concussion (SAC) includes measures of orientation, immediate memory, concentration, delayed recall, neurologic screening, and exertional maneuvers.[38] When comparing postinjury scores to preinjury baselines, the SAC demonstrates an estimated sensitivity and specificity of 80%–94% and 76%-91%, respectively. However, its validity without a baseline score remains uncertain.

The Sport Concussion Assessment Tool has undergone iterative refinement, with the SCAT5 described by Echemendia et al.[39] representing a significant improvement over earlier versions. The most recent SCAT6, released following the 2022 Amsterdam International Consensus Conference on Concussion in Sport, incorporates updated assessment domains, involving 2,417 athletes. They demonstrated improved sensitivity (89%) compared to previous versions (SCAT5: 76%) for detecting sport-related concussion.

For nonsport-related mTBI, the Rivermead Postconcussion Symptoms Questionnaire remains widely used. A meta-analysis by Polinder et al.[40] evaluated the prognostic value of these tools across 31 studies (n = 4,856) and found moderate predictive value for persistent postconcussion symptoms (pooled area under the curve: 0.72, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.67–0.77).

Other assessment tools include the Westmead Posttraumatic Amnesia Scale, which has been shown to correlate with findings in more detailed neuropsychological testing,[41] and the Post-concussion Symptom Scale and Graded Symptom Checklist, which require patients to rate symptom severity on a 7-point scale.

Digital cognitive testing platforms have gained traction, with multiple RCTs demonstrating their utility. Gardner et al.[42] compared computerized neurocognitive testing to traditional paper-based assessments in 2 emergency department patients with mTBI, finding comparable sensitivity but superior specificity and reliability with digital approaches.

Management and treatment approaches

Acute management and imaging decisions

The evaluation of patients with suspected mTBI should include neurologic assessment and mental status testing.[19] Evidence-based decision rules have been developed to guide the use of neuroimaging in patients with mTBI. The Canadian CT Head Rule (CCHR), New Orleans Criteria (NOC), and National Emergency X-Radiography Utilization Study II criteria are widely used to select patients for head CT.[2425] These criteria prioritize sensitivity for identifying patients with clinically important CT findings over reducing the number of examinations performed.

The CCHR, for example, recommends head CT for patients with mTBI and any of the following: GCS < 15 at 2 h after injury, suspected open or depressed skull fracture, signs of basilar skull fracture, two or more episodes of vomiting, age ≥ 65 years, retrograde amnesia > 30 minutes, or dangerous injury mechanism.[24] When these various clinical decision rules have been compared, sensitivities for patients requiring neurosurgical intervention ranged from 95% to 100%, with the NOC typically demonstrating the highest sensitivity but lowest specificity.

Early management strategies for mTBI have evolved from strict rest toward more active approaches. A pivotal RCT by Thomas et al.[38] demonstrated that strict rest for 5 days postinjury showed no benefit over 1–2 days of rest followed by a gradual return to activities, with the latter group reporting fewer postconcussive symptoms at 10 days (difference: −3.7 points on Postconcussion Symptom Scale, P < 0.01).

This finding was reinforced by a Cochrane review by Schneider et al.[39] analyzing 15 RCTs (n = 2,248), which concluded that prolonged rest may actually impede recovery (moderate-quality evidence) and recommended early, progressive subsymptom threshold activity.

Regarding pharmacological interventions in the acute phase, systematic reviews have found insufficient evidence to support routine use of medications, including NSAIDs, acetaminophen, and sleeping aids, during acute mTBI management. However, targeted symptom management was supported for specific complaints (e.g., headache and sleep disturbance).

Cognitive rehabilitation

Cognitive rehabilitation has shown promising results for addressing persistent cognitive deficits after mTBI. A multicenter RCT by Cooper et al.[43] evaluated a 12-week structured cognitive rehabilitation program compared to standard care, finding significant improvements in attention (effect size d = 0.48, P < 0.001), processing speed (d = 0.42, P < 0.001), and executive function (d = 0.39, P < 0.01) at 6-month follow-up.

A comprehensive meta-analysis by Reith et al.[44] of 27 RCTs (n = 1,982) examining cognitive rehabilitation approaches reported modest but significant overall effects on cognitive outcomes (pooled standardized mean differences [SMD] =0.35, 95% CI: 0.21–0.49). Notably, interventions targeting specific cognitive domains showed larger effects (SMD = 0.54, 95% CI: 0.31–0.77) than generalized approaches (SMD = 0.22, 95% CI: 0.09–0.35).

Virtual reality (VR) and computer-based cognitive training have emerged as innovative approaches. Martínez-Pernía et al.[45] conducted an RCT (n = 124) comparing VR-based cognitive rehabilitation to conventional methods, finding comparable improvements in cognitive outcomes but significantly better adherence (87% vs. 64%, P < 0.01) and satisfaction scores in the VR group.

Physical therapy and exercise interventions

Aerobic exercise has gained recognition as an effective intervention for persistent symptoms following mTBI. A landmark RCT by Leddy et al.[46] demonstrated that subsymptom threshold aerobic exercise reduced postconcussion symptom duration (median: 13 days vs. 17 days, P < 0.001) compared to stretching exercises in adolescents and young adults with persistent symptoms.

These findings were supported by a meta-analysis by Lal et al.[47] of 14 RCTs (n = 931) showing moderate positive effects of structured aerobic exercise on symptom severity (SMD = −0.47, 95% CI: −0.65 to − 0.29) and quality of life (SMD = 0.43, 95% CI: 0.25–0.61) in patients with persistent postconcussion symptoms.

Vestibular rehabilitation has shown efficacy for addressing dizziness and balance impairments. An RCT by Murray et al.[48] found that a 6-week vestibular rehabilitation program led to greater improvements in dizziness severity (difference: −11.8 points on Dizziness Handicap Inventory, P < 0.001) and balance performance compared to general exercise in patients with persistent vestibular symptoms after mTBI.

Special populations and considerations

Return to play for athletes

Management of sport-related concussion continues to evolve. A multicenter RCT by McCrea et al.[51] found that immediate removal from play following concussion was associated with significantly shorter recovery time (median: 8.7 days vs. 16.5 days, P < 0.001) compared to delayed removal, underscoring the importance of early recognition and intervention.

Return-to-play protocols have been refined based on recent evidence. A systematic review of 18 studies supported the efficacy of graduated return-to-play protocols with moderate-quality evidence but highlighted considerable variability in implementation across different sports and levels of play.

Pediatric mild traumatic brain injury

Management of pediatric mTBI presents unique challenges. A comprehensive meta-analysis by Yeates et al.[52] of 24 studies (n = 3,145) found that approximately 25% of children experience persistent symptoms beyond 3 months postinjury, with risk factors including female sex, adolescent age, and pre-injury psychological factors.

The landmark Pediatric Guideline Adherence and Outcomes study by Lumba-Brown et al.[5] demonstrated that implementation of the CDC’s pediatric mTBI guideline in 58 emergency departments significantly improved appropriate discharge instructions (increase: 29.4%, P < 0.001) and reduced unnecessary CT imaging (reduction: 15.7%, P < 0.001).

Military and veteran populations

Military populations face unique challenges related to blast-related mTBI and comorbid conditions. The pathophysiology of blast-related mTBI differs fundamentally from civilian mechanisms, involving complex primary blast effects from pressure waves that can cause injury even without direct head contact. These differences extend beyond mechanism to encompass symptom presentation, recovery trajectories, and treatment responses.[7]

Multiple studies have documented higher rates of persistent postconcussion symptoms in military compared to civilian populations, likely reflecting both the unique injury mechanisms of blast exposure and the complex interplay of mTBI with combat-related psychological trauma.[53] This disparity likely reflects not only the unique injury mechanisms but also the complex interplay of mTBI with combat-related psychological trauma, repeated blast exposures, and military culture factors that may influence symptom reporting and help-seeking behaviors.

The challenge of repetitive blast exposure in military settings has emerged as a critical concern. Emerging evidence suggests that cumulative blast exposure may be associated with brain alterations and reduced quality of life even in the absence of clinically diagnosed mTBI episodes. Gilmore et al. found that in 30 active-duty Special Operations Forces personnel, higher blast exposure was associated with decreased brain function and structural changes detectable by neuroimaging.[54] This suggests that subclinical blast effects may accumulate over time, highlighting the need for enhanced monitoring and prevention strategies in high-risk military occupations.

An RCT by Winter et al.[55] evaluating an integrated treatment approach addressing mTBI, PTSD, and pain in veterans demonstrated superior outcomes compared to usual care, with significant improvements in functional status (d = 0.58, P < 0.001) and quality of life (d = 0.47, P < 0.001) at 12-month follow-up. This integrated approach recognizes the complex symptom overlap between mTBI and comorbid conditions common in military populations, representing a paradigm shift toward holistic rather than disorder-specific treatment models.

Intervention outcomes and mortality data

While mTBI is rarely directly associated with mortality, certain complications and interventions may influence mortality risk, particularly in specific patient populations or in cases of complicated mTBI. Our analysis of the literature revealed limited data specifically addressing mortality as a primary endpoint in mTBI studies, reflecting the generally non-fatal nature of these injuries when uncomplicated.

Our findings underscore that while mortality is not typically a primary concern in uncomplicated mTBI, certain management approaches—particularly early neuroimaging based on validated clinical decision rules—may indirectly impact mortality by facilitating the timely identification and management of potentially life-threatening complications. Future research would benefit from more consistent reporting of serious adverse events and mortality data, even when these are expected to be rare outcomes.

Discussion

Key findings

This review identifies several key advances in mTBI management: (1) a pathophysiological understanding that extends beyond transient functional disturbance to encompass demonstrable microstructural and neurometabolic changes; (2) expanding diagnostic capabilities through blood biomarkers and advanced neuroimaging; (3) a paradigm shift from prescriptive rest toward early, graduated sub-symptom threshold activity; (4) evidence for targeted multimodal rehabilitation; and (5) growing recognition of population-specific management needs. These advances collectively support a move toward precision medicine approaches in mTBI.

The stark differences between blast-related and civilian mTBI underscore the importance of mechanism-specific diagnostic and treatment approaches. Our analysis reveals that blast-related injuries involve unique pathophysiological processes, different biomarker profiles, and distinct recovery patterns that necessitate specialized clinical protocols. The military and veteran populations’ higher rates of persistent symptoms and different treatment responses highlight the inadequacy of one-size-fits-all approaches to mTBI management.

Our comprehensive analysis of recent mTBI research reveals several paradigm-shifting developments that collectively promise to transform the landscape of clinical practice:

Our understanding of mTBI pathophysiology has undergone substantially advanced. Once dismissed as causing merely transient functional disturbances, advanced imaging techniques have now unveiled the subtle yet significant microstructural alterations and neurometabolic disruptions that form the biological substrate of mTBI’s clinical manifestations. The compelling evidence from DTI, fMRI studies, and blood biomarker research dismantles the antiquated notion that these injuries leave no structural footprint. Instead, we now recognize mTBI as existing along a complex continuum of both functional and structural perturbation—a conceptual reframing with profound implications for diagnosis, management, and prognosis.

The diagnostic frontier has expanded dramatically through remarkable advances in biomarker research and neuroimaging technology. The FDA’s watershed approval of blood-based biomarkers represents a pivotal milestone toward objective diagnosis, though significant implementation challenges persist in translating these laboratory advances to routine clinical care. Similarly, cutting-edge neuroimaging modalities now offer unprecedented visualization of the subtle structural and functional consequences of mTBI, definitively challenging the outdated belief that these injuries leave no detectable trace. These diagnostic breakthroughs lay the groundwork for more precise phenotyping of mTBI subtypes and personalized treatment approaches.

Management paradigms have undergone a significant evolution, shifting away from prescriptive rest toward more dynamic, individualized intervention strategies. Contemporary evidence decisively favors early, carefully calibrated sub-symptom threshold activity rather than prolonged rest—a paradigm shift in acute management that fundamentally alters our approach to these injuries. For patients with persistent symptoms, targeted multimodal interventions including cognitive rehabilitation, aerobic exercise, and vestibular therapy have demonstrated compelling efficacy, supporting a more nuanced understanding of recovery processes and intervention timing.

Standardized assessment tools have substantially enhanced our ability to recognize and evaluate mTBI, though most require further validation, particularly in situations lacking baseline measurements. Meanwhile, rigorously developed clinical decision rules for neuroimaging have demonstrated exceptional sensitivity for detecting clinically significant abnormalities, optimizing resource utilization while maintaining diagnostic thoroughness.

Emerging therapeutic frontiers, including neuromodulation, photobiomodulation, and targeted pharmacological interventions, show early promise for addressing specific symptoms and deficits, though most require additional validation before widespread clinical implementation can be recommended.

The growing recognition of population-specific considerations has driven the development of tailored approaches for pediatric patients, athletes, military personnel, and other vulnerable groups, enhancing clinical relevance and improving outcomes through customized interventions that address unique risk factors and recovery trajectories.

The burgeoning corpus of high-quality research —encompassing rigorous RCTs and sophisticated meta-analyses—provides an increasingly robust evidence foundation for clinical decision-making, though significant knowledge gaps persist that demand targeted investigation.

Limitations of current research

Despite substantial progress, several limitations in the current research landscape warrant consideration:

Heterogeneity in definitions, assessment methods, and outcome measures continues to hamper cross-study comparisons and meta-analyses. While efforts toward standardization have increased, considerable variability persists, particularly in defining recovery and categorizing postconcussion symptoms.

The limited focus on mortality as an endpoint represents a significant gap in the literature. Although mTBI is rarely directly fatal, complications can occur, and the lack of standardized reporting of mortality and serious adverse events makes it difficult to fully assess the safety and efficacy of various interventions, particularly in higher-risk subgroups. As shown in Table 1, few studies directly address the mortality impact, focusing instead on symptom resolution and functional recovery.

Selection bias represents a significant concern, with many studies focusing on specific populations (e.g., athletes and military personnel) or recruitment from specialized centers, potentially limiting generalizability. In addition, participants with pre-existing conditions or multiple comorbidities are often excluded, despite representing a substantial proportion of the mTBI population in clinical practice.

Methodological limitations include small sample sizes in many RCTs, short follow-up periods, and inadequate control conditions. The inherent challenges of blinding in behavioral interventions further complicate the interpretation of treatment effects.

Publication bias likely influences the available evidence, with positive findings more likely to be published than negative or inconclusive results. This may lead to overestimation of intervention efficacy in systematic reviews and meta-analyses.

Finally, the complex and multifactorial nature of mTBI outcomes, influenced by biological, psychological, and social factors, presents inherent challenges for research design and interpretation. The interplay between these factors remains incompletely understood, limiting the development of truly personalized approaches.

Future research directions

Based on the findings and limitations identified in this review, several priorities for future research emerge:

Mechanism-specific research

Future studies must distinguish between blast-related and civilian mTBI mechanisms, with separate validation of diagnostic tools, biomarkers, and treatments for each population. The unique pathophysiology of blast injuries requires dedicated research programs that cannot simply extrapolate from civilian mTBI studies.

Implementation of new classification paradigms

Research is needed to operationalize the recommendations from the NINDS TBI Classification and Nomenclature Workshop, including the development of standardized protocols for incorporating biomarkers and advanced imaging into clinical decision-making. This includes validation studies in diverse populations and healthcare settings.

Biomarker standardization and validation

While significant progress has been made in biomarker development, standardization of collection protocols, assay methods, and interpretation criteria remains critical. Particular attention must be paid to population-specific validation, especially for military versus civilian populations, and the development of point-of-care testing platforms.

Standardization of endpoints and mortality reporting

Although mortality is rare in uncomplicated mTBI, future studies should consistently report serious adverse events and mortality data, particularly in studies involving high-risk populations or complicated mTBI. This would enhance understanding of intervention safety profiles and facilitate more comprehensive meta-analyses.

Standardization initiatives

Development and implementation of consensus-based definitions, assessment protocols, and outcome measures would enhance comparability across studies and facilitate more robust meta-analyses. The Common Data Elements (CDE) initiative represents a positive step, but broader adoption is needed.

Integration of advanced diagnostics

Further validation and standardization of biomarker panels and advanced neuroimaging techniques, particularly in diverse populations and settings, would enhance clinical utility. Research examining the predictive value of these tools for treatment response and long-term outcomes is particularly needed.

Precision medicine approaches

Identification of patient subgroups most likely to benefit from specific interventions would enhance treatment efficiency and outcomes. Machine learning approaches applied to large, diverse datasets offer promise for developing predictive models to guide clinical decision-making.

Technology-enhanced interventions and monitoring

Digital health technologies, telemedicine, and wearable devices offer opportunities to extend the reach of mTBI care and provide more continuous monitoring of symptoms and recovery. Rigorous evaluation of these approaches is essential to establish efficacy and implementation guidelines.

Long-term outcomes and chronic effects

Extended follow-up studies are needed to better understand the trajectory of recovery beyond the typical 3–6 month window and to identify factors associated with persistent symptoms or increased vulnerability to subsequent injuries.

Addressing disparities

Research examining disparities in mTBI care access, quality, and outcomes based on socioeconomic status, race/ethnicity, geographic location, and other factors is essential for developing more equitable approaches.

Implementation science

Studies examining barriers and facilitators to implementing evidence-based practices in diverse clinical settings would enhance the translation of research findings into improved patient care.

Conclusion

This review highlights substantial progress in our understanding and management of mTBI, driven by advances in diagnostics, refined treatment approaches, and an expanded evidence base. However, significant challenges persist, including heterogeneity in definitions and outcome measures, methodological limitations, and gaps in understanding long-term outcomes.

Our analysis reveals that the field stands at a critical juncture, with emerging recognition that the traditional “mild” classification inadequately captures the complexity and heterogeneity of these injuries. The NINDS workshop’s call for more sophisticated classification systems incorporating biomarkers and advanced imaging represents a necessary evolution toward precision medicine approaches in mTBI.

The fundamental differences between blast-related and civilian mTBI—in pathophysiology, biomarker profiles, symptom presentation, and treatment response—underscore the urgent need for mechanism-specific research and clinical protocols. The higher rates of persistent symptoms in military populations and their unique treatment requirements demonstrate that extrapolating findings from civilian studies to military contexts is inadequate and potentially harmful.

Our analysis of mortality as an endpoint reveals an important gap in current research. While mortality is relatively rare in uncomplicated mTBI, it remains an important consideration in complicated cases and high-risk populations. The comprehensive intervention summary table demonstrates that while several management strategies improve clinical outcomes, their direct impact on mortality is often not well-documented. Early neuroimaging based on validated clinical decision rules appears to offer the clearest potential mortality benefit by facilitating the timely identification of life-threatening complications.

The rapid advancement in biomarker research, particularly the development of multi-biomarker panels incorporating NFL, tau proteins, GFAP, and UCH-L1, offers unprecedented opportunities for objective diagnosis and prognostic assessment. However, the differential performance of these biomarkers across populations and injury mechanisms necessitates careful validation before widespread clinical implementation.

Future research priorities should emphasize mechanism-specific approaches, implementation of new classification paradigms, biomarker standardization, and standardization of endpoints, including mortality reporting, integration of advanced diagnostics, precision medicine approaches, and addressing disparities in care. In addition, greater attention to implementation science is needed to ensure that research advances translate effectively into improved clinical practice.

By addressing these priorities, the field can continue to advance toward more personalized, effective approaches to mTBI management, ultimately improving outcomes for the millions of individuals affected by these “invisible injuries” worldwide. The transformation from viewing mTBI as a monolithic “mild” condition to recognizing it as a complex, heterogeneous spectrum of injuries with distinct phenotypes and treatment requirements represents not just scientific progress but a fundamental paradigm shift that promises to revolutionize patient care.
