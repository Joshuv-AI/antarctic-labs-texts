AI-Assisted segmentation and volumetric reconstruction of radiographs through multi-angular scintillation imaging
Authors: Seokho Kim, Byungjoon Bae, Do Wan Kim, Yoo Jin Lee, Siyeon Kim, Sungwoo Kim, Jawoon Kim, Cheong Beom Lee, Yongmin Baek, Sree Sourav Das, Jiyoung Boo, Jinho Choi, Mona Zebarjadi, Kyeounghak Kim, Sangeun Cho, Dong Hyuk Park, Kyusang Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractX-ray imaging serves as a fundamental tool for non-destructive inspection. Although conventional radiography is well suited for two-dimensional imaging, it cannot provide volumetric structure. Computed tomography provides three-dimensional reconstruction but remains constrained by bulky instrumentation, high radiation exposure, and cost. Here we demonstrate a patch-type scintillator integrated with multi-stage neural network that segments and reconstructs three-dimensional volumes from sparse angular two-dimensional radiographs. The scintillator is fabricated by electrospraying cellulose nanocrystals onto a bulk cellulose matrix, followed by dip-coating of perovskite, yielding a composite with enhanced radioluminescence under X-ray excitation. This flexible film conforms to complex geometries, enabling distortion-free and multi-angle imaging. Neural networks are trained on synthetic datasets and validated on experimentally acquired avian tibiotarsus radiographs, accurately reconstructing volumetric bone structures. This approach serves as a proof-of-concept for low-dose, accessible artificial intelligence-enabled three-dimensional X-ray imaging, demonstrating the feasibility of recovering macroscopic three-dimensional morphology from as few as three sparse projections.Subject terms: Electrical and electronic engineering, Imaging techniques

Introduction

X-ray imaging is a foundational technology for non-invasive inspection across medicine, security, and industrial evaluation, owing to its deep penetration and rapid structural visualization1–4. In clinical practice, radiography affords rapid frontline assessment of medical conditions, including fractures, air or fluid accumulation in the chest or abdomen, infection such as pneumonia, and tumors5–8. However, conventional X-ray imaging is limited to two-dimensional (2D) projections, inevitably compressing three-dimensional (3D) spatial information into simple grayscale intensity maps, eliminating the critical depth cues. The dimensional reduction remains an intrinsic limitation when evaluating anatomically complex regions where structures overlap. For instance, breast lesions are notoriously difficult to precisely diagnose via standard chest X-ray mammography projections due to the complex 3D geometry of the breasts9,10.

Computed tomography (CT) overcomes these limitations by providing high-fidelity volumetric reconstructions11 of internal structures. However, its deployment in emergency, field, or point-of-care settings is restricted by bulky hardware and operational costs. Alternatively, digital tomosynthesis (DT) generates depth-resolved images by acquiring multiple projections over a limited angular range, providing sectional views at different depths utilized for mammography and chest imaging. Rotational angiography CT (C-Arm) provides 3D reconstructions through rotational imaging around the patient with more compact equipment, delivering real-time procedural guidance for interventional radiology and neurosurgery. However, these techniques still demand specialized equipment and a higher radiation dose than conventional radiography. Furthermore, precise segmentation of anatomical regions of interest (ROI), including bones, organs, lesions, tissues, and vasculature, is critical for diagnostic accuracy12–18, yet has relied on labor-intensive manual annotation by expert clinicians.

Recent advancements in artificial intelligence (AI) technologies have significantly improved both the accuracy and efficiency of anatomical segmentation and 3D reconstruction from 2D X-ray images16–18. Deep-learning frameworks leverage richly annotated datasets to train convolutional and transformer-based networks that infer volumetric structures from limited projections, thereby achieving 2D-to-3D reconstruction. Segmentation accuracy and reconstruction fidelity hinge critically on the quality and diversity of acquired images. However, conventional X-ray imaging systems, constrained by single-angle acquisition, introduce geometric distortions and occlusions that aggravate structural identification.

Addressing these challenges necessitates an interdisciplinary approach that integrates advanced X-ray imaging hardware with sophisticated reconstruction algorithms. On the hardware front, multi-angle X-ray imagers capture complementary projections that enrich the input data space for reconstruction models. In parallel, reconstruction algorithms incorporate statistical shape priors to regularize the solution and constrain it to anatomically plausible manifolds. Such innovations provide patient-specific 3D models, supporting improved diagnostic accuracy, deformity assessment, morphometry and surgical planning.

Here, we demonstrate a hardware and software co-designed approach that integrates a conformable perovskite-based scintillator with multi-stage neural networks to achieve high-precision 3D reconstruction from a limited set of 2D X-ray projections. Our flexible scintillator comprises Cs3MnBr5 perovskite uniformly nucleated within a mechanically flexible and thin cellulose matrix via an electrospray deposition of cellulose nanocrystals (CNs), followed by immersion in a mixed CsBr and MnBr2 precursor. This approach yields a large-area and uniform film whose conformal application to complex specimen geometries eliminates parallax and distortion, thereby delivering sharp and multi-angle X-ray luminescence images. This lead-free, water-based clean-chemistry approach produces a non-toxic, conformable scintillator that supports dense perovskite dispersion at reduced thickness, enabling distortion-free multi-angle acquisition.

On the software side, each planar projection is first processed by the Segment Anything Model (SAM) algorithm19 to delineate target anatomical features. The segmented images are then independently processed using the TripoSR algorithm20, which reconstructs an individual volumetric estimate from each view. A volumetric fusion neural network then consolidates these partial reconstructions into a single coherent 3D model, interpolating regions of low overlap of missing data. This integrated platform, featuring a flexible scintillator with neural network-assisted segmentation and reconstruction algorithms, enables high-fidelity volumetric X-ray imaging from a limited number of projections, offering a pathway toward dose-efficient imaging with broad potential for point-of-care clinical and industrial non-destructive testing applications. Overview of Three dimensional Image using Flexible Platform

X-ray imaging is achieved by transmitting X-rays through an object, where variations in absorption generate contrast, revealing internal structures. We have developed a multi-angle X-ray imaging system that employs conformal patch-type scintillator film wrapped around the specimen, thereby enabling flexible selection of incident X-ray angle and distortion-free acquisition of multi-angle projections (Fig. 1a). The overall imaging and reconstruction pipeline is summarized in Supplementary Fig. 1. Upon X-ray excitation, the flexible film composed of Cs3MnBr5 perovskite dispersed within a cellulose matrix emits green luminescence (519 nm). With a thickness of only ~ 220 µm, this scintillator film conforms seamlessly to the shape of the target object. Such conformability maintains close contact between the specimen and the imaging plane, resulting in enhanced edge definition while minimizing distortion. This allows the scintillator film to be wrapped around curved surfaces, such as the human body (Fig. 1b). Furthermore, our approach decouples the scintillator from the detector, allowing the use of high-resolution imaging sensors to capture detailed X-ray projections suitable for tomographic algorithms.

Workflow for X-ray image segmentation and 3D reconstruction via a flexible scintillator.

a Schematic of the end-to-end imaging and reconstruction process. A flexible scintillator adheres tightly to the target object through a transparent adhesive patch, converts incident X-rays into a visible light. Sequential radiographs are captured from multiple angles, and each projection is processed by a segmentation algorithm to delineate the region of interest (ROI) and extract key anatomical features. These segmented 2D images are then fed into a neural network, which volumetrically fuses the segmented data to generate a 3D reconstruction. b Photograph of the fabricated scintillator film conformally wrapped around a human forearm using a transparent dressing, illustrating its mechanical compliance and intimate contact with complex surface geometries.

For reconstructing 3D structures from multi-viewpoints of X-ray projections, a region-identifying neural network detects diagnostically relevant areas, then SAM produces precise and pixel-level segmentation across the entire dataset. Each segmented image is fed into TripoSR, a transformer-based network that generates a high-fidelity volumetric model of the specimen. The resulting partial 3D models are subsequently fused through a volumetric U-net-based fusion network, which reconciles inter-view inconsistencies and fills occluded regions to yield a coherent 3D model. This approach provides selective high-precision 3D reconstruction, particularly valuable for long-term monitoring of skeletal pathologies such as osteosarcoma, and holds promise for deployment in resource-limited clinical settings.

Result

Fabrication and analysis of flexible thin-film scintillator

Scintillators convert high-energy radiation, such as X-rays and gamma rays, into visible light; thus, they are essential for radiation detection and X-ray imaging. Among various scintillating materials, metal-halide perovskites have emerged as promising candidates due to their high radioluminescence (RL) quantum yield and tunable emission spectra. In this work, we employed a lead-free, all-inorganic metal-halide perovskite Cs3MnBr5 as the active scintillation medium, which exhibits intense green luminescence under X-ray excitation. However, achieving a homogeneous perovskite film over large areas poses significant fabrication challenges. Furthermore, conventional perovskite-based scintillator films usually require hundreds of micrometers of thickness to effectively absorb transmitted X-rays, leading to substantial material consumption and cost inefficiencies21,22. To address this limitation while maintaining structural integrity and absorption efficiency, we have fabricated a composite scintillator in which Cs3MnBr5 perovskite is embedded within a 220 µm thick cellulose fiber matrix. This hybrid architecture permits low-cost and scalable fabrication of a flexible scintillator film capable of X-ray imaging.

Figure 2a presents a schematic illustration of the scintillator fabrication process. Achieving a uniform Cs3MnBr5 coating on bundled cellulose fibers via solution process is inherently challenging due to the coffee-ring effect. The capillary flows during drying drive solute migration toward the fiber edges, resulting in a physical thickening of the crystal layer at the perimeter. This mass transport leads to a ring-like deposition pattern where the intense edge emission arises from the accumulation of emitting material. To mitigate this, we first applied CNs via electrospray onto the fiber matrix, establishing a high density of nucleation sites that disrupt the outward capillary flow and promote uniform distribution of the precursors (Supplementary Fig. 2). Subsequent annealing at 80 °C for 4 h strengthens hydrogen bonds between the CNs and the underlying cellulose matrix, locking in the interfacial architecture.

Then, we employed a dip-coating process that forms Cs3MnBr5 perovskite crystals uniformly across a high-surface-area cellulose scaffold. Conventional syntheses of Cs3MnBr5 perovskite crystals, including HBr solution-based coating23, lyophilization-assisted recrystallization (LARC)24,25, hot-injection26,27, and mechano-solid-state reactions methods28, often rely on hazardous solvents and multistep procedures. In contrast, our approach uses only deionized (DI) water and mild annealing (50 °C–70 °C), significantly reducing chemical complexity and environmental impact. In our process, CNs-coated cellulose matrix was immersed in DI water containing 3 mmol CsBr and 1 mmol MnBr2 precursors, allowing sufficient interaction between the β-D-glucose monomers in cellulose and the dissolved precursors. The nucleation of perovskite nanocrystals is driven by Lewis acid–base coordination, where hydroxyl (–OH) and glycosidic (C–O–C) groups in cellulose serve as electron donors to the vacant d orbitals of heavy metal ions in the precursors. This interaction fosters strong electrostatic binding with the precursors surrounding the cellulose scaffold (Supplementary Fig. 3a), resulting in the formation of stable coordinate covalent bonds that anchor Cs3MnBr5 nanocrystals with a grain size of 10–20 nm along the fiber network (Supplementary Fig. 3b). The fabrication process exhibits excellent repeatability, as demonstrated by structural and compositional consistency across five independently fabricated batches evaluated by non-resonance Raman spectroscopy (Supplementary Fig. 4). The Raman peaks of the Cs3MnBr5 at 111.5 cm−1, 160.5 cm−1, and 167.8 cm−1 were reproducibly observed across all films, and quantitative comparison of baseline-corrected peak intensities yields a relative standard deviation (RSD) of 4.2%, confirming high batch-to-batch uniformity.

Following the dip-coating process, the composite films were dried in a vacuum oven for 18 h at a temperature range of 50 °C–70 °C, allowing gradual evaporation of the solvent while preserving the crystallized perovskite on the network of cellulose fibers. Given the inherent water solubility of Cs3MnBr5 perovskite crystals, moisture exposure poses a severe degradation risk, necessitating protective encapsulation. To address this, we applied and cured a thin transparent polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) overlayer on the composite film, imparting superior environmental resilience to scintillators. PDMS encapsulation mitigates moisture-induced ion migration and phase degradation under high-humidity conditions (Supplementary Fig. 5)29,30. To evaluate signal stability under practical imaging conditions, RL image measurements were initiated following a 30-minute post-fabrication stabilization period and recorded at 20 min intervals over 80 min, a duration exceeding the typical multi-angle projection acquisition time. Total intensity drift over this period was 1.3%, confirming stable RL emission throughout a realistic imaging workflow. Moreover, the PDMS layer minimized cumulative mechanical damage of cellulose due to repeated bending by reducing localized stress concentrations (Supplementary Figs. 6 and 7). Therefore, the PDMS encapsulation enables the flexible perovskite–cellulose scintillators to maintain structural integrity and optical performance under realistic operating conditions.

To investigate the effect of CNs on the crystallization of Cs3MnBr5, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were performed. In the absence of CNs, CsBr and MnBr2 precursors tended to aggregate randomly (Supplementary Fig. 8a). However, when CNs were present, precursors preferentially nucleated and crystallized around the CNs (Inset of Fig. 2b). Complementary small-angle X-ray scattering (SAXS) profiles extracted from the MD simulations also showed the broad SAXS feature at lower q values in the precursors with CNs, implying enhanced particle coalescence with effective crystallization31 (Fig. 2b). Analysis of the radial distribution functions (RDFs) for Cs-Br and Mn-Br pairs further substantiated this effect. Secondary peaks corresponding to bulk Cs3MnBr5 (5.35 Å and 6.59 Å for Cs-Br; 6.20 Å and 6.55 Å for Mn-Br, Supplementary Fig. 9) became significantly more intense in the CNs-containing system (Fig. 2c). Furthermore, the velocity autocorrelation functions (VACFs) decayed more rapidly in the presence of CNs compared to the system without CNs, indicating reduced ionic mobility and accelerated crystallization kinetics of Cs3MnBr5 when CNs are incorporated32,33 (Supplementary Fig. 10).

Density functional theory (DFT) calculations were carried out to further investigate the thermodynamic favorability of Cs3MnBr5 crystallization with CNs. The simulated formation energy (Ef) of Cs3MnBr5 when interfaced with CNs (2.46 eV) was substantially lower than that in the absence of CNs (3.76 eV), indicating that CNs render nucleation of Cs3MnBr5 perovskite more thermodynamically favorable. Charge density difference and Bader charge analyses further revealed a charge transfer from the Mn site to the O atoms in cellulose, indicating strong Mn–O interactions at the interface (Fig. 2d). The directional electron redistribution is illustrated in the figure by an arrow pointing from Mn to O, accompanied by the transferred charge, highlighting the electrostatic nature of the interfacial interaction. This charge redistribution originates from the difference in electronegativity between Mn and O, where the more electronegative oxygen atoms attract electron density from the Mn centers. As a result, partial charges are induced, giving rise to electrostatic attraction at the interface, which supports the presence of strong interfacial interactions between the precursors and the cellulose matrix. Together with our MD simulation results, these first-principles insights demonstrate that the cellulose scaffold actively facilitates the crystallization of Cs3MnBr5, primarily through the interactions between Mn and O atoms in perovskite and cellulose, respectively.

To experimentally investigate the chemical interactions between CNs-coated cellulose matrix and the incorporated Cs3MnBr5 perovskite crystals, we conducted Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy on both the unmodified cellulose matrix and the perovskite-cellulose hybrid (Fig. 2e). In the pristine cellulose spectrum34, the β-D-glucose monomer exhibits a hydroxyl (O–H) stretching vibration at 3444 cm−1 and a corresponding bending mode at 1631 cm−1. Upon perovskite incorporation, distinct stretching vibrations34 associated with –CH and –CH2 moieties are observed between 2850 and 2923 cm−1. Furthermore, the glycosidic linkage (C–O–C), which originates from the β-1,4-glycosidic bonds between β-D-glucose units and forms the backbone of the cellulose polymer35, is evidenced by a prominent absorption band at 1068 cm−1. These bands undergo subtle shifts and intensity changes, confirming the establishment of hydrogen-bond and coordinate-bond interactions at the cellulose and perovskite interface.

Furthermore, the CNs with a net negative charge through their functional groups were quantitatively verified through zeta potential measurements (Supplementary Fig. 11). The negatively charged surface of CNs facilitates electrostatic interactions with Cs+ and Mn2+ ions in DI water, potentially leading to their localized accumulation near the CNs surface36–38. The subsequent interaction of these ions with Br- in the solution could lead to a locally elevated concentration of precursors, which promotes nucleation under suitable conditions. Thereafter, the adsorption and association of additional ions onto these initial sites on the cellulose matrix contribute to further crystal growth. Figure 2f shows the characteristic green emission at 519 nm from the Cs3MnBr5 crystal under 365 nm UV excitation, corresponding to an external quantum efficiency (EQE) of 46.89% as determined by quantitative integrating sphere measurements (Supplementary Fig. 12). This emission originates from a radiative transition of the excited electron from the 4T1(G) state to the ground state 6A1 within the MnBr42- tetrahedral units, which serve as the luminescent centers. The transition corresponds to an intra-3d electronic transition of Mn2+ ions, typically resulting in a narrow-band, sharp emission. The inset images are photographs of the scintillator film under white and a 365 nm UV light, respectively, showing its white appearance in the absence of excitation and bright green emission under UV illumination39,40.

To confirm the chemical integrity of the synthesized material, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was conducted on fully dried scintillator films (Fig. 2g). The XPS spectra showed binding energies corresponding to Cs 3d5/2 (724.02 eV), Mn 2p3/2 (641.05 eV), and Br 3d5/2 (68.86 eV), confirming the successful formation of the intended Cs-Mn-Br chemical bonds. Quantitative elemental analysis further indicated atomic composition of Cs (35.07%), Mn (10.66%), and Br (54.27%), in close agreement with the stoichiometry of Cs3MnBr5 perovskite crystals. These results validate that the self-assembled precursors have crystallized into the targeted perovskite composition with preserved stable chemical bonding within the cellulose matrix, suitable for scintillator applications.

Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) was conducted to assess the spatial uniformity of Cs3MnBr5 perovskite within the cellulose scaffold (Fig. 2h). The scanning electron microscopy (SEM) image (the top left panel) shows the cellulose matrix consisting of intricately intertwined fibers with diameters ranging from 2 μm to 20 μm. Elemental maps for Cs, Br, and Mn, displayed in a clockwise order from the top right, exhibit a homogeneous coating of perovskite species along the fiber surfaces. Quantitative EDS analysis shows atomic ratios of Cs, Mn, and Br at 38.6, 10.2, and 51.1%, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 13), closely aligning with the targeted stoichiometry and further confirmed with complementary XPS measurements. This concordance confirms the uniform dispersion of the scintillator components throughout the cellulose framework.

Following confirmation of its elemental composition, we verified the crystallographic phase of the Cs3MnBr5 scintillator film by X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis (Fig. 2i). Cs3MnBr5 crystallizes in a tetragonal crystal structure, wherein Mn2+ centers are coordinated by four Br- anions, and Cs+ ions occupy the interstitial sites. The lattice parameters are a = b = 9.71 Å, c = 15.46 Å, with orthogonal angles of α = β = γ = 90.00° (Supplementary Fig. 14). The XRD spectrum of the composite film (blue line) exhibits distinct peaks attributable to both the CNs (black line) and the Cs3MnBr5 phase (red line), indicating the coexistence of both components with preserved structural integrity. The diffraction peak at 29.8° (denoted by asterisks) corresponding to the (310) crystal plane with a d-spacing of 3.0 Å was enhanced while the (213) plane diffraction at 26.4° was diminished relative to the bulk Cs3MnBr5 perovskite reference (Supplementary Fig. 14a), indicating a preferential orientation or slight lattice distortion of [MnBr4]2- tetrahedra, likely induced by Mn–O interactions with the CNs, as well as by the accumulation of cesium bromide on the surface. The characteristic XRD peaks observed at 14.8°, 16.4°, and 22.7° align well with the (11¯0), (110), and (200) planes of CNs, respectively.

High-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HR-TEM) analyses further confirm the formation of a well-ordered Cs3MnBr5 crystal on the cellulose matrix (Fig. 2j). Distinct lattice fringes with a periodic spacing of 3.0 Å are clearly resolved, matching with (310) plane spacing of the tetragonal perovskite. The selected area electron diffraction (SAED) pattern in the inset of Fig. 2j, along with the fast Fourier-transformed (FFT) image (Supplementary Fig. 14b), reveals distinct diffraction spots, indicating the uniformly oriented crystalline domains. In contrast, the control sample without CNs (Supplementary Fig. 15) exhibits disordered lattice fringes and diffuse diffraction patterns, indicating significantly lower crystallinity. These observations validate the high crystallinity of Cs3MnBr5 on the cellulose matrix.

Radioluminescence characteristics of scintillator

Halide perovskite-based scintillators that incorporate heavy metal cations and halogen anions demonstrate high X-ray absorption and efficient radiative recombination, attributed to the quantum confinement effect and extended carrier lifetimes41–45. Conventional perovskite scintillators, however, typically require a film thickness up to 800 μm for optimal scintillation performance, which presents significant fabrication challenges22. By contrast, our composite architecture utilizes a heterostructure wherein only a sub-micron-thick perovskite crystal layer is grown on a cellulose matrix pre-coated with CNs, ensuring effective scintillation performance with a total thickness of approximately 220 μm (Supplementary Fig. 16). The scintillation process within this composite system is further facilitated by X-ray absorption and charge transfer from the cellulose matrix, the dominant volumetric component, to the perovskite crystallites.

Figure 3a schematically illustrates the scintillation process within a heterostructure composed of Cs3MnBr5 and cellulose with energy bandgaps of 2.4 eV and 5.5 eV, respectively. In Cs3MnBr5, the radiative properties are primarily governed by the Mn2+ d–d transitions within a tetrahedral crystal field, which splits the Mn d-orbitals into discrete energy levels of 4Eg(D), 4T2(D), 4A1, 4E(G), and 4T2(G)46,47. An extended Mn–Mn distance of approximately 7 Å, derived from theoretical calculations, in Cs3MnBr5 leads to strong exciton confinement and efficient self-trapped–exciton emission25,48. Consistent with this band alignment, UV-Vis absorption measurements yield optical band gaps of 2.02 eV for Cs3MnBr5 and 5.13 eV for cellulose (Supplementary Fig. 17), in reasonable agreement with theoretical predictions. Upon X-ray irradiation, both the perovskite and cellulose absorb high-energy photons via photoelectric effect, generating energetic electrons and excitons with absorption coefficients in X-ray spectrum on the order of 105 cm−1 and 103 cm−1, respectively49,50.

First-principles calculations were performed to probe the electronic structure of the cellulose–Cs3MnBr5 heterostructure. The (001) crystallographic plane of Cs3MnBr5 was selected as the interfacial surface for the heterojunction model, as it is the most thermodynamically stable and prevalent facet during crystal growth51–55. The projected density of states (PDOS), referenced to the Fermi level, is presented in Fig. 3b. The PDOS of cellulose reveals that its lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) lies at approximately 4.1 eV, whereas the Mn-3d states in Cs3MnBr5 are located at ~ 2.14 eV (Supplementary Fig. 18). To assess interfacial charge redistribution, we calculated work functions for the Br-terminated Cs3MnBr5 surface, cellulose, and the combined heterostructure (Supplementary Fig. 19). The intermediate work function of the heterostructure implies electrons diffuse from cellulose to Cs3MnBr5 upon contact, supported by the charge-density analysis in Fig. 3c. The three-dimensional charge density map (left) shows regions of depletion (blue) and accumulation (red), while the planar-averaged profile (right) highlights electron depletion in the cellulose region (blue shaded area) and accumulation in the Cs3MnBr5 layer (red shaded area). Integrating the charge difference yields a net transfer of electrons from cellulose to the perovskite, indicating a thermodynamically favorable donor–acceptor interaction at the interface (Supplementary Materials and Methods: Density Functional Theory Calculations). To further support the interfacial charge transfer predicted by first-principles calculations, we performed high-resolution XPS measurements (Supplementary Fig. 20). The Cs 3 d, Mn 2p, and Br 3 d core levels in the hybrid film exhibit a consistent negative shift of approximately 0.5-0.6 eV relative to pristine Cs3MnBr5. This shift indicates enhanced electronic screening associated with increased electron density in the Cs3MnBr5 phase, in agreement with the DFT-predictions of electron transfer from cellulose to the perovskite.

Following absorption, excited carriers in the perovskite rapidly undergo thermalization via phonon interactions, losing excess energy until they relax to the lowest excited state (4T1(G)). Meanwhile, high-energy excitons are induced in the cellulose matrix upon X-ray excitation and are subsequently transferred to the perovskite via interfacial charge transfer. The LUMO energy level of the cellulose is significantly higher than that of the 4Eg(D) level of Mn2+, facilitating efficient charge transfer (Fig. 3b). Subsequently, the well-defined d–d crystal field transitions in Mn2+, from 4T1(G) to the ground state (6A1)25,56, enable efficient radiative recombination, resulting in the emission of 519 nm photons.

Figure 3d shows the RL spectra of the perovskite-cellulose hybrid film measured at various accelerating voltages. In all conditions, the composite exhibits a dominant emission peak at 519 nm, and the integrated RL intensity increases linearly with dose rate, indicating suitability for contrast-based X-ray imaging (summarized in Fig. 3e). The emission wavelength and full width at half maximum (FWHM) remain unchanged across dose rates, confirming that incorporation of the cellulose matrix enhances RL intensity through charge transfer without perturbing the intrinsic spectral signature of Cs3MnBr5.

To benchmark scintillation performance under X-ray excitation, we compared the RL output of our hybrid film against a commercial CsI:Tl scintillator and two reported Mn-based scintillators under standardized conditions (200 kVp, 20 mA, 10 s) (Supplementary Fig. 21). The hybrid film exhibited a maximum RL intensity of 0.14 a.u. with a narrow FWHM of 45 nm. By comparison, the commercial CsI:Tl reference showed a lower peak intensity of 0.06 a.u. with a substantially broader FWHM of 134 nm, while (Ph4P)2MnBr4 and (Ph3PH)2MnBr4 yielded intensities of 0.07 a.u. and 0.02 a.u. (FWHM of ~40 nm each). To provide a quantitative metric, we calculated light yield by calibrating integrated RL intensity against the CsI:Tl standard ( ~ 54,000 photons MeV-1)57,58. The hybrid film reached approximately 41,000 photons MeV-1, 76% of the commercial benchmark, while the two Mn-based references yielded ~ 4400 and ~ 18,100 photons MeV-1, respectively. Given that the composite is fabricated via a simplified DI water-based synthesis, this light yield confirms the competitive scintillation efficiency at substantially reduced processing complexity.

To assess the effect of CNs on scintillation performance, cellulose matrices with and without CNs were fabricated, and their RL intensities were compared over a controlled range of perovskite loadings (Fig. 3f). The perovskite content was precisely adjusted by dispensing fixed-concentration solution in volumes of 50 to 300 μL. At lower volumes (50–100 μL), the presence of CNs had minimal influence on RL intensity. In contrast, at volumes exceeding 150 μL, a pronounced enhancement was observed, with the 300 μL CNs-containing sample exhibiting approximately twice the RL intensity of its CNs-free counterpart. Linear fits of RL intensity versus solution volume yielded slopes of 4.6 (with CNs) and 1.3 (without CNs), indicating a ~ 3.5-fold increase in sensitivity. This enhancement arises from interfacial charge transfer facilitated by CNs, which accelerates radiative relaxation of X-ray–induced excitations, as well as uniform crystal growth across the surface promoted by CNs.

To probe the underlying photophysics, time-resolved photoluminescence (TR-PL) measurements were performed for perovskite–cellulose composites with varying Cs3MnBr5 weight percentages of 90, 75, 25, and 10 wt% (Fig. 3g). In the solution-processed composites, the measured PL lifetimes decrease monotonically from 355.35 μs, 324.24 μs, 287.35 μs, to 261.82 μs, respectively, as the perovskite fraction is reduced, consistent with accelerated radiative recombination dynamics in composites with lower perovskite content59,60. This acceleration trend is most pronounced in the fully integrated hybrid scintillator film composed of cellulose paper, CNs, and Cs3MnBr5, which exhibits the shortest lifetime of 247.61 µs (Supplementary Fig. 22).

X-ray Imaging using a flexible thin-film scintillator

Conventional flat-panel X-ray detectors, which integrate rigid photodetectors on a TFT array with bulk scintillator film, are prone to geometric distortion and limited spatial accuracy when imaging non-planar structures. Although such detectors generally eliminate the veiling glare and uneven field response characteristic of image intensifiers, their planar architecture constrains close object coupling and can introduce parallax errors at oblique incidence. By contrast, our approach employs a patch-type scintillator in conjunction with a high-resolution image sensor. As demonstrated in Fig. 4a, the flexible scintillator films conform seamlessly to the surface of target objects, thereby eliminating planar-detector distortions and improving edge definition. A transparent pressure-sensitive adhesive (PSA) ensures secure, wrinkle-free adhesion, enabling the capture of undistorted X-ray projections from multiple viewpoints without loss of spatial resolution.

To assess the flexibility and mechanical stability of the platform, we conducted a tensile stress–strain test using a universal testing machine (UTM) and performed a cyclic bending test on the scintillator film. The uniformly coated PDMS acts in parallel with the cellulose matrix in response to mechanical stress, significantly enhancing mechanical robustness. Specifically, the tensile stress at maximum load increased from 10.15 MPa to 16.56 MPa, and the elastic modulus improved from 655.45 MPa to 1355.41 MPa, representing an approximately two-fold increase (Supplementary Fig. 6). Bending induces alternating tensile and compressive strains at the perovskite on cellulose composite, which can initiate microcracks and degrade luminescence performance in folded regions. To quantitatively analyze the effect, we subjected 20 μm-thick films to 1000 bending cycles at a radius of 2 cm. PL measurements taken after repeated bending and relaxation revealed that the scintillator film without PDMS exhibited structural fracture after approximately 270 bending cycles, accompanied by a sharp decrease in PL intensity at the folded region. In contrast, the PDMS-coated scintillator film showed no visible damage even after 1000 cycles, indicating that the composite architecture maintains structural integrity and emission efficiency under prolonged mechanical deformation (Supplementary Fig. 7). Having established the mechanical robustness and operational stability of the hybrid film, we next evaluated its imaging capabilities. To provide a standardized measure of the resolving power of the system, we derived the modulation transfer function (MTF) using the slanted-edge method. The measured ESF (Supplementary Fig. 23a) was differentiated to obtain the line spread function (LSF), which was then fitted with a Gaussian function (R2 = 0.92) (Supplementary Fig. 23b). This analysis yielded an MTF20 (limiting resolution) of 4.1 lp mm−1, surpassing the American College of Radiology (ACR) standard of 2.5 lp mm−1 (Supplementary Fig. 23c). This demonstrates that the thin-film geometry effectively prevents lateral light spreading, ensuring sufficient spatial resolution to support structural recovery in the downstream 3D volumetric reconstructions.

To validate the distortion-free imaging performance of our flexible scintillator, we imaged a curved calibration target from multiple viewing angles (Fig. 4b). The target comprised bar patterns of 6 mm, 4 mm, and 2 mm pitch, as well as 13 mm × 13 mm crossbar arrays on a cylindrical surface. Individual projections were acquired at various orientations (Supplementary Fig. 24). Distortion mapping-based registration confirmed that the multi-angle dataset reproduces the true spatial dimensions of the test patterns, in contrast to the geometric errors observed in single-view acquisition (Fig. 4c).

Multi-angle radiography also provides an effective means to disentangle overlapping structures that are otherwise obscured in planar X-ray projections. Whereas a single frontal view collapses all components into one intensity map, masking critical internal features, acquiring images from rotated perspectives allows individual elements to be resolved. Figure 4d illustrates the experimental configuration for imaging a cylindrical structure containing embedded metal rods, achieved by conformally wrapping a scintillator film around the cylinder surface. Sequential X-ray acquisitions spanning 30° to 210° at 60° intervals produce four complementary radiographs, with individual views capturing either four distinct rods or varying degrees of rod overlap (Fig. 4e). By integrating information across these perspectives and analyzing the spatial distribution of attenuated regions, the underlying rod arrangement can be inferred. Comparison of the computationally derived positions (blue) with the ground-truth rod configuration (yellow) confirmed high positional accuracy (Fig. 4f), with RMS values of 0.798, 0.158, 3.877, and 0.963 for rods 1 through 4, respectively. This enhanced resolution of spatially complex arrangements is particularly critical in medical imaging, for densely packed anatomical structures, demanding precise delineation for reliable diagnosis. For instance, the carpal bones, eight small, tightly clustered bones in the wrist, are often mischaracterized in planar radiography, which has an estimated diagnostic accuracy of approximately 70% compared to CT scans61. This limitation increases the risk of clinical complications such as nonunion, osteonecrosis, and post-traumatic arthritis62.

To evaluate the clinical utility of the patch-type scintillator for precise measurement, we performed multi-angle radiography of a chicken tibiotarsus (Fig. 4g). Radiographs acquired at different viewing angles produced distinct morphological variations (Fig. 4h). Furthermore, to evaluate image quality under realistic acquisition conditions, we quantified the contrast-to-noise ratio (CNR) from 32-bit radiographs. Using the mean intensity of the cortical bone ROI (μsignal = 163.9) and an adjacent background region (μbackground = 1.5, σbackground = 8.3), we obtained a CNR of 19.5, well above the Rose Criterion (CNR > 5)63 threshold of 5, confirming that the system provides sufficient contrast for reliable structural discrimination under practical imaging conditions. From three sparse-view projections, we extracted sufficient angular and structural information to perform volumetric reconstruction after masking irrelevant regions and integrating the multi-angle data (Fig. 4i). Also, the projection angles are neither fixed nor restricted to a predefined set. The reconstruction operates using the per-view pose and acquisition geometry provided for each capture (Supplementary Fig. 25), allowing full flexibility in angular configuration. Supplementary Fig. 26 quantifies reconstruction quality as a function of angular sparsity by plotting reconstruction metrics versus the number of projection views. The results show that reconstruction accuracy improves as additional angles are incorporated, while a three-view configuration is still sufficient to recover the essential 3D morphology required for portable imaging applications. Beyond angular flexibility, reliable 3D volumetric reconstruction in portable settings requires signal fidelity at reduced radiation doses. To validate this, we further conducted a task-based evaluation of fracture detectability under controlled dose-dependent conditions (Supplementary Fig. 27). As the incident dose decreases, signal contrast diminishes, and the signal-to-noise margin narrows. Our results show that fracture structural discontinuity remains quantitatively distinguishable at baseline exposure (3 mAs, 80 kVp) and at dose levels reduced to ~ 80 % and ~ 64 % of baseline. However, at ~ 51 % of baseline, the CNR drops to 1.9, below the Rose Criterion threshold of 5, and the fracture boundary becomes statistically unresolvable. This establishes a well-defined detectability threshold and confirms that the system supports diagnostically relevant imaging within a dose range compatible with sparse-view acquisition. Such capability is particularly valuable for anatomically complex regions, such as the acetabulum, where accurate classification, preoperative planning, and postoperative monitoring rely on comprehensive structural information that single-view radiography often fails to provide63–65.

Neural network-based 3D reconstruction

Figure 5a shows the workflow for 3D reconstruction, which integrates deep learning-based segmentation with a triplane‑based Neural Radiance Field (NeRF) framework using sparse‑view X‑ray projections (step-by-step visualization in Supplementary Fig. 28). A high‑resolution digital hand phantom was placed at the center of a virtual X‑ray gantry (Fig. 5b). Three flat‑panel detectors at 0°, + 30°, and – 30° each captured a single 2D projection (Views 1–3), emulating clinical constraints on angle count. Diagnostically relevant anatomy was segmented by an ROI network specialized on hand X-ray data. Specifically, we employed the hand gesture identification (MMpose)66 algorithm as a Prompt Predictor Network (PPN) with memory–attention blocks to emit positive foreground and negative background point prompts to a frozen SAM image encoder (See methods). Prompts were passed to the SAM mask decoder to produce pixel-accurate masks restricted to clinically meaningful regions19. The segmented views were then encoded and mapped into a triplane representation for subsequent NeRF-based volumetric reconstruction.

Figure 5c summarizes the network that generates a 3D object. SAM employs a Vision Transformer (ViT)67 backbone that tokenizes each X-ray into non-overlapping patches and uses self-attention to relate distant spatial tokens. With point prompts as queries, SAM maintains segmentation consistency across views and produces precise delineate masks that surpass coarse region-based approximations (Fig. 5d and Supplementary Fig. 29), providing a consistent and anatomically precise representation of target structures for the subsequent 3D reconstruction.

The segmented images are then fed into TripoSR20, an implicit model specifically designed to recover high-fidelity 3D volumes from sparse-view without dense volumetric scans (e.g., CT or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)). Rather than relying on explicit voxel grids or point cloud priors, TripoSR employs a triplane NeRF68 representation, decomposing the 3D structure into three orthogonal feature planes (XY, XZ, YZ) to enable efficient volumetric inference from sparse inputs. A structured comparison of representative ultra-sparse reconstruction approaches and their acquisition requirements is provided in Supplementary Table T2. Furthermore, by leveraging TripoSR, we utilize the inherent capability of NeRF to reconstruct continuous 3D volumes from sparse, irregularly sampled angles. This computational resilience naturally complements the physical constraints of sparse-view X-ray imaging, transforming a limited set of conformal projections into a dense volumetric model. Radiance F is modeled by Fx,d=(σx,L(x,d)), where x denotes a 3D point in space, d represents the viewing direction, σ(x) is the volumetric density, and L(x,d) represents grayscale intensity at point x. A transformer decoder with cross-attention maps 2D ViT features onto the three triplane, thereby learning consistent spatial correlations across views and enforcing geometric coherence in the reconstructed volume.

A Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) decodes triplane features to predict volumetric density and grayscale intensity at any 3D query point. These continuous-valued fields are then composited along each X-ray projection ray via the volumetric rendering integral Ir=∫tntfTtσrtLsrt,ddt, where I(r) represents the synthesized intensity, T(t) is the accumulated transmittance modeling X-ray attenuation, σ is the spatial density, and Ls is the radiance emitted at each point along the ray. This continuous representation captures subtle internal density variations (e.g., bones) without explicit surface modeling and preserves fine structural details, including curvature and edge sharpness. Training uses a composite loss function that combines volumetric rendering loss with silhouette consistency loss, and smoothness regularization (see methods), to balance fidelity and geometric regularity.

To reconstruct a complete 3D bone volume from multi-angle radiographs, we fuse view-specific reconstructions through a neural network pipeline. First, each view-specific mesh is normalized into a common coordinate frame centering at the centroid and scaling to fit within [− 0.5, 0.5]3. The normalized meshes are voxelized into binary occupancy grids and are then merged by a volumetric 3D U-Net with attention gates, trained to integrate overlapping geometries, fill view-dependent voids and reconcile inconsistencies. A loss function comprised of the Dice coefficient and binary cross-entropy (BCE) was used to ensure that anatomical features and bone morphology are retained in the final output.

For synthetic validation, a 3D anatomical model generated virtual radiographs at three orientations, center frontal (Fig. 5d-2), 30° to the left (Fig. 5d-1) and 30° to the right (Fig. 5d-3). For the network training, we employed more than 5000 datasets generated from multiple anatomically distinct hand models (Supplementary Figs. 30–31). Each projection was first segmented via the SAM model to delineate osseous structures, followed by the reconstruction with TripoSR into three independent 3D density volumes. Finally, the view-specific reconstructions were registered into a common frame and fused through a volume integration network, providing a cohesive 3D representation of the bone anatomy (Fig. 5e). Chamfer Distance (CD) was employed to quantitatively assess the geometric consistency, yielding a value of 0.017 for bone reconstructions. This was supported by a structural similarity index (SSIM) of 0.852 ± 0.037 which measures perceptual similarity in local contrast and structure, the peak signal-to-noise ratio (PSNR) of 19.09 ± 4.52 dB which reflects the relative level of reconstruction error, and the root-mean-square error (RMSE) of 0.127 which captures the average voxel-wise discrepancy between the two volumes, confirming the fidelity of the recovered 3D morphology despite sparse input data69,70. Supplementary Fig. 32 shows the reconstruction accuracy across varying noise levels (Gaussian noise intensity factor 0–10 %) and beam energies (50–80 keV), Supplementary Table 1 and Supplementary Fig. 33 show the system-matched degradation stress test, evaluating reconstruction fidelity under controlled degradation modes, including beam hardening, PSF blur, detector noise, and scatter, applied to simulated projections. Supplementary Fig. 34 further evaluates workflow robustness to out-of-plane (z-axis) angular misalignment across offsets of ± 15°.

In summary, we developed a flexible patch-type perovskite scintillator by hybridizing Cs3MnBr5 with a cellulose matrix and seeding nucleation with CNs to promote uniform film growth across a large area. This flexible platform conforms intimately to complex geometries, enabling the acquisition of distortion-free, multi-angle 2D X-ray images that serve as input for AI-driven 3D reconstruction. After identifying the ROIs, the SAM accurately isolates target structures, while the TripoSR network reconstructs these segmented images into high-fidelity 3D models. While modern CT systems provide unparalleled image quality for comprehensive clinical diagnostics, their bulk and infrastructural requirements limit their use in emergency or point-of-care settings. Our integrated platform presents a complementary and versatile alternative. By combining a flexible, conformable scintillator with AI-driven sparse-view reconstruction, it enables high-fidelity 3D imaging in resource-constrained environments where traditional CT is inaccessible. Ultimately, by overcoming the geometric limitations of conventional 2D X-ray imaging, while significantly reducing cumulative radiation exposure compared to CT scans, our integrated AI algorithms with the flexible scintillation platform represent a feasibility-level demonstration of a versatile and patient-friendly approach toward 3D diagnostic imaging applications.

Methods

Fabrication of scintillator patch

The flexible scintillator film is fabricated through a sequential two-step process. First, CNs are uniformly distributed onto a cellulose matrix (No. 6, ADVANTEC) using an electrospray system. A 1 wt% of CNs dispersion in DI water is delivered via syringe pump (EP100, NanoNC) through a single-capillary spinneret with a 22-gauge needle under 21 kV (HV30, NanoNC) at 30 cm tip-to-substrate distance, then dried at 80 °C for over 4 h. Subsequently, Cs3MnBr5 microcrystals are grown using a 0.075 mM precursor solution by dissolving 3 mmol of CsBr (99.999%, Sigma-Aldrich) and 1 mmol of MnBr2 (98%, Sigma-Aldrich) in DI water. The CNs-coated matrix is then immersed in this solution and dried in a convection oven, initially at 50 °C for 6 h, followed by 70 °C for 12 h. Layer-by-layer stacking of CNs-coated cellulose matrix enabled precise control of film thickness. Finally, the hybrid composite is passivated with a PDMS overcoat, Sylgard 184 (DOW) mixed at a 10:1 base-to- curing agent ratio, degassed, applied evenly and cured at 50 °C for 24 h, to yield the encapsulated flexible scintillator film.

Molecular dynamics simulation

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulation was conducted by using Materials Studio 2024. The simulation cell comprised 450 CsBr, 150 MnBr2, and 40 cellulose (C6H10O5) molecules arranged to approximate the early-stage nucleation environment of Cs3MnBr5. Simulations employed the COMPASS III force field, which provides ab initio-derived parameters for both organic and inorganic species. Temperature was maintained at 450 K, corresponding to the crystallization temperature of Cs3MnBr5, using the Nosé–Hoover–Langevin thermostat in the canonical (NVT) ensemble to ensure proper sampling of thermal fluctuations. The integration timestep was set to 1 fs, and each trajectory was propagated for 2 ns. Post-processing analyses were conducted with the Forcite module to extract small-angle X-ray scattering (SAXS) profiles, radial distribution function (RDF), and velocity autocorrelation function (VACF), providing insight into structural ordering and dynamical behavior during hybrid crystallization.

Density functional theory calculations

Density-functional theory (DFT) calculations were performed to evaluate the electronic band structure and interfacial charge transfer. Calculations used the QM/MM simulation cluster [NFEC-2025-02-303332] and Vienna ab initio simulation package (VASP 5.3.5)1–3 with the Projector Augmented Wave (PAW) method4. The Perdew Burke Ernzerhof (PBE)5 form of the generalized gradient approximations (GGA)6 described exchange and correlation. A kinetic energy cutoff of 550 eV was applied for the plane-wave basis set, and electron occupations were treated with Gaussian smearing with a width of 0.05 eV. Structural optimizations were conducted using the conjugate gradient (CG) algorithm7 until the residual forces fell below 0.01 eV Å−1.

The tetragonal Cs3MnBr5 structure (space group I4/mcm, Materials Project ID: mp-27332)8 provided the bulk starting geometry. Relaxed lattice parameters (a = b = 9.70 Å, c = 15.45 Å) agreed with experiment to within ~ 0.7%. A cluster-like thin slab was used to represent the nucleation process of Cs3

MnBr5. The (001) facet was chosen due to its low Miller index, as the dominant facet is generally stable and thus tends to be a less active site in crystallization. 3 × 3 × 1 Monkhorst-Pack k-points grids were used for the calculation of slab models for nucleation. The density of states (DOS) calculation showed good agreement with previous first-principles studies9. A denser k-mesh of 21 × 21 × 13 was employed for DOS calculations. The formation energies (Ef) are calculated by Ef = Etotal – ∑ n · μ, where Etotal is the total energy of Cs3MnBr5 with and without cellulose during the nucleation process, μ is the chemical potential of Cs3MnBr5 and cellulose, and n is the number of components in the stoichiometry.

To model surface terminations, both Cs–Br-terminated and Br-terminated slabs were constructed, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 35a. A vacuum layer of 20 Å was included to eliminate spurious interactions between periodic images. Increasing the vacuum thickness beyond 20 Å does not affect the calculated work function; therefore, a 20 Å vacuum was used consistently throughout the study. The DOS, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 35b, indicated that the Br-terminated slab better preserved the bulk-like electronic structure. Besides, total energy calculations of two different slabs (Br-terminated: − 3.72 eV atom−1 vs Cs-Br-terminated: − 3.47 eV atom−1) showed that the Br-terminated slab is energetically more favorable. The thickness of the perovskite slab and the corresponding k-point mesh were chosen based on convergence studies of the DOS (Supplementary Fig. 35c) and work function. Ultimately, a 5-layer symmetric perovskite slab and a Γ-centered k-mesh of 5 × 5 × 1 were used for structural relaxation and self-consistent field (SCF) calculations, while a denser 21 × 21 × 1 mesh was employed for DOS computations.

Characterizations of scintillator patch

For Fourier-transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy, 150 mg powders of both CNs and the hybrid composite were prepared in pellet form. A background spectrum was first acquired, after 64 measurements were averaged to yield the final spectrum. UV-visible absorption measurements were performed at room temperature using an HP 8453 UV−vis spectrometer (Agilent) with a 10 mm quartz. Prior to analysis, Cs3MnBr5 dissolved in DI water was dispersed in isopropyl alcohol (IPA), and the solvent baseline was subtracted. Spectras were acquired from 200 to 800 nm range at a scan speed of 10 nm s−1. For PL measurements, a 405 nm diode laser (L405-5W, Deloslaser) was coupled to a microscope through a single-mode optical fiber (P1-305A-FC, Thorlabs) and positioned via a piezoelectric stage (Albatross, Nanofocus). The excitation beam was split 50:50 (BSW10R, Thorlabs) and filtered by a long-pass edge filter (FF02-409/LP-25, Semrock). Time-correlated single-photon counting (TCSPC) measurements used a 375 nm picosecond diode laser (BDS-SM Lasers, Becker & Hickl) via a free space mirror mount system. Laser intensities were modulated with a variable neutral density (ND) filter, and emission was collected through a multi-mode optical fiber (M50L02S-A, Thorlabs) into a spectrometer (SR-303I-B, Andor) with PL spectra acquired via SOLIS software (Andor), and lifetime data was recorded using custom-built software. The microscope setup and white light illumination was controlled by NANOimage software (Nanofocus Inc.). Mechanical robustness was assessed by subjecting the films, mounting on an elastic support, to 10 mm bending at a speed of 2 mm s−1 using an STS-RT-1AXIS (Sciencetown). After a predefined number of bending cycles, PL from the bent region was measured to assess performance retention. Radioluminescence (RL) spectra were conducted under ambient conditions using an X-RAD 320 system (Precision Inc.) operated at 200 kVp and 10 mA and acquired with a spectrometer (CCS200, Thorlabs) through a 200 μm core optical fiber.

Radioluminescence measurements

Radioluminescence spectra were acquired across the diagnostic X-ray range (50–250 kVp, 5–40 mA) using a X-ray irradiator (X-RAD 320TM, Precision, USA). The flexible scintillator film was placed orthogonally to the incident beam, and the emitted light was collected via a 200 µm core optical fiber (Thorlabs) into a compact fiber-coupled spectrometer (CCS200, Thorlabs), covering 200–1000 nm wavelength range. All measurements were performed at room temperature under ambient conditions. To verify the delivered dose, Gafchromic™ EBT3 radiochromic films (Ashland Inc., USA) were placed immediately behind the scintillator to quantify absorbed radiation through optical density analysis of the exposed films.

X-ray Imaging procedure

X-ray radiography was performed using a custom imaging module under diagnostic settings of 80 kVp and 3 mA with a 1 second exposure (EXSYS Portable, DRTEC). Image acquisition was carried out with a flat-panel TFT detector (model EXT 2430H, DRTECH), with the flexible scintillator film placed directly on top of the detector surface. Data acquisition and processing were conducted using the ECali1 software. For three-dimensional reconstruction, projections were acquired at multiple orientations (0°, ± 30°). This sparse-view strategy substantially reduces cumulative radiation exposure by reconstructing volumetric data from only three projections. Imaging performance was assessed by measuring spatial resolution with opaque patterns, evaluating edge sharpness, and performing structural visualization across the various viewing angles.

Neural network algorithm

Physics-based synthetic data generation

To overcome the scarcity of paired volumetric-radiographic clinical data, we established a high-fidelity simulation pipeline (Supplementary Fig. 1) utilizing the gVirtualXRay (gVXR). This approach enabled the deterministic generation of large-scale training datasets with ground-truth geometric precision. To ensure anatomical consistency across the synthetic samples, we employed various distinct 3D hand models (Supplementary Fig. 37), which were normalized to a fixed sagittal height of 200 mm and centrally aligned within the simulation volume. We employed a parallel-beam configuration to emulate far-field acquisition, thereby minimizing perspective distortion and simplifying subsequent geometric calibration. The virtual detector was modeled as a planar sensor array with 1440 × 1440 pixels and a pixel pitch of 0.2 mm, establishing a fixed spatial sampling frequency. Radiographic synthesis utilized monochromatic beams with energies spanning 0.05–0.08 MeV, and photon statistics were modeled by constraining the flux to 500–1000 photons per ray, simulating varying dose regimes.

Each simulated scene comprised a dual-component phantom consisting of registered surface meshes for skeletal and soft-tissue structures. The bone component was assigned to a multi-element cortical-bone composition at a density of 1.85 g cm−3, while soft tissue was approximated as a water-equivalent homogeneous medium with a density of 1.0 g cm−3. Photon transport was computed via ray-casting, where intensity reduction followed the Beer–Lambert law of exponential attenuation based on the cumulative path length through these distinct material densities. To foster generalization in the reconstruction pipeline, we generated a heterogeneous dataset by varying ray energies, photon noise statistics, and anatomical morphologies. For each hand model, multi-angle projections were acquired by stochastically sampling azimuthal angles across a full angular range. This process yielded a paired dataset connecting 2D radiographic projections with their corresponding 3D volumetric ground-truth, providing a robust foundation for supervised learning.

ROI identification Network

The ROI identification pipeline was designed to isolate diagnostically relevant regions in X-ray images and generate point-based prompts for subsequent segmentation using the SAM. First, raw X-ray images were enhanced using gamma correction to improve bone visibility. Images were normalized to [0, 1], transformed via logarithmic scaling for better dynamic range, and then gamma-corrected (γ = 0.5) to brighten mid-tones while preserving bone contrast. The resulting images were inverted so that bone regions appear as high-intensity areas suitable for downstream processing. Next, MMPose, a pre-trained hand pose estimation network, was employed to detect 21 anatomical keypoints corresponding to the hand skeletal structure (wrist, finger joints, and fingertips). The model outputs keypoint coordinates (x, y) along with confidence scores for each detected landmark. These coordinates were used for the prompt generation for SAM: we converted detected keypoints into positive and negative point prompts for SAM-based bone segmentation:

Positive prompts include: (1) all detected keypoints except fingertips (16 points), and (2) interpolated points between the wrist and each finger base to ensure coverage of metacarpal bone regions. Interpolation factors (α = 0.45–0.55) were empirically tuned to place prompts along bone shafts.

Negative prompts include: (1) extrapolated points beyond each fingertip (extending 10% past the detected tip along the finger direction) to exclude non-bone regions, and (2) external boundary points placed at a fixed margin (50 pixels) outside the hand bounding box (top, left, right, and corner positions) to explicitly mark background regions.

Segment anything model

The Segment Anything Model (SAM) was applied to perform bone segmentation using point-based prompts generated by the ROI network. Image encoder of SAM was built on a Vision Transformer (ViT-Huge) backbone and comprised 32 transformer blocks, each with a hidden dimension of 1280 and 16 attention heads. Input X-ray images are tokenized into 16 × 16 spatial patches, enabling the self-attention layers to capture both local and global contextual features. Point prompts, annotating positive (bone) and negative (background) pixels, are converted by the prompts encoder into positional embeddings and incorporated as auxiliary tokens alongside the image tokens. A lightweight transformer-based mask decoder, consisting of 2 layers and 256 hidden dimensions each, then produces a 256 × 256 pixel-wise segmentation mask using a sigmoid activation. TripoSR 3D Reconstruction Network

The TripoSR model employs a ViT encoder consisting of 12 transformer blocks with a hidden dimension of 768, and 12 attention heads. Segmented X-ray images are first resized to 512 × 512 pixels and tokenized into non-overlapping 16 × 16 patches. These patches were subsequently processed by the transformer encoder to extract 2D feature representations. These encoded features are fed into a transformer-based decoder incorporating cross-attention layers which project them onto three orthogonal triplane feature grids, each with a resolution of 64 × 64 and a feature dimension of 40. The decoder comprises 16 transformer layers, each with 16 attention heads and a hidden dimension of 1024, alternating self-attention over the triplane tokens with cross-attention to the encoder features.

With X-ray image input, the model simulates X-ray formation by tracing rays through a volumetric domain and sampling radiometric and structural information at discrete points along each ray. 3D U-Net fusion network

Multi-view 3D reconstructions were fused using a 3D U-Net with attention-gated skip connections. Each view-specific mesh from TripoSR was normalized to a canonical coordinate frame [-0.5, 0.5]3 and voxelized into a binary occupancy grid at resolution 192 × 192 × 192. The N-view voxel volumes were stacked as input channels to the network. The encoder path consisted of four stages with progressively increasing feature channels (16-32-64-128-256), each employing two 3 × 3 × 3 convolutions with batch normalization and ReLU activation, followed by max pooling. Evaluation protocols and training/test parameters

To ensure complete reproducibility, reconstruction performance is evaluated on a held-out test split generated via a fixed random seed. The dataset, organized by sample identifiers, contains multiple radiographic projections and their corresponding ground-truth 3D bone geometries. We employ a deterministic 80/10/10 split (train/validation/test), ensuring all reported metrics represent the full test distribution rather than a curated subset. For each test case, the sparse input is constructed by sampling three views from predefined angular acquisition windows ( − 180° to − 135°, − 45° to 45°, and 135° to 180°), with a minimum angular separation constraint enforced to avoid near-duplicate views.

Prior to 3D volumetric estimation, all 2D projection images are preprocessed. Intensities are normalized, log-scaled to compress dynamic range, gamma-corrected (γ = 0.5), and inverted to enhance cortical bone contrast. To isolate the target anatomy and suppress background structure, 21 anatomical key-points are estimated and converted into positive and negative point prompts for the SAM. The resulting segmentation mask gates each radiograph to a consistent region of interest (ROI). This preprocessing and ROI-gating strategy is applied identically across training and testing phases to prevent data leakage.

To avoid artificially inflating performance metrics, reconstruction outputs and ground-truth volumes are evaluated within a deterministic canonical coordinate frame defined by the acquisition geometry. Both prediction and target are converted into discrete voxel occupancy grids utilizing identical coordinate normalization and alignment parameters. Per-case iterative closest point (ICP) registration is deliberately omitted prior to metric computation, as volumes share a common canonical frame, ICP is unnecessary, and its application would risk concealing intrinsic geometric failure modes and rotational instabilities. Quantitative evaluation is performed in the 3D voxel domain using a fixed grid resolution and static binarization thresholds, eliminating post-processing-dependent variance. When surface-based metrics are required, voxel occupancies are converted into point clouds using a fixed occupancy threshold (0.5) and a capped sampling budget (up to 10,000 points), with coordinates normalized to [− 1, 1]. Standard mesh extraction, smoothing, and decimation are utilized exclusively for qualitative visualization. All reported quantitative metrics are derived directly from the fixed voxel and point-cloud conversion rules described above.

Volumetric fidelity is quantified using Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio (PSNR) and Root Mean Square Error (RMSE), computed directly in the 3D voxel domain. PSNR uses a fixed dynamic range with a maximum value of 1.0. Structural Similarity Index (SSIM) is evaluated in 3D using a localized Gaussian window (size 7, σ = 1.5) with standard stability constants. Macroscopic geometric accuracy is quantified via the Chamfer distance between thresholded voxel-derived point clouds11,12, calculated as the average bidirectional nearest-neighbor distances subject to the sampling budget and spatial normalization described above.
