Terahertz Time-Domain imaging of feet of diabetic and non-diabetic patients
Authors: Goretti G Hernandez-Cardoso, Monica Ortiz-Martinez, Blanca O Murillo-Ortiz, Alfredo Campos Mejia, Lauro F Amador-Medina, Gerardo Gutierrez-Torres, Edgar S Reyes-Reyes, Irving Salas-Gutierrez, Enrique Castro-Camus
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe present a dataset of terahertz time-domain spectral images of the feet of 80 individuals with type 2 diabetes and 98 non-diabetic subjects. The dataset includes demographic information such as age, sex, weight, height, smoking status, and body mass index. For diabetic participants, the results of the Semmes-Weinstein monofilament test used to assess peripheral neuropathy are also provided. To our knowledge, this dataset represents the largest collection of in vivo cutaneous terahertz reflection spectra from human subjects published to date. The data are made available to support independent analysis and may enable further investigation of the dielectric properties and structure of human skin, as well as potential effects of diabetes. Scripts for loading and visualizing the data are provided in both Matlab and Python. The dataset and accompanying scripts are publicly available at https://doi.org/10.6084/m9.figshare.31052176.

Background & Summary

Terahertz time-domain spectroscopy (THz-TDS)1 and imaging2 are techniques that have evolved dramatically since their introduction in the late 1980s and 1990s3. A milestone was the replacement of Ti:sapphire lasers4, by much more economical and robust rare-earth-doped fiber lasers5, which in turn has enabled turn-key commercial systems which are now used in many relevant environments such as in industry. While no clinical application of THz radiation is routinely used yet, this is still a field of vigorous investigation owing to the potential benefits that it may provide6,7. Firstly, THz radiation is non-ionizing, thus, safe to be used at low power in living organisms. Secondly, its potential to detect hydration variations in tissues opens a plethora of possible diagnostic uses.

One of those potential applications appeared in 2017, when the possibility of correlating the hydration level of the feet of subjects with the presence or absence of diabetes was demonstrated8. Subsequently, in 2022, a much wider study demonstrated that the dehydration level does not only correlate with the presence or absence of diabetes, but also with the presence and severity of peripheral neuropathy, a typical long term consequence of diabetes that together with peripheral vascular disease are the underlying cause of what is called diabetic foot syndrome, which produces ulceration in up to 15% of diabetics and leads to partial or total amputation in up to 5% of those suffering from diabetes9.

In this article, we present the whole dataset of THz time-domain-spectral images corresponding to a total of 80 type-2 diabetic (45 females and 35 males) and 98 non-diabetic (70 females and 28 males) subjects, obtained with a first evaluation of a technique which we call “Moisture MApping by Terahertz” (MMAT) as a diagnostic test of diabetic foot syndrome. Although, there is at least one other database10, this data-set is to the best of our knowledge the largest publicly available set of in-vivo terahertz measurements on humans to date.

Methods

Terahertz image acquisition

All terahertz imaging data shared in this work were acquired following the experimental setup and measurement protocol reported in the previously published study by Hernandez-Cardoso et al.11. To improve reproducibility and traceability, the main characteristics of the acquisition scenario and measurement workflow are summarized here.

The data were obtained using the MMAT scanner, which integrates a fiber-coupled THz-TDS system into a dedicated imaging platform designed for in-vivo plantar measurements. The MMAT scanner, shown in Fig. 1(a), consists of an elevated platform incorporating a 9.52 mm-thick (3/8 in) high-density-polyethylene (HDPE) measurement window and a chair. During image acquisition, patients were seated and instructed to place both feet flat on top of the HDPE window, ensuring uniform contact across the plantar surface (Fig. 1(b)). A Terahertz transceiver module is positioned under the HDPE window in order to scan the plantar surface from below, without direct contact between the optical components and the patient (Fig. 1(c)).

MMAT scanner and measurement configuration for plantar Terahertz imaging. (a) Schematic figure of the MMAT; (b) upper view of the MMAT and patient position during imaging acquisition; (c) lateral view of the MMAT system. The images shown are schematic illustrations generated using AI-based tools for explanatory purposes.

Prior to imaging, patients were requested not to apply moisturizing products to their feet. The HDPE window was cleaned with isopropanol before each acquisition. Patients were instructed to remain stationary throughout the scan to ensure stable contact conditions. Both feet were scanned simultaneously, with a total acquisition time of less than ten minutes per terahertz image.

The terahertz imaging system is driven by a Yb:fiber femtosecond laser, whose output is divided into pump and probe beams. The pump beam excites a photoconductive antenna acting as a terahertz transmitter, while the probe beam is routed through a mechanical delay line and directed to a photoconductive receiver for time-resolved detection. The transmitter and receiver are coupled using an AXA5001 collinear adapter, forming a normal-incidence transceiver operating in reflection geometry, as schematically illustrated in Fig. 2(a). The collinear transceiver is mounted on motorized rails and translated as a single unit under the HDPE window, enabling raster-scan imaging of the plantar surface. Our system generates 1000 waveforms per second and the raster scanning speed was set to 100 mm/s acquiring one pixel per millimeter, therefore, the waveform associated to each pixel consists of the average of 10 waveforms. The typical waveform from our dataset has a 1.2 THz bandwidth at -35 dB, a dynamic range of about 37 dB at 300 GHz and a spectral resolution of 6 GHz. Each image consists of 240 × 245 pixels separated by 1 mm.

The output of a Yb:fiber femtosecond laser driving photoconductive emitter and detector; (b) Schematic representation of the propagation of the terahertz pulse and its interaction with the HDPE measurement window and the plantar tissue; (c) Illustration of the relationship between spatial pixels and terahertz time-domain waveforms in MMAT imaging.

Terahertz signal structure

Terahertz images acquired with the MMAT scanner are constructed by recording a complete terahertz time-domain waveform at every raster position across the plantar surface. Each pixel in the two-dimensional image therefore corresponds to a full temporal electric field trace that encodes the local interaction of the terahertz pulse with the HDPE window and the feet.

For every spatial point, the THz-TDS system captures a raw electric field waveform consisting of two dominant reflections whose origin is described here. The raw terahertz data acquired for each feet-pair are stored as a three dimensional tensors, these are directly the data that we provide in our database without further processing. The first index corresponds to the number of spatial pixels along the horizontal (x) direction, the second index corresponds to the number of spatial pixels along the vertical (y) direction, and the third index correspond to the sampled terahertz time-domain waveform from each pixel at a time step of 0.1 ps. This structure can be expressed as data(x,y,t), where each (x, y) coordinate maps to a full time-domain waveform (Fig. 2(c)).

Each three-dimensional data set is stored in a binary file with a . picomage extension. To facilitate data reuse and visualization, we provide Python and MatLab scripts that allow users to read, load, and inspect these files.

The final shared dataset comprises measurements from 178 participants, including both feet for each patient, resulting in approximately 58,800 spatial points per foot. For each . picomage file we supply the corresponding clinical and demographic metadata. This organization allows other users to directly explore the raw terahertz signals, independently of the original processing and interpretation, which we published separately at11.

Participants and study design

A total of 178 volunteers participated in a cross-sectional clinical screening study designed to investigate the relationship between plantar skin hydration and diabetic foot syndrome11. The group included 80 adults (45 female and 35 male) with type-2 diabetes and 98 non-diabetic controls (70 female and 28 male), all recruited from clinical facilities of the Instituto Mexicano del Seguro Social (IMSS). Our database contains only patients from the Unidad Medica de Alta Especialidad 1 Bajio (Leon, Gto. Mexico). Demographic and clinical variables collected for each participant included age, sex, weight, height, smoking status, body mass index (BMI), and for diabetic participants, the results of the Semmes-Weinstein monifilament (SWM) test used to evaluate peripheral neuropathy.

Ethical approval and consent

The study protocol was approved by the National Commission of Scientific Research of IMSS (protocol R-2018-785-031, with amendments FE-2020-785-17 and FE-2020-785-18). All volunteers were informed of the study objectives, procedures, potential risk, and their rights as participants. Written informed consent was obtained from every participant prior to data acquisition. All procedures adhered of institutional and national research ethics guidelines.

Data Records

All terahertz time-domain images are available at12 and are provided exactly as acquired by the MMAT scanner, without post-processing. The repository contains raw terahertz imaging data, participant metadata, and example scripts for data access and visualization.

Raw terahertz imaging data

The raw terahertz data are provided in two compressed (ZIP) files corresponding to the two study groups. The first one, “Non-diabetic control group”, contains 98 files with the . picoimage extension. Each file corresponds to a single MMAT scan acquired from one control participant. The second folder, “Participants with type-2 diabetes”, contains 80 . picoimage files corresponding to MMAT scans acquired from participants diagnosed with type-2 diabetes.

Each . picoimage file stores a three-dimensional array of signed 16-bits integers (int16) with dimensions (240, 245, 1600). The first dimension (240) corresponds to the number of spatial pixels along the x direction, the second dimension (245) corresponds to the number of spatial pixels along the y direction, and the third dimension (1600) corresponds to the number of sampled points in the terahertz time-domain waveform at each spatial location. The spatial sampling interval is 1 mm in both the x and y directions and a time-step of 0.1 ps in the temporal dimension. Thus, each (x, y) vector of the array represent a complete terahertz electric-field waveform associated with a specific pixel on the plantar surface.

Data access scripts

To facilitate reuse and reproducibility of the raw terahertz time-domain imaging data, the repository includes a Python and MatLab scripts named read_picoimage.py and Mlab_reader.zip. These scripts provides a complete example of how to read binary .picoimage files, extract the three-dimensional waveform dataset, and visualize representative outputs.

The scripts parses the file header to retrieve metadata (e.g., number of spatial pixels, pixel size, number of temporal samples, and interval between samples) and loads the full dataset into a three-dimensional array of size (NX, Ny, Nt), where each spatial pixel contains an associated time-domain waveform. Technical Validation

All measurements were acquired with a commercial, fiber-coupled terahertz time-domain spectroscopy system integrated including a terahertz imaging platform for raster scanning (API Teragauge 1000/160). The system is based on a terahertz control paired with fiber-coupled transmitter and receiver modules operating in reflection geometry. The imaging platform provides motorized computer-controlled scanning with fixed geometry and repeatable positioning.

All plantar scans were performed using the same image scanning configuration, including fixed raster-scan geometry, spatial step size, scan area, and acquisition parameters. The relative alignment between the terahertz transceiver and the HDPE window was maintained constant for all measurements.

The placement of the feet of the participants was systematized using marks on the scanning platform, which helped to homogenize distance, orientation, and support between subjects. The HDPE window was cleaned between scans. Repeat scans acquired under identical conditions exhibited consistent waveform morphology and amplitude, indicating repeatability of the combined terahertz control unit and imaging platform.

The data that we are presenting were acquired within the framework of a previously published study11. While the present Data Descriptor does not include signal processing or quantitative analysis, the commercially validated acquisition hardware supports the reliability and re-usability of the raw terahertz waveform data.

It is worth mentioning that we conducted a series of tests to assess the reproducibility of the terahertz measurements. Two consecutive measurements were performed on a single subject (not included in the study cohort), separated by a 10-minute interval, while the feet remained immobile on the HDPE window throughout the experiment. As can be readily seen from Eqs. 1 and 2, the ratio Esam/Eref is independent of Ein, thereby eliminating contributions arising from fluctuations in the spectrometer response due, for instance, to temperature variations or changes in laser intensity. When evaluating the image obtained by integrating Esam/Eref over the frequency range from 100 GHz to 300 GHz and subsequently integrating over all foot pixels, the variation between the two measurements was found to be smaller than 0.3%. This result appears to rule out any significant changes associated with the occlusion effect13. We believe this is primarily because the occlusion effect develops on a timescale of tens of seconds, whereas subjects require several minutes to be positioned in the MMAT scanner, followed by approximately 9 minutes for image acquisition. Consequently, the occlusion effect is likely to have reached a quasi-steady state before the scan begins. In addition, a third measurement was performed on the same subject 24 hours later, yielding an average variation of approximately 7%. This result further demonstrates the good quantitative repeatability of the measurement protocol.

Data availability

The complete dataset described in this article, including raw terahertz imaging data, participant metadata, and supporting documentation is publicly available in the Figshare repository under the title “Data set for: Terahertz Time-Domain imaging for feet of diabetic and non-diabetic patients”. The dataset can be accessed via the digital identifier (DOI): (https://doi.org/10.6084/m9.figshare.31052176).

The repository includes the raw MMAT scanner output files (.picoimage) for all participants, the corresponding metadata file (Metadata.xlsx), and example scripts for data access and visualization. The data are released under the Creative Commons Attribution 4.0 International (CC BY 4.0) license, permitting reuse of the data provided that appropriate attribution to the original authors and source is given.

Code availability

Script for reading, loading, and visualizing the raw terahertz imaging data are provided as part of the Figshare repository associated with this dataset (https://doi.org/10.6084/m9.figshare.31052176). These include a Python script (read_picoimage. py) that demonstrate how to decode the proprietary file format . picoimage, access the three-dimensional data arrays, and extract individual waveforms or two-dimensional images. The script provided is intended to facilitate data reuse and reproducibility and can be adapted for custom analysis pipelines. No additional proprietary software is required to access or inspect the shared data.
