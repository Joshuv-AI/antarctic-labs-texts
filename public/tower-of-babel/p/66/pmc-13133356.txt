Non-linear and switchable light preferences of nocturnal loach (Misgurnus anguillicaudatus)
Authors: Yuki Yoshikawa, Keiko Okano, Toshiyuki Okano
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLight conditions have substantial effects on living organisms through a variety of aspects of individual life, such as survival, feeding, reproduction, stress responses, and behavioral rhythms. In this study, to evaluate the importance of light conditions for nocturnal fish, the spontaneous spatial preferences and swimming activity of the loach (Misgurnus anguillicaudatus), a nocturnal freshwater fish, was investigated using controlled light intensity gradients. Despite observing individual variations in dark preference responses, these preferences were not consistent at the individual level when measured repeatedly at four-week intervals. This suggests that individual differences in light/dark preferences may reflect state-dependent variations rather than fixed traits. When the light intensity in the bright area was changed from 2.4 to 400 µW/cm2, the loaches showed a non-linear relationship between light intensity and light/dark preference. These results suggest that loaches usually avoid dim areas near the light–dark boundary and sometimes change their light/dark preferences. This behavior may contribute to their compartmentalization of safe dark areas surrounded by barriers and their habitat expansion by occasionally climbing over the barriers and migrating to suitable locations further away. Overall, this study compared the light-intensity-dependent behavioral patterns of nocturnal loaches with those of diurnal animals and proposed a model to explain their photoregulation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-44631-0.Keywords: Loach, Negative phototaxis, Swimming behavior, Freshwater fish, Light-dependent behaviorSubject terms: Ecology, Ecology, Zoology

Introduction

For aquatic animals, the ambient photic environment is an external factor that influences adaptive behaviors, such as feeding, predator avoidance, and reproduction1–4. Depending on light intensity and wavelength, fish exhibit various light-responsive behaviors, such as phototaxis or light/dark preference, which reflect their ecology and adaptive strategies to cope with new challenges5–9.

Diurnal animals tend to prefer light to darkness, and this dark-avoidance response may be related to diurnal activity10,11. However, the light/dark preference of diurnal fishes is saturated and reversed under excessively bright light conditions. Specifically, zebrafish (Danio rerio)10 and short-spined sea urchins (Strongylocentrotus intermedius)12 exhibited positive phototaxis under 3.29 × 1017 photons/m2/s and 200 lx of light, respectively, but showed negative phototaxis under high light intensities. Similarly, when dim light intensity is increased to 25–200 lx, some diurnal fish species move from bright areas (~ 2500 lx) to the boundary between light and dim areas13. Furthermore, light preference also varies according to rearing conditions, life history stage, and measurement method14–16. These light-responsive characteristics may guide diurnal animals to establish themselves in an appropriate light environment11,12, thereby minimizing anxiety and stress14,17. In contrast, nocturnal animals are believed to simply prefer dark areas regardless of light intensity.

Correspondingly, crepuscular animals prefer dim light conditions and are thought to be diurnal animals whose optimal light intensity has shifted downward18. Accordingly, the photophobic behavior of nocturnal animals is hypothesized to result from a shift in optimal light intensity towards complete darkness. Additionally, light signal input to the central nervous system, which controls behavior, may be reversed in nocturnal animals compared with that in diurnal animals. This behavior may be partly clarified by characterizing the relationship between light intensity and dark preference in nocturnal animals; however, such studies in fish are scarce.

Loach (Misgurnus anguillicaudatus, Fig. 1a) is widely distributed in stopover areas, such as rice paddies and wetlands in East Asia, including Japan and China19. In addition, loaches are excellent experimental models owing to their unique relatively small size and species-specific characteristics. These include barbels for smell and taste, Weber’s organs that assist in hearing, and intestinal respiration. Considering these physiological and behavioral characteristics, chronobiological studies have been conducted to elucidate the ecology of loaches20. Adult loaches exhibit nocturnal activity rhythms under 12-h light/12-h dark conditions; however, these rhythms are weakened under constant darkness. This suggests that activity rhythms are strongly controlled by external light conditions and weakly controlled by internal circadian clocks20.

These findings raise questions regarding the importance of these traits in the physiology and ecology of loaches. The dominance of light signals suggests that under certain conditions, such as in shady areas in ponds, loaches may be active at night and during the day. This hypothesis prompted further investigation into the characteristics of light/dark responses of loaches during the day. Considering that most studies on light/dark preferences in fish have been conducted using a temporal combination of constant-intensity light and complete darkness, this study used isolated areas with different light intensities to observe the behavioral preferences of loaches. Accordingly, this study revealed a nonlinear relationship between light avoidance and light intensity, thereby suggesting a possible change in light/dark preference. Moreover, this study discusses the ecological importance and photoregulation of light-responsive behavior in nocturnal and diurnal animals.

Results

Light/dark preference under solar spectrum-oriented light conditions

Using the experimental device shown in Fig. 1, with the color balance of the light source adjusted to a natural environment (solar spectrum-oriented [SSO] light, left panel in Fig. 1d), the light/dark preferences of the loaches were examined through behavioral trials (Fig. 2a). When loach light/shade preference was quantified as the shade ratio, the average shade ratio was significantly higher than expected by chance (no light/shade preference, defined as 0.5, chi-square [χ2] goodness-of-fit test, p < 0.01; Fig. 2b, Supplementary Table S1). However, approximately 26% of the individuals (24 of 94 individuals) showed shade ratios lower than 0.5, and clear light preferences were observed, albeit few in number (Fig. 2b and c). No pronounced difference in swimming activity was observed between the shaded and light areas (Fig. 2d).

Next, the study examined whether individual variation in light preference was consistent over multiple tests. Regardless of light preference in the first experiment, most individuals in the low group (11/12) showed a dark preference in the second experiment (shade ratio > 0.5; Fig. 2e and f). In contrast, two of the 17 individuals in the high group showed low shade ratios (Fig. 2f), whereas the overall analysis of the shade ratios in the re-examined fish revealed significant dark preferences (χ2-test, p < 0.01; Supplementary Fig. S1). Furthermore, no substantial relationship was found between shade ratios in the first and second experiments (Fig. 2f, Supplementary Table S2). This suggests that the light/dark preference is not innate and interchangeability exists between no-light, light, and dark preferences. In this experiment, no considerable differences in shade ratio were observed based on individual body size, developmental stage, or experimental time, and all experiments were conducted during the light period (between ZT0 and ZT14; Supplementary Fig. S2).

Correlation between light/shade activities and shade ratios under SSO light

To investigate individual behavioral patterns, data obtained from the aforementioned first experiment (Fig. 2b and d) were further analyzed by comparing the light/shade activities and shade ratios of each fish (Fig. 3). Although shade activity showed no correlation with shade ratio (r = − 0.070; Fig. 3a), light activity showed a weak negative correlation with shade ratio (r = − 0.404; Fig. 3b). This implies that shade preference may be related to reduced swimming activity in the illuminated area. When light activities were plotted against shade activities, a moderate positive correlation was observed (r = 0.566; Fig. 3c). This suggested that swimming activities in the light and shaded areas reflected individual motivation, which was regulated independently of the light condition.

Dark preference and behavioral pattern under equimolar mixed light

Under equimolar mixed (EQM) light conditions (right panels in Figs. 1d and 4a), the loaches consistently preferred the shaded area across all tested light intensities (Fig. 4b and c). The highest shade ratio was observed at 7.5 µW/cm², and the value gradually decreased with increasing light intensity. First, behaviors under experimental conditions were measured using relatively high light intensities (400 µW/cm2 or 100 µW/cm2 or 20 µW/cm2, bright light condition). Comparison of the shade ratios revealed that loaches could discriminate and preferred the shade area, even in light areas with a brightness as low as 20 µW/cm2 (Fig. 4b, Supplementary Tables S3–S5). Thereafter, the light intensity of the light area was lowered to 7.5 µW/cm2 and 2.4 µW/cm2 (dim light condition). The loaches still avoided the light area even at a light intensity of 2.4 µW/cm2 (Kruskal–Wallis test, p = 0.019 vs. 0 µW/cm2 negative control, Fig. 4c, Supplementary Tables S6–S8). Among all the examined conditions, the shade ratio was highest at 7.5 µW/cm2, and the value gradually decreased as the light intensity increased up to 400 µW/cm2. The shade ratio at 7.5 µW/cm2 was significantly higher than that at 2.4 µW/cm2 (Kruskal–Wallis test, p = 0.041, Supplementary Table S8).

Swimming activity was significantly higher in the shaded area than in the light area across all examined light intensities (Fig. 4d and e, Wilcoxon signed-rank test, p < 0.01; Supplementary Tables S9 and S10). Differences in activity among different light intensities were more pronounced under dim light conditions.

Furthermore, under all the examined light conditions (400–2.4 µW/cm2), shade activity was notably higher than that under complete darkness (0 µW/cm2, dark/dark experiments). These results suggest that light conditions affect both the loach position and swimming activity.

When comparing light activity (left panel in Fig. 4f) or shade activity (right panel in Fig. 4f) under relatively high light intensities (400–20 µW/cm2), no marked differences in activity were observed in either area (Supplementary Table S11). In contrast, notable differences were observed at low light intensities (20–2.4 µW/cm2, Fig. 4g, Supplementary Tables S12 and S13). Shade activity was significantly lower at 2.4 µW/cm2 than at 20 µW/cm2 and 7.5 µW/cm2 (right panel in Fig. 4g, p = 0.01 and 0.046, respectively, Dunn’s test with Bonferroni correction, Supplementary Table S13), and light activity was lower at 7.5 µW/cm2 than at 20 µW/cm2 (left panel in Fig. 4g, p = 0.037, Dunn’s test with Bonferroni correction, Supplementary Table S13).

Correlation between light/shade activities and shade ratios under EQM light

In the shaded area, the shade activity and shade ratios were positively correlated (Fig. 5a, Supplementary Figs. S3 and S4, Supplementary Table S14) at all light intensities. This suggests that preference for shaded areas may be related to swimming motivation. In contrast, the light activity and shade ratio were negatively correlated (Fig. 5b, Supplementary Table S14). This implies that light/dark preference may be related to swimming activity. No strong correlation was observed between light and dark activities under bright light conditions (400–20 µW/cm2, Fig. 5c, Supplementary Table S14). However, under dim light conditions, the opposite correlations were observed. Specifically, negative and positive correlations were observed under 7.5 µW/cm2 and 2.4 µW/cm2, respectively (Fig. 5c, Supplementary Table S14).

Light/shade preference of loach depends on relative light intensity

Figure 4b and c suggest that light avoidance at a light intensity of 7.5 µW/cm2 is stronger than that at brighter light intensities. This can be explained by two possible mechanisms. First, loaches prefer relatively dark areas, with peak light avoidance occurring around 7.5 µW/cm2. Second, loaches may avoid light with an intensity of 7.5 µW/cm2. In the latter case, loaches will prefer 100 µW/cm2 to 7.5 µW/cm2. To test these possibilities, loach behavior was investigated when the two areas were illuminated with light of different intensities; 100 µW/cm2 and 7.5 µW/cm2 (Exp. A: 100 µW/cm2 vs. 7.5 µW/cm2, Fig. 6a and b). The ratio of locations in the dark side (termed “dim ratio”) was significantly greater than 0.5 (χ2-test, p = 0.001, Fig. 6c, Supplementary Table S15). This indicates that loaches avoid the bright area regardless of the absolute light intensity. In addition, the loach behavior under the conditions of Exp. B (100 µW/cm2 vs. 2.4 µW/cm2) and Exp. C (7.5 µW/cm2 vs. 2.4 µW/cm2, Fig. 6b and d) were measured. Under both conditions, the dim ratios were significantly higher than 0.5 (χ2-test, p = 0.0002 and 0.0046, respectively, Fig. 6d, Supplementary Table S15), and light avoidance at a light intensity of 100 µW/cm2 (vs. 2.4 µW/cm2, Exp B) was significantly stronger than that at 7.5 µW/cm2 (Mann-Whitney U test, p = 0.011 vs. 2.4 µW/cm2, Exp C, Fig. 6d, Supplementary Table S16). This differed from the weaker avoidance at 100 µW/cm2 than at 7.5 µW/cm2 in the experiments using complete darkness as the reference (Fig. 4b and c).

Discussion

We investigated the spatial preferences of loaches under different light conditions, thereby revealing nonlinear and wavelength-dependent responses to light and shade preferences. Loaches exhibited pronounced shade preference in all experiments, except for a minor population that showed a preference for light (Figs. 2 and 4). Light/dark preferences were not maintained over repeated measurements at four-week intervals (Fig. 2e and f), which suggests that individual differences may reflect state-dependent variation rather than fixed traits.

In the experiments using SSO light, no pronounced differences in swimming activity were observed between the light and shaded areas (Fig. 2d). This differed from the results obtained from the experiments using EQM light (Fig. 4d–g). Such a spectrum-dependent difference was also observed in the relationship between shade activity and shade ratio (Figs. 3a and 5a) and light activity vs. shade activity (Figs. 3c and 5c). The major difference between SSO and EQM lights is that the former contains stronger blue light than the latter contains; therefore, blue light may be a key factor in inhibiting swimming activity in light areas. Alternatively, the spectral composition may change the mode of swimming behavior through an unidentified complex pathway.

In experiments using different EQM light intensities, loaches avoided medium-intensity light more strongly than they avoided high-intensity light when presented in darkness. However, medium-intensity light was preferred when presented with high-intensity light (Fig. 6c and d), thereby indicating that loaches do not avoid medium-intensity light most strongly. Consistently, under these conditions, swimming activity was significantly higher in areas with medium-intensity light than in areas with high-intensity light (Fig. 6e, Table S17). Notably, loaches avoided high-intensity light more strongly than they avoided the medium-intensity light when presented with dim light (Exp. B and Exp. C in Fig. 6d). Thus, the relative intensity between brighter and darker areas is likely important for light avoidance, to which light adaptation of the visual system and/or higher cognitive processes may contribute.

Clearly, light/shade preferences in the nocturnal loach are not a simple reversed version of light preferences in positively phototactic animals that avoid light that is too strong or too weak and prefer optimal brightness. This is because a negative photoresponse to a fixed intensity of light divides animals into brighter and darker areas, leading to undesirable photoresponse behaviors. Light preference should help both diurnal and nocturnal animals to move and stay in areas of optimal light intensity. Indeed, the shade preferences of loaches will aid them in remaining in dark or dim areas under various light conditions. Such a photoresponse in nocturnal animals can be considered as a case in which the optimal intensity of light of the diurnal and crepuscular animals has shifted close to complete darkness. Intense light that exceeds the normal range of the visual system evokes negative photoresponses in both nocturnal and diurnal animals.

Considering these speculations, a conceptual model (Fig. 7) illustrating the profiles of light avoidance and preference in diurnal and nocturnal animals was proposed. Light environments were classified into three ranges: dark, non-saturable, and saturable (Fig. 7). The blue line represents a possible profile for nocturnal animals, such as loaches, under dark–dim light conditions, which are assumed to be similar to the experimental conditions, including complete darkness (Fig. 4). Under these conditions, the avoidance index peaked within the non-saturable range and gradually decreased in the saturable range. Under (medium) light conditions, the profiles of nocturnal animals may change with light adaptation (green line), which may be similar to the experiment shown in Fig. 6. Diurnal animals generally exhibit positive phototaxis up to optimal luminance (Fig. 7), whereas they exhibit negative phototaxis in response to excessive light intensity. This can induce the saturation of photoreceptive systems, such as vision or extraretinal photoreception11.

A model for light intensity-dependency of light preferences in diurnal and nocturnal animals. The light environments (horizontal axis) are divided into three ranges with different light intensities: complete darkness (dark); dim-to-medium intensity, where the light-sensitive system functioned normally (non-saturable); and high intensity, where the light-sensitive system is saturated (saturable). The vertical axis indicates the avoidance index, which is a virtual index representing avoidance of the light environment. The red line schematically shows the light responses of diurnal animals (ref11, whose avoidance index exhibits a trough under optimal light conditions. The blue line shows the light response of nocturnal animals, with the lowest response in the dark-to-dim range. The green line represents the light response of nocturnal animals adapted to dim–medium light conditions, which extends from normal vision to a bright range and shifts the avoidance index profile.

This model demonstrates that light preferences in nocturnal and diurnal animals are mirror-symmetrical up to a certain light intensity below visual saturation. The light signal at this intensity may oppositely influence light preference, and excessively intense light induces negative phototaxis through a mechanism common to both nocturnal and diurnal animals. Considering the benefits of shade preference for loaches, the following behavioral patterns of loaches in natural environments can be inferred. First, if loaches accidentally swim to bright spaces, such as sunny areas in the pond, they will swim slowly while searching for darker areas to avoid predators. When they find a darker area, they swim into it with increased swimming activity. Thereafter, the loaches change their behavior from escaping to feeding and maintain high swimming activity within the dark-shaded area (Fig. 8). The dark shaded area is surrounded by an edge area of intermediate light intensity that elicits the strongest shade preference, presumably separating the dark, safe area from the bright, dangerous area.

A model for adaptive changes in loach swimming behavior under natural conditions. Loaches move slowly in the light area to avoid detection by predators, such as birds and fish. They swim to shaded areas across transition areas with increasing swimming activity. In the shaded areas, the loaches are active feeders. A small population of loaches may spontaneously switch to the light type and swim out of the shaded area, thereby potentially contributing to habitat expansion.

This mechanism may reduce the risk of predation by diurnal predators, such as birds, and increase the survival rate of individuals. However, it may also increase the risk of food depletion by confining populations to enclosed dark areas. Therefore, loaches may spontaneously and temporally switch their light preference from shade type to light or intermediate type, with a shade ratio of approximately 0.5 (Fig. 8). Such preference switching may allow loaches to disperse from enclosed shaded areas to bright environments, subsequently switch back to the shade type, and consequently swim in other patches of shaded areas. This may increase their survival rates and expand their population.

However, this hypothesis assumes that loaches live in their natural environment during the day. Most loaches are more active at night than during the day under a 12-h light/dark cycle20. Therefore, loaches may expand their habitat range at night. However, nocturnal predators, such as catfish and dragonfly larvae, are active only at night21,22. Therefore, this study hypothesized that loaches lose their vision and can only move around a relatively small area at night. Given their highly developed chemoreceptive systems, loaches may use chemicals, rather than light, at night.

Although the experiments were conducted during the light period, the loaches were more active in the shaded areas than under constant dark conditions. This suggests that loaches may be crepuscular or diurnal, rather than nocturnal, under natural conditions. In addition, loaches exhibit diurnal behavior when fed during the day23, which implies that they may flexibly alter their behavioral rhythms depending on their environment. Environmental factors, such as rearing condition history, developmental stage, and experimental timing, influence light-responsive behaviors in fish14,24,25. Considering these issues, the current results and behavioral models obtained using laboratory-reared loaches under controlled photic conditions may not necessarily be generalized to behavioral responses under natural conditions. Of particular interest in the future is the identification of factors that influence or control individual differences in shade preferences. Candidate factors include the spectral characteristics of the ambient light, developmental stage, nutritional status, and other internal physiological conditions. Notably, the behavior of the loaches was not completely conserved under the SSO and EQM lights. Moreover, studies using wild-caught individuals are also important to evaluate the expression of these behaviors in natural environments.

In this study, a novel system was designed and constructed to measure the selective preferences of fish for various photic environments (Fig. 1). The intensity of light and its spectral properties were strictly controlled, and the light and shaded areas were alternated periodically. To the best of the authors’ knowledge, no studies have used such a system, although tanks with white/black-colored walls or controlled illumination have been used to assess the light-dependent behavior of fish8,11,17,26. This system, which uses video tracking, allows for the quantitative analysis of loach movements, which likely reflect their light preferences and motivations. The present system is readily applicable for the characterization of the photic preferences of other fish species under various conditions.

Methods

Ethics statement

All experiments were conducted in accordance with the guidelines and regulations of Waseda University. All protocols were approved by the Committee for the Management of Biological Experiments at Waseda University, and experimental animal care was conducted with permission from the Animal Experiment Committee of Waseda University (2023-A052, 2024-A064). The protocols adhered to the ARRIVE guidelines.

Animals

Loach individuals were obtained via artificial insemination in a laboratory. All individuals were sexually immature and ranged from six months to three years of age (Fig. 1a).

Artificial insemination was performed as previously described27–29. The adult broodstock was obtained from a local supplier (Okawa Shoten, Toyohashi City, Aichi Prefecture, Japan). Females were acclimated to 26.5 °C under a 14:10 h light–dark cycle (lights on at 09:00, off at 23:00) and selected for oviposition. Each female (mean body weight = 15.0 g) was intraperitoneally injected with 200 IU of human chorionic gonadotropin (Mochida Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) dissolved in 0.9% NaCl solution (200–250 µL per fish). Approximately 16–18 h after hormone injection, animals were immersed in ice-cold water (approximately 0–4 °C) for cold water anesthesia. The eggs were collected by applying gentle pressure to the abdomen. Testes were removed from sexually mature males reared under the same photoperiod and temperature conditions. Sperm suspensions were prepared by homogenizing the testes in Ringer’s solution (5.6 g NaCl, 0.2 g KCl, 0.2 g CaCl₂ per 1000 mL of deionized water) using a mortar and pestle. The eggs and sperms were placed in a Petri dish and gently mixed for 5 min. Thereafter, the same volume of desalinated water was added and fertilization continued for another 5 min. Debris were removed, and the fertilized eggs were transferred to a tank maintained at 26.5 °C to hatch. The eggs hatched within 1–2 days of fertilization. From the third day after hatching, the larvae were fed crushed brine shrimp yolk (Nichido Artemia 100). After approximately two weeks, they were fed newly hatched Artemia twice daily.

Larvae and juveniles were maintained at 26.5 °C under a 14:10 h light–dark cycle photoperiod. The fish were fasted for 48 h before behavioral testing. All behavioral experiments were conducted during the light period (ZT0 and ZT14). The fish were kept until they died of natural causes.

Experimental device

A video camera equipped with an infrared light-emitting diode (LED; HC-VX992M, Panasonic) was placed in front of a transparent acrylic tank (133 × 367 × 155 mm) to record the swimming behavior of the loach (Fig. 1b and c). An infrared filter (R72, 49 mm, Urth, Australia) was attached to the camera to prevent light conditions from being recorded in the video. The left and right areas of the tank were separated by a thick black acrylic separator (0.5 mm) with a rectangular cutout (30 × 123 mm) that contacted the bottom. The light leakage through the rectangular cutout was attenuated as it spread into the shaded area, reaching approximately 15% of the total shaded area.

Full-color Red Green Blue LEDs (OSTCXBEAC1S, OptoSupply, Hong Kong) and near-ultraviolet (UV) LEDs (UV light chip 3 W LED 1001801, ELELAB, Tokyo, Japan, Supplementary Fig. S5, near UV: λmax = 392 nm, full widths of half maximum [FWHM] = 16 nm, blue: λmax = 450 nm, FWHM = 20 nm, green: λmax = 522 nm, FWHM = 33 nm, Red: λmax = 629 nm, FWHM = 18 nm) were placed facing downward on the bottom of the tank and lit. LED illumination was controlled via a home-made instrument using PIC® microcomputers (PIC16F1705, Microchip Technology). The light intensity of each LED was controlled by regulating the forward current (non-pulse-width modulation control). Two light conditions were used: SSO and EQM light. SSO light was adjusted to approximate the spectral distribution of natural sunlight, whereas EQM light consisted of four LED wavelengths (near-UV, blue, green, and red) with equal photon fluxes. EQM light was used because the underwater light environment differs substantially from sunlight, and EQM light is suitable for future experiments using different combinations of color-balanced light sources. In the experiments shown in Figs. 2, 3, 4, 5 and 6, SSO (left panel in Fig. 1d) and EQM (right panel in Fig. 1d) lights were used.

Experimental protocol

Each behavioral trial consisted of eight consecutive sessions of 600 s each (Figs. 2a and 4a). During each session, the light and shaded areas were alternated between the left and right compartments to avoid positional bias. In the experiments using EQM light, each 600-s trial session was separated by a 10-s interval of intense SSO light (400 µW/cm2) (Fig. 4a). In the control experiment, the loaches were kept under dark conditions during each measurement period, with alternately assigned pseudo-light and shaded areas (Fig. 4a, right).

To blind the experiments, Experimenter 1 recorded the fish swimming behavior, and Experimenter 2 randomly changed the names and dates of the recorded video data files and returned them to Experimenter 1. Experimenter 1 was blinded to the experimental conditions for each file and analyzed the data using UMATracker30. After analysis, the data, including the fish centroid coordinates in each frame and the shade ratio of each compartment, were shared with Experimenter 2, and the experimental conditions were deciphered.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (version 29.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The χ² goodness-of-fit test was used to test for bias in the shading ratio (p < 0.05, two-tailed). To compare shade ratios, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used for two paired groups, and the Mann–Whitney U test was used for two unpaired groups (both p < 0.05, two-tailed). For multiple comparisons, the Kruskal–Wallis test with Dunn–Bonferroni correction (p < 0.05, two-tailed) was used.
