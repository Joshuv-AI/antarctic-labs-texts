Surface deterioration and poor handling of sports mouthguards for young football players promote bacterial attachment and colonisation requiring mechanical cleaning
Authors: Raghu Nandan Badari, Bita Zaferanloo, Vito Butardo, Huseyin Sumer
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesThis study aimed to assess bacterial contamination on sports mouthguards and their storage cases used by children under 12, and to determine how surface deterioration and poor handling practices influence bacterial attachment and biofilm formation.Materials and methodsMouthguards and cases were collected from junior football players. Culturable bacterial isolates were identified using 16S rRNA sequencing. Biofilm formation was assessed using crystal violet assays. Surface roughness was measured via 3D profilometry, and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was used to visualise bacterial colonisation. Cleaning efficacy was tested using mechanical brushing with toothpaste and simple water rinses.ResultsThirty-eight culturable bacterial isolates representing 13 genera were identified, with Pseudomonas putida being most prevalent. Most isolates were of environmental origin, indicating contamination through improper handling and storage. Used mouthguards exhibited significantly increased surface roughness (Ra = 173.88 μm) compared to new ones (Ra = 0.713 μm), correlating with enhanced bacterial adhesion. SEM revealed approximately eightfold higher bacterial colonisation on deteriorated surfaces. Mechanical cleaning with a toothbrush and toothpaste achieved 98% bacterial removal, whereas water rinsing removed only 60–70%.ConclusionsMouthguard use leads to surface wear that promotes bacterial colonisation, particularly when coupled with poor storage and cleaning practices.Clinical relevanceChildren’s sports mouthguards require regular mechanical cleaning and hygienic storage to prevent microbial accumulation and reduce oral health risks.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-025-07016-9.

Introduction

Children's participation in athletics offers numerous health benefits, including physical fitness, social interaction, and improved mental wellbeing. However, it also introduces the risk of dental injuries, particularly in contact sports. To mitigate these risks, the use of mouthguards has become standard practice. These devices, primarily made from ethylene vinyl acetate (EVA), are essential for protecting the mouth and teeth and have also been suggested to play a role in concussion prevention [22, 26].

Mouthguards were first introduced in the late 1800 s by boxers who used crude versions to protect their teeth. Since then, the design and materials used in mouthguards have evolved significantly. Modern mouthguards are made from materials like EVA, which offers a combination of strength and energy absorption, essential for effective protection during athletic activities [22]. Despite these advancements, the issue of microbial contamination remains a concern. Studies have shown that mouthguards can harbor a variety of microorganisms, including potentially pathogenic bacteria, fungi, and viruses [41]. Despite their protective benefits, mouthguards can quickly become contaminated with oral bacteria and may serve as a vector for microbial transmission to the host, potentially affecting oral and systemic health [13, 31].

The oral cavity is a complex microbial ecosystem, hosting a dynamic and diverse range of microorganisms, including bacteria, fungi, viruses, and protozoa. Saliva, with its antimicrobial components, helps regulate this microbial community, maintaining a balance that is crucial for oral health. The predominant bacteria in the oral cavity are from the Streptococcus genus, but there is a wide range of other bacterial species present as well [8]. However, when a mouthguard is introduced to the oral cavity, it can disrupt this balance by providing a new surface for microbial colonization. This can lead to the establishment of biofilms, which are communities of microorganisms encased in a self-produced extracellular matrix. Biofilms on mouthguards can serve as reservoirs for bacteria, enhancing their adhesion and growth, which poses significant health risks [13]. These bacteria can form complex biofilms on the surfaces of teeth or oral devices such as mouthguards, further complicating efforts to maintain oral hygiene.

Biofilms are particularly problematic because they are more resistant to antimicrobial agents and mechanical cleaning compared to planktonic (free-floating) bacteria. The bacteria within these biofilms can include opportunistic pathogens that can cause oral lesions, infections, and even systemic diseases if they enter the bloodstream [28]. Streptococcus mutans and Lactobacillus species, known for their role in dental caries, are among the bacteria commonly found in oral biofilms [1].

The contamination of mouthguards is influenced by several factors, including the material properties, frequency of use, and the cleaning and storage practices employed by the users. Proper maintenance and cleaning of mouthguards are crucial to minimizing health risks. Various studies have explored different cleaning methods, including mechanical cleaning, chemical disinfectants, and ultrasonic cleaning, highlighting the importance of regular and effective cleaning to prevent microbial contamination and biofilm formation [11, 32].

In this study, we examined the hygienic use of mouthguards in a children's football team, focusing on the presence of culturable bacteria on the mouthguards and their storage cases by swabbing them prior to use at a training session and compared them to the bacteria found in the oral cavity. We also assessed the impact of surface roughness on microbial attachment and biofilm formation. Additionally, we tested various cleaning techniques to determine the most effective method for minimizing microbial contamination and ensuring the safety and hygiene of mouthguards used by young athletes. By understanding the microbial dynamics associated with mouthguard use and identifying effective cleaning protocols, we aim to contribute to the overall health and well-being of children participating in sports.

Materials and methods

Study population and sample collection

Study participants were recruited from a local Australian Football League (AFL) boys' team under the age of 12 (Under-12) following institutional ethics approval and informed parental consent. Ten anonymous male participants (age range: 11–12 years) who regularly used mouthguards during football training and matches were enrolled in the study. Samples were collected immediately before a scheduled training session using sterile Transwab®Amies transport swabs (Medical Wire & Equipment, Corsham, UK; MW170). Three distinct samples were obtained from each participant: (1) mouthguard surface: by systematic swabbing of the inner and outer surfaces using a crosshatch pattern; (2) storage case: by thorough swabbing of the internal surfaces and corners; and (3) oral cavity: by standardized swabbing of the buccal mucosa, tongue, and palatal surfaces. All swabs were maintained at ambient temperature (20–25°C) during transport and processed within 24 h of collection. Culturable bacteria were used as a proxy measure of microbial contamination in the three types of samples collected. Samples were streaked onto sterile Nutrient Agar (Neogen, Lansing, MI, USA) plates using the quadrant streak technique for isolated colonies and incubated aerobically at 37 °C for 18–24 h. Control swabs exposed to air and unused transport media were included to monitor environmental contamination.

Isolation and identification of bacteria

Initial bacterial identification was attempted using Matrix-Assisted Laser Desorption/Ionization Time-of-Flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS). However, due to low similarity matches (< 70%) with environmental isolates, molecular identification methods were employed instead. Morphologically distinct colonies were randomly selected from Nutrient Agar (NA) plates and sub-cultured to obtain pure isolates. These isolates were grown in Luria–Bertani (LB) broth (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) for DNA extraction following established protocols [34]. The 16S rRNA gene was amplified using MangoTaq™ PCR Master Mix (Meridian Bioscience, Memphis, TN, USA) and the universal bacterial primers 27 F (5′-AGAGTTTGATCCTGGCTCAG-3′) and 1492R (5′-TACGGYTACCTTGTTACGACTT-3′). PCR conditions consisted of initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 35 cycles of denaturation at 94 °C for 15 s, annealing at 55 °C for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 45 s, with a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min. The success of amplification \was verified by gel electrophoresis and PCR products were purified using QIAquick PCR Purification Kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany). Purified amplicons were quantified using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and sequenced using the 27 F primer at the Australian Genome Research Facility (AGRF, Melbourne, VIC, Australia). Bacterial identification was performed by comparing sequences against the NCBI nucleotide database for environmental isolates and the Human Oral Microbiome Database (HOMD) for oral cavity isolates.

Biofilm characterization

Biofilm formation capacity of all bacterial isolates was assessed using a modified crystal violet assay [12]. The biofilm activity of bacterial isolates from mouthguards, cases, and the oral cavity was compared by standardising all isolates to the 0.5 MacFarland standard. The isolates were innoculated onto 96-well culture plates, stained using crystal violet, and analysed at 595 nm using a spectrophotometer. A positive control, Staphylococcus aureus MRSA strain, was was used [21, 27] along with negative control comprising of Nutrient Broth (Accumedia, Neogen Corporation, Lansing, MI, USA) alone were included. Following incubation, wells were washed three times with sterile distilled water to remove non-adherent cells. Biofilms were stained with 125 μL of 0.1% crystal violet solution for 10 min at room temperature. Excess stain was discarded and plates were gently washed with distilled water until runoff was clear. After air-drying, bound crystal violet was solubilized by adding 200 μL of 30% acetic acid and incubating for 15–20 min at room temperature.

Biofilm formation was quantified by transferring 125 μL of the solubilized crystal violet solution to a fresh 96-well microtiter plate and measuring absorbance at 595 nm using a POLARstar Omega spectrophotometer (BMG Labtech, Ortenberg, Germany). Experiments were performed in triplicate and results were expressed as mean optical density ± standard deviation. Values greater than the negative control indicated biofilm formation capability, with higher absorbance values suggesting stronger biofilm development.

Surface roughness analysis

A few studies have found a strong association between roughness and adherence to bacteria [4, 6, 23]. Hence, 3D profilometry was used to check for mouthguard roughness. Surface topography of mouthguard materials was analyzed using a Contour GT-K 3D optical profilometer (Bruker, Billerica, MA, USA) optical profilometer equipped with Vision64 software. Three types of samples were examined: new EVA mouthguard material (unused control), used mouthguards collected after one season, and artificially roughened EVA sheets. For each analysis, 1 cm2 sections were cut from the molar region, where maximum wear typically occurs due to repeated biting and clenching actions.

Surface roughness was quantified using the Ra value (roughness average), representing the arithmetic mean height derived across the entire measured length. For each sample, a minimum of 80% of available data points were analyzed to ensure measurement accuracy. New mouthguard material served as a baseline control, while various grades of sandpaper (P40-P320) were used to create standardized roughened surfaces for comparative studies. The P40 grit was found to produce surface characteristics (Ra = 171.306 μm) most closely matching those of used mouthguards (Ra = 173.88 μm).

Contact angle by sessile drop technique

Surface wettability was assessed using the sessile drop technique with a contact angle goniometer (FTA1000 instruments, Schaumburg, IL, USA). Static contact angles were measured using 5 μL drops of Milli-Q water at room temperature (20–25°C). Contact angles were calculated at the three-phase interface (air–water-solid) to determine surface hydrophobicity/hydrophilicity [17]. Measurements were performed in triplicate for each surface type. This comprehensive surface characterization approach aligns with previous studies linking surface roughness to bacterial adherence [4, 6, 23] and provides quantitative data for comparing material changes during mouthguard use.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

Bacterial attachment and cleaning efficacy were visualized using scanning electron microscopy [42]. EVA samples were fixed for 2 h at room temperature in 2.5% glutaraldehyde in 0.1 M phosphate buffered saline (PBS) solution. After fixation, samples were washed three times with PBS and dehydrated through an ascending ethanol series for 20 min at each concentration (60%, 70%, 80%, 90% and 100%). Dehydrated samples were air-dried for 48 h at room temperature under dust-free conditions. Specimens were mounted on aluminum stubs and sputter-coated with gold to ensure electrical conductivity. Imaging was performed using a ZIESS Merlin field emission scanning electron microscope (FE-SEM) (Carl Zeiss Microscopy GmbH, Jena, Germany) operating at standardized beam conditions. For bacterial attachment studies, both new and roughened EVA surfaces were examined before and after exposure to standardized bacterial suspensions. For cleaning efficacy evaluation, samples were imaged following each cleaning protocol to assess bacterial removal. Multiple fields were examined for each sample, and representative images were captured at various magnifications (primarily at 2000 ×) to document surface topography and bacterial distribution patterns.

Mouthguard cleaning analysis

EVA coupons (25 mm × 25 mm) were prepared in two surface conditions: smooth (new) and roughened (P40 sandpaper-treated to simulate wear). The coupons were sterilized by UV exposure for 30 min per side, then immersed in a 0.5 McFarland standard suspension of Pseudomonas putida in Nutrient Broth for 1, 24 and 48 h at 37 °C for the bacterial attachment study. While the following cleaning protocols were evaluated following 24 h of incubation of EVA coupons with bacteria: (1) cold water rinse (20°C ± 2 °C), (2) warm water rinse (40°C ± 2 °C); (3) alcohol-containing mouthwash (All Smiles Total Care, NSW, Australia); (4) hydrogen peroxide mouthwash (Colgate® Peroxyl, Colgate-Palmolive, Sydney, NSW, Australia); (5) denture cleaning tablet solution (Polident, GlaxoSmithKline Consumer Healthcare, Sydney, NSW, Australia), prepared according to manufacturer's instructions); and (6) manual brushing with toothpaste (Colgate® Total Cavity Protection, Colgate-Palmolive, Sydney, NSW, Australia).

For liquid-based cleaning methods (1–5), coupons were immersed in 15 mL of the respective solution in 50 mL sterile conical tubes and agitated at 180 rpm for 3 min in a temperature-controlled shaking incubator. For the manual brushing protocol (6), coupons were brushed with a soft-bristled toothbrush (Colgate® Advanced Ultra Soft, Colgate-Palmolive, Sydney, NSW, Australia) using standardized horizontal strokes (20 strokes, approximately 30 s) with 0.5 g toothpaste. Following treatment, coupons were rinsed with 10 mL sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and swabbed thoroughly using sterile cotton swabs. Each swab was transferred to 1 mL of sterile Nutrient Broth and vortexed for 60 s to release bacteria. The resulting suspension was serially diluted and plated onto Nutrient Agar plates within 2 h of collection. Plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h before colony counting. Bacterial reduction was calculated as a percentage relative to uncleaned control coupons. All experiments were performed in technical triplicates with three biological replicates. Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism (version 10.0; GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). For bacterial attachment studies, A two-way ANOVA was used to examine the effects of surface condition and incubation time on bacterial attachment. For cleaning studies, Two-way ANOVA was conducted to assess the effects of mouthguard condition (new vs used) and disinfection method (six cleaning treatments) on microbial removal efficacy. Where significant effects or interactions were detected, Tukey’s multiple comparisons test was used to compare group means. Differences were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05.

Results

Our research on children's sports mouthguards revealed four key findings that highlight potential oral health concerns. First, we identified a diverse array of environmental bacteria on mouthguards and storage cases, distinctly different from typical oral microbiota. Second, these bacterial isolates demonstrated significant biofilm-forming capabilities, particularly those found in storage cases. Third, surface analysis revealed that regular mouthguard use leads to substantial deterioration, creating conditions that enhance bacterial attachment. Finally, we evaluated various cleaning methods, establishing that mechanical cleaning is most effective for bacterial removal. These findings collectively demonstrate how mouthguard wear patterns and maintenance practices of young football players impact bacterial colonization, with implications for oral hygiene protocols in youth sports.

Culturable bacteria analyses

Analysis of culturable bacterial contamination from children's mouthguards revealed a diverse microbial community, with 38 distinct bacterial isolates identified across 13 genera (Table 1). The most prevalent organism was Pseudomonas putida, frequently detected on both mouthguards and their storage cases. Among the identified genera were Pseudomonas, Enterobacter, Staphylococcus, Stenotrophomonas, Microbacterium, Granulicatella, Shewanella, Ochrobactrum, Kocuria, Bergeyella, Chryseobacterium, Klebsiella, and Paracoccus. Notably, while the mouthguards and cases harbored this wide variety of bacteria, the oral cavity samples showed markedly less diversity, with only two predominant genera: Staphylococcus and Streptococcus. Most bacteria isolated from mouthguards and cases were environmental species typically associated with soil, plants, and aquatic environments, rather than common oral microbiota (Fig. 1). Biofilm formation of bacterial isolates

Next, we assessed whether the isolated microbes could form a biofilm. Some bacteria can develop biofilms on surfaces of dental appliances, making them more embedded and thus more challenging to remove. Analysis of biofilm formation capacity revealed that the majority of bacterial isolates from mouthguards and cases demonstrated significant biofilm-forming abilities (Table 2). Notably, Klebsiella oxytoca, isolated from storage cases, exhibited the highest biofilm formation with an absorbance value of 1.493 ± 0.478, substantially exceeding the positive control S. aureus (0.215 ± 0.014). Among mouthguard isolates, Enterobacter ludwigii showed the strongest biofilm formation (0.291 ± 0.099), followed by Pseudomonas putida (0.183 ± 0.023). Storage case isolates generally demonstrated more robust biofilm formation than mouthguard isolates, with several species including E. asburiae (0.437 ± 0.034), M. testaceum (0.239 ± 0.077), and P. aeruginosa (0.233 ± 0.081) showing strong biofilm formation capacity. Interestingly, oral cavity isolates showed variable biofilm formation abilities: while S. oralis exhibited substantial biofilm development (0.448 ± 0.117), S. epidermis showed minimal biofilm formation (0.052 ± 0.027), comparable to the negative control (0.050 ± 0.007). We hypothesized that the surface roughness on the mouthguards will increase following a season of use in sports activities as a result of chewing and clenching, repeated impacts, abrasion, and exposure to oral fluids. Through usage, the mouthguard will disfigure and become jagged and sharper because of this, which may lead to cuts within the cheek and act as a site for infections [12]. Several studies have found a strong association between roughness and adherence to bacteria [4, 6, 23]. Furthermore, this would lead to a greater surface area along with crevices in the EVA material which could promote bacterial attachment and make them more difficult to clean. Therefore, 3D profilometry was used to check for mouthguard roughness of EVA surfaces.

Surface characterisation of mouthguards

Surface roughness analysis demonstrated substantial differences between new and used mouthguard materials. New EVA sheets exhibited minimal surface roughness with an Ra value of 0.713 μm, while used mouthguards showed dramatically increased surface roughness with an Ra value of 173.88 μm after one game season of use. To establish a reproducible model of mouthguard wear, various grades of sandpaper were evaluated (Table 3). The P40 grit sandpaper produced surface roughness (Ra = 171.306 μm) most closely matching that of used mouthguards. Surface roughness can impact physicochemical characteristics, such as wettability. Wettability is highly dependent on the relationship between roughness and hydrophobicity. It is measured by the contact angle at the interface where air, water and a solid meet. Low-contact angle values indicate that a surface is hydrophilic, enabling water to spread and adhere to the surface. Conversely, high contact angle values indicate hydrophobicity where the surface tends to repel water [17]. In this study, surface characterization further revealed significant changes in material properties between new and used mouthguards. 3D profilometry showed distinct topographical differences, with used mouthguards displaying deep impressions and irregular surface patterns compared to the relatively smooth topology of new materials (Fig. 2A). Contact angle measurements demonstrated that surface modifications also altered material wettability (Fig. 2B). New EVA surfaces exhibited a contact angle of 63.45°, indicating moderate hydrophobicity. In contrast, both used mouthguards and artificially roughened surfaces showed significantly reduced contact angles of 35.97° and 32.18° respectively, demonstrating increased hydrophilicity. Bacterial surface attachment to EVA

We then set out to determine whether a used mouthguard would promote bacterial attachment compared to a new one. To investigate how surface deterioration influences bacterial colonization, we compared P. putida attachment to new and roughened EVA surfaces over time and performed two-way ANOVA statistical analysis. Evaluation of mouthguard cleaning methods

Given the presence of various strains of bacteria on children’s mouthguards from environmental contamination and considering that the mouthguards become rougher through a season of use leading to the promotion of bacterial attachment, we next set out to determine an effective cleaning approach. The effectiveness of common cleaning protocols varied significantly in their ability to remove P. putida from contaminated EVA surfaces. Manual brushing with toothpaste emerged as the most effective method, achieving nearly 98% bacterial removal from both new and roughened surfaces (Fig. 4A). Chemical mouth disinfectant showed moderate effectiveness, with alcohol-based and hydrogen peroxide-containing mouthwashes removing 86–88% of bacteria. Simple water-based cleaning methods proved least effective, with cold and warm water rinses removing only 60% and 70% of bacteria, respectively. Discussion

Our study reveals four key findings that highlight oral health concerns associated with children's sports mouthguards. Analysis of 38 bacterial isolates across 13 genera showed that most bacteria on mouthguards and storage cases originated from environmental sources rather than the oral cavity, with Pseudomonas putida being predominant, aligning with previous observations by Glass et al., [12]. Biofilm characterization demonstrated that many isolates, particularly from storage cases, possessed significant biofilm-forming capabilities, with Klebsiella oxytoca showing exceptional capacity that exceeded the positive control strain—a concerning finding given that biofilms enhance bacterial resistance to cleaning [27]. Surface analysis revealed that regular use led to substantial material deterioration, with surface roughness increasing from 0.713 μm in new materials to 173.88 μm in used mouthguards, creating conditions conducive to bacterial colonization, supporting previous studies linking surface roughness to bacterial adherence [6, 23]. Finally, evaluation of cleaning methods demonstrated that mechanical cleaning with toothbrush and toothpaste achieved nearly 98% bacterial removal compared to 60–70% with water rinses, suggesting that current maintenance recommendations favoring simple water rinsing may need revision to better protect young athletes' oral health.

Environmental bacterial contamination

The predominance of environmental bacteria on children's mouthguards and storage cases raises significant concerns about current storage and handling practices. Our identification of soil and plant-associated bacteria, particularly Pseudomonas putida, rather than typical oral microbiota, suggests that contamination occurs primarily through environmental exposure during sports activities and improper post-use handling. The presence of opportunistic pathogens like Stenotrophomonas maltophilia, previously associated with biofilm formation in dental unit waterlines [7], and Klebsiella oxytoca, known for hospital-acquired infections [33], is particularly concerning. Additionally, the isolation of Enterobacter species, which have been linked to various nosocomial infections, suggests that contaminated mouthguards could potentially serve as vectors for infection, especially when surface damage creates entry points through mucosal ulcerations, as previously documented [14]. These findings emphasize the need for more stringent protocols regarding mouthguard handling and storage between uses, particularly given that young athletes may be less attentive to proper hygiene practices.

Biofilm formation capabilities

The characterization of biofilm formation capabilities among our isolates revealed concerning patterns of bacterial persistence. Most notably, storage case isolates demonstrated remarkably robust biofilm formation, with Klebsiella oxytoca showing the highest activity (absorbance 1.493 ± 0.478), substantially exceeding both the positive control S. aureus strain (0.215 ± 0.014) and other isolates. This finding is particularly significant given that biofilms enhance bacterial resistance to both antimicrobial agents and mechanical cleaning [27]. Mouthguard isolates also showed considerable biofilm-forming abilities, with Enterobacter ludwigii (0.291 ± 0.099) and Pseudomonas putida (0.183 ± 0.023) demonstrating strong biofilm development. The ability of these environmental bacteria to form biofilms on EVA surfaces aligns with previous observations by Silva-Lovato et al. [32], who demonstrated that microbial attachment and biofilm formation are influenced by surface characteristics of dental appliances. This robust biofilm formation capability, particularly among storage case isolates, suggests that current cleaning practices may be insufficient to prevent bacterial colonization and highlights the need for more rigorous maintenance protocols.

Surface deterioration and bacterial attachment

Our surface analysis findings demonstrate a clear relationship between mouthguard wear and increased bacterial colonization potential. The dramatic increase in surface roughness from 0.713 μm in new materials to 173.88 μm in used mouthguards establishes that regular sports activity significantly deteriorates the EVA surface. This finding supports previous observations linking surface roughness to bacterial adherence [4, 6, 23]. The physical changes in the material were accompanied by changes in surface wettability, with contact angles decreasing from 63.45° in new surfaces to 35.97° in used mouthguards, indicating increased hydrophilicity that favors bacterial attachment.

To assess bacterial colonisation on deteriorated surfaces, Pseudomonas putida was selected as a representative strain from our culturable bacterial analyses. Although P. putida is generally regarded as a low-pathogenicity, opportunistic environmental bacterium, it possesses biofilm-forming capabilities, surface adherence traits and ease of culture make it an ideal model for evaluating early-stage colonisation and surface interaction [5, 36]. Although other more clinically significant pathogens, such as Stenotrophomonas maltophilia, Klebsiella oxytoca, and Enterobacter species, were also identified and could pose greater health risks, our study did not directly test these higher-risk organisms. Future investigations may benefit from incorporating a broader panel of clinically relevant species to further validate cleaning efficacy across different microbial threats.

This enhanced bacterial attachment was particularly evident in SEM analysis, which revealed extensive bacterial aggregates within the surface irregularities created by wear. These findings suggest that current recommendations for mouthguard replacement intervals may need to consider surface deterioration as a critical factor, not just the obvious signs of wear and tear.

Effectiveness of cleaning method

Our comparative analysis of cleaning methods revealed significant differences in bacterial removal efficacy, with mechanical cleaning emerging as the superior approach. While cold and warm water rinses, commonly recommended by manufacturers, only removed 60–70% of bacteria, mechanical brushing with fluoride toothpaste achieved nearly 98% bacterial removal from both new and roughened surfaces. Chemical disinfection methods showed intermediate effectiveness, with alcohol-based and hydrogen peroxide mouthwashes achieving 86–88% bacterial reduction. These findings align with earlier studies by D'Ercole et al. [10] and Silva-Lovato et al. [32] on dental appliance cleaning and EVA surfaces [15]. The effectiveness of mechanical cleaning likely stems from its ability to physically disrupt bacterial biofilms and access bacteria within surface irregularities, as confirmed by our SEM analysis which showed near-complete removal of bacterial cells from both smooth and roughened surfaces following brushing. The marked superiority of mechanical cleaning over simple rinsing suggests that current manufacturer recommendations for mouthguard maintenance may be inadequate, particularly given the increased surface roughness and bacterial attachment we observed in used mouthguards.

Practical and clinical implications

Based on our findings, several practical recommendations can be made to improve mouthguard hygiene among young athletes. First, although our study focused primarily on bacterial removal rates using Pseudomonas putida, a non-pathogenic environmental bacterium, the results provide preliminary insights into cleaning effectiveness. Mechanical cleaning with fluoride toothpaste and a soft-bristled brush showed substantially higher bacterial removal than water rinsing, but further investigations may be required for mouthguard cleaning management. For example, the effect of wear caused by cleaning using a toothbrushing on mouthguard surfaces, other clinical evaluation such as plaque evaluation data along with other dirt indicators on the mouthguards such as organic debris should also be considered.

Storage cases require equal attention as well, as they demonstrated significant bacterial contamination with robust biofilm-forming species. Both mouthguards and cases should be thoroughly dried before storage to minimize moisture that could promote bacterial growth. Regular inspection of mouthguard surfaces for signs of wear is crucial, as our surface analysis showed that regular use significantly increases roughness (from 0.713 μm to 173.88 μm), creating conditions favorable for bacterial attachment. Sports organizations should consider implementing hygiene protocols that include regular cleaning demonstrations and establishing guidelines for mouthguard replacement based on surface deterioration rather than just obvious damage. These recommendations are particularly important given our observation that environmental bacteria, rather than typical oral microbiota, were the predominant contaminants [12], suggesting that current storage and handling practices are inadequate for preventing contamination during sports activities.

Limitations of current study and future directions

Several limitations and future research directions emerge from our study. While we identified significant bacterial contamination and characterized biofilm formation, our analysis was limited to culturable aerobic bacteria from a single sports team over one season. A significant limitation is that culture-based methods can detect only about 1% of bacterial species, potentially missing important unculturable microorganisms that could impact oral health. Future studies should employ culture-independent molecular techniques such as next-generation sequencing to provide a comprehensive profile of the total bacterial community, including fastidious and unculturable species. Metagenomic approaches would also reveal functional genes related to antimicrobial resistance and virulence factors within the mouthguard microbiome. Additionally, future studies should consider larger cohorts across multiple sports and longer durations to better understand how different activity patterns affect mouthguard deterioration and bacterial colonization. Although we demonstrated that surface roughness increases significantly with use, further time-series investigation is needed to establish precise relationships between duration of use, frequency of cleaning, and surface deterioration rates. While sandpaper of varying grits was used on new EVA surfaces to simulate mouthguard wear and approximate surface roughness for some experimentation, this method may not fully replicate the complex mechanical and biological factors involved in actual sports-related deterioration. Future studies could incorporate in vivo wear testing or biomechanical simulation models to better mimic real-world mouthguard usage. Additional research should explore the long-term effects of different cleaning agents on EVA material properties, as our study focused primarily on bacterial removal efficacy. The development of new mouthguard materials with enhanced antimicrobial properties or modified surface characteristics could provide technological solutions to reduce bacterial colonization. Furthermore, while we identified several environmental bacterial species, including Pseudomonas putida and Klebsiella oxytoca, future studies should investigate potential seasonal variations in bacterial contamination and the possible transmission of these bacteria between team members sharing storage facilities.

Conclusion

This study shows that children’s sports mouthguards can become reservoirs for environmental bacteria, largely due to poor storage and inadequate cleaning. Regular use leads to surface degradation that facilitates biofilm formation, particularly by soil- and plant-associated microorganisms. Notably, mechanical cleaning with fluoride toothpaste removed up to 98% of bacteria, compared to just 60–70% with water rinsing. These findings support the need for improved maintenance protocols, including effective cleaning, timely replacement, and proper storage, to reduce microbial colonization and potential health risks for young athletes.
