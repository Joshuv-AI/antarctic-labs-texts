Loss of Ambulation Due to Calcific Myonecrosis: A Rare but Reversible Complication of Wilson’s Disease
Authors: Abhishek Vaingankar, Divyani Garg, Ayush Agarwal, Divya M Radhakrishnan, Awadh Kishor Pandit, Madhavi Tripathi, Achal K Srivastava
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
We report two cases of established Wilson’s disease (WD) presenting with calcific myonecrosis of the pelvic girdle in association with pelvic fractures. Despite initial improvement with chelation, subacute worsening of ambulation in both led to a consideration of neurologic deterioration on chelation therapy. However, evaluation revealed calcific myonecrosis of the hip joint and adjacent muscles as the cause. Both patients improved with intensive rehabilitation. A review of literature indicates that although osseomuscular complications are reported in WD, calcific myonecrosis is a hitherto unknown entity which, if recognized early, may be reversed, preventing disability and unnecessary change in chelation regimens.

Introduction

Wilson’s disease (WD) is a rare disorder of copper metabolism due to autosomal recessive mutation of ATP7B gene on chromosome 13q14-21. The loss of function of ATP7B gene causes toxic accumulation of copper in the hepatocyte and neurologic damage. However, WD affects many organs beyond the liver and brain. The musculoskeletal manifestations may vary from calf pain and arthropathy to refractory osteomalacia, osteoporosis, and recurrent fractures.[1] We report two cases of WD presenting with calcific myonecrosis of the pelvic girdle.

Case Report

Case 1

A 22-year-old male presented with behavioral disturbances and dysarthria for 1.5 years. Since 10 months, he had developed asymmetric rest and postural tremors involving the right upper and lower limbs, leading to restricted immobility. Family history was noncontributory. The presence of a Kayser–Fleischer (KF) ring, low serum copper (21 µg/dL) and ceruloplasmin (<9.88 mg/dL), and elevated 24 hours urine copper (145 µg/dL) confirmed the diagnosis of WD. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the brain showed T2/fluid attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) hyperintensity in the basal ganglia, thalamus, dorsal midbrain, and pons. He was initiated on d-penicillamine (250 mg once daily, incremented every 15 days to reach 1000 mg/day), pyridoxine (40 mg/day), and trihexyphenidyl (6 mg/day). The patient showed a sustained improvement post-copper chelation, but after 1 year, he became gradually confined to a wheelchair over a period of 1 month. Initial consideration included neurologic deterioration in the setting of chelation therapy. However, examination revealed severe pain around the hips with restricted movements. Noncontrast pelvic computed tomography (CT) was suggestive of calcific myonecrosis of both hip joints [Figures 1 and 2]. Serum calcium, phosphorus, magnesium, thyroid, and parathyroid levels were normal. The patient’s copper chelation was continued, and he was started on an intensive rehabilitation program and calcium and vitamin D3 supplementation (vitamin D3 level 15 ng/mL, normal >20 ng/mL). At 1 year, the patient reported improvement in pain and ambulation, albeit with restriction of hip movements. The patient has been planned for surgical management in the form of excision of calcium deposits with hip arthroplasty if improvement plateaus with conservative measures.

Case 2

A 25-year-old male developed an intermittent tremor involving the left hand, which further progressed to gait difficulties and psychiatric symptoms over a period of 8 months. Family history was negative for similar complaints or jaundice. On examination, the patient had wing-beating tremor, facial dystonia, and KF rings. MRI of the brain showed T2/FLAIR basal ganglia and midbrain hyperintensities. Based on clinical features, low serum copper (18 µg/dL) and ceruloplasmin (10.81 mg/dL), and elevated urinary copper (188 µg/dL) level, he was diagnosed to have WD. He was initiated on oral d-penicillamine, which was gradually increased over 2 months to 750 mg/day, along with pyridoxine 40 mg/day and rehabilitative exercises. After a brief period of improvement over 6 months, he developed severe lower limb pain (left > right) with restricted movement following a trivial fall and became confined to a wheelchair over the subsequent 3 weeks. Evaluation revealed extensive edema and calcification involving the muscles of the pelvic girdle bilaterally associated with pelvic fractures on CT scan [Figure 3]. Serum calcium, phosphorus, magnesium, thyroid, and parathyroid levels were normal. He was started on calcium and Vitamin D3 supplementation (vitamin D3 level was 11 ng/mL). Chelation was continued. The patient was advised conservative management with rehabilitation by the orthopedics team. He showed gradual improvement and could ambulate with the help of a stick at 3 months of follow-up.

Review of Literature

A search was conducted in PubMed using the keywords “Wilsons disease,” “Wilson”s disease,” “bone,” “osteomuscular,” “osseus,” “osseomuscular,” and “skeletal” in varying combinations. We included studies pertaining to osteomuscular complications of WD, confined to English language and human studies. The search was conducted on May 6, 2024. The search yielded 159 results. Titles and abstracts of the articles were screened, and full texts of 109 papers were retrieved for analysis. Discussion

We report two cases of WD with extensive heterotopic ossifications of the muscles around the pelvic girdle associated with pelvic fractures, leading to loss of mobility. Both patients were already on treatment for WD, which included chelation and rehabilitation. However, despite initial improvement, both patients developed lower limb and pelvic pain and grew confined to a wheelchair, leading to a consideration for possible neurologic deterioration. With prompt recognition of calcific myonecrosis and intensive rehabilitation, both showed improvement. The risk factors for calcific myonecrosis may have been a relatively restricted mobility in Case 1 and trivial trauma in Case 2. The possible mechanisms of calcification in WD are likely to be multifactorial, including severe nature of WD, leading to central nervous system (CNS) injury, nonambulatory status in both patients, muscle involvement, and microtraumas as a part of WD. Any deterioration after initial improvement in WD may indicate neurologic deterioration as a result of chelation, noncompliance to treatment, or lack of rehabilitation. However, from these two cases, it is evident that mechanical factors such as heterotopic ossification/calcific myonecrosis may contribute to deterioration, and their recognition may avoid disruption or modification of chelation therapy.

Whereas hepatic failure and neurodegenerative or psychiatric disorders are the most common manifestations in the natural course of WD and have been well studied, less is known about the osseomuscular manifestations. A review of literature revealed that osseomuscular manifestations are exceedingly rare and include synovitis, early osteoarthritis, osteoporosis, rickets, and osteomalacia.[1234567891011121314151617] Lesser known manifestations include spontaneous fractures, scoliosis, epiphyseal dysplasia, genu varum, avascular necrosis of the femoral head, and osteochondritis dissecans. Causes of bone disease in WD include renal tubular acidosis, cirrhosis, a direct action of copper, or a combination. An important consideration in the Indian population is a higher predilection for rickets, especially as a presenting manifestation of WD, in the absence of hepatic or CNS manifestation.[11] Despite an extensive literature search, we did not find any report of WD presenting with heterotopic ossifications or calcific myonecrosis.

Conclusion

Osseomuscular manifestations are rare in WD. This case series highlights calcific myonecrosis as an extremely rare complication of WD. Timely recognition may prevent disability.
