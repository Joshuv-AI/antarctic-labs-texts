Vitamin K1 in indigenous vegetables from upper northern Thailand: High-performance liquid chromatography measurement and scenario-based intake assessment relevant to warfarin dietary counseling
Authors: Chumaphorn Rodseeda, Nakit Chansamut
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTVitamin K1 intake is an important modifiable factor in dietary counseling for warfarin patients; however, region-specific food composition data in Thailand are limited. This small compositional pilot study aimed to determine Vitamin K1 concentrations in five commonly consumed indigenous vegetables from Upper Northern Thailand and to summarize estimated Vitamin K1 intake under predefined portion-size scenarios. Fresh vegetable samples were collected from local markets in Phayao Province and analyzed by the high-performance liquid chromatography with fluorescence detection. Estimated Vitamin K1 intake was modeled for three consumption scenarios (100, 300, and 1200 g/day) and compared to a benchmark intake of 150 μg/day from the literature. The concentration of Vitamin K1 ranged from 71.3 to 212.5 μg/100 g fresh weight, with Gymnema inodorum having the highest concentration. The 100 and 300 g/day scenarios were used as primary descriptive scenarios, whereas the 1200 g/day scenario was retained only as a conservative sensitivity analysis. These findings should be interpreted as descriptive intake estimates relevant to dietary counseling and not as evidence of altered anticoagulation outcomes. Data on the Vitamin K1 composition of different regions may help to provide more locally adapted dietary advice for patients taking warfarin.

INTRODUCTION

Warfarin continues to be widely used for the prevention and treatment of thromboembolic disorders, but stable anticoagulation is often difficult to achieve because of its narrow therapeutic index and significant interindividual variability. Dietary Vitamin K intake is particularly important among the modifiable determinants of warfarin response.[1] The most common dietary form of Vitamin K, phylloquinone (Vitamin K1), occurs mainly in green leafy vegetables and other plant foods.[2] Variations in Vitamin K1 intake, especially sudden increases or decreases, have been linked to instability of anticoagulation control, while a more regular intake may help to keep the international normalized ratio values stable.[3] Hence, dietary counseling is often recommended for patients receiving warfarin.[4] The value of such counseling depends, in part, on the availability of reliable food composition data. Quantitative data on Vitamin K1 content in vegetables are scarce in Thailand, especially in the north where vegetables are a common part of daily meals.[56] This issue is a barrier to the provision of locally relevant dietary advice for warfarin-treated patients. Therefore, the aim of this study was to quantify Vitamin K1 concentrations in five commonly consumed indigenous vegetables of Upper Northern Thailand using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) with fluorescence detection (FLD) and summarize scenario-based intake estimates to support portion-aware and consistency-oriented dietary counseling. The findings are expected to provide regional food composition data that may inform more context-specific dietary advice for patients on warfarin.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Determination of Vitamin K1 in vegetables

Sample collection and preparation

Five commonly consumed indigenous vegetables from Upper Northern Thailand – phak chiang da (Gymnema inodorum), phak paew/phak pai (Persicaria odorata), phak wan (Sauropus androgynus), phak phaya yo/phak mun kai (Clinacanthus nutans), and phak kan song/phak kan thong (Colubrina asiatica) – were purchased from local markets in Phayao Province. Three independent composite samples were collected for each vegetable. Edible portions were washed, air-dried, and homogenized. Approximately 100 g of each sample was extracted with 100 mL methanol, sonicated for 15 min, centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 10 min, partitioned with 100 mL hexane, vortex-mixed for 2 min, centrifuged at 5700 rpm for 10 min, and evaporated under nitrogen before analysis.

Chemicals and standards

The phylloquinone standard was obtained from Sigma-Aldrich. Methanol, hexane, dichloromethane, acetic acid, anhydrous sodium acetate, and zinc chloride were analytical grade. Ultrapure water was produced using a Milli-Q system.

High-performance liquid chromatography testing

Vitamin K1 was quantified by HPLC with FLD based on the AOAC in-house method STM No. 03-160 with minor modifications.[7] Separation was performed on a C18 column (4.6 mm × 150 mm, 5 μm) with postcolumn zinc reduction. The mobile phase was methanol: dichloromethane (900:100, v/v) containing zinc chloride, sodium acetate anhydrous, and acetic acid. The flow rate was 1.0 mL/min for 28 min, the injection volume was 20 μL, and the retention time was approximately 11 min. For each composite sample, one extract was prepared and duplicate HPLC injections were performed from the same extract; therefore, the duplicates represented analytical injection replicates rather than independent extraction replicates. Results were expressed as μg/100 g fresh weight.

Scenario-based intake assessment and descriptive benchmark comparison

Estimated Vitamin K1 intake was summarized using a scenario-based intake approach designed for dietary counseling and intake communication, rather than a toxicological risk-assessment model.

Intake assumptions

Mean Vitamin K1 concentrations (C, μg/100 g) were used to estimate intake under three portion-size scenarios: 100 g/day, 300 g/day, and 1200 g/day. The 100 g/day and 300 g/day scenarios were used as the primary descriptive scenarios for conventional and higher intake levels. The 1,200 g/day scenario was retained only as a conservative sensitivity analysis to illustrate the upper range of modeled intake and was not considered representative of usual daily intake in the general population.

Intake metrics

Estimated Vitamin K1 intake was calculated as (C × IR)/100, where C is the Vitamin K1 concentration in μg/100 g fresh weight and IR is the intake rate in g/day. As Vitamin K1 is an essential nutrient, the analysis was limited to intake-based estimates and descriptive intake-to-benchmark ratios.

Reference value and descriptive comparison

A literature-based benchmark intake of 150 μg/day was used only as a descriptive comparator for intake assessment.[8] This value was not treated as a toxicological reference dose, a universal clinical threshold, or a practical cutoff for warfarin management. Because individual warfarin response depends on baseline diet, indication, dose, genetics, adherence, comorbidities, and INR monitoring, intake estimates above this comparator should be interpreted as signals for dietary review and consistency-oriented counseling, not as direct evidence of anticoagulation instability or the need for dose adjustment.

Intake communication

Results were presented as estimated Vitamin K1 intake and intake-to-benchmark ratios. Categorical management labels were not assigned, because the benchmark was used only as a descriptive comparator. A portion-size equivalent corresponding to the 150 μg/day comparator was also calculated to provide a descriptive counseling aid.

RESULTS

Vitamin K1 concentrations in vegetables

G. inodorum had the highest Vitamin K1 concentration (212.5 ± 60.1 μg/100 g fresh weight), followed by C. asiatica (147.0 ± 22.6 μg/100 g). Lower concentrations were observed in S. androgynus (76.8 ± 14.1 μg/100 g), C. nutans (76.45 ± 2.90 μg/100 g), and P. odorata (71.3 ± 11.2 μg/100 g) [Table 1]. Variability was greatest in G. inodorum and lowest in C. nutans. Formal statistical comparisons were not performed because of the small sample size. Descriptive benchmark comparison

Estimated intakes were descriptively compared with the literature-based 150 μg/day comparator. At 100 g/day, G. inodorum had an intake-to-benchmark ratio of 1.42, C. asiatica had a ratio of 0.98, and the remaining vegetables had ratios of approximately 0.48–0.51 [Table 2]. At 300 g/day, all vegetables had ratios above 1.0. Under the 1200 g/day sensitivity scenario, ratios ranged from 5.70 for P. odorata to 17.00 for G. inodorum. These ratios describe modeled intake relative to a literature comparator only and should not be interpreted as clinical risk categories.

The estimated portion ranged from about 70.6 g/day for G. inodorum to about 210.4 g/day for P. odorata. These values are intended to support portion-aware dietary discussion and should not be used as fixed clinical cutoffs for warfarin management.

DISCUSSION

The present, small, compositional, and pilot study investigated Vitamin K1 concentrations in commonly consumed indigenous vegetables in Upper Northern Thailand and summarized modeled intake under predefined portion-size scenarios. Among the vegetables analyzed, phak chiang da (G. inodorum) contained the highest amount of Vitamin K1. When the conventional 100 g/day scenario was used, only G. inodorum exceeded the 150 μg/day literature comparator, whereas C. asiatica was close to this comparator. At 300 g/day, all vegetables exceeded the comparator, reflecting the combined influence of vegetable type and portion size. The 1200 g/day scenario produced substantially higher estimates, but this scenario was retained only as a conservative sensitivity analysis and should not be viewed as usual intake or as the main basis for interpretation. The revised analysis intentionally used estimated intake and intake-to-benchmark ratios rather than body-weight-normalized dose metrics or lifetime average daily dose. Vitamin K1 is an essential nutrient and has no established toxicological upper intake level; therefore, a conventional environmental risk-assessment framework may overstate the implication of intake estimates.

The 150 μg/day comparator was used only as a literature-based reference point for intake communication.[8] It should not be interpreted as a universal clinical threshold, a clinical management category, or a stand-alone trigger for warfarin dose adjustment. Instead, intake estimates above the comparator may help identify vegetables and portion sizes that warrant dietary review, patient education, and emphasis on consistency of intake. These findings are consistent with previous evidence that Vitamin K intake variability, rather than Vitamin K intake alone, may influence anticoagulation stability, whereas a consistent intake pattern may support more stable control.[9101112] Ethnobotanical surveys have reported that indigenous vegetables such as G. inodorum and P. odorata are widely consumed in the Northern Thai diets.[13] However, the present data should be interpreted cautiously because the study was not designed to measure actual population intake, INR changes, warfarin dose adjustment, time in therapeutic range, or clinical outcomes.

The findings therefore represent preliminary compositional and modeled-intake information rather than direct evidence of altered anticoagulation outcomes. The variation in Vitamin K1 concentrations observed across vegetables is biologically plausible and may reflect plant species, cultivar, growing conditions, maturity, storage, and season.[1415] Thai compositional studies also support that plant foods and fermented soybean products can contribute measurable Vitamin K forms.[1617] HPLC with FLD and postcolumn reduction is an established method for phylloquinone determination in foods.[18] Nevertheless, the study had important limitations. Only three composite samples per vegetable were collected from local markets in one province, duplicate measurements were analytical injection replicates rather than independent extraction replicates, and no formal statistical comparisons were performed. Therefore, the results should be framed as preliminary regional surveillance data and should not be generalized to all Northern Thai vegetables, seasons, geographic settings, or patient dietary patterns.[141519] Larger studies incorporating seasonal sampling, extraction replicates, dietary intake assessment, and longitudinal INR-related outcomes are needed to clarify the clinical relevance of these compositional findings.

CONCLUSION

This small compositional pilot study provides preliminary region-specific data on Vitamin K1 concentrations in five indigenous vegetables from Upper Northern Thailand. Scenario-based intake estimates varied by vegetable type and portion size, with G. inodorum having the highest concentration and the lowest portion equivalent for the 150 μg/day literature comparator. The findings should be interpreted as descriptive intake estimates to support consistency-oriented dietary counseling, not as clinical risk categories or evidence of altered anticoagulation outcomes. Further studies with larger sample sizes, seasonal sampling, dietary intake assessment, extraction-level replication, and longitudinal INR-related outcomes are needed.
