A case report of acute kidney injury due to the use of rifampicin in Brucella induced spondylodiscitis
Authors: Lena Bakar, Dilek Yağcı Çağlayık
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBrucellosis is the most frequently encountered zoonotic disease in regions like Turkey. Osteoarticular involvement is the most common complication, and standard treatment involves doxycycline and rifampicin. Rifampicin is generally well tolerated; however, hepatotoxicity is a known side effect, and acute kidney injury (AKI) has rarely been reported in the literature. We present a 79-year-old male with Brucella spondylodiscitis who developed progressive AKI (creatinine rising from 0.99 to 2.14 mg/dL) following 14 days of rifampicin therapy. Renal function fully recovered after rifampicin discontinuation and supportive hydration over three months. Clinicians should monitor renal function closely during rifampicin therapy and consider drug-induced AKI early, doxycycline was discontinued prior to any renal deterioration, and ciprofloxacin was continued throughout recovery, strongly implicating rifampicin as the causative agent.Keywords: Rifampicin-induced acute kidney injury, Rare side effects of rifampicin, Drug-induced nephrotoxicity, Adverse drug reaction

Introduction

Brucellosis remains particularly prevalent in developing countries, such as Turkey where transmission primarily occurs through contaminated sheep and goat milk. Other routes of transmission to humans include direct contact with infected animals or their secretions through bruises and lacerations on the skin, inhalation of infected aerosols, and conjunctival inoculation [1].

Brucellosis can affect any organ or system in the body. Therefore, it mimics several human disorders [2]. The disease is a systemic infection with a diverse clinical spectrum, ranging from asymptomatic to fatal. Osteoarticular involvement is the most common complication observed in up to half of the cases of brucellosis. Sacroiliitis, spondyloarthritis, and peripheral arthritis are common osteoarticular lesions [3]. The lumbar vertebrae are the most commonly affected spinal segment. Doxycycline and rifampin are usually preferred for Brucella spondylodiscitis. Notably, nephrotoxic effects have also been documented, a consideration of particular relevance in polypharmacy settings.

Rifampicin is one of the agents frequently used to treat infections caused by Mycobacterium, Brucella, and device-related infections, such as prosthetic valve endocarditis [4]. During its use, side effects such as hepatotoxicity, flu-like syndrome may develop [5]. As a potent inducer of cytochrome P450 enzymes, rifampicin carries important drug-interaction implications. Nephrotoxicity has been described as a rare but serious complication; since the first description by Poole et al. in 1971, sporadic cases of rifampicin-induced AKI have been published, predominantly in anti-tuberculosis therapy contexts. In this case report, we describe a 79-year-old man who developed renal toxicity during the use of rifampicin due to Brucella spondylodiscitis. While rifampicin-induced AKI is well-documented in anti-tuberculosis regimens, its occurrence during brucellosis treatment presents unique diagnostic and therapeutic challenges, particularly in elderly patients where polypharmacy and age-related comorbidities complicate the clinical picture. This report aims to emphasize the clinical vigilance required when managing multi-drug regimens for zoonotic infections and highlights the successful resolution of AKI through prompt recognition and tailored drug substitution without the need for invasive procedures.

Case presentation

A 79-year old male patient, a butcher by profession, was referred to our hospital for an infectious disease due to the detection of spondylodiscitis on lumbar magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) performed while being examined for low back pain that had been present for 20 days. The patient had benign prostatic hyperplasia and coronary artery disease. In his family history, his brother had a Brucella infection. The patient had a history of raw milk consumption and contact with small animals, and presented with constitutional symptoms including weight loss; in conjunction with this epidemiological history, serological testing for brucellosis was requested. The Brucella tube agglutination (Wright) test resulted as 1/1280. Sampling was performed for spondylodiscitis; however, no organism was isolated in culture. The patient was admitted with Brucella spondylodiscitis, and treatment with doxycycline and rifampicin was initiated. Doxycycline was subsequently discontinued due to gastrointestinal intolerance and replaced with ciprofloxacin. Before treatment initiation, liver and renal function parameters were within normal limits, with alanine aminotransferase (ALT) 48 U/L, aspartate aminotransferase (AST) 42 U/L and serum creatinine 0.99 mg/dL. After 14 days of treatment, ALT and AST levels increased to 148 U/L and 92 U/L, respectively, indicating hepatocellular injury. (Fig. 1 provides an alternate day ALT and AST levels.).

In parallel, renal function deteriorated, with serum creatinine levels rising to 1.88 mg/dL. Rifampicin was discontinued with suspicion of AKI related to rifampicin. Supportive hydration therapy was administered during follow-up. Despite the cessation of rifampicin, serum creatinine peaked at 2.14 mg/dL before gradually returning to baseline. The 24-hour urine protein excretion was 130 mg, and there was no hematuria. (Fig. 2 provides an alternate day serum creatinine levels.).

The autoimmune screening results were also negative. (Table 1 presents autoimmune markers and serum immunoglobulin levels.) These markers were evaluated primarily to rule out rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis or secondary systemic vasculitis during the initial diagnostic uncertainty when serum creatinine was actively rising. Eosinophilia was not detected in the blood or urine samples. Renal biopsy could not be performed because of the patient’s age and comorbidities. Rifampin and ciprofloxacin treatment was revised to ceftriaxone, ciprofloxacin and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole. At the end of the three-month follow-up period, the patient's serum creatinine levels returned to normal. Discussion

This case highlights a rare but clinically important complication of rifampicin therapy: acute kidney injury in the context of Brucella spondylodiscitis treatment. Since it was first mentioned by Poole et al. numerous case series and reports of rifampicin-induced nephrotoxicity have been published [6]. However, the vast majority of these reports are documented within anti-tuberculosis therapy contexts, where medication adherence, dosing schedules, and patient profiles differ significantly [7], [8]. A similar case by Miao and Li involved additional nephrotoxic exposures including gentamicin and contrast media, limiting direct comparison [9]. Some reports have specifically highlighted the increased risk of re-exposure [10]. In contrast, our case provides a distinct clinical scenario where AKI developed rapidly within 14 days of a standard, first-line brucellosis regimen in an elderly patient, without any confounding nephrotoxic co-prescriptions. To objectively assess the relationship between rifampicin and the development of AKI in our patient, we applied the Naranjo Adverse Drug Reaction (ADR) Probability Scale. The case scored a 6, which classifies the association as "Probable". This score was derived from the temporal relationship between drug initiation and AKI (Day 14), the recovery of renal function upon drug withdrawal, and the absence of other primary causes despite the patient's comorbidities. While the AKI was relatively mild and resolved promptly with hydration, the primary clinical novelty and learning point of this presentation lie in the therapeutic challenge it poses. Brucellosis requires prolonged combination therapy, and the sudden loss of a cornerstone drug like rifampicin demands immediate, careful regimen restructuring (in our case, transitioning to ceftriaxone, ciprofloxacin, and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole) to ensure complete eradication of a deep-seated bone infection while protecting recovering renal function. The mechanism of renal failure may vary from case to case and usually presents as acute interstitial nephritis with tubular necrosis. Less common presentations include rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis and light-chain proteinuria with polyclonal K and X chains, respectively. Most cases are secondary to the adhesion of anti-rifampicin antibodies to the renal tubular epithelium and subsequent complement binding. Renal function usually returns to normal after discontinuation of the drug [5]. In evaluating acute kidney injury (AKI) in an elderly patient, alternative etiologies such as volume depletion, sepsis, and alternative drug-related nephrotoxicities must be thoroughly excluded. In our patient, there were no clinical or laboratory signs of volume depletion or severe sepsis/septic shock at the time of renal deterioration. Moreover, doxycycline had been discontinued prior to the onset of AKI, and ciprofloxacin was continued throughout the recovery period without causing further renal compromise, effectively ruling them out as primary contributors. Although a renal biopsy remains the gold standard for definitive histopathological characterization, it could not be performed due to the patient's advanced age and multiple comorbidities. Consequently, the diagnosis of rifampicin-induced AKI remains presumptive but highly probable based on the strict temporal relationship and a Naranjo score of 6 suggesting acute interstitial nephritis (AIN) or acute tubular necrosis (ATN). Importantly, immune-mediated hemolysis leading to hemoglobin cast nephropathy is another critical mechanism described in the literature. In our case, the absence of an active urine sediment, significant proteinuria, combined with completely normal hemolytic markers (lactate dehydrogenase and bilirubin) points toward a transient, likely cell-mediated or immune-complex ATN/AIN rather than a robust pigment nephropathy secondary to hemolysis. Our clinical observations align with the landmark study by Muthukumar et al. [5], which underscored that rifampicin-associated renal failure frequently presents with a tubulointerstitial pattern and typically carries a favorable prognosis upon prompt drug cessation.

Several studies have supported RIF desensitization in patients with RIF-induced AKI [10]. According to other studies, patients with RIF-induced ATIN may experience more severe kidney damage if RIF is re-administered, even at lower doses. Desensitization therapy should be avoided [11].

The principal limitation of this report is the absence of renal biopsy, which precluded definitive histopathological characterization of the AKI subtype.

Conclusion

Early recognition and prompt discontinuation of the offending agent are essential for full recovery of renal function. Given that both rifampicin and doxycycline carry nephrotoxic potential, clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for a multidrug etiology when AKI develops during brucellosis treatment. Our case highlights that even when a definitive biopsy is lacking, a highly probable diagnosis can be safely managed through close monitoring and the strict exclusion of alternative causes. Regular monitoring of renal function parameters throughout rifampicin-based therapy is strongly recommended.
