From Picture Archiving and Communication System to AI production: development of a preprocessing pipeline for musculoskeletal radiological images from real-world data sources
Authors: Luke Farrow, Mingjun Zhong, Katie Wilde, Lesley Anderson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AimsComputer vision, automated interpretation of radiological images using AI algorithms, has seen considerable recent interest in the domain of musculoskeletal disease. The use of routinely collected healthcare data provides a significant potential source of information but is often complex and disorganized. We therefore set out to develop an AI-driven preprocessing pipeline for radiological hip and knee images taken from a regional NHS Picture Archiving and Communication System (PACS) system.MethodsDe-identified Scottish regional imaging data was ingested and stored in a specialist platform specifically designed for safe healthcare AI development as part of the AI to revolutionize the patient Care pathway in Hip and knEe aRthroplastY (ARCHERY) project. The preprocessing pipeline consisted of initial identification and sorting of anteroposterior (AP) hip and knee images using a semisupervised learning approach, followed by isolation of images with, and without, orthopaedic implants present. Successful execution was assessed through analysis on designated test sets using standard performance metrics.ResultsA total of 27,550 radiological images were available for inclusion. This comprised 10,111 designated pelvis and 6,496 knee radiographs, from 2,571 and 1,981 patients, respectively. Testing revealed perfect model performance for the identification of AP hip and knee images using a semisupervised ResNet model with a squeeze and excitation block (100% accuracy; recall/precision/area under receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) and kappa all 1.00). Implant identification model performance using a Vision Transformer architecture was excellent for both the hip (accuracy 99.3%, recall 0.99, precision 0.96, AUROC 0.99, kappa 0.97, F1 score 0.97) and knee (accuracy 96.3%, recall 0.86, precision 0.97, AUROC 0.93, kappa 0.89, F1 score 0.91).ConclusionWe demonstrate successful development of an AI-driven preprocessing pipeline for musculoskeletal images collated from routine NHS data sources. Use of such ‘real-world’ data is likely key to development of clinically useful healthcare AI algorithms.Cite this article: Bone Jt Open 2026;7(3):309–315.

Introduction

There has been a recent growing interest in the application of digital radiological images for clinical diagnosis and risk prediction, a domain known as computer vision (CV).1 It is one of the main areas of interest for clinical applications of AI, particularly as use of clinical images in analytic processes has historically relied on human interpretation of images, which would often significantly limit the associated inference (for example, classification into the four Kellgren-Lawrence gradings of osteoarthritis (OA)).2 Such processes were also limited by issues with inter and intraobserver reliability, which has been a documented issue across many musculoskeletal radiological domains.3

Most current clinical applications of CV have been performed with well-curated, bespoke datasets.4,5 This process is undoubtedly effective at creating high-quality annotated (labelled) images but takes considerable amounts of time, effort, and expense to create.6

One alternative solution is the use of clinical images from routinely collected data sources, such as a regional or national Picture Archiving and Communication System (PACS), which stores digital radiological images on a large scale. In these systems, medical images are stored in the Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine (DICOM) standard, a universal language that allows for a consistent approach to storage of the images and their associated clinical information (for example, patient identification, date of image acquisition, and anatomical region).7

The main issue with most PACS (and other digital health records) is the potential for irregularities associated with clinical coding input, as well as the need for further preprocessing of this data before it can be used for inference tasks.8

We therefore set out the test development of an AI driven semi-automated preprocessing pipeline of radiological hip and knee images, taken from a regional NHS PACS system, in order to obtain images suitable for clinical inference tasks (prediction of patient selection for surgery according to preoperative radiological imaging).9

Methods

Data environment

All data preprocessing and analysis was performed in the Safe Haven Artificial Intelligence Platform (SHAIP), developed by Canon Medical Systems Europe in conjunction with two Scottish Safehavens (Grampian Data Safe Haven (DaSH) and Glasgow).10 The platform was created as part of the iCAIRD programme, a £15 million Scottish national collaboration across academia, industry, and the NHS, and is specifically designed for AI development and testing.

Data are ingested into the SHAIP under the oversight of the Grampian Data Safe Haven (Grampian DaSH), a trusted research environment held within the University of Aberdeen that ensures compliance with the ‘Five Safes Framework’ that govern health data research using NHS data.11 All data are anonymized using a ‘hidden in plain sight’ approach to de-identification, which is performed prior to leaving NHS systems ensuring that no protected health information (PHI) is available to academic researchers.12

The SHAIP functions on the use of docker containers, allowing researchers to build and use their own contained research environment which includes any necessary software packages to undertake AI development. The docker used as part of this study is freely available.13 All data preprocessing and analysis was performed using Python v. 3.6 (Python Software Foundation, USA)14 or R Studio (Posit, USA).

Patient cohort

All included patients were part of the AI to Revolutionize the patient Care pathway in Hip and knEe aRthroplastY (ARCHERY) project. The study protocol is freely available which comprehensively details the study methodology.15 Further details are also available in other published literature utilizing ARCHERY study data.9

In brief, the cohort included all individuals aged ≥ 16 years who were referred to regional elective orthopaedic healthcare services within the northeast of Scotland between January 2015 and January 2022, with the words ‘hip’ or ‘knee’ and ‘arthritis’ or ‘pain’ included in their associated documentation. For this particular preprocessing project only patients who had available DICOM (radiological image) files were included. For the majority of patients, there were multiple images available across a variety of anatomical sites, dates, and image orientations.

Patient characteristics

A total of 27,550 radiological images were available for inclusion in the preprocessing pipeline. Radiographs of the abdomen, ankle, chest, clavicle, spine, elbow, femur, foot, hand, head, leg, neck, patella, sacroiliac joint, scapula, shoulder, upper limb, and wrist were all excluded. There were 10,111 pelvis and 6,496 knee radiographs, from 2,571 and 1,981 patients, respectively.

Full demographic profiles for both the hip cohort (pelvis radiographs) and the knee cohort (knee radiographs) are shown in Table I. For the hip cohort, 61.3% (1,576/2,571) were female, with an overall mean age of 66.9 years (SD 12.9). A total of 32.0% (1,747/2,571) had a recorded diagnosis of hip OA. Respectively, for the knee cohort 55.5% (1,099/1,981) patients were female, with an overall mean age of 63.7 years; 30.3% (600/1,981) had a recorded diagnosis of knee OA.

Preprocessing methods

Full details of the data flows are included in Figure 1. The initial step was to iterate across of all the folders and subfolders containing DICOM files (identified by a .dcm extension) and create a list of the image files. A specific DICOM processing programme (Pydicom) was used to extract relevant metadata from the files (patient identifier, study date, body part examined, instance number, institution name). This was then used to create detailed directories containing layers of the individual files allowing for extraction of pelvis and knee files alone. High-quality uint16.png image files were then created from the .dcm files to create a cohort of images that would be compatible with pre-existing computer vision algorithms such as ResNet.

Processing pipeline.

Following this sorting process, it was identified that despite the DICOM metadata tags, a significant number of images were stored under the incorrect tag (for example, spine radiographs contained within the pelvis images). A semi-supervised ResNet50 v2 model,16 based on a fix-match philosophy,17 and including a squeeze and excitation block,18 was therefore trained to detect anteroposterior (AP) pelvis and knee images. These algorithms were selected as well-established techniques for transfer semisupervised learning tasks. In both circumstances only 50 cases of the positive class (AP knee or pelvis image) were included as annotated data. The trained model was then used to iterate over the file directory and sort the files based on their classification as either the correct image (AP knee or pelvis), the incorrect image, or uncertain (with a confidence threshold set to 0.5). For the purposes of this task, any images that were not either an AP pelvis or knee were designed as ‘other’ and included radiographs of other anatomical regions or lateral images.

Following final manual adjustment of any incorrect image paths the images were then sorted, and AP pelvis images were split into left and right hips. A fine-tuned Vision Transformers model19 was then used to stratify images into those with and without an arthroplasty implant present. Again, the trained model was used to iterate over the file directory and classify the files into those without an implant, with an implant, or implant uncertain (the confidence threshold was again set to 0.5). The final collated images were then suitable for use of inference in computer vision tasks in relation to the native joint (hip or knee) or implants. For the purposes of this task any nonarthroplasty-related implants were considered part of the other category.

Statistical analysis

Analysis was performed for both the recognition of the AP knee/pelvis images, and also the implant identification/sorting. In both circumstances, a separate test set was used to understand performance of the models detailed above. For each anatomical site and sorting process, performance metrics were calculated. These included: accuracy, recall, precision, F1 score (harmonic mean of recall and precision), area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUROC), kappa score. A confusion matrix was also constructed detailing the predicted and true positive and negative values.

Ethics

Ethical approval for the ARCHERY study was granted by the North Node Privacy Advisory Committee (NNPAC) on 13 October 2021. This includes permissions from the following organizations: NHS Grampian Caldicott Guardian, Research Ethics Committee, NHS Grampian Research & Development, NHS Grampian Information Governance, and University of Aberdeen Research Governance. Code for the preprocessing steps detailed here are freely available.20

Results

AI outputs and performance metrics for hips

A total of 10,111 images were included in the initial sorting of images into AP pelvis and other. Of these, 200 were labelled, with 50% used for the semisupervised learning process and the other 50% for testing. Following ten epochs of model training with the sharpening temperature set to 0.4 and the confidence threshold for pseudo-labelling set to 0.9, there was perfect performance on the test set, with 100% accuracy (recall/precision/AUROC and kappa all 1.00).

Following a split of the AP pelvis radiographs into right and left sides, training of a model to discriminate between those images with and without hip implants was performed using 5,065 radiographs. Of these, 3,545 were used for model training (100 epochs), with the 760 images used for model validation and testing, respectively. On testing on the trained model, there were three incorrect predictions (all false positives). Performance metrics were as follows: accuracy: 99.3%, recall 0.99, precision 0.96, AUROC 0.99, kappa 0.97, F1 score 0.97. The associated confusion matrix is shown in Figure 2.

AI outputs and performance metrics for knees

A total of 6,496 knee radiographs were included in the initial sorting of images into AP knee and other. Of these, 200 were labelled, with 50% used for the semisupervised learning process and the other 50% for testing. Following ten epochs of model training with the sharpening temperature set to 0.4 and the confidence threshold for pseudo-labelling set to 0.9 there was again perfect performance on the test set, with 100% accuracy (recall/precision/AUROC and kappa all 1.00).

Training of a model to detect knee implants was performed using 2,147 images (following exclusion of all non-AP images for the analysis). Of these, 1,503 were used for model training (100 epochs), with the 322 images used for model validation and testing, respectively. On testing on the trained model, there were 12 incorrect predictions (all false negatives). Performance metrics were as follows: accuracy 96.3%, recall 0.86, precision 0.97, AUROC 0.93, kappa 0.89, F1 score 0.91. The associated confusion matrix is shown in Figure 3.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study to identify and develop a preprocessing pipeline for musculoskeletal images from routinely collected healthcare data within NHS systems. It highlights the complex and disorganized nature of these information sources for AI inference tasks, with many radiographs stored under the incorrect DICOM image tag or requiring further processing to identify suitable images. Use of such a pipeline, particularly through the inclusion of semisupervised learning, can significantly speed up the successful processing of such images, essential when working at the scale of data required to effectively train computer vision models.

The potential importance of routine clinical data sources in the care of musculoskeletal disease has previously been highlighted several times,21,22 with high-quality data one of the notable keys to the development of ongoing development of AI within the healthcare system and progress towards clinical practice integration.6,23 While there are bespoke musculoskeletal datasets available, these are often curated at significant cost and are limited in scope and scale compared with the breadth of information stored within electronic healthcare records.24 In addition, the modified nature of the data can lead to difficulties with later clinical practice adoption due to differences in a variety of factors influencing image acquisition and storage when compared with ‘real-world’ information. Use of images and healthcare information that provide a true reflection of clinical systems, and the ability to process this information expeditiously, can therefore be considered a key priority to help solve the current excitement-implementation gap that exists around AI systems within musculoskeletal health.

As the field of AI continues to develop, there has been successful demonstrations of AI-driven preprocessing pipelines in other medical specialties. For example, Haghighat et al (2022)25 demonstrated the ability to use AI to successfully assess the quality of digital histopathology slide images within large pathology datasets. Several authors have also demonstrated the ability to segment CT scan images to look at specific organ pathology, such as liver or lung disease, or to perform automated measurements of key clinical parameters.26-29 Such developments, for example, the creation of a deep-learning pipeline to predict stroke and COVID-19 death of those with atrial fibrillation, have even been deployed on a national scale.30 There are, however, several infrastructure-related hurdles that currently prevent image-based AI developments on a national scale, with a highlighted need for transformation of this data environment and great interoperability been healthcare systems and data sources.28-30

Limitations of the study include a lack of standardization, which could limit algorithm training, but reflects the real-world nature of the data. This includes the use of non-total knee arthroplasty implants in the ‘other’ category for implant analysis that may require greater training volume to help distinguish more complex implants or combinations of implants. As noted in Table I, there was also a lack of ethnic diversity in the cohort.

Strengths of this study include the large regional cohort of musculoskeletal radiological images uploaded from an NHS PACS system, and safe development of the processing pipeline within a specially designed AI platform (SHAIP). The code for the preprocessing steps has been made freely available to allow for further adoption on other data sources. It is, however, important to recognize that the code will likely require further refinement for new data, and that the preprocessing pipeline could be improved by further development of other additions, such as segmentation on the region of interest or automated assessment of image quality. Use with complex imaging modalities, such as CT or MRI, may require a more multifaceted approach to preprocessing than currently demonstrated.

In conclusion, use of an AI-driven preprocessing pipeline can significantly assist with the effective use of data from complex and poorly structured sources, such as NHS systems. Use of such ‘real-world’ data is likely a key part of development of future clinically useful healthcare AI algorithms. This work presents a first step in pipeline development, with a need for further refinement of preprocessing techniques to unlock the conversion of high-volume routinely collected data sources into high-quality datasets that underpin effective clinical inference.

Take home message

- This study demonstrates novel AI methodology to extract and process real-world imaging data for analytic purposes.
