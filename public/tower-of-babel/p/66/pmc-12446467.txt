Discovery and characterization of Sager Creek virus, a new orthohantavirus in prairie voles (Microtus ochrogaster)
Authors: Nathaniel Mull, Mert Erdin, Michael Letko, Stephanie N. Seifert, Tarja Sironen, Teemu Smura, Kristian M. Forbes
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
We report the discovery and characterization of a new orthohantavirus species in prairie voles (Microtus ochrogaster) named Sager Creek virus (SACRV). We performed molecular and ecological characterization of SACRV, including genome sequencing, assessment of its potential to infect human cells, and monitoring of wild vole populations. Phylogenetically, SACRV is most closely related to Prospect Hill virus, another Microtus-borne American orthohantavirus. Cell entry assays of SACRV pseudotypes were successful in both prairie vole and human cells, demonstrating zoonotic potential. Virus prevalence in wild vole populations was approximately 20% throughout the vole reproductive seasons, reflecting the reservoir host capacity of prairie voles to maintain and transmit SACRV in nature. As only the second fully sequenced arvicoline-borne orthohantavirus in the Americas, this discovery also provides insights into global orthohantavirus evolution and distribution, while our molecular and ecological assessments highlight the utility of the prairie vole-SACRV relationship as a model pathogen system.

Introduction

Zoonoses—diseases caused by pathogens transmitted from animals to humans—are a major threat to global human health and security, as evidenced by outbreaks of COVID-19, Zika virus disease, and Mpox1–3. Many zoonotic pathogens are transmitted exclusively from animals to humans or have only limited human-to-human transmission chains4. In these systems, particularly when pathogen infectiousness or dispersal outside of its host is limited, human infection risk is restricted to the distributional range and habitat of the animal reservoir or vector, and human health risk can be best mitigated by understanding and addressing spillover hazards within the context of reservoir host-pathogen ecology5.

Orthohantaviruses (family Hantaviridae, genus Orthohantavirus) are a group of global zoonotic viruses that exemplify the importance of pathogen surveillance and characterization. Rodents are the primary hosts of orthohantaviruses, though several orthohantaviruses are also found in shrews and moles6,7. Notably, each human orthohantavirus infection is acquired from rodents, with the possible exception of rare human-to-human orthohantavirus transmission limited to Andes virus (ANDV7,8). Pathogenic orthohantaviruses can cause several human diseases with case fatality ranging from <0.1% to 40%. Arvicoline rodent-borne orthohantaviruses cause the mildest disease, Nephropathia Epidemica (NE), and sigmodontine and neotomine rodent-borne orthohantaviruses cause the most severe disease, Hantavirus Cardiopulmonary Syndrome (HCPS6,9). Orthohantaviruses continue to be discovered following human disease cases, including the majority of known orthohantaviruses in the Americas, where most disease cases are HCPS10.

Despite the implications of orthohantaviruses for human health, characterization beyond genetic analysis and surveillance in wildlife is rare. Even genetic characterizations are usually incomplete, with sequencing of many orthohantavirus genomes limited to one or two of the three RNA segments (S, M, and L segments10). Isolation of the virus from wildlife samples in cell culture is usually considered the gold standard for supporting a reservoir host role, as it demonstrates infectious virus and the potential for onward transmission11,12; however, hantaviruses are notoriously difficult to isolate in cell culture13. Other molecular techniques, such as cell entry assays, can provide an important means to evaluate the potential for new viruses to infect other species and tissues in vitro, including human tissues.

Consistent with many wildlife-borne pathogens, our understanding of orthohantavirus spillover risk suffers from incomplete information regarding host roles and infection dynamics7. This fundamental information is necessary to understand whether putative host species can maintain the virus in nature, shedding and transmission pathways, and whether exposure risk for other species, such as humans, varies spatially and temporally. For most orthohantaviruses, insufficient monitoring of infections in wildlife host populations has been conducted to address these questions. Viral shedding in saliva, urine, or feces (a prerequisite for transmission) has been demonstrated for the few well-studied hantavirus-host systems14–16. However, orthohantavirus traits, particularly ecological traits influenced by host ecology and life history, are likely to vary among viruses and host species9,17, necessitating characterization of individual viruses to understand and compare the human health risks associated with each virus, particularly those that could pose risks to human health.

Here we report a new orthohantavirus species that was identified in prairie voles (Microtus ochrogaster) in the United States, named Orthohantavirus sagercreekense, or Sager Creek virus (SACRV). We present whole genome analyses as well as detailed viral characterization based on molecular and ecological traits that support prairie voles as a reservoir host for SACRV. Beyond implications for human health, as only the second fully sequenced arvicoline-borne orthohantavirus in the Americas, this discovery provides important insights into global orthohantavirus evolution and distribution. Further, prairie voles have proven themselves to be effective model organisms18,19, demonstrating the potential of the prairie vole-SACRV relationship as a model orthohantavirus system. By integrating this virus discovery with ecological and molecular characterization, we also illustrate a framework for understanding novel host-pathogen systems and their potential human health risks.

Results

We identified and characterized SACRV using several ecological and molecular analyses by monitoring and sampling wild prairie voles using a combination of terminal sampling (i.e., from euthanized animals) for organ tissue samples and longitudinal sampling (i.e., capture-mark-recapture; CMR) to collect temporal data. Terminal samples were collected from 2020—2021 as part of a previous study investigating seroprevalence of orthohantaviruses among rodent communities20 and additional samples were collected in 2022 for the present study. All longitudinal sampling was conducted for the present study in 2022, and longitudinal samples were limited to blood and saliva due to non-lethal collection. Orthohantaviruses cause persistent infections in their rodent hosts, so serology (detection of antibodies) was used to determine the infection status of released voles during longitudinal sampling21.

Our ecological analyses included monitoring temporal fluctuations in prairie vole populations and SACRV prevalence in their populations. Vole host demographics were modeled as predictors of infection, and they provide important information on the influence of host ecology on infection dynamics. Our molecular investigation included whole genome sequencing, nucleotide and protein phylogenetic analyses, virus pseudotype cell entry assays, and attempted virus isolation in cell culture. These analyses provided information on the phylogenetic relationship of SACRV to other orthohantaviruses and its ability to enter and be maintained in cells of various species, including humans and the prairie vole host. Several molecular techniques, including RNA extraction, reverse transcription PCR (RT-PCR), and gel electrophoresis, were used to analyze samples for both ecological and molecular characterization.

Prairie vole abundance

We captured 37 unique prairie voles on CMR grids, including 54 total captures once accounting for recaptured voles. Prairie vole abundance appeared to vary seasonally, increasing from April to June, then decreasing from June to August, but differences between months were not statistically significant (χ2 = 5.62, p > 0.2; Supplementary Fig. 1). No prairie voles were captured from any site in April, likely due to excessive standing water from spring rain typical of wet prairies. The majority of prairie voles were captured in two of the three CMR grids, with only two voles captured at the third site, and each vole captured only once (Supplementary Table 1).

SACRV prevalence

Prevalence of SACRV was consistently high in prairie vole populations across several metrics. Seven of 42 (16.7%) previously sampled prairie voles were seropositive for orthohantaviruses20, 3/10 (30.0%) voles captured during terminal sampling for additional molecular characterization were reverse transcriptase polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) positive for orthohantaviruses, and 9/41 (22.0%) samples from 8/36 (22.2%) unique voles captured on CMR grids tested for orthohantavirus antibodies were seropositive. No blood samples were available for one additional vole captured on the CMR grids, and serostatus did not change for any vole caught in multiple months. Prevalence was similar across months in 2022 alone (new samples from the current study; χ2 = 0.6, p = 1; Supplementary Fig. 1) and using combined longitudinal and terminal data from all three years of prairie vole sampling (χ2 = 2.2, p > 0.8; Supplementary Fig. 2). Neither sex nor body condition were correlated with seroprevalence (p = 0.7 and p = 0.9, respectively; Supplementary Table 2), though there is a trend of males and larger (i.e., positive body condition residual) individuals being more likely to be seropositive. All seropositive voles were in reproductive condition.

SACRV sequences

Lung samples from all seven of the seropositive voles collected in 2020—2021 were RT-PCR positive for orthohantavirus. We tested 6/7 RT-PCR positive vole lung samples, and we obtained SACRV sequences from 4/6 of these lung samples (Supplementary Table 1). We acquired complete coding region sequences (CDS) for all three SACRV segments (S, M, and L) from two separate lung samples; we also obtained complete CDS of two additional S segments and one additional M segment from lung samples from separate voles. In addition to the complete CDS, we obtained partial CDS for one M segment and two L segments, each from separate vole lung samples. Each of the S, M, and L segment sequences was identical to each other, so only one SACRV strain with complete CDS was used in the subsequent phylogenetic analyses.

SACRV phylogenetically clustered with Prospect Hill virus (PHV) and Isla Vista virus (ISLAV), and this clade further grouped together with other arvicoline-borne orthohantaviruses (Fig. 1 and Supplementary Fig. 3). Accordingly, SACRV had the highest pairwise sequence identity against PHV: 82.2%, 76.0%, and 78.6% nucleotide identity for S, M, and L segments, respectively, and 94.2%, 87.3%, and 93.3% amino acid identity for S, M, and L segments, respectively. There was a sum of 316 amino acid substitutions between SACRV and PHV; there were 25 amino acid substitutions in the 433 amino acids-long nucleocapsid protein encoded in the S segment, 109 substitutions in the 649 amino acids-long Gn subunit and 36 substitutions in the 488 amino acids-long Gc subunit of the glycoprotein precursor encoded in the M segment, and 146 substitutions in the 2155 amino acids-long RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (Fig. 2a). The pairwise evolutionary distance (PED) exceeded the 0.1 cutoff value applied for a new orthohantavirus species designation (Fig. 3). Primary cell line and viral entry

We isolated primary cells from kidney tissues collected from RT-PCR negative prairie voles and subsequently infected them with vesicular stomatitis virus (VSV)-based luciferase reporter pseudotypes bearing orthohantavirus glycoproteins. Along with the other orthohantavirus pseudotypes tested, SACRV pseudotypes measurably entered a standard human liver cell line (Huh-7) and two separate prairie vole kidney primary cell cultures (MiOch-K1 and MiOch-K3), but prairie vole lung cell (MiOch-Lu1) entry was minimal for all viral pseudotypes (Fig. 4). Virus isolation

We attempted to isolate SACRV in cell culture from two oral swabs and three lung samples from RT-PCR-positive prairie voles. Consistent with many studies attempting to isolate orthohantaviruses, we found no evidence of viral replication via cytopathic effect or by qRT-PCR from the blind passage attempts.

Discussion

Here we identify and characterize a novel orthohantavirus in prairie voles. Despite two pathogenic Eurasian arvicoline-borne orthohantaviruses, Puumala virus (PUUV) and to a lesser extent Tula virus (TULV), being well-studied7, arvicoline-borne orthohantaviruses in the Americas have largely been unreported. Although there have been individual segments of several other proposed arvicoline-borne orthohantaviruses in North America24,25, SACRV is only the second to be fully sequenced, and that meets all ICTV criteria for a novel orthohantavirus species22,26. We also present a combination of ecological and molecular characterizations that support prairie voles as the reservoir host of SACRV27,28, describe SACRV-prairie vole ecology, and highlight the virus’s potential to infect humans and as a model system in disease ecology.

The close relationship between SACRV and PHV is expected. Both SACRV and PHV are hosted by voles in the genus Microtus with overlapping distributions, and orthohantaviruses and their hosts are generally co-phylogenetic, especially at narrow taxonomic scales29,30. The first orthohantavirus described in the Americas was PHV isolated from M. pennsylvanicus in 198531, but reports of surveillance and characterization of PHV and other arvicoline-borne orthohantaviruses in the Americas have been limited since then (but see refs. 24,32). The low pairwise identities, high PED values, and numerous amino acid substitutions indicate that there are likely to be a large number of unidentified orthohantaviruses within the arvicoline-borne orthohantavirus lineage, and several arvicoline rodents with distributions expanding from the northern United States into Canada have been identified as probable orthohantavirus hosts via predictive modeling33. Surveillance in this region is therefore a priority for arvicoline-borne orthohantavirus identification and discerning orthohantavirus phylogeny.

We observed no differences among SACRV sequences in lung samples obtained from different voles, likely due to a localized sampling area. Only one complete sequence is available for PHV, and only a single S segment sequence (but not M or L sequences) is available for ISLAV, precluding a comparison in intraspecific diversity among other New World arvicoline-borne orthohantaviruses. However, given that the more widely sampled Old World arvicoline-borne orthohantaviruses such as PUUV and TULV show relatively high intraspecific sequence diversity34,35, further studies are warranted in order to assess the intraspecific sequence diversity and geographical distribution of SACRV and other New World arvicoline-borne orthohantaviruses.

As an arvicoline-borne orthohantavirus, if SACRV does cause human disease, it will likely be a mild disease similar to NE rather than the severe HCPS associated with other American orthohantaviruses9. Because medical orthohantavirus screening in the Americas is usually limited to cases with symptoms indicative of HCPS, there is strong potential for SACRV infections to go un- or misdiagnosed (e.g., ref. 36), and SACRV pseudotypes were able to infect human cells to a similar degree as other human-pathogenic orthohantaviruses. The susceptibility of cultured prairie vole kidney cells to SACRV provides support for transmission via urine. Although we were also able to detect SACRV RNA in lung tissue samples, SACRV displayed a poor capacity to infect lung tissue cultures. Negative results in cell culture experiments are difficult to interpret, as the lack of entry may be a result of primary cell culture conditions and may not fully reflect the true biology of this virus37. Regardless, the positive results we observed in vole kidney cells support viral transmission via aerosolized SACRV in urine, consistent with other arvicoline-borne orthohantaviruses, particularly PUUV, which causes thousands of human infections per year in Europe7,15,38. Limited positive selection associated with the amino acid substitutions between SACRV and PHV further supports abundant similarities between SACRV and other arvicoline-borne orthohantaviruses (Fig. 2b), though further investigation of the effects of various substitutions on virus ecology, epidemiology, and pathogenicity is warranted.

Stable SACRV prevalence in vole populations supports the ability of voles to maintain the virus in nature and the utility of the SACRV-prairie vole system as a model to investigate orthohantavirus dynamics in nature. Infection prevalence was consistently high across all study years using multiple metrics and remained high throughout the entire period when prairie voles were abundant from late spring to mid-late summer. Although we found no statistically significant effect of vole sex or body size on seroprevalence, likely due to small sample sizes given the strong demographic correlations, the trends in our data are consistent with other orthohantavirus studies that find increased infection prevalence in animals that are large, male, or reproductively active (e.g., refs. 39–41). Because prairie voles are often found in grasslands with diverse rodent communities20, this system could also be used to study concepts dependent upon orthohantavirus community dynamics, such as dilution and amplification effects42. In contrast, other orthohantavirus systems in the Americas, such as Sin Nombre virus (SNV) and ANDV, usually have drastic annual fluctuations in prevalence, cause severe disease in humans with case fatality up to 40%, and often occur in locations with low species diversity43,44.

We used a combination of field and laboratory techniques to identify and characterize a novel orthohantavirus in prairie voles. The complementary suite of ecological and molecular characterizations described in this study provides a broad understanding of SACRV and how it compares to other orthohantaviruses, as well as implications for public health and disease ecology. Evidence provided by our characterization indicates human disease cases that present symptoms of NE within the prairie vole distributional range should be investigated for SACRV infection, and that the SACRV-prairie vole relationship can provide a new model system to investigate orthohantaviruses in nature. Zoonoses of wildlife origin are an increasing threat to global human health and security, and surveillance and characterization of novel zoonotic pathogens is essential to global preparedness45.

Methods

All live animal handling and sampling procedures were approved by the University of Arkansas Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC), protocol number 20028, and the Arkansas Game and Fish Commission, permit numbers 102820194 and 030820211.

Longitudinal rodent sampling

Prairie voles were trapped monthly from April–August at three sites during 2022 to monitor changes in vole abundance and infection seroprevalence using CMR (Supplementary Fig. 4). Traps for CMR were set in 7 × 7 grids (n = 49 traps per grid) with 10 m spacing between traps. Every month, traps were set overnight for 2–4 consecutive nights, with frequent storms and associated flooding limiting the number of trapping days in earlier months and a combination of extreme heat and drought limiting the number of trapping days in later months (Supplementary Table 3).

Traps were set overnight, and captured animals were processed the following morning. Upon first capture, all voles were marked with an aluminum ear tag for unique identification (National Band & Tag Company, Newport, KY, USA). Vole mass, head and body length, and reproductive condition were recorded. Testes descended into the scrotum were used to indicate male reproductivity; lactating or enlarged nipples and/or a perforate vagina were used to indicate female reproductivity. During the first capture each month, a blood sample was collected from the submandibular vein of each individual prairie vole, and for a subset of voles, a mouth swab was collected and placed in PBS. Blood and mouth swab samples were immediately placed on ice in the field and then stored at −20 °C upon returning to the laboratory. All animals were released at their respective trap locations following sampling.

Several prairie voles died in their traps prior to processing in July, likely due to heat stress. These animals were salvaged and later dissected, and heart samples were collected and placed in PBS for immunofluorescence assays (IFAs; see below). Prairie vole abundance is reported as the average number of unique individuals captured from all three sites combined per trapping day each month.

Immunofluorescence assays

Blood samples from voles captured on CMR grids were tested for antibodies against orthohantaviruses using IFAs, consistent with methods described previously20. Briefly, PUUV-infected slides, which are cross-reactive for other orthohantaviruses, were used to test for orthohantavirus IgG antibodies46,47. Blood samples diluted in PBS were incubated on slides, followed by several wash cycles, and then fluorescent polyclonal rabbit anti-mouse FITC conjugate was added and incubated on the slides, followed by additional wash cycles. Slides were examined under a fluorescent cell imager for reactive antibodies.

Infection prevalence analyses

Prairie vole abundance, measured via capture success, and infection prevalence, as a percentage of voles that were either seropositive or PCR-positive, was compared across months and based on prairie vole demographics; orthohantaviruses cause chronic infections in their rodent hosts15, permitting serology to be a sensitive and effective method of determining infection status. Differences in monthly abundance and infection prevalence were assessed using Monte Carlo chi-square tests with 12,000 replicates for A) longitudinal (i.e., CMR) data only, and B) combined longitudinal and terminal data. Demographic correlates of CMR prairie vole seroprevalence were analyzed using a binomial generalized linear mixed model (GLMM) using the lme4 package in R48,49, with seroprevalence as the response variable; sex, body condition, and an interaction between sex and body condition as predictive variables; and animal ID and site as random effects. Body condition was calculated as the residual from a linear regression between total head and body length and mass50. Reproductive status was omitted from the GLMM because all seropositive voles were in reproductive condition.

Terminal rodent sampling

Samples for molecular characterizations were collected from euthanized prairie voles captured from grassland sites in Benton and Washington Counties, Arkansas, USA, during 2020–2022 and include samples utilized from a previous study20 and new additional samples. Transects of Sherman live traps (H. B. Sherman Traps, Tallahassee, FL, USA) were baited and set overnight. The following morning, prairie voles were euthanized and either dissected in the field immediately following euthanasia when very fresh samples were required or frozen and then dissected at a later time. During field dissections, lung and kidney samples were lightly diced and placed in separate microcentrifuge tubes containing fetal bovine serum (FBS) with 10% dimethylsulfoxide solution for cryo-preservation of the primary cells used in downstream cell entry assays. A second lung sample was collected from each individual vole and placed in a separate collection tube to test for infection status using RT-PCR, and a third lung sample was lightly diced and placed in viral transport medium (VTM) for virus isolation attempts. Collection tubes were immediately placed on ice; upon returning to the laboratory, samples for RT-PCR were then stored at −20 °C and samples for cell line establishment and virus isolation were stored at −80 °C.

Reverse transcription PCR and sequencing

RNA was extracted from lung samples using Invitrogen TRIzol (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), following the manufacturer’s guidelines. A portion of the orthohantavirus L segment was amplified from these samples using an RT-PCR protocol consisting of the conversion of RNA to cDNA followed by two rounds of nested traditional PCR51,52. We used gel electrophoresis in 2% agar to verify amplification of the orthohantavirus L segment in PCR products. Sanger sequencing was then performed for preliminary orthohantavirus identity.

For full genome sequencing, lung samples from RT-PCR-positive voles were homogenized with a metal bead using a bead mill homogenizer (VWR) at 3000 rpm for 3 × 15 seconds with 5 second intervals, filtered through 0.8 μm Vivaclear MINI PES filters, and treated with Benzonase and Micrococcal nucleases for two hours at 37 °C53, followed by RNA extractions with TRIzol reagent according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Host rRNA was then removed using NEBNext rRNA depletion kits (human/mouse/rat), and libraries were constructed using NEBNext Ultra II RNA library preparation kits (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA). Samples were then sequenced with a NovaSeq 6000 system (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA), and raw data were quality-filtered with fastp54, de novo assembled with Megahit55, followed by homology search using SANSparallel56 and blastp57 against UniProt/TrEMBL58 or NCBI virus database, respectively, implemented in the LazyPipe pipeline59,60.

ORF finder (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA) was used to detect open reading frames (ORFs), and Expasy translate (Swiss Institute of Bioinformatics, Lausanne, Switzerland) was used to translate amino acid sequences. One representative complete genome sequence of all known orthohantavirus species was downloaded from GenBank and used as a reference dataset in the phylogenetic analysis. The sequences were aligned using MAFFT v7.487, and maximum-likelihood phylogenies based on nucleic acid and protein coding sequences of each RNA segment were constructed with IQ-TREE2 software23,61. ModelFinder62 implemented in IQ-TREE2 was used to determine the best-fit substitution model for each alignment. Nucleotide trees were constructed using models Q.insect+I + G4, Q.insect+R5, and Q.insect+I + I + R4 for the S, M, and L segments, respectively. Sequence Demarcation Tool (SDT) Version 1.2 (University of Cape Town, Cape Town, South Africa) was then used to compare protein pairwise identities of each RNA segment to closely related orthohantaviruses. Finally, TREE-PUZZLE Version 5.2 (TREE-PUZZLE) was used to perform PED analysis with a cutoff of 0.1 for species distinction22,63.

Separate analyses were conducted using only the clade of arvicoline-borne orthohantaviruses. In these analyses, we used all orthohantaviruses that formed the arvicoline-borne cluster identified based on the earlier phylogenetic characterization. Isla Vista virus was excluded from this analysis due to a lack of complete M and L segment sequences. Maximum-likelihood phylogenies based on amino acid sequences were again built using IQ-TREE2 in conjunction with ModelFinder, with Q.insect+G4, Q.insect+R3, and Q.insect+I + G4 providing the best-fit models for the S, M, and L segments, respectively. Estimates of synonymous and non-synonymous substitution rates (dS-dN), determined using FUBAR64, were then used to infer purifying selection within arvicoline-borne orthohantaviruses. Site-specific entropy ratios of SACRV and PHV amino acid sequences were measured via DAMBE65; since there were only two sequences in the analysis, each rate observation was measured as an entropy of 1, and no change was measured as an entropy of 0.

Viral pseudotypes and cell entry assays

Primary vole cells were recovered from lung and kidney samples as described above, consistent with methods previously described66,67. Briefly, tissues were washed in 1× PBS and incubated in 0.25% trypsin solution (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 10 minutes at room temperature while the tissue was gently homogenized. Trypsinized tissues were briefly centrifuged to gather cell debris, and supernatants were used to seed T25 flasks containing Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM)/F-12 supplemented with non-essential amino acids, 12% FBS, 1 mM L-glutamine, 50 U/mL penicillin, 50 μg/mL streptomycin, 1 μg/mL amphotericin, and 1 mM sodium pyruvate. Cells were expanded to T150s and stored in liquid nitrogen at first passage.

Plasmids containing codon-optimized versions of orthohantavirus spikes were kindly provided by Dr. Jay Hooper at USAMRIID. These glycoprotein genes were subcloned into pcDNA3.1+ using standard PCR-based molecular cloning techniques68. Vesicular stomatitis virus expressing a dual eGFP and firefly luciferase reporter was pseudotyped with viral glycoproteins, consistent with methods previously described69,70. Briefly, human embryonic kidney T293 cells were seeded in a six-well format and transfected with the indicated viral glycoproteins. At 24 hours post-transfection, cells were infected with vesicular stomatitis virus glycoprotein G (VSV-G) bearing “seed particles”, washed after one hour, and incubated with low-serum media for another 24 hours. Supernatants containing pseudotyped VSV were clarified by centrifugation, aliquoted, and stored at −80 °C.

We compared viral pseudotype entry of SACRV and a suite of other viruses into host cells. Prairie vole lung, prairie vole kidney, and human liver cells (Huh-7) were seeded into black 96-well plates at a density of ~2 × 105 cells/mL. Twenty-four hours post-plating, cells were inoculated with equivalent volumes of viral pseudotypes, centrifuged at 1200 × g at 4 °C for 1 hour, and then incubated overnight at 37 °C. Luciferase was measured with BrightGlo reagent (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) at 24 hours post infection following the manufacturers’ instructions. To eliminate trace levels of non-specific luciferase signal from the assay, we compared the luciferase signal from pseudotypes bearing viral glycopriatins against negative control pseudotypes that were produced without viral glycoprotein (“bald pseudotypes”). The “relative entry” values reflect the increase in luciferase signal as a result of the addition of viral glycoprotein and allow for more accurate comparison between different cell lines69,71. See Supplementary Table 4 for GenBank accession numbers for glycoprotein sequences used in viral pseudotype cell entry assays.

Virus isolation in vitro

We attempted virus isolation by spin inoculation with field-collected prairie vole samples, including two oral swab samples and three homogenized lung tissue samples from RT-PCR-positive voles. We overlaid samples on Vero-E6 cells and prairie vole-derived primary kidney cells at 1000 × g for 1 hour. Cells were then washed with 1× PBS and supernatant replaced with fresh culture media and incubated at 37 °C for 14 days with a negative control (cell culture media alone); a Sin Nombre orthohantavirus isolate was used as a positive control.

We collected samples and visually inspected for cytopathic effect at 1 hour post-inoculation, and we then inspected and checked for evidence of cytopathic effect again at 5, 8, 11, and 13 days post-inoculation. Because orthohantaviruses rarely cause cytopathic effect and none was observed, we collected supernatant on days 3, 5, 8, 11, and 13 post-inoculation for qRT-PCR to detect evidence of viral growth. We then repeated this experiment with a blind passage from D5 and D13 supernatant on the Vero-E6 and prairie vole kidney cell lines. Virus isolation attempts were conducted under Biosafety Level 3 laboratory conditions using protocols approved by the Washington State University Institutional Biosafety Committee.
