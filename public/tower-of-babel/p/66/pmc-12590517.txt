A Self‐Assembling Peptide with Nanoparticle‐to‐Fibril Transformation Exhibits Multimodal Antimicrobial Activity through Membrane Disruption and Quorum‐Sensing Inhibition
Authors: James Mwangi, Demeke Asmamaw, Min Yang, Dawit Adisu Tadese, Brenda B Michira, Yi Wang, Sheng‐wen Zhou, Gan Wang, Ziyi Wang, Qiu‐Min Lu, Ren Lai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractQuorum sensing (QS) plays a crucial role in biofilm formation, virulence, and antibiotic resistance, making it an attractive target for combating multidrug‐resistant pathogens. Here, self‐assembling peptides are designed by incorporating amphiphilic monomers with distinct hydrophobic and hydrophilic termini to achieve optimal self‐assembly dynamics and interaction with bacterial membranes. The lead peptide, SAP2‐PEG, spontaneously forms nanoparticles that transform into fibrillar networks under membrane‐mimicking conditions. The peptide exhibits potent antimicrobial activity against critical nosocomial pathogens at low micromolar concentrations and disrupts both pre‐formed and nascent polymicrobial biofilms. Mechanistically, SAP2‐PEG disrupts bacterial membranes while simultaneously inhibiting QS by downregulating key genes, including the las and rh1 systems in Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and the agr system components in Staphylococcus aureus, as validated through transcriptome analysis. Notably, the peptide showed effectiveness against polymicrobial biofilms in both wound and catheter‐associated infections. In vivo, it significantly reduced P. aeruginosa lung and S. aureus systemic infection. These findings establish SAP2‐PEG as a promising therapeutic candidate that combines direct antimicrobial action with QS disruption for combating biofilm‐associated infections.Keywords: antimicrobial resistance, polymicrobial biofilms, quorum sensing, self‐assembly

Introduction

The rapid spread of multidrug‐resistant (MDR) pathogens represents one of the most pressing challenges in modern healthcare, threatening to render conventional antibiotic therapies ineffective against life‐threatening infections.[ 1 , 2 ] This crisis has been exacerbated by the ability of pathogenic bacteria to form biofilms, structured microbial communities encased in self‐produced extracellular polymeric substances that confer profound resistance to antimicrobial agents and host immune responses.[ 3 , 4 ] Central to the formation and maintenance of these biofilms is quorum sensing (QS), a bacterial communication system that enables microorganisms to coordinate collective behaviour based on population density by producing and detecting signalling molecules.[ 5 , 6 ] In Gram‐negative bacteria, QS functions through multiple interconnected circuits involving N‐acyl homoserine lactones and quinolone‐based signals, regulated by key genes including lasI/lasR, rhlI/rhlR, and pqsA.[ 7 ] Similarly, Gram‐positive pathogens utilize peptide‐based QS systems, particularly the accessory gene regulator (agr) system, to regulate virulence and biofilm formation.[ 8 , 9 ] The critical role of QS in bacterial pathogenesis has made it an increasingly attractive target for therapeutic intervention.

Traditional approaches to combating biofilm‐associated infections have primarily focused on developing new antibiotics or enhancing the delivery of existing agents. However, the inherent resistance of biofilms to antimicrobial penetration, combined with the metabolic heterogeneity and dormant subpopulations within these communities, limits the success of such strategies.[ 10 ] The dense extracellular matrix, composed of polysaccharides, proteins, and extracellular DNA, acts as a physical barrier, limiting the penetration and activity of antimicrobials within biofilms.[ 11 , 12 ] Recent advances in nanotechnology and peptide engineering have highlighted the potential of self‐assembling peptides (SAPs), particularly those that combine direct antimicrobial action with the ability to disrupt QS.

SAPs represent a promising class of biomaterials that can spontaneously organize into well‐defined nanostructures through non‐covalent interactions. This process is primarily driven by hydrophobic interactions, where specific peptide sequences, such as those containing alternating polar and hydrophobic amino acids, tend to form stable structures, including β‐sheet fibrils, nanotubes, and hydrogels.[ 13 ] The amphiphilic nature of these peptides creates a driving force for self‐assembly, as hydrophobic residues cluster together to minimize contact with aqueous environments, while hydrophilic residues interact favorably with water molecules.[ 14 , 15 ] The resulting nanostructures exhibit remarkable antimicrobial activity and membrane‐disrupting capabilities, making them highly effective against bacterial pathogens and resilient biofilms. The ability to incorporate cationic sequences, such as lysine and arginine‐rich motifs, enhances their electrostatic interactions with negatively charged bacterial membranes, leading to membrane permeabilization and bacterial cell death.[ 16 ] Additionally, the dynamic nature of the non‐covalent interactions enables these antimicrobial nanostructures to respond to bacterial microenvironments, such as acidic pH conditions within infected tissues or elevated protease activity, allowing for targeted antimicrobial action while minimizing cytotoxicity to host cells.[ 17 , 18 ] Beyond their direct antimicrobial activity, specific amino acids have been shown to interfere with bacterial QS systems. Aromatic amino acids such as phenylalanine and tryptophan can disrupt QS signalling by competing with natural autoinducers or by modulating the binding affinity of QS receptors.[ 19 , 20 ] The strategic incorporation of these QS‐disrupting amino acids into self‐assembling peptide frameworks offers the potential to create multifunctional therapeutics that simultaneously target bacterial membranes and disrupt communication networks.

Building upon these concepts, we designed self‐assembling peptides to combat multidrug‐resistant (MDR) pathogens through a rational, modular approach that combines membrane disruption and QS interference. Our design incorporates three key functional modules: i) hydrophobic aromatic residues that facilitate π‐π stacking interactions and provide the driving force for self‐assembly; ii) cationic residues that enable electrostatic interactions with negatively charged bacterial membranes and provide the antimicrobial activity; and iii) C‐terminal PEG conjugation, which shield the peptide from proteolytic degradation and modulates the amphiphilic balance between hydrophobic and hydrophilic domains. This amphiphilic design enables the peptides to exist as stable nanoparticles (NPs) in aqueous solution and undergo morphological transitions in a membrane‐mimicking environment, thereby creating a membrane‐disrupting nanofibrillar network. Based on the above design strategy, we designed and screened self‐assembling AMPs with optimal activity, characterized their assembly behaviour, antimicrobial efficacy, biocompatibility, and mechanism of action in vitro, QS inhibition, and validated their therapeutic potential in vivo using polymicrobial biofilm and bacterial infection models (Scheme 1 ).

Results

Peptide Design Strategy

To develop effective self‐assembling AMPs capable of dual membrane disruption and QS inhibition, we employed a de novo design approach based on amphiphilic molecular architecture. Our strategy centered on creating peptides with distinct hydrophobic and hydrophilic domains that would facilitate controlled self‐assembly while incorporating specific amino acid residues known to interfere with bacterial communication systems[ 21 ] (Figure 1A). Three peptide variants (SAP1‐PEG2, SAP2‐PEG2, and SAP3‐PEG2) were synthesized and characterized (Table S1, Supporting Information). Each peptide contained 19 residues, with strategically positioned hydrophobic and hydrophilic residues segregated to opposite ends to create an amphipathic structure optimal for both antimicrobial activity and self‐assembly behavior (Figure 1B). The hydrophobic segment was designed to promote intermolecular interactions driving self‐assembly, while the hydrophilic region contained strategically positioned cationic residues (lysine and arginine) to enhance electrostatic interactions with negatively charged bacterial membranes. Cationic residues (Lys2, Arg6, Lys10, Arg15) were strategically distributed to optimize electrostatic interactions with anionic bacterial membrane components, with the Arg/Lys ratio modulated across variants to fine‐tune membrane selectivity and hemolytic profiles. Key to our design was the incorporation of QS‐disrupting amino acids within the peptide sequence. Aromatic residues, particularly phenylalanine (F) and tryptophan (W), were incorporated to potentially compete with natural autoinducers and modulate QS receptor binding affinity.[ 20 ] The amphiphilic nature of the designed peptides was intended to drive the spontaneous formation of nanoparticles in aqueous environments, with subsequent transformation into fibrillar networks under membrane‐mimicking conditions that would enhance antimicrobial potency through the multivalent presentation of active domains (Figure 1C). Finally, C‐terminal PEGylation was performed to improve bioavailability and strengthen resistance to proteolytic degradation. PEG shields peptides from proteolytic degradation and modulates the amphiphilic balance between hydrophobic and hydrophilic domains.[ 22 , 23 ] Following preliminary screening, SAP2‐PEG was selected for further experimental validation due to its lower hemolytic and cytotoxic activity to mammalian cells.

Characterization and self‐assembly behaviour of SAP2‐PEG

Circular dichroism spectroscopy revealed that all three peptides adopted predominantly random coil conformations in phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS), as evidenced by characteristic negative ellipticity around 200 nm (Figure 1D). However, upon exposure to membrane‐mimicking conditions using 30% trifluoroethanol (TFE), the peptides underwent significant conformational changes, with SAP2‐PEG showing the most pronounced structural transition toward β‐sheet‐rich assemblies, indicated by the appearance of negative ellipticity around 218 nm and reduced intensity at 200 nm (Figure 1E). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis confirmed the predicted morphological transformations of the designed peptides (Figure 1F). In aqueous solution, SAP2‐PEG spontaneously formed spherical nanoparticles with diameters ranging from 50 to 200 nm. Upon addition of 30% TFE to mimic membrane environments, the peptide underwent dramatic structural rearrangement, transitioning from discrete nanoparticles to extended fibrillar networks. This morphological transformation was further enhanced in the presence of bacterial membrane components, with 10% lipoteichoic acid (LTA) and 10% lipopolysaccharide (LPS) promoting the formation of dense fibrillar meshworks that exhibited optimal structural organization for antimicrobial activity.

SAP2‐PEG Shows Potent Broad‐Spectrum Antimicrobial Activity with Minimal Toxicity

The antimicrobial efficacy of the designed peptides was evaluated against a panel of clinically relevant pathogens (Figure 1G). All the peptides exhibited potent, broad‐spectrum activity against both Gram‐negative and Gram‐positive bacteria and fungi, including C. albicans and C. auris. SAP2‐PEG demonstrated superior broad‐spectrum activity with minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) ranging from 1 to 4 µm against most tested organisms (Figure 1G; Table S2, Supporting Information). Notably, the peptides' performance was particularly impressive against several multidrug‐resistant clinical isolates of P. aeruginosa and S. aureus (Table 1 ), where it maintained sub‐micromolar MIC values, similar or superior antimicrobial activity to conventional antibiotics such as colistin, gentamicin, and vancomycin (Tables S3 and S4, Supporting Information). The Clinical characteristics and antimicrobial susceptibility profiles of the clinical isolates are shown in Tables S5 and S6 (Supporting Information). The consistent efficacy of SAP2‐PEG across phylogenetically diverse bacterial species, including both reference and nosocomial strains, indicates its potential as a broad‐spectrum antimicrobial agent.

Since toxicity is a major limiting factor to the clinical applicability of many AMPs,[ 24 ] we evaluated the safety and selectivity of SAP2‐PEG. Hemolytic activity assessment using mouse erythrocytes revealed excellent biocompatibility profiles, with SAP2‐PEG showing minimal hemolysis (<5%) at concentrations up to 128 µm, significantly lower than the positive control LL‐37 (Figure 1H). Cell viability assays using mouse macrophage RAW264.7 cells further confirmed the favourable therapeutic window, with SAP2‐PEG maintaining >80% cell viability at concentrations well above the MIC values (Figure 1I). These results validated our design strategy and established SAP2‐PEG as the lead compound for subsequent mechanistic and efficacy studies. Consistent with the in vitro results, in vivo toxicity evaluation revealed that the peptide did not induce any significant damage to target organs, including the liver, kidney, and spleen. Histopathological examinations of the organs for both PBS‐injected mice and peptide‐injected (10 and 20 mg kg−1) mice for one week did not show any signs of nephrotoxic kidney injury (nephrotoxicity) or drug‐induced liver injury (hepatotoxicity) (Figure S4, Supporting Information). These results were confirmed by measuring the levels of biochemical markers associated with normal metabolism or organ function, including albumin (ALB), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), total protein (TP II), triglycerides (TG), and total cholesterol (TC) in serum. Following intraperitoneal injection with the peptide, levels of the tested biochemical markers remained within the normal ranges (Figure S5, Supporting Information). Lack of substantial alteration in biochemical parameter levels indicates that SAP2‐PEG has a favourable safety profile and does not appear to cause significant liver and kidney damage or metabolic function disruptions.

SAP2‐PEG Nanoparticles Transform into Bacteria‐Trapping Nanofibrils

To investigate the proposed bacterial entrapment mechanism of SAP2‐PEG following fibril formation in the presence of cell wall components, we performed a dose‐dependent bacterial aggregation assay using P. aeruginosa ATCC10145 and S. aureus ATCC25295 cultures treated with varying concentrations (0–80 µm) of SAP2‐PEG for 4 h. Visual assessment of bacterial suspension turbidity after staining with Congo Red dye revealed clear, concentration‐dependent effects of SAP2‐PEG treatment on both bacterial species. As shown in Figure 2A, untreated control cultures (0 µm) maintained uniform red turbidity throughout the tube, indicating dispersed bacterial growth. However, SAP2‐PEG treatment induced progressive bacterial aggregation and sedimentation, with visible biomass settling becoming apparent at concentrations ≥20 µm. At the highest concentration tested (80 µm), both P. aeruginosa and S. aureus suspensions showed dramatic clearing of the supernatant with substantial bacterial aggregate formation at the tube bottom, indicating extensive bacterial entrapment within peptide‐induced networks.

To visualize the bacterial entrapment phenomenon at the cellular level and confirm direct peptide‐bacteria interactions, super‐resolution structured illumination microscopy (SIM) was employed using dual fluorescent labeling. Bacterial membranes were stained with DiD (DilC8, green fluorescence) while biotinylated SAP2‐PEG was detected using Alexa Fluor 568 (red fluorescence). Control bacterial cultures displayed typical dispersed morphology with individual cells clearly separated and distributed throughout the field of view, showing only green membrane fluorescence with no peptide association (Figure 2B). Following SAP2‐PEG treatment at 20 µm concentration, profound morphological changes occurred in both bacterial species. Treated P. aeruginosa and S. aureus formed dense, aggregated clusters characterized by extensive colocalization of green bacterial membranes with red peptide fluorescence, providing direct visual evidence of peptide‐bacteria binding and entrapment.

SAP2‐PEG Shows Optimal Stability in Physiological Conditions

Proteolytic degradation assays were performed to assess the stability of SAP2‐PEG in the presence of proteolytic enzymes. SDS‐PAGE analysis showed that SAP2‐PEG demonstrated significant resistance to proteolysis, with detectable peptide bands even after 6 h of incubation with trypsin or chymotrypsin (Figure S6A, Supporting Information). To quantify the degradation kinetics, high‐performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) was used to monitor the degradation of SAP2‐PEG in the presence of trypsin and chymotrypsin (Figure S6B, Supporting Information). In the case of trypsin, SAP2‐PEG showed relatively rapid degradation, with ≈14%–17% of the peptide degraded after 30 min of incubation (retaining 83%–87% intact peptide). Degradation continued steadily over time, with ≈50% of the peptide remaining intact after 120 min, and reaching ≈20%–24% intact peptide after 6 h across all tested conditions. SAP2‐PEG exhibited notably higher stability when exposed to chymotrypsin, with only 11%–15% degradation after 30 min and retaining 32%–35% intact peptide after 6 h, suggesting that it may be more resistant to degradation by specific enzymes. Moreover, the MIC of the peptide was not significantly affected following incubation with the indicated enzymes (Figure S6C, Supporting Information).

Additionally, we examined the stability of SAP2‐PEG under physiological conditions, including the presence of physiological salts and serum. In the presence of 150 mM physiological salts (MgCl2, FeCl3, CaCl2, NaCl, and KCl), SAP2‐PEG maintained its antimicrobial activity. Notably, monovalent cations such as K+ and Na+ did not significantly impact the peptide's antimicrobial effectiveness. However, divalent cations like Fe3+, Ca2+, and Mg2+ slightly elevated the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of SAP2‐PEG (1.52–2.5‐fold) due to competitive binding with the negatively charged bacterial membranes (Figure S6D, Supporting Information). When tested in serum, SAP2‐PEG remained stable in the presence of 12.5% and 25% serum, with minimal effects on MIC against P. aeruginosa and S. aureus (Figure S6E, Supporting Information). However, at 50% serum, there was a significant increase in MIC (8.5‐fold and 10‐fold for P. aeruginosa and S. aureus, respectively), suggesting that higher serum concentrations may promote peptide degradation.SAP2‐PEG rapidly kills bacteria by primarily targeting microbial membranes

To elucidate the mechanism by which SAP2‐PEG exerts its antimicrobial activity, we investigated its effects on bacterial membrane integrity and dynamics through multiple complementary approaches. Time‐kill assays revealed that SAP2‐PEG exhibits concentration‐dependent bactericidal activity against both P. aeruginosa and S. aureus (Figure 3A). At concentrations of 1× and 2× MIC, SAP2‐PEG achieved significant bacterial reduction within 30 min of exposure, with CFU counts decreasing by ≈2–3 log units. Complete bacterial killing was observed within 60 min at 2× MIC concentrations for both pathogens. Notably, at 1× MIC, SAP2‐PEG exhibited superior performance in clearing bacteria compared to gentamicin. The rapid onset of antimicrobial activity suggests a direct interaction with essential cellular components rather than inhibition of metabolic processes.

To better understand the mechanism underlying SAP2‐PEG's antimicrobial action, we investigated its ability to disrupt microbial membranes. To assess membrane integrity, we measured inner membrane potential using the fluorescent dye DiSC3[ 5 ] (Figure 3B). SAP2‐PEG treatment resulted in dose‐dependent membrane depolarization in both bacterial species, as evidenced by increased fluorescence intensity over time. At concentrations of 1–4 µm, SAP2‐PEG caused progressive membrane depolarization, with higher concentrations producing more rapid and complete depolarization. The depolarization kinetics closely paralleled those observed with the positive control (2% Triton X‐100), indicating that SAP2‐PEG compromises membrane electrical gradients essential for cellular viability. Furthermore, analysis of membrane fluidity using Laurdan fluorescence revealed that SAP2‐PEG treatment significantly alters bacterial membrane dynamics (Figure 3C). The laurdan generalized polarisation (GP) values increased in a concentration‐dependent manner for both P. aeruginosa and S. aureus, indicating increased membrane fluidity, suggesting substantial disruption of lipid packing within the bacterial membrane.[ 25 ]

Live/dead fluorescence microscopy using SYTO 9/propidium iodide staining provided visual confirmation of SAP2‐PEG's membrane‐disrupting activity (Figure 3D). Control bacteria displayed predominantly green fluorescence, indicating intact membranes, while SAP2‐PEG treatment (2–4 µm) resulted in a dose‐dependent shift to red fluorescence, demonstrating membrane permeabilization and cell death in both S. aureus and P. aeruginosa. Consistently, flow cytometric analysis using SYTO green staining further quantified the extent of membrane damage across bacterial populations (Figure 3E). SAP2‐PEG treatment caused concentration‐dependent shifts in fluorescence intensity patterns, with higher concentrations producing more pronounced population shifts toward compromised membrane states. For S. aureus, the percentage of cells with compromised membranes (Q2+Q3 quadrants) increased from 8.83% in untreated controls to 82.4% and 91.0% at intermediate and high SAP2‐PEG concentrations, respectively. Similarly, P. aeruginosa populations showed a progressive shift from 11.9% membrane‐compromised cells in controls to 68.3% and 83.1% following treatment. The flow cytometry data revealed heterogeneous responses within bacterial populations, suggesting that membrane disruption occurs progressively across individual cells.

SEM analysis revealed that SAP2‐PEG treatment induces significant morphological changes in bacterial cell surfaces (Figure 3F). Control P. aeruginosa and S. aureus cells maintained their characteristic rod‐shaped and spherical morphologies, respectively. Following SAP2‐PEG treatment (2× MIC), both species exhibited surface roughening, membrane blebbing, and formation of pore‐like structures (indicated by yellow arrows), consistent with membrane disruption and cellular damage. TEM analysis provided further detailed visualization of SAP2‐PEG's effects on internal cellular architecture (Figure 3G; Figure S7, Supporting Information). Control bacteria displayed well‐defined cell walls and uniform cytoplasmic density. SAP2‐PEG treatment resulted in cytoplasmic condensation, formation of electron‐dense regions, and apparent membrane discontinuities (blue arrows), indicating severe cellular damage and loss of membrane integrity.

SAP2‐PEG Exhibits Multiple Complementary Mechanisms of Action

Beyond its direct antimicrobial action, SAP2‐PEG exhibits multiple complementary mechanisms that synergistically contribute to bacterial cell death through the coordinated disruption of critical cellular processes. Measurement of intracellular ROS levels using DCFH‐DA fluorescence revealed that SAP2‐PEG treatment significantly increases oxidative stress in both bacterial species (Figure 3H). ROS production increased in a concentration‐dependent manner, with levels reaching 10–12‐fold above control values at higher SAP2‐PEG concentrations. This dramatic elevation in ROS potentially contributes to microbial death through multiple pathways, such as disruption of essential enzymatic functions and sustained oxidative stress that overwhelms bacterial antioxidant defense systems, ultimately triggering cellular dysfunction and death.[ 26 ]

Gel electrophoresis analysis revealed that SAP2‐PEG exhibits strong DNA‐binding properties (Figure 3I). Increasing concentrations of SAP2‐PEG (0–32 µm) progressively retarded DNA migration for both P. aeruginosa and S. aureus genomic DNA, indicating formation of stable SAP2‐PEG‐DNA complexes. Complete DNA binding was observed at concentrations ≥16 µm, demonstrating the peptide's ability to interact with and sequester bacterial genetic material. This DNA‐binding activity likely contributes to bacterial death by interfering with DNA replication and transcription, thus effectively impeding essential cellular processes such as protein synthesis and cell division, as evidenced by other DNA‐binding peptides.[ 27 , 28 ]

BODIPY fluorescence displacement assays demonstrated that SAP2‐PEG exhibits powerful binding affinity for lipoteichoic acid (LTA), a major structural component of Gram‐positive bacterial cell walls (Figure 3J). SAP2‐PEG showed superior LTA binding compared to LL‐37 and vancomycin across the tested concentration range (0–64 µm), with binding curves indicating high‐affinity interactions. Similarly, SAP2‐PEG displayed significant binding affinity for lipopolysaccharide (LPS), the major outer membrane component of Gram‐negative bacteria (Figure 3K). The LPS‐binding profile of SAP2‐PEG was superior to both LL‐37 and polymyxin B at lower concentrations, indicating powerful interactions with Gram‐negative cell envelope components. The rapid killing effects of SAP2‐PEG, combined with its membrane‐targeting properties and complementary mechanisms of action, may reduce the likelihood of resistance development, a major issue for many conventional antibiotics that target a specific bacterial pathway. Indeed, as observed in our results (Figure S8, Supporting Information), SAP‐PEG showed a reduced propensity for the development of resistance compared to gentamicin against P. aeruginosa and S. aureus.

SAP2‐PEG Exhibits Vigorous Anti‐Polymicrobial Biofilm Activity

Biofilm disruption is a critical therapeutic target since biofilms contribute significantly to antimicrobial resistance development and chronic infections.[ 29 ] SAP2‐PEG displayed potent dose‐dependent biofilm inhibitory activity against both mono‐ and polymicrobial biofilm communities (Figure 4A; Figure S9, Supporting Information). SAP2‐PEG achieved >80% biofilm inhibition at 16 µm and nearly complete inhibition (>95%) at 32 µm, showing superior performance compared to gentamicin across all tested concentrations. This inhibitory effect was consistently observed from 2 µm onward, with statistical significance achieved at concentrations of 4 µm or higher. Microscopic visualization of biofilm formation on cover glass surfaces confirmed the quantitative inhibition data (Figure 4B). Control biofilms showed dense, multilayered bacterial communities with extensive extracellular matrix formation. In contrast, treatment with increasing concentrations of SAP2‐PEG resulted in a progressive disruption of the biofilm architecture, characterized by visibly reduced biofilm density and disaggregated bacterial clusters.

Beyond inhibition, SAP2‐PEG exhibited significant capability to eradicate pre‐established biofilms (Figure 4C). Against mature 24‐h biofilms, SAP2‐PEG achieved >60% biofilm eradication at 8 µm and >80% eradication at 16–32 µm, substantially outperforming gentamicin, which showed minimal eradication activity even at high concentrations. This eradication capability is particularly clinically relevant, as it suggests potential therapeutic utility against established chronic infections. SEM analysis of biofilm eradication from polycarbonate membranes provided a detailed visualization of SAP2‐PEG's antibiofilm mechanism (Figure 4D). Control biofilms exhibited dense, 3D architecture with extensive bacterial aggregation and extracellular matrix formation. Treatment with 2 µm SAP2‐PEG resulted in visible biofilm thinning and structural disruption (indicated by red arrows). At 4 µm, substantial biofilm disaggregation was observed, with exposed membrane surfaces becoming visible. Complete biofilm eradication was achieved at 8 µm, with only scattered individual bacterial cells remaining on the membrane surface, demonstrating the peptide's ability to penetrate and disrupt established biofilm matrices.

To quantitatively assess the impact of SAP2‐PEG on specific biofilm components, we employed a multimodal fluorescence microscopy approach. As shown in Figure 4E, treatment with 10 µm SAP2‐PEG resulted in significant disruption of biofilm extracellular polymeric substances (EPS) compared to untreated control biofilms. The live/dead cell staining revealed that while control biofilms exhibited dense, confluent cell coverage with predominantly viable cells forming a thick biofilm matrix, SAP2‐PEG‐treated biofilms displayed a 95.25% reduction in fluorescence intensity with scattered viable cells and increased cell death, indicating compromised biofilm integrity. Similarly, staining for extracellular DNA (eDNA) using Hoechst 33342 (blue fluorescence) revealed a substantial decrease (95.23%) in the eDNA content of treated biofilms relative to untreated controls. A similar profound effect was observed for lipids stained with Nile red, where control biofilms showed abundant lipid signals throughout the matrix, but SAP2‐PEG treatment resulted in an 87.03% reduction in lipid fluorescence with fragmented patches, suggesting significant disruption of membrane integrity and lipid‐containing EPS components. Additionally, exopolysaccharide staining using Calcofluor White Stain (Blue fluorescence) revealed a drastic reduction (65.23 %) in fluorescence intensity after treatment with SAP2‐PEG. These results indicate that SAP2‐PEG penetrates and effectively targets multiple components of biofilm EPS matrix, a particularly significant phenomenon given that traditional antimicrobials often struggle to penetrate biofilm matrices, leading to treatment failure.[ 30 ]

SAP2‐PEG Disrupts Quorum Sensing and Inhibits Virulence Factor Production

Given the importance of QS in bacterial pathogenesis, we sought to evaluate the effects of SAP2‐PEG on QS gene expression and the production of key virulence factors. Using the P. aeruginosa ATCC10145 GFP reporter strain, which is specifically engineered to express green fluorescent protein (GFP) following the activation of QS pathways. GFP expression results in measurable fluorescence that correlates with QS activity intensity. SAP2‐PEG demonstrated significant quorum‐sensing inhibitory activity (Figure 4F; Figure S10, Supporting Information). Treatment at both 1 and 2 µm, representing sub‐MIC concentrations, resulted in a substantial reduction in fluorescence signal compared to the untreated control, indicating effective suppression of QS‐regulated gene expression since the reporter system only produces measurable fluorescence when QS pathways are actively functioning. The observed fluorescence reduction was comparable to that achieved with the positive control erythromycin (ERY 10 µm), a known QS inhibitor, validating the specificity of SAP2‐PEG's anti‐QS activity (Figure S10, Supporting Information). This quorum‐sensing disruption likely contributes to reduced virulence factor production and impaired biofilm formation through interference with bacterial cell‐to‐cell communication networks. Indeed, SAP2‐PEG significantly reduced the production of key P. aeruginosa virulence factors. Pyocyanin production, a critical virulence factor and oxidative stress mediator, was decreased by >60% at sub‐MIC concentrations, with statistical significance observed across multiple treatment concentrations (Figure 4G). Similarly, elastase activity, which contributes to tissue damage and immune evasion, was significantly suppressed by SAP2‐PEG treatment (Figure 4H). Hemolytic activity, another vital virulence determinant, was also substantially reduced (Figure 4I), indicating broad suppression of virulence mechanisms without necessarily achieving bacterial killing.

SAP2‐PEG also significantly impacted the motility of P. aeruginosa, a pathogen known for its diverse motility mechanisms, including flagella‐mediated swimming, type IV pili‐mediated twitching, and biosurfactant‐mediated swarming.[ 31 ] These motility mechanisms are crucial for biofilm formation and the establishment of persistent infections.[ 32 , 33 ] As shown in Figure 4J, untreated control cultures exhibited robust motility with extensive migration from the central inoculation point, forming distinct halos of bacteria for both swimming (0.3% agar) and swarming (0.5% agar) media plates. However, treatment with sub‐MIC concentrations of SAP2‐PEG resulted in significant, dose‐dependent reductions in bacterial motility across both assay conditions. At 1 µm (0.32 × MIC) SAP2‐PEG, swimming motility was substantially impaired, with bacteria confined to a much smaller zone around the inoculation site compared to the expansive migration observed in controls. This inhibitory effect was even more pronounced at 2 µm (0.65 × MIC) SAP2‐PEG, where both swimming and swarming motilities were profoundly inhibited, with bacterial growth restricted to the immediate vicinity of the initial inoculation point (Figure 4J). To confirm that inhibition of quorum sensing and bacterial motility was not due to bacterial death, we conducted a growth inhibition assay using the same concentrations. Our findings revealed that at both 1 and 2 µm SAP2‐PEG concentrations, bacterial growth curves were virtually indistinguishable from the untreated control, showing normal proliferation kinetics over the 28‐h observation period (Figure S11, Supporting Information). In contrast, at the MIC concentration of 3.2 µm, bacterial growth was substantially inhibited throughout the 28 h. These results conclusively suggest that the observed reductions in bacterial motility and quorum‐sensing activities occur at sub‐lethal concentrations, suggesting that SAP2‐PEG specifically targets these virulence mechanisms without compromising bacterial viability.

SAP2‐PEG Disrupts Critical Biological Processes in Both P. aeruginosa and S. aureus

Comprehensive transcriptome profiling revealed widespread changes in gene expression patterns in both P. aeruginosa and S. aureus following treatment with 4 µm SAP2‐PEG for 1 h. Volcano plot analysis demonstrated substantial differential gene expression, with P. aeruginosa showing 222 significantly altered genes and S. aureus exhibiting 137 differentially expressed genes (Figure 5A,B). The magnitude of these transcriptional changes suggests that SAP2‐PEG has a profound effect on fundamental cellular processes, rather than targeting isolated pathways.

Gene Ontology enrichment analysis revealed that SAP2‐PEG treatment significantly impacted multiple critical biological processes in both pathogens (Figure 5C,D). In P. aeruginosa, the most significantly affected pathways included transcriptional regulation, cellular response to stimuli, and multi‐organism processes, with powerful effects on biofilm formation and virulence factor production. Cell envelope organization and membrane integrity pathways were also substantially disrupted, consistent with the peptide's membrane‐targeting properties. S. aureus showed similar patterns of pathway disruption, with significant effects on transcriptional regulatory activity, cellular response to stimuli, and multi‐organism processes. Notably, pathways involved in cell wall organization, membrane transport, and small molecule metabolic processes were extensively disrupted, indicating a comprehensive breakdown of cellular homeostasis.

KEGG pathway analysis revealed that SAP2‐PEG treatment induced significant perturbations in core metabolic pathways in both organisms (Figure 5E,F). In P. aeruginosa, biosynthesis of secondary metabolites, microbial metabolism in diverse environments, and metabolic pathways were among the most significantly affected processes. The disruption of biotin formation, cofactor biosynthesis, and amino acid metabolism suggests that SAP2‐PEG interferes with essential biosynthetic processes required for bacterial survival and growth. S. aureus exhibited similar metabolic disruption patterns, with particular effects on ABC transporters, metabolic pathways, and biosynthesis of secondary metabolites. The downregulation of teichoic acid biosynthesis and peptidoglycan biosynthesis pathways indicates specific targeting of cell wall synthesis processes, which is consistent with the peptide's mechanism of action involving cell envelope disruption.

Detailed analysis of individual gene expression changes revealed species‐specific responses to SAP2‐PEG treatment (Figure 5G,H). In P. aeruginosa, critical virulence and resistance genes showed significant differential expression. The flagellar hook‐length control protein (flhK), quorum‐sensing transcriptional regulator (rhlR), and type IV pilus assembly protein (pilV) were substantially downregulated, indicating disruption of motility and cell‐to‐cell communication systems. Multiple antibiotic resistance genes, including multidrug efflux pumps (mexT, mexR) and beta‐lactamase genes, were also significantly affected, suggesting that SAP2‐PEG treatment may compromise bacterial antibiotic resistance mechanisms. S. aureus exhibited distinct but complementary responses. The agr system, which is central to S. aureus virulence factor production and biofilm formation, showed coordinated downregulation across all major components. Additionally, genes involved in cell wall synthesis (murF, pbp), biofilm formation (icaA, icaB), and antibiotic resistance (mecA induction factors) were significantly affected. Furthermore, both bacterial species showed significant disruption of their respective quorum‐sensing systems. In P. aeruginosa, the las and rhl QS circuits were extensively affected, with downregulation of key regulatory components including lasR, rhlR, and associated signal molecule synthesis genes. This disruption accounts for the observed reductions in virulence factor production and biofilm formation capabilities. S. aureus showed even more pronounced effects on quorum sensing, with the entire agr regulon being significantly downregulated.

Transcriptome analysis confirmed upregulation of several pathways involved in bacterial survival and stress response (Figure S12, Supporting Information). In P. aeruginosa, upregulation of critical stress response genes, including ohrR (hydroperoxide stress response), ygiW (stress tolerance), srfA (stress response facilitator), and oxyR (oxidative stress regulator), indicates the bacterium's attempt to combat oxidative damage and membrane perturbation. Similarly, membrane repair systems were activated, as evidenced by increased expression of ymgE and phnB (stress response membrane proteins), along with DNA repair mechanisms including ada, recA, and ercC‐2. The upregulation of efflux pump components (msbA, sugE) and universal stress proteins (uspE, uspA, uspL) further indicates a coordinated cellular defense response against antimicrobial assault (Figure S12A, Supporting Information). In S. aureus, a parallel stress response was observed with upregulation of glsB (stress response membrane protein), ctsR (transcriptional regulator of stress response), gsp13 (general stress protein), and spxA (thiol/oxidative stress sensor). The activation of DNA repair systems through recO and recF, combined with enhanced expression of cell wall‐active antibiotic response proteins (liaF) and peroxide stress regulators (perR), reflects the bacterium's recognition of severe cellular damage (Figure S12B, Supporting Information). Notably, both species exhibited upregulation of genes involved in lipid metabolism and regulation of cell wall biosynthesis, suggesting attempts to restore membrane integrity. RT qPCR confirmed the downregulation of key QS genes in both pathogens (Figure S12C, Supporting Information).

SAP2‐PEG Shows Efficacy Against Wound Polymicrobial Biofilm Infection

Polymicrobial biofilms, particularly those involving S. aureus and P. aeruginosa, are major contributors to chronic wound infections and catheter‐associated infections.[ 34 ] To evaluate the therapeutic potential of SAP2‐PEG against polymicrobial biofilm infections, we established a murine wound infection model using a dual‐species biofilm of S. aureus and P. aeruginosa grown on polycarbonate membranes (Figure 6 ). The experimental timeline involved biofilm formation on day 0, followed by wound creation and biofilm implantation on day 2, with daily treatment and monitoring through day 9 (Figure 6A). Visual assessment of wound healing revealed marked differences between treatment groups (Figure 6B). Vehicle‐treated wounds showed persistent inflammation and delayed healing throughout the 9‐day observation period. In contrast, both gentamicin and SAP2‐PEG treatments showed progressive wound closure, with SAP2‐PEG‐treated wounds showing the most favourable healing progression and reduced inflammatory appearance (Figure S13, Supporting Information). Quantitative wound healing analysis confirmed these visual observations (Figure 6C). SAP2‐PEG treatment resulted in significantly accelerated wound closure compared to vehicle control, with healing kinetics superior to gentamicin treatment. The wound healing time analysis revealed that SAP2‐PEG achieved more rapid and complete wound closure across all time points measured.

Microbiological analysis revealed that SAP2‐PEG treatment significantly reduced both S. aureus and P. aeruginosa colony‐forming units (CFUs) in the infected wounds (Figure 6D,E). SAP2‐PEG exhibited comparable or superior antibiofilm activity to gentamicin against both bacterial species, with S. aureus CFUs reduced by ≈3–4 logs and P. aeruginosa CFUs reduced by 2‐3 logs compared to vehicle controls. In addition, analysis of inflammatory markers showed that SAP2‐PEG treatment significantly reduced interleukin‐6 (IL‐6) and tumor necrosis factor‐alpha (TNF‐α) levels compared to vehicle controls (Figure 6F), indicating effective modulation of the inflammatory response. These results suggest that SAP2‐PEG not only provides antimicrobial activity but also helps resolve infection‐associated inflammation. Moreover, histopathological examination of wound tissues revealed distinct differences between treatment groups (Figure 6H). Vehicle‐treated wounds exhibited extensive tissue necrosis, inflammatory cell infiltration, and poor tissue architecture. Gentamicin treatment showed modest improvement in tissue organization. Notably, SAP2‐PEG‐treated wounds revealed well‐organized tissue architecture, reduced inflammatory infiltrate, and evidence of active tissue repair and regeneration.

SAP2‐PEG Shows Efficacy in Catheter‐Associated Biofilm Infection Model

To further validate the clinical relevance of SAP2‐PEG, we employed a catheter‐associated infection model using subcutaneously implanted silicon catheters pre‐colonized with polymicrobial biofilms and treated with 60 µg SAP2‐PEG or gentamicin (Figure 6I). Visual examination of the catheter implantation sites revealed striking differences between treatment groups (Figure 6J). The vehicle group showed extensive lesion formation and tissue necrosis around the catheter implantation site, indicating severe local infection and tissue damage. Remarkably, SAP2‐PEG‐treated animals demonstrated minimal lesion formation and preserved tissue integrity at the catheter‐surrounding tissues implantation sites, suggesting effective control of biofilm‐associated infection and prevention of local tissue damage. Indeed, biofilm biomass quantification from explanted catheters using crystal violet staining showed reduced biofilm biomass on SAP2‐PEG‐treated catheters compared to controls (Figure 6K). Consistently, quantitative analysis of catheter‐associated biofilms confirmed significant reductions in bacterial burden with SAP2‐PEG treatment. Both S. aureus and P. aeruginosa CFUs were substantially reduced on SAP2‐PEG‐treated catheters (Figure 6L,M), with efficacy comparable to or exceeding gentamicin treatment. Analysis of inflammatory markers in the catheter infection model paralleled the wound infection results, with SAP2‐PEG treatment leading to significant reductions in IL‐6, TNF‐α, and MCP‐1 levels (Figure 6N–P), indicating effective control of local inflammatory responses.

Histopathological analysis of peri‐catheter tissues revealed that SAP2‐PEG treatment preserved standard tissue architecture and reduced inflammatory cell infiltration compared to vehicle controls (Figure 6Q). The tissue surrounding SAP2‐PEG‐treated catheters showed minimal inflammatory changes and maintained structural integrity, contrasting sharply with the extensive tissue damage and inflammatory infiltrate observed in vehicle‐treated controls (Figure S14, Supporting Information). These comprehensive results demonstrate that SAP2‐PEG exhibits potent efficacy against polymicrobial biofilm infections in both wound and catheter‐associated infection models, combining effective antimicrobial activity with beneficial anti‐inflammatory properties and superior tissue healing outcomes.

SAP2‐PEG Shows Efficacy in In Vivo Models of P. aeruginosa Lung Infection and S. aureus Peritoneal Infection

To evaluate the systemic therapeutic potential of SAP2‐PEG against monomicrobial infections, we established two distinct infection models: P. aeruginosa lung infection and S. aureus peritoneal infection. We developed an acute pneumonia model by intranasal inoculation of mice with 1 × 10⁷ CFU mL−1 of P. aeruginosa, followed by intravenous treatment 2 h post‐infection and assessment at 18 h (Figure 7A). SAP2‐PEG treatment resulted in a significant dose‐dependent reduction in pulmonary bacterial burden compared to vehicle controls (Figure 7B). Both 1 and 2 mg kg−1 doses of SAP2‐PEG achieved substantial CFU reductions, with efficacy comparable to colistin treatment at 1 mg kg−1. Analysis of inflammatory markers revealed that SAP2‐PEG treatment effectively modulated the pulmonary inflammatory response. TNF‐α levels and IL‐6 levels were significantly reduced in a dose‐dependent manner compared to vehicle controls (Figure 7C,D). These findings indicate that SAP2‐PEG not only provides antimicrobial activity but also helps resolve infection‐associated pulmonary inflammation.

Histopathological examination of lung tissues confirmed the therapeutic benefits of SAP2‐PEG treatment (Figure 7E). Vehicle‐treated lungs exhibited extensive inflammatory cell infiltration, alveolar damage, and hemorrhage characteristic of severe pneumonia. In contrast, SAP2‐PEG‐treated lungs at both doses showed dramatically reduced inflammatory infiltrate, preserved alveolar architecture, and minimal tissue damage. The 2 mg kg−1 dose showed particularly impressive tissue preservation, with lung histology approaching that of healthy controls. Colistin treatment also showed therapeutic benefit, though with some residual inflammatory changes.

To assess SAP2‐PEG activity against Gram‐positive pathogens, we established an S. aureus peritoneal infection model, treating intraperitoneally with 10 mg kg−1 administered 2 h post‐infection and evaluating at 18 h (Figure 6F). SAP2‐PEG treatment significantly reduced bacterial dissemination to multiple organs (lungs, liver, kidney, and spleen) compared to vehicle controls, with efficacy comparable or superior to vancomycin (Figure 7H–K). Consistently, inflammatory marker analysis revealed that SAP2‐PEG treatment effectively controlled systemic inflammation, with a significant reduction in IL‐6 and TNF‐α (Figure 7G). Comprehensive histopathological examination of major organs revealed that SAP2‐PEG treatment provided substantial protection against infection‐induced tissue damage (Figure 7L).

Discussion and Conclusion

The escalating prevalence of MDR pathogens and their associated biofilm infections underscores the urgent need for novel therapeutic strategies that can effectively circumvent conventional mechanisms of antibiotic resistance. Here, SAP2‐PEG emerges as a promising candidate for this purpose due to its multi‐modal therapeutic approach that combines direct antimicrobial activity with inhibition of QS.[ 35 ] The amphiphilic nature of the peptide, characterized by distinct hydrophobic and hydrophilic termini, appears crucial for achieving optimal self‐assembly dynamics that facilitate both membrane interaction and biofilm penetration, which may explain the peptide's superior performance against established biofilms that typically present significant barriers to conventional antimicrobials. Here, the selection of P. aeruginosa and S. aureus as model organisms is particularly justified given their clinical significance as leading causes of healthcare‐associated infections.[ 36 , 37 ]

The unique nanoparticle‐to‐fibril transformation exhibited by SAP2‐PEG under membrane‐mimicking conditions or in the presence of cell wall components represents a pivotal mechanism directly linking structural dynamics to dual antimicrobial efficacy. This structural reorganization likely occurs when cationic peptide domains encounter anionic bacterial surface components, with the resulting electrostatic attraction triggering conformational changes that expose buried hydrophobic regions and promote β‐sheet formation and subsequent fibril assembly.[ 38 ] This structural reorganization enhances both antimicrobial mechanisms simultaneously as the resulting fibrillar networks can physically entangle bacterial cells while continuously releasing active peptide units, creating a dual‐action antimicrobial system that combines immediate membrane disruption with prolonged bacteriostatic effect.[ 39 ] Indeed, results from the bacterial aggregation essay and SIM super‐resolution microscopy provided comprehensive visual evidence supporting the proposed dual‐action mechanism whereby SAP2‐PEG's fibrillar transformation simultaneously constrains bacterial movement through physical entrapment and compromises membrane integrity, resulting in synergistic bactericidal effects across both Gram‐positive and Gram‐negative species.

Inhibiting QS can significantly diminish pathogenicity and restore antibiotic sensitivity, as noted in several studies exploring QS blockers.[ 40 , 41 , 42 ] The concept of AMPs with dual QS inhibitory and bactericidal activities has a promising therapeutic strategy, with several peptides demonstrating this multifaceted approach.[ 43 ] For instance, tryptophan‐containing peptides have been shown to impair QS and biofilm development in MDR P. aeruginosa while exhibiting synergistic effects with conventional antibiotics.[ 20 ] Similarly, other AMPs have shown the capacity to suppress P. aeruginosa and other pathogens at sub‐inhibitory concentrations via disruption of QS pathways, highlighting the therapeutic potential of targeting both bacterial viability and virulence simultaneously. Consistent with other studies, our findings revealed that SAP2‐PEG operates through a sophisticated dual‐target mechanism, simultaneously disrupting bacterial membranes and inhibiting quorum‐sensing pathways by downregulating key QS genes, including the las and rhl systems in P. aeruginosa and the agr system in S. aureus. When compared to established small molecule QS inhibitors such as furanone derivatives, halogenated compounds, and natural products like cinnamaldehyde and garlic extracts,[ 44 ] SAP2‐PEG demonstrates superior advantages through its dual‐action mechanism that combines QS inhibition with direct bactericidal activity. In contrast, traditional QS inhibitors typically function as bacteriostatic agents that require combination with conventional antibiotics for complete bacterial eradication.[ 45 ] The multi‐target mechanism of SAP2‐PEG fundamentally reduces the likelihood of resistance development through several molecular and biological principles that distinguish it from single‐target agents.[ 46 ] However, resistance development remains possible through mechanisms such as proteolytic degradation or active efflux pumps that could simultaneously affect both antimicrobial and anti‐QS functions. Nevertheless, the dual targeting approach may still provide some advantage by requiring bacteria to overcome multiple selective pressures, potentially slowing the emergence of fully resistant phenotypes compared to single‐target therapies.

The effectiveness of SAP2‐PEG against polymicrobial biofilms, particularly in chronic wounds and catheter‐associated infections, emphasizes its clinical relevance given the increasing incidence of these co‐infections, which are further complicated by enhanced antibiotic resistance due to inter‐species synergies.[ 47 ] Given that catheter‐related bloodstream infections represent a significant cause of healthcare‐associated morbidity and mortality,[ 34 ] the ability of SAP2‐PEG to reduce biofilm biomass on catheter surfaces substantially suggests that it could be developed as a catheter coating to prevent device‐associated infections. Furthermore, the demonstrated systemic efficacy against both P. aeruginosa lung infection and S. aureus peritoneal infection, with comparable or superior performance to standard‐of‐care antibiotics (colistin and vancomycin), establishes SAP2‐PEG as a potential therapeutic for serious systemic infections, while the dose‐dependent efficacy observed in the pneumonia model, combined with the preservation of standard organ architecture across multiple tissues, suggests a favorable therapeutic window and minimal off‐target toxicity.

The innovative self‐assembly and dual‐target mechanism of SAP2‐PEG represents a transformative advance in the development of antimicrobial peptides. By simultaneously addressing multiple bacterial survival strategies, particularly in the context of MDR infections, SAP2‐PEG promises to expand the antimicrobial armamentarium. However, despite the promising attributes of SAP2‐PEG, the observed significant increase in MIC values in the presence of 50% serum raises important considerations for clinical translation, suggesting potential interactions with serum proteins, proteolytic degradation, or sequestration by serum components that could compromise therapeutic efficacy in vivo. Beyond serum stability challenges, it is also imperative to consider the hostile proteolytic environment at infection sites, particularly those involving P. aeruginosa, which secretes an extensive arsenal of virulence‐associated proteases, including elastases A and B (LasA and LasB), alkaline protease AprA, protease IV, and collagenase.[ 48 ] These bacterial exoproteases can potentially cleave SAP2‐PEG at vulnerable peptide bonds and compromise both its structural integrity and antimicrobial activity. Similarly, S. aureus secretes several proteases, such as Aureolysin, which could contribute to peptide degradation in mixed infections or device‐associated biofilms where both pathogens co‐exist.[ 49 ]

Future development strategies should therefore address both serum and bacterial protease stability through comprehensive approaches, including chemical modifications such as D‐amino acid substitutions at critical cleavage sites or cyclization to reduce accessible termini while maintaining antimicrobial activity. Additionally, further evaluation of its pharmacokinetics, stability, and long‐term potential for resistance development, alongside optimization of delivery strategies across various clinical scenarios, including topical applications for wound care and systemic treatments for severe infections, is necessary for successful clinical translation of SAP2‐PEG.

Experimental Section

Peptides Synthesis

The peptides were synthesized by Syn‐peptide Co., Ltd, Shanghai, China, and their molecular weights were confirmed via matrix‐assisted laser desorption/ionization–time‐of‐flight mass spectrometry (MALDI–TOF MS). The synthesized peptides were purified by high‐performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) to achieve a purity of > 95%.

Determination of Self‐Assembly Ability

The ability of the peptides to self‐assemble was confirmed using transmission electron microscopy (TEM). Peptide solutions 100 µm in PBS (pH 7.4) were observed using a transmission microscope (Jeol, JEM‐1011, Japan). Briefly, 10 µL of the peptide solution was dropped onto a TEM grid and allowed to adsorb and aerate for 2 min, leaving a film of the peptide on the grid. A drop of negative stain solution of 2% uranyl acetate was added to the grid, and after aeration, high‐resolution TEM images of the peptide sample at various magnifications were acquired.

Evaluation of Antimicrobial Activity

The Minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) assay was used to determine the antimicrobial activity of the peptides against bacteria and fungi based on previously reported protocols.[ 50 , 51 ]

SIM Super‐Resolution Microscopy for Peptide‐Bacteria Interaction Visualization

Bacterial cultures were prepared as described above and treated with 20 µm SAP2‐PEG for 2 h at 37 °C to allow for fibril formation and bacterial interaction. For dual fluorescent labeling, bacterial membranes were stained with DiD (DilC8) (Sigma) by adding the dye to a final concentration of 5 µm and incubating for 30 min at room temperature in the dark. Biotinylated SAP2‐PEG was prepared by conjugating biotin to the peptide N‐terminus during synthesis. Following peptide treatment and bacterial membrane staining, samples were washed twice with PBS to remove unbound dye, then incubated with streptavidin‐Alexa Fluor 568 (1:1000 dilution) for 45 min at room temperature to detect biotinylated peptide. After final washing steps, 10 µL aliquots were placed on glass coverslips and examined using super‐resolution structured illumination microscopy with appropriate filter sets for DiD (excitation 644 nm, emission 665 nm) and Alexa Fluor 568 (excitation 578 nm, emission 603 nm). Control samples included untreated bacteria with membrane staining only, and peptide‐treated bacteria without biotin conjugation to confirm specificity. Image acquisition was performed using the Multi‐SIM imaging system (Beijing Nanolnsights‐tech Co., Ltd) with a 100X/1.49 NA oil objective (Nikon CFI SR HP Apo), semiconductor lasers (405 nm)/solid‐state lasers (488,561,640 nm), and a sCMOS (Complementary Metal‐Oxide‐Semiconductor) camera (Photometrics Kinetix). Further image processing and colocalization analysis were conducted using ImageJ software.

Induction of Resistance Development

A multi‐passage resistance induction study adapted from de‐Breji et al. 2018[ 52 ] was employed to evaluate the potential development of resistance to SAP2‐PEG and gentamicin by P. aeruginosa and S. aureus.

Biofilm Inhibition and Eradication

Biofilm inhibition and eradication were performed for both single‐species and polymicrobial biofilms based on previous protocols, with minimal modifications to accommodate polymicrobial biofilms.[ 53 ]

Efficacy of SAP2‐PEG on Polymicrobial Biofilm‐Infected Wound Infection Model

To assess the in vivo efficacy of SAP‐2 peptides against polymicrobial biofilm‐associated wound infections, a murine wound model was utilized using female BALB/c mice. Full‐thickness excisional wounds were created on the dorsal surface of the mice, and the wound was covered with a polycarbonate membrane containing a mixed biofilm of clinical strains P. aeruginosa, P. a 11, and S. aureus, S. a 01 for 48 h. Afterward, mice were randomly assigned to four treatment groups (n = 7): Groups receiving 60 µg SAP2‐NH2 and SAP2‐PEG, the group receiving 60 µg gentamicin (positive control), and the infected untreated control group. Wound progression was monitored over time, and images and wound measurements were taken using a camera‐supported colony counting instrument (Shineso, iCount110). On day seven, post‐start of treatment, mice were anaesthetized, and tissue samples were collected to quantify the bacterial colony‐forming units (CFUs) within the biofilm‐infected wounds. Selective media, Mannitol Salt Agar (S. aureus), and Cetrimide Agar (P. aeruginosa) were used to isolate the two pathogens from wound tissue homogenates. Additionally, skin sections were collected for histological analyses and an ELISA assay to evaluate treatment outcomes.

Efficacy of SAP2‐PEG in a Polymicrobial Biofilm‐Catheter Infection Mouse Model

To evaluate the antimicrobial activity of the peptide in a polymicrobial biofilm‐catheter infected mice model, we used 10‐week‐old Balb/c female mice using a protocol adapted from a previous study.[ 47 ] First, silicone catheter segments (1 cm length) were incubated with a mixed inoculum of S. aureus S. a 01 and P. aeruginosa P. a 11 (1:1 ratio, 1 × 106 CFU mL−1 total) in tryptic soy broth (TSB) media, at 37 °C for 24 h to allow biofilm to attach. Afterward, the catheters were washed with sterile PBS to remove any non‐adherent cells. The catheters were surgically implanted subcutaneously in mice 24 h before starting treatment. Mice were placed into four groups (n = 6 mice per group): Mock group (sterile catheters), Untreated group (biofilm + PBS), Test group (biofilm + 10 mg kg−1 SA2‐PEG), and positive control group (biofilm + 10 mg kg−1 gentamicin). Mice were subcutaneously injected with 10 mg kg−1 of the compounds or PBS for five consecutive days. On day 6, the mice were sacrificed, blood was collected for ELISA assay, and skin sections were collected for CFU determination and histopathological analysis. Moreover, the catheters were collected and processed as follows to quantify the biofilm biomass: the catheters were gently washed with water to remove any adherent cells and tissues, and then stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 15 min. After washing the excess dye, the crystal violet was solubilized with 95 % ethanol, and the absorbance was measured at 595 nm using a microplate reader. Biofilm biomass was expressed as the mean absorbance value.

Efficacy of SAP2‐PEG in an Acute Lung Infection Model

For the acute lung infection model, female BALB/c mice (10 weeks old, 20–24 g) were infected via intranasal instillation with 1 × 10⁷ CFU mL−1 of P. aeruginosa P. a 11 suspended in sterile saline. Two hours post‐infection, mice received treatment via intranasal administration (1.0 and 2.0 mg kg−1 SAP2‐PEG, 1.0 mg kg−1 colistin, or PBS for negative control). At 18 h following the initial bacterial challenge, mice were euthanized, and lung tissues were harvested for analysis. Bacterial burden in lung homogenates was determined by CFU enumeration on centrimide selective agar plates. Inflammatory responses were assessed through enzyme‐linked immune ELISA measurement of cytokine levels in lung tissue homogenates. Additionally, lung tissue sections were prepared for histopathological examination using H&E staining to evaluate tissue damage, inflammatory cell infiltration, and overall pathological changes associated with the infection and treatment response.

Efficacy of SAP2‐PEG against S. aureus Systemic Infection

For the systemic infection model, female BALB/c mice (10 weeks old, 20–24 g) were challenged via intraperitoneal injection with a lethal dose of 2 × 107 CFU mL−1 S. aureus S. a 01. Two hours post‐infection, mice received 10 mg kg−1 SAP2‐PEG or vancomycin treatment. At 18 h following the initial bacterial challenge, mice were euthanized, and major organs, including kidneys, lungs, liver, and spleen, were aseptically harvested for bacterial burden analysis. Tissue homogenates were serially diluted and plated on mannitol salt agar media to determine CFUs per gram of tissue. Organ sections were also prepared for histopathological examination and H&E staining.

Statistical Analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM) unless otherwise specified. Sample sizes (n) for each experimental group and analysis are reported in the figure legends and results section. Statistical differences between groups were assessed using one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Dunnett's multiple comparisons post‐hoc test for comparisons against a single control group. Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 10.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). For all statistical tests, p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
