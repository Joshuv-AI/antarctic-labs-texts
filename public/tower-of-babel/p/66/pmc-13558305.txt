AI and machine learning for early identification of eating disorders: a narrative review and Indian contextual insights
Authors: Yutika Shirgaonkar, Devaki Gokhale
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEating disorders (EDs) are a growing global health concern and are increasingly associated with negative body-image perceptions linked to social-media exposure, particularly among adolescents. Early identification and screening of individuals at risk can influence effective patient management and care. AI models, such as machine learning, natural language processing, or wearables, have shown early promise as potential screening tools, offering early prediction, improved accuracy, reduced reporting bias, and better user compliance than conventional methods.MethodsLiterature from PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science (2015–July 2025) produced 606 records after applying Boolean operators “OR” for synonyms and “AND” to link core concepts. 23 studies were included using the PICOT framework. Data extraction examined predictors, algorithms, metrics, and limitations.ResultsThe findings on predicting EDs highlight six major domains: traditional machine-learning (ML) models, sensor-based systems, mobile and wearable technologies, natural language processing (NLP), questionnaire-based models, and integrated risk prediction frameworks. Among all the domains, sensor-based and wearable systems, along with NLP from social-media data, demonstrated the highest accuracy. While traditional questionnaire-based models integrating validated tools with AI/ML have shown moderate performance, they remain limited by small sample sizes, standardised settings, and a lack of validation in low-resource settings, such as India.ConclusionsAI-based predictive models demonstrate promising early potential for scalable, likely less biased, and non-stigmatising detection of EDs. Despite the promising performance demonstrated across multiple AI-based approaches, certain challenges remain, including generalizability, ethical considerations, and real-world implications. Although most AI-based eating-disorder research has been conducted in high-income countries, evidence from developing and culturally diverse settings remains limited, where cultural variations and social stigma co-exist. In such contexts, the use of multimodal systems can support early prediction of eating disorders. Future research should be culturally diverse, integrate multimodal approaches with social and peer factors, and use validated frameworks for effective practice.Level of Evidence: Level V. Introduction

Eating disorders are serious mental health conditions characterised by persistent disturbances in eating behaviours and maladaptive or ruminative thoughts related to food, body weight, and body shape, often resulting in significant physical, psychological, and social impairment [1]. Globally, eating disorders impose a significant health challenge, with a reported prevalence increase from 3.4% in 2000 to 7.8% in 2018 and 22. % especially among children and adolescents in 2023 [2]. Projections to 2035 indicate continued growth in disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) and incidence, especially among women and young adults in high socio-demographic index regions [3]. In India, available data suggest that eating disorders affect 2–3% of the general population, consistent with National Mental Health Survey data [4], but the proportion of adolescents identified as being at risk for eating disorders rises to 24.7% among individuals aged 10–19 years, reaching 28.4% in 16–19 years and 41.3% high-risk in urban 15–18 years [5, 6]. Overall, eating disorders affect a notable proportion of the population, particularly urban youth. Although eating disorders are commonly observed in female populations, increasing evidence suggests that males and gender-diverse individuals are also affected and may present with different behavioural and psychological patterns [7, 8]. The most common cause for eating disorders has been linked to excessive use of social media, which increases the risk by promoting social comparisons, influencer role modelling, and thereby creating an exacerbating feedback loop among vulnerable populations [9–11].

Diagnosis of eating disorders relies primarily on conventional methods, such as structured clinical interviews (e.g., the Eating-Disorder Examination) and self-report screening tools (e.g., the Eating Attitudes Test-26), alongside Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders-5 (DSM-5) criteria that assess behavioural, cognitive, and physical symptoms. These approaches also have some important limitations. People may deny or hide their symptoms, making detection difficult. They may not identify early or mild cases, or cases that look different across cultures. They also do not capture different patterns of weight loss, such as gradual, progressive loss; rapid, acute loss; cyclical fluctuations in weight; hidden weight loss with normal BMI; malnutrition [12, 13]; or new types of eating disorders, such as restrictive food intake disorder [14]. In addition, all the above-listed methods are primarily targeted towards the western population, wherein eating disorders may not always suit local, cultural and healthcare needs of resource-limited developing countries, such as India. Eating disorders in developing countries are often misunderstood or judged negatively as "lifestyle choices," linguistic barriers in non-English speaking populations may exist, and acute shortages of mental health professionals are often observed [15]. These conventional methods result in self-report bias, lack of cultural suitability, and dependence on highly trained professionals, which may limit early screening and identification of eating disorders. Hence, the development of AI approaches to support large-scale early screening and identification has become imperative.

AI machine learning (ML), particularly, has emerged as a transformative tool in healthcare by processing multimodal data, such as self-reports, physiological signals, social media, and records, to enable scalable early identification and screening of eating disorders [16]. Given the challenges of conventional methods, the rise of AI-powered screening tools offers a theoretically grounded alternative by leveraging digital data, such as social-media activity, body-related signals, and app-based questionnaires. These systems may help identify people who would not normally seek medical help because of stigma, high costs, or limited access to healthcare services [16]. Technologies, including NLP on social media to detect eating-disorder linguistic markers [17] and wearable sensors that track physiological precursors (e.g., heart rate variability predicting binge episodes), may reduce some limitations associated with self-report measures and enable large-scale early screening in low-resource settings [18]. Although traditional clinical assessment methods for eating disorders may be associated with perceived stigma due to direct evaluation, AI-assisted screening methods may help reduce this hurdle, even though they are managed by human-derived data, by offering more private and less judgemental pathways. However, they should be supervised by professional clinicians [19]. For instance, McClure et al. [20] reviewed 45 studies that used random forests and support vector machines (SVMs) to classify eating-disorder diagnoses with > 70% accuracy, aiming to predict the presence or absence of an eating disorder or to classify different ED subtypes [20]. Certain Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers–Long–Short-Term Memory (BERT–LSTM) models classify eating disorder with 98% accuracy by detecting linguistic markers of disordered eating [21], while wearable and motion-based algorithms predict the risk via heart rate variability and activity patterns [18, 22]. These AI approaches may support risk stratification in underserved populations like urban Indian youth, integrating predictors such as body dissatisfaction, emotional symptoms, and digital footprints for proactive screening beyond stigma-bound interviews.

Although clinical interviews and self-report screening tools are commonly used to assess eating disorders, AI systems should primarily be considered as early screening and identification tools, rather than replacements for comprehensive psychopathological evaluations, such as open-ended clinical interviews [23–25].

Current AI applications for eating-disorder identification demonstrate a strong performance in Western cohorts but lack validation in diverse, low-resource settings, such as India, where cultural stigma and the absence of AI models validated on Indian-language social-media data, along with data from different sources such as body signals, mobile app-based daily assessments, and online behaviour patterns that have not been properly tested in people from different cultural and language backgrounds. Eating-disorder assessment includes, first, screening, which refers to identifying people who may be at risk of developing eating disorders, often before clear clinical symptoms appear. Second, “Prediction” means assessing the possibility of future symptoms or behaviours, using behavioural, physiological, digital, or long-term data. And finally, “diagnosis” is performed by qualified healthcare professionals using validated psychiatric criteria. Therefore, this narrative review maps AI/ML studies on eating-disorder prediction and screening across global and Indian contexts, categorising models by data modality, performance metrics, populations, and outcomes while integrating nutritional perspectives with AI to enable early identification of disordered eating patterns and diet-related risk factors.

Search methodology

Search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was conducted across databases, including PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science, for articles published in English between January 2015 and July 2025. We chose 2015 as the starting year, because it marks a shift in healthcare research towards modern AI methods. This change was led by significant advances in machine learning, deep learning, and data-driven analysis, as well as greater access to large clinical datasets and stronger computing performance. Together, these factors led to the rapid growth and clinical use of AI in medical research from the mid-2010s onward [26, 27]. The search query focused on artificial intelligence and machine-learning applications for the early prediction of eating disorders. Search terms included “artificial intelligence,” “machine learning,” “neural networks,” “deep learning,” AND “eating disorders,” “anorexia nervosa,” “binge eating,” “bulimia nervosa,” combined with terms related to early prediction and detection, such as “early prediction,” “early detection,” “risk prediction,” “screening,” “classification,” “identification,” “diagnostic support,” and “predictive modeling.”

Boolean operators were applied using “OR” for synonyms and “AND” to link core concepts. Although “Binge-Eating Disorder (BED)” was not included as a separate search term, we intentionally used the broader term “binge eating”, because it represents the main feature of the disorder and is commonly used in AI and machine-learning studies. We also included the general term “eating disorders,” which allowed inclusion of studies on BED as well as other DSM-5 eating disorders, such as Avoidant/Restrictive Food Intake Disorder (ARFI) and Other Specified Feeding or Eating Disorder (OSFED). Overall, the search strategy was designed to include as many relevant studies as possible by using broader behavioural and category-based terms rather than only formal diagnostic names. The studies covered predictor variables, such as demographics, psychological features, and sensor data (including heart rate and electrodermal activity). In addition to PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science, a supplementary search of Indian/domestic journals and regional sources was conducted to identify locally published studies. However, no additional eligible articles relevant to the review objectives were identified.

Filtering strategy

A total of 606 bibliographic records were retrieved from three databases. Following duplicate removal and title/abstract screening, 150 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility. Of the 127 excluded articles, the main reasons included lack of AI/ML-based prediction models (n = 42), absence of evaluation results (n = 24), irrelevant populations or outcomes (n = 35), duplicate datasets (n = 14), and unavailability of full English text (n = 12). Finally, 23 studies met the inclusion criteria based on the PICOT framework. The framework ensured focused inclusion of relevant studies. Data extraction

For each selected article, we extracted comprehensive metadata and study details (see Supplementary 1), including the author and year of publication, the study design and sample size of the study population, and predictor variables, such as demographics, psychometrics, sensors (e.g., heart rate, electrodermal activity), and behaviours. We also captured the type of AI or AI-based system employed, algorithms, sensors, apps/wearables, and text/natural language processing, alongside assessment tools and questionnaires such as standardised instruments, clinical scoring systems, ecological momentary assessment (EMA) tools, and expert-labelled data used for model training and validation. Model performance metrics (e.g., AUC, sensitivity, F1-score), as well as identified strengths, limitations, and risk factors, were systematically documented to enable synthesis across studies. Performance metrics varied across studies, reflecting differences in study design, outcome type, and reporting standards. Therefore, direct comparison between studies should be done carefully. Some studies reported only descriptive findings rather than model performance results, and this was clearly noted.

Hybrid structural equation modelling–artificial neural network model (SEM–ANN)

Within this methodological landscape, a 2020 study by Maryam M. Kheirollahpour and colleagues provided an illustrative example of a hybrid SEM–ANN model to predict eating behaviours among 340 Malaysian university students, focusing on body shape concern (BSC), body appreciation (BA), and the Emotional Eating Scale (EES). SEM analyses showed that higher BSC scores were associated with greater engagement in unhealthy behaviours, particularly snacking. In contrast, BA demonstrated a protective role by promoting healthier eating patterns and reducing overall dietary unhealthiness. Specifically, BSC had the strongest positive association with snacking (β = 0.385, p = 0.01), indicating greater unhealthy behaviour. Although BA also showed a positive association with snacking, it was linked to healthier practices, including low-fat eating (β = 0.432) and planning ahead (β = 0.472). EES emerged as the strongest predictor of emotional eating, which was also associated with a preference for sweets and a tendency to skip meals. The hybrid SEM–ANN model outperformed standalone SEM, increasing R2 by 27% to 0.647 and reducing MSE by 9.6% to 0.548, with an optimal architecture of 3-17-8 (3 inputs, 17 hidden neurons, 8 outputs). Sensitivity analysis confirmed that EES is most influential for emotional eating, BA for healthy eating, and BSC for snacking [29]. Collectively, these findings highlight how hybrid models can retain psychological interpretability while materially improving predictive performance for specific eating behaviours.

Machine learning

Supervised ML models can predict eating-disorder outcomes using clinical and psychological data, although their advantage over traditional regression methods is often modest in BED prediction [30]. This 2023 study by Lauren N. Forrest, Valentina Ivezaj, and colleagues compared ML models against traditional regression for predicting BED treatment outcomes in 191 adults from a randomised controlled trial. ML models (elastic net, random forests) offered minimal gains over traditional regression, with AUCs from poor to fair (0.49–0.73) across outcomes. Random forests paired with optimism-corrected bootstrapping inflated performance (AUC 0.93–0.94) due to bias, while unbiased methods showed no significant superiority. Consistent predictors across modelling strategies included higher baseline binge frequency and weight/shape dissatisfaction for greater binge reduction; higher weight bias internalisation for persistent psychopathology; and rapid early response with lower rumination for subsequent weight loss, suggesting that carefully specified traditional models may already capture the dominant clinical signals in this context [31].

ML model for anorexia nervosa (AN) risk with single nucleotide polymorphism (SNPs)

Genetic risk prediction studies illustrate both the promise and current limitations of applying ML to complex, polygenic eating disorders, such as AN. In a pioneering 2015 study, Guo and Wei applied machine-learning methods to genome-wide association data to predict AN risk, analysing 3,940 AN cases and 9,266 controls from the AN Genetics Network (ANGN) consortium. Among the evaluated models, logistic regression with Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regularisation achieved the highest moderate predictive performance (AUC = 0.693), closely followed by support vector machines (AUC = 0.691). In contrast, gradient boosting trees performed worse (AUC = 0.623). Three SNPs (rs9982741, rs6092077, and rs2230513) were consistently ranked among the top predictors across repeated shuffling procedures, although none reached genome-wide significance (P < 0.05). The models exhibited low sensitivity (11%) but high specificity (97%), and predictive performance improved significantly with increasing training sample size (P < 0.05). Overall, this study represented an early and important advancement in eating-disorder prediction by introducing polygenic risk modelling for AN, using Genome-Wide Association Studies (GWAS)-derived SNPs alongside clinically relevant traits, such as weight overvaluation [32].

Oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) metabolic ML

Metabolism-focused ML studies are increasingly moving beyond self-reported data by using real-time physiological tests, such as OGTT. This 2025 study by Marianna Rania, Anna Procopio, and colleagues applied machine learning on OGTT data to detect BED in 281 adults with obesity. A comparison of a shorter OGTT protocol (Model 1) with an extended 5-h protocol (Model 2) was conducted. Model 2 outperformed Model 1, with a sensitivity of 0.75 vs. 0.50, a specificity of 0.67 vs. 0.80, and an accuracy of 0.71 vs. 0.67. Critical predictors were late-stage hypoglycemia (120–300 min), glucose levels, and insulin sensitivity; extended OGTTs boosted BED detection in obesity. This OGTT–ML approach offers a complementary physiological perspective for identifying BED by using objective markers, such as hypoglycemia and insulin resistance. However, it is meant to support, not replace, clinical assessment, which is still important for understanding the psychological aspects of BED [33].

Machine-learning-based early warning system

In a 2020 study, Ioannidis, Serfontein, and colleagues evaluated early warning systems (EWS) for detecting clinical deterioration among 36 hospitalised patients with AN, comparing traditional rule-based tools with machine-learning approaches. The analysis incorporated multimodal inpatient data, including anthropometric measures, clinical observations, laboratory parameters, and demographic characteristics. Among rule-based tools, MARSIPAN–guidance-based Modified Early Warning System (MARSI–MEWS) demonstrated the strongest performance (AUC = 0.916), outperforming Physical Risk in Eating-Disorders Index (PREDIX) graded (AUC = 0.865), NEWS (AUC = 0.828), and PREDIX binary (AUC = 0.785), while the ML–EWS achieved superior discrimination in independent samples (AUC = 0.980); however, only moderate inter-rater agreement (intraclass correlation 0.52) was observed, highlighting implementation challenges in routine practice. Together, these findings underscore the potential of AN-specific, multimodal EWS, particularly when coupled with ML, to enable earlier identification of deterioration by prioritising objective physiological signals over potentially biased self-reports [34].

mmEat (mmWave sensing)

Developed by Yucheng Xie et al. in 2022, it is a radar-based system for non-invasive, environment-invariant monitoring of fine-grained eating behaviour. The study involved over 1,000 eating activities from six participants, yielding an average recognition accuracy of 97%, with a false detection rate of 5% across varied utensils, environments, and non-dietary activities. Crucially, mmEat can discriminate subtle motion differences such as cutting with a knife vs. eating with a fork alone without retraining when the setting changes, supporting its claim of environment-invariant performance. mmEat supports long-term dietary tracking for health interventions, such as detecting overeating. It demonstrates mmWave's superiority for subtle gesture recognition, with potential applications in calorie estimation [35].

The RABiD (Rapid automatic bite detection)

Video-based systems targeting eating microstructure provide a complementary approach for passive assessment. This system was developed by Dimitrios Konstantinidis, Kosmas Dimitropoulos, et al. in 2020. The RABiD system provides automated quantification of eating microstructure, including bite rate, frequency, duration, and patterns, from video data, offering a gold-standard alternative to biased self-reports in eating-disorder assessment. Its high precision (F1 = 0.948) enables scalable analysis of eating-disorder-specific dysregulated behaviours, such as chaotic binge patterns, prolonged restraint, or accelerated compensatory intake [36].

Facial landmark tracking (video-based)

The rule-based bite-counting system developed by Tufano et al. (2024) automates the detection of eating events from standard meal videos, utilising MediaPipe's 468 3D facial landmarks to track mouth-opening ratios and movements in 15 participants. It processes features, such as bite count, eating rate, and food texture without the need for wearables, achieving 72.9–79.8% accuracy with annotations and 71.4% in leave-one-out validation [37]. This illustrates that lightweight, explainable computer vision pipelines can still yield useful performance even when deep-learning infrastructure or large training sets are unavailable.

Wearable passive sensing

The observational protocol outlined by Presseller et al. [18] pioneers wearable passive sensing to forecast transdiagnostic binge-eating (BE) episodes triggered by negative affect in 30 diverse adults, integrating Fitbit-derived heart rate (HR) and electrodermal activity with EMA [18]. The main idea was that wearable devices measured body signals related to stress and emotions, enabling tracking of mood changes over time. They could identify increases in negative feelings that might lead to binge eating while also reducing the need for frequent self-reports, which could be tiring or influence behaviour. Thus, sensors with AI/ML models offer a promising paradigm shift towards objective, passive, and real-time ED monitoring.

A deep-learning framework using a wearable watch

One prominent line of work focuses on leveraging smartwatches for free-living detection of eating episodes. Zhang and colleagues developed a deep-learning framework that uses Apple Watch accelerometer and gyroscope signals, collected over 3,828 h from 34 adults, to classify eating vs. non-eating in 5-min windows across demographics, including age, health literacy, and eHealth app usage. These eating detection patterns enable passive, free-living quantification of intake frequency, timing, and duration, which are core eating-disorder phenotypes that are often invisible during self-reporting. The system achieved a high AUC (0.951 aggregated) and low false positives (0.17/h) over 3,828 real-world hours, supporting objective tracking of dysregulated patterns, such as chaotic binges, nocturnal eating, or prolonged fasts in anorexia/bulimia [38].

Empatica E4 wristband

Some studies have used wearable sensors to directly detect eating-disorder behaviours, rather than only identifying when meals are consumed. For example, C. Ralph-Nearman, L. E. Sandoval-Araujo et al.'s 2024 pilot study applied machine-learning models to passive physiological data collected via the Empatica E4 wristband, including heart rate, electrodermal activity, and skin temperature, to detect eating-disorder-related behaviours in six participants during everyday life. The models achieved a mean accuracy of 91% (range: 84–99%), with 92% specificity and 90% sensitivity. By using passive sensing, this approach complements self-reported symptoms and enables more comprehensive phenotyping by combining physiological signals with the patient’s own experiences [39].

Oura Ring via the RADAR–remote monitoring technology (RMT) infrastructure

At the platform level, systems like RADAR-base demonstrate how data from wearables and smartphones can be combined to study eating disorders at scale. Zulqarnain Rashid and Amos A. Folarin et al.'s 2024 platform study scales RADAR-based RMT infrastructure to 500 participants using Oura Ring wearables and smartphones for multimodal digital phenotyping of mental/physical conditions, including eating disorders. It streams physiological (e.g., HRV), behavioural/mobility, environmental, speech, and EMA data via ML/signal processing for insights into eating-disorder progression/recovery [40]. Such infrastructures move beyond single-study prototypes towards reusable ecosystems in which eating-disorder-relevant digital markers can be identified, validated, and eventually translated into clinical decision support at scale.

SmartEater app with BISCUIT (Best items scale that is cross-validated, unit-weighted, informative, transparent)

In a 2023 two-study protocol, Arend, Kaiser, and colleagues developed and piloted the SmartEater mobile application, implementing the BISCUIT machine-learning algorithm to enable idiographic prediction of binge-eating episodes. Using intensive EMA data collected over 14 days from 13 female participants (12 with bulimia nervosa and 1 with binge-eating disorder), the model leveraged real-time self-reports to forecast imminent binge-eating events, demonstrating the feasibility of personalised, app-based risk prediction [41]. For each participant, BISCUIT selected a small, idiographic subset of predictors capturing contextual and affective states and used them to predict binge-eating episodes in the subsequent 2.5 h, achieving, on average, an AUC of 0.80 with specificity around 0.87 and sensitivity around 0.79.

Mobile apps

Despite these research advances, evaluations of publicly available eating-disorder and mindful-eating apps reveal a substantial evidence-practice gap. In a 2024 mixed-methods study, Guluzade and Sas evaluated 27 top-rated free mobile applications targeting mindful eating and eating disorders, combining structured content analysis with qualitative assessment of 1,248 user reviews. The app’s functionality was examined across various domains, including self-tracking, intervention strategies, usability, and ethical considerations. Most apps prioritised tracking features, such as eating behaviour, emotions, hunger and satiety cues, symptoms, physical activity, sleep, weight, and nutrient intake while offering limited evidence-based therapeutic support. The findings highlight substantial gaps in current ED-focused apps and underscore the need for rigorously designed, personalised digital tools to improve engagement, adherence, and clinical outcomes, particularly in underserved populations [42].

Responding to this gap, recent conceptual work by Linardon, Torous, and colleagues [43] has articulated a framework for AI-enabled smartphone applications that integrate passive signals (for example, accelerometry, GPS, mobility, sleep, social communication, and physiological data) with active self-report for eating-disorder assessment, monitoring, and treatment. They argue that modelling both active and passive inputs at the individual level could yield highly tailored digital phenotypes, capable of detecting early warning signs of deterioration and adapting intervention content in real time. Realising this vision will require rigorous validation of algorithms across diverse clinical populations, careful attention to user experience and ethics, and robust data governance structures. Collectively, these studies position digital apps and wearables as central components of the next generation of scalable, precision-focused eating-disorder care.

NLP and knowledge graphs

Benítez-Andrades and colleagues built on contextual transformers (BERT) by enriching tweet representations with Wikidata knowledge graph embeddings, creating hybrid contextual embeddings that combined linguistic content with structured semantic relations among entities, such as disorders, symptoms, and body-image concepts. This AI approach used NLP to analyse text data, while knowledge graphs organised related concepts to improve contextual understanding [44, 45]. On a manually labelled dataset of 2,000 eating-disorder-related tweets, transformer models demonstrated higher classification performance than classical machine learning and earlier deep-learning baselines. This work demonstrates that augmenting language models with external domain knowledge can sharpen the discrimination of nuanced eating-disorder-related discourse, particularly where short posts provide limited context.

Transfer learning

Transfer learning studies extend this paradigm to non-English and under-resourced languages. Pilar López-Úbeda, Flor Miriam Plaza-del-Arco et al.'s 2021 study pioneers transfer learning for Spanish anorexia detection on 5,707 Tweets, benchmarking Transformers (Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers–BERT/Cross-lingual Language Model–XLM) against LSTM/Bidirectional Long–Short-Term Memory (BiLSTM)/ Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) using word embeddings. Language-specific models, such as the Spanish BERT (BETO), achieving a 94.13% F1-score, outperformed multilingual alternatives, suggesting the importance of linguistic and cultural specificity in eating-disorder detection [46].

Topic modelling

In a 2020 study, Hwang, Kim, and colleagues applied topic modelling, a method that identifies common themes in large text data by grouping frequently occurring words, to understand emotional eating patterns at scale across 185,950 posts and 3.5 million comments from the Reddit community r/loseit to characterise emotional eating (EE) behaviours. Using Latent Dirichlet Allocation (LDA), the authors identified four dominant EE themes: EE1 (defeat and disgust), EE2 (perceived physical changes), EE3 (diet-related queries), and EE4 (coping strategies, including extreme dietary approaches, such as ketogenic diets). An accompanying Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD) classifier achieved high predictive performance (accuracy = 90.64%) in identifying EE-related content. Overall, the models demonstrated over 90% accuracy in detecting emotional eating behaviours, highlighting the potential of large-scale social-media analytics for behavioural health and eating-disorder research [47]. These findings show how unsupervised topic models can reveal behavioural and support patterns that might inform the design of personalised digital interventions for emotional eating.

Recurrent neural network (RNN) with gated recurrent units (GRU–RNN) with NLP

Stella Mąckowska and Bartosz Kościel’s (2024) study applied a GRU–RNN to 250 Polish-language body-image narratives to distinguish healthy individuals from those with anorexia nervosa (AN). The model achieved an error rate of 18–31%. AN narratives showed more past-tense verbs and negative adjectives, while irony-detection errors ranged from 3% to 15% [48].

Heuristic NLP

Yaakov Hacohen-Kerner and Natan Manor’s 2022 study investigated automated classification of anorexia-related content using 200 Hebrew-language blog posts, labelled as anorexic or non-anorexic. The authors employed a hybrid heuristic framework integrating n-gram features, orthographic patterns, and psycholinguistic indicators. A “k-out-of-n” feature fusion strategy combined with a random forest classifier (372 features) achieved the highest performance, with an accuracy of 90.63%. By comparison, traditional RF–TF–IDF baselines achieved 79.75% accuracy, while HeBERT (Hebrew BERT) reached 77.5%, outperforming multilingual deep-learning models. This work shows that, in low-resource settings, carefully designed language features combined with traditional machine learning can perform as well as, and in some cases better than, complex neural models for detecting anorexia-related content [49].

AI/ML redefines the role of questionnaires in eating-disorder research

AI/ML models trained on questionnaire-based data combine standardised psychological and behavioural assessments with machine-learning to improve the prediction of eating-disorder risk and outcomes. Studies using validated instruments such as the Eating-Disorder Examination (EDE), the EDE-Questionnaire (EDE–QS), and validated measures (Beck Depression Inventory (BDI) and Body Uneasiness Test demonstrated improved predictive performance compared with traditional statistical approaches. Our analysis of AI and machine-learning applications reveals a diverse array of technologies that identify early risk factors for eating disorders across physiological, behavioural, psychosocial, and linguistic domains (Supplementary 2, Fig. 4). Logistic regression and ML-based systems analyse genetic markers, vital signs, and blood tests to detect deterioration signals, such as low BMI, glucose fluctuations, and hypoglycemia, while CNNs, rule-based systems, unsupervised learning, and facial landmark tracking monitor behavioural patterns, such as meal timing, utensil use, bite frequency, and erratic eating. Decision trees and ANN models pinpoint key psychosocial risks, including body dissatisfaction, emotional eating, and body shape concerns, complemented by NLP techniques such as RNNs, LDA, and BERT that uncover negative linguistic patterns, emotional eating topics, and disordered content on social media. Discussion

AI methods in eating disorders can be distinguished between three levels of evidence. First, technical performance, which refers to how well a model performs in a controlled setting, is measured by metrics, such as AUC, F1-score, or accuracy. Second, experimental feasibility means whether the system can actually work in real-world or everyday conditions. Finally, clinical readiness refers to whether the system has been properly tested in real patients, reviewed by experts, and integrated into clinical practice. We observed that across the 23 studies reviewed, NLP and sensor-based AI/ML models demonstrated better technical performance, with accuracies of > 97% (BERT/KG hybrid and RABiD sensor models), mmWave sensing systems reaching 97.5% accuracy, and RABiD sensor models reporting F1-scores of 0.948. Thus, transformer-based NLP models showed high accuracy in detecting disordered eating patterns from social-media text, outperforming traditional machine-learning approaches (Table 2). Whereas wearables and mobile-based systems showed strong predictive capability, such as the Apple Watch (AUC = 0.951) and Empatica E4 (accuracy = 91%), these systems raised concerns about user burden, privacy, and long-term adherence. Sensor-based and wearable systems were especially useful for capturing detailed, real-time patterns that precede or occur during disordered eating behaviours [39]. In comparison, traditional ML systems (ML–EWS models) achieved moderate predictive performance, with AUC values up to 0.980; however, they are limited in generalizability. Questionnaire-based AI/ML models demonstrated comparatively lowest performance (AUC = 0.88–0.93; sensitivity = 0.88; specificity = 0.85) (Table 7). However, combining psychometric questionnaires with ML may still support early detection and treatment [51].

Most studies should still be considered proof-of-concept rather than clinically ready systems. High AUC values do not necessarily indicate real-world usefulness, as false positives may still occur. High false-positive rates may also create unnecessary psychological stress and increase healthcare burden, particularly in vulnerable populations. Reporting only the AUC can mask important problems, because even a model with an AUC of 0.90 may still produce false positives, depending on the cutoff used and the prevalence. Studies such as Rashid et al. and Linardon et al. [40, 43] addressed experimental feasibility rather than predictive performance, and their findings are not directly comparable to classification metrics. Before clinical use, AI systems must meet frameworks such as the FDA Software as a Medical Device (SaMD) guidelines or CDSCO Medical Device Rules in India [53–55]. Therefore, even the highest-performing models identified in this review should currently be considered research tools rather than ready-to-use clinical systems.

An important consideration across AI-based predictive models is that the concept of "early identification" varies depending on the prediction timeframe and intended clinical application. Some models focus on short-term behavioural prediction using passive physiological monitoring, for example, in a study by Ralph-Nearman et al. [39]. In contrast, other AI/ML models are designed for longer-term risk stratification, identifying individuals at elevated risk weeks, months, or even years before clinical onset based on psychological, behavioural, genetic, or social-media-derived indicators. These two approaches differ fundamentally in their clinical applicability, infrastructure requirements, and preventive intentions. The former focuses on preventing upcoming episodes in individuals who have already been diagnosed, and the latter enables population-level pre-onset screening in at-risk groups. This distinction is particularly important in resource-limited settings, such as India, where wearable-monitoring infrastructure remains limited but scalable screening tools have significant potential.

The current landscape of eating-disorder assessment and diagnosis in India and other developing countries remains highly limited by poor recognition, inadequate infrastructure, and sociocultural barriers. Eating disorders are still viewed as uncommon Western conditions. This often leads to delayed diagnosis and poor help-seeking behaviour [15, 56]. Recent Indian evidence indicates a rising burden of eating disorders: a 2025 study among 860 adolescents in Chennai found that 24.7% screened positive for high eating-disorder risk, while earlier Indian research among adolescent girls also reported substantial body-image concerns and disordered eating cognitions [5, 57]; another study from urban Tamil Nadu reported a 23.1% prevalence of eating disorders among 350 participants [58]. Positioning our findings of the increasing shift in eating-disorder risk, a narrative review published in 2024 revealed a rising trend in eating-disorder in India with adolescent age, higher economic status, family history of mental illness, female sex and personality identification crisis [59]. Despite this growing burden, AI-based interventions or educational tools remain limited in India. Existing screening tools, such as the EDE-Q and EAT-26, are not regularly validated across multilingual and culturally diverse populations [60]. Furthermore, a lack of psychiatrists, clinical psychologists, and qualified eating-disorder specialists further limits timely assessment. In this context, AI-driven technologies may help identify significant diagnostic gaps by enabling scalable, affordable, and remotely accessible screening tools. Smartphone-based screening tools that integrate validated questionnaires with ML classifiers, such as the EDE–QS-based decision tree models identified in this review [50], can be modified into low-cost mobile applications for schools, primary care, community settings or resource-limited settings. NLP models that can analyse multilingual social-media content, wearable-based behavioural monitoring, and smartphone-integrated ecological assessment tools could help identify vulnerable people earlier [43, 46, 48]. In such a way, scalable, AI-powered screening systems could help reduce major diagnostic gaps in developing countries like India.

The applicability of NLP findings to non-Western, linguistically diverse settings, particularly in India, warrants specific consideration [15]. Using NLP to detect eating disorders in India can be challenging, because many people may hide their emotional distress due to social stigma. Unlike in Western countries, where people may openly discuss eating-disorder symptoms online, individuals in India may describe their experiences indirectly through cultural expressions, metaphors, physical complaints, or religious practices, such as fasting [60]. For example, loss of appetite framed as desire, not illness, body shame framed through superstitious purity concepts, physical complaints described as purging or restriction framed as digestive disease or malnutrition described as ‘always tired’. Religious practices used as a cover for fasting, spiritual purity or delaying/avoiding meals under the frame of ritual obligation [61, 62]. This can make it difficult for standard NLP models to identify disordered eating behaviours accurately. Another challenge is the frequent mixing of English with regional languages, such as Hindi, Tamil, or Bengali, which can reduce the accuracy of AI models like BERT, which are mainly trained on English-language data [63]. Therefore, NLP-based detection systems in India need to be culturally adapted to understand local expressions, multilingual communication, and culturally specific meanings. Without these adaptations, these models may fail to identify vulnerable individuals, creating an important health equity concern [64].

Emotional dynamics and disordered cognitions are an important aspect of ED aetiology [65]. Several NLP/text- and EMA-based mobile systems demonstrated strong capability to capture these dimensions. For example, topic modelling, which worked on Reddit posts to detect dominant themes of emotional eating, and GRU-based RNNs trained on body-image narratives in Polish differentiated individuals with anorexia nervosa from controls, partially by detecting increased use of past-tense verbs and negative adjectives [47, 48]. Similarly, EMA-based mobile apps collected real-time data on cravings, guilt, and negative emotions, which were then used in machine-learning models to predict binge-eating episodes (Table 4). We also observed that questionnaire-based AL/ML models highlighted emotional variables, such as emotional eating scores, body-image inflexibility, distress, and weight/shape dissatisfaction as among the most important predictors of eating-disorder risk and outcomes (Table 6). Overall, NLP- and EMA-based systems directly encode affective language, momentary mood, and cognitive behaviour into risk-prediction models. In addition, most systems predict outcomes based on symptoms, self-reported emotions, BMI, online language, and device-captured physiology, but very few models integrate family dynamics, environmental stressors, peer relations, economic constraints, and healthcare access [66].

A systematic evaluation indicates repeated and interconnected limitations that hamper the generalizability of the reported findings. Small sample sizes and overfitting represent the most prevalent concerns. Studies such as Presseller et al. (n = 30) [18], Ioannidis et al. (n = 36) [34], Tufano et al. (n = 15) [37] and Ralph-Nearman et al. (n = 6) [39] trained and evaluated models on samples too small, especially for complex methods, such as deep learning and random forests. In small sample sizes, the models learn noise specific to the training set rather than the true results, affecting the performance metrics. This was demonstrated by a study by Forrest et al. [30], which showed that random forests with optimism-corrected bootstrapping yielded AUCs of 0.93–0.94, whereas unbiased evaluation yielded AUCs of only 0.49–0.73. Another important limitation was the lack of external validation. Most studies also lacked external validation, limiting confidence in real-world applicability [67]. Class imbalance is another overlooked issue. For instance, eating-disorders’ positive cases are comparatively smaller than negative cases. Thus, if this is not addressed, the imbalance can bias models towards predicting the majority class. Only one study in this review by Ren et al. [50] specifically addressed the class imbalance using the Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique (SMOTE). Further demographic bias limits the generalizability of the current models. Many studies have used highly selected, homogeneous samples, such as predominantly White, well-educated participants in Western clinical trials [30, 32] or university students in single-country settings [29, 50]. Similarly, studies based on Turkish or Malaysian cohorts may not generalise to other cultural settings [29, 52]. On similar lines, dataset shifts are rarely discussed in the reviews, but they are equally important. Dataset shift, where real-world data differs from training data, was also rarely examined [68]. Explainability also remains an important gap in the clinical context of machine learning. Few studies in this review utilised a black-box framework, including deep neural networks, random forests, and transformer-based NLP models, without providing clinically relevant explanations of model outputs [29, 60]. In eating disorders, clinician trust and ethical transparency are essential, underscoring the importance of explainability for implementation [69]. In such scenarios, modern explainable AI (XAI) offers several practical benefits. Methods such as SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) can help quantify the contribution of each input feature to a model’s prediction—for example, an increase in the electrodermal activity, a specific linguistic marker in social-media text, or an unusual body language while rating, which can flag a risk of eating disorders. Another XAI method is LIME (Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations). This method can be useful for understanding how ML models make predictions based on different associated factors. For example, LIME can be used to identify which factors most influence the model’s decision, such as eating behaviours, emotional language, body-image issues, social-media behaviour, or wearable sensor data. Similarly, attention visualisation in transformer models (e.g., BERT) helps identify the words or phrases driving predictions, such as language related to body image, food restriction, or vomiting behaviours [69]. Such explainable AI systems provide clinically actionable insights that support, rather than replace, clinician judgement [70].

Big data integration in eating-disorder research faces several challenges, including inconsistent diagnostic criteria across countries, incompatible data formats from EHRs, wearables, genomics, and social media, and difficulties in analysing multilingual and culturally complex data using NLP models. Limited infrastructure, computational resources, and strict international privacy regulations further restrict data sharing and reduce model generalizability. At the same time, although AI-based systems and passive sensing tools can provide useful behavioural and physiological insights, patients’ emotions, thoughts, body-image concerns, and lived experiences remain essential for diagnosis and treatment. Therefore, AI should support, not replace, specialist clinical assessment and psychological evaluation.

Strength and limits

This study provides a comprehensive synthesis of AI/ML-based studies across diverse modalities, including sensor-based systems and NLP, and maps emerging trends for ED early detection while highlighting critical gaps, such as limited Indian validation. The review is based on a structured PICOT framework and multiple databases. It provides a thematic distribution of studies that offer valuable context for future research. The review also highlights important contextual gaps in developing countries such as India, where cultural diversity and social stigma influence both diagnosis and help-seeking behaviours. In addition, the interdisciplinary perspective used in this study enhances its relevance across clinical nutrition, mental health, and digital health domains. A key strength of this work lies in its integration of nutritional perspectives with AI-based approaches. However, certain limitations must be acknowledged. As a narrative review, the study may be subject to selection bias and limited reproducibility. Heterogeneity in study designs and populations restricts comparability, while the predominance of high-income country data limits generalizability. Besides, limited focus was given to gender-related factors across the included studies. Most of the models did not report results distinctly for males and females, or consider the known differences in how eating disorders affect both genders separately. In addition, we limited our review to studies published in English as a deliberate methodological choice. However, this may have excluded relevant research in other languages, which could reduce how well the findings apply to non-English-speaking populations. In addition, the small sample sizes and limited external validation in primary studies further constrain the findings. Furthermore, because the search terms focused mainly on machine-learning and AI methods, the high prevalence of ML models in the review should be understood as partially a result of the search strategy, rather than as an exact estimate of their overall use in the field.

Future research

Future research should explore how AI-based systems, with explainable AI, can assist clinicians rather than replace them in the assessment and treatment of eating disorders. From a human–computer interaction perspective, the most practical and responsible approach is for AI and human experts to work together, with AI models helping analyse large amounts of behavioural, physiological, linguistic, and digital data to identify early risk patterns that may be difficult to detect through routine clinical assessment alone, while clinicians use these outputs as decision-support information to guide their final clinical judgement and diagnosis. Considering the empathic nature of eating-disorder care, having human involvement and interaction remains an essential component in the care system. This is known as a human-in-the-loop (HITL) framework [71]. This also means that AI results should always be understood alongside the patient’s own experiences, cultural background, and personal goals, as these cannot be fully understood from sensor data or language patterns alone. International guidelines for eating-disorder AI datasets and reporting standards may help improve reproducibility, transparency, and reliability across studies [72]. However, AI systems are not automatically free from bias. If the training datasets on which the prediction model depends come mainly from a defined population, AI may reproduce the same disparities when making decisions. In addition, these models are trained on human-derived data, which may encode existing societal biases. Therefore, effective detection of eating disorders requires the use of diverse, representative data to reduce bias. Researchers should also follow strict ethical and privacy protocols, similar to those proposed by Bentan et al., to ensure responsible use of healthcare data and minimise psychological harm [73]. Thus, AI can be a promising tool, alongside clinicians, for early prediction and identification of eating disorders and for preventing further complications.

What is already known on this subject?

AI and machine learning have advanced early detection of eating disorders through tools, such as wearable sensor tracking, natural language processing analysing social media for linguistic risk markers, and predictive models integrating multimodal data in Western populations, thereby potentially reducing the self-report biases inherent in traditional screening.

What does this study add?

This study synthesises AI-based approaches for early detection of eating disorders, highlighting the potential of multimodal systems for improved screening. It also identifies key gaps, including limited cultural representation, insufficient validation across diverse populations, and the need to incorporate social and peer-related factors.

Conclusion

This narrative review provides early-stage translational evidence suggesting the potential use of AI and machine learning in early eating-disorder detection, leveraging diverse modalities such as wearables (e.g., Empatica E4), sensors (e.g., mmEat), and NLP (e.g., BERT–LSTM) to complement traditional clinical assessment, including self-report and interview-based approaches, which are still essential for understanding the patient’s personal experiences and clinical condition. While traditional ML models demonstrate heterogeneous performance (AUCs of 0.49–0.98), sensor-based systems have shown stronger performance in controlled settings. However, persistent challenges include small Western-centric sample sizes, risks of overfitting, cultural biases, and limited interpretability. AI is widely used in developed countries; however, its application remains limited in developing nations, such as India, where cultural diversity and social stigma often influence the recognition and reporting of eating disorders. In such contexts, multimodal systems integrating linguistic patterns, behavioural data, physiological signals, and digital footprints offer a promising yet early-stage approach to improving early identification and screening. These findings suggest that AIs may have the capacity for scalable, objective risk stratification among vulnerable urban youth and highlight the need for future large-scale longitudinal studies integrating multimodal data repositories, explainable AI frameworks, ethical safeguards, and clinician-co-designed pilots to enable precision screening and timely interventions in real-world settings. A key point is that most of the systems reviewed in the study are still in the early stages of development and have mainly been tested in small or limited research settings. Therefore, more studies in larger populations are needed before its routine application in the real world. The clinical application of these tools should mainly be used for early screening and identifying individuals who may need further evaluation. However, proper diagnosis and treatment planning still require a detailed clinical assessment and a detailed discussion with a trained healthcare professional.
