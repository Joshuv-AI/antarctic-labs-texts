Quantifying care, qualifying experiences: a systematic review of measurement-based care in psychiatry from patient and provider perspectives
Authors: Ayan Dey, Ze’ev Lewis, Josh Posel, Rachel Yunqiu Pan, Karen Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMeasurement based care (MBC) is a patient-centered approach that is gaining popularity in healthcare systems, particularly in mental health settings. However, attitudes towards MBC vary among mental health clinicians and patients, leading to variable implementation.ObjectiveThis systematic review synthesises clinician and patient perspectives on the benefits and drawbacks of measurement-based care (MBC) in psychiatry.Study selection and analysisWe searched Ovid MEDLINE, EMBASE, EBM Reviews, APA PsychINFO and CINAHL databases from inception to January 2024. After screening 1644 titles and abstracts, 48 full papers were reviewed, and 24 studies were ultimately included. Quality assessment was conducted using the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool, and key patterns were extracted using thematic analysis.FindingsThe review reflects opinions of 901 patients and 2831 clinicians across various settings. Patients valued MBC for enhancing communication, self-awareness and reducing stigma. However, they expressed concerns about the adequacy of measures in reflecting their clinical state and uncertainty about how responses influence treatment decisions. Clinicians appreciated MBC for improving patient involvement, tracking treatment response and enhancing communication efficiency. Concerns included inadequate capture of clinical complexity, potential reporting biases, time constraints, insufficient training and concerns with respect to data usage and privacy.Conclusions and clinical implicationsWhile patients and clinicians recognise significant benefits, including enhanced communication, improved insight and more structured clinical decision-making, they also identify important limitations. These include concerns about the adequacy of scales to capture complex clinical presentations, potential impacts on the therapeutic alliance and increased administrative burden. Moving forward, successful integration of MBC into routine care will require addressing these challenges through improved clinician training, clear guidelines for interpretation, greater transparency with respect to how data will be used and more seamless integration with existing clinical workflows.PROSPERO registration numberPROSPERO CRD420250651562.Keywords: PSYCHIATRY, Depression & mood disorders, Anxiety disorders

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

This systematic review synthesises perspectives from 901 patients and 2831 clinicians, gathered from 24 studies, on the use of MBC in psychiatric practice across various settings. It reveals shared appreciation for improved communication, treatment monitoring and patient self-awareness. However, it also highlights concerns about the adequacy of measures in capturing clinical complexity, potential negative impacts on the therapeutic relationship, concerns with respect to the use of data for performance monitoring and implementation challenges.

The findings emphasise the need for developing more comprehensive and adaptive assessment tools and improving clinician training in MBC implementation and interpretation. Introduction

Measurement-based care (MBC) is an evidence-based practice that involves two key processes: systematic collection of patient data using validated instruments, such as symptom rating scales, and the application and sharing of these data to guide clinical decision-making.1 2 This approach enables clinicians to monitor symptoms and functioning longitudinally, facilitates collaborative treatment planning and supports shared decision-making with patients.1 MBC has gained prominence in response to demands for patient-centred care, particularly in psychiatry and clinical psychology, where it supports standardisation and enhances provider communication.2,4 When integrated with clinical judgement, MBC can improve treatment efficacy by detecting early signs of poor response or subtle signs of clinical deterioration that may otherwise be missed.5 It can also improve efficiency by helping clinicians identify problem areas for targeted symptom management.1 Furthermore, by actively engaging patients in the monitoring of their own progress, MBC fosters greater patient involvement and empowerment by allowing for more opportunity for shared decision-making.1 6 This, in turn, can enhance treatment adherence and improve outcomes.6

The rationale for this systematic review stems from the discrepancy between the empirical support for MBC, demonstrated best in populations with depression and/or outpatient treatment settings and its limited adoption in clinical practice. Despite evidence that MBC improves mental health treatment efficacy, efficiency and adherence,7 less than 20% of behavioural health practitioners use it in practice.8 This gap between research and implementation necessitates inquiry into barriers to its adoption. Accordingly, this review aims to systematically examine existing literature on patient and clinician perspectives towards MBC, explore current clinical use, assess perceptions of MBC’s impact on patient experience and engagement and investigate potential unintended negative effects. By addressing these objectives, this study aims to bridge the gap between evidence and practice and to identify strategies to increase MBC implementation in both outpatient and inpatient mental health settings.

Methods

The study protocol is available at PROSPERO (CRD420250651562). Findings are presented according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) guidelines.9 The manuscript title was amended from the time that the protocol was registered.

Selection criteria

The published literature was searched for quantitative randomised controlled trials, quasiexperimental studies, cross-sectional and pre–post study designs, qualitative and mixed-method studies. Case reports, commentaries and review articles were excluded from the studies. The eligibility criteria are provided in table 1.

Search strategy

We developed a comprehensive search strategy to identify relevant journal articles on MBC in mental health, with a focus on patient and clinician perspectives. The following databases were searched from inception to January 2024: Ovid MEDLINE, EMBASE, EBM Reviews—Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials, APA PsychINFO and CINAHL (via EBSCO). Specifically, the search combined three main concepts: (1) mental health including medical subject heading (MeSH) terms and keywords for psychiatry, mental disorders, anxiety disorders, bipolar disorder and mood disorders; (2) measurement-based care, encompassing terms related to routine outcome monitoring, patient-reported measures and feedback-informed treatment in mental health settings; and (3) patient and clinician perspectives, incorporating MeSH terms and keywords to capture viewpoints and attitudes of both patients and healthcare providers. These concepts were combined using Boolean operators, with results limited to English language and human studies. Although the initial search had no publication year limit, only studies published from 2000 onward were included. Studies involving mental healthcare in both primary and specialist settings were eligible. One additional study was identified through citation searching. The full search strategy is described in the online supplemental index 1.

Study selection

The search results were uploaded to the systematic review management software Covidence to remove duplicates. Two reviewers (AD and KW) independently screened titles and abstracts against inclusion/exclusion criteria. Any uncertainties were resolved through discussion, with doubtful cases being considered for full-text review. Full texts were then independently reviewed by at least two reviewers (AD, KW, JP, ZL and RYP), with inter-rater agreement ranging from 0.77 to 1. Disagreements were resolved by discussion or, if needed, by majority vote among the five reviewers.

Data extraction

All five reviewers independently extracted data from included studies using a standardised form in Microsoft Excel, capturing study details, populations, methodology and descriptions of patient and provider perspectives on MBC. AD reviewed all extraction tables, and discrepancies were resolved by discussion or, if necessary, consultation with a third reviewer.

Quality assessment

The same reviewers assessed study quality using the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool.10 Studies were rated as ‘high’, ‘medium’ or ‘low’ quality based on the number of criteria met. For quantitative, qualitative and cross-sectional studies, if a study met all five criteria, it was classified as ‘high quality’. Whereas studies that met three to four criteria were classified as ‘medium quality’ and studies that met only one or two criteria were classified as ‘low quality’. For mixed-method studies, the lowest component score determined the overall rating. Disagreements were resolved through discussion with an independent third reviewer.

Data analysis

Due to heterogeneity in settings, populations, interventions and outcome measures, a meta-analysis was not feasible. Instead, a narrative synthesis was conducted, allowing for more flexibility in organising findings from extracted heterogeneous data. The robustness of the synthesis was assessed by considering the quality, relevance and potential biases. Individual findings from studies were merged into themes through consensus among reviewers.

Results

Study selection

The findings of the search strategy are summarised in figure 1 as a PRISMA flow chart.9 11 After removal of duplicates, a total of 1433 titles and abstracts were screened. From these 48 full papers, 24 papers were included in the final review. The references of included studies were reviewed for potential studies and one new study was included. A total of 24 articles were excluded for the following reasons: unrelated to MBC (n=14), not a full paper (n=4), editorial/commentary (n=2), review paper (n=1), non-mental health related (n=1) and study focused on screening rather than patient/clinician perspectives (n=2).

Characteristics of included studies

The designs of included studies were mixed methods (n=5), observational (n=8) and qualitative (n=11). Cumulatively across studies, this survey reflects the opinions and attitudes of a total of 901 service users/patients and 2831 clinicians across various professions (psychiatrists, psychologists, therapists and nurses). Practice settings also varied across studies; mixed (n=1), outpatient (n=20) and inpatient (n=3). Characteristics of the included studies are reported in online supplemental table S1.

Quality appraisal

Of the 24 studies included, 13 were judged to be of ‘medium quality’, 9 were judged to be of ‘high quality’ and only two were judged as ‘low quality’—indicating that this review primarily most reflects the results of well-designed studies. This is also reported in online supplemental table S1.

Patient attitudes towards the benefits of MBC

Patient-reported benefits of MBC are broadly observed across both outpatient and inpatient settings and among diverse populations, including youth, adults and older adults (see online supplemental table S2 for full details). The most commonly reported benefits include enhanced communication with providers, as MBC offers a valuable avenue for patients to express experiences that may be difficult to articulate verbally, especially among those with mood, psychotic and personality disorders.12,19 This is particularly valued by some patients with borderline personality disorder (BPD), who report that their therapists gain a better understanding of them through MBC.16 Notably, contrary to some clinician concerns, many older adults and their carers are enthusiastic about electronic MBC platforms and report regular computer use.18 Patients also highlight that MBC improves insight into their own mental health conditions—a benefit noted across age groups, settings and diagnostic categories.12,17 At least one study found that MBC reduced stigma and facilitated broaching sensitive topics,17 such as communicating concerns about sexual dysfunction to providers or discussing illness experiences with loved ones.16 In outpatient settings, including primary care and specialty clinics, patients appreciate the efficiency of MBC, particularly when administered electronically or in waiting rooms and report that it helps focus assessments on relevant symptoms.17 MBC is also described as fostering optimism and engagement,14 16 especially when feedback is shared, and is generally perceived as quick and easy to use.18

Patient-reported limitations of MBC

Despite these benefits, patients also report several limitations of MBC that are consistent across settings and age groups, though certain themes are more prominent in specific populations. The most frequently cited limitation is that standard MBC scales and questionnaires do not adequately capture the complexity of patients’ clinical states or experiences, particularly in cases involving comorbidities or atypical presentations.1214,17 20 For example, one study reported that less than a quarter of patient respondents were satisfied with the accuracy of the Quick Inventory of Depressive Symptomatology questionnaire,22 while others noted concerns with the wording of certain questionnaires that can negatively interfere with interpretation.15 20 21 23 Another frequent concern is uncertainty about how questionnaire responses and scores will affect overall treatment.12 17 20 23 Some patients admit to altering or obfuscating their answers to avoid changes in care, reflecting insecurity about disclosing their symptoms and concerns about confidentiality and who can access their scores.12 23 Other limitations include the tediousness of repeated questionnaire completion, concerns about straining patient–provider relations,17 and frustration when MBC results are not discussed or used in care planning.16 18 There are also concerns that completing MBC questionnaires may be upsetting for some, triggering distress in the absence of a therapist, particularly among those with trauma histories or BPD.16 Some patients also worry that targeted questionnaires may be suggestive for those with unstable personality structures.16 Lastly, patients reported dissatisfaction with completing non-adaptive questionnaires throughout their treatment.12 16 17 Non-adaptive (static) scales use a fixed set of questions for all patients at each assessment, regardless of individual progress or symptom change.24 In contrast, adaptive scales tailor questions to the individual’s current health status and previous responses.24 The lack of adaptation in static scales was perceived by some patients as repetitive, contributing to disengagement and dissatisfaction with MBC.

Clinician perspectives towards the benefits of MBC

Clinician perspectives mirror many of the findings from patients, with reported benefits spanning outpatient and inpatient settings and both generalist and specialist contexts. Key benefits include the belief that MBC facilitates shared decision-making and enhances patient engagement across diagnostic groups, including depressive, anxiety, psychotic and substance use disorders.12,1518 23 25 MBC is valued for its role in tracking treatment response in a standardised manner, with applicability across the lifespan and diagnostic criteria.1215 16 26 29,32 Clinicians report that MBC improves the focus and efficiency of consultative interviews, particularly when self-administered scales are completed in advance.12 14 18 23 27 29 31 Clinicians spoke positively of the potential for the integration of MBC into clinical documentation as a time-saving measure.18 This was a stance also shared by primary care clinicians.30 Many clinicians also found that MBC helps facilitate efficient interclinician communication by quantifying disease severity in a standardised manner (eg, a Yale-Brown Obsessive-Compulsive Scale Score above 24 indicating severe obsessive–compulsive disorder).1213 27,29 Beyond tracking treatment effects, some clinicians believe that such scales help increase objectivity and inform treatment decisions (eg, distinguishing between mild vs moderate disease in the context of limited functional impairment).1618 26 29,35 This can be particularly helpful for less experienced clinicians or general practitioners who may not be as familiar with diagnostic criteria.15 18 28 Clinicians, especially generalists, also highlight the role of MBC in supporting quality improvement initiatives and early detection of clinical deterioration.30

Clinician perspectives towards limitations of MBC

These benefits are balanced by several common limitations. The most frequently reported limitation is that MBC scales do not adequately capture complexity, particularly in patients with atypical presentations or multiple comorbid conditions.15 16 18 21 23 26 27 29 31 32 36 37 The second most reported limitation is concern about biases in patient reporting, due to both conscious and unconscious influences such as the desire to please the clinician and/or show progress.15 16 18 21 23 26 27 29 32 These concerns are noted across diagnostic groups and settings, especially in populations with severe illness or poor insight. Time constraints and increased administrative workload are cited as barriers, particularly in under-resourced or high-volume clinics,12 13 16 25 27 31 32 37 and are exacerbated when scales are difficult to review due to lack of electronic medical record integration.1221 25 30,32 37 Some clinicians feel that the increased administrative burden associated with MBC should be compensated18 33 and report frustration related to logistical issues of data capture and storage.29 31 32 37 Concerns about workflow and technological barriers are echoed by clinicians in community outpatient primary care.30 Some clinicians also express worries that MBC can result in depersonalisation of interactions and negatively impact the therapeutic alliance, a concern noted across clinicians working with patients with mood, anxiety and personality disorders.16 18 21 31 36 Limited training and unclear guidance on interpreting MBC results are also reported as barriers, especially among generalists and less experienced clinicians.1326 30,32 Concerns about data use for performance evaluation or administrative purposes are particularly salient in large systems and among clinicians serving vulnerable populations.16 33 Notably, while concern around data use is a strongly endorsed barrier, it does not correlate with decreased use of MBC; rather, the greatest decrease in use is associated with perceived low clinical utility and administrative burden.33 Finally, there are concerns that MBC is not suitable for all patients, such as those with cognitive difficulties, illiteracy, poor insight or limited English proficiency.18 20 29

Discussion

The implementation of MBC in mental health services has garnered significant attention in recent years. Patients and clinicians alike acknowledge the potential of MBC to transform the therapeutic landscape, although with reservations and highlighted limitations that warrant careful consideration. This discussion aims to distill actionable insights for MBC researchers, clinicians and clinical leaders focused on implementation and sustainment, while also addressing the needs of patients, families and care partners involved in MBC co-design projects.

From the patient’s perspective, in both inpatient and outpatient settings, the benefits of MBC are notably centred around enhancing communication with healthcare providers and fostering a deeper understanding of their own mental health conditions. The ability for patients to express their experiences through standardised scales and questionnaires facilitates a more open dialogue with clinicians and empowers patients by granting them insight into the nuances of their mental health.1215,17 This dual advantage underscores the potential of MBC to bridge the communication gap between patients and providers, thereby promoting a more collaborative approach to care. The efficiency and ease of using MBC, including via electronic platforms, further facilitate communication and collaboration in a time-saving manner.18 29 38

However, the discussion of benefits would be incomplete without addressing the limitations highlighted by patients. The primary concern revolves around the adequacy of MBC tools in capturing the full spectrum of the patient’s clinical state, particularly in populations with comorbidities or atypical presentations.1214 15 17 20,22 Some patients expressed discomfort with the suggestiveness of scales targeting a single condition, while others doubted the accuracy of non-adaptive scales that do not evolve alongside their condition.16 This gap between patient experiences and the quantitative assessments offered by MBC scales raises questions about the comprehensiveness and sensitivity of existing assessment tools. Furthermore, patients often express uncertainty about how their questionnaire responses will influence clinical decision-making, highlighting the importance of transparent communication about the role of MBC in care planning and treatment adjustments.12 16 22 29 Best practices for communicating the role of MBC in clinical decision-making emphasise the importance of transparency, shared interpretation and collaborative use of patient-reported data. Effective communication should include providing patients with a clear rationale for MBC at the outset, discussing how data will be used in shared decision-making to inform treatment planning and ensuring that feedback is reviewed collaboratively with patients during clinical encounters.24 39 Such practices are recommended not only to enhance patient engagement and understanding but also to address concerns about confidentiality, promote trust and reinforce the value of MBC as a tool for ongoing individualised treatment.

Clinician perspectives offer a professional counterpoint to patient views, emphasising the utility of MBC in enhancing patient involvement, tracking treatment response and streamlining clinical communication.12,1518 23 25 There is relative consensus among clinicians regarding the value of MBC in objectifying mental health assessments and informing treatment adjustments, reflecting a broader trend towards evidence-based practices in healthcare. Yet, clinicians are also cognisant of the limitations, particularly concerning the complexity of mental health conditions that may not be fully captured by standardised scales.1517 18 21,23 25 27 28 33 38 Additionally, concerns regarding patient reporting biases and the potential for MBC to encroach on the therapeutic alliance further underscore the nuanced challenges inherent in integrating MBC into routine care.15 17 18 22 23 25 28 38 Concerns around a lack of compensation for what is perceived as an additional burden33 38 and fear of the potential for MBC data to become a metric for performance evaluation,16 33 help highlight initial clinician apprehension towards MBC implementation.

With respect to target clinical populations, while the evidence for benefits of MBC is strongest among adult clinical populations with mood, anxiety and psychotic disorders, there is also emerging but limited evidence supporting the utility of MBC among youth and older adults.40,42 The positive reception of MBC among older adults and their carers (particularly in the context of electronic platform usage) challenges preconceived notions about the technological adeptness of this demographic.29 The limitations of MBC are most pronounced in clinical populations with complex presentations (eg, severe mood disorders, BPD, psychosis and comorbid substance use), cognitive impairment and those with language or literacy barriers. There are also concerns related to the suggestibility of some measurement tools for patients with unstable personality disorders that may limit their utility in this patient population.16

Moving forward

In synthesizing these perspectives, it becomes evident that while MBC holds considerable promise in enhancing mental healthcare, its successful implementation hinges on addressing the outlined limitations. We propose three guiding principles for MBC implementation in mental healthcare: standardisation, transparency and simplification. This aligns with frameworks and guidelines such as the recent ‘APA Professional Practice Guidelines on Measurement-Based Care’ and the Institute for Healthcare Improvement’s innovation report on ‘Advancing Measure-Informed Care in Mental Health’.43 44

To address concerns about capturing clinical complexity and lack of adaptability, the development of more nuanced and comprehensive assessment tools should be prioritised. Transdiagnostic tools such as the Kessler Psychological Distress Scale (K10/K6) and Computerized Adaptive Testing (CAT) systems are promising options,45 46 particularly for patients with comorbidities or atypical and/or complex presentations. CAT systems use item response theory (IRT), a modern measurement approach that models how individuals respond to specific questionnaire items based on their underlying level of a trait (eg, depression or anxiety).46 This allows for assessments that are both more precise and adaptive, as IRT enables the selection of the most relevant questions for each individual, reducing redundancy and enhancing the clinical relevance of symptom monitoring in MBC.47

Clinician training and guidelines are critical to successful implementation. Existing curricula provide frameworks for teaching clinicians to interpret results, communicate findings and integrate feedback into shared decision-making.47 48 Best practices for effective communication suggest providing patients with clear rationale for MBC at the outset, discussing how data will be used in shared decision-making to inform treatment planning and ensuring that feedback is reviewed collaboratively. These practices help address concerns about confidentiality, promote trust and reinforce the value of MBC as a tool for ongoing individualised care.49 At last, simplification through leveraging technology will be essential to avoid exacerbating existing administrative burdens. Automation via electronic medical record integration and adoption of adaptive scales can streamline data collection and tracking.44 46 For instance, CAT systems can reduce question redundancy by up to 50% compared with static measures, improving feasibility without sacrificing accuracy.19 50 These strategies, combined with sensitivity to diverse patient and clinician needs, can help ensure that standardised assessments complement, rather than eclipse, the individualised nature of mental healthcare

Strengths and limitations

Strengths of this review include the large number of studies screened, quality appraisal, inclusion of mostly moderate-quality to high-quality studies, coverage of age groups from children and youth23 25 to older adults29 and the comparison of patient and clinician perspectives on MBC. Limitations include the exclusion of grey literature and non-English articles, as well as substantial heterogeneity between studies, which limited analysis to a narrative format. Additionally, most studies excluded patients with communication difficulties or significant cognitive impairment, who may have the greatest challenges completing MBC independently.

Conclusions and clinical implications

In conclusion, while MBC holds significant promise to enhance mental healthcare by improving patient engagement, clinical efficiency and decision-making, its full potential remains constrained by challenges related to individualisation, integration and transparency. This review underscores the importance of prioritising MBC implementation in populations and settings with the strongest evidence of benefit, alongside developing tailored strategies for patients with complex presentations. Achieving meaningful impact will require improved training, seamless integration into healthcare systems and a collaborative effort to refine MBC tools and practices. Ultimately, the path forward lies in ensuring that MBC supports, rather than compromises, the quality and personalisation of mental health services.
