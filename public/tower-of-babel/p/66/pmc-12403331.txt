Deciphering metabolic regulation in overripe grapes through multi-omics analysis of non-targeted metabolome, proteome, and transcriptome
Authors: Ning Shi, Jing Cheng, Xiao-Tong Gao, Hao-Cheng Lu, Meng-Bo Tian, Ming-Yu Li, Chang-Qing Duan, Jun Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAs climate warms, the sugars and secondary metabolites in grapes gradually become uncoupled during development, resulting in the phenomenon of overripe fruit becoming more frequent around the world, especially in some arid regions. However, the key metabolic regulations to grape overripening are still poorly understood. To address this, we conducted a multi-omics study on Cabernet Sauvignon overripe berries over two years, analyzing the non-targeted metabolome, transcriptome, and proteome, aiming to provide a theoretical basis for delayed harvest strategies based on grape quality.ResultsThe delayed harvests caused significant changes in berry transcriptome and proteome. Genes and proteins responding to environmental changes were up-regulated, while those related to metabolism were down-regulated, indicating the primary function of berries during the overripening stage shifted away from extensive metabolite synthesis. The key enzyme genes and proteins linked to changes in metabolites like glucose, pyruvate, malate, alanine, γ-aminobutyric acid, and resveratrol, were identified.ConclusionsIn hot regions, the overripening stage of grape berries may be detrimental to their quality. This study highlights the core molecular changes during grape overripening and offer valuable insights for optimizing grape harvest date.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07235-w.Keywords: Overripening, Multi-omics analysis, Transcriptome, Proteome

Background

Grapes are non-climacteric fruits, meaning their development and ripening occur gradually and continuously without any clear involvement of the plant hormone ethylene, involving various rates of metabolite synthesis, degradation, and transport. This leads to changes in metabolite content as the grapes mature [1]. These metabolites significantly influence wine flavor [2], making the selection of the optimal ripeness for harvesting crucial to the quality and style of the resulting wine. Traditionally, grape sugar-acidity balance has been closely linked to wine quality, with total soluble solids (TSS) being a key marker for determining grape ripeness and the ideal harvest time [3]. However, TSS alone is no longer sufficient to describe berry ripening due to the effects of global warming, which allows grapes to accumulate sugars more easily while secondary metabolites, essential for flavor, lag behind [4]. Moreover, the growing demand for diverse wine styles means that standard ripeness levels may not meet all production needs, leading winemakers to consider additional factors when determining the “optimal ripeness” of grapes. As a result, alternative ripeness indicators, such as phenolic ripeness [5] and aromatic ripeness [6] have been introduced. In addition, many wineries and production areas grow large volumes of grapes, often of the same variety. Due to the vast planting areas and the limited processing capacity of wineries, grapes cannot always be harvested quickly, leading to unintended overripening.

The overripening can trigger various physiological, biochemical, structural, and compositional changes in the grape berries. During the overripening stage, the grape skin cells gradually lose vitality, and the berries experience water loss, a process also known as dehydration [7]. Dehydration has a significant impact on berry metabolism and physiology. Firstly, it concentrates sugars and flavor compounds in grape berries due to the water loss, which can notably affect the quality and organoleptic properties of the subsequent wines [8]. Secondly, dehydration leads to water stress in the berries, resulting in numerous biochemical and molecular changes. Cirilli et al. [9] found that water loss alters the transcription of genes regulating volatile compound accumulation in grape berries. Zenoni et al. [10] identified a core set of genes in dehydrating, which regulate ethylene and auxin metabolism, as well as oxidative and osmotic stress responses, defense mechanisms, cell walls and carbohydrate metabolism. An experiment on late-season dehydration of grapes conducted in Australia showed that the onset of berry cell death after the berry was dehydrated, and the volatile compounds of the grapes and wines was significantly changed [11]. This information suggests that the grapes start senescing gradually at some point in the late stages of ripening. As a result, understanding how overripening affects grape and wine flavor profiles has become a focus of interest.

Previously, we have revealed the targeted metabolome changes of grape berries during the overripening stage and their impact on wine flavor profiles [8]. The results showed that as the grape overripened, dehydration of the grape was observed, the concentration of anthocyanins increased, and the levels of hexanal and 2-hexanal necessarily decreased significantly. Costantini et al. [12] applied an integrative functional genomics approach based on the targeted metabolic (free and bound terpenoid compounds) and genome-wide transcript profiling of Moscato Bianco ripening berries. Ghan et al. [13] conducted RNA-seq analysis on the skins of seven grape varieties, including Cabernet Sauvignon, Merlot, and Pinot Noir, etc. The results showed that the transcript abundance of most photosynthetic genes decreased with the increase of sugar levels in berries, while the transcript abundance of other processes such as nucleic acid metabolism, chromosome organization, and lipid catabolism increased. In recent years, omics-based research has been conducted to clarify the regulation of metabolites synthesis in plants, such as tree peony [14] and Opisthopappus taihangensis [15]. To better understand the molecular and physiological processes involved in the late stages of berry ripening, building on previous work by Gao et al. [8], this study attempts to deepen our understanding of mechanisms involved in overripening by analyzing the non-targeted metabolome, transcriptome, and proteome of grapes, revealing the metabolic changes during the overripening stage. These multi-omics analyses aim to provide a theoretical foundation for delayed harvest strategies based on grape quality.

Materials and methods

Vineyard, experimental design and sampling

The plant materials (Vitis vinifera L. cv. Cabernet Sauvignon) used in this study were collected from a commercial vineyard of CITIC NIYA Wine CO., LTD. located in Manas County, Xinjiang (44°17′ North, 86°12′ East). The vines were planted in 2010 using a modified vertical shoot positioned system (M-VSP) [16]. The row orientation was the east–west direction with a row and vine spacing of 2.9 m × 0.8 m. In this experiment, grapes were harvested at three ripeness levels each year (Supplementary Fig. 1). Due to the typical semiarid climate in this region, wine grapes ripen rapidly under such climatic conditions. Local producers usually harvest the grapes when their sugar content reaches 23 ~ 24°Brix. Therefore, the first ripeness level (R1) was at the time of commercial harvest [September 7th, 2015 (106 days after anthesis) and September 10th, 2016 (101 days after anthesis] when the total soluble solids of berries reached 23°Brix with 1.13 g berry weight and 60.2% juice yield. The second ripeness level (R2 was when the total soluble solids of berries reached about 25°Brix with 1.02 g berry weight and 58.82% juice yield [September 16th, 2015 (115 days after anthesis) and September 21st, 2016 (112 days after anthesis)]. The third ripeness level (R3) was 27°Brix with 0.95 g berry weight and 57.41% juice yield [September 25th, 2015 (124 days after anthesis) and September 30th, 2016 (121 days after anthesis)]. The detailed physicochemical parameters of these grape samples have been published in our previous study [8]. A randomized block design was used, with each ripeness level consisting of three replicates, and each replicate consisting of a block including 30 vines. To prevent changes in sink-source relationship from affecting subsequent harvests, each block was harvested only once. For each replicate, 250 grape berries were randomly sampled from both sides of 30 vines. Grape samples were stored in a − 40 °C refrigerator for subsequent detection and analysis.

Determination of grape δ13C values

Fifty grape berries were ground in liquid nitrogen and then oven-dried at 80 °C for at least 48 h until reaching a constant weight. The dried sample was sieved through a 100-mesh screen, and 0.1 mg of the powder was placed in a small tin cup for combustion at high temperatures in a Thermo Flash EA-HT elemental analyzer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The δ13C values of the berries were determined using a Thermo DELTA V Advantage mass spectrometer, which measured the 13C to 12C ratio of the resulting CO2 and compared it to the international standard Pee Dee Belemnite. Typically, a δ13C value higher than -26‰ indicates the presence of water stress, while values above − 21.5‰ suggest severe water stress [17].

UPLC-QTOF-MS-based untargeted metabolomics analysis

Samples preparation and untargeted metabolomics analysis were performed with the assistance of Shenzhen BGI Genomics Co., Ltd. Fifty grapes of each replicate were crushed and deseeded under liquid nitrogen. 25 mg of sample was accurately weighed and ground into powder, 800 µL of pre-cooled precipitant (methanol: acetonitrile: purified water = 2:2:1) was added and sonicated on ice for 10 min. Then all samples were placed in a − 20 °C refrigerator for 20 min. Samples were centrifuged at 4 °C, 25,000 × g for 15 min to take the supernatant. The extraction was repeated once for the remaining residue. The supernatants obtained from the two extractions were combined and then lyophilized in a freeze-drying machine. Finally, the samples were reconstituted by 600 μL of 10% methanol aqueous, sonicated on ice for 10 min and then centrifuged at 4 °C, 25,000 × g for 15 min. The final supernatant obtained was used for untargeted metabolomics analysis. According to Want et al. [18], the quality control (QC) samples were produced by pooling aliquots of all extracted samples to be analyzed.

The untargeted metabolites were separated using an ultra performance liquid chromatography (UPLC) system (Waters, UK). An ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 column (100 mm × 2.1 mm, 1.8 μm, Waters, UK) was used for the reversed phase separation. The column oven was maintained at 50 °C. The flow rate was 0.4 mL/min and the mobile phase consisted of solvent A (water + 0.1% formic acid) and solvent B (acetonitrile + 0.1% formic acid). Gradient elution conditions were set as follows: 0 ~ 2 min, 100% phase A; 2 ~ 11 min, 0% to 100% B; 11 ~ 13 min, 100% B; 13 ~ 15 min, 0–100% A. The injection volume for each sample was 10 μL. A high-resolution tandem mass spectrometer Xevo G2 XS QTOF (Waters, UK) was used to detect metabolites eluted form the column. The Q-TOF was operated in both positive and negative ion modes. For positive ion mode, the capillary and sampling cone voltages were set at 3.0 kV and 40.0 V, respectively. For negative ion mode, the capillary and sampling cone voltages were set at 2.0 kV and 40.0 V, respectively. The mass spectrometry data were acquired in Centroid MSE mode. The TOF mass range was from 50 to 1200 Da and the scan time was 0.2 s. For the MS/MS detection, all precursors were fragmented using 20–40 eV, and the scan time was 0.2 s. The mass spectrometry data analysis was performed using the Progenesis QI software (version 2.2, Waters, MA, USA) and metaX package, and the annotated metabolites were mapped to the KEGG pathway database (meta__vvi_Release_92.0).

iTRAQ-based proteomic analysis

Samples preparation and proteomic analysis were performed with the assistance of Testing Center of Beijing Protein Innovation Co., Ltd. Fifty grapes of each replicate were deseeded and ground into powder under liquid nitrogen. 2 g of sample was weighed and 5 mL of Buffer A (50 mM Tris, 2 mM EDTA, 100 mM KCl, 700 mM sucrose and pH 8.0) was added. The mixture was sonicated at 4 °C for 15 min, Buffer B (Tris-saturated phenol) was added and the supernatant (the phenolic phase) was aspirated by centrifugation after 3 min of shaking. ​​Next, add​​ Buffer A to the tubes and ​​repeat​​ the above steps once. 8 mL of Buffer C (0.1 M sodium acetate–methanol solution) was added to the tubes and precipitated for 4 h. The supernatant was removed by centrifugation and the precipitate continued to be washed with Buffer C twice. Then the precipitate was washed twice with acetone and centrifuged to remove the supernatant. The resulting precipitate was resuspended in lysis buffer (8 M urea, 30 mM HEPES, 1 mM PMSF, 2 mM EDTA, 10 mM DTT) and sonicated for 5 min. The final supernatant was collected by centrifugation at 20,000 × g for 30 min, and DTT was added to a final concentration of 10 mM. The solution was heated in water at 56 °C for 1 h. After removal, IAM was quickly added to a final concentration of 55 mM and kept aside from light for 1 h. The protein quantitation was performed using the Bradford method [19].

100 μg of protein from each sample was desalted by ultrafiltration with triethylammonium bicarbonate buffer, digested with 3.3 μg of trypsin per 100 μg of protein at 37 °C for 24 h, and then labeled according to the instructions supplied with the iTRAQ (isobaric tags for relative and absolute quantitation) Reagents-8PLEX Multiplex Kit (SCIEX). Samples R1, R2, and R3 from 2015 were labeled with 113, 114, and 115 iTRAQ tags, respectively. Samples R1, R2, and R3 from 2016 were labeled with 116, 117, and 118 iTRAQ tags, respectively. Then the iTRAQ-labeled samples were isolated and purified using an Agilent 1200 series HPLC system. Finally, the desalted peptide mixture was detected using a Q Exactive mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The raw data was analyzed using Proteome Discoverer software (version 1.4; Thermo Scientific). The Cabernet Sauvignon grape protein library (http://cantulab.github.io/data.html) was selected for the annotation of proteomics data. The specific procedures for isolation and purification, MS parameters and data analysis were described by Hong et al. [20]. The differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) were determined by a fold change (FC) threshold of > 1.3 and p value < 0.05. The DEPs were annotated using Gene Ontology (GO) and the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG). The raw data of proteome has been deposited into the iProx database with the accession number IPX0012523000.

Transcriptome sequencing

For each replicate, A total of 50 randomly selected grape berries were deseeded in liquid nitrogen and ground to powder with 1 g of PVPP. The total RNA from 100 mg grape powder was extracted using the Spectrum™ Plant Total RNA Kit (Sigma-Aldrich, St Louis, MO, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The RNA concentration and quality were detected using Implen Nano Photometer P330 (Munich, Germany) and agarose gel electrophoresis. The RNA libraries were subsequently constructed and then sequenced using an Illumina HiSeq™ 4000 (Illumina, Inc., San Diego, CA, United States) to produce 150 bp paired-end reads. Raw reads were quality-checked using FastQC software, and adapters and low-quality bases were trimmed using SOAPnuke software. Using Bowtie, align the reads to the reference gene sequences under analysis. Then, use HISAT2 to align the reads to the Cabernet Sauvignon grape reference genome from the GreenPhyl database (https://www.greenphyl.org) [21]. Next, employ StringTie to reconstruct and sort the aligned data. Then, apply Cufflinks to integrate transcript data from all berry samples and merge these with the reference gene sequences. Use Cufflinks’ Cuffmerge tool to combine transcripts from all samples. For newly discovered transcripts, only retain those that lie within intergenic regions of the reference or within introns of annotated genes. Gene expression levels were normalized by determining the fragments per kilobase of transcript per million fragments mapped (FPKM) using RSEM software. Genes with an average FPKM > 1 in samples from at least one harvest date per year were defined as expressed genes. A total of 24,217 expressed genes were analyzed for subsequent analyses. The R package ‘DESeq2’ was used to identify differential expressed genes (DEGs) based on a log2 fold change (FC) threshold of > 2.0 and a false discovery rate (FDR) of ≤ 0.05. KEGG categories were performed to annotate DEGs using the R package ‘Clusterprofile’. The raw RNA sequencing data generated in this study have been deposited in the NCBI Sequence Read Archive (SRA) under the BioProject accession number PRJNA1285413 (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/bioproject/PRJNA1285413).

Quantitative real-time PCR

Quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR) was conducted on 12 randomly selected DEGs to verify the accuracy of the sequencing data. Total RNA was extracted using the same method and reverse transcribed using HiScript II Q RT SuperMix for qPCR Kits (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). The RT-qPCR system and procedure were described in detail by Sun et al. [22]. The relative expression of genes was calculated based on the expression of reference genes. VviUbiquitin1 was used as the reference, and the primer sequences used were shown in Supplementary Table 1.

Statistical analysis

The error bars depicted on graphs denote the standard error of the mean value. The SPSS 26.0 (IBM, USA) was used for statistical analyses of data at p < 0.05 (Duncan’s multiple range test). GraphPad Prism 8.0.1 was used for graphical representations. The R-3.6.1 was used for bioinformatics analyses and mapping. Time-series clustering was performed using fuzzy C-means (FCM) algorithm via the ‘mFuzz’ package. Visualization included heatmap generation with ‘pheatmap’.

and supplementary plots constructed using ‘ggplot2’. Functional enrichment analysis for GO and KEGG pathways was executed with ‘clusterProfiler’ under adjusted p-value < 0.05 significance threshold. Differential expression analysis between berry developmental stages was carried out using ‘DESeq2’.

Results

Water status of grape berries

The δ13C values of the grape samples at different ripeness levels are shown in Supplementary Table 2. In 2015, the grape berries did not experience water stress, and the δ13C value rose in R2 berries, indicating a temporary decrease in water content, but returned to normal in R3 berries. However, in 2016, the δ13C values were significantly higher than in 2015 and increased progressively with the delayed harvest. The δ13C of overripe berries (R2 berries and R3 berries) in 2016 remained below − 26‰, indicating that the grapevines were subjected to water stress during the overripening stage.

Metabolomic profiles of grape berries

The principal component analysis (PCA) of grape samples and quality control (QC) samples showed that the QC samples were well clustered in both positive and negative ion modes (Supplementary Fig. 2a, b), indicating that the untargeted metabolic data was highly reproducible and reliable. Based on the QC samples, we removed low-quality data and ion peaks with coefficients of variation greater than 30% from the raw data, and 1,021 and 530 metabolites in positive ion mode and negative ion mode were finally identified, respectively (Supplementary Table 3). Next, we log-transformed the untargeted metabolite data from grape samples of different maturities, based on which PCA was carried out as shown in Fig. 1a, b. The score plot of PCA showed that PC1 and PC2 distinguished grape samples according to vintages and ripeness levels, indicating that vintage and ripeness level had a significant effect on the metabolomic profiles of grape berries. Furtherly, the orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) found 264 and 165 differentially expressed metabolites (DEMs, VIP value > 1.0) between commercially harvested berries and overripe berries in positive ion mode and negative ion mode, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 3a, b). These metabolites were mainly involved in sugar metabolism, amino acid metabolism and secondary metabolism (Supplementary Table 3 and Fig. 1c).

Proteomic characteristic of grape berries

A total of 1270 proteins detected in all berry samples. We performed a fuzzy c-means time series analysis to classify these proteins into five Clusters according to the variation trend (Supplementary Fig. 4a), and proteins with membership > 0.5 in each Cluster were used as core proteins for KEGG enrichment analysis (Supplementray Fig. 4b). The abundance of protein related to metabolic pathways and biosynthesis of secondary metabolites in overripe berries was down-regulated, especially the proteins related to sugar metabolism, fatty acid metabolism and amino acid metabolism. Next, we selected differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) in grape berries at the overripening stage, using commercially harvested grapes as a control. A total of 345 and 457 DEPs were identified in 2015 and 2016, respectively. As the harvest date was delayed, the number of DEPs between overripe berries and commercially harvested berries increased, and the number of up-regulated DEPs in over-ripe berries was greater than that of down-regulated DEPs (Fig. 2a). Notably, 197 core DEPs were identified in two years (Fig. 2b). GO enrichment of these core DEPs was mainly involved in signal transduction, photosynthesis and chloroplast correlation, and protein structure changes (Supplementary Fig. 5). These core DEPs were further classified according to functional annotations and analyzed for their relative expression (Fig. 2c). The abundance of DEPs related to gene expression, protein processing and degradation, and stress response was up-regulated in overripe berries. Photosynthesis related protein, Rab signaling protein, ADP-ribosomal factor and aquaporin showed plasticity during overripening period.

Transcriptomic characteristic of grape berries

The transcriptomic offline data, filtering data and alignment statistics results are shown in Supplementary Table 4. The linear regression analysis between the expressions assessed by RT-qPCR and RNA sequencing validated that expression profiles obtained by RNA sequencing were reliable (Supplementary Fig. 6). The principal component analysis (PCA) based on the FPKM of expressed genes showed the three biological replicates of each treatment were mostly clustered together in PCA, suggesting a strong correlation between the replicates (Supplementary Fig. 7). PC1 and PC2 revealed variance in vintages and ripeness levels of grape samples. It is worth noting that for the distribution in the PC1 direction, the R1 treatments were always located on the leftmost side, followed by R3, while the R2 treatments were always located on the rightmost side. This indicated that the genetic changes are not simply a linear rise or fall during the over-ripening stage of the grape berries. The transcriptome-based PCA underscored the plasticity of harvest date on grape transcriptomic characteristics. Fuzzy c-means time series analysis (Supplementary Fig. 8a) and KEGG enrichment analysis based on genes with membership > 0.5 in each Cluster (Supplementary Fig. 8b) showed that genes related to RNA, pentose, and amino acids metabolism were up-regulated, while genes related to sugar decomposition and carbon metabolism were down-regulated in the overripening stage. In addition, genes related to flavonoid and fatty acid metabolism were significantly regulated among different ripeness levels. The differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in overripe grape berries were selected using commercially harvested grapes as a control (Fig. 3a). With the delay of the harvest date, the number of DEGs in overripe berries increased, and the number of up-regulated genes was greater than that of down-regulated genes. Furthermore, 2986 core DEGs were identified as showing differences in two years (Fig. 3b). The KEGG enrichment analysis showed that the functions of core DEGs mainly involved RNA transport, RNA degradation, spliceosome, plant hormone signal transduction, protein processing in the endoplasmic reticulum, MAKP signal pathway-plant and the metabolism of sugar, acid and amino acid (Fig. 3c).

Dynamics of grape sugar metabolism during the overripening stage

As mentioned above, most core DEMs were mainly involved in the metabolic pathways of sugar, amino acid and secondary metabolites. Herein, we integrated the metabolome, proteome, and transcriptome data to reveal the performance of sugar metabolism, amino acid metabolism, and secondary metabolism during the overripening stage. The DEMs, DEPs, and DEGs involved in sucrose inversion and glycolysis pathway during the overripening stage are shown in Fig. 4. During the overripening stage, the abundance of D-glucose (12.44_179.0547 m/z) was up-regulated, while the abundances of D-glucose-1-phosphate (5.05_259.0231 m/z) and UDP-glucose (5.35_566.0505n) showed a tendency to up-regulated and then down-regulated (except for glucose-1-phosphate in 2015). At proteomic and transcriptomic levels, overripe berries significantly up-regulated the expressions of 5 β-fructofuranosidase genes (g390650, g589680, g350940, g589650, and g130750) and protein (g551810.m01) in sucrose inversion pathway. In terms of glycolysis pathway, the expressions of 1 VviGAL (g471410), 1 VviHK (g326110), and 1 VviPK (g234370) were down-regulated in overripe berries, and conversely, 1 VviENO (g235450) and 2 VviPK (g122800 and g382840) were up-regulated.

After the glycolysis pathway, pyruvate enters the TCA cycle by generating acetyl CoA in the presence of the pyruvate dehydrogenase (Supplementary Fig. 9). R2 significantly up-regulated the abundance of oxalosuccinate (0.48_208.0458 m/z), while R2 and R3 significantly down-regulated the 2-oxoglutarate (1.04_145.0132 m/z) abundance. The abundance of succinate (1.77_117.0182 m/z) was significantly down-regulated in overripe berries. Regarding the changes in the transcriptome and proteome during the overripening stage, the expressions of 1 VviACO (g043510), 1 VviIDH3 (g353110), 1 VviIDH1 (g525290), 1 VviFUM (g068690), 1 VviMDH (g520160) and 1 VviCS (g388890) were up-regulated in the overripening stage, while the expressions of 1 VviACO (g123090) and 1 succinyl-CoA ligase gene (g174360) were down-regulated. In addition, the expressions of 2 VviMDH (g114580.m01 and g124030.m01) were up-regulated in overripe berries.

Dynamics of grape amino acid metabolism during the overripening stage

Glutamate is an important intermediate in amino acid metabolism, and the dynamics of glutamate-centered amino acid metabolism during the overripening stage is shown in Fig. 5. Two ions (0.63_163.0447 m/z and 0.63_147.05298n) were identified as glutamate, which were down-regulated in overripe berries. The abundance of 4-aminobutanoate (0.63_103.0634 m/z) was significantly up-regulated in overripe berries in 2015, while L-glutamine (0.62_145.0608 m/z) was significantly down-regulated in overripe berries in 2016. Two ions 0.58_173.1036 m/z and 4.92_213.0757 m/z annotated as arginine were identified, the abundance of 0.58_173.1036 m/z was up-regulated and then down-regulated during the overripening stage of 2015, whereas it was down-regulated during the overripening stage of 2016. The trend of ornithine (0.58_131.0816 m/z) was consistent with arginine (0.58_173.1036 m/z). Two ions (0.60_132.0292 m/z and 0.62_116.0344 m/z) annotated as aspartate were identified, and the abundance of 0.60.132.0292 m/z was down-regulated and then up-regulated at the overripening stage. At the proteomic level, the glutamate decarboxylase protein (g559380.m02) was significantly down-regulated in overripe berries, except for R2 berries in 2015. The glutamine synthetase protein (g190380.m01) and prolyl 4-hydroxylase protein (g001030.m04) were significantly up-regulated in R2 berries in 2016 and 2015, respectively. The alanine transaminase protein (g232540.m03) was significantly down-regulated in overripe berries in 2016, and the VviAAT (g358430.m04) was significantly down-regulated in R3 berries in 2016. At the transcriptomic level, the glutamate dehydrogenase genes, glutamate decarboxylase gene, glutamine synthetase gene, proline dehydrogenase genes, prolyl 4-hydroxylase genes, VviAAT genes, and asparagine synthase genes showed significant differences in the overripening stage. Among them, 2 genes of glutamate dehydrogenase (g487110 and g267500) were significantly up-regulated in R3 berries in 2015, and the glutamate decarboxylase gene (g000530) was significantly up-regulated in overripe berries in 2015. The proline dehydrogenase genes (g401310 and g137760) were significantly up-regulated during the overripening stage in two years. The glutamine synthetase gene (g518410) and 3 prolyl 4-hydroxylase genes (g487770, g293780, and g416840) were significantly up-regulated and down-regulated in R3 berries in 2016, respectively. The VviAAT genes g176090 and g274490 were significantly up-regulated and down-regulated in R3 berries in 2016, respectively. The asparagine synthase gene g188320 was significantly down-regulated in R2 berries in 2016, and g468600 was up-regulated in R2 berries in 2015.

Dynamics of grape aromatics metabolism during the overripening stage

The lipoxygenase pathway (Supplementary Fig. 10) is associated with the synthesis of C6/C9 aroma compounds in berries and consists mainly of enzymes such as VviLOX, VviHPL, and VviADH. There was a significant differential expression of 7 VviLOX genes in overripe berries. During the overripening stage in 2015, g355420 was significantly up-regulated in R2 berries, while g096070 showed significant up-regulation in overripe berries. In contrast, g121680 and g381440 were significantly down-regulated in overripe berries, and g501400 was significantly down-regulated in R3 berries. In 2016, at the overripening stage, all 6 VviLOX genes, except for g355420, were significantly down-regulated in R3 berries. The expression of the VviHPL gene and protein followed a consistent trend during the overripening stage, with both being significantly down-regulated in R3 berries in 2016. For VviADH, the expression of g320300, g073740, g320280, and g073710 was significantly up-regulated in overripe berries in 2015. In 2016, g046070, g052380, g052390, and g046060 were significantly down-regulated in R3 berries, while g288310 was significantly up-regulated in R3 berries in the same year. The abundance of VviADH protein was significantly down-regulated in overripe berries in 2016. Regarding the metabolites associated with these enzymes, the abundance of 13(S)-HPOT (7.67_309.2055 m/z) was significantly down-regulated during the overripening stage in 2015. Similarly, the abundance of 3-hexenal (0.68_81.0694 m/z) was significantly down-regulated in R3 berries in 2016, aligning with the expressions of VviHPL and VviHPL.

The terpene synthesis pathway (Supplementary Fig. 11) comprises the MVA pathway and the MEP pathway, which produce the terpene precursors IPP (isopentenyl diphosphate) and DMAPP (dimethylallyl diphosphate). In the MEP pathway, the expression of VviDXS was significantly down-regulated in R3 berries in 2015, while the expression of VviMCS showed significant down-regulation in R3 berries in 2016. In the MVA pathway, there were 3 VviHMGCR genes that were significantly differently expressed during the overripening stage, with g308990 being significantly up-regulated in overripe berries in 2015, while the g067090 and g202690 were significantly up-regulated and down-regulated in R3 berries in 2016, respectively. The isopentyl pyrophosphate isomerase (IPPI) is able to catalyze the interconversion of DMAPP and IPP, and the expression of the VviIPPI was significantly down-regulated in R2 berries in two years. In addition, the expression of VviGGPS was significantly up-regulated in R3 berries in 2016. For the metabolites, the abundance of (S)-3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA was significantly down-regulated during the overripening stage in two years, while the (R)-5-phosphomevalonate showed significant up-regulation in R2 berries in 2015.

The MEP pathway can also provide precursors for carotenoid metabolism (Fig. 8). Followed by phytoene desaturase (PDS) and ζ-carotene desaturase (ZDS). The expression of VviPDS was significantly down-regulated in R3 berries, while 2 VviZDS genes were significantly down-regulated in R2 berries. Next, the VvicrtISO showed a significant down-regulation in R2 berries in 2015. Discussion

The growth and maturation of grape berries involve intricate processes of metabolite synthesis and conversion, tightly controlled by genes and metabolic pathways [23]. In our previous study, we revealed the effects of overripening on grape targeted metabolomes and wine sensory quality [8]. Although separate histological techniques can provide insights into the physiology of grape berries, multi-omics analysis techniques based on the transcriptome, proteome and metabolome can better elucidate the physiological and biochemical processes of grape berries from different dimensions. In this study, we found some interesting phenomena worth discussing.

Transcriptomic characteristic is plastic at the overripening stage of grapes

High-throughput sequencing technologies are recent and continually evolving techniques that offer an effective tool for studying the molecular mechanisms in grape berry development. Deluc et al. [24] analyzed the transcriptome of Cabernet Sauvignon grapes across seven developmental stages and revealed that around 60% of transcripts varied between at least two stages, indicating a complex array of molecular changes involved in grape ripening. Zamboni et al. [25] found that genes linked to physiological rhythms, RNA splicing, protein hydrolysis, and chromosome organisation are regulated during grape maturation. Ghan et al. [13] discovered that transcriptomic differences in overripe grape skins were primarily associated with processes such as senescence, autophagy, catabolism, physiological rhythms, RNA splicing, protein hydrolysis, and epigenetic regulation. Similarly, in this study, genes related to RNA modification and hydrolysis, protein processing, and other functions in grape berries were significantly regulated during the overripe stage. In addition, we also found that genes related to various metabolic pathways were also significantly regulated. The PCA showed that the transcriptome of berries from different years and different harvest dates could be distinguished (Supplementary Fig. 7). Notably, while the year is typically one of the most significant factors affecting grape development, in this study PC1 distinguished grape samples of different ripeness levels, indicating that ripeness level is the main factor driving changes in the berry transcriptome. Among them, the overripe stage mainly affected the transcriptome of the R1-R2 stage, while having a smaller impact on the transcriptome of the R2-R3 stage. This may be due to the grapes being in the overripening stage. Dal et al. [26] found that the primary factor influencing variations in the berry transcriptome from the green stage to maturation was the year, but the impact of the year decreased as the berries ripened. In addition, the differences in years suggested that the grape transcriptome during the overripening stage was still able to respond to changes in the external environment and had plasticity. The fuzzy c-means time series analysis revealed that the core genes involved in carbon metabolism, glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, photosynthesis, and pyruvate metabolism were down-regulated during the overripening stage, suggesting a reduction in sugar catabolism-related processes in overripe berries. Likewise, previous studies have shown that transcripts of proteins associated with photosynthesis and carbon assimilation are more highly expressed at the green stage and decline as maturation progresses [24, 27]. This may indicate that the decline in these functions begins at veraison stage and continues until the overripe stage. The veraison is a critical stage for the synthesis of secondary metabolites, which can be significantly affected by climatic variations from different years [26]. The KEGG pathways related to secondary metabolism were enriched in Cluster 8 and Cluster 9. Notably, gene expression in these two Clusters was higher in commercial harvest berries in 2016 than in 2015, which may be related to the extremely hot climate in July–August of 2015 [8]. The expression of phytohormone-related genes is believed to play a key role in berry development and ripening. However, in this study, the corresponding KEGG pathways were not enriched, primarily because the expression of these genes was low at the overripening stage, and the changes observed were minor and lacked a consistent pattern. Previous studies have indicated that most phytohormone-related transcripts undergo significant changes during veraison but remain relatively stable throughout the ripening process [24].

Overripening causes dynamic changes in grape proteomic characteristic

The processes of ripening and senescence in grapes involve a large number of physiological and biochemical reactions, and these reactions have staged characters [25]. Previous studies on the proteome of grapes have focused on the developmental stage of berries and have indicated that the proteome differed significantly at different developmental stages, with important proteomic changes occurring mainly at the veraison stage [28, 29]. It has been shown that protein expression in berries remains active after harvesting in a manner similar to the ripening process, with differences occurring in proteins involved in metabolism [30]. In this study, as the harvest date was delayed, the number of DEPs in the overripe berries gradually increased, with a higher number of up-regulated proteins, suggesting that proteins are still predominantly synthesized in the berries during the overripening stage. This was also supported by subsequent analysis of core DEPs. Core DEPs involved in gene expression and protein synthesis were significantly up-regulated in overripe berries, while several heat shock proteins were significantly up-regulated (Fig. 2c). Heat shock proteins belong to a highly conserved family of proteins that play an important role in protein folding, unfolding, and transport in prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells [31]. Meanwhile, core DEPs involved in protein degradation were identified and down-regulated during the overripening stage. The core DEPs involved in photosynthesis and energy showed a significant difference in the regulatory trends at the overripening stage between 2015 and 2016, potentially due to variations in climatic dryness in September of those years [8]. Cramer et al. [32] found that water deficit can up-regulate the proteins linked to photosynthesis and photorespiration. Our result further supports the adaptability of grape photosynthesis and energy transfer in response to water status. In addition, we found consistent trends with endocytosis-associated signaling proteins (e.g., Rab signaling proteins and ADP ribosomal factors) and multiple transporter proteins (e.g., aquaporin), which may also be associated with water stress. However, studies on the mechanisms associated with these proteins in grape berries are limited, and whether these signaling proteins are associated with water stress in the berries remains to be investigated further. In terms of metabolism, the time-series analysis indicated that the abundance of proteins related to carbon metabolism and glycolysis pathway was down-regulated during the grape overripening stage, consistent with the energy release and senescence state of grape berries [33]. In addition, most of the DEPs associated with secondary metabolites showed a down-regulation trend during the overripening stage, implying a slowdown in berry metabolism and energy supply.

Maturity plays an important role in metabolomic profiles of grape

The KEGG analysis revealed that DEMs at the overripening stage were mainly associated with sugar metabolism, amino acid metabolism, and secondary metabolism. Therefore, we focused on these metabolic pathways for multi-omics analyses. Sugars are the primary soluble solids in grape berries, and their development is marked by a significant accumulation of these sugars. Coombe et al. [34] proposed that the rise in sugar concentration in grapes close to harvest is linked to water loss in the berries. Our earlier study similarly demonstrated that the increase in total soluble solids during the overripening stage was associated with a notable decrease in berry weight [8]. The fructofuranosidase correlates with sucrose flow into the grape berries [28]. In the present study, we found that the β-fructofuranosidase genes and protein in berries were significantly up-regulated during the overripening stage, indicating that sucrose from leaves continued to be supplied to overripe berries. β-Fructofuranosidase is associated with the breakdown of sucrose, and the upregulation of β-fructofuranosidase indicates that sucrose entering the berry is actively hydrolyzed to contribute glucose and fructose to the grapes, which indicated that the increase in glucose was not solely due to water loss. In addition, although the abundance of pyruvate did not vary consistently over the two years, it followed the same trend as the pyruvate kinase genes (g240230, g560050) and protein (g153980.m01), suggesting that it may be regulated by these genes and proteins. Malate is primarily synthesized during the early stages of berry development, largely derived from phosphoenolpyruvate, which is catalyzed by phosphoenolpyruvate carboxylase and malate dehydrogenase [35]. However, in this study, this pathway was not regulated during the overripening stage. As the veraison begins, malate levels decline due to its involvement in various metabolisms [36]. In the TCA cycle, two VviMDH proteins were up-regulated during the overripening stage, facilitating the conversion of malate to oxaloacetate, which likely explains the decreased abundance of malate in overripe berries.

The maturity of grapes significantly influences nitrogenous compounds in the berries [37]. Early in berry development, glutamine is the dominant amino acid, which can be converted to glutamate by glutamate synthetase and serves as a precursor for synthesizing other amino acids like arginine and proline [38, 39]. Glutamate, glycine, aspartate, and asparagine reach their highest concentrations when the total soluble solids in the berries are between 10 and 15°Brix but decline as ripening progresses [40]. In contrast, arginine and proline concentrations increase as the berries ripen [38]. In the present study, proline dehydrogenase expression was markedly up-regulated during the overripening stage, facilitating proline catabolism, with its transcript peaking at late stages of grape development [24]. However, the abundance of proline still showed a slight up-regulation trend during the overripening stage, consistent with the earlier findings, suggesting that proline synthesis continues during this stage. γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA) is synthesized from glutamate, and typically increases in response to biotic and abiotic stresses [41]. However, in 2016, the abundance of γ-aminobutyric acid was lower in R3 berries than in commercially harvested berries, and a similar pattern was observed at the overripening stage in 2015. Additionally, glutamate decarboxylase, the enzyme involved in GABA production, was down-regulated during overripening, mirroring the GABA trend. This suggested that GABA changes during the overripening stage may not be linked to dehydration due to overripening. Furthermore, asparagine remained relatively stable during fruit ripening, while arginine was significantly down-regulated in 2016, indicating that amino acid dynamics in overripe berries differ from those in the ripening stage. However, the observed amino acid trends were generally inconsistent with the expression patterns of their related synthetic genes and proteins, necessitating further investigation.

Phenolic compounds play a crucial role in shaping the color and flavor of grapes and wines [42]. During the overripening stage, the abundances of most metabolites in the phenylpropanoid pathway remained relatively stable. Furthermore, the expression trends of genes such as VviPAL, VviC4H, Vvi4CL, VviC3’H, VviCOMT, and VviF5H were largely consistent, suggesting potential coordinated regulation among these genes. This coordinated gene expression may contribute to a steady metabolic rate, helping maintain stable levels of the related metabolites [43]. Stilbene compounds accumulate during ripening and are able to respond to abiotic stresses on grapes [44]. Zenoni et al. [10] found that stilbene accumulation is the major metabolic event in phenylpropanoid metabolism during grape postharvest dehydration. In the present study, resveratrol was up-regulated at the overripening stage with berry water loss, which may be related to the VviSTS genes (g484420 and g199250). In flavonoid metabolism, while the expression trend of VviCHS aligned with gene expressions in the phenylpropanoid pathway, the down-regulation of the VviCHS protein indicates a reduced flow of coumaroyl CoA into the flavonoid pathway, leading to a slower synthesis rate of flavonoids in overripe grapes. This suggests that changes in flavonoid concentrations during the overripening stage are likely driven by concentration effects and degradation rather than new synthesis.

In our previous study, we observed a significant decline in the concentration of 2-hexenal in overripe grapes. The expression patterns of VviLOX and VviHPL during berry development have been shown to align with the accumulation of C6 aldehydes [45]. It has also been proposed that the reduction in C6 aldehydes during berry maturation may be linked to their conversion into C6 alcohols by alcohol dehydrogenases (ADH) [46]. In this study, Two VviLOX genes were down-regulated in overripe berries, but VviHPL and VviADH did not show consistent expression trends across both years. Notably, in 2016, VviLOX, VviHPL, and VviADH were synergistically down-regulated in R3 berries, with corresponding protein levels of VviHPL and VviADH also significantly reduced. This could be related to the severe water stress experienced by R3 grapes in 2016, as Deluc et al. [47] reported that some VviLOX genes are down-regulated in response to water stress during post-veraison berries. Terpenes provide grapes and wines with pleasant floral and fruity aromas [48]. In terpene metabolism, terpene synthases (TPS) produce sesquiterpenes, monoterpenes and triterpenes using farnesyl pyrophosphate (FPP), geranyl pyrophosphate (GPP) and geranylgeranyl pyrophosphate (GGPP) as substrates, respectively. However, during the overripening stage, the expression of most VviTPS genes and proteins was low, with no significant differences observed between the two years. This suggested that terpene metabolism is not actively regulated during the overripening stage. In the carotenoid synthesis pathway, although the upstream genes VviPDS and VviZDS were significantly down-regulated in R3 berries in both years, no consistent trend was observed in the abundance of zeaxanthin. In 2016, the abundance of ABA and ABA glucose ester were up-regulated in R3 berries, but VviNCED did not show a consistent expression pattern. At the same time, CCD, a key enzyme in synthesizing norisoprenoids, and its gene VviCCD were significantly down-regulated in R3 berries in 2016. It is speculated that the down-regulation of VviCCD may have shifted the carotenoid metabolic flux towards the NCED pathway, indirectly leading to the increased abundance of ABA and ABA glucose ester.

Conclusions

In this study, we explored the dynamics of the non-targeted metabolome, transcriptome, and proteome of Cabernet Sauvignon grape berries during the overripening stage. We found that gene expression, protein synthesis, and degradation became more active as the harvest date was delayed. Genes responding to external stimuli were upregulated, and proteins related to photosynthesis, Rab signaling, ADP-ribosomal factor, and aquaporins reacted to berry water loss. However, most genes and proteins related to primary and secondary metabolism showed down-regulation, suggesting a slowdown in material metabolism and energy supply. The multi-omics analysis revealed that sugar accumulation during the overripening stage resulted from both water loss concentration and the action of enzymes like β-fructofuranosidase. Overripe berries also had distinct amino acid profiles compared to commercially harvested ones, though these differences did not consistently align with gene and protein expression. There was strong co-expression of genes related to phenolics metabolism during the overripening stage, but most phenolics did not show significant changes. Most flavonoid metabolism-related genes and proteins were affected by year at the overripening stage. Genes and proteins in the lipoxygenase pathway responded to water stress, while terpene and carotenoid metabolism remained inactive.

In conclusion, unlike developing berries, the core function of overripe berries is no longer the metabolites synthesis. This suggests that delayed harvest strategy may be detrimental to vineyards in arid regions where grapes enter the overripening stage earlier. This study reveals the metabolic dynamics during the overripening stage, and offers valuable insights for grape harvesting strategies. In addition, the relevant genes and proteins screened in this study by multi-omics analysis can be used for the next step of molecular biology function validation.
