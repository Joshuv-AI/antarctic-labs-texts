Comparative efficacy and safety of Ranibizumab and Aflibercept for neovascular age-related macular degeneration: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Xiaoqin Zhao, Yunfeng Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRanibizumab and Aflibercept are widely used anti-VEGF agents for treating neovascular age-related macular degeneration (nAMD). This meta-analysis compares their efficacy, anatomical outcomes, treatment burden, and safety profiles.MethodsA systematic search of PubMed, Cochrane, and Google Scholar yielded 1,734 unique records. After screening and applying eligibility criteria, 11 studies were included. Primary outcomes were changes in best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA), central retinal thickness (CRT), proportion of dry retina, and injection frequency. Subgroup analyses and funnel plots assessed heterogeneity and publication bias.ResultsAflibercept showed a trend toward greater BCVA improvement compared with Ranibizumab, although the difference did not reach statistical significance (MD: 3.87 letters; 95% CI: −0.47 to 8.21; p = 0.07; I2 = 97%). Similarly, CRT reduction tended to favor Aflibercept (MD: −85.35 µm; 95% CI: −178.45 to 7.75; p = 0.09), but with substantial heterogeneity. The proportion of dry retina was significantly higher with Aflibercept (MD: 18.74%; 95% CI: 15.20 to 22.28; p < 0.001), with no heterogeneity (I2 = 0%). Although Aflibercept was associated with fewer injections (MD: −0.91; 95% CI: −1.67 to −0.15; p = 0.02), this finding was accompanied by substantial heterogeneity (I2 = 93%) and should be interpreted cautiously given the variability in study design, patient populations, and treatment regimens across included studies. Moreover, although the reduction in injection frequency was statistically significant, its magnitude was modest and its clinical importance remains uncertain given the high heterogeneity and very low certainty of evidence.ConclusionsBoth agents demonstrated comparable visual efficacy for nAMD. No statistically significant differences were observed for BCVA improvement or CRT reduction, and the available evidence does not establish clear superiority of either treatment. Although differences in dry retina outcomes and injection frequency were observed, these findings should be interpreted cautiously because of substantial heterogeneity, mixed study designs, and predominantly low-certainty evidence. Introduction

Neovascular age-related macular degeneration (nAMD) is a major cause of vision loss in the elderly, severely affecting quality of life and daily functioning [1]. AMD primarily affects individuals over 55 years of age and accounts for approximately 6–9% of global blindness in this population. Among these cases, the neovascular form is responsible for the majority of severe, rapid vision loss, and if left untreated, a substantial proportion of patients progress to legal blindness due to irreversible central retinal. [2] nAMD involves subretinal neovascularization that, if untreated, can cause fluid leakage, retinal damage, and progressive vision loss [3]. Anti-vascular endothelial growth factor (anti-VEGF) therapy has transformed nAMD management by effectively slowing disease progression and enhancing visual outcomes [4, 5]. These agents act by binding key pro-angiogenic ligands, particularly VEGF-A, thereby inhibiting endothelial proliferation, vascular leakage, and neovascular growth; some agents such as aflibercept additionally target VEGF-B and placental growth factor (PlGF), providing broader suppression of angiogenic signaling and potentially enhancing fluid resolution and reducing vascular permeability. [6] Differences in molecular structure confer variable binding affinity and pharmacokinetics, with aflibercept demonstrating higher affinity and longer intraocular VEGF suppression compared with ranibizumab or bevacizumab, which may allow extended dosing intervals. [6] Clinically, anti-VEGF therapies are typically administered via repeated intravitreal injections using fixed, pro re nata, or treat-and-extend regimens to maintain disease control over time [6]. Furthermore, broader ligand blockade, including VEGF-B and PlGF, has been associated with improved anatomical outcomes by promoting more complete fluid resorption and stabilization of vascular permeability [6]. Ranibizumab and Aflibercept are widely used anti-VEGF agents for nAMD treatment [7]. Ranibizumab, the first approved, targets and neutralizes VEGF-A, a central driver of pathological angiogenesis and vascular permeability [8, 9]. Aflibercept, developed later, targets VEGF-A, VEGF-B, and PlGF, potentially enhancing fluid resolution and disease control [10, 11]. Both drugs are delivered by intravitreal injection and require frequent dosing to remain effective [12]. This intensive regimen places a burden on patients and healthcare systems, underscoring the need for strategies that maintain efficacy while reducing treatment demands [13].

Large multicenter RCTs, such as the CATT [14], IVAN [15], and VIEW 1/2 trials [16], have provided valuable insights into the relative efficacy of Ranibizumab and aflibercept, yet their findings are not entirely consistent. The CATT and IVAN trials generally demonstrated comparable improvements in best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) between treatment strategies, whereas the VIEW studies suggested that aflibercept may achieve similar visual outcomes with longer treatment intervals. In addition, several real-world studies have suggested that aflibercept may provide advantages in anatomical outcomes, including greater central retinal thickness (CRT) reduction and a higher proportion of dry retina on optical coherence tomography (OCT), although these findings remain inconsistent across studies [17, 18] indicate that patient populations, dosing regimens, and follow-up durations may influence treatment outcomes.

Despite extensive research, the comparative efficacy and safety of Ranibizumab and Aflibercept remain uncertain [19]. Some studies report similar visual acuity improvements, while others note anatomical differences, including fluid resolution and retinal thickness [20]. Observational evidence suggests Aflibercept may reduce treatment burden through less frequent dosing and fewer injections [21]. However, previous studies have differed substantially in study design, retreatment protocols, follow-up duration, and outcome definitions, making it difficult to determine whether meaningful differences exist in functional outcomes, anatomical response, or treatment burden. In particular, evidence regarding CRT reduction, retinal fluid resolution, dry retina achievement, injection frequency, and safety remains inconsistent. Given these inconsistencies, an integrated synthesis of both randomized and real-world evidence is required to clarify whether clinical differences truly exist between these two agents [22]. Because aflibercept has broader molecular targets and potentially longer VEGF suppression, it is clinically relevant to explore whether these pharmacologic differences translate into improved anatomical control or reduced injection burden without compromising visual outcomes. These inconsistencies highlight the need for a comprehensive evaluation of their clinical performance across diverse studies and patient populations [23].

This systematic review and meta-analysis directly compares Ranibizumab and Aflibercept in nAMD management, evaluating primary outcomes including BCVA, CRT, and dry retina proportion defined by OCT findings, as well as secondary outcomes including injection frequency and safety [14]. Although RCTs provide the highest level of evidence, combining them with high-quality observational studies enables a more comprehensive understanding of therapeutic effectiveness under real-world conditions [24]. Integrating different study designs within a meta-analytical framework is methodologically feasible, provided that advanced statistical approaches-such as hierarchical mixed-effects or meta-regression models-are used to account for heterogeneity in study protocols and patient characteristics [25]. Accordingly, this review aims to synthesize evidence across diverse settings to clarify the currently available evidence regarding functional outcomes, anatomical response, and treatment burden associated with Ranibizumab and Aflibercept and support evidence-based treatment decisions in clinical practice [1].

Methods

This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted in strict accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. This study is registered with PROSPERO under the registration number CRD420251183216.

Search strategy

A comprehensive search was conducted in PubMed, Web of Science, Embase and Cochrane to identify relevant studies published through December 2024. The search terms combined Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and free-text keywords such as: “Ranibizumab” AND “Aflibercept” “Neovascular age-related macular degeneration” OR “nAMD” “Anti-VEGF therapy” OR “intravitreal injections” “Efficacy” AND “safety”. The details of searching strategy were depicted in Table S1. Database-specific search strings, including Boolean operators and MeSH/Emtree terms, are provided in Supplementary Table S1 to ensure reproducibility of the search strategy. No restrictions on publication language were initially applied during the search process. Gray literature and conference abstracts were screened where sufficient data were available. However, unpublished studies and inaccessible full texts may still contribute to potential publication bias.

Eligibility criteria

Population (P): Patients diagnosed with nAMD. Studies including mixed populations were eligible only when data for nAMD patients could be separately extracted.

Intervention (I): Intravitreal injection of Aflibercept.

Comparator (C): Intravitreal injection of Ranibizumab.

Outcomes (O): Primary outcomes included changes in BCVA and CRT. Secondary outcomes included injection frequency, proportion of eyes achieving a dry retina, treatment burden, and incidence of adverse events (e.g., intraocular pressure elevation, ocular inflammation). Dry retina was defined according to OCT-based absence of retinal or subretinal fluid when reported.

Study Design (S): Randomized controlled trials, cohort studies, or case-control studies with a minimum follow-up of six months. Only comparative head-to-head studies directly evaluating Ranibizumab versus Aflibercept were included.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion and exclusion criteria were predefined to ensure the selection of relevant studies.

Inclusion criteria

Eligible studies included those directly comparing Ranibizumab and Aflibercept for nAMD treatment; designed as randomized controlled trials, cohort studies, or case-control studies; reporting at least one primary outcome, such as BCVA or CRT; and with a minimum follow-up of six months. Studies using standard intravitreal dosing regimens, including monthly, pro re nata (PRN), or treat-and-extend approaches, were eligible.

Exclusion criteria

Studies were excluded if they assessed other anti-VEGF agents (e.g., Bevacizumab) without direct comparison to Ranibizumab or Aflibercept, were case reports, editorials, or non-comparative studies, or lacked sufficient data for quantitative synthesis. Studies focused exclusively on other retinal diseases, such as myopic choroidal neovascularization or polypoidal choroidal vasculopathy without separable nAMD data, were also excluded when applicable.

Study screening and data extraction

All identified records were imported into EndNote for duplicate removal. Two independent reviewers screened titles and abstracts, followed by full-text assessment of potentially eligible articles. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion, and a third reviewer was consulted when necessary. A standardized data extraction form was used to collect key information, including: study characteristics (authors, year, design, location, and sample size); patient characteristics (age, sex, baseline vision); intervention details (dosing regimens for Ranibizumab and Aflibercept); outcomes (BCVA change, CRT reduction, dry retina rate, injection frequency, and adverse events); and statistical analyses (reported metrics such as mean differences, odds ratios, and p-values).

When studies reported multiple follow-up time points, the longest follow-up period meeting inclusion criteria was preferentially extracted for quantitative synthesis. If standard deviations (SDs) were not directly reported, they were estimated from available confidence intervals, standard errors, interquartile ranges, or p-values when feasible according to Cochrane recommendations. For studies including both eyes from the same patient, data were extracted as reported, and this limitation was considered during risk-of-bias assessment and interpretation of results.

If data allowed, subgroup analyses were prespecified according to study design (RCTs vs. observational studies), treatment regimens (monthly vs. treat-and-extend), or follow-up duration (≤12 months vs. >12 months). Subgroup differences were explored descriptively, and p-values for subgroup analyses were interpreted cautiously because these analyses were exploratory in nature.

Quality assessment

The methodological quality of the included studies was assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) [26] for observational studies, covering selection, comparability, and outcome domains, and the Risk of Bias 2 (ROB2) tool [27, 28] for randomized controlled trials, evaluating randomization, blinding, and outcome reporting. Two independent reviewers conducted the assessments, with discrepancies resolved through discussion. Quality assessment results are presented in Table 1 and Fig. 2.

Additionally, the certainty of the evidence for each major outcome was evaluated using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) approach, considering risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias. GRADE certainty ratings were determined independently by two reviewers, and evidence certainty could be downgraded based on these five domains. Sensitivity analyses excluding studies with higher risk of bias or lower NOS scores were additionally performed to evaluate robustness of pooled estimates.

Data synthesis

Data synthesis was conducted using R (version 4.3.2) with the meta package. A random-effects model was applied to account for between-study heterogeneity. The DerSimonian–Laird estimator was used for between-study variance estimation. Statistical analyses included primary outcomes: mean differences (MDs) for changes in BCVA, CRT, and dry retina. and secondary outcomes: mean injection frequency, treatment burden, and the proportion of adverse events, including intraocular pressure elevation. Heterogeneity was evaluated using the I2 statistic. Heterogeneity was interpreted as low (I2 < 25%), moderate (25–50%), or high (>50%). Where appropriate, prediction intervals were additionally assessed for outcomes with substantial heterogeneity, including BCVA, CRT, and injection frequency. Publication bias was assessed via funnel plots and Egger’s test. Sensitivity analyses were performed by excluding studies with high risk of bias or small sample sizes.

Results

Study selection and screening process

A comprehensive literature search was conducted in PubMed, Web of Science, Embase and Cochrane. The initial search yielded 2831 records. After removing 1097 duplicates, 1,734 records were screened based on predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Ultimately, 11 studies were identified as eligible [29–39]. The study selection process is illustrated in the PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 1).

Quality assessment of included studies

The quality of included studies was assessed using validated tools. Observational studies were evaluated with the NOS, scoring between 6 and 9, indicating high methodological quality. RCTs were assessed using the revised ROB2 tool. ROB2 results showed low risk of bias for randomization and outcome measurement, with minor concerns regarding allocation concealment in one study. Characteristics of the included studies

The 11 included studies comprised nine observational studies and two RCTs, with follow-up durations ranging from 6 to 24 months and sample sizes between 77 and 2,457 participants. Most studies enrolled treatment-naive patients with neovascular nAMD. The mean age of participants ranged from 69.4 to 82.5 years, with an approximately equal sex distribution.

Ranibizumab was administered at 0.5 mg per injection, either monthly or using a treat-and-extend (T&E) regimen, while Aflibercept was given at 2 mg per injection with similar dosing strategies. Statistical data synthesis

Primary outcomes

The predefined primary outcomes included BCVA, CRT, and dry retina proportion. Effect estimates for continuous outcomes are reported as MDs with corresponding 95% CIs, while heterogeneity was quantified using I2 and τ2 statistics. Prediction intervals (PIs) were additionally assessed for outcomes with substantial heterogeneity.

For BCVA improvement, the pooled mean difference was 3.87 ETDRS letters (95% CI: −0.47 to 8.21; p = 0.07), showing a numerical trend toward better outcomes with Aflibercept, but without statistical significance. Substantial heterogeneity was detected (I2 = 97%, τ2 = 14.0738), primarily due to differences in follow-up duration and baseline characteristics among studies, such as those by Skrzypczak et al. [29] and Wong et al. [32] (Fig. 3). The prediction interval was wide, indicating considerable variability in the potential treatment effect across different clinical settings.

Forest plot illustrating the meta-analysis results for BCVA improvement

For CRT reduction, the pooled mean difference was −85.35 µm (95% CI: −178.45 to 7.75; p = 0.09), indicating a nonsignificant trend favoring Aflibercept. Given the absence of statistical significance and the wide confidence interval, these findings do not support a definitive anatomical advantage of either agent. Heterogeneity was extremely high (I2 = 99%, τ2 = 4513.1057), likely due to variations in anatomical response and imaging protocols across studies, such as those by Parikh et al. [34] and Waldstein et al. [35] (Fig. 4). The corresponding prediction interval was also broad, suggesting uncertainty regarding the consistency of CRT outcomes between future studies.

Forest plot depicting the meta-analysis results for CRT reduction

The subgroup analysis stratified by study design (observational vs. RCTs) demonstrated consistent effect directions across both subgroups, but none of the results reached statistical significance. In the observational studies, the pooled mean difference was −82.39 (95% CI: −251.88 to 87.09; I2 = 97.9%), while for randomized controlled trials, it was −90.58 (95% CI: −1119.77 to 938.61; I2 = 99.3%). The overall pooled estimate was −85.45 (95% CI: −178.57 to 7.67; I2 = 98.5%), indicating substantial heterogeneity among included studies. The test for subgroup differences was not significant (χ2 = 0.01, df = 1, p = 0.93), suggesting that study type was not a major source of heterogeneity. These subgroup analyses should be interpreted cautiously because they were exploratory and involved multiple comparisons. Given the inclusion of different study designs, heterogeneous patient populations, and varying treatment regimens, these findings should not be interpreted as evidence of superiority of either treatment. Overall, these results suggest comparable anatomical effects between treatments rather than a clear superiority of one agent (Figure S1).

To explore whether study design influenced the treatment effect, a meta-regression was performed with study type (RCT vs. observational study) as a covariate. As illustrated in the bubble plot, the regression line showed a slight downward trend from observational studies to RCTs, implying that RCTs tended to report smaller CRT reductions compared with observational studies. However, this association was not statistically significant (p > 0.05), indicating that any observed differences should be interpreted as exploratory rather than confirmatory (Figure S2). Because substantial residual heterogeneity remained after meta-regression, study design alone did not adequately explain the between-study variability.

For the proportion of patients achieving a dry retina, the pooled difference was 18.74% (95% CI: 15.20–22.28), favoring Aflibercept with statistical significance (p < 0.001). This consistent anatomical outcome across studies supports a more robust finding relative to other primary outcomes, such as BCVA or CRT. No heterogeneity was detected (I2 = 0%, p = 0.90), indicating consistency across studies, including those by Skrzypczak et al. [29] and Wong et al. [32] (Fig. 5). Compared with BCVA and CRT outcomes, this endpoint demonstrated greater consistency and narrower uncertainty across studies. Subgroup analysis of primary outcomes revealed significant differences, with CRT reduction identified as the main source of heterogeneity (χ2 = 62.92, df = 2, p < 0.01) (Fig. 6).

Secondary outcomes

The meta-analysis of injection frequency showed a pooled mean difference of −0.91 injections (95% CI: −1.67 to −0.15), indicating a numerical reduction in injection frequency with Aflibercept. Although this result reached statistical significance (p = 0.02), the absolute difference was small and may not be clinically meaningful, particularly considering that some individual studies (Table 2) reported fewer injections with Ranibizumab. Therefore, this apparent benefit should not be interpreted as definitive evidence of a lower treatment burden, as it may partly reflect study-specific dosing regimens rather than a true pharmacologic advantage. Substantial heterogeneity was observed (I2 = 93%, τ2 = 0.5653), likely attributable to differences in treat-and-extend protocols and follow-up durations across studies, including Chakravarthy et al. [31] and Waldstein et al. [35] (Fig. 7). The prediction interval for injection frequency was wide, further indicating uncertainty regarding the consistency of this effect across different study populations.

Forest plot showing the meta-analysis results for injection frequency

Among observational studies, the pooled mean difference was −0.95 (95% CI: −1.86 to −0.04; I2 = 94%, p < 0.0001), showing a small but variable reduction in injection frequency with Aflibercept. Although this result reached statistical significance, the high heterogeneity limits the strength of this finding, as several studies reported comparable or even fewer injections with Ranibizumab. For the RCT subgroup, the pooled mean difference was −0.70 (95% CI: −1.00 to −0.40), demonstrating a consistent trend in the same direction, though based on a single trial and therefore limited in generalizability. The overall pooled effect was −0.92 (95% CI: −1.68 to −0.15; I2 = 93.0%), indicating a numerical difference that was statistically significant but modest in magnitude. Taken together, these results suggest a possible trend toward fewer injections with Aflibercept rather than a clear reduction in treatment burden.

The test for subgroup differences (χ2 = 0.39, df = 1, p = 0.53) was not significant, implying that study type did not substantially influence the pooled effect size. In summary, both observational and randomized studies demonstrated a generally similar direction of effect. However, because heterogeneity exceeded 75% for most outcome measures and the included studies differed substantially in design, patient characteristics, and dosing regimens, these findings should be interpreted cautiously and should not be considered definitive evidence of reduced treatment burden with Aflibercept (Figure S3).

To explore the potential impact of study design, a meta-regression analysis was conducted with study type as a covariate. As shown in the bubble plot, the regression line exhibited a mild upward slope, indicating that RCTs tended to report smaller reductions in injection frequency compared with observational studies. Nevertheless, this relationship was not statistically significant (p > 0.05), suggesting that observed differences are more likely attributable to clinical practice variation rather than intrinsic differences in drug efficacy (Figure S4). The exploratory nature of the meta-regression and the persistence of substantial residual heterogeneity should be considered when interpreting these findings. The funnel plot for injection frequency showed slight asymmetry, indicating potential publication bias (Fig. 8).

Adverse events and safety profiles

The safety of Ranibizumab and Aflibercept was relatively similar when the results of the studies were compared. Both drugs showed low rates of ocular side effects like intraocular inflammation and endophthalmitis. However, Aflibercept was reported to have fewer instances of intraocular pressure increase to a dangerous level (3.5%) compared to Ranibizumab (7.2%) [36]. Systemic adverse events, including arterial thromboembolic events, were infrequent and occurred with similar frequency in both treatment arms. According to Freund et al. [36], Aflibercept has been associated with reduced IOP risks, which could be another safety bonus. Because safety data were inconsistently reported across studies and pooled quantitative synthesis was not feasible for several safety endpoints, these findings should be interpreted cautiously. While these findings suggest comparable safety profiles overall, the observed differences should be interpreted cautiously due to limited event numbers and study-level variability.

Publication bias and sensitivity results

Funnel plots were constructed for all primary and secondary outcomes. Plots for BCVA improvement (Fig. 9), CRT reduction (Fig. 10), and dry retina proportion (Fig. 11) appeared generally symmetrical, suggesting no substantial publication bias. In contrast, the funnel plot for injection frequency (Fig. 8) showed slight asymmetry, possibly due to underreporting of smaller studies or those with neutral findings.

A leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was conducted to assess the influence of individual studies on the overall pooled effect. As shown in Figure S5, sequential omission of each study did not materially change the direction or magnitude of the effect estimate (MD range: −23.4 to −17.4). The pooled mean difference remained negative throughout, indicating consistent trends toward greater CRT reduction in the treatment group. None of the individual omissions substantially altered the statistical significance or heterogeneity (I2 range: 97.3%–99.0%), suggesting that no single study disproportionately influenced the overall result. Additional sensitivity analyses excluding studies with higher risk of bias or smaller sample sizes produced similar effect directions, supporting the relative robustness of the findings despite persistent heterogeneity.

GRADE rating

According to the GRADE assessment, the overall quality of evidence for all key outcomes, including BCVA improvement, CRT reduction, dry retina rate, and injection frequency, was rated as very low. The downgrading mainly resulted from substantial heterogeneity among studies and wide confidence intervals, indicating imprecision. Although the risk of bias was generally low and no serious indirectness or publication bias was detected, the inconsistency across trials weakens the certainty of the findings. In addition, the large number of exploratory subgroup analyses and multiple comparisons may increase the risk of type I error and should be considered when interpreting nominally significant findings. The substantial heterogeneity observed across most outcome measures (I2 > 75%) further limits the strength and generalizability of the pooled estimates. Therefore, the pooled estimates should be interpreted cautiously, and further well-designed RCTs with larger sample sizes are warranted to confirm these results (Table S2).

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis incorporated 11 high-quality studies to evaluate the efficacy and safety of Ranibizumab and Aflibercept in the treatment of nAMD. A side-by-side comparison of outcomes is presented to highlight the similarities and differences across studies. Overall, the current evidence suggests broadly comparable functional outcomes between the two agents, and no clear superiority was demonstrated for BCVA or CRT outcomes. Although some anatomical findings, such as dry retina proportion, numerically favored Aflibercept, these observations should be interpreted cautiously because most outcome measures showed substantial heterogeneity and low certainty of evidence.

Visual acuity outcomes

Both Ranibizumab and Aflibercept significantly improved BCVA, with gains maintained throughout the study period in each group. In the RIVAL trial, Gillies et al. [38], reported a 24-month BCVA gain of +6.6 letters for Ranibizumab and +4.6 letters for Aflibercept, with no statistically significant difference. Similarly, Skrzypczak et al. [29] found no significant difference in BCVA changes between the two agents, although Aflibercept demonstrated better visual stabilization.

Garweg et al. [30] and Wong et al. [32] reported comparable improvements in BCVA, indicating that both agents effectively preserve and enhance visual acuity in treatment-naive patients. Minor variations, such as the +3.2-letter BCVA gain observed after switching from Aflibercept to Ranibizumab in Rouvas et al. [37], suggest that individual patient differences may contribute to outcome variability. Importantly, the pooled meta-analysis did not demonstrate a statistically significant superiority of either agent for BCVA improvement, and the confidence interval included both potential benefit and no meaningful difference. Therefore, the available evidence supports functional equivalence rather than definitive superiority of one treatment over the other.

Anatomical outcomes: central retinal thickness and dry retina

The primary distinction between the two agents emerged in anatomical outcomes. Studies by Waldstein et al. [35] and Wong et al. [32] demonstrated that Aflibercept achieved greater CRT reduction than Ranibizumab. For example, Waldstein et al. [35] reported a reduction of −172 µm with Aflibercept versus −160 µm with Ranibizumab. Similarly, Freund et al. [36] found superior fluid resolution in eyes treated with Aflibercept.

A higher proportion of patients achieved a dry retina with Aflibercept. Wong et al. [32] reported that 75% of patients treated with Aflibercept showed no retinal fluid accumulation, compared to 41.7% with Ranibizumab. This advantage aligns with Aflibercept’s higher VEGF-A binding affinity and its ability to inhibit placental growth factor (PlGF), enhancing anti-permeability effects. However, Chakravarthy et al. [31] found no significant anatomical improvement in patients switched from Ranibizumab to Aflibercept, highlighting the need for individualized treatment approaches.

Importantly, the dry retina outcome represented one of the most consistent findings in this review, with a pooled absolute difference of approximately 19% favoring Aflibercept and no observed heterogeneity (I2 = 0%). However, CRT reduction showed substantial variability across studies and did not reach statistical significance in the pooled analysis. Therefore, the apparent differences in anatomical outcomes should not be interpreted as definitive evidence of greater anatomical efficacy overall.

Injection frequency and treatment burden

Injection frequency and treatment burden are critical considerations for both patients and healthcare systems. Across studies, Aflibercept was generally associated with fewer injections, particularly under treat-and-extend protocols. Garweg et al. [30] reported an average of 11.7 Aflibercept injections over 24 months, compared to 10.5 with Ranibizumab.

This aligns with findings by Gillies et al. [38], which reported comparable functional outcomes between the two agents, with approximately 17 injections over 24 months. However, Aflibercept may offer an advantage through extended dosing intervals, such as Q8 regimens, as noted by Wong et al. [32].

The meta-regression findings suggested that the observed variability in injection frequency between RCTs and real-world studies primarily reflects differences in clinical practice rather than intrinsic drug efficacy. In real-world settings, the flexibility of treat-and-extend regimens likely contributes to the lower injection burden observed with Aflibercept. Clinically, these findings underscore the importance of individualized dosing strategies to balance efficacy and treatment adherence.

Nevertheless, the pooled difference in injection frequency was relatively small, highly heterogeneous, and supported by low to very low GRADE certainty. Given the inclusion of different study designs, heterogeneous patient populations, and varying dosing regimens, the observed reduction in injection frequency should be considered suggestive rather than conclusive evidence of lower treatment burden. Therefore, the current evidence does not conclusively establish a clinically meaningful reduction in treatment burden with Aflibercept.

Safety and adverse events

Both agents demonstrated favorable safety profiles across all included studies. Gillies et al. [33] and Parikh et al. [34] reported similarly low rates of ocular adverse events, such as intraocular inflammation and endophthalmitis, with no significant difference between the two treatments. However, Freund et al. [36] found that Aflibercept was associated with a lower incidence of intraocular pressure elevation compared to Ranibizumab (3.5% vs. 7.2%), which may be clinically important for patients with glaucoma.

Major safety events, including arterial thromboembolic complications, were rare and did not differ significantly between the two agents. Additional studies included in this review also demonstrated generally comparable safety outcomes, although quantitative synthesis of safety endpoints was limited by inconsistent reporting and low event frequency. Although pooling of safety outcomes was attempted, sparse events and inconsistent reporting across studies precluded formal meta-analysis for several safety variables. Consequently, small between-group differences in adverse events should be interpreted cautiously. These findings support the long-term safety and tolerability of both Ranibizumab and Aflibercept in managing nAMD.

Subgroup findings and population-specific insights

Subgroup analyses further explored the differential efficacy of the two agents. Waldstein et al. [35] reported that higher baseline CRT was associated with greater anatomical improvement in patients treated with Aflibercept, suggesting superior efficacy in reducing retinal thickness. Similarly, Wong et al. [32], noted that Aflibercept demonstrated slightly better fluid resolution than Ranibizumab, particularly among Asian patients, although overall functional outcomes remained comparable. Rouvas et al. [37] reported limited BCVA improvement in patients who switched from Aflibercept to Ranibizumab, suggesting that cross-over therapy may benefit selected non-responders and underscoring the importance of individualized treatment strategies.

However, these subgroup and exploratory analyses should be interpreted cautiously because multiple comparisons increase the risk of type I error, and most subgroup findings were not supported by statistically significant interaction testing.

Heterogeneity and evidence certainty

Considerable heterogeneity was observed across studies, particularly in anatomical and injection frequency outcomes. Subgroup analyses revealed consistent effect directions across randomized and observational studies, indicating that study design itself did not explain the variability in treatment response. The meta-regression analysis supported this conclusion, suggesting that differences in baseline characteristics, disease chronicity, and treatment regimens were more likely contributors to heterogeneity. Importantly, heterogeneity exceeded 75% for most major outcome measures, underscoring the substantial variability among included studies and limiting the strength and generalizability of pooled estimates. These findings highlight the complex interplay between biological variability and real-world practice patterns in determining therapeutic outcomes.

The sensitivity analysis further strengthened the credibility of these findings by confirming that no single study disproportionately influenced the overall results. The robustness of pooled estimates, despite high heterogeneity, supports the general reliability of the conclusions while emphasizing the need for cautious interpretation.

From an evidence quality perspective, the GRADE assessment rated the certainty of evidence as low to very low for most outcomes. This downgrading primarily stemmed from imprecision and inconsistency among studies, rather than from bias or publication effects. The substantial heterogeneity, wide confidence intervals, and inconsistent anatomical findings substantially reduced certainty regarding comparative efficacy. Therefore, even where numerical trends favored Aflibercept, the overall certainty supporting these observations remained limited. The wide confidence intervals and diverse study designs underscore the need for standardized outcome definitions and harmonized imaging criteria in future RCTs. Improving methodological uniformity and follow-up consistency will be critical to enhancing confidence in comparative effectiveness evidence.

Comparison with existing literature

The findings of this review align with previous meta-analyses and real-world studies indicating comparable visual outcomes between Ranibizumab and Aflibercept. Consistent with prior meta-analyses, our study found no statistically significant difference in BCVA improvement between the two agents, while anatomical outcomes showed greater variability across studies. Compared with previous reviews, the present analysis additionally incorporated more recent real-world evidence and explored heterogeneity through subgroup and meta-regression analyses. However, because of the substantial heterogeneity, mixed study designs, and low to very low certainty of evidence, the current findings should not be interpreted as proof of superiority of either treatment. Nevertheless, these findings should be interpreted cautiously because the certainty of evidence for most outcomes remained low or very low. Taken together, the heterogeneity, sensitivity, and GRADE analyses collectively suggest that while current evidence supports Aflibercept’s potential benefits, the certainty of this conclusion remains limited. Future large-scale, multicenter RCTs using uniform treatment regimens and real-world validation cohorts are warranted to clarify the true comparative advantages of these two agents.

Clinical implications

Both Ranibizumab and Aflibercept demonstrate high efficacy in treating nAMD, offering significant improvements in visual function and disease control. Current evidence suggests that Aflibercept may provide more consistent retinal fluid resolution in some patients, whereas functional visual outcomes appear broadly comparable between treatments. Aflibercept’s enhanced anatomical efficacy and extended dosing intervals may reduce treatment burden and improve adherence, particularly in patients with persistent retinal fluid or limited clinic access. However, these potential advantages should be interpreted within the context of substantial heterogeneity and low-certainty evidence. Therefore, treatment selection should consider individual factors such as baseline retinal thickness, lesion size, and prior therapeutic response.

Limitations and future directions

This review has several limitations. The included studies vary in design, follow-up duration, and patient characteristics, potentially influencing the outcomes. Additionally, the 24-month data collection limit prevents assessment of long-term efficacy and stability. A potential limitation is that some studies may have included data from both eyes of the same patient, which could lead to within-subject correlation and may inflate the precision of effect estimates in some analyses. However, this effect is expected to be small and unlikely to materially affect the overall conclusions. Importantly, the substantial heterogeneity across studies and the low to very low GRADE certainty for most outcomes represent major limitations of the current evidence base. Future studies should aim to reduce heterogeneity by adopting consistent retreatment protocols, harmonized imaging endpoints, and standardized definitions of response. Future head-to-head randomized controlled trials with standardized treatment regimens, predefined outcomes, harmonized OCT criteria, and longer follow-up durations are needed to better evaluate long-term functional outcomes, retinal atrophy, structural changes, and safety profiles. Integrating quantitative imaging biomarkers and patient-reported outcomes will be crucial to refine individualized treatment decisions and guide precision medicine approaches in nAMD management.

Conclusion

This systematic review and meta-analysis demonstrate that both Ranibizumab and Aflibercept are effective in treating nAMD. While functional outcomes appear comparable, Aflibercept was associated with greater anatomical improvement and a potential reduction in treatment burden, which may be relevant in selected clinical contexts rather than implying a general therapeutic preference. However, these findings should be interpreted cautiously because the included evidence was characterized by mixed study designs, substantial heterogeneity, and predominantly low to very low GRADE certainty. Importantly, the present analysis did not demonstrate definitive superiority of either agent for visual outcomes, and the observed anatomical or treatment-burden differences should be considered suggestive rather than conclusive. These findings reinforce the value of individualized treatment strategies in nAMD management and underscore that further well-designed studies are needed to clarify the clinical implications of these differences and to optimize patient care.
