Association between maternal preconception blood pressure and spontaneous abortion: a population-based cohort study in China
Authors: Jieyun Yin, Shenfengxiang Feng, Xuan Hu, Wanyi Fu, Jiaxin Li, Xinyi Lyu, Chuanyu Zhao, Hanbin Wu, Jueming Lei, Hongguang Zhang, Yuan He, Yuanyuan Wang, Zuoqi Peng, Ya Zhang, Ying Yang, Xu Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMaternal preconception blood pressure (BP) is associated with various adverse pregnancy outcomes, but its relationship with spontaneous abortion (SA) remains controversial. We aimed to evaluate the relationship between preconception BP and SA.MethodsThis population-based cohort study used data from participants in the National Free Preconception Examination Program between 2013 and 2019. Maternal preconception BP was categorized according to the 2017 ACC/AHA guidelines. The relationship between these BP categories and SA incidence was analyzed using multivariable logistic regression to generate adjusted odds ratios (aORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Additionally, restricted cubic splines were employed to assess the dose-response relationship between maternal BP and the risk of SA.ResultsThe study included 7 067 998 women, of whom 208 508 (2.95%) experienced a SA. Maternal preconception elevated BP (aOR, 1.02; 95% CI: 1.00-1.05), stage 1 hypertension (aOR, 1.06; 95% CI: 1.04–1.08), and stage 2 hypertension (aOR, 1.25; 95% CI: 1.20–1.31) were all associated with an increased risk of SA, demonstrating a gradient relationship. Our results also show that there is a “J”-shaped dose-response relationship between maternal preconception BP and the risk of SA, with the lowest risk observed at a systolic BP of 110 mm Hg and a diastolic BP of 70 mm Hg. Sensitivity analyses excluding individuals with hypotension or self-reported hypertension yielded results consistent with the main analysis.ConclusionsPreconception elevated BP and stage 1 hypertension were associated with an increased risk of SA, although the strength of these associations was weaker than that observed for stage 2 hypertension. There was a “J”-dose-response relationship between maternal preconception BP and the risk of SA, with the lowest risk of SA at systolic BP of 110 mm Hg and diastolic BP of 70 mm Hg.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12916-026-04937-2.Keywords: Spontaneous abortion, Maternal preconception blood pressure, Dose-response relationships, Retrospective cohort study

Background

Spontaneous abortion (SA), also called miscarriage, can result in a variety of short-term obstetrical complications, including premature birth and placental abruption [1]. It is estimated that about 10%-20% of all clinically confirmed pregnancies end in SA [2], and there is a significant risk for recurrence [3]. Despite its prevalence, the underlying causes and mechanisms of SA remain poorly understood. Since SA typically occurs in the early stage of pregnancy and is closely linked to women’s preconception health, exploring preconception modifiable risk factors during this period is crucial for improving pregnancy outcomes.

Maternal preconception blood pressure (BP) has long been a focus in obstetrics. Traditionally, preconception hypertension is defined as a systolic BP (SBP) ≥ 140 mm Hg and/or a diastolic BP (DBP) ≥ 90 mm Hg, which is linked to increased risk of fecundability reduction [4], pregnancy complications as well as adverse pregnancy outcomes, such as gestational hypertensive disorders [5], preterm birth (PTB) and small for gestational age (SGA) [6]. However, research on the association between preconception hypertension and the risk of SA has produced inconsistent results, with four studies suggesting hypertension is a statistically significant risk factor [7–10] while others do not find supporting evidence [11–15]. It is important to note that several of the studies suggesting a link were conducted in women had history of pregnant loss [10], or undergoing fresh embryo transfers or assisted reproductive techniques [7, 8], therefore makes their findings less applicable to the general population. Conversely, studies performed within the general population often have a small number of SA cases among women with hypertension (ranged from 5 to 76) [9, 11–15], which may hinder their ability to draw definitive conclusions. Therefore, a large-sample cohort study is still necessary to more accurately assess the relationship between hypertension and the risk of SA, especially in the general population.

In 2017, the American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association (AHA/ACC) updated their hypertension guidelines [16], based on observational data indicating a heightened risk of cardiovascular disease (CVD) not just in non-pregnant adults with BP ≥ 140/90 mm Hg but also in those classified with stage 1 hypertension (BP of 130–139/80–89 mm Hg) and elevated BP (BP of 120–129/<80 mm Hg). Despite these updates, the effectiveness of these new hypertension criteria for identifying women at high risk of SA remains uncertain, as the relationship between the newly defined categories (i.e., stage 1 hypertension and elevated BP) with the risk of SA was only investigated in a small sample size study (797 participants) [10]. Consequently, further evaluation of these new categories concerning the risk of SA is still warranted.

In light of this, our study, based on a large, country-based population database with sufficient number of SA cases, aimed to evaluate the associations of the newly defined BP categories and SA, which is crucial for formulating effective prenatal recommendations.

Methods

Data sources and study design

This retrospective cohort study was based on the National Free Preconception Checkups Project (NFPCP), which was jointly launched in 2010 by the National Health Commission and Ministry of Finance of the People’s Republic of China to provide free preconception health screening services for couples of childbearing-ages preparing for pregnancy. This project mainly involves conducting health examinations and following up on pregnancy outcomes, so as to scientifically assess the fertility risk of reproductive-aged couples and to help prevent birth defects or other adverse pregnancy outcomes (see Additional file 1: Supplementary Methods ) [17–21]. The detailed design, organization, and quality control have been published previously [18, 22]. A comprehensive NFPCP implementation flowchart is also provided (Additional file 1: Fig. S1).

Between 2013 and 2019, a total of 16 148 098 women participated in the NFPCP. We excluded 683 311 participants who were lost to early pregnancy follow-up, and 5 938 371 women who failed to conceive within one year. Among the remaining 9 526 416 participants, 2 251 602 women were lost during the pregnancy outcome follow-up period. We further excluded participants with multiple pregnancies (n = 39 736), ectopic pregnancies or medically induced abortion (n = 115 707), as well as those with implausible BP values (SBP < 60 or > 220 mm Hg, and DBP < 40 or > 140 mm Hg, based on a prior NFPCP study [6]) or missing preconception BP information (n = 51 373) (Fig. 1). Ultimately, 7 067 998 participants were included in the primary analysis.

Flowchart for the Study Population Based on the National Free Preconception Checkups Project (NFPCP). Abbreviations: BP, blood pressure; SBP, systolic blood pressure; DBP, diastolic blood pressure

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board at the National Research Institute for Family Planning in Beijing, China, and conducted in accordance with the guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki [23]. Furthermore, this study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting Guideline and all participants provided written informed consent before enrollment.

Exposure

Women who responded “Yes” to the question “Have you ever been told by a doctor or other health care professional that you had hypertension?” or who self-reported currently taking prescribed antihypertensive drugs for the management of hypertension on the baseline questionnaire were classified as having diagnosed hypertension. Maternal preconception BP was measured by locally trained health workers using an electronic sphygmomanometer in a quiet room after at least 10 min of rest, with the cuff size determined based on arm circumference.

Outcome

The primary outcome was SA, defined as fetal death or pregnancy loss, occurring before 28 weeks of gestation. Trained local health personnel conducted follow-up calls to women who had successfully conceived to gather information on pregnancy outcomes. Information on SA was obtained from participants’ self-reports and further validated using participants’ medical records or pregnancy certificates obtained from local health-care organizations.

Covariables

To visually delineate the potential confounders affecting the relationship between preconception BP and SA, we constructed a directed acyclic graph (DAG) informed by both a literature review and biological plausibility [6, 24, 25] (Additional file 1: Fig. S2). These preconception covariates included maternal age at last menstrual period (LMP; 20–24, 25–29, 30–34, 35–39, ≥ 40); body mass index (BMI), calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters square, and classified as underweight (less than 18.5 kg/m2), normal (18.5–23.9 kg/m2), overweight (24.0–27.9 kg/m2), or obese (28.0 kg/m2 and more); parity (0 or ≥ 1); smoking (yes or no); passive smoking (no, occasionally and often); alcohol consumption (no, occasionally and often); diabetes (yes or no); ethnicity (Han or other), educational level (Junior high school and below or Senior high school or above); harmful environmental exposures (including heavy metals, radiation, high temperature, noise, pesticides, new decoration, paint, other organic solvents, fever, common cold; yes or no); medicine taking (yes or no); folic acid intake (yes or no); infections (including hepatitis B virus, Neisseria gonorrhoeae, Chlamydia trachomatis, Toxoplasma gondii, cytomegalovirus, or Treponema pallidum by detecting their specific-serum antibodies; yes or no) and history of abortion (including whether has a history of induced or spontaneous abortion; yes or no). Detailed information on the covariates is presented in Additional file 1 (Table 1). Covariance diagnostics were performed on all variables, and no collinearity issues were found based on the variance inflation factor and tolerance value.

Statistical analysis

In this study, all variables were described as categorical variables, expressed as numbers (n) and percentages (%). The χ² test or Kruskal-Wallis H test was used to compare the distributions of baseline characteristics among the different groups. According to the 2017 ACC/AHA guidelines [16], maternal BP was classified as follows: (1) normal BP (SBP < 120 mm Hg and DBP < 80 mm Hg), (2) elevated BP (SBP 120–129 mm Hg and DBP < 80 mm Hg), (3) stage 1 hypertension (SBP 130–139 mm Hg and/or DBP 80–89 mm Hg) and (4) stage 2 hypertension (SBP ≥ 140 and/or DBP ≥ 90 mm Hg or self-reported hypertension). We examined the associations between these BP categories and the risk of SA using a multivariable logistic regression model. To address potential selection bias potentially resulting from participant loss to follow-up after pregnancy, we also applied inverse probability weighting (IPW) for correction. The logistic regression model was constructed using the same covariates as the primary analysis to estimate each participant’s probability of loss-to-follow-up. The inverse of this estimated probability was then used as a weight in the primary analysis [26]. Additionally, we also modeled SBP and DBP as a continuous variable and graphed the adjusted OR using restricted cubic splines with 3 knots (at the 10th, 50th, and 90th percentiles) to assess the dose-response relationship. The selection of knots for the RCS curves was guided by the minimization of Bayesian Information Criterion [27], and nonlinearity trends were tested by the Wald statistics. In subgroup analyses, we investigated whether the association between maternal preconception BP categories and SA, after adjusting for the co-variables mentioned above, differed by maternal age at LMP, history of abortion, preconception BMI, and ethnicity. Moreover, we did the following sensitivity analyses to assess the robustness of the associations: (1) To avoid the bias caused by behavioral changes (e.g., taking antihypertensive drugs) resulting from individuals being aware of their hypertension diagnosis, we excluded self-reported cases of hypertension; (2) As the RCS plot suggested a ‘J’ shaped relationship between BP and SA, and given that the 2017 ACC/AHA hypertension classification criteria do not specifically define hypotension, we reclassified BP levels by defining hypotension (SBP < 90 mm Hg or DBP < 60 mm Hg) based on evidence from the literature [28], and excluded individuals with hypotension from the original normal BP group; (3) We fitted analogous models treating the lower limit of each BP category as a cutoff for diagnosing hypertension. For example, for stage 1 hypertension, we compared women with SBP ≥ 130 mm Hg and/or DBP ≥ 80 mm Hg to those with SBP < 130 mm Hg and DBP < 80 mm Hg [29]; (4) Fourth, we calculated the E-values to explore the minimum strength of association that an unmeasured confounders would need to have with both maternal preconception BP and SA to fully explain away the estimated association, conditional on the measured covariates. Finally, population attributable fraction (PAF) and 95% CIs were calculated based on methods described by Bruzzi et al. [30] and Efron and Tibshirani [31] to assess the benefits at the population level of controlling hypertension.

Statistical analyses were performed using R software version 4.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing Vienna, Austria). All test were two-side, and a P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 7 067 998 women were included in the study, of whom 208 508 (2.95%) experienced SA. Regarding BP categories, 5 049 491 (71.44%), 585 410 (8.28%), 1 316 965 (18.63%), 116 132 (1.65%) women had normal BP, elevated BP, stage 1 hypertension and stage 2 hypertension, respectively. As shown in Table 1, women who were older, overweight or obese, multiparous, had diabetes, and reported preconception exposure to tobacco, alcohol, harmful environmental factors, and infections, as well as medication use and a history of abortion, were more likely to have hypertension. These findings remained consistent even when excluding women with self-reported hypertension (Additional file 1: Table 2).

As shown in Table 2, after adjusting for covariates, the ORs for SA gradually increased with higher preconception BP: elevated BP (aOR, 1.02; 95% CI, 1.00-1.05), stage 1 hypertension (aOR, 1.06; 95% CI, 1.04–1.08), and stage 2 hypertension (aOR, 1.25; 95% CI, 1.20–1.31), with a significant linear trend (P < 0.001). The corresponding aORs using IPW methods were 1.02 (95% CI, 1.00-1.04), 1.06 (95% CI, 1.04–1.07), and 1.25 (95% CI, 1.20–1.30), respectively.

Figure 2 illustrates the RCS plots, which indicate nonlinear relationships between SBP and DBP with SA (P for nonlinearity test < 0.001), showing ‘J’-shaped associations. The lowest risk of SA is observed when SBP is 110 mm Hg and DBP is 70 mm Hg. This suggests that both increases and decreases in SBP and DBP are linked to a higher risk of SA. However, the impact of low DBP levels on SA is relatively less significant compared to that of SBP, consistent results were obtained in the population excluding those who self-reported hypertension (Additional file 1: Fig. S3). When setting SBP at 110 mm Hg and DBP at 70 mm Hg as the reference, we recalculated the risk of SA for every 5 mm Hg increase in SBP (ranging from 70 to 180 mm Hg) and DBP (ranging from 50 to 120 mm Hg). The findings are generally consistent with those presented in Fig. 2 (Table 3).

Sensitivity analyses, including exclusion of individuals with self-reported hypertension (Additional file 1: Table 3) or hypotension (Additional file 1: Table 4), as well as employing the diagnostic criteria of each BP grade as thresholds for abnormal BP (Additional file 1: Table 5), yielded results that were generally consistent with the main analysis. E-values for the observed point estimates ranged from 1.16 for the association between elevated BP and SA to 1.81 for stage 2 hypertension and SA (Additional file 1: Table 6). Notably, stage 1 hypertension contributed the most to the population-level burden (PAF 1.11%) (Additional file 1: Table 7).

Discussion

This large retrospective cohort study involving over seven million individuals confirms that preconception hypertension is associated with an increased risk of SA, particularly stage 2 hypertension. After conducting sensitivity analyses with different BP group classification criteria and excluding individuals with self-reported hypertension or hypotension, these associations remained largely stable. Additionally, the study identified a significant ‘J’-shaped relationship between preconception BP and the risk of SA, with the lowest risk occurring when SBP is 110 mm Hg and DBP is 70 mm Hg. Meanwhile, due to the relatively high PAF of stage 1 hypertension, healthcare providers should consider implementing tiered interventions for women at varying stages of hypertension. Emphasis should be placed on lifestyle modifications and, when appropriate, pharmacological management to achieve healthy BP levels before pregnancy.

Our analysis indicates that preconception stage 2 hypertension (≥ 140/90 mm Hg) is associated with a 1.25-fold increased risk of SA in the general population. This relationship is strong even among women with normal BMI or age < 25 years. A few studies have examined this relationship within the general population. For instance, a study from Anhui province in China also found a statistically significant association between stage 2 hypertension and SA (aOR, 2.27; 95%CI, 1.27–4.04) [9]. Conversely, investigations conducted in North Queensland [13] and England [11] (aOR, 1.33; 95%CI, 0.90–1.97) reported statistically non-significant associations. Moreover, findings from a prospective cohort study involving 17 248 rural Chinese women indicated a neutral relationship between SA and stage 2 hypertension (aOR, 1.00; 95%CI, 0.64–1.56) [14]. Other research, such as a matched case-control study of 550 cases against 1091 controls (aOR, 0.42; 95%CI, 0.15–1.16) [15] and an Iranian study with 4818 participants (aOR, 0.60; 95%CI, 0.50–0.80) [12], even reported a negative association between miscarriage and stage 2 hypertension. These differing conclusions may arise from varied definition of SA and small sample sizes, which can lead to imprecise estimates. However, our study recruited over seven million individuals with more than 20 000 cases of SA, boasting sufficient statistical power and therefore reliable results.

BP varies continuously across a wide range. Unlike previous studies that either simply categorized BP [7, 9, 12–15] or overlooked the potential non-linear relationship between BP and SA risk [8, 10], we identified a significant “J”-shape relationship between preconception BP and the risk of SA. Similarly, Zhu et al. [32] noted “J”-shaped dose-response relationships of BPs during pregnancy with outcomes like SGA and PTB.

Our findings specifically indicated that preconception SBP < 110 mm Hg or DBP < 70 mm Hg are associated with an increased risk of SA. Although research on this topic is limited, available evidence suggests that maternal hypotension may lead to poor pregnancy outcomes, including stillbirth [33]. This underscores that while elevated maternal BP is widely recognized for its adverse effects, low BP—though potentially less severe—still deserves clinical attention due to its meaningful implications.

At present, the exact mechanism linking maternal hypertension to SA is not fully elucidated. One possible explanation is the higher prevalence of vascular placental malperfusion in hypertensive patients [34], which may increase the risk of SA due to placental damage and fetal vascular hypoperfusion [35]. Additionally, inflammation, oxidative stress, and endothelial dysfunction caused by hypertension may also play a role [36–41]. Furthermore, emerging evidence suggests that SA is more common in women with a tendency toward thrombosis [42], while studies showed that hypertension can create a prothrombotic environment [43]. Take together, these provide a theoretical basis for our research findings regarding the increased risk of SA under the condition of hypertension. It’s important to note that both subclinical and clinical target organ dysfunction and injury can occur even in individuals with stage 1 hypertension [44–46]. Therefore, it is also biologically plausible that the risk of SA may be elevated among women with stage 1 hypertension. Likewise, the exact mechanisms connecting hypotension to an increased risk of SA remain unclear. One hypothesis is that chronic reductions in uterine blood flow could lead to placental hypoperfusion, which in turn may hinder fetal growth and thereafter increase the risk of SA [47, 48]. Therefore, further research is essential to validate these findings and to gain a better understanding of the mechanisms connecting both high and low preconception BP with the risk of SA.

This study presents several key advantages. First, to the best of our knowledge, we are the first to examine the dose-response relationship between maternal preconception BP and SA using data from a large population-based cohort study involving over seven million participants. Second, by systematically categorizing maternal preconception BP and conducting a series of sensitivity analyses, the results were largely consistent with the main analysis, indicating the robustness of our results. Hence, our findings highlight the significance of preconception health assessments, especially concerning BP management. Since hypertension is modifiable, addressing it before conception may help to reduce the risk of SA. It is necessary to enhance the awareness of women planning to conceive regarding the impact of preconception BP on pregnancy.

Nevertheless, our research also has several limitations. First, since the NFPCP did not differentiate between different types of SA or provide the gestational age at the time of SA when collecting pregnancy outcome information, we are unable to assess the BP related association for different types of SA. Second, women might experience BP changes during the preconception period, which could introduce misclassification bias. However, 67.83% of the women in the NFPCP conceived within 3 months after the preconception checkup, making substantial BP changes during this brief interval unlikely for most participants. Third, SA was self-reported, which might introduce misreporting and recall bias. However, birth outcomes were further validated using medical records obtained from local healthcare organizations or pregnancy certificates reviewed by trained staff. In addition, previous studies have showed that mothers usually had a reasonably accurate recall of birth outcomes from pregnancies occurring within the past 30 years [49]. Fourth, some early cases of SA may be misdiagnosed as delayed menstruation or participants may have been reluctant to report such outcomes, potentially leading to an underestimation of the incidence of SA. Furthermore, although maternal BP was only measured once in the NFPCP, which may have resulted in an overestimation of individual BP levels [50], previous studies using this database have shown that a single BP measurement can still adequately reflect the basic BP status of Chinese couples [4, 51]. Finally, not all women who attempted conception had a documented pregnancy outcome within the one-year follow-up period, either due to failure to conceive or loss to follow-up after conception. Recent studies have suggested that preconception hypertension is associated with prolonged time to pregnancy (> 12 months) [4], highlighting its detrimental effect on fecundity. This implies that hypertension may contribute to the unrecorded cases in our study. For women conceived within one-year, baseline characteristics of those lost to follow-up after conception were largely similar to those retained in the cohort (Additional file 1: Table 8). Furthermore, the adjusted OR obtained using the IPW method was consistent with the primary analysis results, further confirming the robustness of our findings.

Conclusions

This population-based retrospective cohort study confirms that preconception hypertension is associated with an increased risk of SA, particularly stage 2 hypertension. Additionally, the study illustrates a “J”-shaped relationship between preconception BP and the risk of SA, with the smallest risk observed when SBP of 110 mm Hg and DBP of 70 mm Hg, indicating the importance of maintaining normal preconception BP levels to improve pregnancy outcomes.
