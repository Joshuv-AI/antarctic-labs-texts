Precocial Weaning Influences Anxiety, Social and Maternal Care but Not Cognition in African Striped Mice, Rhabdomys dilectus chakae
Authors: Maria K Oosthuizen, Neville Pillay
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTWeaning is a stressful stage in the lives of young mammals. Early weaning, in particular, can lead to long‐lasting physiological and psychological changes. We investigated the effects of precocial weaning on anxiety, social behavior, cognition, and maternal behavior in adult African four‐striped mice (Rhabdomys dilectus chakae). Mice weaned at 12 days exhibited higher anxiety levels and reduced exploratory behavior in a novel environment compared with those weaned at 16 and 20 days. Early‐weaned mice also showed decreased allogrooming and body contact, and increased aggression during social interactions. Females weaned early, groomed, and huddled their pups less, although they spent more time in proximity without touching their pups. Spatial learning and memory were not affected by the age at weaning. Early weaning induces stress‐related changes in anxiety, exploration, and social behaviors, all of which are of ecological relevance. Although precocious weaning did not affect cognition, R. d. chakae are likely to be anxious and poor mothers, which could also impact the fitness of their offspring. These effects manifested in behaviors of ecological significance, suggesting that early weaning may influence the fitness of the four‐striped mouse.

Introduction

Mammalian neonates differ significantly in their degree of neonatal development, with some being relatively well developed, independent, and precocial, while others are poorly developed, dependent, and altricial (Derrickson 1992). Most rodents are born altricial and rely heavily on their mothers and alloparents for thermoregulation and nutrition during the first few weeks of their lives (Derrickson 1992; Schubert et al. 2009). Weaning is the transitional process when young mammals detach from their mothers to achieve nutritional and behavioral independence and is characterized by a sharp decrease in maternal investment (Bailoo et al. 2020).

In natural environments, weaning is a gradual process that occurs over several days or weeks, with its ultimate duration depending on the size of the litter and the physical development of the young (Mendl and Paul 1991). Weaning represents a critical developmental period for young mammals, marked by significant structural, behavioral, neurological, and physiological changes (Bateson 1994). Psychologically, young mammals can experience heightened stress and anxiety due to the sudden loss of maternal care, which serves as a vital source of security and stability (Mandalaywala et al. 2014; Richter et al. 2016). The mother‐offspring relationship provides essential social interactions, and its disruption can lead to emotional changes and/or challenges during the early stages of independence (Hennessy et al. 2009). Physiologically, weaning triggers responses in the hypothalamic‐pituitary‐adrenal (HPA) axis to manage the stress associated with separation and independence (Kikusui and Mori 2009; Hennessy et al. 2009). Additionally, this process occurs synergistically with changes in energy metabolism when young mammals transition to a solid diet (Janssens and Messer 1988).

The abrupt disruption of the mother‐offspring relationship during weaning can be inherently stressful and is associated with physiological and behavioral changes that influence the adult phenotype (Richter et al. 2016). Early‐life stress caused by permanent maternal separation overstimulates the HPA axis, resulting in prolonged corticosterone elevation (Kikusui et al. 2009; Kikusui and Mori 2009; Würbel and Stauffacher 1997). Persistently high corticosterone levels have widespread effects on the body, including neuroendocrine, neural, and behavioral changes. Corticosterone acts through glucocorticoid receptors (GR) and mineralocorticoid receptors (MR) in the brain, which regulate and modulate behavioral, physiological, and cognitive functions (Koning et al. 2019; Timmermans et al. 2019).

Glucocorticoid receptors in the hippocampus are strongly implicated in the negative feedback regulation of glucocorticoids and are essential for the termination of the HPA axis response to stress (Gjerstad et al. 2018; Liu et al. 1997). Early life stress, such as prolonged mother‐offspring separation, can reduce GR expression in the hippocampus, decreasing the negative feedback by glucocorticoids and increasing the glucocorticoid expression in response to stress (Kikusui and Mori 2009; Liu et al. 1997). Upon removal from the mother, mice that were weaned early (at 14 days) exhibited elevated corticosterone levels for up to 48 h postweaning, while corticosterone levels of mice weaned a week later at 21 days returned to normal levels within two hours (Kikusui et al. 2009). Moreover, in response to a novel cage and testing on the elevated plus maze, early‐weaned mice showed lower GR expression at 8 weeks old, and higher basal and stress‐related corticosterone levels compared with normally weaned mice (Kikusui et al. 2006).

Early life stress during critical developmental periods induces neural changes that can lead to alterations in cognition, learning, and memory. Glucocorticoids are involved in the initiation of myelin synthesis, and early stress events can initiate the myelination process prematurely and significantly in certain brain areas related to the limbic system associated with anxiety behavior (Chan et al. 1998, Kikusui et al. 2007, Kikusui and Mori 2009). Early weaning can be associated with a reduction in the mossy fiber density in the hippocampus and could be related to the consistent learning deficits (Huot et al. 2002). Stressful events during brain development change the expression of several stress‐related genes, which can affect neuronal proliferation (Farrell et al. 2016; Korosi et al. 2012; Mirescu et al. 2004; Tanapat et al. 1998). High glucocorticoid levels can suppress both neurogenesis and brain‐derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF); rat pups separated from their mothers display reduced levels of both BDNF and neurogenesis (Liu et al. 2000; Mirescu et al. 2004). Such changes modulate the function of several brain regions associated with the limbic system, such as the hippocampus and amygdala (Daun et al. 2020; Youssef et al. 2019).

Changes related to the limbic system result in functional behavioral changes. Behavioral changes in response to early social stress include a heightened response of the HPA to novelty and stress (Kikusui et al. 2006), and related to the stress response, increases in anxiety‐related behaviors and aggression (Ito et al. 2006; Kikusui and Mori 2009; Kikusui et al. 2006; Nakamura et al. 2003). Early weaning constitutes a form of developmental disruption and can elicit behavioral responses similar to those observed in animals subjected to other types of early life adversity. Early‐weaned animals are significantly more aggressive compared with normally weaned animals, which may be related to anxiety (Kikusui and Mori 2009; Nakamura et al. 2003). Early‐weaned mice also show an increased prevalence of abnormal behavior, such as stereotypic behavior as adults (Jones et al. 2010; Latham and Mason 2008; Würbel and Stauffacher 1997).

Maternal care regulates the behavioral expression of their offspring later in life. Animals that did receive parental care, but less so than others, show altered behavioral responses as adults, such as increased startle responses and decreased exploration in the open field test (Caldji et al. 1998). These effects also extend to maternal behavior, which seems to be strongly affected by the early life environment of the mother (D.D. Francis et al. 1999, D. Francis et al. 1999, Rymer and Pillay 2013). Early‐weaned rats show a reduction in nest building (Nováková 1975) and less consistent maternal behavior by performing less grooming and licking of their pups (D.D. Francis et al. 1999, D. Francis et al. 1999; Kikusui and Mori 2009). Reduced maternal care also disrupts sensorimotor gating: mice or rats subjected to early separation show weaker prepulse inhibition (PPI) and larger acoustic startle responses (ASR), effects that are amplified by social isolation (Bailoo et al. 2020), vary by genetic background (Millstein et al. 2006), and can be ameliorated by an enriched postdeprivation environment (Ellenbroek and Cools 2002).

The response to weaning‐associated early life stress appears to be sexually dimorphic. Males are more vulnerable to early life stress than females, with differences apparent both in behavior and in the brain. Early‐weaned male mice exhibit precocial myelination, decreased (BDNF) protein levels in the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex, and reduced neurogenesis in the dentate gyrus of the hippocampus, whereas females do not (Kikusui and Mori 2009).

Nevertheless, previous research has exclusively been conducted in the laboratory. Yet, under tightly controlled laboratory conditions, weaning may diverge substantially from naturalistic processes because it occurs under distinct environmental and social pressures. Consequently, such studies may offer a limited perspective on weaning, with findings that are potentially specific to the artificial conditions in which they were conducted. The present study aims to evaluate the ecological validity of laboratory‐based weaning research by examining a wild‐derived mouse species that has not undergone laboratory breeding and artificial selection for several decades.

African striped mice of the genus Rhabdomys are small, diurnal and crepuscular murid rodents (40–60 g) that are widespread in Southern Africa in various habitat types, ranging from arid areas to scrublands and grasslands (Skinner and Chimimba 2005). Striped mice occurring in different habitats display flexible social structures, with mice inhabiting dry areas being more social, whereas those occurring in mesic areas are predominantly solitary. Our study model was the solitary‐living mesic occurring Rhabdomys dilectus.

R. dilectus follows a rapid yet progressive trajectory to nutritional independence. Field and laboratory observations show that feeding on solid food starts as early as postnatal day (PND) 10, when young spend a significant amount of time outside the nest (Brooks 1982), at which point they can even be fostered successfully into unrelated litters (Pillay 2000). Suckling frequency then declines steadily, and functional weaning (i.e., complete cessation of milk intake) is normally achieved by PND 15–16 (Brooks 1982; Willan 1982; Pillay 2000). Most early studies, including those cited here, referred to the once‐monospecific R. pumilio; however, the eastern populations on which they were based have since been reassigned to R. dilectus following the eastern‐versus western‐lineage split (Rambau et al. 2003). Juveniles disperse within a few days of weaning and show minimal physiological stress when separated from kin (Mackay et al. 2014), consistent with the solitary life history of R. dilectus. Consequently, removal before PND 15 constitutes precocious weaning, whereas removal at PND 20 prolongs maternal association beyond the average weaning window. Our three manipulation ages, PND 12 (precocious), PND 16 (typical), and PND 20 (prolonged), therefore encompass the period during which nutritional transition, HPA‐axis maturation, and early social buffering interact (Moriceau and Sullivan 2006; Curley and Champagne 2016).

Precocious weaning affects a composite set of phenotypic responses in rodents. Yet, previous studies have usually focused on one or a few of these traits but failed to take a comprehensive approach. In addition, the species tested were almost exclusively lab‐bred/strains. We tested whether precocious weaning negatively impacts ecologically relevant factors, such as an emotional response (challenge of open space), social competitive ability, spatial learning and memory (locating food in the environment), and maternal behavior of striped mice bred from wild‐caught parents. We hypothesized that weaning offspring early (i.e., soon after eating solid food and reduced suckling) would show behavioral and cognitive impairment. We made four predictions. Compared with later‐weaned striped mice, we expected that precociously weaned striped mice would be more anxious in a novel open field environment, lose more social encounters, perform worse in cognitive tasks, and show reduced maternal care behavior.

Materials and Methods

Our study population comprised wild‐caught individuals originating from Alice (32°48’S; 26°52’E), Eastern Cape Province, South Africa. The study animals were housed at the University of the Witwatersrand in climate‐controlled rooms (14L:10D cycle, L: 05h00–19h00; 20–24°C; 30%–60% relative humidity), conditions that match midsummer day length and climate at the capture locality. Rhabdomys is naturally diurnal‐crepuscular (Schradin 2006, Ackermann et al. 2020), with peak activity between 07h00–10h00 and 16h00–19h00 h (Dewsbury and Dawson 1979). All behavioral tests were therefore conducted within these peak‐activity windows.

We established first‐parity breeding pairs that were housed in glass tanks (46 × 30 × 35 cm) with a clear PVC front and mesh lids. Tanks were furnished with wood shavings, a plastic nest box (13 × 9 × 10 cm) and hay for bedding, and enrichment devices (e.g., cardboard/plastic pipes, twigs, paper). Mice were fed on Epol mouse cubes (Epol, Pretoria West, South Africa), fruits, and vegetables daily, and were supplemented with commercial parrot food twice a week. Water was available ad libitum.

We selected 12 pairs of mice that produced 6 or more pups (mean litter size was 6.8 ± 1.1 pups (range 6–8), with equal numbers of males and females, from our breeding colony. Striped mice start eating solid food at 10 days of age, when they spend increasing time exploring away from the maternal nest (Pillay 2000). Two offspring (one male and one female) were removed from each litter at postnatal day 12, 16, and 20, resulting in 12 females and 12 males per weaning cohort. No experimental culling was performed apart from the scheduled weaning manipulations, and surplus animals were weaned on day 20 and housed in our colony. They were housed alone in plastic cages (30 × 20 × 15 cm) under conditions described above.

When they were approximately 70 days old, and all animals were sexually mature, each treatment cohort, designated as 12D, 16D, and 20D were subjected to three experiments, and females were assessed for their maternal behavior in a fourth experiment. Because the peak activity period of striped mice is in the morning, all experiments took place between 07h00 and 11h00 (Ackermann et al. 2020, Schradin 2006). All behaviors were scored from video recordings, and the observer (NP) was blind to all treatments.

Experiment 1. Anxiety and Exploration in a Novel Environment

Tests were conducted under fluorescent light (361 lux) in glass tank arenas (45 × 30 × 30 cm) with wire mesh lids. Tanks were layered with wood shavings, and between tests, they were cleaned, washed with warm soapy water and 70% alcohol, and allowed to air dry before new wood shavings were added.

All animals were tested twice on consecutive days to determine whether they habituated to the novel arena on the second day. On the two test days, the experimental mice were released into the center of the arena using a small, closed plastic carrier (15×10 cm), which was washed between tests. A video camera was mounted above the arena to video‐record the behavior of each mouse for 10 min. Ethovision XT Version 9 video‐tracking software (Noldus, the Netherlands; http://www.noldus.com) was used to create a 3 cm peripheral zone in the inner perimeter of the tank, and the following parameters were recorded: (i) the duration of time spent in the center and periphery of the arena; and (ii) the number of transitions between the periphery and the center and back. The first transition was not considered because the mice were introduced to the arena in the center. We assessed anxiety by analysing the difference between the time spent in the center and the periphery, and exploratory behavior as the number of transitions between the center and the periphery.

Experiment 2. Social Interaction

At approximately 80 days old, the social behavior of the mice was assessed through dyadic encounters. Each experimental (focal) juvenile was paired with a novel, unrelated, same‐sex conspecific from our colonies (N = 72 stimulus individuals; no animal served in more than one trial). Animals were matched for age (± 2 days) and body mass (± 2 g). because body size is a positive predictor of winning contests in striped mice (Schradin 2004).

Dyadic encounters took place in neutral glass tanks (60 × 30 × 35 cm) with perforated lids. Tanks were lined with a 1 cm deep layer of wood shavings. Each tank was divided by an opaque partition. The test and stimulus animals were randomly placed on either side of the partition for 5 min to acclimate to the arena. The barrier was removed, and the behavior of the dyad was video recorded for 15 min. The first minute of the recordings was discarded because of the disturbance of removing the barriers. One of us (NP) sat in the room to terminate dyads that engaged in damaging fights, but any aggression was mostly chases and occasionally boxes, and no animals were harmed in the study. After encounters, dyads were separated and returned to their home tanks, and the arena was cleaned with alcohol, soap, and air‐dried.

We recorded allogrooming, cumulative aggression (boxing, chasing, tumbling), an event count (true frequency), and while close body contact was scored as a state, and duration (s) were extracted; a detailed ethogram in Table S1. Behavior of the focal (test) mouse was scored from video recordings in Observer XT software (Zimmerman et al. 2009), using a continuous focal‐sampling rule for the entire 14 min encounter. A randomly selected 20% subsample (12 videos) was re‐scored by an independent observer; interobserver agreement was excellent (Cohen's κ = 0.87 across the five behavioral categories).

Experiment 3. Spatial Cognition Test

At approximately 90 days old, the spatial learning and memory ability of all three treatments was assessed in a spatial maze for eight consecutive days, followed three days later by a probe trial. We used a 5‐hole Barnes maze (1.2 m in diameter and 50 cm high) constructed of galvanized steel. The floor of the maze was covered with a 1 cm‐thin layer of wood shavings, and a transparent carrier could be placed in the middle of the maze. The five holes were equidistant apart and 2 cm from the bottom of the base along the walls of the maze. Each hole contained a short (3 cm) closed plastic tube placed on the outside of the maze. Sunflower seeds were used as an incentive and placed in one of the tubes during experiments. Five different landmarks (e.g., rock and plastic plants of about 10 cm height) were placed on the surface of the maze in fixed positions to serve as visual spatial cues. There were no external maze cues in the room apart from a single camera lens mounted directly above the maze and a seated observer who varied their position randomly outside the maze for each animal tested. Soiled bedding and feces were removed, and the wood shavings were tossed and raked between trials to redistribute the odor from earlier test subjects.

Mice were tested individually. None received sunflower seeds for 10 days before tests in their home cages (i.e., since the end of Experiment 2) and in their home cages for the duration of Experiment 3. Subjects underwent training for 5 days (days 1–5) in the maze, followed by 3 days of testing (days 6–8). On Days 1 and 2, mice were placed individually in the carrier in the middle of the maze facing north. They were released and given 5 min to explore and habituate to the maze. During this time, all holes were blocked with clear Perspex placed on the outside between the maze and the tubes. On Day 3, all holes were accessible, and all tubes were baited with two sunflower seeds. On Day 4, three randomly selected holes were baited with two sunflower seeds, and on Day 5, only one of the previously baited holes had seed. This hole became the permanently baited hole for a particular individual for the test phase (below). On each training day, trials were terminated when the mouse located the seeds in less than 5 min. If the mouse failed to find the seeds after 5 min, it was gently prodded toward the baited well. During the training phase, the sunflower seeds were placed in the front of the tube to encourage learning, whereas sunflower seeds were placed at the back of the tube during the test phase (below).

On Days 6 to 8 (test phase), mice were tested by changing the maze configuration daily in the following sequence: (i) the maze contained landmarks for navigation as on Day 5 (landmarks); (ii) the landmarks were removed (no landmarks); and (iii) the maze rotated by 100° so that the baited hole was moved but in same position relative to the landmarks (rotated). Each mouse was given three 3 min trials with 3 min intertrial intervals, and their latency to locate the baited hole was recorded. On Day 11, a single 3 min probe test was conducted to assess the memory of the previously baited hole in the same position as the test on Day 6 and including the landmarks. No bait was used during the probe test, so mice had no potential olfactory perception of the seeds. We recorded the latency (in seconds) to locate the previously baited hole, the time (in seconds) spent in proximity (within 3 cm) of the previously baited hole, and the number of other holes visited (errors) during the probe test before finding the baited hole.

Experiment 4. Maternal Behavior

At approximately 120 days of age, experimental females were paired with unrelated, age‐matched males from our colony that had been weaned between postnatal day 15 and 17 (mean ± SD = 15.9 ± 0.7 days). Breeding pairs were established and housed in glass tanks (46 cm × 30 cm × 32 cm), as described above. Pairs were provisioned with food and water, as described above.

At parturition, sires were removed from the tank and returned to the colony, and litter size was recorded. Maternal care was scored on PND 2, 4, and 6 for 30 min twice daily (10:00–12:00 h and 15:00–16:00 h), generating 3 h (10,800 s) of data per female. We video‐recorded the following behaviors (Schradin and Pillay 2003): Near—dam within <2 cm of any pup without physical contact; Huddling—dam's ventrum in contact with ≥50% of one or more pups (includes kyphotic and lateral nursing postures); and Groom pups—dam licks or sniffs any part of a pup. Because nursing could not always be distinguished from huddling, these actions were pooled under huddling (Rymer and Pillay 2012).

Behavior of the focal dam was scored from the videos in Observer XT (Zimmerman et al. 2009) using a continuous focal‐sampling rule for each 30 min session, and data were summed across the 3 observation days. Thirty‐six videos (20% of the dataset) were rescored blind by an independent observer; interobserver agreement was excellent (Cohen's κ = 0.91 across the three categories).

This study complied with the current laws and regulations in South Africa, and the research adhered to the ABS/ASAB guidelines for the ethical treatment of animals in research (Bee et al. 2020). Animals were provided with environmental enrichment, and the welfare of the animals was monitored daily. The experimental procedures did not have any obvious negative effects on the welfare of the striped mice. Dyads were carefully monitored to ensure that no animals were harmed. At the end of the study, all experimental animals were kept as part of the breeding stock of the colony. Experimental procedures were approved by the Animal Ethics Screening Committee of the University of the Witwatersrand (AESC 2010/55/2A, 2012/13/3, 2013/18/2A).

Statistical Analyses

Statistical analyses were performed in R 4.2.1 (R Core Team 2022). Generalized linear mixed models (GLMMs) were fitted using the glmmTMB package, which accommodates Gamma, Poisson, negative‐binomial, and beta families within a unified syntax and provides robust model diagnostics. No data points were excluded.

Before modeling, all outcome variable distributions were inspected graphically and via Shapiro–Wilk tests. Right‐skewed duration data (e.g., time in the arena center, body contact, huddling, and grooming) were modeled with a Gamma distribution (log link). Counts of events (center–periphery transitions, allogrooming episodes, aggressive acts, maze errors) were checked for overdispersion and zero‐inflation. Where the dispersion ratio exceeded 1.2 (performance::check_overdispersion), the negative‐binomial family was used with a log link; otherwise, a Poisson model was retained. Where visual or formal checks suggested excessive zeros, zero‐inflation parameters were considered. Latency data in the spatial maze, being right‐skewed, were also fitted using a Gamma family with a log link. Proportional outcomes (e.g., time spent near pups) were nonnormally distributed and modeled with a beta family and logit link.

Model diagnostics were assessed using the DHARMa package, including Q–Q plots for residuals, formal dispersion tests, and zero‐inflation tests, to verify the adequacy of model fit and the appropriateness of distributional choices. Where DHARMa indicated significant misspecification, the model structure was reconsidered.

All models incorporated the crossed random‐effects structure, individual identity nested within litter identity nested within weaning treatment, specified as (1 | Treatment/Lit_id/Rep), to account for the nonindependence of individuals from the same litter across treatments. Selected random effects were excluded where models became overparameterized. For repeated measurements within animals, a random slope for the relevant within‐subject factor (e.g., day of testing) was included. Fixed effects common to all models were weaning treatment (12, 16, or 20 days), sex, and their interaction; experiment‐specific factors were then added as outlined below.

For Experiment 1 (novel‐arena anxiety and exploration), the day of testing (Day 1 or 2) was included as an additional fixed effect, with a random slope of day within animal. Anxiety (center‐time) was modeled with a Gamma family; exploration (center–periphery transitions) with a negative‐binomial family. We replaced zero values with a small positive value (0.001) for the anxiety data, ensuring compatibility with the distributional assumption and enabling valid inference. For Experiment 2 (dyadic social interaction), no additional fixed factors were included. Allogrooming and aggression were modeled as negative‐binomial counts; body‐contact duration used a Gamma family. Mass was included as a covariate. For Experiment 3 (spatial cognition), maze condition (landmarks, no‐landmarks, rotated, probe) was included as a composite (phase) fixed effect with an animal‐specific random slope; errors were modeled as negative‐binomial, and latencies/durations as Gamma. For Experiment 4 (maternal care), grooming and huddling were analyzed as Gamma durations; the proportion of time near pups used a beta model.

All main effects and interactions were evaluated using Type III Wald chi‐square tests (car:Anova), which report χ2, degrees of freedom, and p‐values for each fixed effect and interaction in every model. When any fixed effect reached significance at α = 0.05, pairwise mean differences were obtained using Tukey‐adjusted contrasts in the emmeans package (Lenth et al. 2020).

For beta models, contrasts are reported on the original probability scale after back‐transformation.

Results

Experiment 1: Anxiety and Exploration in a Novel Environment

Time in Center—Anxiety

The time that striped mice spent in the center of the arena was significantly affected by the age at weaning (χ2 = 74.0, df = 2, p < 0.001), with 12D mice spending markedly less time in the center than either 16D or 20D mice (both post hoc p < 0.001). There was no significant difference between the 16D and 20D groups (p = 0.997). The day of testing also had a significant effect (χ2 = 73.7, df = 1, p = 0.007), with mice spending slightly less time in the center on the second day. Sex (χ2 = 1.45, df = 1, p = 0.23) and all interaction terms were not significant (see Table 1, Figure 1, Table S2).

Experiment 2: Social Interaction

Allogrooming

Age at weaning significantly influenced the frequency of allogrooming (χ2 = 17.21, df = 2, p < 0.001; Table 2). Mice weaned at 12D allo‐groomed significantly less frequently than those weaned at 16D (p = 0.009) or 20 D (p = 0.001; Table S3). The 16D and 20D groups did not differ significantly (p = 0.990). No clear sex difference in allogrooming was detected when accounting for body mass and treatment (χ2 = 0.33, df = 1, p = 0.570). The interaction between age at weaning and sex was not significant (Table 2).

Body Contact

Body contact increased dramatically with age at weaning (χ2 = 90.69, df = 2, p < 0.001). The 12D mice showed almost no body contact, significantly less compared with the 16D (p < 0.001) and 20D mice (p < 0.001). There was no difference in the duration of body contact between the 16D and 20D mice (p = 0.998; Figure 3B). There was no overall sex difference in the duration of body contact (χ2 = 1.32, df = 1, p = .250). The interaction between sex and age was not significant (χ2 = 2.20, df = 2, p = .330, Table 2, Figure 3B, Table S3).

Aggression

Aggression increased significantly with earlier age at weaning (χ2 = 351.13, df = 2, p < 0.001). Mice weaned at 12 days exhibited substantially higher aggression compared with those weaned at 16 days (p < 0.001) and 20 days (p < 0.001), and animals weaned at 16 days were more aggressive than those weaned at 20 days (p < 0.001). Males were markedly more aggressive than females (χ2 = 116.42, df = 1, p <.001). There was no significant interaction between sex and age at weaning (χ2 = 1.32, df = 2, p = 0.518; Table 2; Figure 3C; Table S3).

Discussion

We studied the consequences of early weaning in a wild‐derived solitary rodent. Removing pups three days before the average weaning age resulted in a stress phenotype including heightened anxiety, reduced exploration, impaired social affiliation, increased aggression, and impaired maternal care. However, surprisingly, spatial cognition was unchanged. These findings highlight how life‐history strategy may buffer certain cognitive functions but create persistent emotional and social susceptibilities.

Early weaning is stressful for animals, and therefore, most of the effects that emerge in animals that are weaned early are stress‐related. Early weaning results in elevated corticosterone, reduced hippocampal GR density, and lowered BDNF, especially in males (Kikusui et al. 2008; Kikusui and Mori 2009). These endocrine changes concur with our behavioral anxiety data and are expected to weaken negative feedback on the HPA axis, creating hyperreactivity. Differences in levels of mother‐infant interactions can alter gene expression that can affect a range of behavioral and cognitive aspects later in life, including stress reactivity, social and reproductive behaviors, learning and memory, and reward‐mediated behaviors (Curley et al. 2009; Lehmann and Feldon 2000; Lippmann et al. 2007; Meaney 2001). Early life stress, such as insufficient maternal care, can induce long‐lasting behavioral deficits in humans and other mammals (Caldji et al. 1998; Carlyle et al. 2012; Chin et al. 2022).

Early weaning also down‐regulates oxytocin and vasopressin (Curley et al. 2009), peptides that synergistically promote social approach and modulate sensorimotor gating (Nelson and Panksepp 1998; Bailoo et al. 2020). While we did not directly measure these peptides or their downstream effects on prepulse inhibition (Huber et al. 2005; Li et al. 2002; Bailoo et al. 2020), the reduced allogrooming and heightened aggression we observed are consistent with suppressed oxytocinergic signaling. These early alterations in the neurochemicals persist into adulthood and can affect the anxiety and social behaviors of the animals.

Rodents frequently freeze or remain immobile when introduced into novel environments, or they display thigmotactic behavior (remain on the sides of an arena) (Ganella and Kim 2014; Riebe and Wotjak 2012). Freezing and thigmotaxis are typically associated with anxiety (Simon et al. 1994). Maternal care in neonatal rats appears to direct their behavioral responses to stress, such that high care lowers the fear response in offspring (Caldji et al. 1998). When maternal care is removed early, offspring responses to stress correspond to or exceed the fear responses of low maternal care offspring. Typically, early‐weaned pups display increased fearfulness, and this effect is long‐lasting (Kikusui et al. 2004; Kikusui and Mori 2009).

We found that early‐weaned four‐striped mice were more anxious compared with their littermates that were weaned later, regardless of sex. In contrast, sex differences were apparent in early‐weaned laboratory rats, where males showed increased anxiety levels compared with females (Ito et al. 2006, Kikusui et al. 2009). Exploratory behavior plays a significant role in foraging and predator avoidance (Russell et al. 2010). Faster exploration and lower anxiety provide animals with significant adaptive advantages by locating resources better, leading to improved survival and reproductive success. Exploratory behavior is linked to the development of cognition, social, and motor skills (Baxter and Switzky 2008) but is attenuated by anxiety (Heinz et al. 2021). The reduction in exploration and time in the center of an open arena in 12D striped mice compared with those weaned later therefore reflects a combined anxiety response, consistent with laboratory evidence that later weaning increases exploratory drive (Curley et al. 2009). Unlike some laboratory mouse strains (Ito et al. 2006; Kikusui et al. 2009), we did not detect sex differences in anxiety, possibly because R. dilectus shows limited sexual size dimorphism and baseline HPA divergence.

The timing of weaning can significantly affect social behavior in animals. Typically, rodent pups that are weaned later engage in more positive social interactions compared with pups that were weaned earlier (Curley et al. 2009). Early weaning tends to lead to higher anxiety levels (Caldji et al. 1998) that can negatively impact their willingness to engage in social activities such as playing and grooming (Shimozuru et al. 2007). Allogrooming and body contact reduce stress and reinforce social bonds (Yates et al. 2022). Both behaviors declined in 12D striped mice, while aggression increased, creating a socio‐affective outcome typical of oxytocin‐suppressed mammals. Similar outcomes occur in hand‐reared compared with mother‐reared macaques (Macaca mulatta) (Winslow et al. 2003) and early‐weaned piglets, cats, and mice (Ahola et al. 2017; Jarvis et al. 2008; Kikusui et al. 2004). Whether these differences in social response result in lower adult fitness through reduced social tolerance and increased injury warrants field testing.

Early‐weaned striped mouse females had similar litter sizes to females that were weaned later, but they showed behavioral differences. Early‐weaned mothers groomed and huddled less. They spent more time in proximity to the pups but not actually touching them. Since anxiety and behavioral changes persist into adulthood in early‐weaned mothers, altered maternal behavior can be expected. Oxytocin and vasopressin are involved in social behaviors, maternal care, and bonding (Bosch 2013), and these two peptides are reduced in early‐weaned mice (Curley et al. 2009). These effects also extend to maternal behavior, which seems to be strongly affected by the early life environment of the mother (D.D. Francis et al. 1999, D. Francis et al. 1999, Rymer and Pillay 2013). Early‐weaned rats show a reduction in nest building (Nováková 1975) and less consistent maternal behavior by performing less grooming and licking of their pups (D.D. Francis et al. 1999, D. Francis et al. 1999; Kikusui and Mori 2009). Early‐weaned mothers may transmit stress phenotypes to offspring, a possibility that could be tested in the future using cross‐fostering studies.

Spatial learning and memory were surprisingly unaffected by early weaning. This was unexpected given the data from the other experiments and the extensive evidence that chronic early stress impairs hippocampal‐dependent tasks (Kosten et al. 2012). Research on early‐life manipulations reports variable effects on learning and memory, including impairment, enhancement, or no effect (Kosten et al. 2012). The outcome depends on stressor duration and timing: short‐term stressors may enhance memory, whereas chronic stressors impair learning, particularly when administered during the first two postnatal weeks (Kosten et al. 2012). Learning and memory are also influenced by early life experiences, such as nutritional deficits (Nováková et al. 1962) and stress (Mackay et al. 2014).

Our null result for spatial cognition contrasts with the clear anxiety and social deficits, suggesting domain‐specific resilience or buffering. Two mutually nonexclusive mechanisms could explain this: (i) early‐weaned pups began solid feeding sooner, potentially improving energy gain (Abou Ward et al. 2008) and enhancing cognitive development; (ii) as a solitary species, R. dilectus shows attenuated stress responses to maternal separation compared with group‐living congeners (Mackay et al. 2014), suggesting life‐history‐specific neuroprotection for cognitive functions essential to solitary navigation. Life‐history buffering could ameliorate stress effects on spatial phenotypes essential for solitary navigation. However, these mechanisms remain speculative and require direct neurobiological and nutritional assays to validate.

A confounding issue is the staged‐removal design (removing one male and one female at three ages). This standardized disturbance across litters but potentially imposed stress on remaining siblings, though litter identity explained <5% of the variance. Whole‐litter manipulations at single ages would enable us to disentangle the effects of early weaning from other potential explanations (e.g., changes in mother‐offspring social dynamics following litter size reduction) for the observed phenotype.

For solitary R. dilectus, we predict early‐weaned individuals will: (i) select safer microhabitats and curtail exploratory forays; (ii) show reduced social tolerance in territorial encounters; (iii) provide less direct maternal investment (grooming/huddling); and (iv) delay dispersal or make shorter movements postindependence. Whether these behavioral alterations under controlled settings reduce fitness requires validation in natural settings to test the demographic consequences of the behavioral changes studied here.

Studying wild‐derived species under controlled conditions bridges laboratory neuroscience and behavioral ecology. Our results demonstrate that even brief (3‐day) earlier weaning can modify behavioral phenotypes in ways consistent with HPA and neuropeptide dysregulation documented in laboratory strains. However, free‐living animals experience more variable weaning contexts; R. dilectus pups may leave nests as early as PND 10 (pers. obs.), suggesting natural populations may exhibit resilience or context‐dependent compensation. Moreover, we lacked endocrine assays to quantify corticosterone, glucocorticoid receptors, oxytocin, and vasopressin to link behavior and neurochemistry directly. These should be considered in future studies and also include hippocampal and amygdala assessments to explain the cognition‐emotion dissociation.

Conclusions

Early weaning in R. dilectus resulted in a stress phenotype affecting a suite of behaviors involving anxiety, exploration, social behavior, and maternal care, while spatial cognition remained intact. These findings highlight how life‐history strategy may buffer certain cognitive functions but create persistent emotional and social susceptibilities that are of ecological relevance. Although precocious weaning did not affect cognition, R. dilectus chakae is likely to be anxious and poor mothers, which could also impact the fitness of their offspring. These effects manifested in behaviors of ecological significance, suggesting that early weaning may influence the fitness of the four‐striped mouse.
