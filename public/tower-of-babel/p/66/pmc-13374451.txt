Association between Helicobacter pylori infection and colonic diverticulosis: a retrospective observational study in an asymptomatic Austrian endoscopy-based screening cohort
Authors: Andreas Völkerer, Sarah Wernly, Georg Semmler, Maria Flamm, Mathias Ausserwinkler, Jutta Horejs-Hoeck, Anabel Berger, Hannah Hofer, Elmar Aigner, Christian Datz, Bernhard Wernly
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveColonic diverticulosis is highly prevalent in ageing populations, yet the role of microbial factors remains unclear. Helicobacter pylori (HP) exerts systemic and microbiome-modulating effects and has been linked to extragastric inflammatory states, rendering an association with diverticulosis biologically plausible. However, this hypothesis has not been examined in asymptomatic screening populations with simultaneous endoscopic diverticulosis classification and histological HP confirmation. This study aimed to investigate whether HP infection is associated with colonic diverticulosis in an asymptomatic Austrian screening cohort.MethodsWe performed a retrospective observational study of 5646 asymptomatic adults undergoing screening colonoscopy with concurrent oesophagogastroduodenoscopy (2007–2020). Diverticulosis was classified endoscopically by distribution; HP status was determined histologically from gastric biopsies. Multivariable logistic regression adjusted for metabolic, sociodemographic and lifestyle factors. A subset with follow-up colonoscopy (n=510) was analysed descriptively.ResultsDiverticulosis was present in 37% of participants and HP infection in 19%. No association was found between HP infection and diverticulosis in univariable (OR 1.01, 95% CI 0.88 to 1.15; p=0.935) or fully adjusted analysis (OR 0.86, 95% CI 0.72 to 1.02; p=0.078), with an effect size too small to be clinically meaningful. Subgroup analyses by diverticular distribution and interaction analyses across age, sex, metabolic syndrome and lifestyle factors showed consistent null results. In the longitudinal subset, diverticulosis prevalence at follow-up did not differ by baseline HP status (63.9% vs 62.1%; p=0.749).ConclusionsIn this large screening cohort with simultaneous endoscopic and histological confirmation, HP infection was not associated with colonic diverticulosis across subtypes or risk strata. These findings do not support HP status as a clinically useful marker of asymptomatic diverticulosis. In future microbiome-focused studies of diverticulosis, HP infection may be best regarded as a potential confounder or marker of broader host, socioeconomic or healthcare-related factors.Keywords: DIVERTICULAR DISEASE, HELICOBACTER PYLORI, GASTROINTESINAL ENDOSCOPY

Introduction

Colonic diverticulosis is one of the most common findings in the ageing gastrointestinal tract. Prevalence rises from approximately 10% in adults under 40 years to 50%–70% in those older than 80 years.1 Although most individuals remain asymptomatic, diverticulosis carries substantial clinical and economic burden because of its potential progression to diverticulitis, bleeding, perforation or chronic symptoms.2,4 The pathogenesis is incompletely understood. Established risk factors include advanced age, genetic predisposition, connective-tissue weakness and cardiometabolic factors such as obesity and hypertension.5,8 Emerging evidence points to a role of the gut microbiome in both diverticula formation and the transition from asymptomatic diverticulosis to symptomatic diverticular disease.9,12 Low-grade mucosal inflammation has been proposed as a linking mechanism, although recent rigorous studies question its relevance in uncomplicated diverticulosis.13 14 Helicobacter pylori (HP) is a highly prevalent gastric pathogen that colonises approximately 43% of the global population.15,17 Beyond its well-established role in peptic ulcer disease and gastric cancer, HP infection has been associated with systemic effects including alterations of the intestinal microbiome, low-grade inflammation and metabolic dysregulation.18,22 These properties make an association with colonic diverticulosis biologically plausible. However, existing data are sparse, heterogeneous and conflicting—with one large registry study reporting an inverse association that did not persist longitudinally,23 and a smaller histology-based study reporting a non-significant positive trend.24

Previous studies have almost exclusively examined symptomatic diverticular disease or diagnosis-coded endpoints, often in small or retrospective cohorts, and rarely used histological confirmation of HP status. Critically, asymptomatic diverticulosis detected at screening colonoscopy represents an earlier stage in the disease spectrum and should be distinguished from clinically manifest diverticular disease. To our knowledge, no study has combined endoscopic diverticulosis classification with simultaneous histological HP confirmation in a large asymptomatic screening population—the methodological approach best suited to examine this association without the confounding effects of symptoms, healthcare utilisation or diagnostic coding.

We therefore performed a large endoscopy-based cohort study to investigate the association between histologically confirmed HP infection and endoscopically detected colonic diverticulosis in asymptomatic individuals undergoing colorectal cancer screening. We adjusted comprehensively for metabolic, sociodemographic and lifestyle confounders and performed prespecified subgroup and interaction analyses. In a subset with repeat colonoscopy, we also explored longitudinal associations. Based on the proposed biological mechanisms linking HP to systemic inflammation and microbiome disruption, we hypothesised that HP infection would be associated with higher odds of diverticulosis; the present study was designed to test this hypothesis.

Methods

Study design and population

This was a single-centre, retrospective observational cohort study using data from the Salzburger Kolon-Karzinom Prävention und Intervention (SAKKOPI) registry, which prospectively collects information on individuals undergoing colorectal cancer screening in Salzburg, Austria. Between January 2007 and March 2020, participants in the SAKKOPI study project were offered optional same-day oesophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD) in addition to screening colonoscopy as part of the local study protocol. Same-day EGD was a study-specific procedure and not part of routine colorectal cancer screening practice in Austria. Screening was available from age 45 and fully covered by health insurance. Participants were recruited through primary care referrals or voluntary enrolment in an opportunistic screening programme. Of 6429 individuals who underwent one to three screening examinations and provided written informed consent for research use of their data, we excluded those with colorectal cancer-related symptoms, incomplete clinical or laboratory data, inadequate questionnaire responses or endoscopies that did not meet international quality standards. The final study population comprised 5646 asymptomatic adults. The selection process is shown in figure 1. This cohort has been described in detail previously.25 Reporting followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) recommendations for observational studies; the completed checklist is provided as online supplemental file 1.

Flow diagram of patient enrolment. H.p., Helicobacter pylori.

Clinical assessment

Participants underwent a standardised 2-day evaluation. On day 1, vital signs, medical history and laboratory tests were obtained. On day 2, EGD and colonoscopy were performed by experienced endoscopists under conscious sedation with propofol, with or without midazolam. All patients completed a standardised questionnaire covering medical history, family history, lifestyle factors and medication use (including proton pump inhibitors (PPI)). Body mass index was calculated according to WHO criteria. Arterial hypertension was defined per European Society of Cardiology guidelines.26 Metabolic syndrome was diagnosed according to the joint consensus criteria of the International Diabetes Federation, American Heart Association and National Heart, Lung and Blood Institute.27 Smoking status was categorised as never, former or current smoker.

Assessment of colonic diverticulosis

Colonoscopies were performed according to international quality guidelines.28 Diverticulosis was recorded as present or absent and classified by distribution as left-sided, right-sided or pandiverticulosis (involvement of both sides).

Assessment of HP infection

HP status was determined by histological examination of gastric biopsies obtained during EGD. Biopsies were taken from both the antrum and corpus according to standardised protocols and evaluated by experienced gastrointestinal pathologists using established diagnostic criteria.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as median with IQR or mean±SD, as appropriate. Categorical variables are reported as frequencies and percentages. Group comparisons were performed using the Mann-Whitney U test, Student’s t-test or χ² test as appropriate.

The primary outcome was the presence of colonic diverticulosis (binary). We used multivariable logistic regression models with robust standard errors to calculate ORs and 95% CIs. Model 1 was unadjusted. Model 2 was adjusted for age, sex and metabolic syndrome. Model 3 was fully adjusted for individual metabolic syndrome components (abdominal obesity, triglycerides, high-density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol, hypertension and fasting glucose) plus sociodemographic and lifestyle factors (education, diet quality, alcohol consumption, smoking status and PPI use).

Prespecified interaction analyses were performed to assess effect modification by age, sex, metabolic syndrome components, education, diet quality, alcohol intake, smoking and PPI use. Multinomial logistic regression was used to evaluate associations with diverticulosis distribution (left-sided, right-sided, pandiverticulosis), using no diverticulosis as the reference category.

In a retrospective longitudinal subgroup with follow-up colonoscopy data (n=510), associations between baseline HP status and diverticulosis at follow-up were examined descriptively using χ² tests. No multivariable modelling was performed in this subset due to sample size. All analyses were conducted with StataNow V.18.5. A two-sided p<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Study population

The study included 5646 asymptomatic adults undergoing screening colonoscopy with concurrent EGD. Of these, 4574 (81%) were HP negative and 1072 (19%) were HP positive by histology. The median age was 58 years (IQR 52–66), and 52% were male. Participants with HP infection were slightly older (median 59 vs 58 years) and showed modestly higher metabolic risk: higher body mass index (27 vs 26 kg/m²), greater prevalence of overweight/obesity (86% vs 80%), abdominal obesity (57% vs 52%) and metabolic syndrome (46% vs 40%; all p≤0.001). Lifestyle factors differed only marginally, although HP-positive individuals more frequently reported higher alcohol intake (≥2 drinks/day: 16% vs 13%) and lower educational attainment (43% vs 37%). Prevalence of colonic diverticulosis and association with HP infection

Colonic diverticulosis was present in 37% of the overall cohort. There was no difference in crude prevalence between HP-negative and HP-positive participants (p=0.23; figure 2). In univariable logistic regression, HP infection was not associated with diverticulosis (OR 1.01, 95% CI 0.88 to 1.15; p=0.935). This null association persisted after adjustment for age, sex and metabolic syndrome (model 2: OR 0.93, 95% CI 0.80 to 1.07; p=0.323) and in the fully adjusted model accounting for individual metabolic syndrome components plus sociodemographic and lifestyle factors (model 3: OR 0.86, 95% CI 0.72 to 1.02; p=0.078).

Effect modification

Prespecified interaction analyses revealed no evidence of effect modification by age, sex, metabolic syndrome or its individual components (all interaction p>0.24). Likewise, no significant interactions were observed for education, diet quality, alcohol consumption or PPI use. Exploratory longitudinal analysis

In a subset of 510 participants with repeat endoscopy (median interval, 114 months), baseline HP status was not associated with diverticulosis at follow-up (63.9% in HP-positive vs 62.1% in HP-negative; p=0.749). Among the 192 participants with diverticulosis already present at baseline, the incidence of diverticulitis during follow-up was low and did not differ by baseline HP status (p=0.821). Information on HP eradication therapy was available for only a minority of participants.

Discussion

In this large endoscopy-based screening cohort of 5646 asymptomatic adults, histologically confirmed HP infection was not associated with the presence of colonic diverticulosis. The null finding was consistent across unadjusted and fully adjusted models, across anatomical distributions of diverticula, and across all prespecified subgroups and interaction analyses. Exploratory longitudinal data from 510 participants with repeat colonoscopy corroborated the absence of any association between baseline HP status and diverticulosis at follow-up.

These results argue against a clinically meaningful role of HP infection in the formation or persistence of asymptomatic colonic diverticulosis. Although HP exerts systemic effects on inflammation and the intestinal microbiome that could theoretically influence colonic wall integrity, the present data provide no support for such a link in the earliest, preclinical stage of disease.

Previous studies on this topic have yielded conflicting results, largely because of important methodological differences. Kuloglu et al reported a numerically higher HP prevalence in patients with diverticulosis, but the analysis was small, retrospective, and lacked adjustment for confounders.24 In contrast, the large Danish registry study by Bartels et al found a lower prevalence of coded diverticular disease among HP-positive individuals; however, that association was based on hospital diagnosis codes (predominantly symptomatic cases) and disappeared after eradication, and the cohort was substantially younger than ours.23 Our study specifically examined endoscopically confirmed, asymptomatic diverticulosis in an older screening population and used histological confirmation of HP status—features that distinguish it from prior work and make the null result particularly informative.

The strengths of this study include its large size, standardised endoscopic phenotyping, histological verification of HP infection, and comprehensive adjustment for metabolic, sociodemographic and lifestyle factors. The availability of follow-up colonoscopy data, although in a smaller subset, adds a longitudinal dimension rarely available in this field.

Several limitations should be considered. First, the retrospective design precludes causal inference. Second, although the overall cohort was well-characterised, the follow-up subgroup may have been subject to selection bias. Third, PPI use was more frequent among participants classified as HP negative and may have reduced the sensitivity of gastric biopsy-based HP detection, potentially leading to misclassification of HP status and attenuation of a true association. Fourth, histological HP status reflected current detectable infection rather than lifetime HP exposure. Reliable information on previous HP infection and eradication therapy was not available for the full cohort; therefore, participants with previously eradicated HP may have been classified as HP negative, which could have attenuated associations if prior HP exposure has persistent microbiome-related effects. Finally, the study population consisted mainly of Central European adults, and strain-specific virulence factors (such as CagA or VacA) were not assessed.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this large, well-characterised screening cohort shows no association between HP infection and colonic diverticulosis. HP status does not appear to be a useful clinical marker or modifiable risk factor for asymptomatic diverticula formation. In future microbiome studies of diverticular disease, HP infection may be more relevant as a potential confounder or surrogate marker of broader host and socioeconomic factors than as a direct pathogenic driver.
