Gut microbiota in diarrheic and healthy calves: comparative analysis of 16S rRNA gene full-length sequences
Authors: Kyung-Hyo Do, Da-Hye Ryu, Hyun-Jung Ahn, Min-Gyu Kim, Su-Jin Choe, Seung-Hun Lee, Sang-Myeong Lee, Kwang-Won Seo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNeonatal calf diarrhea (NCD) is a leading cause of morbidity and mortality in the dairy industry. Although multiple pathogens have been identified, widespread alterations in gut microbiota composition associated with diarrhea remain poorly understood. This study aimed to characterize the fecal microbiota and potential metabolic pathways in diarrheic and healthy neonatal calves using 16S rRNA gene full-length sequencing. A total of 40 fecal samples (20 normal and 20 diarrheic) were collected, and 16S rRNA gene full-length sequencing was performed.ResultsAlpha diversity measures (Chao1, Shannon, and Simpson indices) were significantly reduced in diarrheic calves (p < 0.01), indicating marked dysbiosis. Principal coordinate analyses, a beta diversity method used to assess difference in microbial composition between groups, revealed distinct clustering of diarrheic versus healthy samples based on both Bray–Curtis and Jensen–Shannon divergences, underscoring a major compositional shift. Taxonomic profiling showed elevated levels of opportunistic/pathogenic bacteria (e.g., Escherichia coli, Salmonella spp., Klebsiella spp.) in diarrheic calves, while beneficial taxa (e.g., Faecalibacterium) were depleted. Functional predictions indicated increased carbohydrate metabolism, notably the N-acetylneuraminate degradation, Entner–Doudoroff, and hexitol fermentation pathways, in diarrheic calves (all adjusted p < 0.05). These findings highlight multifaceted dysbiosis in NCD, including both taxonomic imbalances and heightened carbohydrate metabolic pathways.ConclusionsIdentifying key bacterial species and metabolic signatures may facilitate the development of diagnostic biomarkers and targeted microbiome-based therapies. Conclusively, the findings emphasize the prospective efficacy of interventions aimed at restoring microbial diversity and metabolic balance to mitigate illness severity and enhance calf health.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12917-025-05094-z.Keywords: Neonatal calf diarrhea, Gut microbiota, Dysbiosis, 16S rRNA gene full-length sequencing, Metabolic pathway analysis

Background

Neonatal calf diarrhea (NCD) is the leading cause of mortality in newborn calves [1]. Calves often experience reduced growth after recovering from diarrhea, leading to substantial economic losses [2]. NCD is characterized by frequent, watery, and irregular bowel movements [1], and is primarily caused by infectious agents, including Escherichia coli and Salmonella spp., bovine rotavirus, coronavirus, bovine viral diarrhea virus, and parasites such as Cryptosporidium, Giardia, and Eimeria spp. [3]. Although non-infectious factors such as suboptimal nutrition, environmental stress, inadequate hygiene, and poor management practices may also play a role [3], extensive research has identified multiple bacteria, viruses, and parasites as key contributors to NCD [4–6].

Recent evidence suggests the important role of the gut microbiota in NCD pathogenesis [2, 3]. Disruption or dysbiosis of gut microbiota can impair microbial functions, including nucleotide transport, metabolism, and immune defense [3]. During early development in ruminants, the microbiota colonizes in a sequential manner from birth to weaning [7–9], significantly influencing their health and physiology [10]. Consequently, maintaining a balanced microbiome during early calf development is crucial for health and productivity. Calves with diarrhea exhibit reduced diversity and notable shifts in fecal microbial composition [11], including a decline in beneficial bacteria that are generally associated with protection against diarrhea [11, 12]. However, most previous studies have relied on short-read sequencing or limited genus-level analysis. Here, we applied 16S rRNA gene full-length sequencing and PICRUSt2-based functional inference to comprehensively profile both the taxonomic and metabolic alterations associated with NCD. This integrative approach enables higher-resolution taxonomic assignment and highlights functional disruptions, such as enhanced carbohydrate degradation capacity, that may contribute to disease pathophysiology. Therefore, a more comprehensive approach is imperative to elucidate the full spectrum of dysbiosis in diarrheic calves.

Previous investigations have mostly emphasized specific pathogens associated with NCD [4, 13, 14], with few studies comprehensively comparing the entire fecal microbiota of diarrheic and healthy calves. Understanding how extensive microbial compositional changes influence NCD pathogenesis is essential for identifying potential biomarkers and providing appropriate therapeutic interventions. Therefore, this study primarily aimed to profile the fecal bacterial microbiota in both healthy and diarrheic calves using 16S rRNA gene full-length sequencing and analyze the potential functional pathways between healthy and diarrheic calves to clarify the impact of diarrhea on the neonatal calf gut ecosystem. This study offers valuable insights for future research on diagnostic biomarkers and microbiome-based therapies to improve overall calf health.

Materials and methods

Sample collection

A total of 40 fecal samples (20 normal and 20 diarrheic) were collected in 2024 from neonatal calves (Hanwoo, Korean native beef cattle) aged 2 days to 2 months, the period commonly defined in veterinary medicine as the neonatal stage and recognized as the critical window for the onset of NCD, on local farms in Korea [2]. The sampling dates and geographic information of the enrolled farms are provided in the Supplementary Table 1. Fecal consistency was assessed and categorized as either normal or diarrheic by a single veterinarian, based on standardized criteria including fecal form, texture, and hydration. Diarrheic feces were defined as unformed, loose to watery stools, consistent with previously described [1]. During routine veterinary examinations or daily health checks, fecal samples were collected for diagnostic or observational purposes. The remaining samples were subsequently collected in sterile containers and used for this study. Calves treated with antibiotics within the previous two weeks were excluded. All samples were obtained at the time of veterinary diagnosis, immediately after the onset of diarrhea was observed, and before any treatments (e.g., antibiotics, anti-diarrheal, or supportive therapy). After collection, all samples were transported on ice packs to the laboratory within 24 h and stored at ˗70°C until further processing.

Antigen detection in calf feces

Rectal swab samples were tested for bovine rotavirus, bovine coronavirus, Cryptosporidium, Giardia, and Escherichia coli K99 using an Antigen Rapid BoviD-5 Ag rapid test kit (Bionote, Inc., Hwaseong, Korea) according to the manufacturer’s protocol, and only calves that tested negative were included in the study.

DNA extraction

Bacterial DNA was extracted from 200 mg of fecal sample using the Maxwell RSC Fecal Microbiome DNA Kit AS1700 (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Thereafter, the DNA quality was assayed using a Qubit dsDNA HS assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) on a Qubit fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) following the manufacturer’s protocol.

16S rRNA gene full-length sequencing

The bacterial 16S rRNA full-length region (V1–V9) was amplified using the primers modified from a previous study [15], which comprised 3ʹ specific target sequences and 5ʹ nanopore adaptors, as follows: 16S_27F 5ʹ-AGAGTTTGATCMTGGCTCAG-3ʹ and 16S_1492R 5ʹ-TACGGYTACCTTGTTACGACTT-3ʹ. The polymerase chain reaction (PCR) mixture of 25 μL comprised 10 ng of DNA, 0.1 μM of each primer, and KAPA HiFi HotStart ReadyMix (KAPA Biosystems, Wilmington, MA, USA). The PCR cycle comprised an initial denaturation step at 95°C for 3 min; 30 cycles at 98°C for 20 s, 58°C for 15 s, and 72°C for 30; and a final extension at 72°C for 1 min. The resulting amplicons were purified using AMPure XP beads (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA) and a Native Barcoding Kit 96 V14 (SQK-NBD 114.96; Oxford Nanopore Technologies, Oxford, UK) following the manufacturer’s protocol. DNA concentrations were measured using the Qubit dsDNA HS assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with a Qubit fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Barcoding was performed using the Native Barcoding Kit 96V14 (SQK-NBD 114.96; Oxford Nanopore Technologies). In each 20 µL reaction, 7.5 µL of the first clean-up PCR product was mixed with 2.5 µL of the corresponding native barcode and 10 µL of NEB Blunt/TA Ligase Master Mix (New England Biolabs, MA, USA). The mixture was then gently flicked, briefly centrifuged, and incubated for 20 min at room temperature. Ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid was added to stop the ligation reaction, and all barcoded samples were pooled into a single low-bind tube. The pooled barcoded library was bead-purified and quantified as described above. Adapters were then ligated using the NEB Quick Ligation Module and Native Barcoding Kit 96V14 (SQK-NBD 114.96, Oxford Nanopore Technologies) following the manufacturer’s protocol. The samples were again bead-purified to remove excess adapters and potential contaminants. The adapter-ligated library was gently flicked every 2 min during a 10 min incubation at 37°C to ensure optimal DNA recovery. Following the magnetic separation of the beads, the supernatant containing the final library was transferred to a fresh low-bind tube. Next, 1 µL of the resulting library was quantified using the Qubit dsDNA HS assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and Qubit fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). A total of 50 fmol of the final library was sequenced on an R10.4.1 flow cell (FLO-MIN114, Oxford Nanopore Technologies) using a MinION Mk1B sequencer (Oxford Nanopore Technologies).

Bioinformatic Analysis of 16S rRNA gene full-length sequencing

The Pod5 output files from Nanopore sequencing were base-called using MinKNOW basecaller v24.11.8 (Oxford Nanopore Technologies) in the high-accuracy mode. The quality of the fastq sequences was examined using FastQC [16]. The fastq reads then were demultiplexed and adaptor-trimmed using Porechop v0.2.4 (https://github.com/rrwick/Porechop). Filtlong v0.2.0 (https://github.com/rrwick/Filtlong) was subsequently used to remove reads with an average Q-score < 30 and discard any reads > 1,700 bp. The resulting filtered reads were used for downstream read clustering and polishing. Taxonomic classification was performed based on the BLASTn algorithm using reference sequences retrieved from the National Center for Biotechnology Information database [17]. The bacterial abundances were then analyzed using Kraken2 with Bracken [18] to generate a collapsed taxa table.

MicrobiomeAnalyst was used to analyze the alpha and beta diversities of the microbiota in the two groups [19]. Alpha diversity was assessed using three different indices—Chao1, Shannon, and Simpson indices—to evaluate the richness and evenness of fecal microbiota in neonatal calves. Beta diversity was assessed using principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) based on both Bray–Curtis and Jensen-Shannon divergence (JSD) matrices to visualize compositional differences between groups. For each distance metric, PERMANOVA (Permutational Multivariate Analysis of Variance) was conducted using the adonis2 function (Vegen R package, v.2.6–4) with 999 permutations to test statistical significance of group differences.

Differential abundance analysis was performed using the linear discriminant analysis (LDA) effect size (LEfSe) method (LDA score > 2 and p < 0.05) on the Galaxy web server (https://huttenhower.sph.harvard.edu/galaxy/) [20]. Functional inference analysis of the polished representative sequence of clusters derived from Filtlong was performed using PICRUSt2 [21]. The abundances of predicted pathways based on MetaCyc annotation [22] were statistically tested using an independent t-test to compare pathway abundance between the normal and diarrheic calf groups.

Results

Alpha diversity

The violin plots in Fig. 1 show the distributions of the Chao1, Shannon, and Simpson indices in the diarrheic and normal groups. Compared with the normal group, the diarrheic group exhibited significantly lower alpha diversities for all three indices (Chao1, Shannon, and Simpson indices; all p < 0.01).

Comparisons of alpha diversity between diarrheic (orange) and normal (blue) calf groups. A Chao1 index, (B) Shannon index, and (C) Simpson index. Each violin plot shows the distribution of index values, with the boxplots indicating the medians, interquartile ranges, and individual data points. Statistical significance was indicated as ** (p < 0.01)

Beta diversity

In the Bray–Curtis-based PCoA (Fig. 2A), axes 1 and 2 accounted for 22.5% and 12.2% of the total variance in the distance matrix, respectively. Samples from the normal group generally had lower (more negative) values on axis 1, whereas those from the diarrheic group exhibited higher (more positive) values, resulting in visually separate clusters. PERMANOVA based on Bray–Curtis distances confirmed that these visual differences were statistically significant (pseudo-F = 5.09, R2 = 0.118, p-value = 0.001). In the Jensen–Shannon divergence-based PCoA (Fig. 2B), axes 1 and 2 accounted for 32.6% and 15.4% of the total variance in the distance matrix, respectively. The diarrheic group mainly clustered toward positive axis 1 values, whereas the normal group clustered toward negative axis 1 values. The 95% confidence ellipses showed distinct centroids for the two groups in both plots, indicating that despite a partial overlap, the overall community composition differed in diarrheic and normal groups, as represented by the first two principal coordinates. This clustering pattern was statistically supported by PERMANOVA based on the Jensen-Shannon divergence (pseudo-F = 7.61, R2 = 0.167, p-value = 0.001).

Principal coordinates analysis (PCoA) plots of the fecal microbiota in diarrheic and normal calf groups. Orange and blue indicate diarrheic and normal calf groups, respectively. A PCoA based on Bray–Curtis distances; (B) PCoA based on Jensen–Shannon divergence. Dashed ellipses: 95% confidence intervals for each group

Taxonomy abundance

Overall, the bar plots with individual data points (Fig. 3) revealed variations in median and average relative abundances across multiple phyla, genera, and species when comparing normal and diarrheic calf fecal samples. Figure 3A (phylum level) shows that Bacillota and Bacteroidota were dominant across both normal and diarrheic calves. However, Bacteroidota exhibited a notably lower median and average abundance in the diarrheic group (23.2% and 24.7%, respectively) than in the normal group (40.9% and 41.1%). In contrast, Pseudomonadota was substantially higher in diarrheic calves (median: 28.6%; average: 27.9%) compared to normal calves (median: 5.3%; average: 11.3%).

Relative abundances of bacterial taxa in diarrheic (orange) and normal (blue) calf fecal samples. A–C Phylum-, genus-, and species-level classifications, respectively. x-axis: relative abundance (%) of each taxon; bars: average values for each group; overlaid points: individual sample measurements

At the genus level (Fig. 3B), the diarrheic group showed pronounced increases in Escherichia, Limosilactobacillus, and Lactobacillus compared to the normal group. Notably, the median abundance of Escherichia rose from 1.9% in normal calves to 14.6% in diarrheic calves. In contrast, Faecalibacterium and Lawsonibacter were reduced in diarrheic claves. The median abundance of Faecalibacterium decreased from 2.0% (average: 5.5%) in normal calves to 0.0% (average: 1.4%) in diarrheic calves, and Lawsonibacter decreased from 3.2% (average: 4.9%) to 0.0% (average: 0.1%). Additionally, Salmonella and Klebsiella showed increases in the diarrheic calves. The median abundance of Salmonella increased from 0.0% (average: 1.1%) in normal calves to 3.2% (average: 3.7%) in diarrheic calves, while Klebsiella increased from 0.0% (average: 0.7%) to 2.4% (average: 2.6%).

At the species level (Fig. 3C), Phocaeicola vulgatus and E. coli had notably higher relative abundances in diarrheic calves than in normal calves. For instance, E. coli increased from a median of 1.9% to 14.4%. Similarly, Salmonella enterica and Bacteroides fragilis showed elevated levels in diarrheic samples, whereas Faecalibacterium prausnitzii was markedly depleted. A few Lactobacillus species, such as L. delbrueckii and L. gasseri, remained relatively low in overall abundance but appeared sporadically at higher levels in some diarrheic calves.

Functional pathway analysis

Figure 5 presents the results of the comparative functional pathway analysis of the fecal microbiota in normal and diarrheic calves. Figure 5A compares the predicted functional pathway abundances between these groups, highlighting six significantly different pathways (adjusted p-values < 0.05). All six pathways including the superpathway of glycolysis and Entner–Doudoroff pathway, superpathway of N-acetylneuraminate degradation, hexitol fermentation to lactate/formate/ethanol/acetate, superpathway of hexitol degradation (bacteria), pentose phosphate pathway, and dTDP–N‑acetylthomosamine biosynthesis, showed higher mean proportions in the diarrheic group. Specifically, the largest difference in mean proportions was observed in the superpathway of glycolysis and the Entner–Doudoroff pathway. This was followed by the superpathway of N-acetylneuraminate degradation, the hexitol fermentation route to lactate, formate, ethanol, and acetate, and the superpathway of hexitol degradation (bacteria). Overall, these pathways exhibited differences ranging from about 0.05% to 0.30% in mean proportions. The heatmap in Fig. 5B displays a hierarchical clustering of the predicted pathway abundances for normal and diarrheic calves. Overall, the diarrheic samples cluster together in the right portion of the dendrogram, whereas the normal samples tend to cluster toward the left. Notably, most diarrheic samples clustered together, reflecting similar functional profiles enriched in pathways related to carbohydrate degradation, such as the Entner–Doudoroff and hexitol fermentation routes.

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the potential association of NCD with shifts in the fecal microbiota and characterized these microbial changes at the taxonomic and functional levels. Our analysis of alpha diversity revealed a significantly reduced fecal microbiota diversity in diarrheic calves than in normal calves. The diarrheic group showed lower richness (Chao1) and evenness (Shannon and Simpson indices), indicating an association between NCD and significantly reduced microbial diversity. Lower alpha diversity often signifies a dysbiotic state, implying disrupted gut microbiota balance [23]. This may be related to the overgrowth of pathogenic or opportunistic bacteria and the concurrent depletion of beneficial or commensal taxa [23]. Such disruptions in early life may be associated with alterations in gut barrier function, nutrient utilization, and immune regulation, which could be linked to diarrheal episodes [23].

Our findings align with those in earlier reports demonstrating the correlation between NCD and diminished microbial diversity [11]. Underscoring the importance of a robust and diverse microbiota for gut health in calves. Reduced alpha diversity may serve as an indicator of gut health and disease susceptibility in neonatal calves [3]. Practically, monitoring these diversity indices may help identify at-risk animals sooner, facilitating timely interventions [24]. Additionally, strategies to preserve or restore microbial richness, such as the use of selective probiotics, prebiotics, and optimized feeding practices, may help mitigate diarrhea onset or severity and improve both calf welfare and farm productivity [25–27].

PCoA based on Bray–Curtis and Jensen–Shannon divergence measures indicated clear compositional differences in the fecal microbiota between normal and diarrheic calves. Despite some overlap, the two groups clustered distinctly, with diarrheic samples generally occupying more positive values on axis 1 and normal samples more negative values. The centroids for each group remained separate, indicating a marked shift in microbial community structure [11]. These clustering patterns suggest that NCD is associated with a broad restructuring of the gut microbial ecosystem, rather than merely a reduction or enrichment of a few specific taxa [2, 3]. In particular, the divergence along axis 1, which explained a substantial portion of the total variance, suggested the presence of group-specific microbial assemblages. Similar results in earlier studies on calf diarrhea [2, 3, 11] corroborate the notion of a distinct microbiota composition in diarrheic calves compared with their healthy peers, potentially reflecting pathogen overgrowth and beneficial microbe depletion. In practice, rapid microbiota profiling can help identify at-risk calves before clinical signs appear, enabling targeted management or therapeutic interventions [24]. Moreover, the pronounced shift in microbial community composition may inform future studies aimed at developing microbial or metabolite-based biomarkers for NCD.

Our results indicated that NCD is associated with disruptions in gut microbiota homeostasis, characterized by reduced abundances of beneficial taxa generally considered beneficial namely, those associated with gut health and anti-inflammatory effects (e.g., Faecalibacterium, Lawsonibacter) [11], and increased abundances of opportunistic or pathogenic bacteria (e.g., Escherichia, Salmonella, Klebsiella) [2, 3]. The LEfSe results further highlighted these differences, with E. coli and S. enterica exhibiting high LDA scores in diarrheic samples. These findings highlight association between NCD and disruptions in the gut microbiota, particularly an overrepresentation of opportunistic or pathogenic bacteria and a reduction in potentially beneficial taxa [3]. The increased prevalence of E. coli, S. enterica, and Klebsiella spp. aligns with their known roles in gastrointestinal diseases [2, 3], whereas the decline in Faecalibacterium and Lawsonibacter supports evidence that reduced beneficial microbes may impair gut barrier integrity and immune homeostasis [11]. Moreover, Lactobacillus and Limosilactobacillus, which are often considered beneficial under balanced conditions, were unexpectedly abundant in diarrheic calves, potentially due to small-intestinal mucosal damage leading to decreased lactase activity and the resultant increase in residual lactose in the colon [28, 29]. This elevated lactose could favor the proliferation of lactic acid bacteria under inflammatory or dysbiotic conditions [28, 29]. Furthermore, previous study has indicated that Lactobacillus abundance may increase during the recovery phase from diarrhea [3], suggesting that the higher abundance observed in our diarrheic calves might also reflect a compensatory or transitional response. Taken together, these findings indicate that the role of Lactobacillus in NCD is likely context-dependent, and further longitudinal studies are warranted to distinguish pathological overgrowth from recovery-associated expansion.

The LEfSe results corroborated these patterns, showing that specific taxa not only dominate in diseased states but may also exert an outsized influence on the overall microbial community structure. Pronounced shifts in bacterial composition underscore the importance of maintaining a balanced gut microbiota for neonatal calf health [30]. From an academic perspective, these results add to the growing body of evidence that NCD involves both established pathogens (e.g., Escherichia and Salmonella) and lesser-known contributors (e.g., Limosilactobacillus) [2, 11]. Practically, routine microbiota monitoring may enable the earlier identification of calves at risk and provide preliminary insights for potential interventions, such as probiotics or prebiotics, to restore beneficial taxa or suppress potential pathogens [31]. Moreover, the differential abundance of certain species, including E. coli, L. reuteri, S. enterica, and K. pneumoniae on the pathogenic side, and F. prausnitzii, and L. asaccharolyticus as potential beneficial indicators could inform future development of diagnostic panels or therapeutic interventions.

The results of the present study identified several metabolic pathways that differed significantly between diarrheic and normal calves (Fig. 5). Diarrheic samples displayed higher predicted abundances of pathways related to carbohydrate breakdown, specifically the Entner–Doudoroff pathway, hexitol fermentation, N-acetylneuraminate degradation, pentose phosphate pathway and dTDP–N–acetylhomosamine biosynthesis. These results suggest that the gut microbiota of diarrheic calves is functionally oriented toward rapid carbohydrate utilization and fermentation, possibly reflecting an inflammatory or dysbiotic state conducive to opportunistic microbes [3, 32]. The increased enrichment of the pathways, such as the Entner–Doudoroff pathway and hexitol fermentation, indicates a greater capacity to convert various sugars into energy and fermentation end products, potentially contributing to the clinical manifestations of diarrhea (e.g., increased osmotic pressure and altered short-chain fatty acid profiles) [33, 34]. The shifts in these functional pathways align with the compositional changes observed in diarrheic calves, particularly the overabundance of Escherichia and Salmonella, as many of these taxa thrive on simple carbohydrates such as those present under inflamed gut conditions [35–37]. Compared to alpha diversity, which reflect global community structure but offer limited insight, pathway-based profiling may provide more information for targeted intervention. Monitoring specific metabolic signatures may enable earlier detection of dysbiosis and better inform dietary or therapeutic strategies for NCD management. Understanding and monitoring these functionally enriched pathways may provide a basis for exploratory diagnostic tools and inform future intervention strategies to mitigate dysbiosis and its clinical consequences in NCD.

Nevertheless, our study also has some limitations. First, although our sample size (20 normal and 20 diarrheic calves) provided valuable insights, larger-scale investigations may offer greater statistical power to detect subtle microbial shifts. Second, our study did not incorporate longitudinal sampling; hence, we cannot definitively conclude how the microbiota evolves from pre-diarrheic to post-recovery stages. Third, although we performed functional predictions using PICRUSt2, which infers gene content from 16S rRNA profiles, these predictions do not measure actual gene expression or metabolic activity. Future studies incorporating higher-resolution approaches such as shotgun metagenomics or metatranscriptomics will be valuable to confirm the functional relevance of the microbial shifts observed and to explore gene-level and pathway-specific mechanisms involved in NCD pathogenesis. Therefore, interpretations regarding enriched metabolic pathways should be considered hypothetical and warrant confirmation though metagenomic, transcriptomic, or metabolomic approaches. Fourth, although diarrheic calves in this study were tested negative for major pathogens (bovine rotavirus, bovine coronavirus, Cryptosporidium, Giardia, and E. coli K99) using a rapid antigen detection kit, other infectious etiologies may not have been fully excluded. Therefore, the observed microbial shifts may have been partially influenced by undiagnosed enteric infections, and interpretation of causal relationships should be approached with caution. Future studies should employ longitudinal designs to track microbiota changes from birth through recovery and clarify the cause-and-effect relationships in NCD. Multi-omics approaches (e.g., shotgun metagenomics and metabolomics) can confirm the functional capacities suggested by predictive tools such as PICRUSt2. Fifth, extending this study to different geographical regions and farm management systems may enhance the generalizability of our findings and foster more robust NCD prevention strategies. Additionally, because the calves ranged in age from 2 days to 2 months, age-related physiological changes may have influenced the gut microbiota composition. However, due to the absence of data regarding precise day-of-age, a direct age-matching analysis between groups could not be conducted. While all calves were within the neonatal period, it is known that microbial communities undergo rapid maturation during this time. Therefore, we cannot exclude the possibility that some of the observed microbial variation was due to age-related dynamics rather than solely diarrheal status. Finally, fecal consistency was diagnosed by a veterinarian during routine examinations. However, a standardized scoring system was not applied, and additional health indicators (e.g., body temperature, appetite, mental state) were not systematically recorded. These limitations reduce the granularity of clinical assessments.

Conclusions

In conclusion, the findings of the present study highlight the central role of gut microbiota composition and functionality in NCD development. The consistent reduction in alpha diversity, distinct clustering in beta diversity, marked shifts in key bacterial taxa, and enhanced carbohydrate metabolism pathways in diarrheic calves underscore the multifaceted dysbiosis that potentially compromises gut homeostasis. A deeper understanding of these interactions could eventually guide the development of intervention strategies such as nutritional approaches, probiotic supplementation, and modulation of metabolic pathways to prevent or alleviate NCD and improve calf health and productivity.
