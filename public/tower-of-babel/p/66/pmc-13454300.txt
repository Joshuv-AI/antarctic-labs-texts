VeNet: a lightweight neural network for efficient brain vessel segmentation in endovascular robotic surgery
Authors: Alexandra Bernadotte
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTelesurgery integrates artificial intelligence (AI), robotic systems, sensing technologies, and wireless communication to enable remote and computer-assisted surgical interventions. Beyond remote operation, AI-driven assistance can improve procedural stability and consistency by supporting motion smoothing, tremor reduction, event detection, and workflow guidance. Achieving these capabilities requires accurate, patient-specific anatomical models derived from limited medical imaging data, together with data-efficient and computationally lightweight algorithms suitable for near-real-time use. Robotic endovascular surgery presents additional challenges due to the complex topology of the cerebral vasculature and the limited availability of high-quality annotated imaging datasets. Current vessel segmentation pipelines often rely on manual annotation or classical vesselness filters, while deep learning approaches typically require large annotated datasets and substantial computational resources, limiting their applicability in resource-constrained or real-time settings. Here, we present VeNet, a lightweight neural network designed for three-dimensional segmentation of tubular structures such as blood vessels. VeNet employs a matrix-based operator and contains approximately 6,000 trainable parameters, enabling efficient training and inference on standard CPU hardware. The model demonstrates robust segmentation performance in low-data regimes, making it suitable for scenarios where extensive manual annotation is impractical. Using VeNet, we generated a large, semi-automatically annotated brain vessel magnetic resonance angiography dataset derived from the IXI cohort, with subsequent expert review. Together, VeNet and the accompanying dataset provide a resource-efficient foundation for vascular segmentation and simulation workflows. Introduction

Telesurgery represents a multidisciplinary domain within modern healthcare that integrates artificial intelligence (AI), robotic systems, sensor technologies, and advanced wireless communication. While early telesurgical concepts focused primarily on enabling surgical procedures at a distance, contemporary systems increasingly rely on AI-driven assistance to enhance procedural precision, robustness, and safety.

The benefits of telesurgery therefore extend well beyond remote operation alone. Multi-level AI integration allows telesurgical interventions to be executed with greater accuracy, speed, and consistency compared to conventional, non-robotic approaches. Through AI-assisted control, telesurgical systems can effectively provide “the robot’s hands” even to less experienced surgeons by compensating for involuntary tremor, refining and stabilizing movements, detecting and reporting intraoperative events, and assisting in instrument selection and procedural planning. Moreover, telesurgery has the potential to evolve into an intelligent autonomous platform, enabling a new paradigm of personalized surgery.

Achieving this level of functionality requires artificial intelligence capable of generating accurate, patient-specific anatomical simulations from limited medical imaging data, while simultaneously supporting realistic haptic feedback. To ensure safe and reliable autonomous or semi-autonomous operation, such AI systems must be both data-efficient and computationally resource-efficient, allowing deployment in real-time clinical environments and embedded robotic platforms.

The pace of development across telesurgical domains is heterogeneous, with certain application areas advancing more rapidly than others due to technical and clinical constraints. In particular, telesurgical systems designed for abdominal and laparoscopic interventions have demonstrated substantial technological maturity. Notable examples include the da Vinci Surgical System developed by Intuitive Surgical and the ZEUS Robotic Surgical System1,2. These platforms have established the feasibility of robot-assisted and teleoperated surgery in relatively structured anatomical environments.

In contrast, the development of robotic telesurgical systems for endovascular interventions has progressed at a slower pace. Endovascular surgery, encompassing both cardiac and cerebral procedures, imposes fundamentally different requirements due to the fragility of vascular structures, narrow luminal geometry, and complex three-dimensional vessel topology. At present, only a limited number of robotic platforms are capable of supporting remote or robotic minimally invasive endovascular interventions. For example, the R-One robotic assistance platform (Robocath) focuses on robotic solutions for interventional cardiology with the aim of improving procedural precision and safety3. In the field of robotic endovascular neurosurgery, the LevshaAI research system (Lab Enhanced Vascular Simulating Haptic AI) represents a dedicated solution designed specifically for cerebral vascular interventions4–7. A key component of the LevshaAI research platform is the Personalized Intelligent Vascular 3D Simulator, which integrates intelligent haptic feedback. This simulator generates patient-specific models of cerebral vasculature based on anatomical parameters extracted from medical imaging data, enabling personalized procedural planning, training, and simulation.

Unlike abdominal telesurgical systems, robotic endovascular platforms must support delicate, high-precision manipulations within a highly constrained and deformable vascular environment, necessitating fundamentally different design paradigms.

Consequently, robotic endovascular surgery is intrinsically dependent on high-quality, interactive three-dimensional models of cerebral and cardiac vasculature derived from imaging modalities such as computed tomography angiography (CTA), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and magnetic resonance angiography (MRA). These models must support real-time simulation of vessel curvature, deformation, and interaction with surgical instruments in order to generate physically meaningful haptic feedback and ensure procedural safety.

Despite the clinical importance of such capabilities, developers face a significant shortage of efficient AI methodologies and high-quality annotated datasets required for realistic vascular simulation. This challenge is particularly pronounced for cerebral vasculature, which exhibits intricate topology, fine-scale branching, and high inter-patient variability. These characteristics substantially complicate data annotation and hinder the training of AI-based vessel segmentation models.

At present, cerebral vessel annotation is predominantly performed using manual segmentation or classical image-processing techniques, such as Frangi vesselness filtering, which frequently fail to achieve the accuracy required for clinical-grade vascular simulations. Although AI-based approaches have demonstrated strong potential in medical image segmentation, contemporary deep learning models remain critically dependent on large, accurately annotated training datasets.

A number of brain magnetic resonance angiography (MRA) datasets are available either publicly or under restricted access8–10,14–17. However, there remains a pronounced scarcity of datasets containing detailed annotations of cerebral vessels. To date, only a single publicly reported dataset provides vessel annotations for approximately 100 brain MRA images8. Manual annotation of cerebral vasculature is highly labor-intensive, typically requiring 5–8 hours of expert effort per subject, which severely limits dataset scalability.

These limitations present a critical barrier to the deployment of AI-driven vascular simulation in robotic endovascular telesurgery. Vessel segmentation represents the first essential step in constructing three-dimensional vascular models required for simulation and haptic feedback. Haptic feedback itself emerges from real-time simulation of catheter–vessel interaction, including dynamic vessel curvature, deformation, and potential damage, followed by transmission of calibrated force signals to actuators interfacing with the surgeon. Such real-time simulation pipelines demand segmentation methods that are not only accurate but also computationally efficient.

These considerations motivate three primary objectives. First, there is a need for an automatic or semi-automatic solution for annotating brain MRA datasets that can operate locally within clinical environments without reliance on remote servers. Second, such a solution should achieve state-of-the-art segmentation performance using minimal annotated training data by leveraging domain-specific knowledge of vascular topology. Third, the solution must be suitable for direct integration into robotic telesurgical systems.

In contrast to conventional deep learning approaches that rely on large-scale data-driven feature learning, we explore an alternative paradigm based on the explicit incorporation of domain-specific analytical operators into neural architectures.

Specifically, we consider vessel segmentation as a structured detection problem where prior knowledge about tubular geometry can be encoded directly through differential operators. This motivates a hybrid design in which fixed mathematical operators (e.g., Hessian-based vesselness filters) are integrated into the network as inductive biases, while learnable components adapt these representations to data.

Such operator-induced architectures provide a complementary approach to standard convolutional networks by improving data efficiency, interpretability, and stability in low-data regimes. Within this framework, VeNet can be viewed as an instance of a lightweight operator-aware neural network specialized for tubular structure segmentation.

In this work, we address these objectives by introducing VeNet, a lightweight specialized neural network for cerebral vessel segmentation from MR images. VeNet contains approximately 6,000 trainable parameters and achieves competitive segmentation performance across standard segmentation metrics, including Accuracy, Dice Similarity Coefficient, Sensitivity, Specificity, Precision, and Average Hausdorff Distance, even when trained on critically small datasets. VeNet is optimized for CPU-based training and inference performance, enabling deployment on local hospital hardware and near-real-time processing pipelines.

VeNet has been embedded into the LevshaAI – research robotic endovascular neurosurgery system to support near-real-time vessel reconstruction, three-dimensional simulation, and haptic feedback generation. In addition, the application of VeNet enabled the creation of a large semi-automatically annotated brain vessel dataset based on the IXI MRA dataset, which was subsequently reviewed and verified by neurovascular specialists. Both the VeNet model and the annotated dataset are made publicly available under a Creative Commons Attribution–NonCommercial–NoDerivatives 4.0 International License.

Recent advances in artificial intelligence for robotic endovascular procedures increasingly adopt a system-level perspective, where perception, planning, and control modules are tightly integrated within a unified framework. In this context, vessel segmentation serves as a fundamental perception component that enables downstream tasks such as catheter path planning, navigation, and interaction-aware control.

A recent systematic review of AI-driven solutions for robotic endovascular interventions highlights the emergence of embodied intelligence architectures, where perception modules are coupled with decision-making and control systems to enable semi-autonomous and autonomous operation11,13. In such systems, segmentation quality directly affects the reliability of higher-level modules, including trajectory planning and procedural optimization.

Several recent works have explored integrated pipelines combining vascular segmentation with path planning and intervention guidance. For example, deep learning–based segmentation has been combined with heuristic and optimization-based planning strategies to support percutaneous coronary interventions12. These approaches demonstrate the importance of accurate vascular reconstruction as a prerequisite for safe and efficient navigation in complex anatomical environments.

Within this broader context, the present work focuses specifically on the perception layer, proposing a lightweight and data-efficient vessel segmentation model designed for integration into resource-constrained robotic systems. Unlike prior approaches that prioritize large-scale data-driven performance, VeNet is explicitly designed to operate under limited data and computational budgets, making it particularly suitable for embedded and near-real-time endovascular applications.

Related work

The development of reliable vessel segmentation methods is closely linked to the availability of high-quality annotated datasets and computationally efficient algorithms. In neurovascular imaging, both factors remain limiting, particularly for applications requiring real-time or embedded deployment such as robotic endovascular surgery. This section reviews existing datasets, classical segmentation techniques, and neural network–based approaches, highlighting their respective strengths and limitations.

Existing datasets

The creation of large, well-annotated vascular datasets is challenging due to the labor-intensive nature of expert annotation and the need for clinical validation. Manual annotation of cerebral vessels typically requires several hours per subject and must be performed or verified by trained neuroradiologists or neurovascular surgeons. These constraints have motivated the use of semi-automatic annotation pipelines, data augmentation, and synthetic data generation.

Classical vessel segmentation methods

Early approaches to vessel segmentation rely on intensity-based and differential geometric techniques. Global and local thresholding methods, such as Otsu’s algorithm20, perform image binarization based on histogram statistics. While computationally efficient, these methods are sensitive to noise, intensity inhomogeneity, and partial volume effects, which are common in MRA.

Tubular structure enhancement filters, most notably the Frangi vesselness filter21, analyze eigenvalues of the Hessian matrix to identify line-like structures. Frangi-based methods are widely used as preprocessing or initialization tools but often fail in regions with low contrast, complex branching, or pathological deformation. As a result, they typically require extensive manual post-processing to achieve clinically acceptable segmentation quality.

Neural network–based segmentation

Neural networks have become the dominant paradigm for medical image segmentation, including cerebrovascular applications. 3D U-Net architectures22 extend convolutional networks to volumetric data using encoder–decoder designs with skip connections. While effective for general organ segmentation, standard 3D U-Nets often struggle to preserve thin vascular topology and require substantial annotated datasets and GPU resources.

DeepVesselNet17 introduced orthogonal 2D convolutions to approximate 3D context while reducing memory usage. Its class-balanced loss addresses extreme vessel–background imbalance; however, limited sensitivity to long-range 3D connectivity and reduced robustness across scanners have been reported.

BRAVE-NET23 extends 3D U-Net with multiscale context aggregation and deep supervision, improving segmentation of small vessels and robustness to scanner variability. More recent multi-task CNN approaches integrate voxel-wise segmentation with centerline detection and distance transform learning, using topology-aware loss functions to preserve vascular structure across heterogeneous datasets24. These methods achieve strong performance but remain computationally demanding and rely on large, diverse training datasets.

Limitations and open challenges

Despite significant progress, existing neural approaches face several limitations relevant to robotic endovascular applications. Most state-of-the-art models require large annotated datasets, GPU-based inference, and substantial memory, limiting their applicability in real-time, embedded, or resource-constrained clinical environments. Furthermore, annotation scarcity remains unresolved, particularly for cerebral vasculature with high anatomical variability.

These constraints motivate the exploration of lightweight, data-efficient segmentation models and semi-automatic annotation strategies that explicitly leverage vessel-specific geometric properties. Such approaches aim to reduce annotation burden while maintaining sufficient accuracy for downstream tasks, including patient-specific 3D vascular simulation and haptic feedback in robotic endovascular systems.

In this context, the present work introduces a lightweight neural network designed to support semi-automatic annotation of MRA datasets and efficient vessel segmentation under limited data and computational resources.

Proposed methodology

This approach aims to improve vessel segmentation for personalized surgery and robotic telesurgery providing robust, efficient, and practically plausible solutions.

The core solution is a neural network named VeNet. The introduced model presents a combined approach, integrating traditional methods and advanced specialized neural architectures.

The skull stripping, brain masking, and skull vessels filtering were applied for the test and validation data before VeNet training using the same technique as described in the initial paper8.

Train/validation separation

A 10-fold cross-validation scheme was used, where the dataset was divided into 10 subject-level folds. In each iteration, one fold was used for validation and the remaining folds were used for training. This process was repeated across all folds to obtain averaged performance metrics.

No independent test set was used due to the limited dataset size; instead, cross-validation was used to provide a robust estimate of generalization performance. At no point were samples from the same subject included in both training and validation sets.

Proposed VeNet model

VeNet is a lightweight neural network with only 6K parameters, specialized for 3D segmentation of complex branched tubular structures such as brain vessels.

VeNet can run on CPU and GPU and is optimized for minimal training datasets while achieving competitive segmentation performance, with advantages most pronounced in low-data and resource-constrained settings.

Practically, VeNet can be used for brain vessel segmentation on a local machine in a hospital without accessing a server or for near-real-time processing. This architecture can also be part of more complex neural network-based methods.

The architecture integrates powerful linear algebra, probability theory, and mathematical analysis methods into specialized layers of the neural network.

The VeNet Tubular Features Block uses Hessian matrix operators to detect tubular structures in 3D images. The VeNet architecture consists of parallel and sequentially located convolution blocks and VeNet Tubular Features Blocks (see Fig. 1).

Conceptually, the proposed architecture follows a hybrid design paradigm that combines fixed analytical operators with learnable neural components. Unlike standard convolutional networks, which must implicitly learn geometric priors from data, VeNet explicitly encodes vessel-specific structure through differential operators.

Within the VeNet Tabular Features Block, the Hessian computation and Frangi vesselness transformation are implemented as fixed (non-trainable) operators. These components provide a deterministic mapping from image intensities to geometry-aware features. The Hessian matrix and Frangi vesselness operator act as predefined feature extractors that impose an inductive bias toward tubular structures. These features are then processed by fully trainable components (linear projections and multilayer perceptrons), enabling the network to adapt operator-derived representations to the target data distribution and segmentation task.

Thus, VeNet explicitly separates analytical feature extraction from statistical learning, which differs from conventional end-to-end learned architectures. This separation between fixed operators and learnable mappings reduces the burden on data-driven learning and allows the model to achieve high performance in low-data regimes.

Training strategy

The VeNet Feature Blocks are trained independently of each other. The input image is fed into the VeNet and passed simultaneously through the skip connection and the VeNet feature blocks (Fig. 1). Then, convolutional layers and a sigmoid activation function are applied to the outputs from the skip connection and VeNet Feature Blocks. This architecture has a small number of parameters () and low computational cost, allowing it to run in inference mode on the CPU of any modern PC.

For performance analysis, VeNet and other models were trained on a CPU (Intel Core i7-13700KF) and separately on a GPU (RTX 3060, 12GB) for 50 epochs.

The preprocessing time, including skull stripping, brain masking, and normalization, was approximately 15 seconds per subject under the described experimental setup.

For fitting, we considered four training strategies: 5 MR images, 10 MR images, 20 MR images, 100 MR images. In all cases we consider 10 different training sets (10-fold cross-validation) to obtain mean value metrics.

As can be seen from the above metrics, none of the metrics alone can provide an adequate assessment of segmentation quality. It is always necessary to consider a combination of metrics to evaluate the segmentation result.

For our task, Specificity and Accuracy are not informative metrics, as their values quickly converge to 1 after just a few steps of neural network training. The differences in these values between different models are too small to effectively reflect the success of the training process. This issue arises due to the significant volume imbalance between vessels and the entire image, with the vasculature accounting for only 1–3% of the total head volume. As a result, both TN and TP are relatively small, and we will not consider these metrics in the following analysis.

Experimental setup and reproducibility

To ensure a fair and reproducible comparison, all models were trained and evaluated under identical experimental conditions. This includes consistent data splits, preprocessing pipelines, normalization procedures, and augmentation strategies across all architectures. We trained all the networks selected for comparison on the same datasets and averaged the resulting metrics’ values.

Cross-validation was performed at the patient level, ensuring that all volumetric data from a given subject were assigned exclusively to either the training or validation fold. This design prevents data leakage and ensures that model performance reflects generalization across subjects rather than intra-subject similarity.

Baseline models were implemented according to their original publications and were trained without extensive task-specific hyperparameter tuning. This standardized setup was intentionally adopted to provide a consistent comparison framework and to reflect practical deployment scenarios, particularly in resource-constrained clinical environments where extensive model-specific optimization may not be feasible. This setup ensures that the comparison focuses on architectural efficiency rather than optimization effort.

Results and discussion

The results section is divided into three parts. The first part “Performance and Comparative Analyses” presents the metric results of the proposed VeNet model, a comparative analysis with other neural networks, and CPU/GPU optimisation. The second part “Automatically Annotated Dataset” provides information about the annotated dataset obtained using proposed VeNet model. The third part “Tubular Structures Reconstruction and Simulation for the research robotic endovascular neurosurgery LevshaAI” demonstrates the application of VeNet for vascular reconstruction and simulation in an existing telesurgical system with a personalization module and haptic feedback.

The results suggest that explicitly incorporating domain-specific analytical operators into neural architectures can significantly improve performance under data and computational constraints. In contrast to purely data-driven models, which must learn geometric representations implicitly, VeNet leverages predefined differential operators to encode structural priors. This reduces the effective hypothesis space compared to purely data-driven architectures, allowing the model to focus on anatomically plausible solutions and improving generalization in low-data settings. From this perspective, VeNet belongs to a broader class of operator-aware neural networks, where analytical transformations and learned mappings are combined within a unified framework. Such architectures may be particularly well-suited for medical imaging tasks characterized by strong geometric regularities and limited annotated data.

Performance and comparative analyses

Performance

Performance analysis and comparative analysis were conducted for 8 high-performance neural network architectures used for vessel segmentation task, e.g.: 2D U-Net31,3D U-Net22, nnU-Net Full Res32, DeepVesselNet-UNet17, Context U-Net23, Deep Vision U-Net23, BRAVE-NET23, and introduced VeNet.

Trained on 100 annotated images from Brain Vessel Annotated Dataset from IXI-MRA Dataset8 VeNet achieves competitive performance under low-data and low-parameter constraints performing vessel segmentation task, e.g.: DSC (89,34%), Sensitivity (91,47%), and Precision (87,81%) (Table 1). In our case, the value of Specificity and Accuracy very quickly converge to one for all the models after just a few steps of neural network training, which makes these metrics uninformative.

While VeNet’s performance for some metrics exceeded competitive solutions by 8–11%, some competitive models achieved significant values in Precision and Sensitivity. For example, the U-Net based BRAVE-NET model achieved 87.78% Precision, which was on par with the introduced VeNet model.

The main metrics reached their maximum values and stabilized in less than 50 epochs on a dataset of 100 images.

The learning dynamics of VeNet show more gradual convergence compared to larger models (see Fig. 3), while achieving competitive final performance.

At the same time, due to its compact architecture, VeNet requires substantially fewer computational resources and achieves significantly faster training and inference on CPU compared to larger models.

Performance and parameters ratio

It is worth noting that the VeNet model has only about 6 K parameters, while other existing models have a number of parameters that exceeds this number by several orders of magnitude (Table 1).

The proposed DSCParamLog metric helps us evaluate segmentation quality metrics taking into account the number of neural network parameters. By this metric, the proposed VeNet (DSCParamLog = 23.65) significantly outperforms other models (DSCParamLog < 13) without losing image segmentation quality.

For the tasks of autonomous telesurgical devices with integrated low-resource neural networks capable of training on devices without accessing the server, high values of the introduced DSCParamLog metric are critical.

Performance and training datasets volume

A very strong advantage of the VeNet model is its ability to achieve high-quality segmentation on critically small datasets. It provides high-quality segmentation after training already on 5 images from Brain Vessel Annotated Dataset from IXI-MRA Dataset8,9 (See Table 2).

The model achieved a sensitivity comparable to competing approaches already with 10 images. When trained on 20 MRA scans, VeNet achieved a DSC score of 88.5% and AHD of 32.72%, which are comparable to or exceeding results reported for models such as BRAVE-NET, trained on 100 images.

Such performance is likely attributable to the incorporation of domain-specific mathematical priors within the model architecture, taking into account the specifics of the data itself. Using a block with Frangi filters helps with the tubular structures identification through simple matrix calculations, without relying on complex heuristic feature engineering.

Training a neural network on a small dataset is particularly relevant in cases where there is the lack of annotated data to train larger models. In the case of telesurgery for vascular brain procedures, the lack of annotated data is a huge challenge. The proposed compact and efficient VeNet model addresses this existing data shortage.

CPU optimisation

While GPU training times are comparable across models due to parallelization, VeNet demonstrates a substantial advantage in CPU-based training and inference performance, which is critical for deployment in resource-constrained environments.

VeNet can run on the CPU and significantly reduces the resource requirements for training neural networks. Compared to other competitive solutions, VeNet delivers faster training compared to the evaluated baseline models on both CPUs (33 sec on CPU Intel Core i7-13700KF) and GPUs (18 sec on GPU RTX 3060 12GB), with inference results being particularly impressive (5 sec on CPU Intel Core i7-13700KF). Automatically annotated dataset

Using the proposed method with the help of VeNet, the IXI Dataset9 (200 images) was annotated and titled as “VeNet dataset.”

Note the additional VeNet-annotated dataset (VeNet dataset) was used exclusively for dataset generation and release purposes and was not used in the primary model comparison experiments reported.

This VeNet Dataset complements the recently published annotated dataset (e.g.:“Brain Vessel Annotated Dataset from IXI-MRA Dataset”8 based on the unannotated IXI Dataset9) and is completely homogeneous to it in terms of annotation quality.

The VeNet Dataset was annotated iteratively. Each iteration improved the quality of the automatic segmentation. This process was repeated multiple times. During the process VeNet Dataset was reviewed and verified by specialists (three neurovascular surgeons). Informed consent was obtained from the experts. The introduced annotating approach allows annotating the dataset fast with high performance.

To the best of our knowledge, the VeNet Dataset is among the largest publicly available annotated brain vessel MRA datasets derived from the IXI dataset, comprising 200 annotated volumes. The dataset is publicly available (see Data Availability section below).

Tubular structures reconstruction and simulation for the research robotic endovascular neurosurgery LevshaAI

Tubular structures Reconstruction and Simulation for telesurgery with haptic feedback or other application follows a six step pipeline: (1) images segmentation, (2) centerline extraction, (3) graph pruning, (4) feature extraction, (5) 3D simulation, and (6) Laplacian calculation for haptic feedback.

The six steps are applied for the research robotic endovascular neurosurgery system – LevshaAI system (Lab Enhanced Vascular Simulating Haptic AI) from NeuroSputnik LLC4–7.

All these complex steps are highly dependent on the initial vessel segmentation accuracy and reliability of the pipeline. This article is devoted to the first step, which was performed using the proposed VeNet model. VeNet is currently embedded into the CPU of one of the few dedicated systems for research robotic endovascular neurosurgery – LevshaAI. This VeNet model served as the basis for the entire 3D brain vascular simulation system, including haptic feedback. Personalized surgery was also achieved through near-real-time 3D simulation of the brain vessels of a specific patient, while on the operating table in an angiography machine 4. However, VeNet is not a diagnostic or autonomous decision system (Fig. 4).

VeNet is embedded into the LevshaAI research system (Lab Enhanced Vascular Simulating Haptic AI) for the tubular structures reconstruction and 3D simulation of brain vessels.

It should be noted, that VeNet and other neural network-based solutions face a common issue: they can skip some voxels between different parts of vessels. As a result, the output of neural network segmentation may sometimes exhibit vessel discontinuities. While this has a minimal impact on classical metrics such as DSC, Accuracy, and Sensitivity, it affects the topological structure of the segmented vessels. If we consider the vessel network as a graph for further reconstruction and simulation in personalized surgery, every discontinuity corresponds to a loss of connectivity between vertices in this graph. Consequently, the topological characteristics of the segmented vessel graph differ from those of the ground truth. A potential solution to this problem involves incorporating a term into the loss function that accounts for connectivity properties in graphs. However, this is a complicated task. Another approach could be the use of heuristic methods. For instance, we could analyse the graph’s vertices and impose a connection between those that are within a certain distance threshold. Alternatively, we could introduce a neural network designed specifically to correct such discontinuities.

An alternative direction for addressing topological discontinuities is the incorporation of topology-aware loss functions, such as clDice, which explicitly promote connectivity of tubular structures. While such approaches were not explored in the present work, they represent a promising direction for future development of the proposed architecture, particularly for improving the structural consistency of reconstructed vascular networks.

The study demonstrates that geometric properties and mathematical operators can be integrated into neural networks as separate layers, which significantly enhances their performance.

Conclusion

In this work, we presented VeNet, a lightweight neural network architecture for brain vessel segmentation from magnetic resonance images. The proposed model is specifically designed for tubular structure detection and contains approximately 6,000 trainable parameters, enabling efficient training and inference on standard CPU hardware.

Across multiple evaluation metrics, including Dice Similarity Coefficient, Sensitivity, Specificity, Precision, and Average Hausdorff Distance, VeNet demonstrated competitive performance relative to existing methods, particularly in low-data regimes. Notably, the model maintained stable segmentation quality when trained on critically small annotated datasets (10–20 volumes from the IXI-MRA dataset), highlighting its suitability for scenarios where large-scale manual annotation is impractical.

The architecture was optimized for deployment in resource-constrained environments, such as hospital workstations without access to dedicated GPU infrastructure or cloud-based services. As a result, VeNet can support local, near-real-time vessel segmentation and may also serve as a modular component within more complex medical imaging pipelines.

VeNet has been integrated into the LevshaAI – research robotic endovascular neurosurgery platform, where it is used for near-real-time reconstruction and three-dimensional simulation of cerebral vasculature with haptic feedback. In this context, the model enables patient-specific vascular modeling required for interactive simulation and procedural planning.

In addition to the model itself, we release a semi-automatically annotated brain vessel MRA dataset derived from the IXI dataset. The annotations were generated using VeNet and subsequently reviewed and refined under expert supervision, providing a publicly available resource to support further research on cerebral vessel segmentation and simulation. Both the VeNet implementation and the dataset are distributed under a Creative Commons Attribution–NonCommercial–NoDerivatives 4.0 International License.

Overall, this study demonstrates that explicitly integrating analytical operators into neural network architectures provides an effective alternative to large-scale data-driven models for structured medical imaging tasks, particularly when data availability, computational resources, and real-time constraints are limiting factors.

The proposed VeNet architecture illustrates how operator-induced inductive bias can significantly improve data efficiency while maintaining competitive segmentation performance. This approach may generalize beyond vascular segmentation to other domains where strong geometric priors are available but annotated data are limited. This suggests that operator-aware architectures may provide a general framework for structured learning problems.

Future work will explore extending this operator-aware paradigm to topology-preserving learning, multi-modal imaging, and physics-informed simulation frameworks and focus on broader validation across multi-center datasets, extension to other vascular territories.
