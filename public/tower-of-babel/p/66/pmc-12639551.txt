Magnetic resonance imaging radiomics-based machine learning model for preoperative prediction of pathological grade in pancreatic cancer: A preliminary study
Authors: Jiayi Bao, Yuanqing Liu, Yang Yu, Ximing Wang, Chunhong Hu, Su Hu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo explore the value of MRI radiomics-based machine learning models for predicting the pathological grade of pancreatic cancer preoperatively.Methods125 patients with pathologically confirmed pancreatic cancer who underwent preoperative MRI were retrospectively enrolled. The primary cohort was randomized in an 8:2 ratio into a training cohort (n = 100) and a validation cohort (n = 25). 1316 radiomics features were extracted from contrast-enhanced T1WI arterial phase (AP) or portal venous phase (PVP) images, respectively. After feature reduction and filtering, the best features were selected to construct machine learning models (K-nearest neighbor, KNN; support vector machine, SVM; logistic regression, LR; random forest, RF). Finally, the performance of these models was evaluated using the receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC).ResultsThere were no statistical differences in clinical characteristics between the low-grade and high-grade cohorts (P > 0.05). The best radiomics features selected from the AP, PVP and AP+PVP images were 6, 6 and 10, respectively. Among the four models, the LR machine learning model achieved the best predictive performance. The distribution of the Radscore values was clinically significant between the low-grade and high-grade groups both in the training cohort (median, 0.26 vs 0.99; P < 0.001) and validation cohort (median, 0.63 vs 1.48; P = 0.011). LR model of AP+PVP performed the best with AUC value of 0.81 (95 % CI: 0.72–0.91) for the training cohort and 0.82 (95 % CI: 0.62–1.00) for the validation cohort.ConclusionsMRI radiomics-based machine learning model is a potential non-invasive method to predict the pathological grade of pancreatic cancer.Keywords: Pancreatic cancer, Radiomics, Machine learning, Magnetic resonance imaging

Background

Pancreatic cancer is now the seventh leading cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide, with a 5-year survival rate less than 10 % [1], [2]. Currently, surgical resection is considered the only radical cure for pancreatic cancer [3], but the prognosis remains poor [4]. Pathological grade is a very important independent prognostic factor for pancreatic cancer, and poorly differentiated cancers have a poor prognosis [5], [6], [7], [8]. It is of great importance to evaluate the pathological grade of pancreatic cancer for the individualized treatment [8], [9]. Endoscopic ultrasound-guided fine-needle aspiration (EUS-FNA) is a common method to obtain tumor specimens for pathological examination [10]. However, this technique is highly invasive with the risk of complication such as pancreatitis [11]. Meanwhile, due to the heterogeneity of the pancreatic cancers, the pathological assessment of the specimen obtained by biopsy cannot represent the whole tumor [12]. Therefore, there is an urgent need of a non-invasive and convenient way to provide preoperative pathological grade of pancreatic cancer, which can help guide the clinicians in making an effective treatment strategy.

Radiomics [13], [14] is a quantitative imaging method that can extract more information from conventional medical images in a non-invasive way to improve the accuracy of clinical diagnosis, prediction and prognosis. Computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) based radiomics have been used to differentiate the pathological grades of tumors [15], [16]. N. Chang et al. developed a contrast-enhanced CT-based radiomics model to predict the pathological grade of pancreatic cancer [17]. However, to our knowledge, only few studies that have used contrast-enhanced MRI-based radiomics to predict the pathological grade of pancreatic cancer [18]. MRI has equal performance to CT in the evaluation of pancreatic cancer [19], with the advantage of good soft tissue contrast resolution and avoidance of radiation [20]. Machine learning algorithms can develop models with the best predictive performance by selecting stable features [21]. Therefore, the purpose of our study was to construct machine learning models based on the radiomics features of the arterial phase (AP) and portal venous phase (PVP) of contrast-enhanced MRI for the preoperative prediction of the pathological grade of pancreatic cancer.

Methods

Patients

The study was ethically approved by our institution and the requirement for informed consent was waived as it is a retrospective study. A consecutive cohort of patients who underwent radical pancreatic cancer surgery at our institution between January 2013 and April 2022 was retrospectively identified and screened for eligibility. This consecutive enrollment strategy was employed to minimize selection bias. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients with postoperative pathologically confirmed pancreatic cancer; (2) contrast-enhanced MRI was performed within 2 weeks before surgery. The exclusion criteria were shown below: (1) received any treatment (radiotherapy, chemotherapy or chemoradiotherapy) before the contrast-enhanced MRI examination; (2) incomplete clinical information; (3) poor image quality. A total of 125 patients with pancreatic cancer were finally enrolled in this study. The primary cohort was randomized in a ratio of 8:2 into a training cohort of 100 patients (64 males and 36 females; age 64.1 ± 10.3 years) and a validation cohort of 25 patients (16 males and 9 females; age 64.0 ± 9.3 years). The enrolment process for the primary cohort patients was shown in Fig. 1. Clinical data such as age, sex, and preoperative carbohydrate antigen 19–9 (CA19–9) levels were collected.

Flow chart of the patients enrolled in training and validation cohorts.

Pathological examination

All surgically removed tumors were formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded and hematoxylin-eosin stained. The slices were evaluated by experienced pathologist. The pathological grade of tumors are as follows: well (tumor cells resembling normal pancreatic cells, with few mitotic figures, low atypia, and low invasiveness), moderately(tumor cells with morphologies between highly and poorly differentiated, exhibiting some atypia, increased mitotic figures, but still maintaining certain tissue structure), poorly (tumor cells significantly different from normal pancreatic cells, with high atypia, numerous mitotic figures, and disordered tissue structure), and undifferentiated(tumor cells with extremely irregular morphologies, completely lacking the structural characteristics of normal pancreatic cells, with a large number of mitotic figures, growing in sheets or diffusely) [22]. Based on established clinical prognostic significance, which demonstrates a statistically significant difference in median survival, the tumors were classified into two groups: the low-grade group (well and moderately differentiated; n = 41) and the high-grade group (poorly differentiated and undifferentiated; n = 84) [8], [23].

Imaging acquisition and analysis

All enrolled patients received contrast-enhanced MRI examinations within 2 weeks before surgery with a 3.0-T system (Ingenia, Philip Healthcare, Best, the Netherlands; MAGNETOM Skyra or Verio, Siemens Healthcare, Erlangen, Germany; Signa HDx MR750, GE Healthcare, Milwaukee, USA). The MRI sequences and parameters were as follows: (1) fast spin-echo coronal T2-weighted sequence (TR/TE=1400/85 ms; matrix = 256 ×256; slice thickness = 5–7 mm; slice gap = 6.50–8.40 mm); (2) non-enhanced and contrast-enhanced T1-weighted fat-suppressed sequences (TR/TE = 3.90–4.11/1.24–1.89 ms; matrix = 260 × 320; slice thickness = 3 mm; no slice gap). For contrast-enhanced T1WI images, gadopentetate dextran (Magnevist or Gadovist, Bayer Schering Pharma, Berlin, Germany) were injected intravenously at a rate of 2 mL/sec with a dose of 0.2 mL/kg, followed by 20 mL of saline.

Then, the MRI imaging features of the tumors were evaluated, including the primary tumor location, tumor maximum diameter, presence of pancreatic parenchymal atrophy, pancreatic duct dilation (PDD), common bile duct dilation (CBDD) and vascular invasion. PDD was defined as pancreatic duct diameter greater than 3 mm [24]. CBDD was defined as common bile duct dilation diameter over 10 mm [24]. Vascular invasion was defined as vascular narrowing or occlusion by the tumor, or more than half of the blood vessels are surrounded by the tumor [24], [25]. All MRI images were evaluated by two experienced radiologists who were blind to the pathological results, and any disagreements were resolved by consensus.

Radiomics workflow

Radiomics workflow was as follows: (1) tumor segmentation; (2) feature extraction; (3) feature selection; (4) model construction (Fig. 2).

Flow chart of radiomics.

Tumor segmentation and radiomics feature extraction

Images in Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine format from the Picture Archiving and Communication Systems workstation were used for tumor segmentation. Tumor region-of-interest (ROI) segmentation was performed manually layer by layer in the contrast-enhanced T1WI AP and PVP by the same experienced abdominal radiologist who was unknown of the pathological grade on the ITK-SNAP version 3.8 software (http://www.itksnap.org, Philadelphia, PA V3.8, USA).

Prior to feature extraction, the segmented images were resampled to an isotropic 1 mm³ voxel size and intensity-based Z-score normalization was applied. A total of 1316 radiomics features were extracted from the preprocessed AP and PVP images respectively, using the Feature Explorer (FAE) (V4.2.0, https://github.com/salan668/FAE) open source software.During the feature extraction process, all image type settings and feature type settings were enabled, employing default parameters. The image type settings encompassed diverse transformations such as wavelet, square, and logarithm, among others. The extracted radiomics features included first order, shape, gray level co-occurrence matrix (GLCM), gray level run-length matrix (GLRLM), gray-level size-zone matrix (GLSZM), gray-level dependence matrix (GLDM), and features of neighborhood gray tone difference matrix (NGTDM).

Intraobserver and interobserver agreement

For intra-observer consistency evaluation, 30 images were randomly selected for repetition of tumor segmentation by one radiologist after 2 weeks. The same 30 images were subsequently segmented by another radiologist to assess inter-observer agreement. Intra- and inter-class correlation coefficients (ICCs) values greater than 0.75 were considered good for stability and repeatability of features.

Feature selection

The radiomics features were standardized using Z-score normalization via the Darwin Scientific Research Platform (Beijing Yizhun Intelligent Technology Co., Ltd, China). Feature reduction and filtering were performed on AP, PVP and AP+PVP features respectively. First, a variance threshold filter was used for the first stage of feature priming to remove features with less variation than the threshold to reduce feature dimensionality. After pre-processing the features using normalization, only features with a variance of 0 can be removed at this stage. Then, the best features were selected for subsequent model building, which evaluated the linear correlation of features with grouped category labels by the ANOVA method.

Construction, validation and performance of machine learning models

To address the class imbalance between the low-grade and high-grade groups in the training cohort, the Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique (SMOTE) was applied prior to model training to generate synthetic samples for the minority class (low-grade group). The following four machine learning models were built in AP, PVP and AP+PVP respectively based on the best selected radiomics features: K-nearest neighbor (KNN), support vector machine (SVM), random forest (RF), and logistic regression (LR). Model training and hyperparameter optimization were conducted using a stratified 5-fold cross-validation approach on the training cohort. Stratification ensured that the ratio of low-grade to high-grade cases was preserved in each fold. To prevent data leakage, all preprocessing steps (including feature standardization) were fitted exclusively on the training folds and then applied to the corresponding validation folds.Hyperparameters for all models were optimized via a grid search within the cross-validation loop. The receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC) and the area under the curve (AUC) were used for assessing the performance of the above models. Calculation of accuracy (ACC), sensitivity (SEN) and specificity (SPE) were performed. Decision curve analysis (DCA) was used to evaluate the net benefit of the radiomics models of AP, PVP and AP+PVP in the training cohort at different threshold probabilities [26].

Statistical analysis

SPSS version 25 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and R statistical software (http://www.Rproject.org, version 4.1.3) were used for statistical analysis. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± SD or median and interquartile range (IQR), and categorical variables are presented as numbers and percentages. For continuous variables, consider using the Independent t-test if the data are normally distributed, or the Mann-Whitney U test if the data do not meet the assumptions of normality; for categorical variables, use Fisher's exact test for small sample sizes or the chi-square test for larger sample sizes. Calibration curves and Hosmer-Lemeshow test were used for assessing the performance of the radiomics models. DCA was performed using the “DCA. R” function. A bilateral P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient characteristics

There were no significant differences between low-grade and high-grade groups in terms of sex, age, CA19–9, tumor maximum diameter, primary tumor location, parenchymal atrophy, PDD, CBDD, and vascular invasion, either in the training group or the validation group (P > 0.05, Table 1). This suggests that low-grade and high-grade pancreatic cancer cannot be differentiated simply by preoperative clinical characteristics and conventional MRI findings.Additionally, there were no statistically significant differences in the above characteristics between the training and validation cohorts (p > 0.05, Table 2).

Intraobserver and Interobserver agreement

The intra- and interobserver consistency were assessed of 1316 features in AP and PVP, respectively. Those features with poor ICCs were excluded. Finally, 355 features were excluded due to poor intra- or inter-observer consistency, and 961 features were retained for AP dimensionality reduction. Meanwhile, 1041 features were retained for PVP feature selection after excluding 275 features due to poor intra- or inter-observer agreement.

For the validation cohort using AP+PVP images, the AUCs of LR, SVM, KNN, and RF models were 0.82, 0.76, 0.81, and 0.70, respectively.

The distribution of the Radscore values was clinically significant between the low-grade and high-grade groups both in the training cohort (median, 0.26 vs 0.99; P < 0.001) and validation cohort (median, 0.63 vs 1.48; P = 0.011). High-grade group had higher Radscore than low-grade group (Fig. 4). Discussion

In this study, machine learning models based on contrast-enhanced MRI radiomics features were constructed for predicting the pathological grade of pancreatic cancer preoperatively. Of the four machine learning models, the LR model showed good predictive performance in both AP, PVP and AP+PVP. Moreover, the LR model, based on the radiomics features of contrast-enhanced MRI AP+PVP images, has good predictive ability for the differentiation high-grade from low-grade of pancreatic cancer, which is expected to be a non-invasive method for preoperative prediction of the pathological grade of pancreatic cancer and helps to further the preoperative risk stratification of pancreatic cancer patients to aid in better clinical decision making.

The pathological grade is a very important independent prognostic factor for pancreatic cancers as evidenced by several studies, high-grade pancreatic cancer has shorter survival and higher mortality than low-grade pancreatic cancer[5], [6], [7], [8]. As an emerging field in medical technology, radiomics has been used to solve many clinical problems by a non-invasive method of extracting high-throughput quantitative data from conventional medical images to obtain quantitative information on the shape, texture and heterogeneity of the tumor itself, and to select the most valuable radiomics features and build predictive models [13], [14], [27]. At present, radiomics is now being applied to the differential diagnosis and prognosis of pancreatic cancer [28], [29], [30], [31].

In this study, there was no statistical significance (p < 0.05) in our inclusion of clinical characteristics (sex, age, CA19–9, tumor maximum diameter, primary tumor location, parenchymal atrophy, PDD, CBDD, and vascular invasion) in both the training and validation cohorts, which was consistent with the findings of previous studies [17], [25]. However, for both the training and validation cohorts, there was a statistically significant difference between low-grade and high-grade groups in terms of Radscore calculated by the LR model, indicating that radiomics is an effective tool for preoperatively identifying high and low pathological grades of pancreatic cancer.

Among the four machine learning models, the LR model performed the best and achieved the best predictive performance in AP+PVP, with an AUC value of 0.81 for the training cohort and 0.82 for the validation cohort. In contrast, the RF model exhibited a classic sign of severe overfitting, as evidenced by its perfect performance on the training cohort (AUC=1.00) and a substantial decline in the validation cohort. This overfitting compromised its generalizability and rendered it unreliable for clinical application. In comparison, the SVM and KNN models showed good predictive performance without signs of severe overfitting. Our results initially showed the good predictive efficacy of machine learning models based on contrast-enhanced MRI radiomics features for predicting the pathological grade of pancreatic cancer.

The study by Xie et al. improved the predictive accuracy of radiomics models for the pathological differentiation of pancreatic cancer by integrating radiomics features from both peritumoral and intratumoral regions based on T2WI, DWI, and DCE images, with an AUC of 0.94 for their final model on the validation cohorts [32]. However, this study utilized only single-phase DCE images, and the final model, incorporated only three DCE radiomics features with relatively low weights, failing to fully exploit the dynamic information from the multi-phase enhancement of DCE images, which is essential for reflecting the microvascular architecture and functional status of the tumor. In comparison, the strong performance of our combined AP+PVP model can be attributed to the complementary pathophysiological information captured by the two phases. Among the ten features selected for the final LR model, four were derived from AP and six from PVP, indicating distinct contributions from each phase. AP features, reflective of peak arterial enhancement, may be more associated with tumor hemodynamics such as microvascular density. Conversely, PVP features, capturing the contrast washout phase, may better characterize the tumor microenvironment, including interstitial composition and necrotic regions—hallmarks of high-grade tumors. Therefore, the integration of dual-phase features provides a more comprehensive assessment of tumor pathophysiology for grading. Notably, the key features selected by our LR model—such as wavelet-LHL_glszm_Small Area Low Gray Level Emphasis and wavelet-HLL_gldm_Small Dependence Low Gray Level Emphasis—are predominantly textural and may reflect intratumoral heterogeneity, necrotic regions, or microstructural disorganization, all of which correspond to pathological traits of high-grade pancreatic cancer. Furthermore, while Xie et al.'s approach of combining peritumoral and intratumoral texture features to evaluate tumor characteristics represents a promising direction, solely defining the peritumoral region as a fixed distance outward from the tumor boundary may inevitably include some extrapancreatic tissues [32]. This inclusion of adjacent non-tumor tissue may introduce potential noise into the radiomics analysis, potentially affecting the accuracy of the models.

At present, there are few studies on radiomics of pancreatic cancer based on MRI, probably due to the widespread use of CT in clinical practice and the high cost of MRI examinations. The radiomics model constructed by Chen et al. and Qiu et al. based on CT images achieves moderate predictive accuracy in histopathological grade prediction [17], [25], which is similar to that of our study but lower than the model constructed by Xie et al.’s model incorporating intratumoral and peritumoral radiomics features from T2WI, DWI, and single-phase MRI enhancement [32]. This indicates that both CT and MRI have their own advantages and disadvantages in predicting pancreatic cancer pathological types. MRI has disadvantages such as long imaging time, susceptibility to motion artifacts, and relatively low spatial resolution. However, MRI has greater potential than CT. The advantages of MRI over CT lie in its high-resolution imaging ability for soft tissues, enabling it to capture more detailed information related to tumor pathological characteristics, as well as its multi-parameter imaging capability, which can reflect tumor biological characteristics from multiple angles and improve the accuracy of prediction models. Finally, this study uses LR for the radiomics model. LR can output prediction probabilities, is easy to interpret and computationally efficient for linear data, but weak in handling nonlinearity, multicollinearity, and outliers. RF & SVM are better with nonlinearity; RF has poor interpretability and high complexity, SVM has kernel function selection and complexity issues. KNN is simple in principle but complex computationally and sensitive to sample imbalance. Each model has its own features, and model selection needs to consider application and data characteristics.

However, there are some limitations to this study. Firstly, it was a single-center, retrospective investigation with a relatively small sample size of 125 patients. Although our strict inclusion criteria and the use of cross-validation helped ensure stable predictive performance [33], the subsequent validation cohort consisted of only 25 patients. While this allowed for a larger training set, it resulted in wide confidence intervals for performance metrics (e.g., AUC) and limited the statistical power of the validation. Furthermore, despite employing techniques like SMOTE to mitigate the impact of the natural class imbalance between low-grade and high-grade pancreatic cancer, the small absolute number of low-grade cases remains a constraint. Secondly, although we employed multiple MRI scanners from different vendors and attempted to mitigate inter-scanner variability through image preprocessing and feature normalization (Z-score), we acknowledge that complete harmonization is challenging. However, the predictive performance of our model on the validation cohort, which contained data from all scanners, suggests a degree of robustness to this variability. Future multi-center studies with external validation are warranted to further confirm the generalizability of our model. Finally, AP and PVP images of contrast-enhanced MRI T1WI were used for the radiomics study, but whether other imaging sequences of MRI have better predictive value need to be studied in future research.

Conclusions

Our study provided preliminary evidence that machine learning models based on contrast-enhanced MRI radiomics have good predictive efficacy in predicting the pathological grade of pancreatic cancer, with the LR model based on of AP+PVP performed best.
