Identification of prognostic genes and development of a risk model for pancreatic cancer based on hypoxia- and lipid metabolism-related genes
Authors: Xiangshu Guo, Jianqi Xiong, Longxing Niu, Shaolin Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPancreatic cancer (PC) is associated with a poor prognosis and limited therapeutic options because most patients are diagnosed at an advanced stage. Hypoxia- and lipid metabolism-related genes (HLPGs) play important roles in cancer progression. This study aimed to identify HLPGs-based prognostic genes in PC and explore their potential mechanisms, thereby providing a scientific basis for clinical management.MethodsUnivariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were performed using public transcriptomic data to identify hypoxia- and lipid metabolism-related prognostic genes in PC, and a prognostic model was subsequently constructed. The underlying mechanisms were further investigated using gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA), immune microenvironment profiling, and drug sensitivity analysis in the high-risk group (HRG) and low-risk group (LRG). In addition, single-cell RNA sequencing was used to identify key cell populations in PC.ResultsINPP4B, SLCO1B3, LIPH, TGM2, ACSL5, SLC2A1, and EPHX2 were identified as prognostic genes. EPHX2 was significantly downregulated in PC, whereas the other six genes were upregulated. The prognostic model showed moderate predictive performance in the validation datasets. Pathway enrichment analysis revealed significant enrichment in the ribosome, ECM-receptor interaction, and focal adhesion pathways. Immune infiltration analysis showed that regulatory T cells were positively correlated with both myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) and T follicular helper cells. LIPH showed the strongest correlations with type 17 T helper cells and central memory CD4 T cells. HRG patients were predicted to be more sensitive to the top 10 candidate drugs, including entinostat and sorafenib, and LIPH was positively correlated with sabutoclax_1849 and AZD8055_1059. ScRNA-seq identified ductal cells as the key cell type in PC, which were further clustered into 8 subsets.ConclusionINPP4B, SLCO1B3, LIPH, TGM2, ACSL5, SLC2A1, and EPHX2 were identified as hypoxia- and lipid metabolism-related prognostic genes in PC. Distinct immune cell subsets were also characterized during PC progression. These findings provide a foundation for further mechanistic studies and potential prognostic applications.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-026-05267-8.

Introduction

Pancreatic cancer (PC) is one of the most lethal malignancies and is driven by aberrant DNA mutations that promote uncontrolled cell growth and division. It has the lowest 5-year survival rate among all cancers [1]. The pancreas contains both exocrine and endocrine components, and approximately 95% of PCs originate from exocrine cells. Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) is the most common type of PC [2]. PC is characterized by several biological features, including a dense, hypoxic, immunosuppressive, and poorly drug-permeable microenvironment, together with intrinsic metabolic adaptation and therapeutic resistance. These features contribute to its high invasiveness and marked treatment resistance [3]. In general, the risk factors for PC can be classified into factors related to individual characteristics, lifestyle and environmental exposure, and disease status [4]. In recent years, the incidence of pancreatic cancer has shown a continuous upward trend, and its ranking among cancer-related causes of death has also steadily increased. Moreover, PC is associated with extremely high mortality, with a 5-year survival rate of less than 10% [5], causing more than 400,000 deaths annually worldwide. It is currently the third leading cause of cancer-related death globally and is projected to rise to second place by 2030 [6]. At present, radical surgery combined with chemotherapy remains the only curative treatment for PC. However, because metastasis often occurs at an early stage, approximately 80% of patients have already lost the opportunity for curative surgery at the time of diagnosis, which is also an important reason for its high mortality [7]. Despite major advances in oncology research, 90% of diagnosed patients still die from PC within 5 years [8]. In addition, pancreatic adenocarcinoma (PAAD) currently lacks specific diagnostic biomarkers, and its diagnostic and prognostic evaluation systems still need to be further improved to enhance patient survival outcomes [9]. Therefore, in-depth investigation of the molecular mechanisms and potential therapeutic targets of PC is particularly urgent.

Hypoxia is a state of reduced oxygen supply accompanied by excessive oxygen consumption. According to its duration, hypoxia can be classified as acute or chronic [10, 11]. Tumor hypoxia may result from the high oxygen demand caused by uncontrolled tumor proliferation and from impaired oxygen delivery due to abnormal tumor-associated neovascularization [12]. Hypoxia also contributes to the formation of an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME) by promoting angiogenesis, metabolic reprogramming, extracellular matrix remodeling, epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), p53 inactivation, and immune evasion [13]. Lipids play key roles in cellular signaling, membrane integrity, and energy metabolism [14]. Lipid metabolism is a complex biochemical process that regulates cell survival and death [15]. Moreover, many drugs targeting lipid metabolism have been developed as anticancer strategies, and inhibition of certain key enzymes involved in lipid metabolism may suppress disease progression and metastasis [16]. Studies have shown that hypoxia can upregulate the lipid metabolism-related gene ACSS2, which exerts tumor-promoting effects by regulating lipid metabolism through activation of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway [17]. Lipid homeostasis is also essential for the survival of PDAC cells under hypoxic and nutrient‑deprived conditions. Hypoxia suppresses the biosynthesis of unsaturated lipids, forcing cancer cells to rely on exogenous unsaturated lipids to counteract the toxicity induced by saturated lipids [18]. In addition, significant differences in gene expression associated with oxygen regulation and sphingolipid metabolism contribute to differences in clinical outcomes among PDAC patients [19]. Hypoxia plays an important role in PC progression by driving various forms of metabolic reprogramming, including lipid metabolism, while lipids in turn alter the structure and function of cell membranes [20]. A novel lipid metabolism-related prognostic model has also been developed and validated to predict recurrence and survival in patients with PAAD, showing strong accuracy and stability [21]. Furthermore, hypoxia-induced changes in lipid polyunsaturation and related proteins drive the aggressive metastasis of PC through the PPAR/hypoxia pathway [22]. Notably, studies on hypoxia and lipid metabolism in PC remain relatively limited. Therefore, identifying and validating hypoxia- and lipid metabolism-related genes (HLPGs) in PC is of great significance for understanding its molecular mechanisms and discovering novel prognostic genes and therapeutic targets, which may provide more effective and optimized treatment strategies for PC and ultimately improve patient prognosis.

In this study, we integrated transcriptomic and single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data to comprehensively analyze key genes associated with hypoxia and lipid metabolism in patients with PC and to explore their expression patterns across different cell clusters. This work may help clarify the molecular pathogenesis of PC and provide a scientific basis for improving prognosis, optimizing individualized treatment strategies, and developing novel targeted therapies.

Materials and methods

Data collection

First, RNA-seq transcriptome data for the TCGA-Pancreatic Adenocarcinoma (PAAD) dataset were downloaded from the Genomic Data Commons (GDC) portal (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/) using the TCGAbiolinks R package (v 2.25.3). The RNA-seq data consisted of raw read counts quantified by HTSeq (HTSeq-Counts). Differential expression analysis was performed directly using the raw count matrix. For subsequent survival analysis, correlation analysis, and model construction, the count data were converted to fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM) and further log2(FPKM + 1)-transformed to stabilize variance and optimize data distribution. This dataset included 160 PAAD tissue samples with survival information (data access date: December 16, 2025) [23]. Second, the gene expression profiles of PC-related datasets GSE183795, GSE57495 and GSE212966 were retrieved from the GEO (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). Specifically, GSE183795 (training set) (platform: GPL6244) comprised 139 pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) samples (PDAC group) and 102 normal tissue samples (control group) [24]. GSE57495 (validation set) (platform: GPL15048) included survminer 63 PDAC tissue samples with survival information [25]. GSE212966 (single-cell dataset) (platform: GPL20301) included 6 PDAC samples and 6 normal tissue samples [26]. Moreover, 1,154 HLPGs were obtained from the literature [27] (Supplementary Table 1).

Differential expression analysis

To identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the PDAC and control groups in the GSE183795 dataset, differential expression analysis was performed using the limma package (v 3.56.2) [28], with thresholds of |log2FC| > 1 and adjusted p-value < 0.05. The top 10 upregulated and downregulated DEGs, ranked by |log2FC|, were visualized in a volcano plot, and an expression heatmap was generated to show their expression patterns.

Enrichment analysis and construction of the protein-protein interaction (PPI) network

The ggvenn package (v 0.1.10) [29] was used to identify the intersection between DEGs and HLPGs, which were defined as candidate genes. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was used to annotate gene functions according to the GO database, including biological process (BP), cellular component (CC), and molecular function (MF) categories. GO and KEGG enrichment analyses of candidate genes were performed using the clusterProfiler package (v 4.10.1) [30] (p < 0.05). Candidate genes were then timported into the STRING database (https://string-db.org/) to construct a protein-protein interaction (PPI) network (confidence score > 0.4).

Confirmation of prognostic genes

To screen for genes associated with the prognosis of patients with PC, 160 PAAD tissue samples with survival information from the TCGA-PAAD dataset were analyzed. Univariate Cox regression analysis was performed on the candidate genes using the survival package (v 3.8-3) [31], and prognostic candidate genes were identified after testing the proportional hazards (PH) assumption (p > 0.05). Multivariate Cox regression analysis was then performed on these prognostic candidate genes, followed by another PH assumption test (p > 0.05). Genes meeting these criteria were ultimately defined as prognostic genes.

Establishment of the prognostic model

To construct the risk model, a prognostic risk score was calculated for each PAAD patient in the TCGA-PAAD dataset based on the multivariate Cox regression coefficients of the prognostic genes and their expression levels. The optimal risk score cutoff was determined using the survminer package (v 0.5.0) [32], and PAAD samples were divided into the HRG and LRG accordingly. Risk curves and survival status plots were then generated. Survival differences between the HRG and LRG were evaluated using Kaplan-Meier (KM) survival analysis (p < 0.05).

To evaluate the predictive performance of the prognostic model for PAAD prognosis, the survivalROC package (v 1.0.3.1) [33] was used to generate ROC curves, and the AUC values at 1-, 3-, and 5-year follow-up time points were calculated. An AUC value greater than 0.6 indicated good predictive accuracy. Furthermore, to verify the reliability of the prognostic model, the same method described above was applied for validation in PAAD tumor tissue samples from the GSE57495 When the validation results were basically consistent with those of the TCGA-PAAD dataset, this consistency indicated that the risk model had good validity.

Expression analysis of prognostic genes

To further confirm the expression levels of the prognostic genes, a Wilcoxon test was performed (p < 0.05). The expression of prognostic genes was validated in all samples from the GSE183795 datase.

GSEA

To further investigate biological pathways associated with the HRG and LRG, differential expression analysis was performed between the two groups using the DESeq2 package (v 1.40.2) [34] based on the TCGA-PAAD dataset. The resulting log2FC values were then ranked in descending order to generate a gene list for GSEA. The c2.cp.kegg_legacy.v2025.1.Hs.symbols from the MSigDB (https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/msigdb/) was used as the background gene set. GSEA was subsequently performed using the clusterProfiler package (v 4.10.1) (|NES| > 1 and p < 0.05).

To explore the potential mechanisms of the prognostic genes in PC, GSEA was performed for each prognostic gene using all samples from the TCGA-PAAD dataset and the same criteria described above (|NES| > 1 and p < 0.05). The ggvenn package (v 0.1.10) was then used to identify pathways commonly enriched across all prognostic genes, which were subsequently visualized.

Immune infiltration and immune checkpoint analyses

To investigate immune cell infiltration in the HRG and LRG, the ssGSEA algorithm implemented in GSVA (v 1.46.0) [35] was used to evaluate the infiltration levels of 28 immune cell types [36] in the TCGA-PAAD dataset. Differences between the two groups were analyzed using the Wilcoxon test (p < 0.05), and boxplots were generated for visualization. In addition, the Spearman method implemented in the psych package (v 2.2.9) [37] was used to analyze correlations among differentially infiltrated immune cells and between prognostic genes and these immune cells (|cor| > 0.3 and p < 0.05).

To explore differences in immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs)-related genes between PAAD patients in the HRG and LRG, the expression levels of 38 immune checkpoints [38] were compared between the two groups using the Wilcoxon test (p < 0.05) based on the TCGA-PAAD dataset.

Tumor purity analysis

To further assess differences in tumor microenvironment scores between the HRG and LRG, the ESTIMATE package (v 1.0.13) [39] was used to calculate stromal, immune, and ESTIMATE scores in the TCGA-PAAD dataset based on the ESTIMATE database (https://bioinformatics.mdanderson.org/estimate/). The Wilcoxon test was then used to compare these scores between the HRG and LRG (p < 0.05).

Prediction of chemotherapeutic drug sensitivity

To investigate differences in drug sensitivity between the HRG and LRG based on the TCGA-PAAD dataset, IC50 values for PC chemotherapeutic agents were obtained from the GDSC database (https://www.cancerrxgene.org/). The pRRophetic package (v 0.5) [40] was used to estimate drug IC50 values in PAAD tumor samples. The Wilcoxon test was used to compare IC50 values between the HRG and LRG (p < 0.05). Correlation analysis was then performed between significant drugs and prognostic genes (|cor| > 0.3 and p < 0.05).

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) analysis

The Seurat package (v 5.3.0) [41] was used to analyze scRNA-seq data from the GSE212966 dataset. First, the PercentageFeatureSet function in Seurat was used for quality control (QC). The following QC criteria were applied: (1) cells with 200–4000 expressed genes were retained; (2) genes expressed in at least 3 cells were retained; (3) cells with total gene expression below 10,000 were retained; and (4) cells with mitochondrial gene content below 15% were retained. After filtering out cells and genes that did not meet these criteria, data normalization was performed using the NormalizeData function. The FindVariableFeatures function was then used to extract the top 2,000 highly variable genes (HVGs), and the data were scaled using the ScaleData function. Principal component analysis (PCA) was perfomed on the HVGs using the RunPCA function. Permutation testing was performed on the scaled HVGs using the JackStraw function, and p-values were calculated (p < 0.05). A scree plot was generated using the ElbowPlot function to determine the appropriate number of principal components (PCs).

After PCA dimensionality reduction of the GSE212966 dataset, the optimal number of clusters was determined using the JackStraw and ScoreJackStraw functions. Unsupervised clustering analysis was performed using the FindNeighbors and FindClusters functions based on selected principal components (resolution = 0.4), and cell clusters were visualized using UMAP generated by the RunUMAP function. The FindAllMarkers function was then used to identify marker genes for each cell cluster. Highly expressed genes in each cell cluster were compared with marker genes reported in previous studies [42, 43] for manual cell annotation.

To identify key cell types that differed between PDAC and control samples, the Wilcoxon test was applied to compare annotated cell types in the GSE212966 dataset (p < 0.05). The expression levels and distribution of prognostic genes across different cell clusters were also analyzed. Furthermore, the expression of prognostic genes was compared between PDAC and control samples within each annotated cell type (p < 0.05). The annotated cell type that differed significantly between PDAC and control samples and contained the largest number of significantly differentially expressed prognostic genes was defined as the key cell type for subsequent analyses.

Functional enrichment analysis

To characterize the biological pathways and functional roles of distinct cell types in PC, the analyze_sc_clusters function in the ReactomeGSA package (v 1.14.0) [44] was used to perform functional enrichment analysis for each cell type in the GSE212966 dataset. The top 10 pathways with the largest differences were selected for visualization.

Metabolic pathway analysis

To investigate cellular metabolic activity, the VISION algorithm implemented in the scMetabolism package (v 0.2.1) [45], which covers 85 KEGG metabolic pathways, was used to quantify metabolic activity at single-cell resolution. scMetabolism was further applied to evaluate metabolic activity across distinct cell types, and the DotPlot.metabolism function was used to visualize the results.

Cellular communication and pseudo-time analyses

To analyze intercellular interactions and communication patterns among distinct cell types, separate analyses were performed for PDAC and normal samples from the GSE212966 dataset. First, the CellChat package (v 1.6.1) [46] was used to characterize intercellular communication networks across all annotated cell types. Communication networks between key cells and other cell subsets were then further analyzed to construct cell-cell communication profiles. CellChat was also used to identify ligand-receptor pairs mediating intercellular communication, infer potential interactions between key cells and other cell types, and generate bubble plots for visualization.

To obtain differentiation trajectories of cell subsets within the key cell population, the Seurat package (v 5.3.0) was used for secondary clustering of key cells (resolution = 0.1). The same dimensionality reduction and clustering methods used in the primary analysis were applied. The Monocle3 package (v 2.26.0) [47] was then used to conduct pseudotime analysis of key cells and construct single-cell trajectory plots, with simultaneous evaluation of prognostic gene expression profiles across distinct developmental states.

Statistical analysis

Bioinformatics analyses were performed using R software (v 4.2.1). The Wilcoxon test was used to compare differences between two groups. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. “ns” represents no significance; *, p < 0.05; **, p < 0.01; ***, p < 0.001; and ****, p < 0.0001.

Results

Identification of DEGs

Differential expression analysis identified 281 DEGs between the PDAC and control groups in the training set (|log2FC| > 1 and adjusted p-value < 0.05). Compared with control samples, 175 genes were upregulated and 106 genes were downregulated in the PDAC group (Fig. 1A, B) (Supplementary Table 2).

Acquisition of candidate genes

The intersection between 281 DEGs and 1,154 HLPGs yielded 27 candidate genes for subsequent analysis (Fig. 2A) (Supplementary Table 3). GO enrichment analysis showed that these 27 candidate genes were enriched in 489 GO terms (p < 0.05), including 358 BP terms, such as long-chain fatty acid transport and fatty acid transport; 26 CC terms, such as blood microparticle and microvillus membrane; and 105 MF terms, such as carboxylic acid binding and lipid transporter activity (Supplementary Table 4). KEGG analysis identified enrichment in 23 signaling pathways (p < 0.05), including the adipocytokine signaling and PPAR signaling pathways (Fig. 2B) (Supplementary Table 5).

A PPI network containing 21 key nodes and 35 interactions was subsequently constructed (interaction score > 0.4), after 6 isolated nodes were removed. This network indicated relatively strong interactions among these 21 proteins, including interactions between ALB and CP and between CD36 and FABP4 (Fig. 2C).

Identification of 7 prognostic genes

Based on the 27 candidate genes, 7 candidate prognostic genes were identified after univariate Cox regression analysis and PH assumption testing (p < 0.05, HR ≠ 1, and p(PH) > 0.05). Among these genes, INPP4B, SLCO1B3, LIPH, TGM2, ACSL5, and SLC2A1 were risk factors (HR > 1), whereas EPHX2 was a protective factor (Fig. 3A-H). Multivariate Cox regression analysis and PH assumption testing (p(PH) > 0.05) were then performed for these 7 candidate prognostic genes, and all 7 were ultimately identified as prognostic genes (Fig. 3I).

Establishment of the prognostic model

A risk model was developed using the 7 prognostic genes and their multivariate Cox regression coefficients. The PAAD risk score was calculated as follows: risk score = INPP4B expression × 0.042136435 + SLCO1B3 expression × 0.224469144 + LIPH expression × 0.346856277 + TGM2 expression × 0.260218695 + ACSL5 expression × (-0.037102141) + SLC2A1 expression × (-0.050781587) + EPHX2 expression × 0.029454143 (Table 1). To evaluate this 7-gene risk model, 160 PAAD patients from the TCGA-PAAD dataset were stratified into the HRG and LRG using the optimal risk score cutoff (-0.4696754), resulting in 138 patients in the HRG and 22 in the LRG. Survival time decreased as the risk score increased (Fig. 4A). KM survival analysis showed a significant difference in survival probability between the two groups (p = 0.00013), with the HRG exhibiting lower survival probability, indicating that patients with higher risk scores had poorer prognoses (Fig. 4B). ROC curves at 1-, 3-, and 5-year time points were plotted for the TCGA-PAAD dataset. The corresponding AUC values were 0.70, 0.67, and 0.70, respectively (Fig. 4C), indicating acceptable predictive accuracy in this dataset.

The stability of the prognostic model was further evaluated using the GSE57495 dataset. The results were comparable to those obtained from the TCGA-PAAD dataset, indicating consistent model performance across the two datasets (Fig. 4D-F).

Biological pathway analysis

GSEA was performed for the HRG and LRG, and 40 pathways were enriched (|NES| > 1 and p < 0.05). Significantly enriched pathways included neuroactive ligand-receptor interaction and hematopoietic cell lineage (Fig. 6A) (Supplementary Table 6). INPP4B was enriched in 122 pathways, including ribosome and focal adhesion (Fig. 6B) (Supplementary Table 7). SLCO1B3 was enriched in 120 pathways, including ribosome and ECM-receptor interaction (Fig. 6C) (Supplementary Table 8). LIPH was enriched in 128 pathways, including olfactory transduction and neuroactive ligand-receptor interaction (Fig. 6D) (Supplementary Table 9). TGM2 was enriched in 122 pathways, including ribosome and focal adhesion (Fig. 6E) (Supplementary Table 10). ACSL5 was enriched in 123 pathways, including focal adhesion and regulation of the actin cytoskeleton (Fig. 6F) (Supplementary Table 11). Moreover, the seven prognostic genes were co-enriched in 111 pathways, including oxidative phosphorylation, glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, and the adipocytokine signaling pathway (Fig. 6I and J) (Supplementary Table 14). These co-enriched and differentially enriched pathways may contribute to PC development.

Immune microenvironment-related analysis

In the TCGA-PAAD dataset, the infiltration levels of 28 immune cell types were analyzed between the HRG and LRG (Fig. 7A). The Wilcoxon test revealed significant differences in the infiltration of 21 immune cell types (p < 0.05). Among these, 20 cell types were significantly upregulated in the HRG, including activated CD4 T cells, effector memory CD4 T cells, and macrophages (Fig. 7B). The strongest positive correlations were observed between regulatory T cells and MDSCs (cor = 0.93, p = 2.09e-64) and between regulatory T cells and T follicular helper cells (cor = 0.93, p = 1.21e-66) (Fig. 7C) (Supplementary Tables 15–16). In addition, LIPH was significantly positively correlated with type 17 T helper cells (cor = 0.46, p = 1.42e-09), and TGM2 was significantly positively correlated with natural killer cells (cor = 0.43, p = 1.62e-08) (Fig. 7D-I) (Supplementary Table 17).

Immune checkpoint analysis showed that 27 immune checkpoint genes, including NRP1, CD276, and CD40, were differentially expressed between the HRG and LRG (p < 0.05) (Fig. 7J).

Tumor purity analysis showed that stromal score (p = 0.033), immune score (p = 0.012), and ESTIMATE score (p = 0.017) were significantly higher in the HRG than in the LRG (Fig. 7K), indicating that elevated stromal, immune, and ESTIMATE scores were associated with an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment, which is generally linked to poorer prognosis.

Prediction of chemotherapeutic drug sensitivity

Chemotherapeutic drug sensitivity prediction showed that 94 drugs differed significantly between the HRG and LRG (p < 0.05). Among the top 10 candidate drugs, all were predicted to show higher sensitivity in the HRG, including entinostat and sorafenib (Fig. 8A). Furthermore, LIPH showed the strongest positive correlations with sabutoclax_1849 (cor = 0.72, p = 2.29e-26) and AZD8055_1059 (cor = 0.70, p = 3.89e-22), whereas EPHX2 showed the strongest negative correlation with TAF1_5496_1732 (cor = -0.65, p = 7.52e-21) (Fig. 8B-H) (Supplementary Table 18). These results were derived entirely from in silico predictions and have not been experimentally validated in PDAC cell lines with known risk scores. Notably, sorafenib is not a standard chemotherapeutic agent for pancreatic cancer. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted only as preliminary bioinformatics clues, and their clinical relevance requires further experimental validation.

Ductal cells as key drivers in PC progression

Before QC, the initial scRNA-seq dataset included 69,105 cells and 27,579 genes (Fig. 9A). After QC, 41,339 cells were retained, whereas the number of genes remained 27,579 (Fig. 9B). The top 2,000 HVGs and 30 PCs were selected for cell clustering (p < 0.05) (Fig. 9C-E). UMAP clustering classified the cells into 25 clusters (resolution = 0.4) (Fig. 9F and G), which were annotated using marker genes into 10 cell types (Fig. 9H-J): T cells, fibroblasts, macrophages, ductal cells, B cells, neutrophils, endothelial cells, mast cells, acinar cells, and endocrine cells.

Ductal cells differed significantly between PDAC and control samples (p < 0.01) (Fig. 9K). In addition, all prognostic genes except EPHX2 were expressed in ductal cells and showed significant differences between PDAC and control samples (p < 0.01) (Fig. 9L-N). Therefore, ductal cells were identified as the key cell type for subsequent downstream analyses.

Cellular communication and pseudo-time analyses of key cells

Cell communication analysis revealed that compared with the control group, the number of communications sent by ductal cells to most other cell types was increased in the PDAC group, particularly between ductal cells and acinar cells, neutrophils, B cells, and T cells (Fig. 11A-D). The weight/strength of communications sent by ductal cells to B cells was the strongest in both the PDAC and control groups (Fig. 11E-H). Moreover, among communication signals sent by ductal cells to all other cell types, the strongest interactions were mediated by the MIF-(CD74 + CXCR4) and MIF-(CD74 + CD44) ligand-receptor pairs in both groups (Fig. 11I-J).

The top 20 PCs were selected for cell clustering (Fig. 11K-L). The UMAP clustering classified the cells into 8 cell clusters (resolution = 0.1) (Fig. 11M-N).

Pseudotime analysis revealed that ductal cells progressed from dark blue to light blue and were classified into three stages (1–3) according to their developmental states, with each stage represented by a distinct color. The pseudotemporal distribution of each cell subset showed that clusters 4, 6, and 7 corresponded to the early stage; clusters 1, 2, and 3 corresponded to late developmental stages; and cluster 0 spanned all developmental stages. When stratified by disease status, the developmental trajectories of key cells differed between PDAC and control samples. In the control group, ductal cells were mainly distributed in the early stage, whereas in the PDAC group, they were distributed throughout the entire developmental trajectory, reflecting the impact of disease status on cell developmental dynamics (Fig. 11O). During cell differentiation, INPP4B expression was low in the early stages and peaked in the middle and late stages; SLCO1B3 and LIPH initially increased and then decreased; TGM2 and SLC2A1 exhibited fluctuating expression patterns, and maintained high expression levels in the middle and late stages, and ACSL5 expression gradually increased as differentiation progressed. EPHX2 expression was high in the early stage, decreased in the middle stage, and then rose and fell again in the late stage (Fig. 11P).

Discussion

Hypoxia and lipid metabolism play critical roles in pancreatic cancer (PC) progression, but their mechanisms and effects on the tumor microenvironment remain incompletely understood [19, 27, 48]. In this study, we systematically investigated key hypoxia- and lipid metabolism-related genes (HLPGs) in PC and characterized their expression across different cell clusters.

In the present study, we identified seven genes with significant prognostic stratification value in PC: INPP4B, SLCO1B3, LIPH, TGM2, ACSL5, SLC2A1, and EPHX2. These genes were significantly dysregulated in PC and closely associated with patient prognosis, suggesting their potential value as molecular biomarkers and therapeutic targets for individualized treatment. The prognostic model based on these seven genes achieved time-dependent ROC AUC values ranging from 0.64 to 0.78 at 1, 3, and 5 years. In contrast, the model developed by Xu et al., based solely on lipid metabolism-related genes, showed 1- to 3-year AUC values of only 0.63–0.65 in the TCGA cohort [49]. These findings highlight the value of integrating multidimensional biological features to improve the accuracy of risk stratification in pancreatic cancer.

Among the identified genes, INPP4B appears to be a particularly relevant oncogenic factor in PC. As a key regulator of phosphoinositide homeostasis [50]. INPP4B has been reported to negatively regulate the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway [51], which is one of the most frequently dysregulated oncogenic networks and a major target for anticancer drug development [52]. Previous studies have shown that INPP4B can promote PC cell migration and invasion by regulating fibronectin exocytosis [53], and its upregulation in PC tissues has been associated with enhanced proliferation and reduced apoptosis both in vitro and in vivo [54]. More recently, INPP4B was shown to facilitate malignant progression of PDAC through PIKfyve- and TRPML-1-mediated lysosomal exocytosis [55]. Taken together, these findings suggest that INPP4B may exert pro-tumorigenic effects through multiple mechanisms, including PI3K/AKT signaling, fibronectin exocytosis, and lysosomal exocytosis. This is consistent with our observation that high INPP4B expression predicts poor prognosis in PC.

LIPH also emerged as an important candidate gene linking hypoxia and lipid metabolism. As a member of the mammalian triglyceride lipase family [56], LIPH has been identified as a downstream target of HIF-1α and shown to promote tumor cell migration under hypoxic conditions [57], supporting its close association with both hypoxia and lipid metabolic regulation. Previous studies have highlighted the importance of LIPH in multiple malignancies [58]. In PDAC, LIPH has been reported to connect tumor cells with the tumor microenvironment by activating the LPA/LPAR axis and maintaining ALDOA stability, thereby promoting aberrant aerobic glycolysis and supporting the feasibility of gemicitabine based combination therapy [59]. In addition, LIPH is significantly overexpressed in tumor tissues and has been associated with immune suppression or immune escape, indicating that it may serve as a novel biomarker of poor prognosis in PC [60]. Consistently, high LIPH expression has also been linked to worse overall survival in PAAD [61]. These findings suggest that LIPH may contribute to tumor metabolic reprogramming, microenvironmental remodeling, and immune evasion, and may therefore represent a promising therapeutic target for improving prognosis and chemosensitivity in PC.

Similarly, TGM2 may play a multifaceted role in PC progression. TGM2 is involved in extracellular matrix degradation, apoptosis, and signal transduction [62], and its broad subcellular distribution suggests functional diversity. Increasing evidence indicates that TGM2 participates in tumor proliferation, metastasis, and drug resistance [63]. Hypoxia has been shown to induce TGM2 expression through HIF-1α signaling [64], while TGM2-related pathways have also been implicated in lipid deposition [65], further supporting its relevance to hypoxia and lipid metabolism. In PDAC, TGM2-mediated histone serotonylation at the SCD promoter drives tumor development through lipid metabolic remodeling [66]. TGM2 has also been linked to Akt/mTOR signaling in PC cells [67], gemcitabine resistance through the TGM2-P2RX7 loop by enhancing glucose metabolism and mitochondrial uptake [68], and T-cell suppression through microtubule-dependent secretion of immunosuppressive cytokines [69]. Collectively, these studies indicate that TGM2 may function as a marker of poor prognosis in PC and promote tumor progression, chemoresistance, and immune suppression through coordinated effects on hypoxia signaling, metabolic reprogramming, and the tumor immune microenvironment.

In contrast, EPHX2 was identified as a protective factor in our study. EPHX2 encodes a key metabolic enzyme involved in the degradation of endogenous lipid epoxides, particularly the inactivation of EETs [70]. Although relatively few studies have explored the role of EPHX2 in cancer, available evidence suggests that it participates in lipid metabolism [71]. Because inflammatory or hypoxic stimuli can induce cytochrome P450 epoxygenase-mediated EETs production, and EPHX2 is responsible for hydrolyzing of most EETs in vitro [72], EPHX2 appears to be functionally connected to both hypoxia and lipid metabolism. To date, studies on EPHX2 in PC remain scarce. However, recent evidence suggests that EPHX2 may act as a tumor-suppressive metabolic regulator that inhibits tumor growth by affecting cell-cycle dynamics and interacting with multiple signaling pathways [73]. In addition, EPHX2 has been ranked among the top prognostic markers for PDAC in artificial intelligence-based models. Therefore, the significant downregulation of EPHX2 in PC, together with its association with a favorable prognosis in our study, supports its potential tumor-suppressive role and highlights its possible clinical value in prognostic evaluation and therapeutic exploration.

Overall, our findings suggest that HLPGs are deeply involved in PC progression and prognosis. In particular, INPP4B, LIPH, and TGM2 appear to function predominantly as risk-associated genes linked to oncogenic signaling, metabolic remodeling, therapeutic resistance, and immune suppression, whereas EPHX2 may represent a protective metabolic factor with tumor-suppressive potential. These results extend the current understanding of the interplay between hypoxia and lipid metabolism in PC, and provide a theoretical basis for future mechanistic studies prognostic biomarker development, and targeted therapeutic strategies.

We further found that the seven prognostic genes identified in this study were collectively enriched in several KEGG pathways involving both hypoxia response and lipid metabolic regulation, including OXPHOS, glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, and the adipocytokine signaling pathway. Pancreatic cancer cells undergo substantial metabolic reprogramming in the hypoxic microenvironment, characterized by enhanced glycolysis and altered utilization of metabolic substrates [74]. This metabolic shift helps overcome the suppression of oxidative phosphorylation [75, 76]. The risk genes identified in our model may be closely associated with this process. Mascaraque et al. demonstrated that fatty acid oxidation (FAO), by maintaining mitochondrial OXPHOS activity, is essential for the tumorigenic potential and chemoresistance of pancreatic cancer stem cells (PaCSCs). Inhibition of fatty acid metabolism can suppress OXPHOS, leading to cellular energy depletion and cell death [77]. This provides a plausible explanation for how the risk genes in our model may promote tumor progression by enhancing fatty acid metabolism-driven OXPHOS activity. In addition, dysfunctional adipose tissue has been shown to promote pancreatic cancer initiation and progression in the context of obesity through the secretion of adipocytokines such as leptin and adiponectin [78]. Moreover, co-culture with adipocytes can activate hypoxia signaling in pancreatic cancer cells, suggesting that the adipocytokine signaling pathway may serve as a key hub linking obesity, tumor metabolism, and poor prognosis [79]. Taken together, these seven prognostic genes reveal core mechanisms underlying pancreatic cancer progression from the perspective of hypoxia–lipid metabolism crosstalk, and provide potential biomarkers and a theoretical basis for individualized therapeutic strategies targeting OXPHOS, fatty acid oxidation, or adipocytokine signaling pathways.

Our immune infiltration analysis revealed marked differences in immune microenvironmental features between the HRG and LRG, providing important clues for understanding immune regulation in PC. In particular, activated CD4 T cells, effector memory CD4 T cells, and macrophages showed higher infiltration levels in the HRG. T cells play a central role in cancer immunotherapy [80], and CD4 T cells have long been recognized as key participants in both innate and antigen-specific immune responses [81]. Upon activation through antigen recognition and co-stimulatory signaling, CD4 T cells proliferate and secrete a broad range of cytokines and chemokines [82], thereby shaping antitumor immunity. Previous studies have shown that enhancing tumor-infiltrating CD4 T-cell responses can delay tumor growth, alleviate CTL exhaustion, and reduce Treg abundance in PC models [83]. In addition, CD44, an important functional molecule on central memory CD4 T cells, is closely linked to cell homing, activation, immune memory maintenance, and antitumor immune responses [84]. These findings suggest that activated CD4 T cells and their associated functional programs may play critical roles in immune surveillance and antitumor immunity in PC, potentially influencing disease progression and prognosis through regulation of specific immune responses, T-cell activation, and memory maintenance.

Macrophages also appear to be central components of the PC immune microenvironment. Although pro-inflammatory M1 macrophages can phagocytose tumor cells, M2-like macrophages, including tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), promote tumor growth and invasion [85]. By regulating angiogenesis, lymphangiogenesis, immune suppression, hypoxia, tumor-cell proliferation, and metastasis, macrophages contribute to multiple protumorigenic processes [86]. Moreover, crosstalk between macrophages and cancer cells has been shown to aggravate PC-associated cachexia [87]. Recent evidence further indicates that alterations in macrophage-associated inflammatory signaling can reshape PDAC progression. As one of the most abundant immune infiltrates in the PC stroma, macrophages interact extensively with cancer cells, adipocytes, and other stromal components to regulate metabolism, inflammation, and immune status, thereby promoting an immunosuppressive TME and facilitating tumor initiation and progression [31, 88, 89]. Collectively, these observations highlight the importance of macrophage polarization and intercellular communication in remodeling the PC microenvironment. In particular, M2-like TAM-mediated immune suppression, inflammatory dysregulation, and stromal crosstalk may jointly drive tumor proliferation, invasion, and cachexia, suggesting that targeting macrophage polarization and related inflammatory pathways may help reverse the immunosuppressive milieu in PC.

Single-cell transcriptomics further identified ductal cells as a core cell subpopulation linking hypoxia signaling with lipid metabolic reprogramming in pancreatic cancer. Under hypoxic conditions, tumor cells undergo metabolic reprogramming to maintain survival, characterized by enhanced pyruvate metabolism and aerobic glycolysis. The metabolic phenotype of PC is marked by upregulation of glycolytic enzymes, such as HK2 and PKM2, and preferential conversion of pyruvate into lactate even under aerobic conditions, known as the Warburg effect. This process not only ensures energy supply but also supports lipid biosynthesis [90–92]. In this context, ductal cells exhibited significantly increased metabolic activity in pathways such as pyruvate metabolism and glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, which is highly consistent with the broadly enhanced glycolytic phenotype observed in PDAC. Notably, cell–cell communication analysis showed that the communication strength between ductal cells and immune cells was markedly enhanced in PDAC through the MIF–(CD74 + CXCR4) and MIF–(CD74 + CD44) ligand–receptor axes. MIF is a key hypoxia-induced cytokine. Extracellular MIF mainly binds to CD74 and its co-receptor CD44, thereby activating downstream signaling pathways and promoting cancer cell proliferation and invasion [93, 94]. Previous studies have demonstrated that MIF can interact with CSN5, and that hypoxia-induced CSN5/HIF-1α interaction is substantially impaired in MIF-deficient cells, forming a positive-feedback amplification loop that enhances the hypoxic response in pancreatic cancer cells [93]. Meanwhile, elevated MIF expression is significantly associated with poor prognosis in patients with resectable PDAC, and high MIF expression is independently correlated with shorter survival [95]. Functionally, binding of MIF to CD74/CD44 receptors can activate multiple downstream signaling pathways, including ERK, PI3K–Akt, and NF-κB, thereby regulating cell proliferation, survival, migration, and inflammatory responses [96–98]. Taken together, ductal cells may connect hypoxia signaling, lipid metabolic remodeling, and immune microenvironment regulation through an MIF-mediated single-cell communication network, providing new cellular and molecular insights into the malignant progression of pancreatic cancer. Nevertheless, further experimental validation is required.

In this study, transcriptomic and bioinformatics analyses identified seven hypoxia- and lipid metabolism-related prognostic genes in PC, including INPP4B, SLCO1B3, LIPH, TGM2, ACSL5, SLC2A1, and EPHX2. Based on these genes, we performed expression profiling, enrichment analysis, immune microenvironment characterization, drug sensitivity prediction, and scRNA-seq-based mapping of gene expression across different cell clusters, providing a multi-omics basis for elucidating PC mechanisms and supporting individualized therapy. However, all conclusions of this study are based on bioinformatics analyses. The study lacks validation of the protein expression levels of the seven prognostic genes in an independent pancreatic cancer tissue cohort, as well as in vitro and in vivo functional experiments. Moreover, the results are highly dependent on data quality, algorithmic accuracy, and analytical assumptions, which may introduce noise and lead to false-positive or false-negative findings, making it difficult to fully reflect actual biological processes. Therefore, in future work, we plan to collect independent clinical samples and validate protein expression levels using immunohistochemistry and Western blotting. We will also perform overexpression or knockdown experiments in pancreatic cancer cell lines to assess phenotypes including cell proliferation, migration, invasion, apoptosis, and cell-cycle progression. In addition, nude mouse xenograft models will be established to evaluate the effects of key genes on tumor growth and metastasis in vivo. These analyses will be further integrated with metabolomics and hypoxic microenvironment simulation experiments to explore the specific molecular mechanisms by which these genes regulate crosstalk between hypoxia and lipid metabolism.
