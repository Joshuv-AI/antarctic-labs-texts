Transcriptional profiling of the hippocampus and hypothalamus reveals a potential role of thyroid hormone in gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA) synthesis in dairy cows fed a high-concentrate diet
Authors: Manman Hou, Chen Wu, Qu Chen, Pin Song, Shengyong Mao, Yingdong Ni
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Subacute rumen acidosis (SARA) is a common metabolic disorder in dairy cows fed high-concentrate (HC) diets in intensive farming systems. No typical clinical sign of illness but some common signs include decreased dry matter intake, laminitis and diarrhea are observed in HC cows indicating a certain stressful and depressive behavior. However, transcriptional change in energy metabolic center of the hippocampus and hypothalamus in HC cows has not been reported. Twelve mid-lactation dairy cows were randomly assigned to a HC group (60% concentrate, n = 6) or a low-concentrate (LC) group (40% concentrate, n = 6) for 21 days. At the end of the feeding trial, hippocampus and hypothalamus tissues were collected from dairy cows fed a HC or LC diet for transcriptomic analysis. To verify transcriptomic findings, murine hypothalamic N38 cells were used to investigate the regulatory effect of thyroid hormone on gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA) synthesis in vitro. In the hippocampus, RNA sequencing results revealed that differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were primarily enriched in neuroactive ligand-receptor interaction, serotonergic, and dopaminergic synapse pathways. In the hypothalamus, DEGs were significantly enriched in the neuroactive ligand-receptor interaction and GABAergic synapse pathways. In the hypothalamus of HC cows, the levels of GABA and its key synthetic enzyme GAD65 were significantly reduced, along with decreased free T3 (FT3) concentration and THRB receptor protein expression. In vitro, T3 significantly upregulated both mRNA and protein expression levels of GAD65 and THRB. Under THRB gene silencing, T3 treatment failed to increase the mRNA and protein expression of THRB and GAD65 in hypothalamic N38 cells. These findings suggest that a HC diet alters neurotransmitter pathways and reduces T3 levels and THRB expression in the hypothalamus, which may impair GABA synthesis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12917-025-05078-z.

Introduction

The intensive farming systems for dairy cows have shifted towards feeding high-concentrate (HC) diets to meet their nutritional requirements for high-yield performance [1, 2]. However, these fermentation-friendly HC diets lead to rapid production of organic acids including volatile fatty acids (VFAs) and lactic acid in the rumen. The accumulation of VFAs decrease ruminal pH to non physiological levels, leading to the metabolic disorder of subacute rumen acidosis (SARA) when the rate of organic acids production exceeds the rumen’s ability to metabolize and buffer acids [3–5]. SARA is one of the most common metabolic disorders in ruminant farming systems, and it affects animal feed intake and digestive function, causing a series of complications such as diarrhea, laminitis, liver swelling and even systemic inflammation [6, 7].

SARA usually leads to reduced dry matter intake (DMI), which is considered a clinical sign in SARA diagnosis [4, 8, 9]. Elevated ruminal osmotic pressure and the low-grade inflammation caused by SARA may contribute to appetite depression [6, 10]. Observations of animal behavior have indicated that SARA also results in varying levels of depression (apathy/lethargy) [11], suggesting potential disturbances in the central nervous system (CNS). Our previous study showed that SARA induced by HC diets caused chronic stress with a high level of cortisol and the activation of hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis in dairy goats [12]. These results suggest that SARA may lead to changes in central nervous system regulation. The hippocampus is a brain region that receives input from modulatory neurotransmitter systems such as serotonin, norepinephrine, and dopamine. Environmental factors have been shown to influence hippocampal neurogenesis, which is not only involved in regulating cognitive processes but also associated with depression-like behaviors [13]. As the highest level autonomic nerve center, the hypothalamus is a key brain region responsible for the central regulation of metabolic homeostasis and energy balance. It is well established that the hypothalamus controls food intake through various pathways, including neuroendocrine activities in both ruminants and non-ruminants [14, 15].

Currently, the neural mechanism underlying appetite suppression and behavioral depression in SARA animals remain unreported. Therefore, it is important to investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying the impact of SARA on the CNS by exploring the transcriptome of the hippocampus and hypothalamus in dairy cows. The objective of this study was to explore the changes in genes and signaling pathways in the hippocampus and hypothalamus in HC cows using RNA-seq. Furthermore, hypothalamic N38 cells were used to verify the internal regulation of thyroid hormone on GABA production in the hypothalamus. These findings will contribute to a better understanding of how ruminal metabolic disorders influence neural signaling pathways in energy metabolism regulation and the internal neuroendocrine mechanisms in dairy cows.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

Animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Nanjing Agricultural University. All procedures of the ‘‘Guidelines on Ethical Treatment of Experimental Animals’’ (2006) No. 398 set by the Ministry of Science and Technology, China and the Regulation regarding the ‘‘Management and Treatment of Experimental Animals’’ (2008) No. 45 set by the Jiangsu Provincial People’s Government, were strictly followed during the slaughter and sampling procedures.

Animals and experimental procedures

This experiment was conducted using animals and procedures partially described in a previous study [16], including the diet formulation and feeding management. In this study, we further extended the research by focusing on the collection of brain tissues (hippocampus and hypothalamus) to explore the central regulatory mechanisms affected by high-concentrate diets in dairy cows. Specifically, a total of 12 healthy Chinese Holstein cows (body weight, 651 ± 54 kg; mean ± SEM) during mid-lactation (lactation day, 223 ± 16 d; milk yield, 17.43 ± 4.04 kg/day; mean ± SEM) were selected from a commercial dairy farm in Taizhou, Jiangsu Province, China, for this study. All cows were in their second parity or third parity and housed in individual pens with the same management condition during the experiment period. After a 7-day adaptive pre-feeding period, cows were assigned randomly to a high-concentrate group (HC, n = 6) or a low-concentrate group (LC, n = 6). Cows in the HC group were fed diets in which concentrate accounted for 60% of the dry matter, while the LC group received diets with 40% concentrate on a dry matter basis for 21 days. The cows were fed a total mixed ration (TMR) twice daily at 08:00 and 20:00. The diets consisted mainly of corn, soybean meal, distillers dried grains with solubles (DDGS), alfalfa, oat grass, and corn silage. The diets were formulated to meet the nutritional requirements recommended by the NRC (2001). Specifically, the LC and HC diets provided 16.12% and 16.2% crude protein, 29.92% and 27.75% neutral detergent fiber (NDF), 3.05% and 3.01% ether extract (EE), 17.96% and 30.65% starch, and 1.64 and 1.66 Mcal/kg of net energy for lactation (NEL), respectively. Detailed diet formulation and nutrient composition have been previously reported [16]. Dry matter intake (DMI) (kg/day) was recorded daily (LC = 23.72, HC = 20.82, SEM = 0.68, P = 0.639). Cows were housed in individual pens with individual feeding gates and had ad libitum access to feed and water during the whole experiment.

At the end of the experiment, cows were slaughtered via electric stunning following 12 h fasting. The tissues of the hippocampus and hypothalamus were collected and frozen in liquid nitrogen immediately. Snap-frozen tissue samples were stored at −80 °C until further analysis.

Cell culture and cell viability

To validate the transcriptomic results, we used embryonic mouse hypothalamus cell line N38 (mHypoE-N38) as an in vitro model, as they are well-characterized, readily available, and suitable for studies on hypothalamic regulatory mechanisms. The N38 cells were donated by South China Agricultural University. N38 cells were cultured in low-glucose DMEM (LG-DMEM, Wisent, China) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, TransGen Biotech, China) and antibiotics (100 IU/mL penicillin and 100 IU/mL streptomycin) at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ incubator.

Cell viability was assessed using a Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay (Cellcook, China). N38 cells were seeded in 96-well plates and, upon reaching 85–90% confluence, were treated with T₃ at final concentrations of 0, 10, 50, 100, 200, and 400 nM for 24 h. After treatment, 10 µL of CCK-8 solution was added to each well, followed by incubation at 37 °C for an additional 4 h. The absorbance at 450 nm was then measured. All samples were analyzed in triplicate to ensure accuracy and reproducibility.

Cell transfection

The sequences of small interfering RNA targeting thyroid hormone beta (THRB siRNA) were described by Liu et al. [17]. The siRNA and their corresponding negative controls were synthesized by Tsingke Company (Nanjing, China). The THRB siRNA was transfected into N38 cells using jetPRIME (Polyplus-transfection, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol, and a non-targeting siRNA was used as the negative control. The cells were incubated for 48 h before using in subsequent experiments. All samples were run in triplicate to ensure accuracy and reproducibility.

RNA extraction and quality control

Frozen samples from three cows in each group were employed for transcriptomic analysis by RNA-seq in this study. RNA-seq library preparation and sequencing were performed by Biomarker Technologies Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). Specifically, total RNA was extracted from the hippocampus or hypothalamus using TRIzol reagent (Sangon, Shanghai, China) following the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA concentration and purity were measured using the NanoDrop 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Wilmington, DE). RNA integrity was assessed using the RNA Nano 6000 Assay Kit of the Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 system (Agilent Technologies, CA, USA). Only RNA samples with RNA Integrity Number (RIN) values ≥ 7.0 were used for library preparation and RNA sequencing.

cDNA library construction and transcriptome sequencing

Sequencing libraries were generated using the NEBNext Ultra™ RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (NEB, USA) following the manufacturer’s recommendations and index codes were added to assign sequences to each sample. Library fragments were purified with the AMPure XP system (Beckman Coulter, Beverly, USA) and library quality was assessed on the Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 system. The clustering of the index-coded samples was performed on a cBot Cluster Generation System using the TruSeq PE Cluster Kit v4-cBot-HS (Illumina). After cluster generation, the library preparations were sequenced on an Illumina platform and paired-end reads were generated.

Raw data (raw reads) of fastq format were first processed using in-house perl scripts. In this step, clean data (clean reads) were obtained by removing reads containing adapters, reads containing poly-N and low-quality reads. These clean reads were then mapped to the reference genome sequence using HISAT2 [18]. Only reads with a perfect match or one mismatch were further analyzed and annotated based on the reference genome. Gene expression levels were estimated by fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM) [19]. In order to identify genes with differential expression between groups, biological variability was taken into consideration [20, 21]. Differential expression analysis between two groups was performed using the DESeq R package (1.10.1) [22]. The resulting p-values were adjusted using the Benjamini and Hochberg’s approach for controlling the false discovery rate (FDR). Genes identified by DESeq with FDR ≤ 0.05 and |fold change (FC)| ≥ 2 were defined as differentially expressed. BLAST software was used to annotate the gene ontology (GO) and Kyoto encyclopedia of genes and genomes (KEGG) databases to obtain the gene functional annotation [23].

Quantitative real-time PCR

The extracted total RNA of hippocampus and hypothalamus was treated with RNase-Free DNase (M6101; Promega, Madison, WI, USA) and reverse transcribed to cDNA using the ABScript III RT Master Mix (ABclonal, Wuhan, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

For RNA extraction, N38 cells were seeded in 6-well plates and cultured in LG-DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and antibiotics at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ incubator until reaching approximately 80% confluence, followed by treatment with T3 for 24 h. Total RNA was extracted using the TRIzol reagent (R401, Vazyme, Nanjing, China), and the concentration was measured using a NanoDrop ND-1000 Spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Madison, WI, USA). Total RNA samples were reverse transcribed into cDNA using the ABScript III RT Master Mix (ABclonal, Wuhan, China).

Quantitative real-time PCR was performed using two microliters of diluted cDNA (1:40, v/v) with the Genious 2X® SYBR Green Fast qPCR Mix (ABclonal, Wuhan, China) in an Mx3000P (Stratagene, Palo Alto, CA, USA). GAPDH expression was stable across all groups and was chosen as the reference gene. Details of primer sequences are shown in Supplementary Tables 1 & 2. All the primers were synthesized by Tsingke Company (Nanjing, China). Genes expression data was calculated using 2−ΔΔCt method. All samples were run in triplicate to ensure accuracy and reproducibility.

Western blot analysis

Hypothalamic tissue and N38 cells were lysed using an ice-cold RIPA buffer containing the protease inhibitor cocktail Complete EDTA-free (Roche, Penzberg, Germany). Total protein concentration was determined using a BCA Protein Assay kit (Thermo, USA). Western blot analysis was performed as previously described [24]. In brief, equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes, followed by blocking and incubation with primary antibodies and HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies. Antibodies including rabbit-anti-GAD67 (1:1000; A2938, ABclonal), rabbit-anti-GAD65 (1:1000; A0971, ABclonal), rabbit-anti-THRB (1:1000; AF8157, Beyotime), goat anti-rabbit HRP-conjugated (1:10000; BS13278, Bioword), GAPDH (1:10000; MB001H, Bioword) and ACTB (1:10000; KC-5AO8, Kangchen bio-tech) were used in this study. Protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) reagents and captured using a gel imaging system. Densitometric analysis of the bands was performed using the ImageJ software (NIH, USA). The relative expression levels of target proteins were normalized to the reference proteins.

In some experiments, membranes were cut prior to antibody incubation to probe for multiple targets simultaneously. In several blots, regions with strong non-specific background were masked during chemiluminescent exposure to enhance visualization of specific bands, which may result in incomplete display of membrane edges. All available uncropped Western blot images, including membrane edges and replicates, have been provided in the Supplementary Information.

Glutamate and GABA analysis

The determination of glutamate (Glu) and GABA in hypothalamic tissues was performed by ultra performance liquid chromatography (UPLC) according to the method of Stragierowicz with minor modifications [25]. In brief, frozen hypothalamic tissues were homogenized in 5 equivalents of physiological saline (0.9%), followed by further homogenization after the addition of 4 M perchloric acid before centrifugation. The supernatants were collected for analysis. The protein concentration homogenized in physiological saline was determined before the addition of perchloric acid. The derivatization was performed by mixing 40 µL diluted sample (1:15, v/v) or standard solution (Glu and GABA, Sigma-Aldrich, USA), 8 µL o-phthaldialdehyde (OPA) daily prepared (5 mg OPA/mL of methanol), 30 µL borate buffer (50 mM, pH = 9.9) and 2 µL β-Mercaptoethanol (β-ME). The solution was vortexed, and incubated for 2 min at room temperature and then injected into the UPLC system for analysis.

Chromatographic analyses were performed with an Acquity UPLC system (Waters, USA), using an Acquity UPLC BEH C18 column (1.7 μm, 100 × 2.1 mm) from Waters. The mobile phase consisted of 100 mM acetate buffer (pH = 6.0) and methanol (95:5, v/v). Chromatographic analysis was performed at the column temperature of 35 °C. The flow rate was kept constant at 0.25 mL/min. Fluorescence detection was performed using a Waters Acquity FLR Detector (Waters, USA). The instrument was operated using an excitation wavelength of 340 nm and an emission wavelength of 455 nm. Each treatment group included six cows as biological replicates and only one measurement was performed per cow.

Measurement of thyroid hormone

The method for extracting hypothalamic free triiodothyronine (FT3) was described by Beckett et al. [26]. The level of FT3 was determined by commercial radioimmunoassay kits according to the manufacturer’s protocol, provided by North Institute of Biotechnology Co. Ltd (Beijing, China). The detection range of the product was from 1 to 54 fmol/mL, with a sensitivity of 0.5 fmol/mL. The intra-assay variation was less than 10% (CV < 10%), and the inter-assay variation was less than 15% (CV < 15%). Each treatment group included six cows as biological replicates and only one measurement was performed per cow.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as the mean ± SEM, and statistical differences between two groups were analyzed using an independent-samples t-test via the Compare Means module in SPSS 19.0 for Windows (StatSoft Inc, Tulsa, OK, USA). Statistical differences among multiple groups were evaluated using one-way ANOVA, followed by Tukey’s post hoc test to adjust for multiple comparisons. Differences were considered to be statistically significant at p < 0.05.

Results

Sequencing and reads assembly

Hippocampal and hypothalamic tissues were processed for transcriptome sequencing by the RNA-seq technique. After removing adaptor sequence and low-quality sequence, each sample yielded at least 6.11 Gb of clean data, with a minimum of 94.15% of the bases achieving a quality score of Q30 (base quality ≥ 30). The GC content of the samples ranged from 48.17% to 49.77%. Clean reads of each sample were mapped to the specified reference genome, with mapping ratios ranging from 95.53% to 96.09%, and the uniquely mapped reads accounting for 93.93% and 94.58%.

Correlation analysis of RNA-seq samples revealed that the correlation coefficients (R2) among the three biological replicates within each group were all greater than 0.86, indicating an extremely positive correlation (Supplementary Figure S1A, B). Distribution of gene expression in each sample showed that the median of FPKM remained largely consistent with no significant outliers (Supplementary Figure S1C).

GADs are the rate-limiting enzymes responsible for the production of GABA. The RNA-seq results showed that GAD2 was down-regulated in HC dairy cows compared to the control counterparts, thus the level of GAD protein expression and GABA concentration in hypothalamic tissues was further examined in this study. Western blot results confirmed that GAD65 (encoded by GAD2) was significantly decreased in the hypothalamus of the HC group compared with the LC group (p < 0.05), while no significant difference in the protein level of GAD67 (encoded by GAD1) was noted between these two groups (p > 0.05) (Fig. 5 A). Chromatographic analyses showed that there was no significant difference in the hypothalamic glutamate content (p > 0.05) (Fig. 5B). Effect of interference with THRB on the expression of GAD65 in N38 cells

To further investigate the regulatory mechanism, THRB expression in N38 cells was silenced using THRB siRNA. Following transfection, THRB expression was significantly downregulated at the transcriptional level (p < 0.05) (Fig. 8 A), and GAD65 expression was significantly reduced at both the transcriptional and protein levels (p < 0.05) (Fig. 8A&B). Under THRB gene silencing, treatment with 100 nM T3 failed to induce an increase in the expression of THRB and GAD65 in N38 cells (Fig. 8C&D). Discussion

SARA is a common metabolic disorder in intensive feeding system for dairy cows. Affected cows often exhibit stressful behaviors such as decreased dry matter intake and depressive behavior, normally the more severe the case of SARA, the greater the degree of depression [11, 27]. However, the status of the neural molecular pathways in SARA has not been reported. Feed intake depression is a major issue in dairy production, persisting from late gestation through early lactation [28]. However, this study was conducted in mid-lactation cows, and whether the identified regulatory mechanisms apply to cows during the gestation or early lactation periods remains to be further investigated. In this study, RNA sequencing revealed that significant changes in neurotransmitter pathways occurred in both the hippocampus and the hypothalamus of HC cows compared to the control. It has been reported that hypofunction of monoamine neurotransmitters such as 5-hydroxytryptamine (5-HT), dopamine (DA), and norepinephrine (NE) leads to depression-like behaviors [29, 30], and almost all serotonin receptors within the brain are involved in the development of depression [31, 32]. In rats, a persistent depressive state following chronic stress is associated with a decrease in hippocampal DA [33], and dopamine D1 receptor agonists improve despairing behavior in mice [34]. Our results showed that the pathways of serotonergic synapse, GABAergic synapse, dopaminergic synapse and cholinergic synapse were significantly enriched and related genes expression including HTR1B, HTR2A, HTR4, HTR5A, DRD1, DRD2 significantly decreased in the hippocampus. In addition, neuropeptide Y receptor Y5 (NPY5R) and somatostatin receptor 2 (SSTR2) were significantly down-regulated in the hypothalamus of HC cows. In mice, lowering hypothalamus NPY5R gene expression inhibits food intake [35]. Somatostatin (SST) in the brain involves in controlling feeding behavior via binding to its different receptors [36], and activation or inhibition of SSTR2 in mice can lead to a change in food intake [37, 38].

Hypothalamic GABAergic neurons play a crucial role in feeding control and behavior [39], and inhibition of GABAergic neurons has been found to reduce food consumption [40]. Our study confirmed that the GABAergic synapse pathway was the most significantly enriched in the hypothalamus, and most of the DEGs in this pathway were down-regulated in HC cows compared to control. The majority of GABA in the mammals brain is synthesized from glutamate (Glu) catalyzed by glutamic acid decarboxylase (GAD) with two isoforms of GAD, GAD65 and GAD67 encoded by GAD2 and GAD1 genes respectively [41]. It has been reported that knockout of GAD2 can lead to a marked decrease of GABA content in the brain [42, 43], suggesting the critical importance of GAD2 in maintaining GABA biosynthesis. In this study, a significant decrease of GAD2 gene and GAD65 protein expressions was observed in the hypothalamus of cows in the HC group compared to the LC, which was consistent with the decrease of the hypothalamic GABA level in the HC group cows. Moreover, GABA receptors, GABA-A and GABA-B, have been reported to play important roles in regulating feeding behavior [44], and the gene expression of GABA-A receptor subunits GABRA1, GABRA3, GABRB2, GABRB3, GABRR2 and GABA-B receptor subunits GABBR1 were significantly down-regulated in the hypothalamus of HC cows in this study. Thus, it is reasonable to speculate that, in HC cows, the decrease in hypothalamic GABA synthesis due to lower GAD expression, as well as the inhibition of its specific receptor, may contribute to the observed effects. Although these transcriptomic data provide important insights into the central regulatory mechanisms associated with SARA, the RNA-seq analysis was based on only three biological replicates per group. Given the high individual variability in dairy cows, such a sample size may affect the detection of certain differentially expressed genes. Therefore, future studies with larger sample sizes are necessary to validate the current findings and enhance the robustness of the analysis.

It is well documented that thyroid hormones play a variety of regulatory functions in the GABA system. Clinical data also suggest that many neurological disorders involving the GABAergic system are associated with thyroid dysfunctions [45]. We found that serum T3, T4, and FT3 concentrations were significantly increased in HC cows fed HC diets compared to those in the LC group [46]. However, a significant decrease of FT3 content and receptor THRB expression in the hypothalamic tissues was observed in the HC group compared to the LC group. The above results suggest a weakened negative feedback regulation of thyroid hormone on the central system of the HPT axis in HC cows. THRB knockout mice exhibited high concentrations of T3, suggesting a classic function of THRB on the negative feedback of the HPT axis. Additionally, thyroid hormones influence the synthesis of GABA via regulating the GAD enzyme. It has been reported that hypothyroid rats showed a significant decrease in GAD activity in the brain [47, 48], while exogenous T3 administration can promote GAD activity [49], indicating a positive effect of thyroid hormones on neurotransmitter GABA synthesis. Consistently, our results showed that T3 treatment significantly up-regulated GAD65 mRNA and protein expression through THRB receptor in hypothalamic N38 cells, while THRB silencing led to a decrease of GAD65 expression.

In this study, we used mouse hypothalamic N38 cells as an in vitro model to investigate the molecular mechanism by which T3 regulates GAD65 expression through the THRB pathway. Mouse hypothalamic N38 cells are well-characterized, readily available, and suitable for mechanistic studies, whereas bovine hypothalamic cells are difficult to maintain in long-term culture. However, it is important to note that ruminants such as dairy cows possess unique feed intake regulatory mechanisms, including sensing of ruminal volatile fatty acids (e.g., propionate). In addition, the in vitro experiment lacked a negative control with “silencing treatment + T3 stimulation” making it impossible to rule out the possibility that the effect of T3 was, at least in part, influenced by the transfection process itself. Therefore, this in vitro study has certain limitations, and future research should incorporate primary bovine hypothalamic neurons or conduct in vivo functional experiments to validate these mechanisms in ruminant species.

Conclusion

This study provides the first evidence that high-concentrate (HC) diets alter gene expression in the central nervous system of mid-lactation dairy cows, particularly components of the GABA signaling pathway. The observed inhibition of hypothalamic GAD expression and subsequent reduction in GABA synthesis, mediated through decreased T3 levels and THRB expression, reveals a neuroendocrine mechanism regulating feed intake in mid-lactation cows fed HC diets. However, the applicability of these findings to the periparturient and early lactation periods, when the most significant feed intake depression occurs, requires further validation.
