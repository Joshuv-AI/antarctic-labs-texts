Superb microvascular imaging in the assessment of persistent synovitis after anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction
Authors: Emily K Wright, Stacy E Smith, Molly Zgoda, Eric M Berkson, Simon Goertz, Morgan H Jones, Elizabeth G Matzkin, Bethany Wilcox, Christian Lattermann, Cale Jacobs
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThe purposes of this study were to evaluate the prevalence of persistent postoperative synovitis three months after anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction (ACLR) using ultrasonographic superb microvascular imaging (SMI) and evaluate the intra-rater reliability of SMI synovitis and the level of agreement with other ultrasound synovitis assessment methods.DesignTwenty-three individuals who had undergone primary ACLR took part in this prospective, IRB-approved study (11 females, 12 males; age=28.7±9.7 y; BMI=25.6±3.3 kg/m2). Three months after surgery, longitudinal and transverse ultrasound scans of the suprapatellar recess were performed at midline, medial to midline, and lateral to the midline. In addition to Power Doppler and B mode measures of synovitis and effusion, SMI was graded on two separate occasions at least two weeks apart. SMI intra-rater reliability was assessed with weighted Kappa analyses, and Kappa analyses were also used to assess the agreement between SMI synovitis and Power Doppler synovitis and B mode effusion.ResultsThree months following ACLR, 2 (8.7%) participants had no SMI synovitis, 9 (39.1%) participants had mild synovitis, and 12 (52.2%) had moderate synovitis. SMI synovitis grades demonstrated excellent intra-rater reliability (Kappa=0.93 [95%CI: 0.80, 1.06]) but demonstrated little agreement with Power Doppler synovitis grades (Kappa=0.29) or B mode effusion grades (Kappa=0.14).ConclusionsPersistent postoperative synovitis was common with half of patients demonstrating moderate synovitis. SMI synovitis grading was reliable, but little agreement was noted between SMI and other ultrasound grades of synovitis or effusion suggesting that the different techniques are identifying distinct features of postoperative inflammation.

Introduction

It is estimated that nearly 44 million people worldwide are living with post-traumatic osteoarthritis (PTOA) of the knee [1,2]. As the rates of traumatic knee injuries such as anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) tears continue to rise [3,4], the prevalence of PTOA is also expected to increase. Currently, >1 in 3 people that undergo ACL reconstruction develop irreversible PTOA within 10 years of surgery, demonstrating progressive signs of both OA illness (structural joint changes) and OA disease (pain and reduced functional ability) [[5], [6], [7]].

Inflammation and synovitis are key cogs in a vicious cycle leading to pain and early cartilage degradation following joint injury. One of the primary acute contributors to this inflammatory cascade is intra-articular vascular injury, which commonly occurs at the time of trauma, such as an anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) tear [8]. This vascular disruption results in hemarthrosis and the introduction of blood-derived inflammatory mediators into the joint space, rapidly activating the innate immune response [8]. Macrophages and synoviocytes within the synovium respond by producing pro-inflammatory cytokines [[9], [10], [11]]. This results in upregulation of matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), which leads to changes in the articular cartilage extracellular matrix and glycosaminoglycan loss [12,13]. Cartilage breakdown components further stimulate the innate immune response leading to a cycle of inflammation and subsequent synovitis, enzyme activity, and cartilage degradation [8,11]. Synovial fluid cytokine concentrations remain elevated one month after ACL reconstruction [14], and have been demonstrated to remain elevated as long as five years after surgery [15]. To better understand the role of persistent inflammation and synovitis on PTOA progression after ACL reconstruction, it is necessary to identify techniques to detect synovitis in the early postoperative period using modalities that can be implemented in the clinical setting.

The presence of effusion-synovitis, visualized using magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), has been associated with worse pain and progressive cartilage degradation in patients with knee OA [[16], [17], [18]]. Ultrasound also offers the ability assess multiple features of synovitis such as synovial thickening, synovial hypertrophy, and Power Doppler signal. Both quantitative and semi-quantitative ultrasound measures of synovitis have been found to be reliable, demonstrated construct validity when compared to MRI assessment, and have been shown to be responsive after treatment [[19], [20], [21], [22]].

Superb Microvascular Imaging (SMI) ultrasound protocols may also provide the ability to detect persistent synovitis after ACLR. SMI is similar to conventional Power Doppler protocols, but targets smaller, slower velocity vessels. Power Doppler may be less sensitive to slower blood flow as slow moving signals may appear as flash artifacts or thermal noise or clutter [23,24]. Through the use of clutter suppression, SMI reduces motion artifact while still detecting flow from both smaller and larger vessels, and a color map of blood flow is then superimposed on the B mode image [25]. As such, SMI may be more sensitive to OA-related synovitis as opposed to conventional Power Doppler which targets larger vessels [23,26,27].

While SMI has been used to assess synovitis in patients with established OA and rheumatoid arthritis, it is unknown how SMI assessments compare to other ultrasound and MRI measures after ACL injury or surgery. The purposes of this study were to 1) evaluate the prevalence of persistent postoperative synovitis three months after primary ACL reconstruction using ultrasonographic SMI, and 2) evaluate the intra-rater reliability of SMI synovitis and the level of agreement with other ultrasound synovitis assessment methods. Based on the reported prevalence of effusion-synovitis after ACL reconstruction [28], we hypothesized that at least 30 % of patients will demonstrate moderate to severe synovitis 3 months after surgery and that the measures would demonstrate excellent intra-rater reliability.

Methods

Study design: prospective observational clinical study

Participants

As part of this prospective, IRB-approved study, we recruited individuals during regularly scheduled physician follow-up visits one to two months after surgery. Once the patient consented, an ultrasound was scheduled to coincide with their clinical 3-month postoperative appointment. To be eligible for the study, participants had to meet the following criteria: age 18–39, BMI <40 kg/m2, no prior surgical intervention to treat ligament, cartilage, or meniscus lesions on the ipsilateral knee, and no evidence of radiographic osteoarthritis (Kellgren-Lawrence grade <2) or full-thickness chondral defects identified by MRI or arthroscopically. The osteoarthritis and chondral defects were assessed by treating surgeon. Participating surgeons introduced the study to patients and then the informed consent process was completed with the clinical research coordinator prior to any study activities.

Ultrasound acquisition and synovitis assessment

Dynamic ultrasound scan procedures followed the standardized Outcome Measures in Rheumatology (OMERACT) guidelines[29] and were performed by clinical radiology technologists experienced with ultrasound knee assessment. Participants were positioned supine with knees in 30° of flexion (Fig. 1A). Longitudinal and transverse scans of the suprapatellar recess were performed at midline, medial to midline, and lateral to the midline (six total images). A GE LOGIQ E10 unit with a linear 3–12 MHz probe (L3–12-D) was used with probe frequency = 10 MHz. SMI parameters included: pulse repetition frequency = 11.6k, color map = 5, color frequency = SMI6, color gain = 40 % [23]. Using these settings, SMI has been shown to correlate well with knee OA symptoms and MRI evidence of synovitis [23].Fig. 1A) Patient positioning for ultrasonographic Superb Microvascular Imaging assessment, and B) Superb Microvascular Imaging signal (purple) showing active knee synovitis 3 months after ACL reconstruction.Fig 1

The presence and severity of synovitis was assessed using SMI (Fig. 1B) and Power Doppler for each of the six images (longitudinal or transverse probe orientation; midline, medial, and lateral suprapatellar recess). The presence and severity of synovitis was graded on SMI and Power Doppler images as by a single reviewer (CAJ) in line with the European League Against Rheumatism and Outcome Measures in Rheumatology-OMERACT (EULAR-OMERACT) guidelines [30]. The EULAR-OMERACT guidelines were developed for Power Doppler and we adapted the same grading scale for SMI images to allow for direct comparison between the two techniques. For both Power Doppler and SMI images, No SMI/Power Doppler activity was considered Grade 0 (No synovitis), up to 3 single SMI/Power Doppler spots as Grade 1 (Mild), >3 spots but SMI/Power Doppler signal filling <50 % of the greyscale background as Grade 2 (Moderate), and SMI/Power Doppler signal greater than 50 % of the grayscale background as Grade 3 (Severe). To assess SMI intra-rater reliability, images were graded on two separate occasions at least two weeks apart. Additionally, effusion was graded on a four-point scale using traditional B mode images of the suprapatellar recess using longitudinal probe orientation in line with the quadriceps tendon. Images were graded as: No effusion (Grade 0; no distension of the joint capsule), Mild (Grade 1; small hypoechoic or anechoic region beneath capsule or minimal capsular distension), Moderate (Grade 2; capsular elevation or distension parallel to the joint), or Severe (Grade 3; bulging or convex capsular distension) [[28], [29], [30]].

Statistical analyses

Participant demographic information and the presence and severity of synovitis were reported for the two probe orientations (longitudinal and transverse) and the three locations (midline, medial, and lateral suprapatellar recess). The intra-rater reliability of synovitis grading was assessed using weighted Kappa. Since all ultrasound grading scales in the study utilized 0 to 3 grading scales, weighted Kappas were also used to determine the level of agreement between SMI synovitis and ultrasound Power Doppler synovitis grades and B mode effusion grades. Kappa values < 0.4 are considered to have poor agreement, between 0.4 and 0.75 as fair agreement, and > 0.75 are considered to show excellent agreement [31]. All analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics 29 (IBM, Armonk, NY).

Results

Twenty-three patients provided informed consent and took part in the study (11 females, 12 males; age = 28.7 ± 9.7 y; BMI = 25.6 ± 3.3 kg/m2, Table 1). Ultrasound scans were performed 91.0 ± 19.8 days after ACL reconstruction. When using the worst grade identified in any of the images collected for each participant 3 months following ACL reconstruction, 2 (8.7 %) participants had no synovitis, 9 (39.1 %) participants had mild synovitis, 12 (52.2 %) had moderate synovitis, and no participants had severe synovitis (Table 1). Synovitis was more frequently observed with transverse probe orientation, and with moderate synovitis grades more frequently noted on the lateral suprapatellar images (Table 2). When using the highest synovitis grade across the different regions of the joint, SMI synovitis grades demonstrated excellent intra-rater reliability (Kappa = 0.93 [95 %CI: 0.80, 1.06]). Discussion

SMI demonstrated excellent intra-rater reliability and persistent postoperative synovitis was common with half of patients demonstrating moderate synovitis at 3 months. Synovitis was more frequently observed with transverse probe orientation in the lateral suprapatellar images. While reliable, SMI grades of synovitis demonstrated little to no agreement with other imaging-based methods to grade synovitis and/or effusion. This is not to say that one assessment technique is superior to another, but rather that the different assessment methods appear to be providing different information about the inflammatory status of the joint soon after ACL injury and reconstruction.

ACL injury initiates a complex inflammatory response that persists months and years after surgery [9,14,15,32]. Interestingly roughly 30 % of patients demonstrate a dysregulated inflammatory response after ACL injury [33], and approximately 30 % of patients develop symptomatic PTOA within 10 years after ACL reconstruction [5]. While the etiology of PTOA is undoubtedly multifactorial, elevated synovial fluid concentrations of proinflammatory mediators at the time of surgery are associated with changes in cartilage composition on MRI three years after ACL reconstruction [34]. These prior results underscore the need to better understand the natural history of inflammation after ACL reconstruction to potentially identify novel treatment targets or the optimal timing of interventions to alter PTOA progression.

Imaging modalities may offer non-invasive techniques to assess the inflammatory status of the joint and provide information about the potential role of persistent inflammation on the onset and progression of PTOA after ACL reconstruction. As evidenced by the current results, different imaging modalities may provide information about distinctive characteristics of persistent inflammation after ACL reconstruction. SMI targets blood flow in smaller, slower velocity vessels whereas Power Doppler targets larger vessels, and SMI may be less sensitive to noise than Power Doppler [23,24,26,27]. Conversely, B mode effusion grading does not involve assessing blood flow in the synovium but rather distention of the joint capsule [35]. In the current study, SMI and Power Doppler synovitis grades and B mode effusion grades demonstrated little agreement. Higher synovitis grades tended to be noted with Power Doppler than SMI, and B mode effusion grades tended to be lower than SMI synovitis grades. Regardless of technique that was used, synovitis and effusion were common after ACL reconstruction and future longitudinal studies are needed to better understand the determine whether features of synovitis and inflammation visualized with ultrasound are predictive of PTOA onset or progression.

SMI assessment of suprapatellar synovitis adds to emerging ultrasound assessment techniques used after ACL injury and reconstruction. Initially after injury, SMI has been used to assess vascularity of the torn ACL which may help inform the shared decision-making process for surgeons and patients about whether ACL repair may be a viable treatment option [36]. Additionally, side-to-side comparisons of SMI measures of vascularity may provide information about the maturing ACL repair or graft postoperatively [36]. Prior to surgery, ultrasound measures of hamstring tendon cross sectional area also allows for accurate determinations of hamstring autograft viability [37]. After ACL reconstruction and pertinent to the progression of PTOA, echo intensity has been identified as a valid measure of femoral cartilage composition [38]. Ultrasound measures of infrapatellar fat pad Hoffa synovitis have been found to be comparable to MRI measures after ACL reconstruction [39], and sonographic measures of effusion-synovitis have also been associated with postoperative gait asymmetries [28]. In all use cases, ultrasound measures were valid and reliable and may offer low-cost alternatives to MRI. To date, studies have largely focused on individual sonographic measures, but with the scope and breadth of information that can be gained, comprehensive ultrasound assessments may provide an efficient method for clinicians and PTOA researchers to longitudinally track changes after ACL reconstruction to learn more about the etiology of PTOA and identify potential treatment targets.

This study was not without limitation. First, the primary purpose of this study was to evaluate the technical aspects of using SMI to assess persistent synovitis after ACL reconstruction. The study involved a relatively small sample size with scans performed at a single time point after surgery. Future longitudinal studies are needed to better understand potential patient or clinical factors that are associated with persistent postoperative synovitis, and well as to determine the potential causative role of synovitis on pain and early features of posttraumatic osteoarthritis. Second, the study did not include patients that were treated with quadriceps autografts. It remains unclear if SMI signal in the suprapatellar recess results may be influenced by the graft harvest. Third, we utilized an ultrasound unit from a single manufacturer to perform the scans. SMI has been performed with other ultrasound units from other manufacturers, but it remains unclear if SMI settings and parameters may need to be modified on different units in order to allow for direct comparison of results between devices.

In conclusion, persistent postoperative synovitis was common with half of patients demonstrating moderate synovitis. SMI synovitis grading was reliable, but little agreement was noted between SMI and other ultrasound grades of synovitis or effusion suggesting that the different techniques are identifying distinct features of postoperative inflammation. Future research leveraging ultrasound features of synovitis and other PTOA-related joint characteristics is needed to further elucidate the progression of PTOA after ACL reconstruction.
