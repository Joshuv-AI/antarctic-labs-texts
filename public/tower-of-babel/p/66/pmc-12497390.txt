Non-muscle myosin II is a promising therapeutic target
Authors: Courtney A Miller, Alfredo Quinones-Hinojosa, Steven S Rosenfeld
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractNon-muscle myosin II (NMII) comprises a family of cytoplasmic motors with important roles in both normal biology and disease. In this forum article we describe recent developments that validate NMII as a therapeutic target, and we illustrate how this validation can identify novel and translationally viable approaches to treat a variety of diseases.

Cytoplasmic motors are central to both normal and pathological cell function

Cells contain organelles, vesicles, and receptors, and each of these needs to be transported to and retained within specified locations for the cell to function. Cells are also mechanically dynamic, generating the forces needed to perform actions such as driving separation of chromosomes during mitosis, attachment to the extracellular matrix during differentiation, and cell motility [1]. These functions rely upon a set of enzymes that use ATP hydrolysis to create force and movement. They are collectively referred to as ‘cytoplasmic motors’ and include the dyneins, kinesins, and myosin. While these nanomotors are essential for normal cell physiology, they represent a large untapped superfamily of targets for therapeutic development because their pathogenic activity provides a potential ‘Achilles heel’ for treating a wide array of diseases.

Myosin IIs are druggable members of the myosin superfamily

The myosin superfamily comprises 45 members, with the myosin II family being the most widely expressed. This family consists of muscle (skeletal, smooth, and cardiac) and non-muscle subtypes [2]. Collectively, the non-muscle myosin IIs (NMIIs) (see Glossary) (NMIIA, IIB, and IIC subtypes) are implicated in a number of diseases through their contribution to cytoplasmic transport, endocytosis, cytokinesis, cell polarity, contractility, and neuroplasticity (Figure 1A). Drug discovery and development efforts have recently begun to focus on the myosin II family. The first and only myosin II inhibitor to achieve FDA approval to date is mavacamten (Camzyos™), which targets cardiac myosin II to treat obstructive hypertrophic cardiomyopathy. Mavacamten is an allosteric inhibitor that works by reducing myocardial contractility sufficiently to enable adequate ventricular filling during diastole [3]. The success of this effort demonstrates that in the right context, targeting an ‘essential’ cellular component, such as cardiac myosin II, is therapeutically viable. Several recent studies have extended this to NMII by demonstrating that its isoforms are key elements in driving disease, and that inhibiting NMII can reverse these pathological states (Figure 1A).

(A) Upper panel: NMII consists of two heavy chains. These are composed of three domains: (i) two motor domains at the amino terminus (right side of image) that contain the ATPase site and which bind to blebbistatin and its derivatives, (ii) the lever arm, containing the essential and regulatory light chains (middle, blue and magenta), and (iii) the C-terminal coiled coil (left side of image), which allows NMII to polymerize into minifilaments. Lower panel: illustration of the diverse diseases (upper portion of each box) that depend on an array of NMII functions (lower portion of each box). (B) Inhibiting NMIIA and NMIIB with MT-125 prevents mitochondrial fission. This is a quality control process that removes damaged segments of the mitochondrial membrane that would otherwise leak reactive oxygen species (ROS) into the cytoplasm, where it can damage DNA in the nucleus and peroxidize the cell membrane. This has two potentially therapeutic effects. First, it enables MT-125 to further extend survival by synergizing with radiotherapy, a standard treatment for cancers, which works in part by also generating ROS. Second, the MT-125-induced increase in ROS leads to peroxidation of protein tyrosine phosphatases, which inhibit the function of receptor tyrosine kinase (RTK). This leads to constitutive activation of RTKs, stimulation of RTK downstream effectors, including PI3 kinase (PI3K), AKT, and mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR), and ‘oncogene addiction’ to these oncogenic kinases. Combining MT-125 with inhibitors of the RTK–PI3K–AKT–mTOR pathway thereby leads to synthetic lethality and prolongs survival in models of glioblastoma.

Targeting how NMII is regulated provides one approach to interfering with its function in disease

The enzymatic activity of NMII is regulated by rho kinase (ROCK) phosphorylation of the NMII regulatory light chain, which provides one approach to targeting NMII function in disease states. Elevated NMII activity correlates with poor prognosis in melanoma and the development of treatment resistance in this disease. Building on this observation, Orgaz et al. [4] found that pharmacologically inhibiting ROCK increased reactive oxygen species (ROS), leading to DNA damage, reversal of immunosuppression, and improvement in survival in preclinical melanoma models. Force generation also requires that NMII assemble into minifilaments, composed of 6–25 NMII molecules, in order for it to crosslink and slide actin filaments. This process is highly regulated so that the cell can spatially and temporally control its contractility. Robinson and colleagues have recently found that they can induce cell-wide activation of NMIIB and NMIIC with the small molecule 4-hydroxyacetophenone (4-HAP). This drug appears to induce NMIIB and NMIIC to assume an elongated conformation, leading to their polymerization into force-generating minifilaments. The authors have shown that 4-HAP thereby increases tumor cell stiffness and reduces metastases in preclinical models of pancreatic and colon cancer [5,6].

Allosteric inhibitors provide another approach to interfering with the contribution of NMII to disease

Blebbistatin was the first allosteric myosin II inhibitor to be discovered [7]. It is a pan-myosin II inhibitor that binds to the major actin-binding cleft of all the myosin II family members, locking this motor in a conformation that cannot generate force. While blebbistatin has been very useful in studying NMII functions in a variety of non-muscle cell types, its ability to also inhibit cardiac, skeletal, and smooth muscle myosin II complicates mechanistic work, and the cardiac myosin II inhibition produces toxicity that interferes with its utility for in vivo studies.

To address the need for more selective NMII inhibitors, our team took a medicinal chemistry approach and generated nearly 500 derivatives of blebbistatin with improved properties [8]. Through structure–activity relationship (SAR) work, NMII potency and blood–brain barrier (BBB) permeability were maintained, while cardiac myosin II inhibition was successfully removed. This latter change was central to unlocking the clinical potential of NMII family inhibitors.

Three reports demonstrate the efficacy of these derivatives in preclinical models of methamphetamine use disorder (MUD), glioblastoma (GBM), and axon regeneration. In the first paper [8], the authors tested the therapeutic potential of two promising derivatives in MUD that specifically inhibit NMIIB: MT-110 and MT-228. MUD is a disorder marked by the sustained motivation to consume methamphetamine in spite of negative consequences, and there are currently no FDA-approved therapies [9]. Prior work had demonstrated that maintaining motivation for methamphetamine requires NMIIB-driven actin dynamics in the dendritic spines of a subregion of the brain known to regulate positive and negative responses to stimuli, the basolateral amygdala (BLA). (For detailed information on the underlying mechanisms see [10–12].) It was further shown that acute NMIIB inhibition in the BLA produces sustained remissions in animal models of MUD. Following weeks of daily methamphetamine self-administration, a single low dose of MT-110 or MT-228 disrupted methamphetamine seeking, and the effect of this single administration produced a long-lasting effect through a selective resetting of BLA structural plasticity, suggesting that only one dose may be needed to support lasting remission in MUD. This is significant, as it is difficult for some individuals with MUD to adhere to daily use of a medication. An atomic resolution model of MT-228 bound to myosin II paralogs demonstrated that the structural modifications produce a ~1 Å shift in its orientation in the myosin II binding site relative to blebbistatin, explaining a 10–70-fold increased selectivity of the drug for NMIIB over cardiac myosin II. This also likely explains why repeated administration of either drug to rats produced no change in cardiac output, and there were no significant alterations in weight, clinical chemistries, hematology, or target organ histopathology at doses 7.5–60-fold higher than the minimum effective dose in preclinical models of MUD.

The authors of the second paper [13] had previously shown that genetic deletion of both NMIIA and IIB prevented growth and dispersion of GBM, the most common and lethal of primary brain tumors [14]. This motivated them to examine MT-125, a dual inhibitor of NMIIA and IIB developed in the same medicinal chemistry campaign that produced MT-110 and MT-228. Like the other two drugs, MT-125 is BBB-permeable and well tolerated, even with repeated administration at high doses. As expected for a combined NMIIA/IIB inhibitor, MT-125 blocked GBM invasion both in vitro and in vivo. While these effects should be tumoristatic, the drug was in fact found to be tumoricidal. Further investigation revealed that the underlying mechanism for this effect is related to the role that NMII paralogs play in regulating ROS. NMII isoforms generate contractile force by sliding against actin filaments, and this force is essential to drive a wide variety of essential cell functions. One of these is mitochondrial fission, a process in which NMII-generated force contributes to the excision of damaged mitochondrial membrane segments that would otherwise leak ROS into the cytoplasm. Consistent with this mechanism, Kenchappa and colleagues found that MT-125 lengthened mitochondria and increased ROS content in both the cytoplasm and the nucleus (Figure 1B). Since radiation therapy, a standard treatment for GBM, also kills tumor cells in part through generation of ROS, this predicted that MT-125 should synergize with radiation. Using both immunocompetent genetically engineered and patient-derived xenograft mouse models, the authors demonstrated that MT-125 does indeed synergize with radiation. ROS also has a signaling role in cell biology, as it can stimulate receptor tyrosine kinase (RTK) activity by inactivating protein tyrosine phosphatases. Consistent with this mechanism, the study found that MT-125 also enhances RTK activity and downstream signaling through PI3 kinase (Figure 1B). While RTKs in general stimulate tumor growth, this was not seen in tumors treated with MT-125, since this drug also directly blocks proliferation. Nevertheless, MT-125 increased GBM dependency on RTK signaling through a process referred to as oncogene addiction [15]. This likely explains why combining MT-125 with an FDA-approved RTK inhibitor (sunitinib) was synthetically lethal in vitro, and why the combination of these two drugs was synergistic in vivo, markedly prolonging survival in up to 40% of GBM-bearing mice. A Phase 1 clinical trial is being initiated to test MT-125’s safety and efficacy in GBM.

This study begs the question of why MT-125 is so well tolerated, even though NMIIA and/or IIB are widely expressed across vertebrate tissues and drive multiple cellular functions, and even though their embryonic deletion in mice has been shown to be lethal in utero or shortly after birth [16]. The authors propose that this could be a consequence of two factors: an increased dependency of GBM cells on NMII, and the relatively short half-life of systemically administered MT-125. If tumor cells are considerably more dependent on NMII than normal cells, depriving them of NMII function for the relatively brief period when MT-125 is at an effective concentration likely spares normal cells from toxicity, while still being lethal for tumor cells that are ‘oncogene addicted’ to this class of cytoplasmic motors.

A third recent study [17] extended the known role of NMII in models of peripheral nerve injury [18]. Utilizing small-molecule libraries to identify drugs that stimulate axonal outgrowth in vitro, the authors identified several promising compounds, the most potent of which was blebbistatin. Then, utilizing another derivative from the medicinal chemistry campaign focused on NMII selectivity for improved safety referenced earlier [8], the authors demonstrated that this inhibitor was able to promote axonal regrowth both in vitro and in vivo in preclinical models of axonal crush injury.

Finally, it should be noted that in both normal brain and peripheral nerve, NMIIB is the predominant NMII isoform, although NMIIA is also in microglia, proximal growth cones, and axons. In GBM, NMIIA is the predominant isoform, accounting for nearly 65% of the total NMII, with NMIIB constituting another 30% [14]. This implies that the spectrum of activity for NMII small-molecule inhibitors may benefit from tuning to the cell type and function being targeted.

Concluding remarks

Progress in developing myosin II-based therapies for human disease has likely been hampered by a widely held but untested assumption that inhibiting a target as prevalent in vertebrate tissues as NMII would be toxic. However, the notable tolerability of the recently developed NMII-selective inhibitors highlighted here, as well as their efficacy in several different diseases, emphasizes that the balance between efficacy and toxicity can be successfully managed through the use of small-molecule inhibitors which enable precise tuning of pharmacokinetics. This emphasizes the point that drug toxicity is determined by several variables, not just the prevalence of the drug’s target in normal tissue. This includes how ‘addicted’ pathological cells are to the target compared with their normal counterparts, as well as the activity of the target and how long it is inhibited by a therapeutic intervention. Such variables make it difficult to predict a priori how toxic a promising therapy may be, and emphasize the importance of rigorous preclinical and clinical testing to obtain an empirically determined answer. Future translational directions for this work will be driven by identification of the full complement of diseases whose pathogenesis is dependent upon NMII and whose course can be modified by applying NMII-targeting therapies.
