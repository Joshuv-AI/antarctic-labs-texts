Clinical Outcomes of Concomitant Coronary Artery Bypass Grafting During Ventricular Septal Myectomy
Authors: Tao Lu, Changsheng Zhu, Hao Cui, Zining Wu, Zhengyang Lu, Yanhai Meng, Qiulan Yang, Liukun Meng, Yunhu Song, Shuiyun Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe clinical characteristics and survival outcomes of patients who underwent concomitant coronary artery bypass grafting during septal myectomy have not been well studied.Methods and ResultsWe reviewed patients who underwent both septal myectomy and coronary artery bypass grafting from 2009 to 2020. Causes of concomitant grafting and their impact on survival were analyzed. The median follow‐up period was 5.1 years. A total of 320 patients underwent both grafting and myectomy. Of these, 69.7% and 28.1% underwent grafting attributed to atherosclerotic coronary artery disease and myocardial bridging, respectively. Patients who underwent grafting for coronary artery disease tended to be older, had a longer bypass time, and required more grafts compared with patients undergoing procedures because of myocardial bridging (all P<0.05). Postoperatively, the left ventricular outflow gradient significantly decreased from 85.4 mm Hg to 12.8 mm Hg (P<0.001) without perioperative death. The cumulative survival rates were 96.2% and 97.6% at 5 years in the coronary artery disease and myocardial bridging groups, respectively, and they were comparable to that of general myectomy cohort (hazard ratio [HR], 1.06 [95% CI, 0.47–2.36], P=0.895 and HR 0.75 [95% CI, 0.23–2.46], P=0.636, respectively). Sudden death accounted for 45.5% (5 of 11) of postoperative mortality. Analysis of composite end point events showed decreased morbidity with at least one arterial graft in the overall cohort (HR, 0.47 [95% CI, 0.23–0.94], P=0.034).ConclusionsConcomitant grafting in septal myectomy was found to be a safe procedure. Patients who underwent such surgery experienced favorable postoperative outcomes comparable to those who underwent septal myectomy alone, with a 5‐year survival rate of >95% and improved functional class of >90%.Keywords: coronary artery bypass grafting, coronary artery disease, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, myocardial bridging, septal myectomySubject Categories: Cardiovascular Surgery, Revascularization, Mortality/Survival, Quality and Outcomes

Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM) is a hereditary heart disease characterized by asymmetric hypertrophy of the ventricular septum, and left ventricular outflow tract obstruction is a major feature. 1 Surgical septal myectomy (SM) is an effective approach for treating patients with drug‐refractory obstructive HCM. 2 Postoperatively, there is a significant decrease in the left ventricular outflow tract gradient, with an exceptionally favorable long‐term prognosis.

However, in clinical practice, patients with HCM often have concurrent ischemic heart disease, affecting coronary blood supply and likely leading to a worse prognosis. 3 , 4 Therefore, concomitant coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) may be required with SM when treating patients in severe condition. However, the characteristics and prognosis of such patients undergoing concomitant CABG are not clear.

In this study, we analyzed the surgical indications, clinical features, and operative procedures of patients who underwent concomitant CABG during ventricular SM and investigated their prognostic outcomes.

METHODS

The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

We retrospectively reviewed the data of patients who underwent concomitant CABG during SM between 2009 and 2020 at Fuwai Hospital in Beijing, China. Demographic information, echocardiographic findings, coronary angiography findings, and surgical data were collected.

Inclusion criteria included: (1) diagnosis of HCM, defined as ventricular myocardial hypertrophy in the absence of any other cardiac or systemic disease responsible for the hypertrophy 5 ; (2) diagnosis of ischemic heart disease, including atherosclerotic coronary artery disease (CAD), defined as luminal fixed stenosis due to atherosclerosis, 3 and myocardial bridging (MB), a congenital coronary anomaly describing short segments of coronary artery embedded within the myocardium 6 ; and (3) undergoing both CABG for ischemic heart disease and SM for obstructive HCM. Indications for SM primarily included: (1) left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT) pressure ≥50 mm Hg at rest or provocation, and (2) severe symptoms refractory to medication. All patients had undergone coronary angiography before surgery, and concomitant CABG was collaboratively decided upon by an interventional cardiologist and cardiac surgeon. In general, concomitant CABG was adopted when coronary angiography visually estimated a fixed stenosis severity of at least 50% in any vessel in patients with CAD, 7 or in cases of extensive or deep MB, primarily in the left anterior descending coronary artery (LAD), as in our early report, 8 when MB systolic stenosis was ≥75%. Surgical procedures were performed under general anesthesia and cardiopulmonary bypass. Myocardial protection was achieved using hypothermia and antegrade cold blood cardioplegia. The hypertrophic myocardium was excised via transaortic extended SM as described in our previous study, 9 followed by CABG. The left internal mammary artery (LIMA) and great saphenous vein (SVG) were commonly used as bypass materials. Before chest closure, the mean flow rate and pulsatile index were obtained using transit‐time flow measurement via a Medistim Butterfly flowmeter (Medistim AS). 10

Follow‐up assessments were conducted during outpatient visits or telephone follow‐ups. All‐cause mortality was the primary end point, and composite end point events, including cardiac‐related death and readmissions (such as for heart failure, atrial fibrillation, and stroke), were considered secondary end points. This study was approved by the Fuwai ethics committee (approval number: 2023‐2261), which waived the requirement to obtain informed consent from the individual patients because of the retrospective nature of the study.

Descriptive statistics are presented as median (interquartile range), mean (SD), or number (percentage). Multiple imputation techniques were applied to handle missing values. Between‐group continuous variables were analyzed using ANOVA and Tukey honest significant difference test or Kruskal–Wallis/Dunn tests based on the data type for overall and subgroup comparisons. Categorical variables were evaluated using Pearson χ2 test/Fisher exact test. Preoperative/postoperative comparisons were made using paired t and McNemar tests. Kaplan–Meier curve and log‐rank tests were used for survival analysis, and Cox proportional hazard regression models were used to explore the factors influencing the primary end point. Initial analyses of secondary end point risk factors included 21 variables: sex, age, New York Heart Association classification, atrial fibrillation, graft type, graft number, reason for bypass, anastomosis number, N‐terminal pro‐B‐type natriuretic peptide level, body mass index, body surface area, syncope/length of postoperative stay, diabetes, hypertension, ejection fraction, left atrial diameter, septal thickness, LVOT gradient, cardiopulmonary bypass time, and postoperative LVOT gradient. To minimize multicollinearity, we used least absolute shrinkage and selection operator regression with 10‐fold cross‐validation to calculate the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator penalty (lambda.min: λ). 11 Univariable and multivariable stepwise Cox regression analyses were used to further screen for valuable variables. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 25 (IBM) and R version 4.1.3 (R Foundation).

RESULTS

Among 2403 patients who underwent SM between June 2009 and December 2020, 557 had concurrent ischemic heart disease, including 310 with CAD and 283 with MB. This study included 320 patients with ischemic heart disease who underwent concomitant CABG during SM (Table 1). The median age was 53 years (45.3–60.7 years), with 222 (69.4%) men and 228 (71.3%) patients classified as having New York Heart Association class III or IV. Ten patients had a history of alcohol septal ablation. Among patients who underwent simultaneous grafting, 223 (69.7%) had procedures attributed to CAD, 90 (28.1%) to MB, and 7 (2.2%) to both CAD and MB (Figure 1, Video S1). Canadian angina class III or IV was present in 40.6% of the overall population, 41.3% of the CAD group, and 40% of the MB group. In addition, 95% of patients with angina had stable angina. Most of the operations were completed after 2013 (Figure S1A). In the CAD group, 22 of 190 (11.6%) patients with LAD grafting had LAD stenosis rates from 50% to 70%, and the remainder of the patients had stenosis rates ≥70%. In the MB group, all 85 patients with LAD grafting had systolic stenosis rates ≥75%. Overall, the LAD, diagonal artery, left circumflex artery, and right coronary artery average diameters were 1.79 mm, 1.69 mm, 1.67 mm, and 1.7 mm, respectively, and the average stenosis rates of the same were 79%, 80%, 81%, and 80%, respectively (Figure S1B). The diameter of the target vessel, the LAD, was significantly larger than that of the left circumflex artery (P=0.038). Intraoperative monitoring measured the flow rate and pulsatile index for various grafts: the LAD (LIMA), LAD (SVG), diagonal artery, left circumflex artery, and right coronary artery measured 33 mL/min (2.6), 52 mL/min (1.2), 62 mL/min (1.5), 59 mL/min (1.4), and 53 mL/min (1.2), respectively. The mean flow rates were all >30 mL/min, and the flow rate of the LAD graft was lower than that of the LAD vein, diagonal artery, left circumflex artery, and right coronary artery grafts (all P<0.05). All pulsatile indices were <5.0 (Figure S1C).

SM had significant efficacy, as shown in Table 2. Preoperative and postoperative echocardiography was conducted 7 days (4–12 days) before myectomy and 6 days (4–12 days) after myectomy, respectively. All patients presented with LVOT obstruction. Postoperatively, the LVOT gradient decreased from 85.4±29.5 mm Hg to 12.8±8.9 mm Hg, and the septal thickness and left atrial diameter significantly decreased by ≈7.8 mm and 4.6 mm, respectively (all P<0.001). Moreover, 97% of the patients no longer exhibited systolic anterior motion signs, and 99% showed disappearance of moderate to severe mitral regurgitation. No mortality occurred intraoperatively or within 30 days of operation in this cohort.

Table 3 shows an overall expected operative mortality risk of 1.49% calculated by EuroSCORE II (European System for Cardiac Operative Risk Evaluation II) and a total bypass time of 112 minutes (88–139 minutes). Patients who underwent grafting for CAD tended to be older and had a higher expected mortality risk and longer bypass time, ventilation time, and intensive care unit stay duration than those of patients undergoing procedures because of MB (all P<0.05). However, the length of postoperative stay did not differ significantly between the groups (P=0.489). Most patients (n=131, 58.7%) in the CAD group had a single vessel lesion, with a mean Syntax score of 8.5±7.2. In total, 437 grafts were used, consisting of 95 LIMA and 342 SVG grafts, along with 41 sequential bypasses, resulting in 484 distal anastomoses. Of these anastomoses, the LAD was the target vessel in 59.1% of the overall cohort, 50.3% of the CAD group, and 93.5% of the MB group. The CAD group had a significantly higher mean number of anastomoses and grafts compared with the MB group (P<0.05). In addition, the usage rates of LIMA as a graft for the LAD in the CAD and MB groups were 36.8% and 28.2%, respectively. In this cohort, 12.2% of patients received concomitant mitral valve repair, and 2.5% of patients received concomitant mitral valve replacement. Wound infection and pacemaker implantation were common complications, with ≈2.2% each in the overall cohort.

During a median follow‐up period of 5.1 years (3–7.7 years), the cumulative survival rates of this cohort were 96.7% (95% CI, 94.3–99.2%) at 5 years and 93.6% (95% CI, 89.7%–97.6%) at 10 years. To be specific, the 5‐year cumulative survival rates in the CAD and MB groups were 96.2% (95% CI, 92.8–99.7%) and 97.6% (95% CI, 94.3–100%), respectively. During this period, 11 patients reached the primary end point. Among the 8 cases of cardiac‐related deaths (excluding 1 of implantable cardioverter‐defibrillator [ICD] discharge), 5 were attributable to sudden cardiac death (SCD), which was the leading cause, with a median occurrence at 4.7 years after the operation (Figure 2A). None of the deceased patients had a family history of SCD. Notably, 5 of 6 patients experiencing SCD or ICD discharge had a class 2a/2b ICD recommendation as per the 2020 American Heart Association/American College of Cardiology (AHA/ACC) guidelines 12 (Table S1).

The event‐free survival rate was 79.5% (95% CI, 74.4%–84.9%) at 5 years, and 59 patients reached secondary end points in this cohort. Heart failure and atrial fibrillation were the main causes of these events, occurring at median times of 3.7 and 2.4 years after the operation, respectively (Figure 2B). Of the surviving patients, 287 (92.9%) were categorized as New York Heart Association class I or II at the latest evaluation.

Concomitant CABG in SM for CAD showed no significant difference with CABG for MB in terms of long‐term mortality (log‐rank P=0.54; Figure 3). Moreover, compared with 1273 patients without CAD and MB (Table S2) who only underwent myectomy during this period, neither concomitant CABG for CAD nor CABG for MB exhibited differences in long‐term mortality after adjusting for age and sex (hazard ratio [HR], 1.06 [95% CI, 0.47–2.36], P=0.895 for CAD and HR, 0.75 [95% CI, 0.23–2.46], P=0.636 for MB, respectively). In this concomitant CABG cohort, diabetes and length of postoperative stay were the only factors associated with all‐cause mortality (HR, 4.00 [95% CI, 1.06–15.12], P=0.041 and HR, 1.06 [95% CI, 1.02–1.10], P=0.003, respectively). After adjusting for age, sex, and length of postoperative stay, diabetes remained an important risk factor (HR, 4.65 [95% CI, 1.06–20.41], P=0.042) (Table S3).

Regarding event‐free survival, 12 variables were analyzed after least absolute shrinkage and selection operator regression (Figure S2). All variables met the Cox proportional hazards assumptions, and all continuous variables satisfied the linearity requirement. Grafting of at least one artery showed no significant difference from full venous grafting in the univariable analysis and subset analysis of the CAD and MB groups (P=0.264, P=0.329, and P=0.471, respectively; Table S4). However, grafting of at least one artery demonstrated significant benefits in reducing long‐term morbidity after adjusting for other covariates (HR, 0.47 [95% CI, 0.23–0.94], P=0.034; C index 0.7, Akaike Information Criterion 606.12) (Table 4). Sensitivity analysis showed that this result was consistent with that of the 1‐step multivariable stepwise Cox regression (HR, 0.46 [95% CI, 0.23–0.94], P=0.033).

DISCUSSION

Ischemic heart disease includes atherosclerotic CAD and MB, observed in 15% to 53% and 23% to 41% of patients with HCM, respectively. 3 , 6 , 13 , 14 Although patients with HCM have been known to possess diminished coronary flow reserve, additional epicardial CAD or MB can worsen myocardial ischemia, contributing to an unfavorable prognosis. 12 Concomitant CABG in valve surgery has been reported frequently, 15 , 16 while studies on concomitant CABG in SM are rare. In this cohort, those patients achieved the following favorable outcomes: (1) low to zero operative mortality; (2) long‐term outcomes comparable to those of patients requiring myectomy alone in both CAD and MB groups; (3) >90% achieved an improved functional class; and (4) ≈80% achieved event‐free survival at 5 years.

The confluence of obstructive LVOT and ischemic symptoms is a significant concern. A previous study reported that 59% of patients showed no improvement in symptoms after single revascularization. 17 SM alone does not alleviate epicardial coronary stenosis. Therefore, SM combined with CABG may be indispensable for patients with clear pathologies. Previous studies found that concomitant CABG for CAD in SM was an important factor associated with mortality. 18 , 19 , 20 However, our results seem to differ from this conclusion. We observed the effect that CAD diminished after CABG, leading to the survival rate of patients with CAD being remarkably comparable to those with MB and those who underwent myectomy alone (5‐year survival: 96.2%, 97.6%, and 96.7%, respectively). The reason for this disparity was unclear. It could be attributed to the larger sample size compared with our earlier report (44 patients with HCM), 20 as well as to more refined surgical skills and better scientific management of patients with CAD over recent years. Patients with CAD in our center were also younger (56 years versus 66.3 years) compared with those in Nguyen and colleagues' report. 19 Therefore, a longer follow‐up period might be required to observe the impact of CAD after bypass surgery on mortality in patients with HCM who have also undergone myectomy, especially in an experienced surgical center. Nevertheless, patients with CAD required more anastomoses (1.7 versus 1.03 per person), indicating an increased risk of morbidity (HR, 1.44; P=0.008). Moreover, we observed that the risk of mortality in patients with diabetes was much higher (HR, 4.65; P=0.042), which is consistent with the results of previous studies in general and those of HCM populations. 21 , 22 , 23 Therefore, the prevention, early identification, and timely treatment of diabetes in patients with complex HCM are now equally crucial.

The role of MB in patient prognosis remains debatable; however, it is generally considered a crucial mechanism for myocardial ischemia. 4 , 6 , 24 , 25 For drug‐refractory patients, CABG and surgical unroofing are common procedures. 26 Selection is determined by the degree and duration of compression and the length and depth of the bridge. In this study, nearly 30% of patients underwent CABG because of MB. Regarding the graft type, arterial grafts have proven beneficial over SVG in patients with CAD. However, Bockeria et al recommended SVG for patients with MB because they observed that LIMA patency was lower in a small sample, probably because of competitive blood flow. 27 We did not find significant differences in all‐cause mortality throughout follow‐up between the group with at least one artery graft (LIMA) and the group with a full venous graft. However, the group with at least one artery graft demonstrated a 53% reduced risk of morbidity after adjusting for other covariates (P=0.034). LIMA remains the preferred choice as a graft conduit at the contemporary stage. Meanwhile, since surgical unroofing has been proven safe and effective for MB concurrent with myectomy, 28 we now tend to lean towards unroofing therapy for select patients with MB.

SCD continued to be the primary cause of postoperative mortality in this cohort, consistent with our previous reports. 20 , 29 Assessing individual risk for SCD and patient selection for ICD placement has evolved with the emergence of new risk markers (ejection fraction <50%, left ventricular aneurysm, and extensive late gadolinium enhancement). 30 Even in this specific HCM cohort with multiple operations, the 2020 AHA/ACC guidelines achieved 83% sensitivity in recommending appropriate ICD implantation, 12 suggesting that 4 deaths could have been avoided, albeit regrettably. The reluctance or subdued willingness to undergo ICD implantation following myectomy procedures poses a significant dilemma in clinical practice in China. 31 Moreover, since surgery can alter SCD risk stratification, 32 how to accurately predict SCD in the postoperative population is still inconclusive. 33 Heart failure and atrial fibrillation remain the primary causes affecting patients' quality of life and readmission, with symptoms potentially worsening more than 2 years postoperatively. Regular follow‐up evaluations and timely treatment are essential to improve patient prognosis. In addition, we use social media platforms, such as official accounts and groups, to provide medication advice and dietary and exercise suggestions and establish a postoperative management system. We observed that >90% of patients maintained relatively normal cardiac function at the latest follow‐up.

Limitations

Our study has some limitations. First, this was a retrospective study conducted at a single referral center with a high operation volume for both SM and CABG, and most patients in this cohort had a single‐vessel lesion, which might limit the applicability of our results to other settings. Given the observational nature of this study, it might not be possible to completely rule out the possibility of selection bias (potential treatment allocation bias) of the cohort patients. Second, our evaluation predominantly focused on the bypass coronary arteries, whereas other vessels potentially affected by atherosclerosis (luminal stenosis <50%) and MB (systolic stenosis <75%) pathology may have existed without surgical intervention. Because only a small portion (≈10%) of patients with CAD undergoing CABG had vessel stenosis at 50% to 70%, and there was no medication treatment control group, the benefit of concomitant CABG for this population remains inconclusive. In addition, the use of the LIMA in the CAD group was relatively low in this specific cohort, which might affect the long‐term outcomes in this group. Finally, the lack of postoperative computed tomography data impeded our ability to determine the objective and quantified graft patency rates. However, the assessment of cardiac function based on clinical status remains a more direct and meaningful measure.

CONCLUSION

Concomitant CABG in SM exhibited favorable outcomes in patients with HCM and ischemic diseases refractory to medications at an experienced center. The operative mortality rate was low, and the 5‐year survival rate was 96.2% and 97.6% in the atherosclerotic CAD and MB groups, respectively. Moreover, >90% of the patients achieved an improved functional class, and ≈80% of the patients experienced event‐free survival at 5 years.
