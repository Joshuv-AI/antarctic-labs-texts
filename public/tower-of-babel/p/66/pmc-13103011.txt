A multi-scenario identification of key ecological restoration areas integrating ecosystem service value and landscape risk in the northern Qinling foothills
Authors: Yashi Ye, Kanhua Yu, Yongshuai Wang, Yezhen Liu, Liming Bo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Rapid urbanization and profound land use changes are driving a decline in ecosystem service value (ESV) and a rise in the Ecological Risk Index (ERI). These trends pose significant challenges to regional sustainable development. This study focuses on the Shaanxi section of the northern foothills of the Qinling Mountains. By integrating remote sensing, statistical yearbooks, and GIS spatial analysis, we applied a coupled “value-risk-simulation” framework using the PLUS model. The findings are as follows. (1) From 1990 to 2020, regional ESV demonstrated a “decline–recovery” trend. Regulatory services contributed the most, and forestland and grassland were the primary contributors to ESV. ERI decreased overall, yet high-risk areas remained in central Xi’an and along the Wei, Ba, and Hei Rivers. (2) Simulations showed that the Ecological Protection Scenario (EPS) and Sustainable Development Scenario (SDS) best balance ecological value with risk management. (3) A significant negative correlation exists between ESV and ERI. High–High clusters are predominantly distributed in water bodies and their surrounding forestlands and grasslands. Mean annual temperature, DEM, GDP, and population density were identified as key factors associated with the spatial patterns of both ESV and ERI. This indicates that while these areas possess high ecosystem service functions, they face risks associated with climatic and environmental changes, landscape fragmentation, and human disturbance due to urban expansion. Consequently, our study identifies them as key areas for ecological restoration. Integrating ecological value assessment with risk prediction can provide a scientific pathway for spatially sustainable governance and policy-making in ecologically sensitive urban-transition zones.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-43157-9.

Introduction

Accelerated urbanization and industrialization have triggered significant changes in regional land use patterns1. This has led to the increasing encroachment upon ecological lands, such as grasslands and forestlands. Such encroachment exerts cumulative and regional impacts on ecosystems, resulting in long-term alterations to landscape spatial patterns. Ecosystem services have undergone substantial degradation2, while ecological risks continue to escalate, posing serious threats to ecosystem stability3. Consequently, accurately identifying key areas for ecological restoration and simulating their future trends have become central issues in ecological environmental governance and territorial spatial planning4,5.

Ecosystem Service Value (ESV) serves as a vital means to quantitatively assess the contribution of ecosystems to human well-being. It was refined into 17 categories by Costanza et al.6. Their method, based on the value equivalent per unit area, has supported ecosystem value assessments globally7. In China, scholars such as Xie8,9 developed an ecosystem service value assessment model tailored to terrestrial ecosystems in the Chinese context. Building upon an improved value equivalent per unit area table, they facilitated the widespread application of ESV at regional scales. It is now extensively applied in the assessment and management of diverse natural ecosystems, including forests10, wetlands11, and deserts12. In contrast to ESV, which reflects the positive functional value of ecosystems, the landscape ecological risk index (ERI) reveals potential adverse ecological consequences resulting from changes in landscape patterns13. It focuses on assessing the likelihood and severity of damage to ecosystems caused by human activities3,14, emphasizing the reciprocal feedback relationships between landscape patterns and ecological processes15. Recent ERI assessments have increasingly incorporated multi-scale16,17 and multi-dimensional approaches18, integrated with GIS and remote sensing technologies. These advances enable spatial simulation and prediction of cumulative ecological effects at regional19, watershed20, and urban levels4,5,21,22, providing technical support for ecological restoration and risk management.

Key areas for ecological restoration are spatial units that perform vital ecological functions, such as water conservation, soil retention, and biodiversity maintenance and are fundamental to safeguarding regional and national ecological security23,24. The scientific identification of these areas is instrumental not only for optimizing territorial spatial patterns but also for consolidating national ecological security and enhancing the supply capacity of ecological products. Early studies often relied on single ecological indicators25,26, such as land cover types or habitat quality, which struggled to capture the complex structure and dynamic changes of ecosystems comprehensively. Recently, scholars have increasingly advocated for integrating multi-dimensional elements, including ecosystem services, ecological sensitivity, ecological security patterns, and ecological risks, into a unified assessment framework21,22,27. Among these, the coupled analysis of ESV and ERI is regarded as an effective approach to incorporate both ecological “value” and “risk” perspectives. This method can reveal core regional ecological and environmental issues through the dual dimensions of ecosystem supply capacity and vulnerability, thereby facilitating appropriate ecological management strategies4,5,28,29.

However, most current studies on key ecological restoration areas remain focused on static identification and zoning analysis based on current data. They lack a systematic exploration of dynamic ecosystem evolution under future scenarios. In reality, ecosystems are highly dynamic and sensitive complex systems, influenced by climate change, land use change, and policy regulation, exhibiting significant spatiotemporal uncertainty30. There is an urgent need to extend the temporal dimension and construct dynamic simulation mechanisms capable of predicting future ecological patterns, thereby enabling proactive ecological management and risk prevention. Currently, widely used land use simulation models include CA-Markov31, CLUE-S32, FLUS33, and the rapidly developing PLUS (Patch-generating Land Use Simulation) model26,34. The PLUS model demonstrates significant advantages in simulation accuracy and spatial explicability. This is particularly true for simulating natural landscape types, such as forests, grasslands, and water bodies. By incorporating a random seed generation mechanism and land use expansion probability maps, it can realistically reproduce landscape evolution processes, considering the interactive effects of socio-economic and physical geographical factors34. Furthermore, most existing scenario simulation studies focus on a single policy objective (e.g., natural development or ecological protection), lacking a comprehensive analysis of land use change pathways under multiple policy orientations. This limits the ability to identify ecological risks associated with different development models29,35. Therefore, conducting multi-scenario simulations based on the PLUS model helps systematically delineate future landscape pattern evolution pathways and identify differences in ecological risks under varying policy interventions, providing policymakers with context-specific spatial governance solutions36.

The Qinling Mountains are located in the heartland of China, and their ecological security underpins the foundation for the sustainable development of the Chinese nation. The Shaanxi section of its northern foothills, as a typical mountain-plain transition zone, represents a critical frontline where the conflict between urban expansion and ecological protection is most acute37. In this region, the supply of ecosystem services and the exposure to landscape ecological risks are spatially interwoven and causally coupled, rendering traditional single-perspective assessments or static analyses insufficient to address the complexity and dynamism required for its management. Although methods such as the PLUS model, ESV, and ERI assessments have been widely adopted, there remains a lack of an analytical framework capable of systematically linking the entire chain of “pattern diagnosis, driving mechanism analysis, scenario projection, and decision support” in ecologically stressed urban-transition zones like the northern Qinling foothills. To address this, this study aims to integrate the dual perspectives of service supply and risk exposure within the representative ecological-urban transition zone of the northern Qinling foothills in Shaanxi. By systematically incorporating the Geodetector method, this study applies a “value-risk-simulation” framework to systematically identify key ecological restoration areas (Fig. 1). Furthermore, based on socio-economic development trends, four scenario simulations, Natural Development Scenario (NDS), Ecological Protection Scenario (EPS), Food Security Scenario (FSS), and Sustainable Development Scenario (SDS), were established to explore the impact of future land use changes on the pattern of ecologically critical areas. The results are expected to provide scientific references for optimizing the ecological protection redline, formulating territorial spatial plans, and delineating priority areas for ecological restoration in the northern foothills of the Qinling Mountains.

Materials and methods

Study area overview

As illustrated in Fig. 2, the study area encompasses the Shaanxi section of the northern foothills of the Qinling Mountains, located in central Shaanxi Province, China. It is bounded by the main ridge of the Qinling Mountains to the south and the southern bank of the Wei River to the north. This region primarily includes the mountainous and hilly areas within the cities of Xi’an, Baoji, and Weinan, forming a critical transition zone between the Guanzhong Urban Agglomeration and the Qinling ecological barrier. The climate is warm temperate semi-humid, with an average annual precipitation of 600–900 mm, which coincides with the warm season, creating favorable conditions for water conservation. Vegetation is diverse, encompassing ecosystems such as plantations, natural broad-leaved forests, shrublands, and grasslands. As the watershed divide between the Yangtze River and Yellow River basins, the region holds a pivotal ecological status38.

Data sources and preprocessing

This study utilized multi-source datasets, including: (1) land use data; (2) meteorological data; (3) physiographic data; (4) socio-economic data; and (5) accessibility data. Land use data were obtained from the 30 m spatial resolution China Multi-period Land Use Land Cover Remote Sensing Monitoring Dataset provided by the Resource and Environmental Science and Data Center of the Chinese Academy of Sciences. These data were reclassified into six categories: Cultivated Land, forestland, grassland, water bodies, construction land, and unused land. Meteorological data, including mean annual precipitation and mean annual temperature, were sourced from the National Earth System Science Data Center. Physiographic data, comprising elevation, slope, and soil type, were acquired from the Geospatial Data Cloud. Socio-economic data, mainly including population, GDP, the sown area and yield of grain crops within the study area, and grain yield per unit area, were derived from statistical yearbooks. Accessibility data encompassed distances to primary, secondary, and tertiary roads, railways, expressways, distances to towns, and distances to water systems. To facilitate unified spatial analysis, all raster data were resampled to a uniform pixel size of 30 m × 30 m. Furthermore, considering the environmental characteristics of the study area and the operational feasibility of the research, a 2 km × 2 km grid was selected as the evaluation unit, yielding 4052 units in total. Multi-scenario simulation of land use based on the CA-PLUS model

Model accuracy validation

First, based on previous studies26,29 and the current conditions of the study area, 14 driving factors were selected from three categories: natural, economic, and transportation accessibility. These factors include: mean annual precipitation, mean annual temperature, DEM, slope, soil type, population density, GDP, and distances to primary roads, secondary roads, tertiary roads, railways, expressways, towns, and water systems. These factors were converted to raster format and unified to the same projection coordinate system and spatial resolution as the land use data. Subsequently, the Land Expansion Analysis Strategy (LEAS) was employed to extract land conversion rules, predict the future development trends of various land types, and quantify the contribution of each driving factor to regional land change (see Supplementary Table 1 for specific contribution scores). The robustness of the results was statistically verified using the Root Mean Square Error (RMSE)34, with the following values: cultivated land (0.13), forestland (0.11), grassland (0.12), water bodies (0.04), construction land (0.09), and bare land (0.02). An average error of 8.35% further underscores the scientific reliability of the analysis. Following this, the CARS module was utilized to simulate the 2020 land use pattern, which was then compared in detail with the actual 2020 land use data. The validation results yielded a Kappa coefficient of 0.873. According to the correspondence between the Kappa coefficient and simulation accuracy, a value between 0.8 and 1 indicates that the simulation results possess high accuracy26.

Scenario design

To address different development needs, this study, in accordance with relevant policies such as the Shaanxi Province Qinling Mountains Ecological Environment Protection Master Plan and the Shaanxi Province Territorial Spatial Plan (2021–2035), and referencing studies from adjacent regions and previous research39, established transfer matrices with transition restrictions between land use categories for four scenarios (NDS, EPS, FSS, and SDS) within the CARS module (Supplementary Table 2) to predict land use distribution patterns under each scenario. The adjustment magnitudes of transition probabilities between land use types under each scenario are designed to qualitatively and differentially represent the regulatory intensity of different policy orientations (e.g., ecological priority, food security). The specific values are set based on an analysis of historical conversion rates in the study area and with reference to the parameter ranges used in scenario simulation studies of similar regions33,36,39. This approach ensures that the generated future landscapes exhibit plausible and marked differences, thereby effectively supporting comparative analysis.

Bivariate spatial autocorrelation

The spatial autocorrelation analysis model can reveal the spatial interdependence between different elements. This study calculated the bivariate global Moran’s I to represent the spatial relationship between ESV and ERI42,43, whose value ranges from − 1 to 1. Furthermore, by generating a bivariate local Moran’s I map, the results were visualized for each regional unit to form a LISA (Local Indicators of Spatial Association) cluster map. This map was used to identify local spatial clusters, specifically “hot spots” (High–High clusters) and “cold spots” (Low–Low clusters), and to detect local spatial anomalies between ESV and ERI. The analysis was conducted using GeoDa 1.16 software, with the spatial weight matrix based on first-order Queen Contiguity and Rook Contiguity.

Geodetector

The Geodetector method, a statistical tool for detecting spatial stratified heterogeneity and revealing the explanatory power of various factors on a dependent variable, has become an important analytical tool in fields such as land spatial planning, ecological environmental protection, and land use transition44,45. Regional ESV and ERI are influenced by multiple factors, including geographical and socio-economic elements. As traditional linear regression imposes stringent requirements on data quality, this study, referencing relevant research26,46 and the characteristics of the study area, selected nine indicators: DEM (X1), slope (X2), NDVI (X3), mean annual precipitation (X4), mean annual temperature (X5), population density (X6), GDP (X7), distance to water systems (X8), and distance to roads (X9). The factor detector within Geodetector was employed to quantitatively analyze the impact of each factor on ESV and ERI. By comparing the magnitude of the q-statistic, the explanatory power of each independent variable on the dependent variable was obtained44.

Results

Land use status and prediction results for the Shaanxi section of the northern Qinling foothills

Spatiotemporal evolution characteristics of land use

Figure 3 shows that the land use types in the Shaanxi section of the northern Qinling Mountains are primarily composed of cultivated land, forestland, and grassland. The overall trend showed a significant decrease in cultivated land, forestland, and grassland, alongside a rapid expansion of construction land from 1990 to 2020. Spatially (as shown in Fig. 4), cultivated land dominated the northern part of the study area, while forestland and grassland were predominant in the south over these 30 years. Construction land was highly concentrated in the main urban area of Xi’an and its surrounding regions. Unused land was distributed more diffusely, primarily in the eastern Weinan area of the study region. The results of the land use transfer matrix (Fig. 5) indicate that during 1990–2000, the primary land conversions involved the transformation of 12,347.17 ha of cultivated land and 1345.86 ha of forestland into construction land. For the period 2000–2010, significant changes occurred among cultivated land, grassland, and construction land. Notably, a substantial amount of cultivated land was converted into forestland, grassland, and construction land. The land conversion pattern for 2010–2020 was largely consistent with that of the previous decade (2000–2010). Cultivated land area decreased markedly, with most being converted to grassland and construction land. It is noteworthy that the water body area increased, recovering by 3625.83 ha compared to 2010, primarily through conversions to and from cultivated land and grassland. In summary, from 1990 to 2020, the rapid expansion of construction land in the study area significantly encroached upon cultivated land and forestland. This led to increased pressure on the regional ecological environment and a decline in ecosystem stability.

Multi-scenario simulation of land use

This study simulated 2030 land use patterns for the northern Qinling foothills (Shaanxi section) using the PLUS model. As shown in Fig. 3, except under the FSS, cultivated land decreased under the other three scenarios. Except under the EPS, forestland decreased compared to 2020 under the other three scenarios. Construction land increased to varying degrees under all scenarios except FSS, with the NDS showing the largest increase. Overall, the SDS exhibited the smallest magnitude of change among the four scenarios. Figure 4 reveals that the spatial distribution characteristics of land types under the four scenarios remained consistent with those in 2020. Notably, construction land under the NDS was relatively dispersed. The results of the land use transfer matrix (Fig. 5) indicate that under the NDS, large-scale conversion occurred from cultivated land to forestland, grassland, and construction land, with the largest conversion (14,451.84 ha) being to construction land. This pattern is generally similar to the land conversion characteristics observed in 2020. Under the EPS, land conversion was primarily characterized by the transfer of cultivated land to forestland and grassland, leading to an increase in the area of both. Under the FSS, no cultivated land was converted out; instead, it received inflows of 10,683.09 ha from grassland and 2267.91 ha from water bodies. Under the SDS, the most significant conversion occurred between cultivated land and both grassland and construction land, manifested as the transfer of 6294.78 ha of cultivated land to grassland and 5083.46 ha to construction land. In summary, the multi-scenario simulation results demonstrate that land use evolution is significantly influenced by development models: the NDS leads to severe loss of cultivated land, the FSS involves substantial encroachment on grassland and water bodies, while the SDS and EPS offer more sustainable pathways by balancing ecological conservation and development pressures.

ESV characterization and prediction for the Shaanxi section of the Northern Qinling foothills

Spatiotemporal evolution characteristics of ESV

Temporally (Table 2), the ESV of the Shaanxi section of the northern Qinling Mountains showed a pattern of continuous decline followed by a rebound from 1990 to 2020. The peak value occurred in 1990 at 24.816 × 108 CNY, while the lowest value was recorded in 2010 at 24.273 × 108 CNY. This initial decline was primarily attributable to the reduction in natural land types such as water bodies and grassland between 1990 and 2010. The subsequent recovery from 2010 to 2020 underscores the critical role of natural land in driving ESV changes. In terms of ecosystem service types (Fig. 6), regulating services constituted the primary function in the study area, accounting for approximately 70% of the total ESV and exhibiting the most significant variation, which mirrored the overall trend of total ESV change. Cultural services contributed the least, representing about 3%. Among the secondary ecosystem service functions, hydrological regulation and soil retention accounted for the largest shares, whereas nutrient cycling contributed the least, comprising less than 1% of the total. Analyzed by individual land use type (Fig. 7), the contribution to the total ESV in the Shaanxi section of the northern Qinling Mountains was ranked as follows: forestland > grassland > water bodies > cultivated land > unused land.

To examine spatial heterogeneity, ESV was interpolated using Kriging and classified into five levels via the Natural Breaks method (Fig. 8). The spatial pattern remained generally stable from 1990 to 2020. High-ESV areas were concentrated in southern Xi’an, parts of Baoji (Weibin and Chencang Districts), and southern Weinan, regions characterized by high elevation, extensive forest/grassland cover, and low land-use intensity. In contrast, low ESV levels were found in urban cores such as central Xi’an, Huayin City, and northern Tongguan County, where construction land dominates and ecological spaces have been significantly encroached upon.

Analysis of ESV changes under various scenarios

As shown in Table 2, among the four scenarios, the ESV under both the NDS and EPS was higher than that in 2020, with the NDS yielding the highest ESV (251.84 × 108 CNY) among all scenarios. The lowest ESV was observed under the FSS (240.01 × 108 CNY). Spatially, as illustrated in Fig. 8, the distribution characteristics of ESV under the four scenarios were largely consistent with the patterns observed from 1990 to 2020. The NDS exhibited the largest combined area of highest-value and high-value zones, accounting for 3.82% of the total area, primarily located in water bodies and forest land. This was followed by the EPS, while the FSS had the smallest proportion of these high ESV zones, comprising only 3.27% of the total area. The primary reason for this is that the NDS involved an increase in water body area. Since water bodies possess the highest unit ESV, they significantly influence the overall ESV. Conversely, under the FSS, cultivated land encroached upon ecological lands such as forest land and grassland, leading to ecosystem degradation.

ERI characterization and prediction for the Shaanxi section of the Northern Qinling foothills

Spatiotemporal evolution characteristics of ERI

Using the Natural Breaks method, the ERI for the Shaanxi section of the northern Qinling foothills was classified into five zones: lowest-value zone (ERI ≤ 6.2), low-value zone (6.2 < ERI ≤ 8.9), moderate-value zone (8.9 < ERI ≤ 12.0), high-value zone (12.0 < ERI ≤ 16.2), and highest-value zone (ERI ≥ 16.2). As shown in Table 3, the overall ecological risk in the region was predominantly at low and moderate levels, exhibiting a decreasing trend from 1990 to 2020. Specifically, the area of lowest-risk zones increased from 252,825 ha to 282,700 ha, while the highest-risk zone decreased by 24,425 ha over the same period. Changes in the other three zones were minor. Spatially, as illustrated in Fig. 8, highest and high ERI areas were concentrated along the Weihe River, the Xi’an urban core, and other low-altitude regions, with risk levels clearly decreasing as altitude increased. These findings highlight the need to strengthen ecological protection and restoration efforts, particularly in vulnerable aquatic ecosystems of the study area.

Overall, the ERI under all four scenarios decreased compared to the 2020 baseline. The FSS exhibited the largest reduction, followed by the EPS, the NDS, and finally the SDS. Conversely, the areas of the medium and relatively high ERI zones decreased by 20,000 ha and 35,675 ha. Under the FSS, the areas of the low, relatively low, and medium ERI zones increased by 30,625 ha, 32,775 ha, and 7275 ha, respectively, compared to 2020. In contrast, the areas of the relatively high and high ERI zones decreased by 47,500 ha and 23,175 ha. Under the SDS (2020–2030), the areas of the low and medium ERI zones increased by 28,300 ha and 8050 ha, respectively. Meanwhile, the areas of the relatively low, relatively high, and high ERI zones decreased by 5,900 ha, 24,825 ha, and 5625 ha. Spatially, the distribution characteristics of ERI under these scenarios remained largely consistent with the patterns observed from 1990 to 2020 (Fig. 9).

Identification of key ecological areas

Figure 11 illustrates the bivariate LISA cluster spatial distribution of ESV and ERI across different periods. The spatial pattern remained highly consistent throughout these periods. The High–High (High ESV–High ERI) clusters were predominantly distributed along the banks of major water bodies such as the Wei River, Ba River, and Hei River, as well as in areas with dense water networks like the Changan District and Lintong District of Xi’an. The area of these High–High clusters was greatest in 2020 and under the NDS scenario for 2030. The Low–Low clusters were concentrated along the main ridge of the Qinling Mountains. The High–Low clusters were located in the high-altitude forest and grassland regions in the southern part of the study area. Conversely, the Low–High clusters were predominantly aggregated within the main urban areas of Xi’an, Baoji, and Weinan. Non-significant areas accounted for approximately 65% of the total study area. Based on the spatial association between ESV and ERI, this study identifies the High–High clusters as key areas for ecological restoration28,29.

Discussion

Analysis of dominant driving mechanisms in ESV and ERI evolution

From 1990 to 2020, the ESV in the Shaanxi section of the northern Qinling foothills exhibited a trend of “decline followed by recovery”, while the ERI showed an overall decrease. Quantitative analysis using the Geodetector method revealed that the spatial differentiation of both ESV and ERI is characterized by “shared environmental constraints and differentiated socioeconomic responses”44 (Fig. 12). First, mean annual temperature emerged as the most stable natural underlying factor shaping the spatial patterns of both ESV and ERI (consistently showing the highest q-value), establishing the fundamental constraint of climate change on the regional ecosystem. Topographic gradient was another significant natural factor influencing the spatial heterogeneity of ERI. Areas with low ERI were primarily distributed in the southern mountainous regions of the study area, which are characterized by high forest and grassland coverage and relatively intact ecosystem functions. In contrast, high ERI areas were concentrated in the major urban zones of Xi’an, Baoji, and Weinan, where construction land density is high, confirming that human activity intensity is a key driver elevating landscape risk19,42,43. More importantly, the response pathways of ESV and ERI to socioeconomic factors were fundamentally different. Between 1990 and 2020, the explanatory power of population density and GDP on ESV continuously increased, quantitatively confirming that ecological land loss driven by urbanization is the core mechanism behind ESV decline47,48. In contrast, the association between ERI and socioeconomic factors did not strengthen synchronously after 2010. Against the backdrop of intensified ecological protection policies for the Qinling Mountains implemented by national and local governments during the same period (e.g., large-scale Grain-for-Green projects and soil-water conservation measures), this phenomenon indicates that the “Qinling Mountains Ecological Conservation” policy effectively buffered the amplifying effect of human activities on landscape risk49. Concurrently, the restoration of forestland and grassland driven by these projects also served as a crucial turning point for the recovery of ESV after 2010. Therefore, the “decline-recovery” trajectory of ESV primarily reflects the net change in the quantity of ecological land, whereas the sustained decline in ERI more directly reflects the effectiveness of policy interventions in mitigating landscape structural risk. This differential response mechanism highlights the need to distinguish between the two dimensions of “service supply capacity” and “system structural risk” in ecological management.

Formation mechanism, scenario evolution, and management implications of key ecological restoration areas

The formation of “High–High” clusters results from the direct spatial coupling of superior ecological endowment and high-intensity human disturbance. The areas identified in this study are primarily distributed along major rivers and water bodies, as well as in urban-rural transition zones. Their inherent high ecosystem service capacity is continually subjected to landscape structural pressure from urban expansion and agricultural activities28,42,43. Geodetector analysis confirms that the ERI in these areas responds significantly to socioeconomic driving factors. Furthermore, their spatial extent highly overlaps with known water-environmentally sensitive zones28,50, collectively revealing that human-dominated compound stress is their core characteristic. It is noteworthy that a uniform “high ERI” indicator may correspond to different ecological states. To refine management directives, this study further preliminarily categorizes “High ESV-High ERI” areas into two subtypes based on driving causes and spatial context: ① Persistent Disturbance Type, commonly found at the urban expansion frontier, where the high risk stems from sustained anthropogenic pressure, and the ecosystem is on a clear degradation trajectory14; ② Natural Fluctuation Type, which may occur in ecological restoration project areas or zones subject to natural disturbances, where the high risk reflects a system in an unstable transitional state4,5. This distinction suggests that interventions for “High–High” key areas should evolve from “state identification” to “cause diagnosis” to match more targeted measures. For instance, for the former type, emphasis should be on “strict protection and urgent restoration” to curb disturbance sources and rebuild structure; for the latter, the focus should shift more towards “process protection and assisted recovery” to safeguard natural succession.

Multi-scenario simulation results, from a future perspective, corroborate the differential impacts of various policy pathways on the pattern of key areas. The EPS, by strictly restricting the conversion of ecological land, can effectively curb the expansion of “Persistent Disturbance Type” areas. The SDS, while balancing development needs, optimizes the layout of construction land, helping to alleviate overall pressure on key areas and maintain landscape connectivity. Conversely, the disorderly sprawl of construction land under the NDS would exacerbate the aforementioned degradation risks, while the FSS, in pursuing cropland area targets, may introduce new stresses in ecologically sensitive areas.

In summary, this study proposes a hierarchical and classified precision governance framework: ① For “High ESV-High ERI” key areas, implement “diagnostic-type” priority intervention. Based on the sub-type identification results above, and adhering to the bottom line of strict protection, flexibly adopt different measures ranging from urgent restoration to adaptive management. ② For “Low ESV-High ERI” areas in urban cores, promote “green renewal and risk mitigation” by optimizing landscape structure through blue-green infrastructure. ③ For “High ESV-Low ERI” ecological barrier areas in the remote Qinling Mountains, adhere to “priority protection and natural restoration”. By linking “identification-diagnosis-simulation,” this framework aims to transform ecological assessment into a set of references supporting differentiated spatial governance, thereby providing scientific support for prioritizing regional ecological restoration and selecting strategies.

Research limitations and future prospects

The integrated “Value-Risk-Simulation” framework constructed in this study provides an exploratory practice for the systematic identification of regional ecological restoration priority areas. However, this research still has several limitations that warrant refinement in future work. First, the depth of framework integration needs enhancement. While this study sequentially applied and compared results from the PLUS model, ESV assessment, ERI index, and Geodetector, achieving informational complementarity, the feedback mechanisms between these modules have not been closed-loop represented in the model. This, to some extent, limits the depth of mechanistic simulation. Second, there is uncertainty regarding key parameters and scenario settings. The weight and vulnerability assignments in the ERI assessment, as well as the land use transition probabilities in the PLUS scenario simulations, were primarily adopted from existing studies and adjusted relatively based on policy orientations. Although this ensures methodological comparability and logical differentiation among scenarios, a systematic parameter sensitivity analysis and uncertainty quantification were not conducted. This may lead to certain local deviations in the results, and the statistical robustness of ESV/ERI differences under various scenarios requires further verification. Third, there is an indirect inference of ecological processes. The explanation in the discussion regarding the formation and maintenance mechanisms of “High–High” areas is primarily based on spatial statistical correlations and logical deduction. It lacks direct observation and modeling verification of specific ecological processes or ecosystem resilience.

Addressing the above limitations, future research can deepen in the following directions:① Develop integrated models with coupled feedback mechanisms, for instance, by incorporating ecosystem service flows and ecological risk feedback into land use change simulations. ② Conduct parameter sensitivity analysis and uncertainty propagation assessment, and attempt to combine participatory methods to build scenarios with greater consensus. ③ Integrate multi-source data, such as hyperspectral remote sensing, UAV (Unmanned Aerial Vehicle) monitoring, and ground-based ecological positioning observations, to reveal the key ecological processes driving landscape pattern changes, thereby enhancing the reliability of mechanistic explanations and the refinement of management practices.
