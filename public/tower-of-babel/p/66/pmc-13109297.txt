Protein design drives synthetic biology research of plant natural products
Authors: Xiaopeng Zhang, Yinying Yao, Ye Wang, Yongshuo Ma, Yi Shang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPlant natural products (PNPs) are widely used in pharmaceutical, cosmetic, and food industries. Currently, their commercial supply largely relies on plant extraction, yet, suffers from low yield and limited resources. Synthetic biology provides a promising solution by reconstituting biosynthetic pathways of PNPs in microbial systems. However, unknown enzymatic steps or poor functional performance of plant-derived enzymes often make it particularly challenging for the PNPs production in microorganisms. To address these limitations, much progress has been made in enzyme mining, enzymatic mechanism elucidation, and enzyme optimization by protein design technologies. The deep integration of artificial intelligence and synthetic biology has provided attractive solutions for the sustainable production of PNPs. This review introduces the cutting-edge advances of protein design technologies in PNPs biosynthesis, elaborates on the approaches to resolve the main bottlenecks in PNPs biosynthesis, and discusses the immense application potential driven by the integration of AI and synthetic biology.Keywords: Protein design, Plant natural products, Synthetic biology, Artificial intelligence

Introduction

As the natural treasure trove of bioactive molecules, plant natural products (PNPs) play important and irreplaceable roles in pharmaceuticals, food, and cosmetic industries, due to their diverse structures and numerous functions [1,2]. To date, more than 300,000 PNPs have been globally discovered and functionally identified, including drugs such as paclitaxel [3] and artemisinin [4], and industrial raw materials such as vanillin [5] and carotenoids [6]. Due to their structural complexity, chemical synthesis of PNPs has been challenging, often resulting in issues like high costs and environmental pollution. Currently, the commercial supply of PNPs largely relies on plant extraction, which suffers from low yields and limited natural resources. Although methods like plant tissue culture offer an alternative route, the inflexibility and non-robustness limit their application in scale-up production [7]. In light of these challenges, engineering microbes for the production of valuable natural products provides a promising way for their cost-effective supply, shifting the production from plant farms to cell factories, and thereby overcoming multiple constraints in resources, efficiency, and environment [8,9]. In recent years, with remarkable advancements in synthetic biology and metabolic engineering, it has become possible to successfully synthesize various high-value PNPs by expressing the synthetic pathway in microbial systems [[10], [11], [12], [13], [14], [15], [16], [17], [18]]. For instance, the yield of artemisinic acid, an artemisinin precursor, has reached 25 ​g/L in the engineered yeast, where the fermentation yield of artemisinin in a 100 ​L fermenter can replace the cultivation of 3400 ​ha of Artemisia annua [10]. Ma et al. achieved a β-carotene yield of 39.5 ​g/L in a 5 ​L fermenter using engineered Yarrowia lipolytica, which has reached the industrial mass production level [16].

Despite wonderful achievement for microbial production of PNPs, synthetic efforts to achieve economically visible titers and productivities are frequently hampered by the following challenges. Firstly, due to the biosynthetic complexity of PNPs, the pathway of over 70 ​% of natural products has not been completely uncovered and functionally elucidated [1], which prevents them from being fully synthesized in heterologous systems. Secondly, poor adaptability of plant-derived enzymes in heterologous systems often leads to low activity, poor substrate specificity, and low stability, which makes them suboptimal physical properties and reduces the efficiency and yield of functional enzymes [19]. Thirdly, the cross-species mis-localization of plant enzymes in heterologous systems further weakens the protein activity and stability, particularly for transmembrane proteins [20]. Fourthly, insufficient precursor supply, imbalanced redox power, and metabolic crosstalk and competition can also affect the performance of chassis cells and production yields [21]. To address these issues, many effective engineering strategies have been developed, such as enhancing/reconstituting precursor synthesis pathways, engineering cofactor synthesis pathways, optimizing redox power regeneration systems, and dynamically regulating metabolic flux [22,23].

At the level of enzymes, protein design technologies are assuming an ever-growing role and hold great value in guiding and driving the synthetic biology of PNPs including enzyme mining, enzyme mechanistic elucidation, and enzyme optimization toward more precision and efficiency [[24], [25], [26]]. Through the integrated strategies of data-driven enzyme function decoding, structural simulation-based mechanism prediction, and intelligent design of enzymes, key enabling technologies are provided for the synthetic biology of PNPs. In the future, with the deep integration of artificial intelligence (AI) and synthetic biology, intelligent cell factories are expected to become the core engine for the sustainable production of PNPs. This review will introduce the cutting-edge advancements of protein design technologies in enzyme mining, multimodal modeling of catalytic mechanisms, and comprehensive strategies of enzyme optimization, and systematically present the pathways to solve the bottleneck problems in PNPs biosynthesis, and discuss the limitless prospects in application driven by the integration of AI and synthetic biology.

Protein design for enzyme mining

The complex structure of PNPs usually results in a long and intricate biosynthetic pathway. The elucidation of the whole pathway of target compounds is essential to enable their production in microbial hosts, which represents a critical bottleneck in the synthetic biology of PNPs. Mining novel enzyme resources can help decipher the key nodes in complex metabolic networks and overcome the rate-limiting steps. Traditional enzyme mining mainly relies on sequence similarity searches, which predict the characteristics of query sequences by identifying homologous sequences [27]. Despite the success of sequence-based homology alignment, some key problems remain uncovered because one-dimensional sequences cannot characterize three-dimensional functional properties of proteins [28]. Enzymes with high sequence similarity can exhibit different functions, while those with low homology can share similar functions. Furthermore, it is challenging to effectively mine low-homology enzymes or uncharacterized orphan enzymes, thereby often missing novel functional enzymes without matching annotations in databases. Moreover, homologous enzymes in plants frequently undergo functional diversification due to gene family expansion, making it difficult to distinguish their substrate specificities based solely on sequence similarity [29].

With the rapid development of AI, protein structure prediction algorithms (such as AlphaFold and RoseTTAFold [30,31]), ligand docking algorithms (such as RosettaLigand Docking, DiffDock, and AlphaFill [[32], [33], [34]]), and structure clustering algorithms (such as Foldseek [35]) can rapidly predict and cluster high-precision protein complex structures, making it possible to conduct structure-based enzyme mining, which could circumvent the limitations of traditional sequence homology [36]. Structure-based enzyme mining could be summarized into a three-stage strategy: (1) large-scale structure modeling of target enzyme families; (2) structural branching by of key areas conformations via structural clustering or molecular docking; (3) screening candidate enzymes in plant or microbial chassis (Fig. 1A) [[37], [38], [39], [40]]. Using this strategy, Huang et al. successfully developed novel deaminases as base-editing tools, providing a universal framework for mining low-homology enzyme families in plants and efficient gene editing tools for the synthesis of PNPs based on plant chassis (Fig. 1A) [37]. Through structure clustering, Jordan et al. successfully identified a structural homolog homogentisate solanyltransferase (GlyMa_02G168000) of cannabigerolic acid synthase with sequence homology less than 20 ​% using Foldseek, providing a new component for heterologous cannabinoid synthesis in yeast and demonstrating the advantage of structural clustering in mining orphan enzymes [39]. By integrating transcriptome analysis with structural virtual screening and molecular docking, Yao et al. identified a novel glycosyltransferase involved in salidroside biosynthesis from 82 candidates (Fig. 1B) [40].

Enzyme mining based on structure and AI. A, deaminase mining by structure modeling and clustering [37]; B, transcriptome and structure-based virtual screening for glycosyltransferase involved in salidroside biosynthesis [40]; C, AI-driven enzyme mining pipeline [41].

Researchers have developed specialized enzyme mining databases and pipelines based on structural information for large enzyme families of plants. Since these enzymes usually exhibit high structural similarity with low sequence similarity, researchers abandoned using overall structural homology as the core basis, and instead focused on the essential region for enzyme function. Cytochrome P450s (P450s), one of the largest enzyme families in plants, play critical roles in the diversity and functional modification of PNPs [42]. Although over one million plant P450 enzyme sequences have been identified to date, only several crystal structures have been reported, severely limiting their mechanism elucidation and functional modification [43]. Wang et al. constructed a dedicated plant P450 database and web-based tools for structural construction and ligand docking, which facilitated mining of P450s. They used the interaction between ligands and active centers as the core screening criterion, and combined a scoring function which integrated the total energy of Rosetta and the free energy of ligand binding to quantify the binding strength of the ligand for P450s mining [44].Plant UDP-glycosyltransferases (UGTs), another large enzyme family in plants, are widespread in nature and play key roles in modification of PNPs [45]. To effectively discover UGT candidates and explore their functional mechanism, Liu et al. developed a virtual screening and glycosyl donor prediction tool for plant UGTs. They found a high correlation between the pocket volumes and substrate volumes of UGTs. Meanwhile, by analyzing the similarity of substrate molecules and the affinity of substrate binding, they determined the substrates (Fig. 2A). Focusing on key residues for sugar donor binding rather than the overall structures, they constructed a regression model using these key residues as feature vectors for predicting the type of sugar donors (Fig. 2B). This model drastically reduced the screening scope from hundreds of candidates to fewer than 10, with a glycosyl donor type prediction accuracy of 89.6 ​% [29].

AI-driven enzyme mining tools such as CataPro [41], PLMSearch [46], ESM-Ezy [47], REME [48], and DHR [49] have provided innovative solutions for the biosynthesis of PNPs, particularly for enzymes with low sequence similarity (Fig. 1C). For example, CataPro predicts enzymatic kinetic parameters by constructing an unbiased validation dataset through sequence similarity clustering based on pre-trained models and molecular fingerprints. Using this model, the highly efficient enzyme SsCSO was successfully mined for vanillin biosynthesis with an activity 19.53-fold higher than that of the initial enzyme CSO2 (carotenoid cleavage oxygenase from Caulobacter segnis) (Fig. 1C) [41].

The application of protein design for mechanistic elucidation of enzymes

Enzymes are the core components for PNPs biosynthesis. However, the diversity of enzymes with unknown functions and complex regulatory mechanisms severely restricts the efficient reconstruction of metabolic pathways and functional optimization. Traditional mechanistic research mainly relies on enzymatic kinetics experiments, crystallographic structure analysis, and mutation validation [50]. However, due to some objective obstacles such as membrane-bound characteristics of many plant enzymes and their difficulty in obtaining crystals, the progress in mechanistic studies remains a challenge.

The rapid development of protein design technologies, particularly breakthroughs in structure prediction, molecular simulation, mutation screening, and artificial design, have provided efficient and systematic tools for mechanistic analysis [51]. By predicting structures, molecular docking, and comparing substrate binding sites, researchers can locate substrate binding pockets and catalytic sites of plant enzymes, and clarify the structure-function relationships through experimental validation (Fig. 3A) [[52], [53], [54]]. For example, Chen et al. identified a set of conserved residues and investigated their impacts on product selectivity by summarized 98 mutation sites across 25 plant oxidosqualene cyclases [54]. Additionally, constructing single-substitution libraries (such as alanine scanning) enables systematic evaluation of each residue's contribution to enzyme function [[55], [56], [57], [58], [59]]. For instance, Wang et al. identified key residues that influence rhamnosyl donor selectivity in the saponin 2″-O-rhamnosyltransferase GuRhaGT through structural simulation and site-directed mutagenesis [57].

Static and dynamic mechanism analysis pipelines by protein design technologies. A, static mechanism analysis by structure modeling, evolutionary analysis,and site-directed mutagenesis for identifying key catalytic sites [60,61]; B, dynamic mechanism analysis by MD and QM/MM to elucidate the dynamic catalytic process of TS [62,63].

However, enzyme catalysis inherently involves complex dynamic conformational changes and transition-state transformations. Molecular dynamics (MD) simulation and quantum mechanics/molecular mechanics (QM/MM) [64] are increasingly becoming critical for unraveling these dynamic mechanisms [65]. By constructing an enzyme-substrate complex and performing long-time-scale dynamic simulations, researchers can capture conformational change trajectories of enzymes, identify highly flexible residues, and use enhanced sampling methods to estimate reaction pathways and energy barriers (Fig. 3B) [66]. For example, taxadiene synthase, a rate-limiting enzyme for biosynthesis of anticancer drug paclitaxel, catalyzes geranylgeranyl pyrophosphate to generate multiple products such as taxadiene, iso-taxadiene, and other by-products [62,67]. To better utilize taxadiene synthase, it is necessary to analyze the formation mechanisms for multiple products. Researchers evaluated the feasibility of different deprotonation pathways by MD simulation and QM/MM calculations, and revealed that water bridge possibly induces byproduct formation, providing atomic-level insights into catalytic mechanisms and product selectivity of taxadiene synthase (Fig. 3B) [62,63,68,69].

Overall, combining static structure prediction with dynamic simulation can identify the key catalytic residues and reveal the complex catalytic processes, providing theoretical support for rational enzyme engineering and optimization of PNPs biosynthetic pathways.

Protein design for enzyme optimization

The heterologous biosynthesis of PNPs needs to assemble and express their biosynthesis pathway in heterologous systems [70]. However, many heterologous enzymes often exhibit suboptimal stability and activity in microbial hosts due to adaptation issues. This is because that heterogenous proteins have tightly evolved to adapt to their native intracellular environments (e.g., pH, ion concentration, specific cofactors, unique glycosylation patterns, etc.) [[71], [72], [73], [74]]. These differences in the microenvironment of enzymes prevent them from maintaining their native conformations and catalytic activities in heterologous systems, representing major bottlenecks in high-efficiency microbial PNPs biosynthesis [23,75]. To circumvent these limitations, enzyme optimization strategies based on protein design provide a promising strategic support for the efficient biosynthesis of PNPs [9]. Unlike typical protein design technologies used for drug screening, antibody engineering, or tolerance optimization of industrial enzymes, the enzyme design presented here specifically focuses on optimizing traits of enzymes which are crucial for the construction of the PNPs biosynthesis pathway such as catalytic activity, substrate selectivity, product specificity, and heterologous expression. The commonly used strategies for enzyme optimization mainly include directed evolution, rational design, semi-rational design, and AI-assisted design.

Directed evolution for enzyme optimization

For early enzyme engineering, researchers predominantly employed directed evolution which required neither information of structure nor catalytic mechanism [76] to modify enzymes for PNPs biosynthesis such as linalool [77], naringenin [78], and protoberberine alkaloids [79]. Owing to the randomness of directed evolution, it is possible to realize the optimization of properties such as catalytic activity, substrate selectivity, regio/stereo-selectivity, environmental stability, and heterologous expression compatibility for enzymes related to PNPs. For example, Alberstein et al. developed a method for expressing enzymes from plant phenylpropanoid pathway in yeast cells, establishing a high-throughput screening procedure based on the color intensity of naringenin chalcone and using random mutagenesis to screen for highly active 4-coumarate:CoA ligase. Six color-hyperaccumulating clones were isolated from 50,000 colonies, with the highest catalytic efficiency improved by 5.5-fold. Meanwhile, some selected mutants have relieved the allosteric feedback inhibition by naringenin which is very strong in the wild-type enzyme [80].

It is worth noting that directed evolution typically relies on high-throughput screening methods [81]. It requires substantial resources to construct a dedicated screening platform if the target system lacks rapid detection methods, making it challenging to use directed evolution for functional modification of enzymes.

Rational design for enzyme optimization

Rational design is another commonly used strategy for enzyme optimization for PNPs biosynthesis [82]. Based on deep understanding of the relationship between structure and function of enzymes, the strategies of rational design precisely modify key residues through techniques such as structural simulation and site-directed mutagenesis. Using this strategy, Sun et al. modified the plant cytochrome P450 (CYP72A63) to achieve precise control over chemo- and regio-selectivity in the production of rare licorice triterpenoids. L398I enabled C-29-specific oxidation for 29-OH-11-oxo-β-amyrin by inducing 180° substrate rotation. Hydrophobic mutation of L509I located in the distal active pocket increased glycyrrhetol content to over 90 ​% by inhibiting product binding. T338S increased yields of glycyrrhetinic acid by over 7-fold by reducing the hydrophobicity of the active pocket to achieve complete C-30 regioselectivity (Fig. 4A, B, and 4C) [83]. Ma et al. optimized the Emo glycosyltransferase PcUGT73BF6 for emodin glycoside synthesis by rational design. They found that the serine side chain of S194 located at the entrance of the substrate binding pocket creates steric hindrance to emodin binding, while the long side chain of S394 at the active center restricts glycosyl transfer pathways. Through alanine scanning, molecular dynamics simulation, and binding free energy calculations, the mutation S194G/S394G was shown to shorten the distance between catalytic site and substrate, reduce the reaction energy barrier, and enhance activity by improving protein stability [84].

Pipelines for different strategies by rational design. A (L398I), B (L509I), and C (T338S), controlling chemo-/regio-selectivity of CYP72A63 for rare licorice triterpenoids production [83]; D, rational design of CYP87D20 with evolutionary information for biosynthesis of mogrol precursor [85]; E, rational design of HCinS with dynamics simulation for various products [86]; F, rational design of CYP728B70 based on energy virtual screening for dehydroabietic acid synthesis [87].

In recent years, the rapid development of bioinformatics and computational biology has provided more references such as phylogenetic information, dynamic molecular trajectories, and energy changes for precise and efficient rational design of enzymes [82,[88], [89], [90]].

Firstly, the massive sequence data from next-generation DNA sequencing technology has opened up unparalleled opportunities for investigating protein evolution across different species and co-varying/conservative residues within proteins [91,92].

Multiple sequence alignment (MSA) was usually employed to identify conserved elements in catalytic residues, substrate-binding pockets, and conformational regulatory hotspots, and then to perform the enzyme modification. For instance, the position specific scoring matrix based on MSA can calculate the probability of amino acid occurrence at each position to guide enzyme design [93]. Shan et al. discovered that β-carotene hydroxylase CrtZ exhibited poor thermal stability in the biosynthetic pathway of zeaxanthin, forcing fermentation to be conducted at a low temperature of 20 ​°C and severely limiting industrial production efficiency. To address this, they screened 24 potential sites using position specific scoring matrix for thermostability improvement. M83L increased zeaxanthin production by 12-fold relative to that of wild type when combined with optimization of cofactor and fermentation canditions, and its optimal temperature increased from 20 ​°C to 30 ​°C [94]. Coevolution analysis such as Gremlin algorithm can extract information on the coordinated changes of critical residue pairs from MSA, to calculate the coupling probability of different amino acids to provide optional residue pairs for design [95,96]. For example, Li et al. engineered the substrate specificity of the multifunctional plant P450 (CYP87D20) using coevolution analysis and conservation information, enabling it to perform single-specificity hydroxylation at the C11 site of cucurbitadienol for synthesizing mogrol precursors (Fig. 4D) [85].

On the other hand, the catalytic process of enzymes typically involves a conformational cycle of open state (substrate binding) - closed state (catalytic reaction) - open state (product release). Therefore, by studying the coordination of residue movements (such as loop region oscillation and domain rotation) using MD or QM/MM, it is possible to identify the key residues that drive conformational switching. For example, Su et al. identified three key points that determined the product diversity of HCinS (Hypoxylon-derived 1,8-cineole synthase) by MD and QM/MM simulations, which respectively controlled cyclization, hydroxylation/deprotonation, and product conformation. Based on this, they performed rational design and obtained F236M which increased the proportion of (R)-α-terpineol from 4.9 ​% to 55 ​%, N135H which improved the specificity of (R)-limonene to 87.6 ​%, and T111A which increased the proportion of myrcene by more than 20-fold compared with that of wild type (Fig. 4E) [86]. For stability design, the flexible hinge regions in thermosensitive areas can be identified by analyzing the root mean square fluctuation of the main-chain skeleton and the cooperative movement pattern between domains, and then site-directed mutations or interfacial interaction networks can be designed to enhance the overall rigidity. For example, Bai et al. identified the flexible hinge region of CYP71AV1 by analyzing root mean square fluctuation and cooperative movement patterns, and enhanced the rigidity of this region by introducing salt bridges, which increased the thermostability by 9.5 ​°C and the efficiency of catalyzing artemisinic acid by 2.5-fold [97].

The energy-based calculations are also often used to guide rational design with the goal of minimizing the overall protein energy. Mature tools and integrated platforms for energy function-based design such as Rosetta [98] and FoldX [99] are widely used in enzyme design. For example,Li et al. generated a BlYjiC variant (a UGT for salidroside synthesis) which increased its half-life from only 0.8 ​h–107 ​h by energy virtual screening [100]. Ma et al. modified CYP728B70 which is a key enzyme for dehydroabietic acid synthesis based on energy calculations and multiple sequence alignment. F365M improved the production of dehydroabietic acid from 22.41 ​μg/L to 41.55 ​μg/L, as it optimized the spatial position of substrate C18 methyl group of substrate relative to Fe of Heme, promoting the oxidation reaction for dehydroabietic acid synthesis (Fig. 4F) [87].

The strategies of rational design feature high efficiency and experience reusability—for enzymes with clear structure-function relationships, experienced researchers can directly design mutations based on known rules to shorten the optimization cycle. Usually, only a few dozen carefully designed mutants are generated. However, it heavily relies on the designer's experience and a clear catalytic mechanism, making it less accessible to novices.

Semi-rational design for enzyme optimization

Semi-rational design emerged as a solution to the shortcomings of the former two strategies [101,102]. It is based on the framework of rational design and uses known information to anchor potential beneficial mutation regions; with directed evolution as the flesh and blood, random or saturation mutations are introduced within a limited area to avoid the redundancy of random mutations. This not only reduces the size of the screening library but also breaks the dependence of rational design on “complete structural understanding” [[103], [104], [105]]. For example, Zhu et al. employed the semi-rational design strategy to address the issues of low catalytic efficiency and poor regional selectivity of glycosyltransferase UGT (BS) from Bacillus subtilis in the biosynthesis of salidroside. By combining high-precision structures and multiple sequence alignments, they identified I62 as a crucial conserved site regulating enzyme activity, and after performing saturation mutagenesis, the conversion rate of I62N increased to 61.7 ​% from 43.7 ​% of wild type [105]. Zhou et al. identified key residues regulating the enantioselectivity of para-nitrobenzylesterase (pnbA) for L-menthol biosynthesis derived from Bacillus subtilis through structural analysis and molecular docking. By saturation mutagenesis on two residues located at the entrance and triple-code saturation mutagenesis on 10 residues within the binding pocket, they obtained a five-point mutant which increased the kcat/KM by more than 163-fold compared with that of wild type [103].

Semi-rational design, combining the prominent advantages of directed evolution and rational design, constructs a mutant library that is much smaller than that from directed evolution but larger than that from rational design. Nevertheless, semi-rational design significantly narrows the search scope and does not require a complete understanding of the catalytic mechanism of enzymes. It is applicable to enzymes whose catalytic mechanisms have not yet been fully elucidated.

AI-assisted enzyme design

Multidimensional experimental data continuously generated by technologies such as high-throughput screening, proteomics, and molecular dynamics simulations are systematically integrated and structurally stored through standardized data management platforms, gradually building high-dimensional and multimodal enzyme functional databases. These databases provide a solid foundation for data-driven research paradigms. Against this backdrop, the deep involvement of AI provides a series of AI design tools based on structure, sequence, or dynamics analysis (Table 1) [24,25]. Several studies based on AI-driven design have been reported for functional improvements of the key enzymes involved in the biosynthesis of PNPs. For example, Wang et al. developed CataPro, a deep learning-based prediction model for enzyme kinetic parameters. By conducting a virtual screening of the catalytic kinetic parameters of SsCSO (a carotenoid cleavage oxygenase), they increased the efficiency of SsCSO in converting 4-allyl cinnamaldehyde to vanillin by 3.34-fold [41]. Luo et al. studied the structural characteristics of patatin, a major storage protein in potatoes with hydrolase activity using deep learning tools such as AlphaFold-Multimer [106] and ESMFold [107]. By introducing disulfide bonds into patatin, they improved properties such as potato dough viscosity, enhancing its application value in the food industry [108].

AI-driven tools for protein design and evaluation.

De novo enzyme design, differing from the modification of natural enzymes, refers to the design of enzymes that do not exist or have not been discovered in nature and can catalyze a certain reaction when there is no natural enzyme known to catalyze the reaction [109,110]. For example, the team of David Baker, a pioneer in protein design, used RFDiffusion, a protein design framework based on diffusion models, to de novo design serine hydrolases, improving the catalytic efficiency by more than 100 million times [111,112]. Sarel Fleishman's team designed a new enzyme that catalyzes the Kemp elimination reaction by using physical modeling algorithms rather than pure AI, and this enzyme showed a 100-fold increase in catalytic efficiency [113].

However, AI-driven de novo design is still in its exploratory phase for plant enzyme modification, with significantly lower application rate than in industrial or pharmaceutical enzyme systems. This situation stems from the dual constraints of the unique biological properties of plant enzymes and AI technical bottlenecks where mainstream AI-driven models are primarily trained on bacterial or human protein data and show limited ability to capture plant-specific sequence patterns, structural features, and functional requirements [114]. In light of this situation, researchers developed some AI-driven design platforms specifically for plants. Wang et al. developed P450Diffusion, a de novo design method for P450s based on diffusion models and pocket design principles. The designed non-natural P450s produced 1.3–3.5 times more products than that of natural flavone 6-hydroxylase, which converted apigenin to baicalein in the baicalin biosynthesis pathway [115]. To address the low adaptability of plant-derived enzymes in yeast systems, Gelder et al. proposed mining sequence feature differences between plant and yeast enzymes using machine learning, and translating plant enzyme sequences into yeast-adapted sequences via conditional generative adversarial networks [116].

Chimeric protein design

Protein design for mismatched subcellular localization in heterologous expression

When plant proteins are expressed in microbial hosts, it often causes aberrant subcellular localization, thereby resulting in suboptimal or non-functional catalysts. Protein structure and sequence information can guide the correction of the protein localization. For example, Hao et al. significantly enhanced the functional expression of limonene synthase in the perillyl alcohol synthesis in prokaryotic cells using an N-terminal truncation strategy [126].

In addition, accumulation evidence indicates that subcellular compartmentalization benefits the biosynthesis of PNPs by increasing the local concentration of substrates and enzymes, reducing byproduct formation, and minimizing interference with cellular metabolism. One approach to achieve subcellular compartmentalization is assembling multi-enzyme complexes into spatially ordered structures through protein scaffolds [[127], [128], [129]]. For example, the fusion enzyme of geranyl diphosphate synthase and taxadiene synthase enhanced taxadiene production by eliminating the barrier of enzymatic catalytic compartmentalization [130].

Chimeric protein design for new enzymes with superior activity

By integrating functional domains or modules from different enzymes, novel chimeric proteins with composite functions can be obtained, which can circumvent the sequence limitations of natural proteins. Through rational design or high-throughput screening, catalytic domains, binding domains, regulatory elements, etc., are recombined across species or families, enabling proteins with new properties such as multi-substrate recognition, bifunctional catalysis, or environmental responsiveness [131]. For example, Arnesen et al. enhanced the yield of arjunolic acid from 4.4 ​mg/g to 9.1 ​mg/g by replacing the transmembrane domain of CaCYP714E19p with that of homologous enzyme KsCYP72A397p [132]. Aiming to address the localization problem of McP6H for sanguinarine biosynthesis caused by transmembrane domain incompatibility in yeast, Gou et al. constructed a chimeric enzyme by fusing the transmembrane domain of EcP6H (protopine 6-hydroxylase from Eschscholzia californica) with the catalytic domain of McP6Hs and increased the conversion rate of protopine to sanguinarine from 35 ​% to 85 ​% [133].

How to address the stability and folding issues that may exist in chimeric enzymes?

While chimeric proteins are designed to improve activity and stability, they often exhibit folding abnormalities or stability defects. Current studies mostly combine domains through structural analysis, or link target proteins by relying on 3–5 natural protein scaffolds with known excellent foldability and stability [127,128]; such strategies can be directly verified experimentally due to the limited number of protein combinations involved. However, when large numbers of domain combinations result in numerous chimeric proteins, or when empirically designed chimeric proteins fail to meet functional expectations, computational protein design tools enable efficient prediction and optimization for chimeric enzymes.

Uncontrolled linker conformation is the primary cause of folding abnormalities in chimeric proteins, and exposure of hydrophobic residues at interfaces, electrostatic repulsion, or hydrogen bond deficiency may easily induce aggregation of folding intermediates [134]. Using tools such as Rosetta Linker Design [135,136] and Protein MPNN [117], researchers can predict the appropriate linker length and amino acid composition can be predicted based on the 3D structure of domains, guiding the formation of conflict-free inter-domain conformations while maintaining domain independence. By Combining molecular dynamics simulations such as GROMACS [137] and AMBER [138] to evaluate the dynamic changes of linkers in physiological environments, excluding sequences that exhibit “conformational flipping” or “inter-domain entanglement” can further improve the reliability of folding. Using molecular docking tools such as AlphaFill [139] and DiffDock [34] combined with energy optimization algorithms such as Pythia DDG [140] and FoldX [141], inter-domain binding interfaces can be analyzed and residue mutations designed, enhancing specific interactions such as inter-domain hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions, thus promoting the spontaneous formation of correctly folded conformations in chimeric proteins.

Perspective

The AI-assisted enzyme design is experiencing explosive growth, and its deep integration with computational biology and synthetic biology will provide a promising alternative for the efficient biosynthesis of PNPs in microbial hosts.

Despite the bottlenecks in computational design, AI tools will unleash the enormous potential for enzyme engineering in PNPs biosynthesis by overcoming core challenges and integrating diverse strategies. Firstly, building a plant-specific AI ecosystem has become a core direction to break through bottlenecks for industrial applications. Such an ecosystem, including plant-specific sequence databases, host adaptability prediction modules, and pathway-level validation tools, can specifically address the “adaptation” issues of plant enzymes in heterologous expression systems. Next, a systematic combination of genomics, transcriptomics, proteomics, and metabolomics data could construct an intracellular dynamic network of protein-metabolite interactions, and effectively identify key enzyme nodes and metabolic bottlenecks in biosynthesis pathways of interest. Finally, global optimization algorithms for metabolic networks can be introduced to simulate the compatibility between designed enzymes and endogenous metabolic pathways, and to develop “metabolically friendly” enzymes, breaking the traditional perception that high-efficiency catalysis necessarily comes with metabolic stress.

With the plant-specific optimization of AI algorithms and the iteration of experimental techniques, protein design will gradually upgrade to 'intelligent programming of multi-enzyme systems' from 'single-enzyme function optimization'. Meanwhile, the popularization of open-source platforms and automated experimental pipelines will significantly bring down the technical barriers, driving the synthesis of PNPs from laboratories to industry. Ultimately, this will realize the green manufacturing vision of 'AI design, cellular synthesis'.
