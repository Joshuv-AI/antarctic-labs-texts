Asthma management in pregnancy and lactation: A review of current evidence and best practice recommendations
Authors: Margaret Gleeson, Claire McCarthy, Fergal O’ Shaughnessy, Joanna Carroll, Deirdre Long, Lauren Madden Doyle, Dorothy M Ryan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis review will consider current evidence regarding asthma medications during pregnancy and lactation, combined with advice regarding the importance of diagnostic certainty and best practice asthma management. Data for this review were sourced from a comprehensive search of electronic databases including PubMed, Embase, and Cochrane Library. Additional sources such as clinical practice guidelines, systematic reviews, and relevant reference lists were also consulted. Studies selected for inclusion in this review encompassed observational, case-control, and cohort studies, as well as meta-analyses and reviews published in peer-reviewed journals. Discontinuation of asthma medications during pregnancy is common and compromises asthma control, increasing the risk of adverse maternal and fetal outcomes. Breastfeeding may be unnecessarily avoided when restarting medications post-partum. There is a lack of high-quality research on asthma and allergy medications during pregnancy and lactation. Expert consensus suggests that the benefit of adequate asthma control in pregnancy outweighs the potential risks of commonly used asthma medications. Confirming the diagnosis, ensuring close monitoring and collaborating with specialists is key to safe management of asthma in pregnancy.Subject terms: Diseases, Health care, Medical research

Asthma and pregnancy: Why does it matter?

Asthma is an inflammatory disease of the airways, characterised by variable airflow obstruction presenting clinically as cough, wheeze and dyspnoea1. With a prevalence of 4–12% in women of childbearing age, it is the most common chronic respiratory condition encountered in pregnancy2. The majority of asthma is managed in primary care.

Pregnancy impact on asthma

Physiological and immunological changes during pregnancy can influence asthma control3. These impacts can vary per trimester with asthma control often a dynamic process throughout pregnancy4,5. Hormonal changes have been shown to affect asthma prevalence and severity, with fluctuations around puberty and across the menstrual cycle and pregnancy. Both estrogen and progesterone influence mucosal oedema, mucus production and smooth muscle tone and airway hyperresponsiveness6. Additionally, these sex hormones have functioning receptors in respiratory centres that alter respiratory dynamics7. The immunology of pregnancy is complex with studies supporting and refuting pregnancy as a T-helper type 2 (Th2) high inflammatory state8. As with other physiological processes during pregnancy, Th2 inflammation is likely a dynamic process throughout the pregnancy9. Changes in maternal immunity include upregulation of Th2 inflammatory pathways and physiological cell mediated immunosuppression, both of which have the potential to increase exacerbation rate during pregnancy10. Pregnancy can induce cardiopulmonary changes, including increased oxygen demand and changes in respiratory mechanics and ventilation. Displacement of the diaphragm and an increase in intra-abdominal pressures contribute to a reduction in lung volumes in late pregnancy, while tidal volume and minute ventilation increase from early pregnancy7.

The clinical course of asthma in pregnancy is difficult to predict and can vary significantly between patients11. Asthma severity is the strongest predictor of control in the antenatal period with those having moderate to severe asthma much more likely to deteriorate compared to those with mild disease12. Differentiating severe and uncontrolled asthma can be challenging but definitions remain the same in pregnancy. Severe asthma is defined as “asthma which requires treatment with high dose inhaled corticosteroids plus a second controller (and/or systemic corticosteroids) to prevent it from becoming ‘uncontrolled’ or which remains ‘uncontrolled’ despite this therapy13. ” Uncontrolled asthma is characterised by any one of four criteria of poor symptom control, recurrent exacerbations, hospitalisation for asthma or persistent airflow limitation. Evidence of any one of these four criteria while on current high-dose therapy identifies the patient as having “severe asthma”. Increased maternal age, higher parity, obesity and smoking may be associated with an increased risk of asthma exacerbations in pregnancy14. For most, asthma control typically improves in the third trimester reverting to the pre-pregnancy baseline during the postpartum period15,16.

Asthma on pregnancy

For the majority of women, especially if their asthma is well controlled throughout pregnancy, there is little or no increased risk of adverse maternal or fetal complications and they should have a normal pregnancy. However, compelling evidence suggests that individuals with poorly controlled disease or repeated asthma exacerbations in the antenatal period are at risk of significant maternal-fetal complications (Fig. 1)17–20.

Women with asthma may have an increased time to pregnancy and more frequently need assisted conception compared to those without asthma21. While this is poorly understood, age, asthma phenotype and the absence of disease modifying treatment have been suggested as potential factors22,23. Risk for pregnancy loss may be higher in those with uncontrolled asthma as compared to the non-asthmatic population24. Despite this, most women will go on to have a healthy pregnancy and the primary goal is to optimise disease control prior to conception. Pregnant women with uncontrolled asthma may have an increased risk of other pregnancy related complications including gestational diabetes and hypertension of pregnancy25,26. Given the lack of RCT data, these increased risks may be due to the associated increased systemic steroid exposure rather than poor asthma control alone. As in the non-pregnant population, those exposed to oral corticosteroids (OCS) are at risk of long-term side effects. There is increased risk of adrenal insufficiency with prolonged systemic or high dose inhaled corticosteroid (ICS) exposure, particularly during times of stress such as peripartum27. Pregnant women with asthma have an increased risk of caesarean section, placental complications and post-partum haemorrhage28.

In all chronic respiratory conditions, maternal hypoxia is the main risk to fetal viability, development and maturity29,30. Maternal safety concerns impacts medication adherence and this is linked to poor asthma control, increased acute exacerbations and poor perinatal outcomes31–33. Neonatal risks from poor asthma control include growth restriction, low birth weight, pre-term delivery and perinatal mortality34. Congenital malformations are significantly increased in the pregnant population but with the potential for many confounders including disease severity and medication exposure35. Interestingly maternal asthma, more than paternal, predisposes the fetus to atopy and asthma later in childhood36.

Planning ahead: How should asthma be managed before and early in pregnancy?

Patients should ideally have an asthma diagnosis confirmed before conception. When diagnosing asthma, distinguishing between other conditions that can mimic its symptoms is essential, with knowledge of physiological changes of pregnancy important when interpreting symptoms and diagnostic tests7. Dyspnoea affects up to 70% of pregnancies and is usually physiological, but new or progressive breathlessness warrants careful assessment to exclude cardiopulmonary or pregnancy-related disease37. Chronic cough can be one particular diagnostic challenge (Table 1). Diagnostic uncertainty should prompt exclusion of differentials including anaemia, pulmonary embolism and cardiac disease38. A confirmed diagnosis of asthma will support physicians in emphasizing the advantages of appropriate treatment and possible consequences of poor adherence during pregnancy, facilitating informed and shared decision-making.

Diagnosis involves thorough clinical assessment including a detailed medical history and physical examination. Clinical characteristics such as diurnal variation, triggers and treatment response can supplement clinical suspicion. An improvement in symptoms after inhaled bronchodilators and/or corticosteroids, is supportive though not diagnostic of asthma, whilst a lack of response may suggest poor inhaler technique/adherence or an alternate diagnosis. There are some unique considerations when it comes to diagnostic testing in pregnancy given the changes in respiratory mechanics as a result of the enlarging uterus. Spirometry and peak expiratory flow rate (PEFR) are safe to perform and are unchanged by pregnancy physiology39. As in non-pregnant individuals, improvement in forced expiratory volume in first second (FEV1) > 12% and >200mls and >20% diurnal variation in PEFR meet asthma diagnostic criteria1.

Bronchial provocation, skin prick or patch testing are typically avoided during pregnancy due to the potential risk of provoking a severe reaction40. Impulse oscillometry to assess for increased small airways resistance is safe and could aid diagnosis in those with suboptimal spirometry41.

Asthma as a heterogenous condition can vary over time and between patients in terms of presentation, biomarker profile, disease trajectory and treatment response42. Phenotyping facilitates the stratification of patients into clinically meaningful groups with atopic and non-atopic eosinophilic the most common, however significant overlap is seen in clinical practice43. Asthma which is atopic and/or eosinophilic is termed Th2 high and is typically steroid responsive. The prevalence of Th2 high verses Th2 low asthma is debated, with Th2 inflammation often intermittent and more likely to be detected during exacerbations44. A biomarker-led study involving ICS withdrawal revealed that Th2 low phenotype in severe asthma may be as low as 5%45. In contrast, the World Asthma Phenotype study identified 20–50% of asthma as T2 low46.

Evaluation of asthmatic inflammation is central to asthma diagnosis and monitoring symptom control47. Fractional of exhaled nitric oxide (FeNO) is released from airway epithelial cells and driven by eosinophilic inflammation, best correlating with interleukin 13 (IL-13) cytokine levels and is a useful clinical biomarker for asthma including during pregnancy. The 2024 BTS, NICE and SIGN guideline on asthma proposes a diagnostic algorithm whereby FeNO or blood eosinophils combined with asthma symptoms be used to confirm asthma diagnosis in those aged 16 years and over48. In the absence of high blood or FeNO biomarkers further physiological testing is recommended. FeNO < 25 is considered low or suppressed. FeNO 25–50 is considered intermediate and clinical correlation is required. A FeNO >50 ppb is strongly indicative of airway eosinophilia and likelihood to respond to corticosteroid treatment49. A randomised controlled trial (RCT) looking at the addition of FeNO measurements to a symptoms-based treatment algorithm in pregnant patients demonstrated a reduction in asthma exacerbations50. While elevated FeNO is supportive of asthma diagnosis, raised levels can also be driven by eosinophilic nasal inflammation, highlighting the importance of clinical context when interpreting results. Furthermore, Th2 inflammation may be masked by inhaled and/or oral steroid therapy51. Circulating Th2 cytokines, serum eosinophils and total IgE levels in a healthy pregnancy are reportedly similar to their non-pregnant counterparts52. Indeed, eosinophil counts can be physiologically low in pregnancy, lowest immediately post-partum53. Blood eosinophil and FeNO measurements can also have predictive and prognostic value, with higher levels associated with increased asthma symptom burden, severity of disease and risk of exacerbation9.

There is a paucity of data on maternal Th2 inflammation during lactation however maternal asthma control seems to be unaffected by breastfeeding54. There are many benefits to breastfeeding, including a reduced risk or delayed onset of childhood asthma55,56.

Ongoing Care: How and where should asthma be managed?

The goals of asthma management in pregnancy are the same as in non-pregnant patients: to control symptoms, preserve lung function, prevent and treat exacerbations and minimise medication side-effects57. Based on the convincing evidence to date, several international organisations conclude that the risks of untreated asthma far outweigh any theoretical risk associated with the use of asthma medications during pregnancy58,59.

All available evidence recommends active management of asthma during pregnancy with a personalised treatment plan. Pregnant patients should be assessed early and monitored regularly during the antenatal period1 (https://ginasthma.org/2025-gina-strategy-report/). Recent Dutch guidelines outline an algorithm for managing asthma during pregnancy across midwifery, primary and secondary care35. Additionally, these guidelines recognise the dynamic nature of asthma during pregnancy, advising patients with increased risk of complications be reevaluated at least once in the second trimester of pregnancy.

Psychological stress is an under recognised factor in asthma control, with active allergic disease associated with increased perceived maternal stress, poor symptom control and treatment non adherence60. Awareness and management of stress should be incorporated into asthma care during pregnancy. Patient education remains a cornerstone of asthma management, should be initiated early and reinforced at each patient contact. Poor inhaler technique is seldom reported as a specific factor for pregnancy outcomes but it has been repeatedly linked to difficult to treat asthma including risk of exacerbation and hospitalisation. Peak flow remains a valuable tool for detecting changes in asthma control as well as differentiating it from physiological or non-asthma breathlessness during pregnancy. Education by specialist nurses can reduce night time symptom burden and use of reliever medication in those with severe asthma in pregnancy61. A personalised asthma action plan should be provided with instructions on the day to day and exacerbation management for all patients including information on when to seek medical attention1. Avoidance of triggers is a key factor in preventing loss of control. Symptoms of rhinitis can be hormonally driven in early pregnancy and may not respond to conventional treatments, however. Regular review also provides an opportunity to promote smoking cessation, vaccination and weight management during pregnancy to minimise the risk of acute exacerbations. Co-morbidities such as gastro-oesophageal reflux disease and allergic rhinitis (AR) can mimic, co-exist and exacerbate underlying asthma and should be addressed. Self-management has been highlighted as an important tool for asthma control throughout pregnancy1,62.

Certain patient cohorts are at increased risk for deterioration during pregnancy and should be followed closely by a multi-disciplinary team including an obstetrician and respiratory physician1,63. All patients with a deterioration in asthma control during pregnancy should be considered for specialist review35. High risk groups include those with a history of severe asthma and frequent exacerbations, specifically recent OCS use, hospitalisation or previous ICU admission, those with co-morbid conditions including nasal polyposis, diabetes and obesity as well as those with adverse psychosocial factors such as poor adherence, smoking and deprivation (Table 2). Those on high dose ICS or frequent or maintenance OCS should be considered for regular fetal growth scans in later pregnancy64.

Key considerations: What is the evidence for prescribing in pregnancy?

The most common cause of deterioration in asthma control is due to poor medication adherence66,67. Expectant mothers may perceive increased risk to the fetus from asthma medications, corticosteroids and perhaps less so with bronchodilators68. Almost 50% of women may reduce or discontinue their medications early in pregnancy without specialist input, putting them at risk of complications69. One review of prescriptions dispensed in the first trimester compared to the preconception period saw a reduction of 13%, 23% and 54% of short-acting beta-agonists (SABA), ICS and OCS respectively66.

Pregnant individuals are typically excluded from RCTs due to perceived ethical and practical concerns. As a result, there is a dearth of robust data on the safety and efficacy of many medicines in pregnant and breastfeeding women. Instead, evidence is extrapolated from available data including animal studies, case reports and case series, observational studies, asthma registries and post-marketing surveillance which themselves are associated with methodological challenges. Defining and quantifying exposure to asthma medicines can be difficult. Observations on medication safety in pregnancy are often obtained from patient self-reports or prescription records70. This does not provide information on adherence, inhaler technique or other factors which may independently affect disease control. The severity of the underlying condition can influence observed outcomes, with poorly controlled disease associated with adverse pregnancy outcomes. In asthma this can result in increased use of OCS, medication combinations, overuse of reliever medications, or above standard dosing. Thus, separating whether observed adverse outcomes are due to the medications or the effects of poor disease control can be challenging.

Aside from the question of safety, there are several factors to consider when deciding on asthma therapies during pregnancy and lactation. Firstly, consider the efficacy of the medication in the individual. If established on, and responsive to a treatment regimen before pregnancy, it is reasonable to continue, provided there is no absolute contraindication to its use in pregnancy. In such cases, the risks of poor asthma control outweigh potential medication risks in pregnancy, and treatment should not be routinely reduced or stopped1,71. If a medication is not deemed beneficial then consider discontinuation to minimise exposure where possible with the best time for a trial off therapy before conception.

Secondly, clinical experience is greater with established agents and they may be preferable to newer agents while more data in this population accrues. Thus, when starting new asthma therapies consideration should be given to agents with the most robust safety data (Table 3).

Thirdly, route of administration is important. Inhaled formulations have less systemic absorption than oral agents, though systemic effects are possible with high dose topical use, especially at high doses. Inhaler device selection is important, with some patients best suited to dry powder or metered dose inhaler with a spacer to achieve optimal delivery.

Next, it is important to consider the stage and trimester of pregnancy. Safety profiles of certain medications may, theoretically be more favourable after the first trimester when organogenesis is complete while transplacental transfer of other agents, such as asthma biologics may increase in the third trimester (Table 3)63.

Lastly, factor in breastfeeding plans. Evidence supports that breastfeeding reduces risk or delays onset of atopy and asthma during childhood55. This may be of most relevance to offspring of parents with atopy and/or asthma. Asthma and AR medications are generally considered safe during lactation, and can be continued as normal during breastfeeding59.

Safe prescribing: Which asthma medications can be used in pregnancy?

Bronchodilators

In both non-pregnant and pregnant individuals, SABAs are no longer supported as a first line treatment for asthma, with 2019 GINA guidelines recommending ICS/formoterol or ICS/SABA as required as first line therapy for mild, intermittent asthma1,72,73. Inhaled salbutamol or albuterol are the preferred agents during pregnancy but terbutaline may be continued if already prescribed74. There is no evidence to date that inhaled SABA exposure is associated with fetal malformations or other adverse pregnancy outcome but excessive doses should be avoided11. Reports of an increased risk of cleft lip or palate have been published, but may be confounded by the underlying maternal condition75. Use of higher doses of inhaled salbutamol may lead to systemic effects in the mother and infant, including tachycardia and glucose dysregulation76.

LABAs should always be used in fixed combination with ICS during pregnancy for relief of symptoms when inadequately controlled with ICS and PRN SABA or ICS/LABA can be first-line therapy in cases of persistent symptoms1. To date these have not been shown to be associated with an increased risk of adverse pregnancy outcomes and should be continued in those already established on treatment77. Salmeterol and formoterol are the most widely used, with more evidence in pregnancy for salmeterol. There is limited human experience with newer ultra-LABAs such as olodaterol and vilanterol but were considered low-risk in animal models59.

Short-acting anticholinergic agents are used as an alternative reliever medication to SABAs. Although published evidence is lacking, extensive experience with the use of ipratropium in pregnancy does not raise concerns about its safety in pregnancy and it can be continued if indicated. Long-acting muscarinic antagonists (LAMA) are not considered first-line treatment in asthma, but may be used as add-on therapy, with tiotropium the only agent licenced. While data on the use of tiotropium during human pregnancy are limited, an increased risk of congenital malformations or adverse pregnancy outcomes is not suspected. Evidence of a response to therapy should also be considered when making decisions on LAMA use with some patients seeing no major improvement with its addition. Inhaled bronchodilators are generally considered safe during lactation.

Corticosteroids

As in non-pregnant individuals, ICS are the mainstay of asthma therapy and have been shown to reduce exacerbations, suppress eosinophilic inflammation and improve lung function. Acute exacerbations are a threat to maternal and fetal health specifically hypoxia related complications, and can lead to escalation in asthma therapies with increased fetal drug exposure. Available evidence from almost 40,000 pregnancies does not associate ICS with increased risk of malformations or other adverse pregnancy outcomes77. Beclomethasone, budesonide and fluticasone have a wealth of data to support their use in pregnancy78. Regardless of ICS drug, where possible the lowest dose of ICS should be used to maintain disease control. Clinical biomarkers such as FeNO can aid fine-tuning of ICS dose. With above standard dosing in the first trimester there is some evidence to suggest an increased risk for all congenital malformations but this may reflect systemic absorption at higher doses or be a marker of poorly controlled maternal disease78,79. Absorption of ICS varies and at high doses can result in sufficient absorption to cause adrenal insufficiency, emphasising need for judicious dosing80. ICS are deemed safe for use during lactation.

OCS in asthma are prescribed for acute exacerbations and less frequently as maintenance therapy in severe asthma. Whilst evidence in asthma during pregnancy is limited, OCS have been used in pregnancy for many systemic inflammatory conditions from which relevant data can be interpreted. Most studies have not demonstrated an overall increased risk of congenital malformation following OCS use in the first trimester. While a small number of studies report an increased risk of orofacial clefts, the data are conflicting and inconclusive81. Even if an increased risk does exist, the absolute risk remains low and is likely to be outweighed by protecting the life of the mother and infant by effectively managing the acute exacerbation82. Prematurity, pre-eclampsia and low birth weight have also been reported, but not consistently demonstrated in published literature and a causal association with OCS remains82,83. A potential effect of high-dose OCS or extended courses cannot be ruled out and additional monitoring is recommended. Adverse events can be minimised by limiting the dose and duration of exposure. Gestational diabetes and maternal hypertension are possible complications of prolonged steroid use84. Prednisolone is the drug of choice as the predicted level in fetal blood is only 10% that of the mother, much less than dexamethasone and hydrocortisone85. Women taking regular or repeat courses of OCS may be at risk of hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis suppression. Consideration should be given to the prescription of “stress dose” parenteral steroids at the onset of labour59.

Leukotriene receptor antagonists

Leukotriene receptor antagonists are an alternative controller therapy to inhaled corticosteroids but are not recommended as first-line monotherapy in patients with persistent symptoms86, when used as add on therapy to inhaled corticosteroids, they have similar efficacy to LABA87. Initial studies suggesting adverse outcomes with use later in pregnancy have not been reproduced and may have been confounded by asthma control88. Animal studies have not shown any risk to the fetus and prospective observational studies in humans did not show an increased risk of malformations compared to the background population89,90. Taken together the Asthma and Pregnancy Working Group and British Thoracic Society guidelines suggest that LTRA can be continued if already established on treatment with response demonstrated before pregnancy, with most evidence available for montelukast91. As in the non-pregnant population, montelukast is associated with neuropsychiatric complications and should be monitored closely in vulnerable patients92.

Theophylline

There is considerable data on the use of theophylline in pregnancy and although data are conflicting, adverse outcomes have not been consistently proven in human studies. Use at recommended doses as add-on therapy only, preferably in those with proven clinical benefit pre-conception. Monthly drug levels should be performed during pregnancy as circulating binding proteins are reduced in pregnancy and free drug levels may increase93. Theophylline crosses the placenta and use in the third trimester can be associated with therapeutic serum levels and adverse effects in the fetus94. Transient neonatal withdrawal symptoms can occur following delivery, including irritability, tremor, and tachycardia and vomiting; monitoring for such neonatal adverse effects is advisable. Theophylline has a low concentration in maternal breast milk and is safe in nursing mothers95.

Biologics

The advent of targeted monoclonal antibody therapy licenced for severe asthma has been revolutionary, with currently six licensed therapies available. Current evidence on their use in pregnancy is limited.

All asthma biologics are known to cross the placenta, with the greatest transfer observed later in pregnancy96. Asthma biologics are immunomodulating, but not significantly immunosuppressive and do not preclude the administration of antenatal vaccines97.

Omalizumab, a monoclonal antibody against IgE, is the longest available with the most extensive safety evidence. Limited data from the Xolair Pregnancy Registry (EXPECT) and subsequent case reports do not suggest an increase in adverse outcomes in those exposed during pregnancy98.

Newer monoclonal antibodies used in severe asthma include mepolizumab, reslizumab (anti-IL5), benralizumab (anti-IL5R), dupilumab (anti-IL4/13) and tezepelumab (anti-TSLP). Data on dupilumab in pregnancy primarily relates to its use as a treatment for atopic dermatitis. No adverse pregnancy outcomes are described but the data are too limited to draw firm conclusions99. Data on the use of the remaining agents are extremely limited, however, animal studies have not demonstrated adverse effects on pregnancy outcomes or fetal development100. Further research is needed to establish their safety profile in this cohort. In the context of normal pregnancy immunology, T helper immune responses (Th1 and Th2) are considered to be designed to maintain fetal health and as a result in theory biologic therapy could upset this balance. However, in asthma there is an excessive Th2 immune response and it is unknown whether it is safer to suppress this inflammation in asthma for best maternal and fetal outcomes. Discontinuation of biologic therapy may lead to a deterioration in asthma control and thus benefits may outweigh any potential risks. Recent consensus with international expert opinion support the use of biologic agents throughout conception, pregnancy and breast feeding when clinically indicated, however decisions should be made by a specialist team on a case-by-case basis in consultation with the patient71. The complexity of immunology and physiology in healthy and asthmatic pregnancies highlights the real-world need for RCT studies of biologic and standard asthma therapies in the severe asthma population, so that we can make evidence-based and informed decisions.

Drug properties suggest low penetration into breastmilk given drug particle size, however limited data advises caution in premature neonates and the early neonatal period. Delaying the first post-partum dose to two or more weeks after delivery is one strategy to delay infant exposure101.

Conclusion

Contemporary guidance emphasises that maintaining optimal asthma control during pregnancy is critical, as poorly controlled disease and exacerbations pose greater risk to maternal and fetal health, than that of exposure to standard asthma medications. Management should be patient centred, prioritising accurate diagnosis, proactive surveillance and risk minimisation.

A multidisciplinary approach, incorporating timely specialist input, evidence-based guidelines, and open communication, is essential. This supports shared decision-making to balance the potential risks and benefits of current asthma treatment options.
