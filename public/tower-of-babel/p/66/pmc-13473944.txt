Exploring the feasibility of the my hospital passport app: Helping children identify and share coping preferences in medical procedures
Authors: E.W. Segers, A. van den Hoogen, E.E. Berkelbach van der Sprenkel, L. Schoonhoven, E.M. van de Putte, M. Ketelaar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveChildren frequently experience fear and distress during medical procedures. ‘My Hospital Passport’ app was co-created to help children identify and share their preferred coping strategies. This study explored the feasibility and acceptability of the app in clinical practice.MethodsA qualitative feasibility study was conducted in a tertiary pediatric hospital. Semi-structured interviews were held with children (n = 3), parents (n = 8), and HCPs (n = 10) who had used or supported use of the app. Data were analyzed using reflexive thematic analysis.ResultsAll participants viewed ‘My Hospital Passport’ as a valuable communication tool to enhance predictability, trust, and shared understanding around medical procedures. For children and parents, completing the app encouraged reflection and dialogue about coping preferences, contributing to a sense of control and calm. When HCPs acknowledged these preferences, it fostered partnership and predictability. However, when preferences were overlooked, children and parents reported disappointment. HCPs recognized the app's potential to support communication and continuity of care but noted barriers such as lack of integration with electronic health records and limited time during clinical routines.Conclusion‘My Hospital Passport’ is an acceptable and feasible tool that helps children prepare for medical procedures by articulating and sharing their coping strategies. Its successful use, however, depends on active engagement by HCPs and structural embedding in care routines. Organizational and technological integration are essential to ensure consistent recognition of children's preferences and sustainable implementation in practice.Innovation‘My Hospital Passport’ enables two-way communication on coping strategies, improving procedural support.

Introduction

Children frequently experience fear, pain, and distress during medical procedures in hospital settings [1], [2]. Such distress can have a profound impact on children and their parents. In the short term, it can undermine cooperation through a loss of trust, thereby compromising accurate assessment and effective treatment. Over the long term it is associated with poorer recovery, future engagement with care and healthy development [3], [4]. Addressing this distress is therefore an essential part of safe and effective pediatric care.

A child's response to medical procedures is shaped by a complex interplay of factors, including child-related factors (such as developmental stage, trait anxiety, previous experiences and coping style), professional factors (including communication style, empathy, and consistency), and environmental factors (such as the physical setting, parental presence, and organizational routines [5], [6], [7]. Because these factors interact dynamically, personalized care is needed to achieve adequate pain and anxiety management during medical procedures for the individual child.

Among the most effective non-pharmacological strategies to reduce distress are distraction techniques, moreover, fostering predictability and trust is essential [8], [9], [10]. Distraction, whether offered through play, conversation, or technology, helps children endure difficult moments by shifting their attention away from the painful or threatening stimulus [9], [11]. Predictability fosters a sense of safety and control, but only when healthcare professionals (HCPs) reliably explain, prepare, and keep their promises, creating a trusting relationship that helps children feel safe and supported [10], [12], [13]. This perspective aligns with the transactional model of stress and coping, which emphasizes that coping is a dynamic, goal-directed process that depends on both children's preferences and skills but also on the responses and behaviors of those around them [14], [15], [16]. Children who undergo repeated procedures often develop personal coping strategies that provide distraction, predictability, and a sense of control [17]. Supporting children in identifying and practicing these strategies can further enhance their coping [10], [17], [18].

To facilitate this process, the ‘My Hospital Passport’ app was co-created with children, parents, HCPs and software developers, guided by the Medical Research Council (MRC) framework for complex interventions [10], [19], [20], [21]. Unlike existing preparatory tools that mainly provide procedural information, this app enables children themselves to identify, record, and communicate their preferred coping strategies to HCPs, thereby fostering autonomy, predictability, and individualized support.

This study is part of a broader developmental process in line with the MRC framework. Earlier phases included a scoping review, needs analyses, development and iterative usability testing [10], [19], [20]. The next step is to establish whether the app is feasible in practice. As Skivington et al. emphasize, feasibility testing is a necessary stage before large-scale evaluation and implementation, with acceptability and feasibility as key outcomes [22]. This study therefore evaluates the early-stage feasibility of the app, focusing on acceptability and feasibility as defined by Proctor et al. among children, parents and HCPs [23].

Methods

Study design and setting

The study used a qualitative design to explore experiences and perceptions of feasibility, focusing on acceptability and practical use of the app in clinical care. Conducted at a tertiary pediatric university hospital in the Netherlands, it involved children, parents, and HCPs recruited between October 2024 and April 2025.

Ethical approval and consent

The Research Committee of UMC Utrecht approved the study through a Confirmation Quality Check (reference number 22 U-0456) and determined that it was not subject to the Medical Research Involving Human Subjects Act. The study was conducted in line with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki, 7th revision, October 2013, and complied with the ‘Gedragscode Gezondheidsonderzoek’ and the EU General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR).

If children, parents and HCPs agreed to participate, an assent was obtained from children under twelve and written consent from children from twelve years and older and/or the parents. All children, parents and HCPs gave written informed consent.

All informed consent forms, as well as audio recordings, were stored securely—both in a protected physical archive and on a digitally secured server within the research department.

My hospital passport app

‘My Hospital Passport’ app is a smartphone application available for download on Google Play Store and Apple App Store (see Fig. 1). To access its features, users (children or their parents) must first create an account, after which they are guided through an introductory tutorial before reaching the home screen—the central navigation hub of the app.Fig. 1The ‘My Hospital passport’ app.Fig. 1

The ‘My Hospital passport’ app.

‘My Hospital Passport’ app offers key functionalities to support children during medical procedures. Through the settings menu, users can access age-appropriate information about supportive interventions (e.g., use of virtual reality, distraction tools (toys and bubbles, picture books) and medical procedures. Children can record their personal preferences and coping strategies for upcoming procedures, receive motivational compliments, review their preference history and reflect on their experiences. These features were designed to enhance children's autonomy, predictability, and communication with HCPs, as detailed in a usability study (under review). The app was designed for use across a broad age range and can be used independently or with support from parents or healthcare professionals, depending on the child's age and abilities.

The name ‘My Hospital Passport’ was chosen by the children's council members.

HCPs can view a child's preferences and coping strategies before admission or consultation via a hospital-specific web portal by searching child's name or date of birth. The portal also offers HCPs the possibility to send compliments to patients. Most importantly, the individual information in the app serves as a starting point for conversations about the child's wishes and preferences.

To offer the app to children and parents prior to admission, electronic flyers were posted in the electronic patient portal, printed flyers were distributed, and an animated explainer was shown on the hospital's digital information screens. Items were included in internal news bulletins for HCPs, and each ward organized training sessions for them. A designated HCP ambassador on every ward promoted and supported the app's adoption.

Population and sampling

We aimed for a heterogeneous sample of children who had downloaded the app and filled in their preferences, as well as their parents and HCPs involved in medical procedures. A sample of 10 participants for each group was deemed appropriate for this feasibility study [24].

Children aged 8–18 years were eligible to participate when they were currently admitted to or receiving treatment at the hospital and had prior experience with medical procedures. To ensure familiarity with the intervention, they were required to have downloaded the ‘My Hospital Passport’ app and entered their personal preferences regarding medical interventions. Children with learning disabilities were excluded, and all children needed to be able to understand and speak Dutch.

Parents were eligible when they were the parent or primary caregiver of a child who had undergone medical procedures, had downloaded the app, and had entered their child's preferences. They were likewise required to have sufficient proficiency in Dutch.

HCPs were eligible if they were involved in performing medical procedures with children or in interacting with children and parents who had used the ‘My Hospital Passport’ app, and when they were able to understand and communicate in Dutch.

Data collection and procedure

Children and parents

Between October 2024 and April 2025, eligible children and parents were identified in collaboration with nursing staff. Using purposeful sampling, a diverse group of participants (varying in age, sex, and health conditions) was recruited to ensure a comprehensive understanding of their experiences. The researcher (EWS) coordinated with admission planners and ward nurses to determine suitable candidates.

Eligible participants from both outpatient and inpatient departments were approached for written informed consent. Outpatient families received an information letter before the planned medical procedure to allow time for consideration. Semi-structured interviews were conducted by EWS. Whenever possible, child and parent interviews were conducted separately to ensure independent response; however, some parents spontaneously joined when their child preferred their presence.

The interview guide focused on experiences in downloading the app, completing personal information and coping preferences in the app, and using the app during hospital care, with specific attention to its acceptability and feasibility (e.g., practicality of use, barriers, and integration into care). See Appendix 1 for the full interview guide, which was pilot-tested.

HCPs

To recruit eligible HCPs an invitation was published in newsletters of various hospital wards between November 2024 and January 2025. Participants included HCPs who reached out to the interviewers (EWS with help of BB, a medical student), as well as HCPs who were specifically asked to participate by members of the research team. The final selection of HCPs was criterion based and purposive, to ensure representation of the various medical professions to gather a wide range of perspectives. Included HCPs received an information letter and a consent form. A semi structured interview guide was developed and pilot tested, the interviews explored experiences with supporting children during medical procedures, including their coping rituals and the use of the app, with specific attention to its acceptability and feasibility (e.g., practicality of use, barriers, and integration into care processes). see Appendix 2.

Field notes were taken after each session to capture contextual insights. All interviews were audio recorded and transcribed verbatim.

Analysis

A reflexive thematic analysis approach was used, according to Braun and Clarke's six-phase framework, supported by NVivo 15 software (version 15; QSR International) [25], [26]. The analysis incorporated reflexive thematic examination of interview data to explore children's, parents' and HCP experiences and opinions about the ‘My Hospital passport’ app. The data from children and parents, and from HCPs, were analyzed separately to obtain a comprehensive understanding of feasibility from the two different groups of stakeholders.

The six-phase analytical process began with repeated reading of transcripts to achieve data familiarization and initial code generation. Two researchers independently conducted manual coding to identify emerging themes (EWS, AH), with any coding discrepancies resolved through discussion and third-researcher consultation (MK). Data collection and analysis took place simultaneously. After 11 interviews with parents and children and 10 interviews with HCPs had been conducted and analyzed, the research team judged that the data provided sufficient depth and richness to construct an interpretative narrative that is grounded in the perspectives of the participants. As the research questions could be adequately addressed, no further participant recruitment was needed.

Emerging themes were refined collaboratively within the research team, resulting in an interpretative thematic framework grounded in participant perspectives. Final reporting followed COREQ guidelines to ensure methodological rigor and transparency [27].

Recruitment of children willing to take part in the interviews proved challenging. Barriers included fatigue or illness from ongoing treatment, as well as lack of interest. Notably, in all but two cases, it was the parent—usually the mother—who had taken the initiative to download the app.

One interview was excluded after it became clear that the parent had not actually downloaded the app, believing instead that they were sufficiently capable of supporting their child during medical procedures without additional tools.

HCPs

Ten HCPs were interviewed, representing a range of hospital disciplines involved in ordering or performing medical procedures. Interviews lasted 30–40 min (see Table 1). These HCPs were in positions either to recommend the ‘My Hospital Passport’ app or to consult the child's stated preferences during procedures.

Themes

A central finding was that all participants viewed the app as a tool to enhance communication between children and parents, and between families and HCPs. Moreover, in total six themes emerged from the data, which will be described for children and parents and for HCPs separately.

Children and parents

Participants reported that, when used as intended, the app can increase the predictability of care during medical procedures, leading to greater calm and trust in both children, parents and the healthcare team. Four themes emerged from these data: the app is a starting point for conversation, timing matters, clarity about strategies brings calm, and the app may lead to expectations and disappointments. The themes are further explored below.

A starting point for conversation

Parents especially noted that the app's information and questions prompted reflection and meaningful conversations with their child about coping preferences, discussions that otherwise might not have taken place. Parents and children reported that, without the app, they would likely not have considered coping strategies in preparation for medical procedures.

Parents described the information as clear, sufficient, and helpful for understanding available supportive and distraction-based interventions. Parents appreciated opportunities for children to try options in advance to discover what worked best.

P (Parent)1: “Yes, because in that way [when using the app] I found out that X no longer likes it when, for example, things are explained to her about what is being done. She now felt like, just let them do it and distract me instead. So we arranged it that way, and that also led to a conversation about it. That way, on the day of the surgery, I knew better that this was what she wanted.”

P8: “But that we really talked about what is going to happen, what you need, and things like that. That was nice.”

Timing matters

Participants emphasized the importance of exploring the app well ahead of the medical procedure, allowing time to process the information calmly, talk things through, and make informed choices. Right before a procedure, stress often makes it difficult to absorb new information or consider what suits the child best.

Parents and children also indicated that the app felt less necessary when they already had experience with effective coping strategies for a familiar procedure. In such cases, communicating these needs to HCPs was easier and more natural.

P9: “I think it is important to have it clear beforehand how you would like things to be. And of course things can always change, …..I think it is good to talk that through in advance, especially if you have an older child.”

Clarity brings control

When it was clear which strategies the child would use, both children and parents reported a sense of calm and empowerment. The information clarified what to expect and selecting and recording a coping ritual in advance helped the child feel more in control, knowing what they wanted and that it was documented.

C (Child)1: “That you have it a bit in your own hands, I think.”

P C1: “I think, I saw you at your home, because she was extremely afraid beforehand. That a bit of calm came. A bit of fear was taken away and I thought, hey, oh, you get a choice.”

P9: “Yes, that she feels more understood and that she has more control herself over what happens that day… Yes. Yes. And that everything… Yes, that it often goes the same way. That if it is already decided that it will be handled like that. She really likes that.”

Expectations and disappointment

While children and parents were generally positive about the app itself, its use also evoked certain expectations. By formulating and recording their coping preferences, children and parents anticipated that HCPs would be aware of these wishes and act accordingly. When this did not happen, parent and child reported feeling disappointed—highlighting that the app is not just a stand-alone tool, but part of a broader intervention that requires active engagement from HCPs.

Participants expressed that it would foster a sense of calm and trust if HCPs acknowledged the app and took the child's preferences into account. However, in most of the cases, this recognition was lacking. As a result, parents felt the need to advocate more actively for their child. Parents described tension between advocating for their child and maintaining good relationships with HCPs.

P8: “You want to stand up for your child, but you also don't want to rub professionals the wrong way.”

P9: “You do become, as parents, a bit obliged to be on top of everything. Yes.”

P10: “[to her daughter]But actually you would have found it handy… that they had looked at that and did not ask you for it at the moment when you are caught off guard and the question does not even really arrive.”

HCPs

HCPs likewise recognized the app's potential benefits. Parents and children spoke from direct experience, whereas HCPs mainly reflected on potential benefits from a professional perspective, particularly regarding communication and predictability.

Two themes emerged: ‘the app as a suitable communication tool to enhance predictability’ and ‘not a priority to use’.

Suitable as a communication tool to enhance predictability

HCPs recognized from experience that providing information immediately before a medical procedure is often ineffective. They emphasized that the app should therefore be offered at an earlier stage—ideally at home—so that children and parents can engage with it in a calm setting.

In addition, HCPs noted that the app can promote continuity of care, which is particularly important given frequent staff changes and the involvement of multiple HCPs in a child's treatment. By documenting coping rituals, the app enables any HCP, even those unfamiliar with the child, to tailor their approach more quickly and build trust.

Finally, HCPs valued the app's ability to make coping preferences visible, as this increases predictability in care and helps children and parents feel more at ease. In this way, the app fosters both continuity in approach and a sense of partnership between families and HCPs.

Childlifespec2: “I see it as a tangible tool for children who find it difficult either to take control or to let go of control. It is a helpful way of saying: well, I've written this down, this is my wish, so this is how it should go.”

Nurse1: “And I definitely think it is better to do that in advance, rather than when they are already tense here in the hospital and still have to think about it—because then the answer [of a child] is usually just, “I don't want anything.”

Ane1: “I do think that if such an app is filled in and we can view it beforehand, that might add something to the situation, because on the holding area itself it is actually already a bit late, and you are standing there anyway and can only discuss it then.”

Nurse5: “I think it also gives children a sense of security when things are always done in the same way, and I think they also like it when they feel, hey, you are here, so you know how it goes. But if someone new comes in, they might think, oh dear, what is this person going to do, and is it really going to be the same? Does this person really understand it?”

Not a priority to use

Despite recognizing potential benefits, HCPs identified several barriers. The information recorded in the app is not yet integrated into the hospital's electronic health record, which limits its accessibility during clinical workflows.

Furthermore, asking about a child's coping preferences is not yet embedded in daily practice of most interviewed HCPs. Some nurses noted that parents should be responsible for sharing this information.

Moreover, HCPs reported that their communication typically focuses on explaining the medical procedure itself, rather than exploring coping strategies. While they acknowledged the importance of understanding a child's preferences, there was often little urgency, or in some case insufficient time and space, to actively address them.

Nurse2: “Actually not, but I don't ask for it either. Maybe it should be that way. But it is also never mentioned [by parents of child]. Like, I have a hospital passport, so do you want to do something with that. That never really happens to me either.”

Nurse3: “Yes, because if it's not in the Electronic health record then you don't know that the patient has it. And I'm not going to log in to that website for every patient first and then check: hey, did you fill anything in?”

Nurse5: “What I really miss myself is the part afterwards, checking whether it was actually done [to fill in the app]. I place the control very much with the parent or the child, and then it's uncertain whether it actually happens.”

Pediatrician1: “And then I explain that we would like to do a blood test, and we talk about it with the child and the parents—why we want to take that blood. And then I explain that we can do it right after the consultation straight away.”

Discussion

The findings of this feasibility study indicate that ‘My Hospital Passport’ is a valuable and acceptable communication tool for children, parents, and healthcare professionals of medical procedures in a hospital setting. Children and parents perceived the app as helpful in preparing for procedures, enhancing understanding, and supporting the development of personalized coping strategies. They valued how it fostered a sense of control and calm, and strengthened communication between child and parent before and during hospital visits.

Healthcare professionals acknowledged the potential of the app to support communication and promote children's coping strategies, yet reported challenges in using it consistently, due to limited time, lack of integration within the electronic health record, and insufficient embedding within daily workflows. Consequently, some families felt disappointment when preferences documented in the app were not addressed during consultations, and parents occasionally felt responsible for ensuring that their child's preferences were acknowledged. Overall, while the app was positively received by all user groups, its sustainable implementation requires personal, organizational and technological support to align the expectations of families and healthcare professionals.

The findings of this study confirm that providing children with opportunities for preparation and distraction before medical procedures can help reduce distress. Previous research consistently demonstrates that such approaches, including digital preparatory tools, participatory-designed applications and web based educational approaches, can lower anxiety, enhance understanding, and increase familiarity with the hospital environment by offering child-friendly information about procedures and coping strategies, and are experienced by children and parents as acceptable en feasible to use [19], [28], [29], [30]. ‘My Hospital Passport’ builds on these approaches by integrating children's coping preferences and rituals into a structured communication process that actively involves parents and HCPs.

This study shows that various barriers can hinder the dialogue with children, calling for systemic solutions that embed their voices in everyday care. Healthcare professionals acknowledged the value of ‘My Hospital Passport’ in supporting communication and children's coping during medical procedures. However, its integration into routine pediatric practice proved challenging. These difficulties reflect broader barriers identified in the literature on pediatric shared decision-making and child- and family-centered care [31], [32], [33], [34]. While involving children in decisions about their care is increasingly recognized as a fundamental aspect of high-quality pediatric practice, translating these principles into daily routines remains difficult. Structural barriers such as time constraints and workflow limitations hinder the adequate discussion of coping references, but personal barriers, such as uncertainty about skills, doubts about the value of shared decision-making or procedural comfort, also contribute [20], [32]. Additionally, the prevailing hospital culture often prioritizes medical efficiency over emotional preparation, making it harder to foster meaningful dialogue with children [34]. Overcoming these challenges is essential to ensure that children's voices are consistently heard and integrated into their care.

Using ‘My Hospital Passport’ has the potential to improve the quality of pediatric care. Reducing children's distress not only facilitates smoother procedures but also helps build trust in healthcare professionals and the healthcare system more broadly. Professionals should be more aware of the importance of this trust-building process [8]. Coping is not solely a child-led activity but a shared process requiring active professional engagement, predictability, and keeping promises, which is crucial for sustaining long-term trust in healthcare and future procedures [10], [35], [36]. My ‘Hospital Passport’ offers a practical approach for shared decision-making around coping, yet its effectiveness depends on professionals visibly acknowledging and using children's preferences during clinical encounters. Research on PROMs similarly shows that when clinicians do not adress patient-provided input, patients become less motivated to share such input in the future [37], [38]. Without structural embedding, the app risks remaining a stand-alone tool, raising expectations among families without corresponding changes in practice, which may lead to frustration and loss of trust.

Targeted implementation efforts are therefore essential to ensure sustainable use of the app in daily practice. Persistent barriers, such as the lack of integration with the electronic health record and limited embedding in clinical workflows, require organizational and systemic attention. Applying an implementation framework is crucial to guide this process by identifying barriers and facilitators. The Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR; Damschroder et al.), which includes the domains intervention characteristics, outer setting, inner setting, characteristics of individuals, and process, provides a useful structure for this purpose [39]. The barriers identified in this study appear most likely to relate to the intervention characteristics and inner setting domains, although a more detailed analysis would be required to confirm this.

A key strength of this feasibility study is the triangulation of perspectives from children, parents, and healthcare professionals, which provided rich and nuanced insights into the app's use and perceived value. The use of a qualitative design allowed for an in-depth exploration of experiences across stakeholder groups. Conducting the study within a tertiary university hospital—a complex and high-intensity setting—also strengthens the relevance of findings for generalizability to other pediatric contexts. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. Child participation was limited, with only three out of eleven interviews involving children, leading to underrepresentation of children's voices. Most interviews were initiated by mothers, potentially shaping the parental perspective. As a single-site study, findings may reflect context-specific factors. Moreover, children with learning disabilities were excluded, leaving out an important vulnerable group, and parents who downloaded the app may represent early adopters rather than the wider population. These limitations may have influenced the app's perceived acceptability, underscoring the need for further research in more diverse user groups and settings.

Future research should focus on strategies to better engage children in both development and app use, for instance through developmentally appropriate formats, gamified elements, or peer-based examples. In addition, the potential applicability of ‘My Hospital Passport’ beyond procedural care warrants further exploration, for example in supporting communication of individual needs during hospital admission. Adapting ‘My Hospital Passport’ for vulnerable groups, such as children with disabilities, low health literacy, or diverse language backgrounds, will be important to ensure equitable access. Implementation research is also needed to evaluate how the app can be effectively embedded in electronic health records and routine workflows, using mixed-method designs to assess both impact and process.

Innovation

My Hospital Passport's emphasis on two way communication represents a novel contribution to existing interventions designed to support medical procedures. The app not only provides preparatory information, but also serves as a tool for communication. This enables children and parents to play an active role in their care by recording the child's individual preferences, wishes, and coping strategies in relation to medical procedures. In this way, information that is often known only to the child and their family is made accessible to healthcare professionals in a structured and consistent way.

By giving children and parents a voice and offering them the opportunity to contribute their own knowledge and experience, the app supports family-centered care and encourages greater involvement of children and their families in decision-making regarding procedural support. This may improve more personalized and responsive care across HCPs and healthcare settings.

Conclusion

‘My Hospital Passport’ shows promise as a tool to strengthen trust, predictability, and communication in pediatric care. Its effectiveness, however, depends on healthcare professionals actively acknowledging and using children's preferences, and on the system-level integration of the tool into everyday practice. Without such integration, there is a risk of mismatch, heightened expectations among families without corresponding professional support, which could ultimately undermine the trust the app seeks to foster.
