Advanced metric for evaluating the performance of photovoltaic module enhancement techniques using a lifespan, cost and power-based approach
Authors: Sakhr M Sultan, C P Tso, M Z Abdullah, K Sopian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study introduces and applies the (lifespan, power and cost effectiveness factor) metric as a novel framework for evaluating photovoltaic (PV) enhancement technologies, particularly cooling and reflective systems. The metric represents the normalized ratio between the total lifespan-weighted power improvements contributed jointly by the PV module and its enhancer—after accounting for their combined cost—and the baseline maximum power capability of the standalone PV module normalized by its individual cost. integrates key parameters including enhancer-induced power gain, operational lifespan, and cost alongside the performance characteristics of the PV module. A comprehensive sensitivity analysis was performed to examine how variations in these parameters influence the long-term effectiveness of different enhancement techniques. The results show that although higher power gains generally increase , this improvement is strongly moderated by the enhancer’s lifespan compatibility with the PV module and its associated cost. Enhancers with short service life or high manufacturing cost exhibited reduced long-term effectiveness, even when delivering substantial power improvements. In contrast, low-cost enhancers with moderate power contributions achieved high cost-effectiveness when their operational lifespan matched or approached that of the PV module. The analysis also demonstrates that increases in the PV module’s rated power (PPV, max) decrease due to an enlarged denominator, while reductions in either PV or enhancer cost improve overall performance. To illustrate real-world applicability, a comparative case study of single and double reflectors was conducted. The single reflector achieved a higher value (32.08%) than the double reflector (28.95%), indicating superior cost- and lifespan-efficiency. These findings confirm that the metric offers a robust, transparent, and flexible framework for comparing PV enhancement technologies and can support researchers, manufacturers, and policymakers in optimizing performance and return on investment.Keywords: Photovoltaic module, Cooler, Reflector, Lifespan effectiveness, Power, cost, Assessment, PerformanceSubject terms: Energy science and technology, Renewable energy, Solar energy, Solar thermal energy

Introduction

Solar energy is a clean and sustainable source of power, offering the significant environmental advantage of generating no carbon dioxide emissions. Its adoption has the potential to contribute meaningfully to the mitigation of global environmental challenges. Despite the relatively low conversion efficiency of solar technologies, ongoing research and technological progress present opportunities for improvement. A prominent application of solar energy is through photovoltaic (PV) modules, which directly convert solar irradiance into electricity1. Over the past decade, global PV energy production has experienced substantial growth. According to the International Energy Agency (IEA)2, global PV output rose from 32.20 TWh in 2010 to 1002.9 TWh in 2021—representing nearly a thirtyfold increase. This significant expansion has driven growing interest in further improving PV performance and system efficiency.

One promising strategy to enhance PV efficiency involves the integration of system enhancers such as coolers and reflectors, tailored to local climatic conditions. In high-temperature and high-irradiance environments, cooling devices are particularly effective in reducing module operating temperature, thereby increasing electrical output3–11. These coolers may operate via natural or forced convection mechanisms. To support the development of predictive models for such systems, Mohammad et al.12 applied artificial neural networks (ANNs) to a water-based photovoltaic thermal collector (PVT) system. The model used solar irradiance, inlet water temperature, and mass flow rate as input parameters, and its predictions were validated against experimental results. This ANN-based approach proved advantageous in terms of cost and computational efficiency.

A recent study evaluated a patented hybrid photovoltaic–thermal collector that integrates mini-concentrating thermal elements with a reverse flat-plate design13. Field testing (Qatar and Germany) combined with SAM simulations showed very high combined efficiencies (> 90.00%), delivering thermal outputs up to 170.0 °C, far above the limits of conventional PVT systems. The system demonstrated a strong heat-to-power ratio (6:1) and suitability for industrial heat processes, with financial analysis indicating that large-scale deployment (120.0 MW) coupled with an Organic Rankine Cycle can achieve a LCOE of $44.00/MWh, significantly lower than PV + CSP or PV + battery configurations. It also reduces CAPEX (> 50.00%) and land requirements (40.00–60.00%), offering advantages for desalination, data centers, district cooling, and process heat. However, the concept relies heavily on thermal concentration components and high operating temperatures, which may introduce durability, soiling, and long-term performance uncertainties under harsh climates. Despite these considerations, the system represents a promising next-generation hybrid solar platform aligned with net-zero and high-temperature industrial energy demands.

Another study investigates, for the first time, the feasibility of using a combined photovoltaic/thermal–solar thermal (PVT-ST) system in both hot and moderate climates14. The proposed configuration connects a PVT module in series with a solar thermal (ST) collector, enabling simultaneous production of electricity and high-temperature thermal energy. Experimental results indicate that increasing the working fluid’s mass flow rate significantly enhances thermal output. Under July operating conditions with a mass flow rate of 0.030 kg/s, the thermal efficiencies of the ST, PVT-ST, and standalone PVT systems were 86.30%, 79.10%, and 74.20%, respectively, demonstrating the performance advantage of the hybrid arrangement over conventional PVT designs.

The evaluation of the effectiveness of passive photovoltaic cooling was performed using soy-wax and paraffin-based phase change materials (PCMs) in tropical climates where elevated module temperatures limit PV performance. PCMs were attached to the rear surface of the PV module to absorb excess heat during peak solar hours15. Experiments showed that PCM integration reduced module temperature by up to 10.00 °C and improved voltage stability by 8.000%, resulting in higher power output and potentially longer module lifespan. An economic assessment further demonstrated that PCM-cooled panels achieved a lower LCOE (0.068 $/kWh) compared to uncooled panels (0.076 $/kWh). Overall, the study highlights both the technical and economic suitability of PCM-based passive cooling for PV installations in tropical regions such as Indonesia.

Subsequent innovations in PVT design aimed to overcome common limitations, such as low thermal output. Tao et al.16 introduced a new structure, while Oussama et al.17 reported electrical efficiency gains of 2.000% in winter and 5.100% in summer using an air-type PVT. A single-pass water-based PVT was investigated by Juwel et al.18, achieving thermal and electrical efficiencies of 56.19% and 13.75%, respectively, under variable solar irradiance and flow conditions. Karim and Amori19 developed an analytical model for thermal efficiency optimization in air-based PVTs, reaching 18.04% efficiency, while Chao et al.20 validated simulation results against experimental data. Other studies revealed that incorporating a phase change material (PCM) tank behind PV modules could further enhance the electrical output8. Furthermore, Ali et al.21 examined the use of nanofluids (NFs), finding that SiO2 nanofluids provided more effective cooling than water, resulting in a 12.70% increase in electrical efficiency and a 5.760% gain in thermal performance under 1000 W/m2 irradiance.

In contrast, for colder regions with low solar intensity, reflectors are often favored. The concept of using reflectors to intensify solar irradiance dates back to 1958 demonstrated a 31.20% power gain through outdoor experiments using PV modules integrated with V-trough concentrators22–27. Anand et al.23 showed that optimizing reflector geometry could yield power improvements up to 60.00%. Palaskar and Deshmukh24 achieved a 15.00% output increase using aluminum sheet reflectors. Likewise, Naseer et al.28 recorded a 48.00% power gain from a V-trough PV system in field tests. Elbreki et al.29 explored the combined use of coolers and reflectors, reporting a PV efficiency of 10.68% and a reduced system payback period of 4.800 years. Further research highlighted the influence of reflector tilt angles on PV performance demonstrated a 61.00% improvement in spatial solar capture using a curved reflector30,31. Amanlou et al.32 achieved 36.00% PV efficiency using a flat plate reflector combined with cooling, while Monika et al.33 proposed a three-dimensional PV system with a stainless-steel structure that reached 34.16% electrical efficiency.

In practical applications, the lifespan, power and cost effectiveness factor for PV enhancers, , is expected to fall within the range of 0 to 1, representing a continuum from minimal to maximum performance. In equation form,

To ensure reliable and consistent evaluation when using the lifespan, power and cost effectiveness factor, several criteria must be met. Firstly, all performance assessments should be carried out under identical operating conditions—including solar irradiance, ambient temperature, airflow, and tilt angle—to establish a standardized basis for comparison across different PV enhancers. Secondly, the same type of PV must be used in both the reference (without an enhancer) and the improved (with an enhancer) systems to avoid discrepancies due to varying PV characteristics. Thirdly, the surface area of the PV coolers must remain the same, as differences in area can independently influence heat dissipation and electrical performance, thus affecting the accuracy of the method. However, two notable limitations of this approach are its dependence on environmental factors and PV and enhancer aging. Firstly, since the durability of PV cooling materials can be significantly impacted by extreme temperatures, humidity levels, and UV radiation, the resulting effectiveness factor may differ substantially between geographic locations. Secondly, PV and enhancer aging introduces gradual performance drift—through soiling, material degradation, or corrosion—that may deviate from the assumed degradation rates embedded in the calculation. Therefore, while the method serves as a robust comparative tool, its results should be interpreted carefully when applied across varied climate conditions.

Discussion of the results

The analysis of the reveals several overarching trends that cut across individual cooling configurations. Systems that deliver stable, long-duration cooling—and whose operational lifespan is reasonably aligned with that of the PV module—consistently achieve higher values. In contrast, cooling techniques that provide sharp initial gains but suffer from rapid performance degradation, frequent maintenance, or short component life exhibit noticeably reduced long-term effectiveness. These patterns underline an important practical insight: in real installations, durability outweighs peak cooling strength. Even moderate cooling systems become more advantageous over a 20-year PV lifespan if their performance remains steady and their replacement cycle is minimal. From a design and investment perspective, thus serves as a decision-making lens, guiding practitioners toward cooling technologies that sustain power enhancement relative to their lifetime cost, especially in high-irradiance climates where temperature-induced losses are persistent.

Similarities and difference between , LCOE, and ROI

The three indicators—, LCOE, and ROI—share the common goal of evaluating the techno-economic performance of energy systems, yet they differ in scope, formulation, and the dimensions they represent.

Similarities

All three indicators aim to link technical efficiency with economic feasibility, providing a basis for decision-making in renewable energy system design. Each captures the balance between cost, output, and lifespan — LCOE quantifies cost per energy unit, ROI measures profitability, and integrates energy performance with cost and lifespan, bridging the two perspectives. In essence, they are complementary: a high generally implies a low LCOE and a high ROI.

Differences

While LCOE and ROI are financial metrics, reflecting cost efficiency and profitability respectively, is a hybrid techno-economic indicator that also incorporates engineering parameters such as cooling efficiency, module degradation, and effective power yield. LCOE depends heavily on capital and operational expenditures discounted over time, ROI focuses on revenue relative to investment, and adds a technical dimension—considering how design improvements, lifespan extension, and cooling strategies influence both energy and cost effectiveness. Therefore, complements conventional economic indicators by embedding performance physics into economic assessment, making it particularly valuable for evaluating photovoltaic cooling technologies where energy enhancement and durability directly affect financial outcomes.

Conclusion

The has proven to be a robust metric for evaluating the performance of PV enhancers—particularly coolers and reflectors—by incorporating critical parameters such as power gain, lifespan alignment with the PV module, and associated manufacturing costs. Through a comprehensive sensitivity analysis, it has become evident that the effectiveness of an enhancer is not solely determined by its power contribution or lifespan in isolation, but by the synergistic balance among all influencing factors. Notably, increasing the net power gain (PE) consistently leads to a higher , affirming the proportional relationship between energy enhancement and overall system value. However, even with substantial power gains, the effectiveness can be diminished if the enhancer lifespan (LE) is short or if its cost (ZE) is disproportionately high. Conversely, coolers with modest power improvements can achieve competitive values when their costs are low and their lifespans are well-aligned with the PV module. Adjustments to the PV module’s rated output at STC (PPV, max) reveal an inverse relationship: higher PPV, max values reduce by elevating the denominator, thereby lowering the relative benefit of the enhancer. Similarly, variations in PV and enhancer cost demonstrate that is highly cost-sensitive—an increase in either ZPV or ZE reduces overall effectiveness, whereas cost reductions significantly improve it. Ultimately, the metric highlights the importance of designing PV enhancers that achieve an optimal trade-off among power gain, durability, and economic viability. To use this metric, designers need to calculate the lifetime power gain achieved through cooling, weighting it by the effective joint lifespan of the PV module and its enhancer, and then normalizing this benefit against the total system cost. This allows them to quickly identify which cooling designs deliver true long-term value and which ones lose effectiveness once lifespan mismatches and economic factors are considered. This integrated perspective is essential for making informed decisions in PV system enhancement—particularly in climate-specific contexts where economic and environmental constraints demand tailored solutions. Future design strategies should prioritize not only thermal and electrical performance but also cost-efficiency and lifespan compatibility to maximize long-term benefits and accelerate the adoption of PV cooling technologies. Based on the comparative assessment using , several actionable recommendations emerge for both practitioners and researchers. For real-world deployment, PV cooling technologies should prioritize long-term reliability, minimal maintenance cycles, and lifespan compatibility with the PV module, as these characteristics consistently lead to higher values. System designers are encouraged to adopt durable cooling strategies, apply anti-degradation treatments, and integrate predictive maintenance tools to preserve cooling performance over time. From a research perspective, future work should focus on developing standardized long-term datasets, refining degradation models that incorporate material aging, and exploring adaptive cooling mechanisms capable of responding to dynamic climatic conditions. There is also a clear need for techno-economic optimization frameworks that couple with new metrics capturing embodied energy, and recyclability. Advancing these directions will strengthen the practical relevance of and support the development of PV cooling systems that remain both cost-effective and performance-resilient throughout their operational lifespan.
