Impact of tension-free autograft repair on early sensory recovery in polyethylene glycol (PEG)-mediated axonal fusion following traumatic peripheral nerve injuries
Authors: Barite Gutama, Ronald M Cornely, Ricardo A Torres-Guzman, Sriya Nemani, Franklin Gergoudis, Benjamin Savitz, Anthony Hoang, Huseyin Karagoz, Douglas R Weikert, Yusha Katie Liu, Lesley Wong, Feng Zhang, Panambur L Bhandari, William Lineaweaver, Brian C Drolet, Shady Elmaraghi, J Bradford Hill, Wesley P Thayer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAutograft repairs remain inferior to primary neurorrhaphy, largely due to axonal regeneration across two coaptation sites. PEG–mediated neurorrhaphy has been shown to enhance recovery following peripheral nerve injury by mitigating Wallerian degeneration. Whether PEG can overcome these limitations in human autografts remains unclear. We therefore compared sensory recovery following PEG-mediated primary versus PEG-mediated autograft neurorrhaphy. In this study, prospectively collected clinical data were analyzed for patients undergoing PEG-mediated nerve repair with follow-up to 100 days. Sensory recovery was assessed using two MRCC grading groups: < 3 (no recovery) and ≥ 3 (recovery). Differences across postoperative timepoints between the two groups were analyzed using the Kruskal–Wallis test with Dunn’s post hoc testing and Bonferroni correction. A total of 48 PEG-mediated nerve repairs (42 primary, 6 autograft) were analyzed. The two groups were similar except for shorter operative time in the primary repairs (172.6 ± 124.4 vs. 262.0 ± 80.8 min, p = 0.047). The primary repairs showed significantly lower numbers of MRCC sensory grade ≥ 3 across the follow-ups (Z = − 2.18, p = 0.029). Overall, PEG-mediated autograft repairs may enhance outcomes following peripheral nerve autografting. This study represents the first human comparison of PEG-mediated neurorrhaphy techniques and warrant validation in larger studies.Keywords: Peripheral nerve injury, Polyethylene glycol (PEG), Axonal fusion, Neurorrhaphy, Nerve autograft, Sensory recoverySubject terms: Medical research, Neurology, Neuroscience

Introduction

Peripheral nerve injuries (PNIs) affect approximately 25 million Americans, with upper extremity peripheral nerve injuries (UE PNIs) constituting the most common subtype and accounting for approximately 68.7% to 72.6% of all traumatic PNIs1–4. UE PNIs are especially consequential as losses in sensation, coordination, strength, and dexterity can severely limit hand function and is associated with poor psychosocial function and overall quality of life3,5,6. Functional recovery following PNIs depends on Wallerian degeneration and subsequent axonal regeneration, which occurs at a relatively slow rate of approximately 1 mm per day or 1 inch per month7,8. Despite advances in microsurgical techniques for nerve repair, up to 40% of repairs of PNIs fail to achieve functional recovery with many injuries lead to long-lasting or permanent impairments in sensory and motor function1,9.

Increased tension at the site of nerve repairs can significantly decrease axon diameter and functional recovery10,11. Autografting has become an effective strategy in bridging defects but creates unique barriers. Specifically, autografts require axons to regenerate across a non-native graft scaffold and traverse additional coaptation sites12,13. Even after optimal surgical autograft repair, patients may still experience incomplete, unpredictable, and unsatisfactory recovery14–16.

Polyethylene glycol (PEG) mediated-neurorrhaphy has emerged as a technique to enhance nerve repair by limiting Wallerian degeneration and restoring axonal continuity17–25. By removing water from the site of nerve coaptation, PEG is thought to promote membrane fusion and has been associated with early restoration of action potentials across coaptation sites19,26–29. Our lab group’s randomized clinical trial on PEG-mediated neurorrhaphy of traumatic digital nerve injuries suggested PEG-mediated neurorrhaphy may have early sensory and higher patient reported functional outcomes23. Recently, our lab group has published the randomized control trial result on PEG-mediated neurorrhaphy for traumatic median and ulnar nerve injuries, which suggested patients with PEG-mediated neurorrhaphy may have early functional recovery and improved patient-reported functional recovery25,25.

Despite these findings, key considerations have yet to be addressed in the clinical literature. To our knowledge no studies have examined the role of PEG-mediated axon fusion with autograft nerve repairs in humans. In this prospective cohort study, clinical outcomes of PEG-mediated neurorrhaphy in both primary and autograft reconstructions of digital and mixed motor-sensory nerve injuries of the upper extremities were evaluated. By comparing the two PEG-mediated neurorrhaphy techniques (primary versus autograft), this investigation looks to further expand on existing research evaluating the role of PEG as an adjunct in nerve repair procedures. We hypothesize that nerve autograft with PEG-mediated-neurorrhaphy may promote earlier sensory recovery than primary repair with PEG-mediated-neurorrhaphy.

Methods

Data source

This prospective cohort study utilizes data derived from a previously conducted double-blind, randomized clinical trial (ClinicalTrials.gov ID # NCT02359825) approved by the institutional review board at Vanderbilt University Medical Center (IRB #191,655). All methods were performed in accordance with our institutional guidelines and regulations and were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The design, patient recruitment, and core methodology of the parent trial have been described in detail previously23,25.

Patients were recruited from the emergency department or hand clinics between July 2021 and April 2025. Eligible patients included individuals aged 18–75 years presenting within 72 h of a Sunderland class V traumatic digital and/or mixed motor-sensory nerve injury of the distal forearm. The eligible patients may have multiple nerve injuries, and/or concurrent vascular, tendon, or bony injuries. All patients underwent operative interventions with either primary or autograft nerve repairs. Preoperatively, all patients and/or their surrogate decision-maker (s) provided informed consent for their participations into the study.

Surgical procedure

All procedures were performed by board-certified surgeons with hand surgery privileges at our institution25. Operative management followed standard microsurgical nerve repair principles until the point of nerve coaptation or grafting. The decision to perform primary repair versus autograft reconstruction was made intraoperatively at the discretion of the operating surgeon based on defect characteristics and ability to achieve a tension-free repair.

The polyethylene glycol (PEG)–mediated neurorrhaphy protocol used in this study has been previously described23,25. Briefly, nerve ends were sharply debrided under irrigation with a calcium-free Plasma-Lyte A (Baxter, Deerfield, IL) solution to minimize premature axonal sealing. Following epineural repair with appropriate fascicular alignment for assisting with accuracy of reinnervation, a PEG solution (50% PEG, 3.35 kDa) was applied to the coaptation site for a total of two minutes. This was followed by irrigation with a calcium-containing lactated Ringer’s solution (Hospira, Lake Forest, IL) to facilitate membrane resealing. A schematic of the PEG protocol is used as described in Nemani et al23.

For autograft reconstruction, interposition grafting was performed in an anterograde fashion and the PEG protocol was applied to both proximal and distal coaptation sites25. In the case of cabled nerve autografts, each cable of the nerve autograft was sutured into place first, and then PEG was applied to all the cables at the proximal and distal nerve coaptation sites. Wound closure was performed in accordance with the surgeon’s standard technique.

Postoperative follow-up and data collection

Patients with postoperative follow up to 100 days were included in the study. Outcome assessment methodology was adapted from the parent randomized trial23,25. At each follow-up encounter, trained evaluators who were blinded to treatment details performed standardized sensory testing using Semmes–Weinstein monofilament testing (SWMT) and static two-point discrimination (S2PD). These measures were integrated into the Medical Research Council Classification (MRCC) grading system to quantify sensory recovery.

Demographic variables (age, sex, body mass index, and race/ethnicity), and relevant comorbidities (including tobacco use and alcohol use disorder) were collected. Operative variables (i.e., injured nerve type, repair modality, injury site-to-fingertip distance, injury-to-repair interval, and operative duration) were also recorded. Associated injuries (i.e., tendon or vascular injury) and postoperative complications (i.e., reoperation and amputations) were documented.

Sample size calculation

Sample size estimation was informed by previously published preclinical and clinical data evaluating PEG-mediated neurorrhaphy which was discussed in detailed in our previous publication25. Previous data demonstrates a 50% improvement when implementing PEG-mediated therapy with a standard deviation (SD) of approximately 15%25. If this trial’s benefit is conservatively estimated to be 25% while keeping the same SD, then 6 nerve repairs in the PEG group would allow us to detect a 2-tailed significance of 0.05 with a power of 0.8025.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R (version 2024.4.2.764). Categorical variables were compared using chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests, while continuous variables were analyzed using two-sample t-tests or Mann–Whitney U tests, depending on the data distribution.

For univariate analysis of postoperative sensory function, sensory MRCC grades were grouped into two categories: MRCC < 3 (no sensory recovery) and MRCC ≥ 3 (sensory recovery). For multivariate analysis, the differences in sensory MRCC grades were compared individually across the postoperative days between PEG-mediated primary and PEG-mediated autograft repairs using the Kruskal–Wallis test, followed by Dunn’s post hoc test with Bonferroni correction for all follow-up visits with POD < 100 days. Higher MRCC sensory scores highlight improved sensory recovery. A positive Z value means the primary repair had a higher MRCC sensory score than the autograft across the follow-ups during. A negative Z value means the primary repair had a lower MRCC sensory score than the autograft.

Results

Study population and repair distribution

A total of 23 patients with 48 digital and mixed motor-sensory nerve injuries repaired within 72 h of injury who had followed up within the first 100 days postoperatively periods were included in the analysis. The 100 day follow up was used as a cut-off because of the availability of more complete data up to this time point. All patients included this analysis underwent PEG-mediated neurorrhaphy. Specifically, 19 patients underwent primary repair involving 42 nerves, while 4 patients underwent autograft repair involving 6 nerves (Table 1).

Patient and injury characteristics

Among the study population, there were no statistically significant differences in age, sex, body mass index, race/ethnicity, comorbidities, injury-to-repair time, or postoperative follow-up between primary PEG-mediated and autograft PEG-mediated neurorrhaphy (Table 3). The autograft PEG-mediated neurorrhaphy group demonstrated a trend toward a longer injury-to-fingertip distance compared with the primary PEG-mediated neurorrhaphy group; however, this difference was not statistically significant (mean (SD), 42.50 (24.1) cm vs. 24.83 (11.2) cm; p = 0.2) (Table 3). Although not statistically significant, patients in the autograft PEG-mediated neurorrhaphy group tended to have earlier postoperative follow-up visits on average within the 100-day postoperative study period compared with those in the primary PEG-mediated neurorrhaphy group (32.33 (14.1) days vs. 42.80 (12.2) days; p = 0.12) (Table 3). Primary PEG-mediated neurorrhaphy was associated with a statistically shorter surgery duration compared with autograft repair (262.0 (80.8) minutes vs. 172.6 (124.4) minutes; p = 0.047) (Table 3).

Discussions

In this prospective cohort study, we evaluated early sensory outcomes following primary PEG-mediated and autograft PEG-mediated neurorrhaphies in patients with digital and mixed motor-sensory nerve injuries of the upper extremity. Our results suggests that autograft repairs achieved significantly higher MRCC sensory scores than primary repairs during the first 100 postoperative days, supporting our hypothesis.

Consistent with prior clinical and preclinical studies, PEG-mediated neurorrhaphy appeared to enhance early sensory recovery across both digital and mixed motor-sensory nerve injuries23,25. Prior randomized trials have demonstrated earlier functional recovery, improved patient-reported outcomes, and superior sensory testing metrics following PEG application in primary nerve repairs23,25. Our findings extend this literature by specifically examining autograft reconstructions, an area that remains underrepresented in existing clinical studies. The observation that autograft PEG-mediated neurorrhaphy demonstrated higher MRCC sensory scores than primary PEG-mediated neurorrhaphy suggest two things: (1) PEG may play a particularly meaningful role in overcoming the inherent biological challenges of nerve grafting and (2) Autografting may enhance PEG-mediated axon fusion by reducing the tension at the coaptation site.

In a 40-year series, Mackinnon and Dellon reported that 20–40% of patients achieved very good (M4S3 +) recovery after direct nerve coaptation but that few injuries recovered fully1,1,30,30. Importantly, even minimal tension at the repair site has been shown negatively impact the functional outcome15,31. Nerve grafting, itself, is indicated as an intermediary to reduce tension on a primary nerve coaptation. Classic dogma emphasizes that even 10% of tension on a nerve decreases the blood supply to the coaptation site by 50%16.

However, the results of nerve grafts are worse than for primary nerve coaptation1. Autograft nerve repair traditionally requires regenerating axons to traverse two coaptation sites and repopulate a non-native autograft scaffold, contributing to delayed and often incomplete recovery1. For a nerve autograft with two coaptation sites, it has been shown that 25% of axons will successfully regenerate through the graft1. Considering the distance to the motor/sensory target, there will then be additional axonal loss secondary to chronic axotomy and muscle fibrosis1.

PEG-mediated neurorrhaphy may partially bypass these limitations by restoring immediate axonal continuity, reducing Wallerian degeneration distal to the repair, and preserving end-organ viability23,25,27,28,32. This mechanism is especially relevant for autograft repairs, where axonal loss at both coaptation interfaces can compound regenerative failure. The significantly negative Z value observed in the autograft PEG-mediated neurorrhaphy group compared to primary PEG-mediated neurorrhaphy group suggests that autograft repairs achieved superior early sensory outcomes compared with primary repairs, a finding that contrasts with conventional expectations of inferior autograft repair outcomes and underscores the potential mechanistic advantage of PEG in nerve grafting. An additional observation from this study is that repair-site tension may play an important role in the effectiveness of PEG-mediated axonal fusion. Autograft reconstruction likely mitigates tension at the coaptation site, thereby enhancing the biologic effects of PEG and contributing to the potential superior early sensory recovery observed in autograft PEG-mediated neurorrhaphy.

Although the primary repair and autograft groups were comparable with respect to demographic and clinical characteristics, autograft PEG-mediated neurorrhaphy was associated with a statistically longer operative duration compared with primary repair (Table 3). Based on previous publications from our group demonstrating no significant difference in operative time with the addition of PEG application23,25. We attribute the longer operative time observed in this study to the inclusion of autograft repair in the procedure. Despite this additional operative time, PEG-mediated autograft may have superior early sensory recovery compared with PEG-mediated primary repairs. These findings suggest that shorter operative duration alone may not account for improved sensory outcomes and further support the hypothesis that biologic factors intrinsic to autograft reconstruction—such as reduced repair-site tension and enhanced axonal alignment—may potentiate the effects of PEG-mediated axonal fusion.

Although not statistically significant, it is important to note that the autograft group trended toward having a longer injury-to-fingertip distance, suggesting a greater distance for regenerating axons to travel to reach their target compared with the primary repair group (Table 3). Additionally, the autograft group was, on average, evaluated slightly earlier than the primary repair group, although this difference was also not statistically significant (Table 3). Despite the longer distance required for axonal regeneration, the autograft group appeared to demonstrate a trend toward earlier sensory recovery compared with the primary repair group. However, given the lack of statistical significance, no definitive conclusions can be drawn from this observation, and further investigation with larger sample sizes is warranted.

Overall, these findings suggest that PEG-mediated neurorrhaphy may fundamentally alter the biological behavior of autografts, improving the historical outcome gap between repair strategies in the early postoperative period.

Clinically, these results are particularly relevant given the functional consequences of delayed sensory recovery in the hand. Early restoration of protective sensation and tactile discrimination plays a critical role in dexterity, coordination, and patient quality of life. The higher rates of MRCC ≥ 3 sensory recovery observed in PEG-treated nerves—especially in autograft repairs—suggests that PEG may accelerate meaningful functional recovery during a period when conventional axonal regeneration would otherwise be limited by distance-dependent regrowth rates.

This study has several limitations that merit consideration. Although derived from a randomized clinical trial, the present analysis represents a subset of patients with available early follow-up, resulting in relatively small autograft sample sizes. The small sample size also precluded separate analyses comparing autograft and primary repairs for digital nerves and mixed motor-sensory nerves independently. Additionally, the autograft group was selected intraoperatively at the operating surgeon’s discretion, which may have influenced the patient population included in this study. There was no objective confirmation of tension-free repair in either group beyond what was reported by the operating surgeon, representing an important potential confounder that warrants further investigation in future studies. Furthermore, different donor nerves were used for the autografts, and one out of the two that used cable grafts reported the number of cable grafts used. This represents another potential confounder to consider in future studies, as donor nerve quality and the number of cable grafts may affect the number of axons available for PEG-mediated axon fusion, which may subsequently influence functional recovery. Sensory data was obtained from MRCC sensory scores were derived from clinical sensory testing rather than objective electrophysiologic measures, and motor outcomes were not evaluated in this analysis due to limited available data. Follow-up was limited to the first 100 postoperative days due to scarce long-term data and therefore reflects early recovery rather than long-term functional outcomes. Nonetheless, early sensory recovery is clinically meaningful and may serve as a surrogate marker for downstream functional restoration.

Future studies should aim to validate these findings in larger cohorts, longer follow-up, incorporate standardized motor and electrophysiologic assessments, and evaluate patient-reported functional outcomes. Additionally, biomolecular studies examining PEG-mediated fusion across nerve graft interfaces may further elucidate how PEG modifies axonal survival and regeneration in autografts.

Conclusions

Compared to primary PEG-mediated neurorrhaphy, autograft PEG-mediated neurorrhaphy appears to be associated with improved early sensory recovery following upper extremity nerve repair. These findings suggest that autograft PEG-mediated neurorrhaphy may mitigate the challenges inherent to nerve autografts, particularly the presences of two coaptation sites.
