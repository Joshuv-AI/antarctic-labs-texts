Asymtomatic Subcutaneous Nodules
Authors: Nazneen Bano, Shubham Kumar, Anil Budania, Savita Singla
Published: 2025

A 26-year-old female presented with a history of progressively increasing multiple subcutaneous nodules on bilateral forearms over the past 5 years. These nodules were non-visible but palpable on examination. Clinical evaluation revealed discrete, firm, non-tender subcutaneous nodules of varying sizes (3 × 3 to 5 × 5 mm), predominantly situated on the volar aspect of both forearms [Figure 1]. Notably, the nodules were non-adherent to the overlying skin, and there were no associated surface changes. The remainder of the cutaneous and systemic examinations were unremarkable. Radiographs of the bilateral forearms showed no abnormalities. A 5 mm punch biopsy was performed, completely excising one of the nodules. Histopathological analysis demonstrated epidermal changes including hyperkeratosis, focal follicular plugging, and basket-weave orthokeratosis. In the deeper dermal layers, a cystic structure lined by stratified squamous epithelium lacking a granular layer was observed [Figures 2 and 3].

What is your diagnosis:

Trichelemmal cyst

Discussion

Trichilemmal cysts, also known as pilar cysts, typically manifest as benign, smooth, mobile nodules primarily located on the scalp, although they can occasionally appear on other hair-bearing regions such as the neck, trunk, limbs, or groin. Multiple trichilemmal cysts are infrequently observed within the scalp but are rarely found in a multicentric distribution. This condition can exhibit familial traits and may show autosomal-dominant inheritance patterns.[1]

Ehara Y et al.[2] and Nakamura et al.[3] reported a case of TC on the leg, and Liu et al.[4] reported a case on the dorsum of the hand.

Our case presents a unique instance of multiple trichilemmal cysts occurring over bilateral forearms, which is notably uncommon.

Clinically, trichilemmal cysts appear as firm, smooth-walled, cream-white nodules containing semi-solid cheesy material upon sectioning. Histopathologically, these cysts are lined with stratified squamous epithelium lacking distinct intercellular bridges but displaying peripheral palisading. Characteristic trichilemmal keratinization is abrupt without an intervening granular layer, distinguishing it from epidermal cysts. However, atypical presentations such as invasive growth beyond the cyst wall or malignant transformation, termed proliferating trichilemmal tumors, have been reported in literature.[5]

In our case, we describe a novel presentation where trichilemmal cysts presented as subcutaneous nodules that became noticeable only upon compression, which to our knowledge has not been previously documented. Additionally, the isolated multicentric involvement exclusively over bilateral forearms represents a unique clinical manifestation not reported in existing literature.

Further research is warranted to better understand the pathogenesis and clinical spectrum of trichilemmal cysts, particularly in cases exhibiting atypical features or multicentric distribution. Comprehensive clinical and histopathological characterization is essential for accurate diagnosis and appropriate management of these benign but occasionally challenging lesions.

Learning points

While the scalp is the most common site for trichilemmal cysts, occurring in approximately 90% of cases, other locations should be considered when asymptomatic nodules are observed.

Trichilemmal cysts may manifest multicentrically as subcutaneous nodules with normal overlying skin.
