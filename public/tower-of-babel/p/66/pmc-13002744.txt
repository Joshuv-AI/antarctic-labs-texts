3-Methylcrotonyl-CoA Carboxylase Expression Among Astrocytes and Neurons in the Human Brain and the Effect of Hyperglycemia on the Catabolic Flux of 13C6, 15N-Leucine in Cultured Astrocytes
Authors: Radovan Murín, Jakub Šofranko, Andrej Kováč, Markéta Murínová, Eduard Gondáš
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLeucine is an essential amino acid which is imported into the brain parenchyma with high capacity. Animal studies have demonstrated that leucine plays a significant role in several cellular and physiological processes in brain parenchyma. In addition to its role in protein synthesis, leucine possesses signaling and regulatory functions. Furthermore, leucine catabolism may provide brain cells with amino nitrogen for the synthesis of glutamate and glutamine with an impact on sustaining glutamatergic and GABA-ergic neurotransmission. The entry of leucine’s carbon skeleton into the intermediary metabolism of astrocytes yields the production of ketone bodies and acetyl-CoA. In order to investigate the metabolic capabilities of human astrocytes regarding leucine, we enriched their culture media with 13C₆,15N-leucine and conducted a metabolomic study using liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC–MS) to identify and quantify isotopically labelled metabolites. Furthermore, we employed an antiserum against 3-methylcrotonyl-CoA carboxylase (MCC), the unique enzyme in the irreversible phase of leucine catabolism, to identify MCC-expressing cells both in culture and in situ. Our results indicate that cultured human astrocytes efficiently removed leucine from the medium, which was then enriched with several compounds containing nitrogen and/or carbon atoms derived from leucine. Among the released metabolites, glutamine and citrate were the most abundant. Leucine uptake was independent of glucose concentration; however, hyperglycemic conditions stimulated the capacity for the irreversible catabolism of the leucine-derived carbon skeleton. Immunoprobing with the MCC antiserum confirmed the mitochondrial expression of MCC in astrocytes in culture as well as in situ. In addition to astrocytes, immunofluorescent double-labelling revealed the colocalization of MCC with a neuronal marker in human brain sections. This study confirms that human astrocytes are capable of catabolizing leucine and incorporating leucine-derived atoms into the intermediary metabolism. The presence of MCC in cultured astrocytes underscores their ability to convert leucine into acetyl-CoA and ketone bodies. Introduction

Leucine is an essential amino acid that is actively transported across the blood-brain barrier via large neutral amino acid transporters [1]. In brain parenchyma, leucine participates in a wide array of metabolic and signaling processes that are critical for maintaining neuronal function and brain homeostasis [2–9]. Among its most prominent roles, leucine supports glutamatergic and GABA-ergic neurotransmission by providing atoms for the de novo synthesis of molecules of glutamate, glutamine and GABA, and therefore contributes to sustaining the glutamate/GABA – glutamine cycle [10–16].

Within astrocytes – the key metabolic support cells of the central nervous system – leucine undergoes transamination and the subsequent catabolism of its carbon skeleton, yielding acetyl-CoA and ketone body acetoacetate [4, 15, 17–20]. Since astrocytes are regarded as the primary cellular compartment in the brain for the de novo synthesis of glutamate and its conversion to glutamine [15], leucine transamination is considered to be a critical step for providing the amino group required for these processes [10–15]. Acetoacetate – the product of leucine carbon skeleton catabolism (Scheme 1) – may be enzymatically reduced to 3-hydroxybutyrate, non-enzymatically decarboxylated to acetone, or converted into two acetyl-CoA molecules. Acetyl-CoA molecules are a central metabolic node, fuelling mitochondrial energy production and serving as a precursor for cellular biosynthetic processes [21, 22]. In addition to their metabolic roles, both acetoacetate and 3-hydroxybutyrate act as signalling molecules, donating acetyl groups for epigenetic modifications [23] or as the ligands for specific receptors [23, 24]. While extensive research in animal models has provided substantial insight into leucine metabolism, the extent to which human brain cells utilize leucine metabolism – particularly under varying metabolic states - remains incompletely understood [9, 25].

Simplified scheme of leucine metabolism in human cells. In cells, 13C6, 15N-leucine is transaminated by branched-chain amino acid transaminase (BCAT) to yield 13C5-α-ketoisocaproate (KIC). The amino-group (15NH2-depicted in blue) of leucine is transferred to α-ketoglutarate to generate 15N-glutamate (not shown at scheme). KIC undergoes oxidative decarboxylation catalyzed by the branched-chain α-ketoacid dehydrogenase complex to form 13C5-isovaleryl-CoA. Subsequent mitochondrial reactions, including the incorporation of a 12C atom as part of the carboxyl group (depicted as green) via the 3-methylcrotonyl-CoA carboxylase (MCC) reaction, generate 13C2-acetyl-CoA and 13C3-acetoacetate. The latter, 13C3-acetoacetate, can be further cleaved to yield two isotopomers of acetyl-CoA, containing one and two 13C atoms in their acetyl moiety

Astrocytes play a pivotal role in brain metabolism [26–28], with glucose serving as their primary substrate under physiological conditions. Glucose uptake into the brain parenchyma is regulated by its circulating levels and increased during hyperglycemia [29]. Beyond its role as an energy source, glucose modulates diverse metabolic pathways [30, 31], influencing the processing of other nutrients, including BCAAs [9, 20]. Due to the metabolic flexibility of astrocytes, elevated glucose concentrations may be expected to alter leucine catabolic fluxes, potentially shifting the balance between transamination products and oxidative degradation intermediates that may represent an adaptive mechanism to meet changing energetic and biosynthetic demands.

To address gaps in current knowledge, we investigated leucine metabolism in cultured human astrocytes under euglycemic and hyperglycemic conditions using liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC–MS) metabolomics. Cultures were supplied with uniformly labelled L-[13C6, 15N]leucine, enabling the tracing of carbon and nitrogen atoms through metabolic networks. This approach allows the direct assessment of leucine carbon entry into tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle intermediates such as citrate, as well as nitrogen incorporation into amino acids such as glutamine. Given that 3-methylcrotonyl-CoA carboxylase (MCC) catalyzes the committed, irreversible steps of leucine degradation [17, 32, 33], we also evaluated MCC expression in astrocytes and neurons both in vitro and in situ. The combined isotopic and enzymatic data provide new insight into the interplay between glucose availability and leucine catabolism by human astrocytes and furthermore, that the capability of both astrocytes and neurons in the brain to catabolize leucine may be deduced based on the presence of MCC.

Materials and Methods

All chemicals and solutions, namely ammonium formate, citric acid, sodium 2-oxoisocaproate, glucose, and compounds enriched with 13C and 15N, such as L- [13C6, 15N] leucine, and other amino acids, bovine serum albumin (BSA), Tween-20, Dulbecco’s phosphate-buffered saline (DPBS) and 3-[(3-cholamidopropyl)dimethylammonio]-1-propanesulfonate (CHAPS) were purchased from (Sigma, MO, USA). HPLC-grade acetonitrile was from Honeywell (Lambda-life, Slovakia). The primary antibodies, rabbit anti-3-methylcrotonyl-CoA carboxylase (MCC) [17, 33, 34], and human anti-pyruvate dehydrogenase (PDH) [33], were a gift from Prof. Dr. Bernd Hamprecht (IFIB, University of Tübingen, Tübingen, Germany). The mouse IgGs anti-neurofilament-200 and anti-glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) were purchased from Sigma (MO, USA). The set of affinity-purified secondary antibodies conjugated to Alexa Fluor dyes were purchased from Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA, USA).

Cell Cultures

In this experimental study, we used primary human K1884 astrocytes (Cat. No. N7805-100) obtained from Gibco (Waltham, MT, USA) and normal human astrocytes (NHA; Cat. No. CC-2565) obtained from Lonza Group (Basel, Switzerland). The maximum number of passages for both cell lines is 10. In our study, all experiments were conducted using cells at passage number 6 or lower. Both types of human astrocyte cells were cultured in a specific medium for astrocyte growth, Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM; Gibco, Waltham, MT, USA, Cat. No. 10569010), supplemented with 10% (v/v) One Shot Fetal Bovine Serum (Gibco, Waltham, MT, USA), and with the addition of 1% (v/v) N-2 Supplement, 100 U/mL penicillin, and 0.1 mg/mL streptomycin sulfate, at culture conditions of 37 °C and in a humidified atmosphere enriched to 5% CO2. The culture media were refreshed every three days. The cells were routinely passaged after reaching 80–90% confluency. A solution of 0.5% (w/v) trypsin in phosphate-buffered saline supplemented with 5 mM EDTA was used to detach cells.

For the metabolic experiments, cultured K1884 human astrocytes were incubated under the defined conditions. After reaching approximately 80% confluency, the cells were washed three times with DPBS and subsequently incubated in a fresh RPMI 1640 medium (RPMI; Lonza, Basel, Switzerland, Cat. No. 12-115Q) supplemented with 10% (v/v) One Shot Fetal Bovine Serum for 4 h. Following incubation, the culture medium was collected, centrifuged at 500×g, and the resulting supernatant was used for LC–MS analysis.

For the isotopic tracing experiments, as well as for the experiments assessing the fate of leu and glucose, K1884 astrocytes were allowed to reach 100% confluency, after which the DMEM culture medium was removed and the cells were washed three times with DPBS. The cells were then incubated for 4 h in a specifically prepared incubation medium consisting of DPBS supplemented with calcium and magnesium and containing 2 mM of unlabeled (¹²C₆) leucine or isotopically labeled (¹³C₆,¹⁵N) leucine, in the presence of either 5 mM or 25 mM glucose. After incubation, the culture media were collected, centrifuged at 500 × g, and the supernatants were used for LC–MS analysis.

We verified the identity of both cell lines used in this study against the ICLAC database of commonly misidentified cell lines. Neither of the cell lines, namely K1884, and NHA, is listed as misidentified or cross-contaminated according to the ICLAC database.

Both cell lines were authenticated by the supplier using short tandem repeat profiling prior to the initiation of the experiments. Cells were used at early passages (≤ passage 6) and maintained under standard culture conditions for a limited period; therefore, no additional authentication was performed during the study.

Preparation of Cell Lysates

The culture medium from the attached cells was removed, and the cells were immediately washed three times with ice-cold DPBS. Subsequently, an ice-cold lysis buffer was added to the cells with the following composition: 1% (w/v) CHAPS, 150 mM Tris/HCl pH 7.6, 150 mM NaCl (Sigma, MO, USA) in distilled water. A rubber cell scraper was used to scrape off the attached cells. The lysates were collected in the microfuge tubes, and centrifugated at 10,000 x g for 10 min. The formed supernatants were collected in aliquots and stored for further analyses at -20 °C.

Protein Concentration in Lysates

The commercially available DC Protein assay kit (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA) was used for measuring the total protein concentration in cell lysates. The generated absorbance was measured at 750 nm using Synergy H4 hybrid microplate reader (Bio Tek, Winooski, VT, USA). The concentration was determined using bovine serum albumin which served as an internal standard.

Western Blot Analysis

For electrophoretic separation in 8% SDS-polyacrylamide gels, 40 µg of lysate protein was loaded per lane. The separated proteins were transferred onto a nitrocellulose membrane using a Mini Trans-Blot cell (BioRad Laboratories, Bratislava, Slovakia). The unspecific binding sites on the membrane were blocked with a blocking buffer (BB) consisting of 140 mM NaCl in 20 mM Tris/HCl with a pH of 7.5, 2% (w/w) BSA and 0.05% (w/V) Tween-20 at room temperature for one hour. Subsequently, the membrane was probed with anti-MCC rabbit serum diluted 1:250 in BB. Before and after the application of secondary antibodies, anti-rabbit IgG conjugated to horseradish peroxidase (POD) diluted 1:10,000 in BB, the membrane was rigorously washed with BB without BSA three times for 5 min. A chemiluminescent signal was generated by the POD catalyzed conversion of SuperSignal West Pico Chemiluminescent substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific), as recommended by the producer and the signal was recorded by Chemidoc XRS system (BioRad Laboratories, Bratislava, Slovakia).

The membranes were re-probed with mouse anti-GAPDH antibodies. Both antibodies, anti-GAPDH and anti-mouse IgG-POD, were diluted 1:500 or 1:10,000 in BB.

The obtained chemiluminescent signals for both MCC and GAPDH were processed using Image Studio™ Software (LI-COR, Inc., Nebraska, USA).

Immunofluorescent Staining of MCC in Cultured Cells

The immunofluorescent detection of MCC expression in cultured cells was performed as already mentioned [34]. In brief, cells were grown on glass coverslips in petri dishes. When the cells reached approximately 60% confluency, they were washed with DPBS and fixed with paraformaldehyde for 15 min. The paraformaldehyde fixed cells were treated in a sequence of steps including permeabilization, blocking and incubation with a mixture of the primary antibodies consisting of rabbit anti-MCC serum, human anti-PDH serum and mouse anti-GFAP IgG molecules. All three primary antibodies were diluted into a final ratio of 1:250 in a blocking solution. Subsequently, the cells were washed three times before incubation with the affinity-purified secondary antibodies, i.e., anti-rabbit IgG covalently linked with Alexa Fluor 488 (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), anti-mouse IgG linked to Alexa Fluor 594 (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific), and with TRITC linked anti-human IgG (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The DAPI was added to the second-last washing solution (5 µg/mL; Sigma, MO, USA) to stain the cell nuclei. Negative controls were obtained by omitting the primary antibodies.

Immunohistochemical Detection of MCC Expression Among Brain Cells

Paraffin-embedded human brain tissue was obtained from the Slovak Brain Bank (51-45739480-A0001, SK010922). All experiments were monitored and approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee (Ethics Committee of the Institute of Neuroimmunology, Slovak Academy of Sciences, Slovakia). The 7 μm-thick human brain coronal sections were prepared from the cortex of an adult, anonymized male donor and used for immunohistochemistry analysis, as described in [34]. In brief, after deparaffination, dehydration, and antigen retrieval, the 7 μm-thick brain sections were incubated with a mixture of primary antibodies against MCC with a combination of mouse anti-GFAP or mouse anti-neurofilament 200 at 4 °C overnight. All primary antibodies were diluted to a final ratio of 1:250. After the incubation of the primary antibodies, the sections were washed three times and incubated with a mixture of secondary antibodies containing goat anti-rabbit IgG coupled with Alexa Fluor 488 (1:500), and anti-mouse coupled with Alexa Fluor 594 (1:500) at room temperature for two hours. The visualization of cell nuclei was achieved by incubating sections with a solution of DAPI. FluoroShield mounting medium was used to mount the sections.

Individual samples were scanned using the Zeiss Axio Examiner/LSM 880 confocal system (Carl Zeiss, Jena, Germany) using the Zeiss Plan-Apochromat 40×/1.3 Oil DIC M27 objective. The immunofluorescent signal on stained human brain sections and cells was recorded by using Zeiss Zen Black software. The DAPI were visualized using 405 nm laser, TRITC-labeled PDH with 561 nm laser, Alexa Fluor 488-labeled MCC with 488 nm laser, and Alexa Fluor 594-labeled GFAP or Neurofilament 200 with 594 nm laser. Images were acquired with a resolution of 2048 × 2048 pixels, with the dwell pixel set to 2.06 µs and a pinhole of 1.0 AU. The resulting images were processed using ImageJ v. 1.54 (https://wsr.imagej.net/).

LC–MS Analysis

The analysis of culture media using the LC–MS method was performed as described in a previously published protocol for polaromics [35]. In brief, 20 µl of the collected culture media sample was mixed with 180 µl of acetonitrile (Honeywell, Germany) containing internal standards (IS). The mixture was vortexed and then centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 10 min. The supernatant was transferred to glass vials for LC–MS analysis, which was conducted using a Sequant® ZIC® pHILIC column (Sigma, MO, USA). The internal standards included 13C5-valine, 13C6-glucose, and either 12C6,14N-leucine or 13C6,15N-leucine, depending on whether the cells were incubated with isotopically labeled leucine. The final molar concentration of 13C6-glucose was 0.5 mM, while the levels of amino acids used as IS were 6 µg/ml. All compounds used as IS were purchased from Sigma (MO, USA).

Statistical Analysis

Experiments were performed using three to four replicates of astrocyte cultures at different passage numbers. Statistical comparisons between the two groups were performed using the two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-test. Outlier detection was performed using the ROUT method (Q = 1%) implemented in GraphPad Prism software; no outliers were identified, so no data points were excluded. The normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test before applying parametric tests. Details of the statistical analyses and results are provided in the Supplementary Statistics file. Data analysis was performed using InStat software version 3.10 (GraphPad Software, Boston, MA, USA).

Results

In order to evaluate to what extent human astrocytes are capable of metabolizing essential amino acids, we employed the LC–MS analysis [35] of RPMI 1640 medium collected from cultured brain-progenitor-derived astrocytes (K1884) after a 4-hour incubation. The quantitative analysis of the MS-spectra revealed that astrocytes depleted predominantly arginine from the culture medium, followed by branched-chain amino acids. In addition to BCAA and arginine, the ketogenic amino acid - lysine - and asparagine are also taken up at high rates from the medium compared to other amino acids. However, this analysis showed that it is not glutamate, but rather glutamine that is produced and released into culture media by K1884 cells (Table 1).

The LC–MS analysis of a specific culture medium, containing either 5 mM or 25 mM glucose and 2 mM leucine in DPBS supplemented with calcium and magnesium, was used to measure glucose uptake (Fig. 1A), leucine uptake (Figs. 1B and 2A), and 2-oxoisocaproate (KIC) release (Fig. 2A) over a four-hour period. The K1884 astrocyte cells exhibited a higher glucose uptake in the medium containing 25 mM of glucose, 13.94 ± 0.45 mM, compared to the medium with a 5 mM level of glucose, 2.77 ± 0.34 mM (Fig. 1A). In contrast, leucine uptake from the culture medium was not affected by glucose concentration. The amount of leucine taken up from the specific culture medium was similar under both conditions: 1.36 ± 0.12 mM in 25 mM of glucose and 1.36 ± 0.17 mM in 5 mM of glucose (Figs. 1B and 2A). In parallel, we quantified significant changes in KIC production after 4 h of incubation under different glucose concentrations. In media containing 5 mM of glucose, KIC release reached 0.39 ± 0.11 mM, whereas under 25 mM glucose conditions, it amounted to 0.23 ± 0.03 mM (Fig. 2A). However, the calculated ratio of KIC produced to leucine metabolized showed significant differences depending on glucose concentration. A higher glucose level (25 mM) significantly decreased the KIC to Leu ratio, 17% ± 2%, compared to the 5 mM of glucose condition, 29% ± 10% (Fig. 2B).

It is known that leucine is a ketogenic amino acid that can, in some cases, be irreversibly metabolized and serve as a source of carbon moieties for the TCA cycle. In order to investigate this fact, we cultured astrocyte cells under euglycemic, 5 mM glucose (Fig. 3), or hyperglycemic, 25 mM glucose (Fig. 4), conditions in DPBS supplemented with calcium and magnesium for 4 h. The culture medium also contained 2 mM of leucine, either unlabeled (12C₆ ,14N)-leucine with naturally occurring isotopologues (carbon, hydrogen, oxygen and nitrogen), or uniformly labeled (13C₆ ,15N)-leucine. The LC–MS analysis was used to calculate the ratio of isotopically enriched glutamine, pyroglutamate, and citrate containing 13 or 15 N atoms, relative to their unenriched forms (Table 2). The citrate molecules with two additional mass units (M + 2) were observed in the 13C6, 15N leucine-supplemented medium. This observation suggests that leucine was irreversibly metabolized to acetyl- 13C2 molecules, which were subsequently incorporated into the TCA cycle via an enzymatic reaction mediated by citrate synthase. Pyroglutamate may originate either enzymatically within the glutathione cycle or non-enzymatically during the electrospray ionization (ESI) step in MS analysis. The retention time (Rt) of enzymatically generated pyroglutamate eluted from the column differs from the Rt of pyroglutamate formed directly from glutamine in ESI. In our results, we present only the ESI-generated pyroglutamate (identical Rt as glutamine), which arises by the intermolecular cyclization of glutamine accompanied by the loss of one amino group. Moreover, glutamine and pyroglutamate with two additional mass units (M + 2) were only detected in a medium supplemented with 13C6, 15N leucine, thus confirming that astrocyte cells metabolized leucine to acetyl-CoA molecules. Remarkably, the heavier molar masses (M + 1, M + 2, M + 3) of glutamine, pyroglutamate, and citrate were more abundantly observed under hyperglycemic (25mM Glc) compared to euglycemic conditions (5mM Glc). These findings suggest that at higher glucose levels, an increased proportion of carbon-skeletons derived from leucine are subject to catabolism by further intracellular metabolism [20]. This conclusion is also confirmed by the reduced ratio of KIC to leucine under hyperglycemic conditions (Fig. 2B) compared to euglycemic conditions.

Using LC–MS analysis with 13C-labelled carbon leucine, we demonstrated that K1884 astrocyte cells can catabolize leucine to acetyl-CoA moieties. This process requires enzymes from the leucine catabolic pathway, with the pivotal enzyme involved in the irreversible step of leucine metabolism being 3-methylcrotonyl-CoA carboxylase (MCC). In order to investigate MCC expression, we performed a Western blot analysis on protein lysates obtained from astrocyte cell lines K1884 and NHA, as well as on protein homogenates from human brain cortex tissue. A specific band corresponding to the predicted relative molecular mass of 75 kDa was detected, consistent with the α subunit of MCC (Fig. 3, MCC). The chemiluminescent signal of glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase (Fig. 3, GAPDH) was used as the positive control of the method.

The same rabbit primary antibodies against MCC were used to examine the presence of MCC in cultured astrocyte cells (Fig. 4) and in sections obtained from the human brain cortex (Fig. 5). Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG were applied as the secondary antibodies for the immunofluorescent detection of MCC in both human astrocyte cell lines, namely NHA (Fig. 4, NHA) and K1884 (Fig. 4, K1884), as well as in the human brain cortex (Fig. 5). The green fluorescent signal, generated by the combination of primary and secondary antibodies, was predominantly visible and localized near the cell nuclei. Additionally, polyclonal human anti-pyruvate dehydrogenase, (PDH) combined with TRITC-conjugated secondary antibodies which emit a red fluorescence signal, was used to detect the PDH enzyme complex in NHA astrocyte cells (Fig. 4, PDH). The presence of MCC in mitochondria was confirmed by the appearance of a yellow color in merged views, indicating the colocalization of MCC with PDH, a mitochondrial marker.

No fluorescence signals were observed in the negative controls, confirming that the detected fluorescent signals correspond to the presence of MCC, PDH, NEU, and GFAP in astrocyte cells and brain sections.

Discussion

Our findings demonstrate that cultured human astrocytes consistently take up leucine from the extracellular environment [9, 10, 20], but notably increase its irreversible catabolism under hyperglycemic conditions [9, 20]. Although total leucine uptake was unchanged, the α-ketoisocaproic acid (KIC)-to-leucine ratio decreased significantly at elevated glucose levels in the culture media, indicating that the leucine carbon skeleton was channeled more efficiently into mitochondrial oxidative pathways. Stable isotope tracing with L-[13C6, 15N]leucine revealed an increased incorporation of leucine-derived 13C atoms into citrate and glutamine under hyperglycemia, consistent with enhanced carbon flux into the TCA cycle and nitrogen transfer into amino acid pools. Immunofluorescent analysis revealed the mitochondrial localization of MCC in cultured human astrocytes, and the in situ expression of MCC in both neurons and astrocytes in the human brain. These findings highlight that both cell types possess the enzyme specific for the irreversible step of leucine catabolism.

The relative labeling of citrate and glutamine from [13C6, 15N]leucine provides important clues on the metabolic fate of leucine carbons in astrocytes. Citrate enrichment reflects the entry of labeled carbons into the TCA cycle, whereas glutamine enrichment primarily represents astrocytic nitrogen–carbon transfer to neurons [14]. We observed citrate labeling accompanied by measurable glutamine enrichment, suggesting that leucine can simultaneously sustain TCA cycle activity and contribute to glutamine synthesis, with the balance likely depending on metabolic context. Similar patterns have been reported previously. Salcedo et al. [9] showed that human astrocytes exhibit high citrate labeling by atoms originating from BCAA metabolism, whereas glutamine labeling can be selectively reduced under pathological conditions such as Alzheimer’s disease, indicating a shift toward TCA cycle support at the expense of glutamine export. Furthermore, it was demonstrated that in the R6/2 mouse model of Huntington’s disease, [13C6]leucine metabolism leads to an elevated labeling of TCA cycle intermediates, including citrate, while glutamine labeling was decreased—again consistent with enhanced anaplerosis but impaired astrocytic glutamine synthesis [36]. Placing our results in this context, the ratio of citrate-to-glutamine labeling from [13C6, 15N]leucine in human astrocytes under different glucose conditions could serve as a functional index of whether leucine metabolism is preferentially directed towards sustaining TCA cycle flux or supporting the glutamate–glutamine cycle. Contrary to the notion that hyperglycemia suppresses alternative fuel oxidation, our data supports the hyperglycemia-induced upregulation of leucine breakdown in astrocytes, underscoring their metabolic adaptability.

Like valine and isoleucine, leucine plays multifaceted roles in protein synthesis, metabolic regulation, and cellular signaling via the mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) pathway [2–8, 37–41]. Rodent studies have emphasized its role as a nitrogen donor for glutamate and glutamine synthesis in glial cells, with the initial catabolic steps mediated by branched-chain amino acid transaminase (BCAT) and the branched-chain α-ketoacid dehydrogenase (BCKDH) complex [11, 12]. In many such studies, MCC expression appeared to be glia-specific, being present in astrocytes and other glia but absent from neurons [17, 33]. Our observation of MCC in human neurons challenges this paradigm, aligning with Human Protein Atlas data (www.proteinatlas.org/ENSG00000078070-MCCC1; www.proteinatlas.org/ENSG00000131844-MCCC2) showing a high expression of both MCC subunits in excitatory neurons. This suggests a broader capacity for neuronal leucine catabolism in the human brain than rodent models predict. In addition to MCC, the expression of another biotin-containing mitochondrial carboxylase – pyruvate carboxylase (PC) – has been reported in human brain neurons [42] together with astrocytes [43–47]. While protein expression alone does not confirm enzymatic activity, the presence of MCC and PC in neurons implies that human neurons may participate in both catabolic and anaplerotic reactions, which are processes not traditionally attributed to neurons in rodent studies.

Leucine catabolism (Scheme 1) proceeds through BCAT-mediated transamination, BCKDH-dependent decarboxylation, and conversion to 3-methylcrotonyl-CoA, which MCC transforms into 3-methylglutaconyl-CoA. This intermediate is ultimately converted to acetyl-CoA and acetoacetate [18, 20, 34, 48]. MCC expression among cultured astrocytes is consistent with their potential to generate ketone bodies by leucine catabolism [20, 48] and their capability to incorporate acetyl moieties into the TCA cycle (recent study). The metabolic role of MCC in neurons remains enigmatic at the moment.

Our findings on MCC expression and leucine catabolism in human astrocytes expand upon previous work mapping the distribution of BCAA metabolic enzymes in the human brain. The previous studies documented the expression of a cytosolic isoform of BCAT (BCATc) largely among neurons—especially excitatory pyramidal cells and GABAergic interneurons—while mitochondrial BCAT (BCATm) was localized predominantly to vascular endothelial cells, with little or no immunoreactivity in astrocytes [49]. This distribution contrasts with studies on cultured rodent cells [11, 12], in which BCATm is robustly expressed in astrocytes, supporting the proposed BCAA–BCKA nitrogen shuttle between neurons and glia. Furthermore, the mapping of BCKDH complex component E1α expression reveals neuronal localization and indicates potential differences in cell-type–specific BCAA oxidative capacity between humans and rodents [50].

Our current demonstration that cultured human astrocytes express MCC and can irreversibly catabolize leucine to acetyl-CoA suggests that, despite the apparent lack of BCATm in fixed human astrocytes in situ, these cells can express key downstream enzymes of the leucine degradation pathway. This could reflect induction under culture conditions, selective expression in astrocytic subpopulations, or limitations in antigen detectability in post-mortem tissue. The ability to perform irreversible leucine catabolism in culture indicates that astrocytes may contribute more substantially to BCAA metabolism in the human brain than immunohistochemical surveys alone suggest.

This interpretation is further supported by recent evidence linking an altered BCAA metabolism to neurodegenerative conditions. For instance, defects in BCAA catabolism can exacerbate Alzheimer’s disease pathology through mTOR pathway dysregulation [51]. In addition, Salcedo et al. reported impaired BCAA metabolism in iPSC-derived astrocytes from Alzheimer’s patients, reinforcing the importance of astrocytic BCAA processing for neuronal support [9]. Together, this data and our present results argue for the broader and more integrated role of astrocytes in human BCAA catabolism, with potential implications for metabolic resilience in both health and disease.

Under hyperglycemia, enhanced glycolytic flux [52] and oxidative phosphorylation [53] underlie an increased level of reduced mitochondrial cofactors, e.g. NADH and FADH₂ as well as ATP, that can stimulate the activity of BCAA catabolic enzymes. Elevated oxaloacetate, supplied by pyruvate carboxylase activity [54], facilitates condensation with acetyl-CoA, promoting the integration of leucine-derived carbon into the TCA cycle. The shared initial steps of leucine, valine, and isoleucine catabolism mean that these effects likely extend to all BCAAs. Valine and isoleucine also produce propionyl-CoA, which enters the TCA cycle as succinyl-CoA; data from the Human Protein Atlas (www.proteinatlas.org/ENSG00000175198-PCCA and www.proteinatlas.org/ENSG00000114054-PCCB/) indicate the expression of propionyl-CoA carboxylase in both glia and neurons, suggesting that these anaplerotic routes are broadly available in the human brain.

These results have implications beyond hyperglycemia in diabetes. A dysregulated BCAA metabolism is implicated in Alzheimer’s disease, Huntington’s disease, and other neurodegenerative disorders [36, 40, 55–58]. By supporting TCA cycle function and glutamine synthesis, BCAAs could act as compensatory fuels under metabolic stress [40]. Further studies using functional assays in human neurons and astrocytes, ideally with patient-derived tissue or advanced co-culture/organoid models, are needed to validate these pathways under pathological conditions.

Beyond diabetes, a growing body of evidence implicates the dysregulation of BCAA metabolism in various neurodegenerative diseases [55, 57, 58], mostly marked by mitochondrial dysfunction and deteriorating energy homeostasis. Our demonstration of MCC expression in both astrocytes and human neurons underscores the potential extent of BCAA catabolism in the human brain, contrasting with rodent models where neuronal MCC expression is not expected [17]. Given that leucine, valine, and isoleucine share initial catabolic reactions and converge on key TCA intermediates, an enhanced appreciation of BCAA metabolism could inform strategies to mitigate metabolic deficits in disorders characterized by chronic hyperglycemia, oxidative damage, or impaired mitochondrial performance. By stabilizing or modulating these pathways, clinicians and researchers might better protect neuronal health in conditions ranging from poorly controlled diabetes to progressive neurodegenerative illnesses.

Although our work with two types of human astrocyte cultures offers valuable insights, in vitro systems cannot fully capture the complexity of the human brain, where numerous cell types, e.g., microglia, oligodendrocytes, and a plethora of multifaceted signaling and regulatory molecules could influence leucine metabolism. Future research should explore longer incubation periods, co-culture or organoid approaches, and potential multicellular interactions. Additionally, advanced proteomic or transcriptomic methods may further clarify how enzymes like MCC, PC, BCAT, and BCKDH are regulated in hyperglycemic environments or under different stress conditions. Lastly, examining human brain tissue—particularly originating from diabetic or hyperglycemic patients—may reveal how persistent hyperglycemia alters the expression of key enzymes involved in leucine catabolism.

The limitation of our study lies in the fact that the glucose concentrations used in our research reflect standard in vitro culture conditions rather than the typical physiological glucose levels present in the brain; however, 5 mM glucose may occur physiologically during states of hyperglycemia, and therefore our results should be interpreted as reflecting the impact of commonly used culture conditions with partial relevance to in vivo conditions.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our study demonstrates that human astrocytes efficiently metabolize leucine and significantly intensify its irreversible catabolism in response to hyperglycemia—a metabolic environment frequently encountered in individuals with poorly controlled diabetes. Moreover, the detection of MCC in both astrocytes and neurons underlines a potentially heightened reliance on branched-chain amino acid metabolism in the human brain, contrasting with rodent data. Alongside the recognized expression of pyruvate carboxylase, these findings support the concept that human neurons possess robust catabolic and anaplerotic pathways. The overlapping prime catabolic steps among all three BCAAs further suggest that propionyl-CoA production may play a role in sustaining neuronal and astrocytic metabolism. By illuminating the interplay between hyperglycemia and BCAA utilization, our work may guide the development of new strategies to address metabolic dysfunction in neurological disorders.
