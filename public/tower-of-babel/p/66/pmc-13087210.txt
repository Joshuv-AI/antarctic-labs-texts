Prognostic impact of neurological dysfunction assessed by modified Rankin Scale in acute myocardial infarction
Authors: Lianrong Feng, Miaohan Qiu, Luping He, Aiqin Dong, Huanhuan Ma, Guangren Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAcute myocardial infarction (AMI) patients with a prior stroke face higher in-hospital risks than those with AMI alone, yet the association between neurological status—assessed via the modified Rankin Scale (mRS)—and long-term mortality in AMI patients remains understudied. This retrospective study analyzed 10,084 AMI patients, categorizing them into three groups: no prior stroke, prior ischemic stroke (IS) with mRS 0–1 (no disability), or mRS 2–5 (disability), with mortality tracked via the China National Death Registration System over a median 5.26 years. Among the cohort, 8.9% (n = 893) had prior IS, and 18.1% (n = 1,829) died, including 1,454 cardiovascular deaths. Compared to non-stroke patients, those with prior IS and mRS 0–1 exhibited elevated all-cause (hazard ratio [HR] = 1.42, 95% CI 1.20–1.67) and cardiovascular mortality (HR = 1.46, 95% CI 1.21–1.76), while the mRS 2–5 group faced significantly higher risks (all-cause: HR = 2.18, 95% CI 1.84–2.60; cardiovascular: HR = 2.10, 95% CI 1.73–2.55), all P < 0.001. Prior IS, even without residual disability, is linked to increased long-term mortality in AMI patients, with the highest risk among those with post-stroke disability. Introduction

Ischemic heart disease and stroke remain the top two causes of age-standardized global deaths, a trend projected to persist through 20501,2. Sharing core risk factors (smoking, hypertension, dyslipidemia, diabetes), prior ischemic stroke (IS)/transient ischemic attack affects ~ 6% of acute coronary syndrome patients, linked to 68% higher all-cause mortality3, while 5.1%−10% of ST-elevation myocardial infarction(STEMI)/Non-ST-Elevation Myocardial Infarction(NSTEMI) patients have prior stroke, with increased short-term mortality and in-hospital main adverse cardiovascular events4,5.

As a leading cause of disability6, acute IS is commonly assessed by the modified Rankin Scale (mRS)—a validated tool for long-term mortality risk stratification7,8. However, clinical outcomes of acute myocardial infarction (AMI) patients with varying post-IS neurological disability (via mRS) remain understudied. This study thus explores the association between admission neurological disability and AMI prognosis, particularly whether prior IS without residual disability (mRS 0–1)9 increases mortality.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the study population

A total of 11,019 patients with AMI were initially enrolled, with 105 patients having a history of hemorrhagic stroke, 58 with a history of acute IS within the past month, and 772 who were not the first annual admissions, being excluded. Subsequently, 10,084 AMI patients were included in the final cohort for statistical analysis, among whom 893 (8.9%) had a history of IS. Within this subgroup, 566 patients scored mRS 0–1, while 327 patients scored mRS 2–5. Additionally, 9,191 patients (91.1%) had no history of IS. The study selection process is illustrated in Fig. 1.

All-cause mortality and cause of death

The details of all-cause mortality and cause of death during the follow-up period are summarized in Table 2. Figure 2 presents stacked bar charts displaying the all-cause death distribution in relation to neurological disability. Over a median follow-up of 5.26 years (interquartile range 3.58–6.69), the cumulative all-cause mortality rate was 18.1% (1829 out of 10,084 patients), with CVD deaths accounting for 14.4%, AMI deaths for 5.5%, stroke deaths for 2.6%, other CVD cause deaths for 6.3%, and non-CVD deaths for 3.7%. Compared to the non-stroke group, a higher rate of all-cause deaths was observed in both the mRS 0–1 (27.7% vs. 16.6%, P < 0.001) and mRS 2–5 (44.3% vs. 16.6%, P < 0.001) groups, with similar trends noted across different causes of death, including CVD, non-CVD, AMI, stroke and other CVD causes (all P < 0.001).

Cox proportional-hazards regression model and time interaction term model

The crude and adjusted hazard ratio (aHR) (95% CI) of mortality are presented in Table 3. Compared with patients in the non-stroke group, those in the mRS 0–1 group had a 42% higher risk of all-cause death (aHR = 1.42, 95%CI 1.20–1.67, P < 0.001). This increased all-cause death risk was primarily associated with a 46% higher risk of CVD death (aHR = 1.46, 95% CI 1.21–1.76, P < 0.001), a 61% higher risk of AMI death (aHR = 1.61, 95% CI 1.20–2.15, P < 0.001), and a 61% higher risk of stroke death (aHR = 1.61, 95% CI 1.05–2.46, P < 0.001). However, there was no significant difference in the hazard of other CVD cause death (aHR = 1.28, 95% CI 0.96–1.70, P = 0.10) or non-CVD death (aHR = 1.26, 95% CI 0.86–1.85, P = 0.24) for patients in the mRS 0–1 group compared with those in the non-stroke group.

In addition, the analysis revealed that poststroke disability (mRS 2–5) was also the significant prognostic risk factor for all-cause death (aHR = 2.18, 95%CI 1.84–2.60, P < 0.001) and causes of death listed in Table 3, including CVD (aHR = 2.10, 95%CI 1.73–2.55, P < 0.001), AMI (aHR = 1.95, 95%CI 1.41–2.70, P < 0.001), stroke (aHR = 3.09, 95%CI 2.05–4.66, P < 0.001), other CVD causes (aHR = 1.92, 95%CI 1.41–2.61, P < 0.001) and non-CVD cause (aHR = 2.51, 95%CI 1.72–3.66, P < 0.001).

The proportional hazards (PH) assumption of the Cox regression model was validated via Schoenfeld residual tests and graphical assessments, with results presented in Supplementary Tables 1–2 and Supplementary file 1 (Schoenfeld residual plots). The global Schoenfeld residual test yielded a chi-square statistic of 93.95 (P < 0.001), indicating that the PH assumption was violated for some covariates in the overall model. However, the primary variable of interest—mRS subgroups (non-stroke, mRS0-1, mRS2-5)—exhibited no evidence of PH assumption violation (χ² = 1.85, P = 0.396). Consistent with the statistical test results, graphical inspection of Schoenfeld residuals for mRS subgroups revealed no systematic trend when plotted against follow-up time, with residuals randomly distributed around the horizontal zero line.

For covariates that violated the PH assumption (e.g., statin, aspirin, STEMI, primary PCI), time-varying effect terms were incorporated into the model using the tt() function (interaction between covariates and follow-up time) to address the time dependence. Post-correction, Schoenfeld residual plots for these time-varying covariates showed no obvious trending patterns, confirming the effectiveness of the correction (Supplementary file 1). The stable PH assumption for mRS subgroups ensured that the HRs of all-cause mortality associated with mRS0-1 and mRS2-5 (relative to non-stroke) remained constant throughout the follow-up period. This validation supports the reliability and interpretability of the Cox model results regarding the association between mRS classification and all-cause mortality risk in AMI patients.

Fine-Gray competing risk analyses for mortality

This finding was verified by the Fine-Gray competing risk model, which accounted for non-CVD death as a competing event (Table 4; Fig. 4). In the unadjusted competing risk analysis, with the non-stroke group as the reference, the risk of CVD death was significantly elevated in the mRS 0–1 group (subdistribution HR [sHR] = 1.86, 95% CI 1.55–2.23, P < 0.001); a more pronounced elevation in CVD death risk was observed in the mRS 2–5 group (sHR = 3.16, 95% CI 2.60–3.85, P < 0.001). The unadjusted model exhibited good overall fit (pseudo-likelihood ratio test: χ² = 133, df = 2, P < 0.001).

Effect size interpretation

The 1-, 3-, and 5-year cumulative incidence rates of all-cause, CVD, non-CVD, AMI-specific, and stroke-specific mortality across the three study groups were quantified and presented in Supplementary Fig. 1, with the 5-year all-cause mortality rates documented as 16.6% in the non-stroke group, 26.6% in the mRS 0–1 group, and 41.5% in the mRS 2–5 group. Corresponding 5-year CVD mortality rates were 13.2%, 23.0%, and 33.8% for the three groups, while stroke-specific mortality at 5 years was 2.4%, 4.8%, and 8.7%, respectively. Absolute risk differences between each mRS group and the non-stroke reference group were calculated and illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 2, with the 5-year risk difference for all-cause mortality reaching approximately 10.0% in the mRS 0–1 group and 24.9% in the mRS 2–5 group. For CVD mortality, the 5-year risk differences were 9.8% and 20.6% in the mRS 0–1 and mRS 2–5 groups, respectively, whereas non-CVD mortality showed relatively modest 5-year risk differences of 1.7% and 6.1% in the two mRS groups. All cumulative incidence metrics and risk differences consistently exhibited a stepwise elevation from the non-stroke group to the mRS 0–1 group and further to the mRS 2–5 group across all follow-up intervals.

Quantitative bias analysis was performed by calculating E-values to assess the sensitivity of associations to unmeasured confounding, with results presented in Table 3. For all-cause mortality, E-values were 2.203 in the mRS 0–1 group and 3.308 in the mRS 2–5 group; corresponding E-values for CVD death were 2.299 and 3.647, respectively. For stroke-specific mortality, E-values were 2.571 (mRS 0–1) and 5.435 (mRS 2–5), while E-values for AMI death were 2.573 and 3.464 in the two mRS groups. E-values for non-CVD death were 1.774 (mRS 0–1) and 4.392 (mRS 2–5), and for other CVD causes were 1.920 and 3.255, respectively.

Confounding assessment and covariate adjustment

Substantial baseline differences were observed between baseline mRS groups and the Non-stroke reference group. These differences were quantified using standardized mean differences (SMD), with key imbalances (SMD > 0.1) identified in age (SMD = 0.351), hypertension (SMD = 0.294), heart failure (SMD = 0.230), primary PCI utilization (SMD = 0.179), and tirofiban administration (SMD = 0.170) (Table 1).

To mitigate baseline heterogeneity, multinomial propensity score inverse probability weighting (PS-IPW) was applied, with baseline mRS specified as the primary exposure and adjustment performed for all confounders with SMD > 0.1. Balancing effects of PS-IPW were evaluated using SMD (Supplementary Table 4). After weighting, all confounders except age (SMD = 0.127, approaching the 0.1 balance threshold) achieved SMD < 0.1, confirming effective reduction of baseline imbalance.

A directed acyclic graph (DAG) was constructed to clarify causal relationships between baseline mRS, potential confounders, treatment exposures, and all-cause mortality (Supplementary Fig. 3). In this framework, baseline characteristics including age, hypertension, and heart failure were identified as confounders (directly associated with both baseline mRS and mortality), while primary PCI, tirofiban, and aspirin were classified as treatment exposures (mediated by baseline mRS and directly associated with mortality). This causal structure guided covariate selection to avoid over-adjustment of mediators.

Three Cox proportional hazards models were constructed to assess the robustness of the association between baseline mRS and all-cause mortality (Supplementary Table 5):

Unadjusted model

Only baseline mRS was included as the exposure.

Conventional confounder-adjusted model

Baseline mRS was included alongside confounders (including: age, hypertension, primary PCI, tirofiban, heart failure, aspirin).

PS-IPW weighted model

PS-IPW was applied to balance baseline confounders prior to adjustment for baseline mRS and confounders.

Consistent hazard ratios (HRs) were observed across all models. For mRS 0–1 vs. non-stroke, HRs were 1.84 (95% CI, 1.559–2.166) in the unadjusted model, 1.46 (95% CI, 1.236–1.721) in the conventional adjusted model, and 1.44 (95% CI, 1.213–1.702) in the PS-IPW weighted model. For mRS 2–5 vs. non-stroke, HRs were 3.36 (95% CI, 2.837–3.990), 2.27 (95% CI, 1.906–2.695), and 2.56(95% CI, 2.108–3.106), respectively. All HRs were statistically significant (P < 0.001).

Sensitivity analyses

Stratified analyses showed a consistent trend of association between the baseline mRS 0–1 and all-cause mortality, except for sex (P for interaction = 0.008). Stratifying the participants according to sex, baseline mRs 0–1 had no prognostic value in females (aHR = 1.02, 95%CI 0.76–1.37). In male individuals, baseline mRs 0–1 remained significantly associated with all-cause death (aHR = 1.68, 95%CI 1.37–2.06). Sex also significantly modified the association between mRs 0–1 and CVD death (P for interaction = 0.03). Notably, age also modified the association between mRS 2–5 and both all-cause and CVD mortality, with more pronounced risks in younger patients (P for interaction < 0.05) (Figs. 5 and 6).

Results of Prespecified Subgroup Interaction Analyses

Prespecified subgroup interaction analyses were conducted based on the core model (adjusted for age stratification, hypertension, and heart failure), with False Discovery Rate (FDR) correction applied to control for multiple testing risk (Supplementary Fig. 4):

Age stratification (< 65 years vs. ≥ 65 years) subgroup

Compared with the Non-stroke group, the interaction HR for the mRS 0–1 subgroup was 0.794 (95%CI, 0.513–1.229), with an FDR-adjusted P-value of 0.452; the interaction HR for the mRS 2–5 subgroup was 0.411 (95% CI, 0.275–0.615), with an FDR-adjusted P-value < 0.001.

Sex (Female vs. Male) subgroup

The interaction HR for the mRS 0–1 subgroup was 0.565 (95% CI, 0.396–0.806), with an FDR-adjusted P-value of 0.006; the interaction HR for the mRS 2–5 subgroup was 1.116 (95% CI, 0.788–1.581), with an FDR-adjusted P-value of 0.627.

MI subtype (NSTEMI vs. STEMI) subgroup

The interaction HR for the mRS 0–1 subgroup was 1.086 (95% CI, 0.779–1.514), with an FDR-adjusted P-value of 0.627; the interaction HR for the mRS 2–5 subgroup was 1.316 (95% CI, 0.934–1.854), with an FDR-adjusted P-value of 0.232.

Results of the validation model (additionally adjusted for primary PCI, tirofiban, and aspirin on the basis of the core model) were consistent with those of the core model: the interaction HR for the age-stratified mRS 2–5 subgroup was 0.406 (95% CI, 0.271–0.607), with an FDR-adjusted P-value < 0.001; the interaction HR for the sex-stratified mRS 0–1 subgroup was 0.577 (95% CI, 0.405–0.822), with an FDR-adjusted P-value of 0.006; no substantial differences in interaction HRs or FDR-adjusted P-values were observed for other subgroups between the two models.

Misclassification risk of death causes across the three subgroups

Supplementary Table 6 summarizes the assessment of misclassification risk of death causes across the three subgroups. The mean follow-up time was approximately 8.2 years in all groups. The proportion of CVD death among all-cause death was 81.67% in the Non-stroke group, 82.16% in the mRS 0–1 group, and 81.25% in the mRS 2–5 group, with all subgroups classified as low misclassification risk (CVD death proportion ≥ 80%). The cumulative incidence curves (Supplementary Fig. 5) demonstrated that the mRS 2–5 subgroup had the highest cumulative incidence of CVD, non-CVD, and all-cause death throughout the follow-up period. Compared with the Non-stroke control group, the mRS 0–1 subgroup exhibited elevated cumulative incidence of CVD and all-cause death, while the non-CVD death cumulative incidence was moderately higher in both mRS subgroups relative to the Non-stroke group. All between-group differences in cumulative incidence of death outcomes were consistent with the predefined statistical analyses.

Discussion

In our study, we observed a significant association between a history of prior ischemic stroke in AMI patients and an increased risk of all-cause and cardiovascular mortality during long-term follow-up, regardless of the presence or absence of neurological disability. Cardiovascular diseases, particularly AMI and stroke, remained the primary causes of death in both AMI patients with and without prior ischemic stroke. Our findings make an important addition to the existing knowledge on the relationship between stroke history and prognosis in AMI patients, particularly in terms of the impact of neurological disability on mortality, long-term follow-up, the precise cause of death, and specific stroke types.

Previous research on the effect of prior IS on mortality in patients with AMI has been limited, with most evidence stemming from short-term follow-ups or studies lacking a definitive stroke type. For instance, Howard A. Cooper et al. found that patients with a prior stroke presenting with STEMI had a 60% higher risk of in-hospital death and a 50% higher risk of death within 30 days4. In the BleeMACS registry study, among 765 (5.0%) AMI patients with a prior stroke who underwent PCI during the index hospitalization, approximately 9.5% experienced death after a 1-year follow-up10. In our study, the cumulative all-cause mortality in AMI patients with prior IS during a median follow-up of 5.26 years was 18.1%.

A recent Brazilian stroke cohort study11 revealed that the mRS score 1 month after stroke was a good predictor of long-term mortality, which is crucial for guiding the functional rehabilitation of stroke patients. The study also found that patients with no or mild disability (mRS 0–2) had lower mortality risks, similar to our results.

The mRS 0–1 was a typically excellent functional outcome in the clinical trial12. In a prospective community-based study13, patients with IS who survived for 3 months were categorized into three groups based on a simplified mRS: no significant disability (mRS = 0–1), moderate disability (mRS = 2–3), and severe disability (mRS = 4–5). The Kaplan-Meier 7-year survival estimates for these groups were 67%, 50%, and 23%, respectively. For mRS scores of 2–3 versus 0–1, the HR for death at 3 months was 1.61 (95% CI, 1.10 to 2.38), and for mRS 4–5 versus 2–3, the HR was 2.20 (95% CI, 1.52 to 3.20). After adjustment, the HRs were 1.19 (95% CI, 0.77 to 1.84) and 1.87 (95% CI, 1.18 to 2.95), respectively. Survival duration of very old patients discharged in a state of mRS 0–1 was also longer than the remaining two groups at 47 ± 4.8 months (P < 0.001)14. These findings suggest that mRS 0–1 is associated with the best functional outcome compared to the other two groups.

However, these conclusions are drawn from comparisons among groups with different degrees of neural function impairment rather than compared with a control group without a history of stroke. In our AMI cohort, patients without disability (mRS 0–1) had a lower risk of all-cause and CVD mortality compared to those with disability (mRS ≥ 2) but a higher risk of death compared to patients without a previous stroke. Based on Cox regression analyses, we found that baseline mRS 0–1 increased the risk of all-cause mortality, primarily driven by CVD death but not non-CVD death. Additionally, baseline mRS 0–1 was only associated with higher risks of AMI and stroke death, not other CVD deaths.

Patients in the mRS 0–1 group may receive relatively insufficient attention to and intervention in their cardiovascular risk factors in clinical practice, a phenomenon potentially associated with the absence of obvious neurological symptoms. This hypothesis requires further verification in future studies by collecting data on risk factor management (e.g., blood pressure and blood glucose control) and medication adherence. In our study, higher rates of hypertension and diabetes mellitus, typical risk factors for cardiovascular diseases, were observed in both mRS 0–1 and mRS 2–5 groups compared to the non-stroke group. Given that hypertension control is a cornerstone of stroke prevention, a strict secondary prevention strategy to improve post-stroke hypertension management is needed15. Type 2 diabetes mellitus is significantly associated with a higher 5-year risk of first-time IS. Thus, the use of prophylactic cardiovascular medications, such as statins and antihypertensive drugs, may decrease this risk16.

Inflammatory markers, including interleukin-6 [IL-6], may reflect a pathophysiological process involved in the development of atherosclerosis17. Aging is associated with a chronic low-grade inflammatory state, and older patients with both AMI and IS had higher IL-6 and soluble tumor necrosis factor receptor I levels within the first 48 h, which were associated with a lower lymphocyte count and a higher neutrophil-lymphocyte ratio at 24 hours18. Therefore, in our cohort, the higher age, representing inflammation, in the mRS 0–1 and mRS 2–5 groups may partially explain the worse prognosis for these patients.

We found that patients with a history of stroke, including both mRS 0–1 and mRS.

2–5 groups, were less likely to receive guideline-directed therapy involving medicine and primary PCI. These results are fairly in line with previous publications. Both STEMI and NSTEMI in acute ischemic stroke are linked to increased mortality19,20. Despite this, previous stroke patients are less likely to receive guideline-based MI therapies5. Optimal medical therapy was provided to just half of the eligible patients, leaving room for substantial improvement10. This observation aligns with documented concerns about complications like bleeding, particularly intracranial bleeding, which are often associated with conservative treatment approaches for patients with a previous IS21. However, coronary artery revascularization therapies, including PCI and coronary artery bypass grafting, can improve the in-hospital and long-term outcomes for AMI patients with prior IS22. The risk of IS in PCI-treated AMI patients decreased after the use of ticagrelor23. A cohort study noted that the introduction of ticagrelor was linked to a lower rate of recurrent IS without an increase in intracranial bleeding among AMI patients with a history of IS21. Conversely, a recent retrospective cohort study indicated that the risks of cardiovascular events, including acute IS, were similar between ticagrelor plus aspirin and clopidogrel plus aspirin, but ticagrelor was associated with a higher risk of intracranial hemorrhage24. Therefore, more rigorous prospective randomized controlled trials are warranted.

A previous study revealed that the risk of IS was highest in the first 4 weeks and no longer significantly elevated 12 weeks after discharge from the MI hospitalization, indicating that the first 12 weeks after the index AMI might be the optimal time window for management25.

Our study also has some limitations. First, the retrospective nature of our study hampers the examination of specific details about previous ischemic stroke events, such as infarction size and location. These variables could potentially influence the observed outcomes, but due to data limitations from various local hospitals, we could not fully account for them. Second, as mRS scores were retrospectively extracted from medical records and further verified through manual telephone follow-up to confirm details of neurological function related to ischemic stroke, the lack of real-time assessment at admission may lead to potential misclassification bias; Third, given the high missing rates of Low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (33.99%) and Creatine kinase MB (29.59%), and complex statistical models, multiple imputation was avoided to reduce bias. Future research should aim for prospective data collection and address these confounding factors to better understand their impact on patient outcomes.

Conclusions

This study revealed a significant increase in the incidence of all-cause and cardiovascular disease mortality in acute myocardial infarction patients with a history of ischemic stroke, regardless of the mRS-assessed post-stroke disability status. Although baseline mRS assessment might not be a standard clinical practice, its relative ease of acquisition using available data suggests its potential utility in routine risk stratification for these patients. Future prospective studies are necessary to confirm our study’s findings and enhance our comprehension of how a history of stroke affects outcomes in AMI cases.

Methods

To ensure transparent and comprehensive reporting, this study adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines (Supplementary Appendix: STROBE/RECORD Checklist).

Study design and population

This retrospective cohort study involving patients with AMI was approved by the ethical committee of Cangzhou Central Hospital (2024-052-01). The requirement for informed consent was waived by the ethical committee of Cangzhou Central Hospital due to the retrospective nature of the study.

We identified AMI patients hospitalized at Cangzhou Central Hospital, China, from November 2015 through October 2021 by the electronic health records (EHRs) system. Our institution is a 5599-bed teaching hospital affiliated with Tianjin Medical University. Inclusion Criteria: (1) Patients hospitalized with AMI; (2) AMI diagnosis confirmed by ICD-10 codes (I21.0–I21.9) and clinical evidence (symptoms, electrocardiographic changes, elevated cardiac biomarkers); (3) Availability of complete EHRs data (including demographic information, comorbidities, treatment details) and linkage to the China National Death Registration System (Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention, CDC); Exclusion Criteria: (1) History of prior hemorrhagic stroke (ICD-10: I60–I62); (2) Acute IS within one month before the index AMI. They were excluded in view of the potential for confounding with the etiology of neurological dysfunction; Patients who developed AMI within 1 month of acute IS—when the stroke was still in the acute phase—were excluded to eliminate potential confounding. (3) If there were multiple hospital admissions within the same year, only the first admission was included, and all subsequent admissions were excluded26. We first screened potential patients with prior IS from the hospital’s EHRs using ICD-10 codes for ischemic stroke (I63.x). Subsequently, we conducted a manual review of each screened case’s original electronic medical records, confirming the diagnosis of prior IS based on core evidence including neuroimaging reports, clinical symptom documentation, and prior medical history. This two-step process ensures the accuracy and reliability of the prior IS diagnosis in our study cohort.

Definitions

Previous ischemic stroke was defined as sudden, nonconvulsive loss of neurological function due to brain ischemia confirmed by computed tomography or magnetic resonance imaging. AMI was diagnosed if the patient fulfilled 2 of the following 3 standards: chest pain continuing for > 30 min; dynamic alteration of ST-T appears in the electrocardiogram (ECG); and dynamic increases in troponin I or creatine kinase MB greater than three times the upper limit of normal value.

Exposure definition (admission mRS)

In the patients with prior ischemic stroke, mRS score for residual neurological disability after prior IS was calculated by trained registered nurses with ≥ 3 years of clinical experience in neurology/cardiology, specifically trained in mRS scoring through a standardized program:

Theoretical training: Review of mRS operational definitions (0–5 grades) and case vignettes to clarify distinctions between stroke-related residual disability (e.g., persistent hemiparesis) and acute AMI-related neurological manifestations (e.g., delirium).

Practical training: Scoring 50 anonymized patient records with known mRS outcomes, followed by calibration with a senior neurologist (κ = 0.89, indicating excellent inter-rater reliability).

Quality control: Random 10% of mRS scores were re-evaluated by the senior neurologist during data collection, with discrepancies resolved via consensus to ensure consistency.

The time since last stroke was extracted from EHRs, defined as the interval between the date of prior IS and the date of AMI admission. Median time since last stroke: 3.7 years (interquartile range: 1.2–6.5 years). No patients had prior IS within 1 month (per exclusion criteria), and 92.3% (n = 824) had prior IS > 6 months before AMI, ensuring that the assessed disability was stable and unrelated to acute stroke pathology.

Those patients were further divided into two groups: the mRS 0–1 group (without poststroke disability) and the mRS 2–5 group (with poststroke disability). Patients without prior ischemic stroke were identified as the non-stroke group (control group). The health records of all eligible patients were reviewed manually to confirm the accuracy of the determined group and collect the following data: socio-demographic information (e.g., age, medical history, medications in the hospital, operation information, and laboratory data. Diagnosis codes were used to identify comorbidities associated with both exposures (ischemic stroke and acute myocardial infarction). These included hypertension, diabetes, smoking, anemia, malignant tumor, heart failure, atrial flutter/fibrillation, chronic kidney disease, previous percutaneous coronary intervention, and previous coronary artery bypass graft. Medical data were collected from the EHRs data. The follow-up period for this study initiated on the date of acute myocardial infarction admission (not discharge). In-hospital deaths were fully included in the outcome analysis, with no exclusion. The research linked patients’ data to the China National Death Registration System (CDC) by unique national IDs to obtain accurate vital status, cause, and date of death for all patients. The CDC system collected all in-hospital and out-of-hospital deaths, covering nearly 99% of the countries and districts in China27.

Clinical outcomes

Our primary outcome of interest was all-cause death, and the second outcome was cardiovascular mortality (any cardiovascular diagnosis listed as the main cause of death). CVD death corresponds to ICD-10 codes I00-I99, while non-CVD death refers to all deaths excluding ICD-10 I00-I99;

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were compared by the Chi-squared test, and continuous variables were compared using the t-test or Mann–Whitney U-test as appropriate according to normality and homoscedasticity. We performed power analysis for the Log-Rank test, based on observed sample sizes (non-stroke: n = 9191; mRS 0–1: n = 566; mRS 2–5: n = 327), adjusted HRs (1.42/2.18), control event rate (16.6%), 5.26-year follow-up, and α = 0.05 (two-sided Bonferroni correction). Estimated power was 86.38% (mRS 0–1 vs. non-stroke) and 99.98% (mRS 2–5 vs. non-stroke), both exceeding 80%, confirming sufficient statistical power to detect hypothesized associations. Survival analyses were performed using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared with the log-rank test.

The proportional hazards assumption was formally assessed using Schoenfeld residual tests. Based on the results of the Schoenfeld residual test, choose the Cox model or the model with the time interaction term to report the association neurological disability status and the outcome of AMI. Fine-Gray subdistribution hazard models were used to analyze the data, with CVD deaths as the outcome event and non-CVD as the competing event.

To mitigate baseline confounding from key covariate imbalances (SMD > 0.1), we applied multinomial PS-IPW. Propensity scores were estimated using a multinomial logistic regression model with baseline mRS subgroups (non-stroke, mRS 0–1, mRS 2–5) as the dependent variable and imbalanced confounders as predictors. Covariate balance post-weighting was assessed via SMD, with SMD < 0.1 indicating adequate balance. Weighted Cox proportional hazards models were subsequently used to re-evaluate the association between mRS subgroups and all-cause mortality, confirming the robustness of primary findings. We explored the potential for unmeasured confounding between neurological disability status and mortality by calculating E-value. Prespecified subgroup modifiers include age stratification (< 65 vs. ≥ 65 years), sex (female vs. male), and MI subtype (STEMI vs. NSTEMI). All subgroup interaction analyses were performed with FDR correction to control for the risk of multiple testing. Tests of multiplication interactions for heterogeneity of the effect of neurological function in 2 strata were based on Wald chi-square tests. To verify the robustness of subgroup interaction effects, we performed prespecified subgroup analyses using two models: a core model adjusting for key confounders (age, hypertension, heart failure) and a validation model further adjusting for treatment variables (primary PCI, tirofiban, and aspirin), as depicted in Supplementary Fig. 4. Analyses and figures were performed using SPSS version 27 (IBM Corporation), PASS 15.0.2 and R Studio Version 4.3.3 (RStudio, PBC). Statistical significance was assumed at P < 0.05.
