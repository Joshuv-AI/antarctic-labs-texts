HALP score: A powerful and promising index for predicting prognosis among hypertension adults
Authors: Min Deng, Qian Wu, Dongliang Liu, Xiaohua Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Previous reports have highlighted that inflammation and poor nutritional condition are closely related to a poor prognosis for hypertension. The hemoglobin, albumin, lymphocyte, and platelet (HALP) score is a promising index to reflect potential interaction and synergistic effects between inflammation and nutrition. Our research objective is to excavate the HALP score and specific mortality outcomes of hypertensive adults. This study was a linked-mortality cohort analysis based on participants from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey 1999 to 2018. The HALP score was calculated based on 4 blood parameters, and hypertension was determined by blood pressure measurement, questionnaire, and prescription. All-cause mortality, cardiovascular disease (CVD) mortality, and cancer mortality were defined as the primary outcomes of this study. Cox regression analyses were conducted to estimate the hazard ratio (HR) and 95% confidence interval (CI). Restricted cubic spline analysis and subgroup analyses were performed to assess the robustness of our findings. Our research enrolled 20,256 hypertensive adults (mean age = 53.78 ± 0.22 years), including 10,808 (53.36%) males and 9448 (46.64%) females. During 189,593 person-years of follow-up, 4432 all-cause deaths were documented, with 1227 attributed to CVD and 966 to cancer. When compared with hypertensive participants in the lowest quartile (Q1) of the HALP score, those in the highest quartile (Q4) exhibited a 30% reduced risk of all-cause mortality (HR = 0.70, 95% CI = 0.64–0.77, P trend < .001), a 39% lower risk of CVD mortality (HR = 0.61, 95% CI = 0.49–0.76, P trend < .001), and a 22% lower risk of cancer mortality (HR = 0.78, 95% CI = 0.63–0.96, P trend = .086), respectively. Restricted cubic spline analysis revealed an L-shaped association between the HALP score and each of these mortality outcomes. Higher HALP scores correlated with lower risks of mortality from all causes, CVD, and cancer among adults with hypertension. These findings implicate that the HALP score could be a promising and powerful marker to predict prognosis among hypertensive adults in clinical practice.

1. Introduction

Hypertension, a major contributor to mortality from all causes and cardiovascular disease (CVD), remains a persistent and challenging global public health problem, with an estimation of 1.56 billion in 2025.[1–5] Over the past decade, the United States has seen a rise in mortality rates linked to hypertension – specifically for cancer and CVD – despite a decline in the share of adults with controlled blood pressure.[6,7] Hypertension contributes to both healthcare challenges and economic burdens. While various clinical factors linked to adverse outcomes in hypertension have been explored,[8–12] identifying an optimal, readily quantifiable, and comprehensive prognostic indicator remains crucial for enhanced clinical management.

Previous studies have demonstrated that inflammation is partly responsible for blood pressure elevation and end-organ damage associated with diseases.[13–15] Specifically, C-reactive protein (CRP), a typical acute-phase reactant, correlates with heightened vascular stiffness, accelerated atherosclerosis, and an elevated risk of cardiovascular events in hypertensive individuals.[16] Furthermore, various inflammatory composite indicators, including neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio, monocyte-to-lymphocyte ratio, platelet-lymphocyte ratio, and inflammatory prognostic scoring system, have been linked to a higher risk of CVD and overall mortality.[12,17] Similarly, malnutrition is not uncommon and contributes to low survival in hypertensive patients.[18] Lower serum creatinine/cystatin C ratio is an independent risk factor for poor prognosis in hypertensive patients.[11] The controlling nutritional status (CONUT) score, an alternative nutritional assessment tool, has demonstrated prognostic value in predicting early mortality risk in geriatric populations with hypertension.[12] Nutritional intervention is also essential in blood pressure control.[12,19] However, these previously reported markers mainly reflect selected dimensions of disease status. CRP and leukocyte-based indices primarily capture inflammatory activity, whereas CONUT is more focused on nutritional assessment, and the creatinine/cystatin C ratio is more closely related to renal function and muscle-related physiology. In contrast, the interaction between inflammation and nutritional status is increasingly recognized as clinically relevant in hypertension, as these 2 processes often coexist and may reinforce one another.[20] Therefore, a composite index that simultaneously incorporates multiple pathophysiological dimensions may provide broader prognostic information than single-domain markers alone.

The hemoglobin, albumin, lymphocyte, and platelet (HALP) score, calculated as hemoglobin (g/L) × albumin (g/L) × lymphocyte (/L) / platelet (/L), integrates HALP into a single composite index. As such, it may simultaneously reflect anemia-related physiological reserve, nutritional status, immune function, and systemic inflammatory burden. To some extent, lower HALP values may indicate a less favorable systemic state characterized by lower hemoglobin, hypoalbuminemia, lymphopenia, and/or relatively higher platelet counts, thereby suggesting reduced physiological reserve, poorer nutritional status, impaired immune competence, and a more adverse inflammatory or prothrombotic milieu. Compared with previously reported markers, HALP may offer complementary and potentially broader prognostic information. Since it was first proposed in 2015, HALP has been widely investigated in oncology as a diagnostic and prognostic marker.[21–26] More recently, emerging evidence has suggested that HALP is associated with prognosis in several noncancer conditions, including acute ischemic stroke, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, heart failure, and coronary heart disease (CHD).[27–31]

Nevertheless, the prognostic significance of the HALP score in adults with hypertension has not yet been established. Biologically, lower HALP values may reflect a less favorable systemic state characterized by poorer nutritional reserve, impaired immune status, reduced anemia-related physiological reserve, and greater inflammatory burden. In addition, because age, sex, obesity, diabetes, smoking, and blood pressure control are clinically relevant factors that may influence prognosis in hypertension, they are considered potential effect modifiers of this association. To address these knowledge gaps, we investigated the association between the HALP score and mortality outcomes among adults with hypertension using data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES). On this basis, we hypothesized that a higher HALP score would be associated with lower risks of all-cause, CVD, and cancer mortality in this population.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Study design and population

Administered by the National Center for Health Statistics, NHANES is a biennial, nationally replicated cross-sectional program designed to investigate the risk factors and prevalence of common diseases. This rigorous epidemiological program use standardized protocols where certified healthcare professionals systematically gather multidimensional data encompassing: sociodemographic characteristics; standardized anthropometric measurements; comprehensive medical records; clinical laboratory biomarkers; and quantifiable dietary intake assessments using validated methodologies. Detailed survey methodology, code, and additional technical documentation could be found at https://wwwn.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/Default.aspx.

This study was a linked-mortality cohort analysis based on participants from NHANES 1999 to 2018. Briefly, among the 101,316 individuals enrolled in NHANES 1999 to 2018, participants were excluded sequentially according to the following criteria: individuals without hypertension (n = 73,158); those younger than 20 years (n = 1235); and participants missing data on HALP score (n = 1799), follow‐up information (n = 36), or other potential confounding covariates (n = 4832). Finally, 20,256 subjects were enrolled in the current analysis (Fig. 1).

Flowchart of the study design. NHANES = National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey.

2.2. Hypertension definition

A standardized protocol and set of procedures for blood pressure assessment is available through NHANES. Blood pressure was measured by trained personnel following the standardized NHANES protocol. After 5 minutes of seated rest, participants underwent 3 consecutive blood pressure measurements during the examination, with a fourth measurement attempted if a previous reading was interrupted or incomplete. For this study, average systolic blood pressure (SBP) and average diastolic blood pressure (DBP) were calculated as the mean of all available valid readings.[32] For the current study, the diagnosis of hypertension was based on at least one of the 3 conditions outlined below[33–35]: participants completed a blood pressure questionnaire that included the question: “Has a doctor or other health professional ever told you that you have hypertension?”; Attainment of an average SBP equal to or exceeding 140 mm Hg, or an average DBP equal to or surpassing 90 mm Hg; and participants currently used any antihypertensive drugs. As defined in this study, uncontrolled hypertension was characterized by SBP ≥140 mm Hg or DBP ≥90 mm Hg, regardless of antihypertensive medication use.

2.3. Mortality outcomes

Mortality status was ascertained through linkage to the NHANES public-use linked-mortality file. All-cause mortality was defined as death from any cause. CVD death included rheumatic heart disease, hypertensive heart and renal disease, ischemic heart disease, and heart failure. Cancer death was defined as death from malignant neoplasms. Because the public-use linked mortality file for 2015 to 2018 NHANES provides only heart disease and cancer cause-specific categories, participants from these cycles were included only in the corresponding cause-specific analyses available in the public-use data. The duration of follow-up was determined as the time elapsed from the interview date to the date of death or through December 31, 2019.[36] Leading causes of death were established in line with the International Classification of Diseases, 10th revision. Detailed death causes and codes were shown in Table S1, Supplemental Digital Content 1.

2.4. Covariates assessment

Guided by previous research and clinical experience, we selected appropriate covariates linked to hypertension. Information regarding age, gender, race/ethnicity, education level, marital status, and family poverty income ratio (PIR) was gathered through household interviews. Race/ethnicity was classified into non-Hispanic Black, non-Hispanic White, Mexican American, and other races. PIR was divided into 3 categories: <1.30, 1.30–3.49, and ≥3.50. Marital status was grouped into married, unmarried, and others. Education was split into 5 tiers: <9th grade, 9th to 11th grade, high school, some college, and college or above. Moreover, smoke, congestive heart failure (CHF), CHD, stroke, and hyperlipidemia were evaluated via a set of self-report questionnaires. Following the definition of alcohol consumption from prior literature, we stratified all hypertension individuals into 4 categories: never consumed alcohol (<12 drinks in a lifetime), former consumers (≥12 drinks in 1 year and no drinking in the previous year), mild consumers (≤1 drink/day for women or ≤2 drinks/day for men), and heavy consumers (≥2 drinks/day for women or ≥3 drinks/day for men).[37] Diabetes was identified as a doctor’s diagnosis of the condition, current use of diabetes medications, or ongoing insulin therapy.[38] In addition, we utilized the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration equation to determine the estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR).[39] Details of these questionnaires and formulas could be found in Table S2, Supplemental Digital Content 2.

2.5. Statistical analyses

Missing data were handled using a complete-case approach in the primary analysis, and all data in the current study were accounted for complex survey design according to NHANES analytic guidelines.[40] To be specific, the 10-year sample weights for NHANES 1999 to 2018 were calculated to reflect a nationally representative sample. All analyses incorporated the NHANES masked variance pseudo-strata (SDMVSTRA), masked variance pseudo-primary sampling units (SDMVPSU), and mobile examination center (MEC) sampling weights in accordance with National Center for Health Statistics analytic guidance. A combined 20-year MEC examination weight was constructed as follows: for participants from 1999 to 2002, the 4-year MEC weight (WTMEC4YR) was multiplied by 1/5; for participants from each subsequent 2-year cycle from 2003 to 2018, the 2-year MEC weight (WTMEC2YR) was multiplied by 1/10. Weighted estimates were intended to represent the average US civilian noninstitutionalized population over the combined survey period, rather than a cumulative population count across 1999 to 2018.

HALP score was analyzed both as a continuous variable and as a categorical variable. For categorical analyses, participants were categorized into quartiles according to the weighted distribution of HALP values, with the first quartile (Q1) used as the reference group. Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard error, and categorical variables were expressed as frequency and percentage. To compare differences in baseline characteristics among HALP quartile groups, one-way analysis of variance was used for continuous variables, whereas chi-square tests were applied for categorical variables. Multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression models were applied to compute the hazard ratio (HR) and 95% confidence interval (CI) between HALP score and specific mortality. The initial model (model 1) examined crude associations without any covariate adjustment. Model 2 incorporated basic demographic adjustments including age, gender, race/ethnicity, education, marital status, and PIR. Model 3 was further adjusted for all covariates included in model 2, along with body mass index (BMI), diabetes, smoking status, alcohol consumption status, CHF, CHD, stroke, hypotensive medication use, eGFR, and hyperlipidemia. A linear trend was assessed by treating HALP score as an ordinal variable in these regression models. Moreover, restricted cubic spline (RCS) was carried out to visualize the nonlinear association between the independent variable and the dependent variable. In this model, multivariable adjusted analyses with 4 knots were used because the Akaike information criteria value we calculated at this time was relatively minimal.

Several sensitivity analyses were performed to assess the robustness of the findings. First, considering the effects of different blood pressure control on mortality, we conducted sensitivity analyses to explore whether the association was robust among adults with controlled and uncontrolled hypertension. Second, we conducted another sensitivity analysis using a new hypertensive cutoff value of 130/80 mm Hg in accordance with the American Heart Association guideline.[41] Third, although the majority of variables exhibited minor degrees of data incompleteness, multiple imputation analysis was used to examine whether the main findings were robust. Detailed missing variables were shown in Table S3, Supplemental Digital Content 3. Subgroup analyses stratified by age, sex, obesity, diabetes, and smoking were conducted because these variables were considered clinically relevant factors that might modify the association between HALP score and mortality among adults with hypertension. Besides, the interactions between HALP score and stratified variables were also detected.

Last, in current study, R software (version 4.1.2; R Core Team, The R Foundation for Statistical Computing) was used to analyze all data, and P < .05 was defined as statistical significance. Because of the multiple statistical tests we carried out, we applied Bonferroni correction to address multiple testing. Thus, we divided the significance level of P = .05 by 4, resulting in a significance level adjusted for multiple comparisons.

3.2. HALP score and specific mortality

Over a follow-up period of 189,593 person-years, 4432 all-cause deaths were recorded, with 1227 being CVD deaths and 966 being cancer deaths. Detailed results are shown in Table 2. We established 3 weighted multivariable Cox regression models to determine the associations of HALP score with mortality outcomes. In the crude model (model 1), hypertensive individuals with the highest HALP score showed an inverse association with all-cause, CVD, and cancer mortality, with respective HRs (95% CIs) of 0.62 (0.56–0.69, P trend < .001), 0.54 (0.44–0.66, P trend < .001), and 0.78 (0.63–0.97, P trend = .055). Comparable findings were observed in the fully adjusted model (model 3). In comparison to the lowest quartile (Q1) of HALP score, the highest quartile (Q4) among hypertensive subjects was associated with a 30% lower all-cause mortality risk (HR = 0.70, 95% CI = 0.64–0.77, P trend < .001), a 39% lower CVD mortality risk (HR = 0.61, 95% CI = 0.49–0.76, P trend < .001), and a 22% lower cancer mortality risk (HR = 0.78, 95% CI = 0.63–0.96, P trend = .086), respectively. In addition, RCS further demonstrated an L-shaped curve linking HALP score to each mortality type, suggesting that the association with mortality risk plateaus in the higher HALP exposure range (all P for nonlinearity < .001; Fig. 2). Moreover, Kaplan–Meier curves revealed that survival probability was lower as HALP score increased (Fig. 3).

3.3. Subgroup and sensitivity analyses

To detect whether the associations of HALP score with all-cause mortality as well as CVD mortality remained robust across subgroups stratified by age (<60, ≥60), gender (male, female), BMI (<30, ≥30), diabetes (yes, no), and smoking status (yes, no), subgroup analyses were performed (Fig. 4). In most subgroups, the results were consistent. For subgroup analyses for CVD mortality, we noted that a higher HALP score was only linked to a reduction in CVD mortality among nonsmoking participants. Moreover, no significant interactions were observed among these subgroups. To enhance the robustness of our findings, several sensitivity analyses were performed. Table 3 indicated that a higher HALP score decreased the risk of all-cause and CVD mortality, irrespective of blood pressure control. In contrast, a higher HALP score showed no significant association with decreased cancer mortality among adults with uncontrolled hypertension. Moreover, when the new cutoff value of hypertension was selected as 130/80 mm Hg, the relationship of HALP with 3 different mortalities was still significant (Table S4, Supplemental Digital Content 4). Finally, the results of the multiple imputation analysis were broadly consistent with those of complete-case analysis, supporting the robustness of the main findings (Table S5, Supplemental Digital Content 5).

4. Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study to assess the progStratified by HALP score quartiles, a higher HALP score exhibited a significantly reduced risk of all-cause, CVD, and cancer mortality, even with adjustment for potential confounding factors. For hypertensive adults, all-cause, CVD, and cancer mortality demonstrated an L-shaped association with the HALP score. In addition, sensitivity analysis of blood pressure control status showed that CVD and all-cause mortality remained reduced in the higher HALP group. Furthermore, subgroup analysis demonstrated that HALP can be used for the prediction of overall survival regardless of age, gender, BMI, diabetes, or smoking.

Historically, hypertension has long been regarded as an independent risk factor for the development and prognosis of CVD, impacting approximately 116 million adults in the United States.[42] Among patients with hypertension, systemic inflammation and nutrition are risk factors contributing to poor prognosis and survival.[43] However, many studies have investigated the effect of single nutritional or inflammatory indicators on hypertension, while few studies have adopted a more comprehensive index to evaluate the prognosis of the disease. Malnutrition was common and showed poor clinical outcomes in hypertensive patients,[12,18,44] and a healthy and balanced diet was critical to blood pressure level control.[19] As one of the nutritional indicators,[45] serum albumin stands out because of its anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and antiplatelet aggregation properties. Hypoalbuminemia was proven to be associated with vascular endothelial dysfunction which may lead to the emergence and worsening of hypertension.[46,47] Besides, anemia can increase the preload and decrease the afterload in hypertensive individuals, resulting in increased cardiac output and left ventricular hypertrophy. It has been determined to be a risk factor for both CVD outcomes and all-cause mortality in individuals with hypertension.[31,48]

Meanwhile, previous studies indicate that oxidative stress and inflammation lead to compromised microvascular and macrovascular structures and functions, triggering a vicious cycle of escalating blood pressure and increased vascular stiffness.[13–15] To some extent, serum albumin, lymphocyte, and platelet counts could reflect the body’s inflammatory state. A cohort study from China observed that the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio played an essential part in incident hypertension, especially in male or elderly individuals.[17] Further studies found that the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio was able to forecast all-cause mortality over a 90-day period in individuals with hypertension.[12] An NHANES 1999 to 2010 study constructed an inflammatory prognostic scoring system incorporating lactate dehydrogenase, alkaline phosphatase, lymphocytes, neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio, monocyte-to-lymphocyte ratio, red cell distribution width, and systemic inflammatory response index, and additionally identified that elevated inflammatory prognostic scores were linked to adverse long-term outcomes among individuals with hypertension. In addition, inflammation has an effect on insulin resistance, appetite, and albumin consumption, in which decomposed cytokines can delay gastric emptying, control food intake, and affect muscle catabolism.[49] In addition, anemia and malnutrition, in turn, affect inflammation, producing a vicious cycle that contributes to disease progression.[45,50] Compared with these previously reported markers, the HALP score may offer additional value because it integrates HALP into a single composite index, thereby simultaneously reflecting anemia-related status, nutritional reserve, immune function, and systemic inflammatory burden. In contrast, markers such as CRP and neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio mainly reflect inflammation, whereas CONUT focuses more on nutritional status, and the creatinine/cystatin C ratio is more related to renal and muscle-related physiology. Because adverse outcomes in hypertension are often driven by the interaction of multiple systemic processes rather than a single biological pathway, HALP may provide broader and more clinically relevant prognostic information. Moreover, all 4 components of HALP are routinely measured in clinical practice, making it a convenient and potentially practical marker for risk stratification among adults with hypertension.

Our results are biologically plausible. In hypertensive patients, a low HALP score – driven by anemia, hypoalbuminemia, and lymphopenia – reflects combined immune deficiency and malnutrition. Anemia increases myocardial oxygen demand and promotes left ventricular hypertrophy, accelerating heart failure and atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease.[51] Hypoalbuminemia indicates visceral protein depletion, linked to endothelial dysfunction, vascular leakage, and heightened systemic inflammation.[52] Lymphopenia denotes impaired peripheral immunity and persistent low-grade inflammation, which fails to clear vascular mediators, thereby fostering vascular remodeling, plaque instability, and adverse events.[53] Concurrently, modestly elevated platelets may potentiate a prothrombotic milieu, raising stroke and infarction risks. Conversely, a moderately increased HALP score signifies preserved nutrition, competent cellular immunity, and reduced inflammatory burden: adequate albumin maintains endothelial integrity, normal lymphocytes sustain immune surveillance, and sufficient hemoglobin ensures tissue oxygenation. This homeostatic state counteracts the pro-atherosclerotic and pro-inflammatory effects of chronic hypertension, stabilizing prognosis and yielding a plateau in mortality risk.

HALP can reflect both systemic inflammation and nutritional status.[21] Numerous studies have demonstrated that HALP is capable of predicting the prognosis of certain cancers, including breast, gastric, colorectal, and bladder cancers.[21–25,54] In addition, HALP is increasingly used to evaluate the prognosis of noncancerous conditions like acute ischemic stroke,[28] chronic obstructive pulmonary disease,[27] acute pancreatitis,[55] heart failure,[29,30] and CHD.[31] A prior study demonstrated that the index, not the HALP score, but the modified HALP score, played a notable prognostic role in 3-month mortality among acute heart failure patients.[29] Subsequently, in heart failure patients, Liu et al[30] confirmed the link between HALP and mortality through a larger sample size and extended follow-up period. They found that the HALP score was a more reliable predictor of mortality than the prognostic nutritional index, CRP, and the Meta-Analysis Global Group in Chronic Heart Failure risk score. In addition, a retrospective cohort investigation that utilized the NHANES database (2003–2018) identified that the HALP score possessed potential predictive value regarding the prognosis of CHD, and the area under the curve was 0.61.[31] Another NHANES study focusing on the general population has demonstrated an independent association between HALP score and both all-cause and long-term cardiovascular mortality. Within the hypertension subgroup, the link was likewise noted in all-cause mortality; however, no comparable results were reported in cardiovascular mortality.[56] This might stem from the relatively limited number of hypertensive patients suffering from cardiovascular mortality. Our present study focuses on the relationship among HALP score and prognosis in American hypertensive adults, and we found that HALP was inversely correlated with CVD, cancer, and all-cause mortality. More essentially, HALP was an independent prognostic factor in this study population after fully adjusting the potential influencing factors, suggesting that the HALP score can be a credible, cheap, readily available predictor to assess the risk of death in hypertensive individuals. Nonetheless, extant literature has yet to demonstrate a noteworthy predictive benefit of HALP score in comparison to single or composite biomarkers. To prove the potential for predictive value, further investigation through prospective cohort studies is necessary.

Few studies have provided insight into the link between HALP and specific mortality endpoints in adults with hypertension. Tian et al[28] have identified adverse outcomes in patients suffering from acute ischemic stroke that showed a J-shaped association with the HALP score, suggesting that HALP may be a good predictor of recurrent stroke and death. In the present work, RCS analysis described a nonlinear L-shaped correlation between the HALP score and mortality risk. As the HALP score increased, mortality risk declined; yet, once the score reached a specific threshold, mortality risk exhibited no significant change. Nevertheless, the underlying mechanism of this nonlinear relationship is yet to be clarified. A secondary analysis conducted on a multicenter trial demonstrated that nutritional support did not improve 30-day mortality in hospitalized patients at nutritional risk who had high inflammatory levels at admission,[49] which may explain this. Considered together, nutritional support and inflammation control may be of great importance during the personalized management of hypertension. Further studies will be required to confirm these associations and uncover the underlying biological mechanisms.

Researchers in previous studies have posited that hypertension has a link to cancer, but they have reached conflicting conclusions. Some argued that hypertension resulted from malignancy, putting forward the hypothesis that some cancers might produce hormones that raise blood pressure,[57,58] whereas other studies contended that hypertension might serve as a risk factor for cancer and cancer mortality.[59,60] According to a meta-analysis, patients with high blood pressure tend to die from cancer, specifically renal cell carcinoma.[20] In our stratified analysis, HALP can be used to predict all-cause mortality regardless of age, gender, or BMI. However, among nonsmokers, elevated HALP levels were solely linked to a lessened risk of CVD mortality. When analyzing how blood pressure control status relates to adverse outcomes, we observed that cancer mortality in patients with inadequate blood pressure control was not related to higher HALP scores. This could be attributed to the notion that poor control of blood pressure may have a greater impact on cancer mortality than nutritional and inflammatory status. These findings may help us in the stratified management and treatment of patients with hypertension.

Our research has several notable strengths. First, we conducted a study with a large sample size and 189,593 person-years of follow-up, which is the first to investigate the association between HALP score and specific mortality outcomes of hypertensive adults. Second, NHANES recruited participants across the country, so our results are representative of American adults. Third, the results and conclusions in our study are rational and reliable, because confounding factors were minimized by using Cox proportional hazards regressions and RCS.

However, our study also has certain limitations. First, the HALP score did not perform dynamic measurements to more profoundly reflect individuals’ inflammation and nutrition. Second, there may be recall bias because some measures, including the diagnosis of hypertension, CHF, and stroke, were self-reported by the participants. Third, although we have tried to adjust for multiple factors in different models, there were still possible unmeasured confounders that may affect the results.

Subsequent research should prioritize widening the scope of research to include populations across different ethnic backgrounds, dietary habits, and socioeconomic positions. It is critical for demonstrating the HALP score’s generalizability as a prognostic marker across diverse demographic groups. Furthermore, investigating correlations between the HALP score and other biomarkers of hypertension and CVD is of great importance – it may contribute to a more all-encompassing and clinically practical risk stratification model for clinical application. Besides, conducting longitudinal research that tracks temporal changes in the HALP score and their links to mortality in hypertensive patients remains essential. Finally, future large-scale, multicenter prospective studies with diverse populations are warranted to validate the clinical applicability, predictive stability, and optimal cutoff values of the HALP score for hypertensive risk stratification. These studies could deliver vital insights into the complex interactions between inflammation, nutrition, and the management of hypertension, further informing treatment strategies and improving patient outcomes.

5. Conclusion

In conclusion, a higher HALP score was related to a lower risk for all-cause, CVD, and cancer mortality outcomes in adults with hypertension. These findings implicate that the HALP score, a competent nutrition-inflammation index, could be a promising and powerful marker to predict prognosis among hypertensive adults in clinical practice. More rigorously designed cohort research is necessary to verify these associations.
