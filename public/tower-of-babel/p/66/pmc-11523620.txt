Limited gut bacterial response of tuatara (Sphenodon punctatus) to dietary manipulation and captivity
Authors: Carmen Hoffbeck, Danielle R M L Middleton, Susan N Keall, Chye-Mei Huang, An Pas, Kate Irving, Nicola J Nelson, Michael W Taylor
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe bacteria of a host’s digestive tract play crucial roles in digestion and pathogen resistance. Hosts living in captivity often have more human interaction and antibiotic use, in addition to differences in diet and environment, compared to their wild counterparts. Consequently, wild and captive animals frequently harbour different bacterial communities. We tested whether diversity of diet provided in captivity shifts the gut bacteria of tuatara, an endemic New Zealand reptile, at three captive sites, and examined how the gut community of these tuatara compares to those in the wild. Dietary manipulation did not cause a strong overall shift in tuatara gut bacteria, but individual tuatara did experience bacterial shifts during manipulation, which subsequently reverted after manipulation. We found that Bacteroides, a genus common in most vertebrate guts but rare in tuatara, increased significantly in the gut during manipulation, then decreased post-manipulation. Finally, the gut bacteria of captive tuatara significantly differed from those of wild tuatara, though most of the dominant bacterial genera found in wild tuatara persisted in captive tuatara. This work represents a first investigation of the captive tuatara bacterial community and establishes the sensitivity of the gut community to dietary manipulation and captivity for this relict reptile.Keywords: reptile, captivity, diet, bacterial community

Introduction

The bacteria and other microorganisms living inside of animal hosts—collectively called the microbiota—participate in many functions for their host, particularly in digestion (McFall-Ngai et al. 2013, Colston and Jackson 2016, Song et al. 2020). Bacteria in vertebrate digestive tracts break down polysaccharides and other large molecules into forms digestible by the host (Wilson et al. 2020, Cheng et al. 2022) and participate in pathogen resistance (Kau et al. 2011, Thaiss et al. 2016, Shi et al. 2017, Zheng et al. 2020), both of which heavily influence host health. Though the number of bacterial taxa populating the digestive tract varies among and within host species, for some omnivorous host species, greater diversity in the gut microbiota is associated with a number of positive health outcomes (Xu and Knight 2015), while lower gut diversity is sometimes associated with disease (Zhou et al. 2020, San Juan et al. 2021). More diverse gut communities may confer defense against invading microbial pathogens (Dillon et al. 2005, Kamada et al. 2013, Pickard et al. 2017) and against otherwise commensal species, which can become pathogenic at high abundance (Pickard et al. 2017). While the link between gut diversity and health varies between host species, diverse gut communities can generally also break down a wider range of molecules and utilize a greater number of food sources for the host (Wallace 2008, Kriss et al. 2018). In vertebrates, gut diversity is influenced by host diet (Youngblut et al. 2019, Jin et al. 2021, Du et al. 2022), environment (Rothschild et al. 2018, Chiu et al. 2020, Zhang et al. 2021), and interactions with other hosts (Greenhalgh et al. 2016, Rothschild et al. 2018), all of which can factor into which bacterial species are present in the gut and at what abundance.

Captive management is an important tool for conservation of many species (McKenzie et al. 2017, Tang et al. 2020). As habitat degradation and climate change continue to threaten both local and global populations (Böhm et al. 2013, Pacifici et al. 2015), bringing animals into captivity is a conservation practice that will likely only increase in coming years (Keulartz 2015, Dallas and Warne 2023). However, in captivity, animals usually encounter a different environment and diet to what they would experience in the wild. Captivity in zoos or similar facilities often involves more interaction with humans and denser living conditions, both of which can impact overall animal health (Michel et al. 2016, Meadows et al. 2018) as well as the gut microbiota (McKenzie et al. 2017, West et al. 2019). Many species living in captivity have a less diverse microbiota than those in the wild (Krynak et al. 2017, Wasimuddin et al. 2017), and if they fall sick many are given antibiotics, which may also affect the microbiota (West et al. 2019, 2022). As a result, considering the gut microbiota in endangered species conservation has received increasing focus in recent years (West et al. 2019).

The composition and functions of the gut microbiota have arguably been somewhat understudied for reptiles, particularly compared to their mammalian counterparts (Hoffbeck et al. 2023). Sixteen percent of known reptile species are threatened with extinction (Böhm et al. 2013), with consequences from increasing global temperatures being particularly dire for temperature-determined sex determination (Valenzuela et al. 2019) and for reptile thermal tolerance (Bogert 1949, Towns et al. 2016). In addition to these threats, warming has been shown to shift the gut microbiota in certain reptile species (Moeller et al. 2020, Sepulveda and Moeller 2020), potentially compounding the negative impact of warming temperatures. In some reptiles, the gut microbiota of captive individuals differs from wild individuals of the same species (Kohl et al. 2017, Madison et al. 2018, Tang et al. 2020, Zhou et al. 2020), sometimes with deleterious impacts (Zhou et al. 2020, Eliades et al. 2021). The reptile microbiota will likely be an increasingly important and commonly utilized metric for assessing the health and welfare of reptiles in captivity and to help inform conservation practices for reptiles in the future (Huang et al. 2024).

The tuatara (Sphenodon punctatus) is an omnivorous reptile endemic to Aotearoa New Zealand, where it has lived for over 200 million years (Evans and Jones 2010). Though tuatara once lived throughout the North and South Islands of New Zealand, human colonization and the introduction of rats extirpated the mainland population of tuatara in the 1800s (Towns and Daugherty 1994). Surviving populations exist only on predator-free offshore islands, in fenced mainland sanctuaries, or in captivity (Cree 2014). Until recently, the tuatara gut bacterial community was unstudied in either captivity or the wild. While the gut community of wild tuatara has now been determined (Hoffbeck et al. 2024), little is understood about the potential influences of diet, environmental conditions, interacting species, or translocation. Though tuatara living in enclosed sanctuaries spanning a ∼1000 km latitudinal range have no ongoing contact with each other, these populations still share a majority of their gut bacteria (Hoffbeck et al. 2024), perhaps indicating the presence of a ‘core’ community of key bacteria necessary for tuatara health and digestion. As translocation around the country and captivity are essential practices in tuatara conservation, understanding how dietary items and diversity of diet impact the tuatara gut may inform what tuatara are fed in captivity and how captivity might affect their health, particularly as the link between gut community diversity and host health is yet uninvestigated in tuatara.

In this study, we investigated the effects of altering the diet given to tuatara at three captive sites spanning the length of the North Island of New Zealand: Auckland Zoo, Wellington Zoo, and Victoria University of Wellington. We sequenced the full-length 16S rRNA gene of bacteria living in these tuatara over 3 months, including before, during, and after manipulating the dietary items that tuatara received from keepers. We studied how the gut bacterial community of tuatara at these sites compared to tuatara living in the wild in mainland sanctuaries, whether the bacterial communities of individual tuatara changed over time, and how they reacted to a shift in dietary items, as well as how the gut community correlated with tuatara weight. This study represents a first look at how keeping tuatara in captivity impacts their gut bacteria relative to their wild counterparts and investigates the stability of their gut community, providing new insights into tuatara in captivity.

Materials and methods

Experimental design and sample collection

Individual tuatara in captivity at Auckland Zoo, Wellington Zoo, and Victoria University of Wellington were selected based on accessibility. All three tuatara housed at Victoria University of Wellington were used, along with three tuatara housed at Auckland Zoo and five tuatara housed at Wellington Zoo, for a total of 11 tuatara. The tuatara at Victoria University of Wellington cohabitate in a single enclosure, while the tuatara sampled at Auckland Zoo and Wellington Zoo are housed in separate enclosures. Animal ethics approval was obtained through Victoria University of Wellington (Permission #30011) and Auckland Zoo (Permission 2022-Z004), and research approval was granted through the Department of Conservation (Authorization 50568-FAU). Before beginning the diet trial, a datalogger was left in the enclosure at each site, which monitored hourly temperatures for the duration of the experiment. The normal tuatara diet provided by keepers at all sites consisted of <50% crickets and included additional invertebrates including locusts, mealworms, and silkworms, as well as vegetation items including kawakawa berries. Full lists of provided items and estimated relative amounts are included in the supplement (Supplemental Table 1). Tuatara underwent cloacal swabbing (as per Hoffbeck et al. 2024) and weighing, after which they were tong-fed up to 15–20 adult field crickets (Teleogryllus commodus) weekly for a duration of 4 weeks with no additional dietary items provided. Tong-feeding entails placing individual crickets in the mouth of the tuatara and allowing them to consume each cricket before providing another, ensuring that tuatara actually ate the provided crickets. Before the dietary manipulation, normal feeding practices entailed providing a variety of food items, including crickets, in bowls or released into the enclosure for tuatara to hunt. Though tuatara were able to access other non-cricket insects and plant material in their enclosure during this time, 15–20 adult crickets represent sufficient food to satiate a tuatara and discourage hunting for other dietary items. Tuatara were fed as many crickets as they would consume on feeding days, with multiple feeding visits per week to give tuatara as many crickets as they were willing to consume without risking adverse health outcomes from overfeeding. Crickets for Victoria University of Wellington and Wellington Zoo were from the same source (Inzect Direct®), and crickets for Auckland Zoo were sourced in-house from Auckland Zoo from their normal supply of insects for animal feeding. All crickets were the same species (T. commodus). Tuatara were swabbed once each week, with swabs stored immediately in RNAlater and kept at –20°C until analysis. After 4 weeks, the tuatara returned to their normal feeding and dietary practices. One cricket from the food supply of each site was also selected each week and stored in RNAlater. Tuatara were swabbed again 4 weeks and 8 weeks after cessation of the feeding trial.

DNA extraction and amplification

All swabs were removed from RNAlater, and DNA was extracted using a QIAGEN QIAamp Fast DNA Stool Kit, as recommended by the manufacturer for bacterial DNA collection. Swabs from each captive site were extracted alongside an extraction blank containing no input material. Whole crickets were also extracted using a QIAGEN QIAamp Fast DNA Stool Kit. Briefly, entire crickets were removed from RNAlater and added to a microcentrifuge tube containing InhibitEX buffer and subjected to shaking in a FastPrep machine for 30 s, then processed according to kit instructions. Extracted DNA was stored at −20°C. The near-full length 16S rRNA gene was amplified using the ONT27F-ONT1492R primer pair (Frank et al. 2008) and LongAmp Taq 2X Master Mix. The following thermal cycling conditions were used: initial denaturation at 94°C for 30 s, then 30 cycles of denaturation at 94°C for 30 s, annealing at 55°C for 30 s, and extension at 65°C for 30 s, followed by a final extension at 65°C for 10 min. The presence of a correct-sized amplicon was confirmed using 1% agarose gel electrophoresis, and DNA quantity was measured using an EnSpire Multimode Plate Reader. Following amplification, 20 µl of PCR product from each sample was purified using Agencourt AMPure XP beads and sequenced on an Oxford Nanopore GridION by Auckland Genomics Ltd using the 16S Barcoding Kit SQK-RAB204.

Bioinformatic analysis

Reads resulting from Super High Basecalling on Nanopore were retained for analysis. These were trimmed using NanoFilt (De Coster et al. 2018) to 1500 bases and quality level 10, after which they were processed using Emu (Curry et al. 2022) and the SILVA-138 database (Quast et al. 2013), which resulted in ‘number of taxonomic units’ or ‘NTUs’, analogous to amplicon sequence variants (ASVs). Contaminant sequences were removed prior to downstream analyses using the decontam package in R (Davis et al. 2018; v1.18.0) by identifying bacterial DNA present in extraction blanks with default threshold 0.1, which resulted in the identification of two contaminant sequences. To normalize and compare across samples with different sampling depths, each sample was standardized to 1000 sequence reads using the SRS function in the SRS package (Heidrich et al. 2021; v0.2.3), resulting in the removal of five samples with low sequencing depth (Auckland tuatara 2—week 1, Wellington tuatara 2—week 1, Wellington tuatara 3—week 2, Wellington tuatara 1—post time point 1, and one Auckland cricket sample). A total of 73 tuatara samples and 14 cricket samples remained for analysis (rarefaction curves are included as Supplemental Fig. 5). Full-length 16S rRNA gene sequences obtained previously from a wild tuatara population at Zealandia Ecosanctuary (Hoffbeck et al. 2024) were used for comparison. These earlier samples were collected, extracted, and processed using the same methodology as the captive samples in this study. For comparisons between these wild samples and the captive samples, only samples from Wellington Zoo and Victoria University of Wellington were used due to their proximity and climatic similarity to Zealandia (which is also in Wellington), to control for environmental differences between sites.

Bacterial community alpha-diversity was calculated with the plot_richness function in phyloseq (McMurdie and Holmes 2013; v1.42.0) using the observed diversity metric and Shannon diversity, with statistical significance determined by the Wilcoxon test. Beta-diversity was calculated using the ordinate function in phyloseq with Bray-Curtis dissimilarity and weighted UniFrac (Lozupone et al. 2011), then visualized using non-metric multidimensional scaling (nMDS). Significance was determined for discrete variables using the adonis2 PERMANOVA function in vegan (Oksanen et al. 2018; v2.6–4) using Bray-Curtis dissimilarity with 9999 permutations. Homogeneity of dispersion was checked using the betadisper function in vegan. For the continuous variables tuatara weight, number of crickets eaten, and temperature, the envfit function in vegan was used to determine significance and visualize effect sizes on the bacterial community. Coefficient of variance (CV) was calculated by calculating the mean taxonomic units (‘NTUs’) for each tuatara and dividing the standard deviation by the mean (Wagner Mackenzie et al. 2017). Taxonomy was visualized at the phylum and genus levels using ggplot2 (Wickham 2016; v3.4.0).

Effect of diet manipulation on the tuatara gut community

The bacterial community extracted from cricket samples varied significantly among sites (df = 2, R2 = 0.072, F = 2.986, P < .002), particularly between Auckland Zoo and the Wellington sites, but did not change significantly over the duration of the experiment (P > .05). The cricket bacterial community was significantly different from the community obtained from tuatara swabs at all time points (df = 1, R2 = 0.072, F = 4.104, P < .001) (Supplementary Fig. 1). The tuatara gut community was not significantly impacted by the number of crickets eaten by a tuatara (Supplementary Fig. 2), nor did observed alpha-diversity differ significantly between cricket samples and tuatara samples (P > .05). The remaining analyses consider only tuatara samples, with cricket samples excluded.

Dietary manipulation had a significant but small effect on the tuatara gut community (df = 2, R2 = 0.035, F = 2.928, P < .003), explaining 3.5% of bacterial variation among samples. The most influential factor for the gut community was individual tuatara, which explained 35% of observed variation (df = 7, R2 = 0.351, F = 8.431, P < .001). Interestingly, the effect of manipulation was more significant when accounting for individual, with this interaction explaining 17% of variation (df = 16, R2 = 0.169, F = 1.778, P < .002). Site was also a significant factor, explaining 13% of variation (df = 2, R2 = 0.129, F = 10.907, P < .001), with Auckland samples particularly diverging from Wellington Zoo and Victoria University of Wellington (Fig. 1C). Sex was a significant but small source of variation (df = 2, R2 = 0.047, F = 7.889, P < .001). Temperature at the time of sampling did not account for any significant shifts in gut community (P > .05), nor did the weight of the tuatara at the time of sampling (Supplementary Fig. 2). Though manipulation, individual, site, and sex are significant factors (Table 2), all show a large degree of overlap (Fig. 1C). Observed alpha diversity did not change significantly from pre-manipulation to manipulation (P > .05), but did decrease after manipulation concluded (P < .05). Additionally, tuatara housed at Auckland Zoo had significantly lower alpha diversity than those at both Victoria University of Wellington (P < .001) and Wellington Zoo (P < .01) (Supplementary Fig. 3).

Individual variation in the tuatara gut community during manipulation

Shifts in the gut bacterial community of tuatara were more evident when considering individual tuatara, with the magnitude of these shifts varying among individuals. Notably, tuatara C30177 began the experiment with only minor (<5%) amounts of Serratia in the community, but this genus bloomed in relative abundance to more than 25% of the community by week 2 of feeding manipulation and accounted for more than 90% of the community by week 4 (Fig. 2). Serratia was then undetected in this tuatara in samples taken 1 and 2 months after the end of feeding manipulation. Interestingly, Serratia was detected only at low abundance in the cricket samples (Supplementary Fig. 4) and did not occur in any other tuatara at Auckland Zoo or elsewhere over the course of the experiment.

Individual tuatara gut bacterial composition from pre-manipulation to post-manipulation. Rows 1–3: Auckland Zoo; 4–7: Victoria University of Wellington; 8–11: Wellington Zoo.

For most tuatara, there were no major shifts in the gut community, though there were changes in the relative abundances of various bacteria already present in the pre-manipulation sample (Fig. 2). With some notable exceptions (C30176, Hazel, 230 052, and Spikette), most tuatara exhibited similar communities at post-manipulation time point one and time point two, taken a month apart. Notably, Spikette was found to have a lesion caused by Parannizziopsis australiensis (a known tuatara pathogen) shortly after the cessation of the diet manipulation. Whether this infection might have been influencing her final gut bacterial community requires further investigation. When looking across all seven time points, most tuatara had CV <50%, indicating a fairly stable community over time. Exceptions included The Male (Auckland Zoo), Tiwhiri (Wellington Zoo), Tuatahi (Wellington Zoo), and tuatara 230052 (Wellington Zoo), which had CV > 50% (Supplemental Table 3). However, the core bacterial community (those present in >80% of individuals) shifted during each experimentation window. Nineteen ‘NTUs’ constituted the core in pre-manipulation samples, 9 constituted the core during manipulation, and 12 constituted the core post-manipulation (Supplemental Table 2). Of these, seven core members were consistent through all phases of the experiment: Alysiella, Gallicola, Chryseobacterium, Kingella, Neisseria, Luteimonas, and Snodgrassella.

While the nMDS plot of all tuatara over the course of manipulation showed substantial overlap in gut communities (Fig. 1), the bacterial communities of most individual tuatara were more similar at the end of the experimental period than they were at the beginning (Fig. 3). This appears to be a community dominated by the genera Alysiella and/or Snodgrassella, with these two genera accounting for more than 70% of the final community in 9 out of 11 individuals (Fig. 2), and neither genus being highly abundant in the cricket samples (Supplementary Fig. 4).

Gut community of captive versus wild tuatara

When comparing tuatara only in the Wellington region to better control for climate differences, captive tuatara at Wellington Zoo and Victoria University of Wellington harboured significantly different bacterial communities to wild tuatara at Zealandia Ecosanctuary (df = 1, R2 = 0.128, F = 13.015, P < .001). Moreover, captivity status explained more bacterial variation than when considering Victoria University of Wellington and Wellington Zoo as separate sites (Fig. 5). All bacterial community shifts that arose during manipulation were negligible when compared to the impact of captivity status, as were the effects of sex, though this factor was also statistically significant (Table 2).

nMDS plots showing bacterial communities of tuatara from Zealandia, Victoria University of Wellington, and Wellington Zoo, designated by (A) captivity status, (B) site, and (C) dietary treatment.

Though captivity status was a significant driving factor for variation in the tuatara gut community, the main bacterial phyla and genera present in both groups were largely consistent, and included Alysiella, Chryseobacterium, Gallicola, and Snodgrassella (Fig. 6). Notably, captive tuatara contained a high abundance of Acidipila–Silvibacterium, a genus that was undetected in 177 cloacal swab samples from wild tuatara (Hoffbeck et al. 2024). Wild tuatara also contained Kocuria, a genus which was substantially reduced in captive tuatara (Fig. 6).

Discussion

Our study analysed the gut bacterial community of tuatara in captivity at three sites over the course of a month-long dietary manipulation using full-length 16S rRNA gene sequencing. This allowed us to determine how the captive tuatara gut community responds to shifts in dietary diversity, if it correlates with zoo location, temperature, tuatara weight, or tuatara sex, and how it compares to the bacterial community of tuatara in the wild. This analysis establishes for the first time which bacteria are most common in the gut of tuatara living in captivity and identifies specific bacteria that are influenced by dietary shifts.

Diet manipulation influenced gut communities of individual tuatara

Application of a 4-week manipulation in which tuatara were provided with only a single dietary item by keepers instead of a range of invertebrates did not result in strong shifts in the bacterial community across all tuatara. Though gut communities during the manipulation period did vary significantly from the pre-trial and post-trial periods (F = 2.886, P < .005), phase of the dietary trial explained only 3.5% of variation in the gut community (Fig. 1). The interaction between manipulation and individual was a far more significant driver, explaining some 17% of community variation (R2 = 0.169, P < .001) and indicating that manipulation did affect the gut bacteria of tuatara, however not in a uniform way across different individuals. Though tuatara have previously been shown to have a relatively consistent gut community across individuals (Hoffbeck et al. 2024), individual variation in the gut before and during manipulation was the strongest explainer of variation in the gut community. This may be due in part to the low number of tuatara available for sampling—small sample sizes can magnify individual-level variation in the community (Casals-Pascual et al. 2020). Variation in the sex and age of the animal (Colston and Jackson 2016), behavioural preferences (Cusick et al. 2021), genetic differences (Blekhman et al. 2015, Scepanovic et al. 2019), or even the previous environment of the tuatara may be contributing to the individual-level variation observed in this study.

Though individual shifts were detected over the course of dietary manipulation, the bacterial genera that rose in abundance were not necessarily in high abundance in cricket samples. The genera that saw the greatest increases in relative abundance during manipulation included Staphylococcus, Parabacteroides, Bacteroides, Hafnia-Obesumbacterium, and Pseudomonas (Fig. 4), yet only Parabacteroides and Pseudomonas were among the dominant bacterial genera in cricket samples (Supplementary Fig. 4). Individual tuatara also saw blooms of Serratia and Stenoxybacter (Fig. 2), which were found in the cricket samples at only low abundance. As Parabacteroides, Staphylococcus, Hafnia-Obesumbacterium, and Pseudomonas all decreased in abundance again following cessation of the diet manipulation (Fig. 4), it may be presumed that these bacterial genera were being consumed by tuatara and did not persist in the gut once they were no longer supplied in the diet. Alternatively, these genera may have existed in the tuatara gut at low frequencies prior to experimentation and increased in response to a change in dietary input. As sampling was conducted by cloacal swabbing, it cannot be fully determined if the bacteria obtained in each sample represent consistent community members or transients introduced by diet, but the persistence of genera after the cessation of manipulation is evidence for their residence in the gut rather than as introduced by diet. Interestingly, for multiple reptile species, Hafnia-Obesumbacterium is enriched in wild conspecifics compared to their captive counterparts (Fong et al. 2020, Tang et al. 2020), making it perhaps unexpected that this genus would increase in tuatara during a period of lower dietary diversity. Specifically, Hafnia-Obesumbacterium has been identified in wild lizards, the closest living relatives to tuatara (Hernández et al. 2023). Parabacteroides, by contrast, have been shown to increase in abundance in captive squamate reptiles over time as they move from wild populations to captivity (Zhou et al. 2020), as has Staphylococcus (Tang et al. 2022).

In contrast to the findings of other recent research on wild tuatara (Hoffbeck et al. 2024), tuatara weight did not significantly correlate with bacterial community structure (Supplementary Fig. 2). Tuatara in this study ranged from 375 to 820 g at the start of the study and weighed between 370 and 860 g at the end of the study, with no tuatara gaining or losing significant weight over the course of manipulation (Table 1). Despite a large range in the average number of crickets eaten each week, from as many as 16 crickets on average to as few as 3 on average per week (Table 1), the number of crickets eaten also did not significantly correlate with the bacterial community (Supplementary Fig. 2). This indicates that although there were increases in bacteria found in crickets in the tuatara gut, the amount of these bacteria being consumed was not directly linked to the number of crickets consumed. This is consistent with previous studies, which have also highlighted the limited link of bacteria in a dietary item with gut bacterial community (Bodawatta et al. 2022, Hernández et al. 2024). Further research is required to establish the extent to which the tuatara gut community resembles that of its dietary components under conditions of higher diet diversity.

Individual-level gut communities were largely consistent over all seven time points for a majority of tuatara in terms of alpha-diversity (Supplementary Table 3). When separating samples by manipulation and non-manipulation, the average CV across individuals was very similar (47.69% for non-manipulation samples, 42.39% for manipulation samples). However, there was a strong degree of variation between some individuals: Tiwhiri had a CV of 40.25% during manipulation and 7.15% outside of manipulation, while Tuatahi had 49.32 during manipulation and 91.76% outside of manipulation (Supplemental Table 3). This lack of consistency in stability of gut members mirrors variance in beta-diversity between individuals (Fig. 2). The core community also saw shifts between phases of experimentation (Supplementary Table 2), though seven community members were consistently in the core. These seven members (Alysiella, Gallicola, Chryseobacterium, Kingella, Neisseria, Luteimonas, Snodgrassella) included five members of phylum Proteobacteria, one of Bacteroidota, and one of Firmicutes. While this core broadly resembles that of other reptile species at phylum level (Hoffbeck et al. 2023), the shifts in core community members outside of these five imply a strong level of temporal variation in the tuatara gut.

Tuatara gut communities also appeared to become more consistent across individuals during the final sampling point (Fig. 3), which occurred 3 months from the original sampling and was in the Austral Summer, whereas sampling began in the Austral Spring. Although tuatara in captivity do not enter the level of torpor that those in the wild experience, other reptile species that hibernate have been shown to experience seasonal-driven shifts in the gut community (Keenan et al. 2013, Tang et al. 2019). Bacteroides in particular can become enriched in hibernating reptiles, or if not with hibernation, then as a function of reproductive state (Bunker et al. 2022), with overall bacterial diversity decreasing during the reproductive season. Indeed, seasonal differences can be more pronounced than those between captive and wild reptiles (Keenan et al. 2013). The more consistent communities among individual tuatara in January, their reproductive period, compared to the same individuals in October, may reflect a similar trend in tuatara, though more work is needed to definitively link shifts in the tuatara gut to seasonality.

Bacteroides abundance was significantly influenced during dietary manipulation

The presence of the genus Bacteroides in captive tuatara was of interest, particularly as this genus is very common in the gut communities of many other vertebrates (Colston and Jackson 2016, Wexler and Goodman 2017) and especially in squamate reptiles (Hoffbeck et al. 2023). Despite its prevalence in the closest living relatives of tuatara, wild tuatara from across the length of New Zealand contained virtually no Bacteroides, instead being dominated by Kocuria, Gallicola, and Chryseobacterium (Hoffbeck et al. 2024). In captive tuatara at the outset of the diet manipulation, Bacteroides represented <1% of all bacteria present, but rose to ∼5% of the community during the diet manipulation and remained at ∼5% 2 months following the cessation of manipulation. It is notable that Bacteroides was the only bacterial genus that increased during manipulation that continued to increase following cessation of manipulation, rather than decreasing in abundance (Fig. 4), perhaps indicating a more lasting change in the tuatara gut.

In squamate reptiles, Bacteroides is a key member of the gut community (Hoffbeck et al. 2023), but captive reptile studies on squamates rarely identify Bacteroides as being significantly enriched or depleted in captive reptiles compared to wild conspecifics (Zhou et al. 2020, Tang et al. 2020, 2022, Eliades et al. 2021). However, Bacteroides abundance has been linked to changes in seasonality, particularly increasing in winter for hibernating reptile species (Keenan et al. 2013, Tang et al. 2019). Though 16S rRNA gene data cannot fully elucidate the functional role that Bacteroides plays in the tuatara gut, its absence in wild tuatara and increased abundance in captive tuatara indicate an interaction between the tuatara gut community and diet in captivity.

The captive tuatara community broadly resembles that of wild tuatara, but with key differences

Though the gut communities of captive and wild tuatara from the same region (all tuatara in Wellington are <5 km apart) varied significantly (R2 = 0.128, P < .001; Table 2), the dominant bacteria across tuatara from both environments still contained Alysiella, Arcobacter, Chryseobacterium, Gallicola, and Snodgrassella (Fig. 6). The main genus that was notably missing from captive tuatara was Kocuria, a genus that was present in some individual tuatara in captivity (Fig. 2) but made up a significantly smaller portion of the gut than in wild individuals (Fig. 6). The only genus that showed clear enrichment in captive compared to wild tuatara was Acidipila-Silvibacterium, a bacterium more known from soil than host-associated samples (Lladó et al. 2016). Despite broad similarities at genus level, differences between captive and wild tuatara were stronger than those between manipulation and pre- or post-manipulation captive tuatara, male and female tuatara, and Victoria University of Wellington and Wellington Zoo tuatara (Fig. 5). These differences also potentially include effects of time, as samples from wild tuatara were collected a year prior to those collected from tuatara in captivity.

With somewhat limited research on the reptile bacterial community compared to other vertebrate hosts, existing studies comparing the communities of wild and captive reptiles vary in terms of the effects of captivity. In squamates, the closest living relatives of tuatara, some studies showed significant increases in diversity for captive hosts when compared to wild ones (Tang et al. 2020, 2022), while others showed no difference in alpha-diversity (Zhou et al. 2020, Eliades et al. 2021). All of these squamate studies showed differences in beta-diversity between captive and wild hosts, but with no consistent genera enriched in captive or wild hosts. Indeed, across published reptile bacterial community studies, no host species showed enrichment in any of the dominant bacteria found in this study (Keenan et al. 2013, Madison et al. 2018, Tang et al. 2019, 2020, 2022, Fong et al. 2020, Zhou et al. 2020, Eliades et al. 2021), further identifying the tuatara gut community as unique among reptiles (Hoffbeck et al. 2024). In vertebrates more broadly, the gut community of captive individuals is often associated with lower diversity (Dallas and Warne 2023) and with a shift towards human-associated bacteria (Alberdi et al. 2021). Though specific reptile species show different, sometimes opposite trends in gut diversity and the abundance of specific players in the gut community (Madison et al. 2018, Fong et al. 2020, Tang et al. 2022), a majority of studies confirm that captive reptiles harbour significantly different gut communities to their wild conspecifics (Colston 2017). While the gut communities of most reptile species are yet to be sampled (Hoffbeck et al. 2023), interspecific differences in the directionality of captivity effects make it difficult to predict how the community of a given reptile species will respond to captivity.

Concluding remarks

As a relict reptile and a taonga (treasured) species for New Zealand’s indigenous Māori, the manipulations we were able to apply to tuatara were limited by the low number of tuatara in captivity and the nature of their habitats in captivity. Although tuatara were fed and consumed an increased number of crickets over the course of this study, they were also still able to forage in their enclosures, potentially securing additional dietary items, soil, and vegetation, influencing their bacterial community via unknown vectors. Tuatara in the study also experienced increased handling and likely stress during the dietary manipulation, which could also have contributed to bacterial shifts. Despite these constraints necessitated by the conditions surrounding tuatara as a valued species, this work demonstrates the long-term stability of the tuatara gut, the relative consistency of the gut community among captive tuatara across New Zealand, and the difference between gut communities in captive and wild tuatara from the same climatic region. This study demonstrates that the tuatara gut is influenced by dietary items, with specific bacteria present in the food source being enriched during increased feeding, but that overall the tuatara gut remains fairly consistent both over time in individuals and between populations of wild and captive tuatara. Here we provide further context to the gut community in tuatara and its dietary influences and conclude that keeping tuatara in captivity for conservation purposes remains a productive strategy with no measurable deleterious effect on the tuatara gut community.
