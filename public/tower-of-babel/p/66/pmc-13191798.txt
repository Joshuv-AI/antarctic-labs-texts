Beyond complication rates: Surgical instrumentation, clinical decision‐making, and long‐term outcomes in uterine evacuation
Authors: Mei Zhao, Fuhua Zhou, ChunMei Qiu
Published: 2026

Sir,

Dowthwaite et al. provide valuable real‐world data comparing blind and ultrasound‐guided uterine evacuation, demonstrating similarly low complication rates. 1 While the authors thoroughly acknowledge the limitations inherent to retrospective designs, we believe several underexplored aspects merit further consideration to better inform clinical practice.

Firstly, the differential use of surgical instruments between groups warrants attention. Forceps were used in 68% of ultrasound‐guided procedures compared with only 32% of blind procedures (OR 4.5, 95% CI 3.3–6.2). This substantial difference likely reflects enhanced visualization enabling more targeted tissue removal under ultrasound guidance. Conversely, the higher use of sharp curettage in the blind group (51% vs. 38%) may represent a compensatory strategy to ensure complete evacuation without direct visualization. These instrumentation patterns suggest that ultrasound guidance fundamentally alters surgical technique rather than simply complementing it—a distinction with potential implications for uterine trauma and long‐term fertility outcomes that was not explored. 2

Secondly, the clinical decision‐making process underlying the choice of guidance modality remains unaddressed. The study does not clarify whether ultrasound guidance was planned preoperatively or introduced intraoperatively when difficulties were encountered. In the blind group, 24 manual placental removals were performed compared with only three in the ultrasound group, suggesting that ultrasound may have been selectively employed when manual removal was anticipated. Understanding the timing and rationale for ultrasound utilization would help distinguish whether its benefits stem from preoperative planning or intraoperative rescue—a distinction with significant implications for clinical protocols. 3

Thirdly, the clinical significance of the substantially smaller residual tissue volumes observed in the ultrasound group (0.66 cm3 vs. 8.35 cm3) remains speculative. While the authors did not observe differences in repeat surgery rates, growing evidence links retained tissue volume to the risk of intrauterine adhesion formation and subsequent infertility, independent of whether repeat intervention is performed. 4 Given that the study did not include long‐term follow‐up, the possibility that ultrasound guidance reduces the incidence of Asherman's syndrome or improves subsequent pregnancy outcomes remains an open but important question. 5

Finally, the 57 postpartum cases, which were unevenly distributed between groups, may represent a distinct clinical entity that warrants separate analysis. Postpartum uterine evacuation presents unique challenges due to a larger, softer uterus and altered anatomy. Combining these cases with first‐ and second‐trimester evacuations for primary analysis may dilute procedure‐specific risks. A subgroup analysis focused exclusively on postpartum patients could provide more actionable insights for this high‐risk population.

We appreciate the authors' important contribution and suggest that future studies address the mechanistic role of ultrasound in altering surgical technique, the decision pathways that lead to its use, and the long‐term fertility outcomes that may ultimately define its value.
