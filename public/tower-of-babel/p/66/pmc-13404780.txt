Poverty and Social Deprivation in Patients With Pulmonary Arterial Hypertension—A PHAR Analysis
Authors: Roberto J Bernardo, Di Lu, Haley Hedlin, Daniel Grinnan, Roberto F Machado, Lana Melendres‐Groves, Jean M Elwing, Sandeep Sahay, Steven M Kawut, Jeffrey C Robinson, Vinicio A de Jesus Perez, Roham T Zamanian, PHAR Investigators
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPoverty and social deprivation have been associated with impaired clinical outcomes and survival in several chronic diseases. The association between poverty and clinical outcomes in patients with pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH) remains unclear. We aim to determine the association between federal poverty levels with healthcare use and clinical outcomes in PAH. Using the Pulmonary Hypertension Association Registry, we performed a prospective cohort analysis comparing clinical and socioeconomic characteristics in PAH patients above or below federal poverty levels. We studied the association between federal poverty levels and healthcare use patterns and transplant‐free survival using a Cox proportional hazards model. A total of 1396 participants were included, 411 (29.4%) under poverty levels. The in‐poverty group was mostly non‐White and Hispanic, had mainly public health insurance/Medicaid (37.0% vs. 5.5%; absolute standardized difference [ASD] 0.84), lower educational attainment (17.2% vs. 45.6% college graduate or higher; ASD 0.64), lower rates of employment/retirement (27.3% vs. 71.1%; ASD 0.98), higher frequency of methamphetamine exposure (31.5% vs. 9.8%; ASD 0.57), and lower frequency of idiopathic/heritable PAH (36.0% vs. 47.8%; ASD 0.65), than the not in‐poverty group. While there were no differences in severity of disease or treatment, the in‐poverty group had higher incidence rate ratios of emergency room visits and hospitalizations (IRR 2.19, 95% CI 1.99–2.40, and 1.51, 95% CI 1.36–1.68, respectively). There were no differences in transplant‐free survival (p = 0.10). Poverty was not associated with worse survival in patients with PAH but was associated with higher rates of emergency room visits and hospitalizations, suggesting suboptimal disease control despite similar disease severity.Keywords: federal poverty level, PHAR, poverty, pulmonary arterial hypertension, social determinants of health

Introduction

Pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH) is a progressive disease associated with increased cardiovascular morbidity and mortality [1, 2]. Significant progress in the understanding and management of the disease has been made, with the availability of over a dozen therapies targeting four different therapeutic pathways [3, 4, 5, 6]. However, PAH management is expensive, and its cost can be prohibitive for many individuals and caregivers [7, 8, 9, 10]. Moreover, the severity of symptoms can be debilitating, impeding affected individuals to carry full time jobs, which further imposes additional costs and economic burden on patients and caregivers [11, 12]. There is concern that affected patients living below poverty levels and with social deprivation could face significant barriers accessing proper PAH management [9, 10, 13, 14].

Prior studies studying the association between surrogates of socioeconomic status (SES) and social deprivation in PAH have shown mixed results, with some studies reporting higher symptom burden or lower survival in patients with lower SES, but these findings were not replicated in most recent studies [15, 16, 17, 18]. Studies assessing SES, income, or social deprivation have been limited due to small sample sizes, data pertaining to a single private health care system, or performed outside the United States (US). To our knowledge, the association of poverty levels and social deprivation in patients with PAH has not been studied in a large cohort. We have previously shown that Hispanic patients with PAH had a disadvantageous social determinants of health (SDOH) profile, had a higher frequency of emergency room (ER) visits and hospitalizations despite a similar disease severity, and that while they had overall better survival rates, this survival advantage was mitigated in the presence of a disadvantageous SDOH profile [19]. In the present study, we used data from the Pulmonary Hypertension Association (PHA) Registry (PHAR) to study the association of federal poverty levels (FPL) and clinical outcomes in patients with PAH. We aimed to (i) characterize the baseline clinical, sociodemographic, and SDOH profile of patients with PAH living above or below poverty level, (ii) to investigate the association between FPL and transplant‐free survival, and (iii) to investigate the association between FPL and healthcare use patterns.

Methods

Study Design, Participants, and Setting

We performed a cohort analysis using data from the PHAR, a multicenter, US‐based registry of patients with established care at PHA‐accredited centers as centers of comprehensive care (PHCC). At the time of this analysis, data from 65 PHCCs were available. Enrollment in PHAR started in 2015, and data collected until June 2023 were included in the current analysis. PHAR inclusion criteria have been previously reported [20].

After providing informed consent, participants completed a survey with sociodemographic, SDOH, health care‐related quality of life (HRQOL), and PAH‐specific information. Participants underwent repeated assessments at a frequency with interval variability at the discretion of the study center.

We restricted our analysis to adult participants (age > 18 years at enrollment), with a diagnosis of PAH and with at least one follow‐up visit after enrollment. We excluded patients in whom information related to annual income and/or number of household members was not available. The University of Pennsylvania serves as the single Institutional Review Board (FWA00004028). See Supporting Table E1 for a full list of study investigators and sites.

Study Groups

In the US system, poverty is measured by FPL. Briefly, FPL is the minimum amount of income a household requires to cover essential needs, and is updated annually based on the consumer price index [21]. FPL calculation depends on the reported annual income for an entire family, adjusted by the number of household members (see Supporting Table E2). Based on FPL thresholds, patients were categorized as two main groups: In‐poverty (patients living below or at the poverty line) and Not in‐poverty (patients living above the poverty line).

Study Variables

We performed a comparison of baseline variables including clinical, anthropometric, hemodynamic, and sociometric data. SDOH metrics included health care insurance, educational attainment, and occupation, and in participants in whom ZIP code data were available, we estimated the Social Vulnerability Index (SVI) [22], as a surrogate measure of social deprivation. We also estimated the proportion of patients residing in rural and nonmetropolitan areas [23] (see Online Data Supplement S1). HRQOL was assessed by the EmPHasis‐10 questionnaire [24, 25] and the Medical Outcomes Survey Short Form‐12 (SF‐12) [26].

We also estimated the Family Income to Poverty Ratio (PIR), as another metric of poverty [27]. PIR was calculated as family income divided by poverty threshold, and was stratified as PIR ≤ 1.0, PIR > 1.0– < 5.0, and PIR ≥ 5.0, with higher values reflecting higher income than poverty levels [28]. Compared to FPL, PIR provides a measurement of how many times the family annual income exceeds the poverty threshold.

Statistical Analysis and Outcome Measures

Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation, and categorical variables as counts (percentages). We used the absolute standardized difference (ASD) to compare distributions of baseline characteristics between groups, interpreted using Cohen's guidelines (ASD < 0.2 = trivial difference; 0.2 = small difference; 0.5 = medium difference, 0.8 or higher = large difference) [29, 30] (see Online Data Supplement S1).

Our main study endpoint was a comparison of transplant‐free survival between groups. Transplant‐free survival was defined as time to all‐cause death or transplantation. The differences in transplant‐free survival days were assessed by Kaplan–Meier survival estimates. Two Cox proportional hazards models were developed for further assessment. Model 1 was adjusted for age only, and Model 2 was adjusted for age, sex at birth, PAH etiology, education level, race/ethnicity, and health insurance. Covariates were chosen a priori, based on mechanistic and biologic knowledge of PAH, and our prior experience with SDOH analysis, in line with recommendations for observational studies [31]. Covariates were prespecified and considered the number of observed events to prevent overfitting [32]. Proportional hazards and linearity assumptions were examined.

We also compared transplant‐free survival differences by SVI (stratified as SVI < 0.75 vs. ≥ 0.75) and PIR (stratified as PIR ≤ 1.0, > 1.0– < 5.0, and ≥ 5.0). Cox proportional hazards models were developed, with adjustment for age only (Supporting Text S1). We chose to perform these analyses for confirmatory purposes of study findings, as alternative measures of social deprivation (SVI) and poverty (PIR).

We compared incidence rates of healthcare use patterns between groups. Incidence rates for both ER visits and numbers of hospitalizations were derived from the number of events and follow‐up time (in person‐years) within groups. Incidence rate ratios (IRR) were calculated by dividing the incidence rates in the in‐poverty group, by the incidence rates in the not in‐poverty group.

A two‐sided p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were conducted using SAS version 9.4 and R version 3.6.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

Results

Study Population

From a total of 2573 individuals enrolled in the PHAR, 1396 met the inclusion criteria and were included in the analysis (Figure 1). 686 (26.7%) did not provide their annual income data or the number of individuals in their household, and 165 (6.4%) were missing follow‐up data.

A total of 411 participants (29.4%) comprised in the in‐poverty group. Baseline characteristics are displayed in Table 1. Participants under poverty levels were overall younger (48.9 ± 14.6 vs. 57.0 ± 14.8; ASD 0.58), more likely to be of Hispanic ethnicity (18.5% vs. 7.8%; ASD 0.33), less likely to be White (73.6% vs. 83.0%; ASD 0.30), and mostly located in the Western US Census Region (40.9% vs. 24.0%; ASD 0.40), than participants above poverty levels. There were important differences in the distribution of PAH etiologies (ASD 0.65), with a higher proportion of PAH associated with drugs and toxins exposure (27.7% vs. 6.6%) and a lower proportion of idiopathic/heritable PAH (36.0% vs. 47.8%) in the in‐poverty group, than in the not in‐poverty group. There were no important differences in severity of illness, with a similar distribution of functional class between groups (ASD 0.18), similar exercise capacity and 6‐min walking distance, similar levels of natriuretic peptides, hemodynamic severity, or PAH risk stratification. There were no differences in PAH therapy, with a similar proportion of patients receiving treatment with pulmonary vasodilators, including prostacyclin analogs (31.4% vs. 34.5%, ASD 0.07) and parenteral prostacyclins (20.2% vs. 23.1%, ASD 0.07) (Table 1 and Supporting Table E3). No major differences in the number of visits or the length of follow‐up were observed between groups (Supporting Table E4).

Social Determinants of Health

There were important differences in the baseline sociodemographic profiles between groups (Table 2). The in‐poverty group had a higher frequency of being uninsured (5.4% vs. 0.6%; ASD 0.28) or having public health insurance/Medicaid (37.0% vs. 5.5%; ASD 0.83), and a lower frequency of having private or commercial insurance (16.8% vs. 66.8%; ASD 1.18). The in‐poverty group also had lower educational attainment (college graduate or higher, 17.2% vs. 45.6%; ASD 0.64), lower frequency of being employed or retired (27.3% vs. 71.1%; ASD 0.97), and had a higher frequency of reported use of methamphetamines (31.5% vs. 9.8%; ASD 0.57), as compared with the not in‐poverty group. The SVI scores were higher in the in‐poverty group (26.0% vs. 10.7% with SVI ≥ 0.75; ASD 0.40), and the PIR values were lower (100% vs. 0% with PIR ≤ 1; ASD 0.944) than the not in‐poverty group, reflecting higher social deprivation.

Survival

There were no significant differences in transplant‐free survival between groups. The fully adjusted Cox proportional hazards models did not reveal any association between FPL and transplant‐free survival (hazard ratio 0.76, 95% CI 0.55–1.05, p = 0.10) (Figure 2 and Table 5), and similar results were seen when using SVI and PIR (Supporting Figures E1 and E2 and Supporting Tables E5 and E6). Discussion

In the present study, we showed that, compared to the not in‐poverty group, patients in poverty were overall younger, more likely to be of Hispanic ethnicity and non‐White, mostly located in the Western US, had a higher frequency of drugs and toxins‐associated PAH, higher frequency of reported methamphetamine use, and had a more disadvantageous SDOH profile (with more limited healthcare insurance, lower education attainment and lower rates of employment/retirement). We did not find important differences in baseline disease severity or treatment. We also showed that the in‐poverty group had higher incidence rates for ER visits and hospitalizations, despite a similar severity of illness, and we did not find significant differences in overall survival between groups.

Despite significant advances in diagnosis and management, PAH remains a debilitating disease, associated with significant disease burden on affected individuals [1, 2], impairing their ability to work and leading to unpaid medical leave and disability, contributing to physical and mental stress load on patients and caregivers [10]. In addition, PAH is a costly disease, associated with significant out‐of‐pocket expenses related to the cost of PAH‐specific medications (which can be mitigated via patient's assistance programs), but also to healthcare use such as cost of medical appointments, medical transportation, ancillary testing, and supplemental therapies such as need for oxygen or pulmonary rehabilitation [11, 12]. In fact, the mean average total health care cost per PAH patient is estimated at greater than 98,000 US dollars per year [7]. As such, we and others have previously shared the concern that individuals living under poverty levels and/or with social deprivation could face important barriers to proper access to care [9, 10, 13, 14].

Previous studies investigating the association between poverty or social deprivation surrogates have shown mixed results. In a study of 268 patients with idiopathic PAH from a single center in China, patients in the lowest SES tertile had higher mortality rates, after adjustments for severity of illness or treatment [15]. Similarly, in a study of 243 patients with all forms of PH from a large hospital system, patients in the lower income quartile were more likely to present with more advanced symptoms (worse functional class) [16]. However, these findings were not replicated in recent studies from Scotland. In 280 patients with idiopathic/heritable PAH, no association between social deprivation quintiles and neither functional class nor survival was seen [17]. Similarly, in 495 patients with connective tissue disease‐associated PAH or CTEPH, no differences in survival across social deprivation quintiles were found [18]. The difference between the results in these studies could be related to differences in access to healthcare and different healthcare delivery systems and payors [33], and a universal healthcare system such as in Scotland could mitigate the significance of underlying social deprivation (although healthcare inequality in other chronic diseases has been demonstrated within this same healthcare system [34, 35]). Differences between these studies could also relate to differences in the population under study (all forms of PH vs. certain PAH subtypes), and the methodology used to define SES or social deprivation. These studies are important, although limited by their relatively small sample size and data related to single healthcare systems. The generalizability of these study findings into the US PAH population is problematic, since most of them were performed outside the US, used a nonstandardized definition of poverty or social deprivation applicable to the US system, or were limited to only certain types of PAH. Moreover, to better understand the magnitude of the effect of a given annual household income, this must be accounted for the number of household members within a family. In fact, in the US system, poverty is measured by FPL (poverty guidelines), which depend on the annual income in a given household, adjusted by family size [21].

We have previously shown that Hispanic patients with PAH living in the US had a more disadvantageous SDOH profile, and despite a similar severity of illness and similar PAH treatment, they had higher number of ER visits and hospitalizations, than non‐Hispanic White patients [19]. Similar to other studies [36, 37], we found that Hispanic patients had better transplant‐free survival, but when survival curves were adjusted for SDOH metrics, there were no longer significant differences in survival [19, 38].

We found a similar proportion of patients participating in clinical trials between groups, similar willingness on participating in future clinical studies, and similar satisfaction with their ongoing care. The similar interest in participation on clinical studies could be related to the fact that our study population is already participating in a clinical study (PHAR), and prior participation in clinical studies is predictive of future participation in research [39].

We did not find striking differences on PAH severity or treatment, yet the in‐poverty group had higher incidence rates for ER visits and hospitalizations, as well as a higher number of cumulative hospital days, which suggests patients may not have optimal disease control and have exacerbations of the underlying illness, requiring evaluation in the ER or admission to the hospital (with the associated healthcare costs at a personal and global level), or do not have the means of notifying their providers of changes in their health that require either prompt evaluation or treatment adjustments to prevent further symptomatic decline. It should be noted that the PHAR does not differentiate between the cause of hospitalizations, and we do not know whether the rate of hospitalizations is driven by PAH‐related (i.e., clinical worsening) or non‐PAH‐related hospitalizations [40].

We did not find important differences in transplant‐free survival, although survival was numerically better (but not reaching the threshold for statistical significance) in the in‐poverty group. This interesting finding could have been related to the younger age of patients in the in‐poverty group [41], as one of our hazard models seemed to suggest. It could also be speculated that the differences in survival could be related to the higher proportion of Hispanic patients in the in‐poverty group, a finding that has previously been reported by our group and others, where Hispanic patients have better survival rates in unadjusted analysis [19, 36, 37]. A prior analysis of the PHAR described a relatively low incidence of deaths as outcomes (as compared to hospitalization rates), and the PHAR may not have sufficient longitudinal data yet to assess mortality as a primary outcome [40], so differences in survival rates should be interpreted with caution.

We chose to dichotomize patients as above/under poverty levels based on poverty guidelines. For comprehensiveness, we also performed an additional analysis using PIR. While the dichotomized analysis of above/under poverty could underestimate some associations (mixing in the same group patients barely above poverty levels with those with a significantly higher income), PIR accounts for that factor, at least in theory. However, we did not find differences in survival when using PIR. Finally, we also presented an analysis using SVI, a widely used metric of social deprivation in the US. SVI was derived from available ZIP codes (available on 92% of participants), and no differences in survival curves were observed. We chose to use FPL as our primary study variable instead of SVI because SVI, like most neighborhood‐level indices, is susceptible to ecological fallacy [42, 43]. This fallacy occurs when inferences about individuals are incorrectly drawn from aggregate (area‐level) data [44]. Our aim was to understand the significance of a validated metric of social deprivation, and reported at the individual level, such as FPL, rather than an inferred metric that reflects societal deprivation but cannot be extrapolated to indicate individual vulnerability. We acknowledge that FPL is an imperfect measure of economic disadvantage: it does not adjust for regional cost‐of‐living differences, is based on pretax income, and does not account for variable state‐level public benefit structures. However, FPL remains the most policy‐relevant individual‐level poverty metric available in the US and, we would argue, the most appropriate for this type of investigation. FPL is the threshold used by the federal government to determine eligibility for Medicaid, the Children's Health Insurance Program, Affordable Care Act premium subsidies, the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program, and other safety‐net programs that directly shape healthcare access for disadvantaged populations. In this sense, FPL does not merely describe poverty—it operationally defines who receives assistance, making it uniquely suited to studies examining healthcare utilization disparities. Critically, FPL is measured at the individual household level and thus avoids the ecological fallacy inherent in area‐based deprivation indices such as SVI or the Area Deprivation Index, which assign neighborhood‐level characteristics to individuals. It has been previously shown that area‐level‐derived SDOH measures had low accuracy, sensitivity, and area under the curve when used to predict individual‐level social risks, concluding that these indices are unreliable proxies for personal socioeconomic disadvantage [45]. Similarly, in the Public Health Disparities Geocoding Project, it was shown that among diverse area‐based socioeconomic measures, those incorporating economic poverty data were the most robust in detecting health gradients, outperforming measures based solely on education, occupation, or wealth [46]. Our decision to use FPL aligns with this evidence base: by leveraging a household‐level, self‐reported income metric, we capture individual economic reality rather than an ecologic approximation, while simultaneously anchoring our findings to the metric that determines resource allocation across the US healthcare system.

Our study had several limitations. First, the presented data relate to patients receiving their care at PHCCs, which excludes the care of PAH patients in the community and smaller centers, reflecting selection bias. It has been previously reported important differences in PAH outcomes of patients receiving their care at PH specialized centers (as compared to smaller centers) [47, 48], and it could be hypothesized that any existing disparities and barriers to care may be mitigated once patients get to establish their care at PHCCs, given the resources these centers have, the multidisciplinary approach and the familiarity with patient support and financial systems. Patients who do not have the opportunity to receive their care at PHCCs (for various reasons) may face different barriers and have different outcomes than ones we reported in our study. Second, our operational definition of poverty relied on self‐reported annual income and household size, and participants who did not disclose this information were necessarily excluded from the analysis. In fact, respondents with missing income data are generally not a random subset of a surveyed population but tend to resemble individuals of lower SES, characterized by lower educational attainment and greater socioeconomic disadvantage [49]. Consequently, this exclusion is most likely to have resulted in an underrepresentation of patients living in poverty, rather than in an overestimation of the differences observed. Third, while PHAR has a relatively larger representation of minorities than other US registries, the proportion of minorities is still lower than expected (for US census estimates), and how these data reflect the reality of these minority populations remains unclear [50]. And finally, as rich as the PHAR data are regarding SDOH, it does not allow for a comprehensive assessment of social vulnerability, and what we reported in our study depends on ZIP code‐inferred data. Despite these limitations, our study has important strengths: we presented the largest study to date on the significance of poverty in PAH, we used a data set rich in SDOH metrics, and we used a solid methodology and a standardized definition of poverty.

Conclusions

Patients with PAH living under FPL have a more disadvantageous sociodemographic profile, and despite similar severity of illness, they have suboptimal disease control, having higher frequencies of ER visits and hospitalizations.
