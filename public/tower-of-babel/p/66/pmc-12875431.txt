Transcranial magnetic stimulation improves language and language network functional connectivity in a patient with logopenic primary progressive aphasia
Authors: Alexandra Touroutoglou, Yuta Katsumi, Neguine Rezaii, Thiago Paranhos, Amelia Jones, Daisy Hochberg, Megan Quimby, Shalom K Henderson, Bonnie Wong, Michael Brickhouse, Joan A Camprodon, Bradford C Dickerson, Mark C Eldaief
Published: 2024

Dear Editor, here we report the behavioral and neurophysiological effects of individualized rTMS administration to language networks in a patient with logopenic variant primary progressive aphasia (lvPPA). The core features of lvPPA include impaired single-word retrieval in spontaneous speech, and impaired repetition [1]. There are currently no treatments definitively shown to slow, stop or reverse the progression of the primary speech and language symptoms in lvPPA.

Neuromodulation modalities, such as repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) and transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS) have emerged as putative treatment options. A recent meta-analysis found strong positive effects supporting the efficacy of active tDCS and/or TMS over sham in improving language performance in PPA, specifically naming and grammatical/speech abilities [2]. While such results are encouraging, no published studies, to our knowledge, have explored the mechanisms of action of such effects. Furthermore, prior studies have employed traditional standardized language tests without evaluating measures of functional verbal communication. Here, we investigate whether rTMS—individually-targeted to each PPA patient’s language network—can improve a patient’s standardized language testing and metrics of functional communication. We also explored whether putative language improvements were accompanied by changes in the functional connectivity of a patient’s individually defined language network.

The patient was a 70-year-old right-handed man who presented with word-finding and spelling difficulty 5 years prior to study enrollment. At the time of his experimental participation, he was fully independent in all activities of daily living, had no neuropsychiatric symptoms and had relative sparing of cognitive domains outside of language. The patient was diagnosed with lvPPA by our multi-disciplinary team after a comprehensive speech and language evaluation [3] in accordance with PPA diagnostic criteria [1]. The participant had a CDR global score of 1, fitting criteria for mild dementia. Neuropsychological data indicate relatively spared attention, visuospatial/visuoconstruction skills, and aspects of executive functioning (e.g., response inhibition) as described in Table 1 of Supplementary Material (SM). Elevated signal on amyloid and tau PET imaging supported a likely histopathological etiology of Alzheimer’s disease.

The patient was recruited for a double-blind rTMS clinical trial at the Massachusetts General Hospital Frontotemporal Disorders (MGH FTD). The methods are comprehensively outlined in the SM, and involved a comprehensive language assessment, computational linguistic analysis, MRI data acquisition and processing as well as the application of rTMS. To identify the target for stimulation, the patient underwent a language functional localizer task [4]. This yielded a significant BOLD activation cluster in the caudal middle temporal gyrus. This cluster was subsequently used as a seed for functional connectivity correlation with all other voxels in the brain using resting-sate functional connectivity MRI (fMRI). This identified a significant cluster of functional correlation within the left posterior inferior frontal gyrus (pIFG), which served as the target for stimulation. The decision to stimulate the pIFG is supported by evidence indicating that its stimulation enhances phonological processing and action naming during word production in healthy individuals [5,6]. Additionally, this target was used in a tDCS trial targeting PPA [7].

The patient received high frequency (20 Hz) rTMS over the left pIFG, applied over five consecutive days, with two sessions per day. The period between the last Active TMS session and the first Sham TMS (washout period) was three weeks long. The participant did not receive any medications or speech therapy during the trial. To assess functional connectivity changes after rTMS, we calculated the mean connectivity between the pIFG target and other key language network regions of interest (ROIs). Details on neuroimaging processing are described in the SM. The language tasks used, and the corresponding results at baseline and after active and sham conditions, are outlined in Fig. 1. We used a picture naming description task to evaluate several metrics of functional communication before and after rTMS. Those metrics aim to describe the density of information conveyed by the patients’ utterances and are described in the SM. To determine the statistical significance of the difference of language features of functional communication between the sessions, we used a bootstrapping method on the language samples of age-matched healthy individuals describing the same picture (for details see SM). Descriptive statistics were used to compare the scores on standardized language tasks (Fig. 1).

Following active but not sham TMS, the patient improved on the hypothesized language measures that represented vulnerabilities at baseline—the Boston Naming Test and the WAB repetition scale (Fig. 1B and E).

Computational psycholinguistic analysis of picture-elicited connected speech showed that the patient improved on functional communication measures as well (Fig. 1F). Informativeness of speech increased after active rTMS: the patient was able to retrieve significantly more complex syntactic structures (p < 0.03) with a trend to retrieve more specific words (p < 0.08). Other features showed a trend toward the predicted direction but did not reach statistical significance using a conservative bootstrapping technique. There was no statistical significance nor trend toward the predicted direction after sham for any of the tested variables.

Mirroring the pattern of the behavioral results, the voxel-wise whole-brain analysis revealed that functional connectivity of the pIFG stimulation target was stronger in widespread areas of the cerebral cortex—especially including functionally-connected regions of the language network— immediately following the active stimulation compared with baseline and post-sham stimulation (Fig. 1D). Functional connectivity between the pIFG target and the language network ROIs became stronger by ~39 % immediately following active rTMS relative to baseline, whereas after sham it increased by ~27 %. Since we had only an initial baseline condition, it is most likely that the increase in functional connectivity following sham-TMS merely represented residual connectivity strength after the active-TMS session. Our findings are consistent with increases in brain metabolism observed after rTMS in other variants of PPA [8].

In this study, we provide evidence that non-invasive brain stimulation improves specific language impairments in a patient with lvPPA. While this has previously been demonstrated in other clinical studies and case reports [8–10], we provide a putative mechanism for these effects: language improvements were accompanied by increased functional connectivity within the language network (i.e., the functional connectivity of the pIFG target). Furthermore, this study is the first to use computational linguistic tools to analyze functional communication as an outcome measure in rTMS clinical trials in PPA. Our findings suggest that rTMS increases the informativeness of speech which may offer the opportunity to improve everyday functional communication in patients with PPA.

Our results support an improvement in the informativeness of speech after active but not after sham stimulation. The patient produced richer language after rTMS, using more complex syntax (lower syntax frequency) and trends toward more specific words (lower content word and verb frequency), and higher lexical entropy. Future studies with larger sample sizes will help further elucidate the increase in functional communication following neuromodulation.

Our results support that neuromodulation studies for PPA should define and target the language network in an individualized manner, measure language function following repeated sessions of rTMS, including measures of functional communication, and measure language network functional connectivity. In summary, while not a disease-modifying therapy, our findings support the potential utility of neuromodulation, perhaps in conjunction with speech-language therapy, as a symptomatic treatment capable of improving symptoms and, ultimately, quality of life, in PPA patients.
