Mycobacterium tuberculosis-induced oligodendrocyte apoptosis promotes astrocyte-mediated cell-in-cell formation and phagocytosis in tuberculous meningitis
Authors: Jinli Feng, Feng Qiu, Zubiao Niu, Ming Fang, Jianghui Yang, Li Xiao, Jun Zhao, Qiang Sun, Jiatang Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo determine whether cell-in-cell (CIC) structures exist in tuberculous meningitis (TBM) and to explore their clinical significance.MethodsPathological brain biopsies from TBM patients were retrospectively analyzed using H&E and immunohistochemistry, with a focus on CIC structures. Multicolor immunofluorescence and in vitro coculture of astrocytes and oligodendrocytes were employed to explore CIC characteristics and underlying mechanisms. Flow cytometry was used to assess Bacillus Calmette-Guérin (BCG)-induced oligodendrocyte apoptosis.ResultsCIC structures (CICs) were identified in TBM brain tissues. Astrocytes were confirmed as target cells, while oligodendrocytes and neurons acted as effector cells. Astrocytes engulfed oligodendrocytes both in vivo and in vitro. BCG infection induced oligodendrocyte apoptosis and significantly enhanced CIC formation and phagocytosis. Pretreatment with staurosporine also promoted these processes. CICs observed in TBM primarily resemble internalized types such as phagocytosis.ConclusionMycobacterium tuberculosis-induced oligodendrocyte apoptosis promotes astrocyte-mediated CIC formation and phagocytosis, suggesting CIC as a novel pathophysiological mechanism in TBM.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-11934-y.Keywords: Tuberculous meningitis, Cell-in-cell, Astrocyte, Oligodendrocyte, Apoptosis, Phagocytosis

Introduction

Although much progress has been made in its diagnosis and treatment, tuberculosis (TB) remains a global health threat, especially in developing countries. TB is one of the top 10 causes of death worldwide and is the leading cause of death from infectious diseases. According to the WHO 2020 annual TB report, 10 million people are infected by TB worldwide, resulting in more than 1.4 million deaths. There are more than 100,000 new cases of tuberculous meningitis (TBM) per year, which is the most lethal and disabling form of tuberculosis [1]. Currently, antibiotic treatment for TBM is similar to treatment for general tuberculosis, which often cannot achieve satisfactory effects and leaves many patients with serious disabilities and psychological disorders. Although some novel antituberculosis drugs are becoming available, they are not commonly used in the clinic due to their relatively high cost and severe side effects. China ranks among the top three countries worldwide in terms of the prevalence and associated deaths of tuberculosis, which poses a major threat to public health.

The cell-in-cell (CIC) process describes the phenomenon in which one or more living cells (effector cells) internalize into another living cell (host or target cell), producing biological effects. CIC activity has been known for more than 100 years, but its role in biology has been largely unknown until recently. An increasing number of studies have demonstrated that CIC structures (CICs) may be identified in various homogeneous and heterogeneous cells and is closely related to disease pathogenesis. Tumor, infection, hunger, ultraviolet radiation, and chemical exposure may all induce CICs, which further participate in additional physiological and pathological processes in the body [2, 3].

In several other central nervous system diseases, such as multiple sclerosis, Creutzfeldt‒Jakob disease and metabolic encephalopathy, oligodendrocytes may enter the cytoplasm of astrocytes. Furthermore, intact and activated lymphocytes can be found in the astrocyte cytoplasm in multiple sclerosis plaques and brain tumors [4–6]. To the best of our knowledge, the role of CICs in TBM has not been report. We hypothesized that Mycobacterium tuberculosis (MTB) infection induces oligodendrocyte apoptosis, thereby promoting CIC formation and astrocyte phagocytosis in TBM. This study aims to: (i) identify CIC structures in TBM brain tissue; (ii) determine the mechanistic link between MTB-induced apoptosis and CIC formation; and (iii) explore the pathophysiological significance of this process.

Materials and methods

Patient selection

The brain tissue samples and clinical data of all patients were collected from the Pathology Department at the Department of Neurology and Tuberculosis Medicine of the Eighth Medical Center of the PLA General Hospital. All patients included in this study were ultimately diagnosed with tuberculous meningitis and responded well to anti-tuberculosis treatment. Patients with severe organ dysfunction, tumors, or autoimmune diseases were excluded.

Cell lines

The human astrocyte cell line HA1800 (ATCC, YLK-XB0459h, USA) was maintained in DMEM/F12 (Gibco, C11330500BT, USA) supplemented with EGF (Sigma, E4127, GER) and B27 (Gibco, 17504044, USA). The human oligodendrocyte cell line MO3.13 (Shanghai Cell Bank, BFN607200612, CHN) was maintained in high-glucose DMEM (Gibco, C11995500BT, USA). TrypLE TM Express Enzyme (Gibco, 12604-013, USA) was used for cell digestion. Penicillin‒streptomycin was not added to the cell culture medium to avoid potential interference with future Bacillus Calmette-Guérin (BCG) experiments. BCG was selected for biosafety and its ability to simulate MTB’s immune effects in vitro.

Cell suspension culture

Astrocytes and oligodendrocytes were harvested, resuspended in 1 ml of serum-free medium and stained with Green CellTracker (Invitrogen, C2925, USA) and Red CellTracker (Invitrogen, C34552, USA). The cells were then cultured for an additional 30 min. Next, the cells were centrifuged and rinsed with PBS 3 times before being resuspended in 1 ml of complete medium and mixed at a 1:1 ratio before being seeded onto a Petri dish that was pre-embedded with 0.5% soft agar. The dish was placed in an incubator and incubated in suspension at 37 °C for 8 h. The suspension was collected and centrifuged at 1000 r/min for 3 min, after which the supernatant was discarded. The cell pellet was resuspended by adding an appropriate amount of PBS (5 × 104 cells/200 µl of PBS). Immunofluorescence staining was performed on cytospin (WESCOR CYTOPRO 7620, USA) preparations with 200 µl of the homogeneous cell suspension centrifuged at 400 r/min for 3 min. After centrifugation, the slides were quickly removed and placed in a light-protected box before fixation with 4% PFA for 10–12 min. Then, 20 µl of DAPI (ZSGB, ZLI-9557, CHN) was added dropwise to the cells before they were observed under a fluorescence microscope.

Tissue multicolor fluorescence Immunolabeling

A total of 10 brain tissue samples collected from patients with tuberculous meningitis were used for immunostaining. The markers labeled by the primary antibodies used in this study were GFAP (ZSGB, ZM-0118, CHN) for astrocytes, NeuN (ZSGB, ZM-0352, CHN) for neurons, Olig2 (ZSGB, ZA-0561, CHN) for oligodendrocytes, TMEM119 (Abcam, ab185333, UK) for microglia, CD3 (ZSGB, ZM-0417, CHN) for T lymphocytes, CD20 (ZSGB, ZM-0039, CHN) for B lymphocytes, CD68 (ZSGB, ZM-0464, CHN) for macrophages, LCA (ZSGB, ZM-0138, CHN) for leukocytes, CD177(ZSGB, ZM-0058) for neutrophil, CD1α (ZSGB, ZM-0027, CHN) for dendritic cells and CD57 (ZSGB, ZM-0058, CHN) for NK cells. The secondary antibodies used in this study were Alexa Fluor 568-conjugated anti-mouse (Invitrogen, A11031, USA), Alexa Fluor 568-conjugated anti-rabbit (Invitrogen, A11036, USA), Alexa Fluor 647-conjugated anti-mouse (Invitrogen, A21236, USA) and Alexa Fluor 647-conjugated anti-rabbit (Invitrogen, A21245, USA) antibodies. HRP-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG (CST, #7074, USA) and HRP-conjugated anti-mouse IgG (CST, #7076, USA) were used. Tissue multicolor fluorescent immunolabeling was performed with an Opal Multiplex Tissue Staining Kit (Perkin Elmer, NEL791001KT, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

All slides were counterstained with DAPI to visualize the nuclei and mounted with antifade reagent (Invitrogen, S36936, USA). Multispectral images were taken with the TMA modules of the Vectra® Automated Imaging System (Akoya, Japan) with a 20× objective lens. A Nuance system (Akoya, Japan) was used to construct libraries of each spectrum (DAPI, FITC, Cy5, and Cy3) and unmix multispectral images after minimizing the effect of autofluorescence in high contrast. The InForm automated image analysis software package (Akoya, Japan) was used for batch analysis of multispectral images based on specified algorithms. Z-stack imaging with deconvolution was performed to confirm the intracellular localization of effector cells.

The live-cell workstation for examination of cell-in-cell structure formation

Astrocytes and oligodendrocytes were cultured to optimal cell viability and were digested. The cell suspension was centrifuged, and the supernatant was discarded. The cell pellet was resuspended in DMEM/F12 complete medium, and live cell tracing dyes of different colors were added separately and incubated in an incubator (MCO-15AC, Sanyo, Japan) for 20 min. Then, the dye was removed by centrifugation, and the cells were resuspended in fresh medium and mixed at a 1:1 ratio to 2 ml before seeding in a glass-bottom 6-well plate. The plate was gently shaken and incubated for 1 h. After 2 h, the cell medium was carefully aspirated and replaced with 3 ml of fresh complete DMEM/F12. The cells were further incubated for an additional 2 h before monitoring under a widefield fluorescence live-cell workstation fluorescence microscope (ECLIPSETI, Akoya, Japan) for 24 h. Thirty random fields were chosen for continuous monitoring, and pictures were taken every 20 min.

Apoptosis of oligodendrocytes

Oligodendrocytes were treated with BCG (10 mg/mL) or sterile PBS (control) for 0, 1, 3, or 6 h before apoptosis measurement. Cell apoptosis was measured by flow cytometry (NovoCyte, ACEA, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol.

Statistical analysis

All the statistical analyses were performed using Prism 8 software. The data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Univariate analysis was conducted to compare the differences between groups. A P value < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Results

CICs were found in the brain tissue of TBM patients

A total of 12 TBM patients with biopsy samples (8 females and 4 males aged 13–49 years; Table 1), were identified at the Department of Pathology of our hospital. The clinical symptoms showed improvements in all patients after antituberculosis treatment. H&E staining of some slides revealed rings of transparent vesicles surrounding the nuclei (Fig. 1). Furthermore, IHC examination revealed that many astrocytes (GFAP-positive cells) had one or more nuclei wrapped in hyaline vesicles in the cytoplasm (Fig. 2A). Further examination revealed recently formed CICs, characterized by incoming effector cells that were surrounded by the zona pellucida, nuclei that were squeezed into a crescent-shaped shape; and an incomplete cell membrane that had not yet fully closed (Fig. 2C). Some effector cells had intact nuclei (Fig. 2A and C), while others were shrinking, broken, and stained shallowly (Fig. 2D and B), suggesting that these effector cells died within the astrocyte population. Similar structural formations were observed in NF-labeled brain tissue, with some nuclei wrapped in vesicles and adjacent nuclei pressed into crescents to form CICs (Fig. 2E). Some Olig2 (oligodendrocyte marker)-positive cells also had hyaline vesicles around the nucleus (Fig. 2G), indicating that oligodendrocytes might have entered other cells. Similar structures were also identified in cells expressing Nestin, a cell marker for neural stem cells (Fig. 2F).

Immunohistochemical staining for GFAP was conducted on 10 brain tissues (2 biopsy samples were lost), and the results were consistent with those of previous pathology examinations. The astrocytes that formed CICs were identified in all 10 brain tissues. In addition, CIC formation was not detected in cells expressing LCA, CD68, CD177, CD57, CD20 or CD3.

The target cells for CIC formation were astrocytes, and the effector cells were oligodendrocytes and neurons

To further identify the target cells and effector cells involved in the formation of CICs, we performed polychromatic fluorescent immunolabeling on brain tissue sections. Four subtypes of nerve cells, namely, neurons, astrocytes, microglia and oligodendrocytes, were labeled with NeuN, GFAP, TMEM119 and Olig2, respectively, as were white blood cells, which were labeled with LCA. Olig2- and NeuN-positive cells were identified in GFAP-positive cells through two-channel spectral resolution analysis (Figs. 3A and 4). According to the statistical analysis, the percentages of CICs formed by astrocytes, oligodendrocytes and neurons were not significantly different (Fig. 3B).

In vitro formation of CICs by human astrocytes and oligodendrocytes

Previous studies have demonstrated that astrocytes and oligodendrocytes may form CICs in vivo. To further examine the interaction between these two types of cells, we cocultured human astrocytes HA1800 and the human oligodendrocyte line Mo3.13 at a ratio of 1:1 in suspension. Consistent with previous studies, our data demonstrated that CICs were formed in the coculture (Fig. 5A). Additionally, we documented the process by which oligodendrocytes enter astrocytes to form CICs in adherent culture (Supplementary Video. 1). Furthermore, the disintegration of oligodendrocytes in astrocytes and their escape from astrocytes were observed, indicating that the oligodendrocytes died or survived in astrocytes (Supplementary Video. 2, Supplementary Video. 3). After addition of BCG (10 mg/L) to the culture medium, the number of CICs formed significantly​ increased (Fig. 5B, Supplementary Video. 4).

BCG infection induced oligodendrocyte apoptosis

The morphology of oligodendrocytes changed after treatment with 10 mg/ml BCG for 6 h. More specifically, a frozen cell membrane, a shrunken cell body, decreased cell‒cell connections, increased amounts of coarse cytoplasmic particles and condensed nuclei and other changes in the morphology of apoptotic cells were observed (Fig. 6A). The apoptosis of oligodendrocytes was measured by flow cytometry at 0, 1, 3, and 6 h after treatment with BCG. We found that BCG-induced oligodendrocyte apoptosis started at the first hour after treatment, and the number of apoptotic cells gradually increased. Most of the observed apoptotic cells were in the early stage (Fig. 6B). Oligodendrocytes treated with staurosporine (100 nmol/L) served as positive controls for apoptosis, while those treated with PBS were used as negative controls.

Phagocytosis of oligodendrocyte fragments by astrocytes

Pathological examination of brain tissue sections from TBM patients revealed that oligodendrocyte fragments were engulfed by astrocytes (Fig. 7A, yellow arrow). To better understand this process, we cocultured astrocytes and oligodendrocytes in vitro and documented the phagocytic process by a living cell workstation (Fig. 7B, yellow arrow; ​Supplementary Video 5). Our data showed that compared to that of the controls, the phagocytic activity of the cells after BCG treatment increased (Fig. 7C). Both in vitro and patient sample examination revealed that in addition to the presence of oligodendrocyte fragments, astrocytes might also engulf other cell fragments (Fig. 7A and B white arrow).

Apoptosis promoted the formation of CICs and phagocytosis in astrocytes

To examine the association between CIC formation and apoptosis, we pretreated oligodendrocytes with the apoptosis-inducing agent staurosprine (100 nmol/L) for 1 h before being cocultured with astrocytes. We found that the formation of CICs was significantly greater after pretreatment, as was the phagocytosis of oligodendrocyte fragments by astrocytes, than that in the control group (Fig. 7D).

Discussion

CICs are divided into two categories, namely, internalized CICs (cell cannibalism, phagocytosis, and enclosis) and invasive CICs (entosis and emperipolesis), based on their different activation mechanisms. For internalized CICs, host cells actively surround and phagocytose effector cells, and this process is initiated by the effector cells for invasive CICs. In contrast, CICs can be divided into homotypic CICs and heterotypic CICs based on the cell type involved [7]. In this study, for the first time, we identified the existence of CICs in the damaged brain tissue of TBM patients. Through polychromatic immunofluorescence labeling, we demonstrated that the target cells of the CICs were astrocytes, while the effector cells were oligodendrocytes and neurons. Additionally, we believe that CICs are formed between astrocytes and oligodendrocytes and that undergo emperipolesis.

Astrocytes and oligodendrocytes play important roles in myelination and demyelinating processes. However, the biological importance of CICs formed by astrocytes and neurons needs to be further studied in the future. The formation of CICs was recapitulated in vitro by coculturing human astrocytes and oligodendrocytes. Our results demonstrated that mitotically active cells were more prone to form CICs, which was consistent with the findings in tumors [8]. We also documented the process of oligodendrocyte disintegration and death in astrocytes, as well as the process by which oligodendrocytes protrude out from astrocytes. Furthermore, we found that the formation of CICs was rare under in vitro culture conditions unless the cells were treated with BCG or staurosprine. Our data indicated that the formation of CICs might be associated with apoptosis and increased significantly under pathological conditions. Given that CICs can also be observed in other diseases, such as glioblastoma, the role of CICs might be conserved in the central nervous system. Previous studies have indicated that CIC may play a protective role and is associated with disease severity [9–11]. However, its specific role is not well understood.

As mentioned earlier, astrocytes are commonly involved in the formation of CICs in some diseases of the nervous system. Similarly, our study of TBM demonstrated that astrocytes may form CICs with a variety of cells, indicating the central role of astrocytes in CIC formation. An increasing number of studies have demonstrated that astrocytes may be activated by pathological stimulation and acquire stem cell characteristics [12]. Some researchers proposed that CIC represents a unique type of atavism and promoted the concepts of “mammalian cell progenitor” and “mammalian cell atavism” [13]. In brain tissue, we found that astrocytes involved in the formation of CICs simultaneously expressed the GFAP and Nestin proteins, supporting the idea that CIC may be related to atavism. CIC formation in TBM is likely to be a primitive mechanism that appears under the inflammatory stimulation caused by tuberculosis infection, resulting in changes in their own biological characteristics, thus affecting the development and prognosis of the disease.

Like autophagy, CIC may help cells obtain nutrients and proliferate under starvation conditions [14], and it has been speculated that nutrient deficiency may induce the formation of CICs. Several previous studies have indicated that glucose deficiency can impair cell integrity by activating the biological regulatory energy factor AMPK and forming CICs with adjacent cells [15]. Our data demonstrated that the number of CICs in astrocytes after coculture with oligodendrocytes increased significantly upon BCG treatment, suggesting that BCG infection may promote the formation of CICs. The glucose concentration in cerebrospinal fluid is reduced in patients with TBM due to the consumption by MTB. This reduced glucose level may lead to an energy-insufficient environment and promote the formation of CICs. Additionally, we confirmed that BCG infection can induce the apoptosis of oligodendrocytes, which may increase the formation of CICs. We further demonstrated that similar to BCG infection, treatment with the apoptosis inducer staurosprine also increased the formation of CICs in astrocytes after coculture with oligodendrocytes. The above results suggest that apoptosis may also contribute to the increased formation of CICs.

An increasing number of studies have confirmed that astrocytes have phagocytic functions. A previous study conducted in an ischemic brain injury rat model demonstrated that astrocytes may gain phagocytic functions and engulf surrounding neurons as well as their dendrites, axons, nerve fibers, immune cells, myelin sheaths and other fragments under the regulation of ABCA1 [16]. In adult mice, astrocytes in the CA1 area of the hippocampus may phagocytize unnecessary excitatory synapses through the MEGF10 receptor to maintain the connectivity of the neural circuit and support cognitive function [17]. Both in vivo and in vitro experiments demonstrated that phagocytosis increased simultaneously with the formation of CICs after treatment with BCG or staurosprine, indicating that these two activities might be regulated by the same signaling pathway. Additionally, in addition to ABCA1 and MEGF10, which mediate astrocyte phagocytosis through lipid transport and apoptotic cell clearance respectively, mTOR signaling has been demonstrated to regulate phagosome maturation and vacuole fission, processes essential for both CIC formation and phagocytosis. Given our observation that CIC formation and phagocytosis increased concurrently after BCG or staurosporine treatment, it is plausible that these events are coordinately regulated by converging pathways involving ABCA1, MEGF10, and mTOR. This hypothesis warrants further mechanistic investigation in future studies.

Interestingly, we also detected some fluorescence-negative components in astrocytes in pathological TBM brain tissue. We speculated that astrocytes not only swallowed the fragments of oligodendrocytes but also engulfed fragments of other cells or themselves, similar to what has been reported in previous studies [16, 17].

Previous studies have demonstrated that the interactions between astrocytes and oligodendrocytes are complicated [18–21]. Our data demonstrated that the body and dendrites of astrocytes became hypertrophic and strongly expressed GFAP, indicating their reactivation status. The formation of CICs by reactive astrocytes and oligodendrocytes may be one of the forms of interaction between the two cell types. Oligodendrocytes not only provide nutrition for axons but also produce myelin sheaths. The conduction of action potentials depends on the integrity of the myelin sheath; therefore, oligodendrocytes are important for maintaining the function of neurons [22, 23]. BCG may induce the apoptosis of oligodendrocytes, impairing the integrity of the myelin sheath and the function of neurons. Additionally, apoptosis may lead to the formation of CICs, suggesting that CICs may play a very important role in the pathological process of TBM. Some studies have demonstrated that microglia may engulf functional active neurons after focal cerebral ischemia and that inhibiting this process can prevent delayed loss of neuronal function and cell death [24, 25]. Therefore, we hypothesized that inhibiting the formation and phagocytosis of astrocyte-associated CICs in TBM may reduce demyelination and protect the function of neurons.

With further research, some breakthroughs have been made in understanding the mechanism of CICs formation, which has potential value for clinical disease diagnosis, therapeutic applications and prognostic determination. Most previous studies examining CICs were conducted in oncology. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study on the role of CICs in TBM. We not only examined the pathologies of human brain tissue from TBM patients but also conducted several in vitro experiments with human astrocytes and oligodendrocytes to explore the underlying mechanism involved. For the first time, the complete process of CICs formation with astrocytes and oligodendrocytes and the phagocytic process of astrocytes were documented. In addition, we found that both processes were promoted by MTB-induced apoptosis. The interaction between astrocytes and oligodendrocytes mediated by CICs in TBM patients will help us gain a deeper understanding of the pathological and physiological changes in TBM patients, thus providing a new approach for treating brain injury in TBM patients.

While astrocyte phagocytosis has been observed in non-infectious contexts such as ischemic injury, our study establishes the first direct evidence that MTB-induced apoptosis drives concurrent CIC formation and phagocytic activation in TBM, uncovering a novel pathogenic mechanism.

Our study has several limitations. First, ethical and practical constraints precluded us from obtaining biopsies from untreated TBM patients or healthy controls. This limitation hinders comparative analyses between these groups and our treated cohort. Second, the generalizability of our findings may be constrained by the small sample size (n = 12). Finally, in vitro models inherently struggle to fully replicate the complex in vivo microenvironment of TBM. Future research should address these limitations by utilizing larger patient cohorts, developing more sophisticated modeling approaches, and employing a dvanced techniques such as 3D reconstruction (e.g., confocal microscopy) to further validate the ultrastructure of CICs.

Conclusions

MTB-induced apoptosis promoted CICs formation and astrocyte phagocytosis, CIC may be involved in the pathological process of TBM, and astrocytes may play an important role in this process.
