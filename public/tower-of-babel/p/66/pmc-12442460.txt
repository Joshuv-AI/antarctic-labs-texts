Transitions and patterns in concurrent use of cigarettes and electronic cigarettes: An intensive longitudinal study of adults who smoke cigarettes and recently initiated e-cigarette use
Authors: Thi Phuong Thao Tran, Terry F Pechacek, David L Ashley, Claire A Spears, Nikita G Kute, Scott R Weaver
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:There is a knowledge gap in the short- and long-term tobacco use patterns of adults who smoke cigarettes and initiate use of electronic cigarettes. This longitudinal study describes concurrent use transitions following e-cigarette initiation among adults who smoked cigarettes.Methods:From December 2020-October 2021, US adults who smoked cigarettes and recently (re-)initiated e-cigarette use within the past month (N = 303) were recruited nationally for a one-year online study consisting of weekly surveys for three months followed by three quarterly surveys. At each survey, current e-cigarette use, smoking status, and frequency of use were assessed to define their concurrent use state: neither vaping nor smoking, vaping only, predominantly vaping, equal dual use, predominantly smoking, or only smoking.Results:During the first 3 months, 74 % of participants shifted their usage states at least once, while 29 % shifted between 9 months and 12 months following e-cigarette initiation. Participants predominantly smoking initially tended to either continue predominantly smoking or returned to exclusively smoking. Those predominantly vaping tended to remain in this state or shift to only vaping. Individuals using both products split early between continued dual use, predominantly vaping, and predominantly smoking. Those only vaping tended to continue, although some reverted to smoking. Overall, initial concurrent use states appear somewhat predictive of trajectories and 12-month outcomes.Conclusions:Additional research on predictors (e.g., demographics, product characteristics, use experiences, perceptions and affect, product availability) of these frequent shifts between concurrent use states on weekly, monthly, and quarterly basis and 12-month outcomes is needed.Keywords: E-cigarettes, Dual use, Cigarettes, Longitudinal transitions

Introduction

The potential population health benefits of initiating vaping for people who smoke cigarettes emphasize the importance of transitions to discontinuation of all cigarette smoking (Coleman et al., 2022; U.S. Department of Health and Human Services., 2020). Studies of transitions among cigarettes, vaping, and concurrent use have observed sustained concurrent use as the prevalent pattern (Brouwer et al., 2020; Piper et al., 2020; Gravely et al., 2020; Sharma et al., 2024; Gallegos-Carrillo et al., 2022). However extant longitudinal studies of these transitions have been limited. Participants heterogenous in their time since initiating e-cigarette use could obfuscate initial and established use patterns. Few, naturalistic studies have prospectively examined people who smoke cigarettes from at or near the point of e-cigarette initiation (Conner et al., 2021). Additionally, the several studies employing only long, typically annual, assessments cannot capture dynamic associations and use transitions that occur on a shorter temporal scale and might explain long-term use outcomes, (Brouwer et al., 2020; Coleman et al., 2019; Ebrahimi Kalan and Brewer, 2023; Stanton et al., 2020) while studies limited to short-term intensive or ecological momentary assessments are unable to examine how dynamic associations and trajectories relate to long-term outcomes (Berg et al., 2019; Hedeker et al., 2024). Intensive longitudinal assessment of adults who concurrently smoke cigarettes and use e-cigarettes from at or near their time of initiating e-cigarettes coupled with long-term assessments are needed to identify potentially important initial concurrent use patterns and examine dynamic processes that could drive long-term tobacco use outcomes. Furthermore, most studies on dual use have defined it monolithically, potentially muddying interpretation and obscuring understanding of associations with long-term tobacco use and health outcomes when these associations likely differ by specific pattern of dual use (Gravely et al., 2020; Sharma et al., 2024; Borland et al., 2019; Gravely et al., 2022). Addressing these limitations, this intensive longitudinal study with one-year follow-up describes the concurrent use transitions following e-cigarette initiation among adults who smoked cigarettes.

Method

Study design and participants

Participants were 303 US adults (21 + years old) recruited via targeted social media ads (December 2020 – October 2021) to participate in a one-year, intensive longitudinal survey study consisting of an eligibility survey, 12 weekly surveys (months 0–3) and follow-up surveys at months 6, 9, and 12 (Mavreles Ogrodnick et al., 2024). Eligibility required lifetime use of > 100 cigarettes, and having smoked cigarettes within the past 2 months. Eligibility also required newly initiating e-cigarette use (or re-initiated use after 1 + years of nonuse) within the prior month. Given the lag between e-cigarette (re-)initiation and enrollment, it was possible that some participants discontinued e-cigarette use by the first survey, as was the case among a few participants who began the study as exclusive smokers (n = 5). Survey completion ranged from 84-100 %. The sample characteristics are shown in Table S1. Participants were compensated with a $25 e-gift card for completing the baseline survey, $10 to $30 for each completed weekly survey, and $20 to $40 for each completed quarterly survey, for a maximum total compensation of $280 in e-gift cards.

Measures

At each wave, the concurrent use of cigarettes and e-cigarettes was assessed by questions asking if they currently smoke cigarettes [use electronic nicotine products] some days, every day or not at all and on how many of the past 7 (weekly surveys, except eligibility survey) or 30 days (6-, 9-, and 12-month surveys). Based on these questions, participants were categorized at each wave as follows: neither currently smoking nor vaping, only vaping, predominantly vaping, equal dual use, predominantly smoking, or only smoking (see Fig. S1) (Borland et al., 2019; Pechacek et al., 2016). We characterized the health implications of transitions as likely positive (greatly or completely quitting cigarettes), negative (more or exclusive cigarette smoking), or ambiguous based on the judgement of the authors according to the current state of scientific evidence (see Table S2). As shown in Table S2, transitions from any level of smoking or vaping to no tobacco use (i.e., neither smoking or vaping) were characterized as having positive health implications and transitioning from using neither product to any level of smoking and/or vaping were deemed negative. Since e-cigarette aerosol contains fewer and lower concentrations of most toxic substances compared to the smoke from traditional combustible tobacco cigarettes, switching from only smoking to only vaping or predominantly vaping are deemed positive transitions to reduce health harms (National Academies of Sciences E, Medicine, 2018). Similarly, switching from predominant smoking or dual use to predominant vaping or only vaping is likely to have positive health impact. However, because e-cigarette use is not harmless, switching from neither smoking nor vaping to any level of vaping has negative health implications. The health implications of several categories are noted as ambiguous based on the current state of evidence. For example, dual use exposes users to the toxicants contained in both cigarettes and e-cigarettes which can be impacted by product use behavior; if users do not eventually quit smoking there could be negative health impact (Coleman et al., 2022). It is common for dual users to eventually transition back to exclusive cigarette smoking, and e-cigarette use has been associated with higher risk of smoking relapse among former smokers (Piper et al., 2020; Barufaldi et al., 2021). On the other hand, given the potential for e-cigarette use to facilitate smoking cessation, (Hartmann-Boyce et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2021; Hajek et al., 2019; Chan et al., 2021) switching from only smoking to dual use could eventually have positive health impact if smoking continues to be minimized (and ideally ended) over time.

Statistical analysis

We graphically examined individual-level longitudinal transitions and patterns by initial state and aggregate-level flows between concurrent use states across waves. We calculated the number of weekly transitions during the initial quarter following e-cigarette initiation and for each 3-month period. Except where participants dropped out (i.e., missed all subsequent surveys), if participants missed a weekly survey, we carried forward their last known concurrent use state, and for missed 6-, 9-, and 12-month surveys, we imputed using the nearest survey. We also conducted sensitivity analysis using the new definition of equal dual use with a wider band of ± 1 day in the past week. Analyses were performed using Rstudio version 2021.9.351.

Results

Fig. 1 shows individual-level cigarette and e-cigarette use state across all assessments following initiation of e-cigarettes, sorted by initial cigarette and e-cigarette use status. The modal initial state was concurrent use with predominantly smoking (n = 122; 40.3 %), followed by (equal) dual use (n = 79; 26.1 %), predominantly vaping (n = 53; 17.5 %), only vaping (n = 44; 14.5 %), and only smoking (n = 5; 1.7 %). Within three months of e-cigarette initiation, transitions away from their baseline concurrent use state were common: 74 % of participants transitioned at least once week-to-week, and 58 % were in a different state than baseline at 3 months. Transitioning continued throughout the follow-up period, though less frequently (29 % transitioned between 9 and 12 months). Examining participant temporal flows between states reveals distinct patterns among initial concurrent users according to their relative frequency of vaping versus smoking (Fig. S2). Those who were predominantly smoking tended to remain predominantly smoking or revert to only smoking, whereas those predominantly vaping tended to progress to only vaping or neither smoking nor vaping. The flow of participants who were dual users was roughly balanced between transitioning to predominant or exclusive smoking versus to predominant or exclusive vaping. The frequency of transitions declined with time, though remained fairly substantial (29 % between 9 and 12 months, Fig. 2). The proportion of stasis increased over time. During the first three months, 26 % of transitions were deemed positive compared to the 8 % deemed negative. However, thereafter the proportion of transitions that were positive increased. The above patterns did not substantively change in sensitivity analyses that accounted for a wider band of +/−1 days of use in the past week (Fig. S3 and S4).

Discussion

The results of our study show that initial concurrent use patterns appear to be associated with long-term tobacco and e-cigarette use. We also found, consistent with both long-term (Gravely et al., 2020; Parnham et al., 2023) and short-term follow-up studies, (Gallegos-Carrillo et al., 2022; Piper et al., 2020) that a high proportion of individuals who soon after initiating vaping were exclusively smoking or exclusively vaping tended to continue doing so one year later. Among participants concurrently using e-cigarettes and smoking at our initial assessment, those who were predominantly smoking exhibited a clear reduced tendency to transition to exclusive e-cigarette use relative to those who were predominantly vaping at the initial assessment. This finding aligns with the few studies that have differentiated concurrent use patterns and supports the need for more refined distinction and measurement of concurrent cigarette and e-cigarette use (Gravely et al., 2020; Sharma et al., 2024; Borland et al., 2019). However, while Gravely and colleagues (Gravely et al., 2020) reported a small proportion (11.7 %) of discontinued vaping (i.e., switched to only smoking or neither smoking nor vaping) among those initially predominantly vaping, our study revealed a significantly higher rate, with approximately 40 % dis-continuing vaping after one year of follow-up. These differences could be due to our respective studies employing different subcategories of concurrent use, the greater participant heterogeneity in e-cigarette use history among participants or the longer follow-up period (i.e.,18 months) in Gravely and colleagues’ study (Gravely et al., 2020). Additionally, Levy and colleagues reported that more frequent use of e-cigarettes was associated with an increased likelihood of smoking cessation (Levy et al., 2018). It is consistent with our study that showed a high proportion of individuals only vaping maintained this state, and those who predominantly vaped initially tended to transition to an exclusive vaping state and quit smoking.

The initial approximately 3-month period after e-cigarette initiation was marked by instability in concurrent use patterns with the vast majority transitioning among exclusive and concurrent use states at least once. Overall, these transitions appeared to be more positive (greatly or completely quitting cigarettes) than negative (more or exclusive cigarette smoking) by more than a 3:1 ratio. However, while the number of transitions progressively decreased in subsequently three-month periods, the ratio of transitions deemed positive to those deemed negative equalized. These results suggest that the early period following e-cigarette initiation is distinct and marked by fluctuations in smoking and vaping behaviors. These findings support the importance of future research with intensive assessments during the period following e-cigarette initiation for examining putative driving factors of transitions, such as product characteristics and availability, perceptions and affect, use expectancies, and subjective experiences of e-cigarette use. Although patterns of using cigarettes and/or e-cigarettes tended to stabilize as individuals approached one year following e-cigarette initiation, nearly one-third of participants transitioned between 9- and 12-months following e-cigarette initiation. Among the first intensive longitudinal studies with long-term follow-up of e-cigarette initiation among adults who smoked cigarettes, our findings have several implications for future tobacco regulatory science research and public policy recommendations. Given that many transitions in tobacco use happen in the first three months of initiating e-cigarettes, this could be a critical period for education or intervention to help people transition completely away from cigarettes. Future research should also consider follow-up assessments beyond one-year since longer-term follow-up might be necessary for a more comprehensive understanding of sustained patterns of use.

This study has several limitations. First, while the sample comprised entirely of new adopters of e-cigarettes is a strength, it is a convenience, online sample that is not representative, thereby limiting generalization to the broader population. Second, while sample attrition of 15 % is rather minimal for a one-year longitudinal online study, missing data were singly imputed with the last or temporally nearest value, potentially leading to underestimation of transitions. Third, while our classification scheme for concurrent users is an improvement over much prior research, our sample was not large enough to use the more refined classification system suggested by Boreland and colleagues (Sharma et al., 2024; Borland et al., 2019). Fourth, our study was not designed to quantify the degree of change to permit statistical comparisons among groups defined by initial concurrent use status. Finally, use status was based solely on self-reported data without biochemical confirmation.

Conclusion

Our intensive longitudinal study offers valuable insights into the fluctuating concurrent and sole use transitions of cigarettes and e-cigarettes following initiation of e-cigarette use. While the patterns of using cigarettes and/or e-cigarettes were more likely to stabilize at the 12-month after e-cigarette initiation, longer-term follow-up remains crucial for a comprehensive understanding of these patterns. Future studies aimed at elucidating the antecedent drivers and long-term consequences of concurrent use patterns and trajectories should aim to utilize both frequent (intensive) assessments, particularly during the immediate period following initiation of e-cigarettes, and long-term follow-up assessments and distinguish among the varied use patterns that fall under the concurrent use umbrella and which might have distinct long-term trajectories and outcomes.
