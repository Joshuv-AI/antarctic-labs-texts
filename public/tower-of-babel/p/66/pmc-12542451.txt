Effects of sodium-glucose co-transporter-2 inhibitors on the risk of nephrolithiasis and urinary tract infections in Thai patients with type 2 diabetes: a hospital-based cohort study
Authors: Thitiya Lukkunaprasit, Amarit Tansawet, Sukanya Siriyotha, Panu Looareesuwan, Junwei Yang, Patratorn Kunakorntham, Pokket Sirisreetreerux, Thunyarat Anothaisintawee, Gareth J. McKay, John Attia, Ammarin Thakkinstian
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNephrolithiasis is a multifactorial disease with many contributing factors varying across races, ethnicities, and sociocultural backgrounds. Diabetes is a significant independent risk factor for nephrolithiasis. We aimed to investigate the association between sodium-glucose co-transporter-2 inhibitors (SGLT2is) and the risk of nephrolithiasis in Thai patients with type 2 diabetes (T2D).MethodsThis retrospective cohort study of T2D patients used real-world data from Ramathibodi Hospital, Bangkok, Thailand. Adult T2D patients aged 18 years or older who had received SGLT2is, dipeptidyl peptidase 4 inhibitors (DPP4is), sulfonylureas (SUs), or thiazolidinediones (TZDs) were included. Inverse probability weighting with regression adjustment and Cox proportional hazards models were applied to estimate the effect of SGLT2is on the risk of nephrolithiasis as the primary outcome, and on the risk of urinary tract infections (UTIs) as a secondary outcome.ResultsA total of 17,821 patients were identified between 2015 and 2023, of whom 5,626 received an SGLT2i. The median follow-up time was 1.8 years. The incidence rate of nephrolithiasis was 7.7, 18.5, 20.5, and 12.1 per 1000 person-years in the SGLT2i, DPP4i, SU, and TZD groups, respectively. The risk of nephrolithiasis was significantly lower for patients who had received SGLT2is compared to those who had received DPP4is (HR = 0.45; 95% CI: 0.35, 0.58), SUs (HR = 0.37; 95% CI: 0.28, 0.48), and TZDs (HR = 0.60; 95% CI: 0.43, 0.85). The risk of UTIs appeared lower with SGLT2is compared to DPP4is (HR = 0.85; 95% CI: 0.66, 1.10) and TZDs (HR = 0.78; 95% CI: 0.55, 1.11), with a statistically significant reduction observed only relative to SUs (HR = 0.74; 95% CI: 0.56, 0.96).ConclusionsOur real-world study suggested a lower risk of nephrolithiasis in Thai T2D patients treated with SGLT2is, without any increase in UTIs. These real-world findings identify added benefit for T2D patients prescribed SGLT2is for nephrolithiasis prevention.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13098-025-01979-z.

Background

Nephrolithiasis is a condition associated with declining kidney function, acute kidney injury, chronic kidney disease (CKD), and cardiovascular disease (CVD) [1–4], with reported prevalence of 11% in the US [5] and 1–19% in Asia [6]. Diabetes is a significant risk factor for nephrolithiasis potentially leading to more severe complications in diabetic patients with nephrolithiasis, as evidenced by observational studies and meta-analyses [7–9].

Sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors (SGLT2is) are antihyperglycemic drugs that have been shown to reduce cardiovascular death, heart failure hospitalization, and CKD [10–12]. Interestingly, recent cohort studies have shown reduced nephrolithiasis risk (26–49%) in patients treated with SGLT2is compared to glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1 RAs) or dipeptidyl peptidase 4 inhibitors (DPP4is) [13, 14]. These findings were also supported by a meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials (RCTs), which showed a 36% reduction in nephrolithiasis risk in patients with type 2 diabetes (T2D) receiving SGLT2is compared to placebo [15]. However, this evidence is largely derived from Caucasian populations and does not sufficiently consider the multifactorial variation in nephrolithiasis etiology, including genetics [16], diet [17, 18], ethnic, and sociocultural backgrounds. A single large-scale Asian population-based cohort study using propensity score matching, demonstrated a similar risk reduction (46%) for SGLT2is compared to DPP4is [19].

Urinary tract infections (UTIs) are a T2D complication associated with nephrolithiasis [20]. Although the US FDA has issued a safety warning regarding UTIs and SGLT2i use, findings from meta-analyses are inconclusive [21–25]. Therefore, this real-world, single centre, hospital-based Thai cohort study was conducted to investigate the effects of SGLT2is on nephrolithiasis risk, as well as UTI occurrence in T2D patients.

Methods

Study design, data sources, and study participants

This study emulated a target trial by using a retrospective cohort design, utilizing real-world T2D data from a data warehouse maintained by the Department of Clinical Epidemiology and Biostatistics at Ramathibodi Hospital, Bangkok, Thailand, from January 2010 – December 2023. Adult T2D patients were identified from the hospital databases using ICD-9/ICD-10 codes and/or antihyperglycemic medication prescriptions. T2D patients were included in this study if they received any of the following antihyperglycemic medications; SGLT2is, DPP4is, sulfonylureas (SUs), or thiazolidinediones (TZDs), with documented use from January 2015 onwards, corresponding to the initial availability of SGLT2is. Patients who developed nephrolithiasis before or within one month of initial antihyperglycemic medication prescription were excluded (Fig. 1). The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Faculty of Medicine, Ramathibodi Hospital, Mahidol University (COA MURA2024/736).

Study treatments, outcomes and covariates

The study treatment was SGLT2is while the comparators included DPP4is, SUs, and TZDs which were identified from the hospital medication databases., The index date (time zero) was defined as the initial prescription date for any of these medications, regardless of subsequent prescriptions, to emulate an RCT with an intention-to-treat (ITT) approach.

The primary outcome was nephrolithiasis, defined using ICD-10 and ICD-9-CM codes (N20.0, N20.1, N20.2, N20.9, N13.2, 55.01, 55.03, 55.04, 55.11, 56.0, 56.2, and 98.51) and confirmed by imaging reports (intravenous pyelography (IVP), kidney, ureter, and bladder ultrasound (KUB US), abdominal US, KUB Computerized Tomography (CT), and abdominal CT). The secondary outcome was post-index-date UTIs, identified by ICD-10 codes (N10, N12, N15.1, N15.9, N30.0, N30.8, N30.9, N39.0) and confirmed by urine analysis and antibiotic prescriptions. Patients were followed up from the index date until the first outcome occurrence or the end of the study period (December 2023), whichever occurred first.

Patient baseline characteristics were obtained at the index date and included age, sex, body mass index (BMI), healthcare coverage schemes [i.e., Civil Servant Medical Benefit Scheme (CSMBS) versus others (Universal Health Coverage, Social Security Scheme, and self-pay)], diabetes duration, fasting plasma glucose (FPG), glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), uric acid, total cholesterol (TC), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), triglycerides, comorbidities including hypertension (HT), CVD, peripheral vascular disease (PVD), CKD, diabetic retinopathy (DR), history of UTIs, dyslipidemia (DLP). Other medications recorded included insulin, statins, renin-angiotensin system (RAS) inhibitors (including angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEis) and angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs)), thiazides, and loop diuretic (furosemide). Data were extracted from electronic medical records (demographics), the laboratory database (laboratory values), ICD-9/10 codes (comorbidities), and the medication database (prescriptions). Diabetes duration was defined as the time from diabetes diagnosis until the index date. eGFR was calculated using the 2009 Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration creatinine Equation [26]. BMI classification was according to Asia–Pacific guidelines [27].

Statistical analysis

The date of initial SGLT2i prescription was matched to the date of an initial prescription of the other comparators within 6 months. Baseline patient characteristics were described by treatment groups as numbers (%) for categorical variables and mean (standard deviation [SD]) or median (interquartile range [IQR]) for continuous variables. These corresponding data were compared between groups using Chi-squared/Fisher’s exact tests and analysis of variance/Kruskal-Wallis tests, where appropriate. Missing values at baseline were imputed using multiple imputations by chained equations with 80 replications.

Treatment effect models with inverse probability weighting (IPW) were applied to estimate the effects of SGLT2is as follows. First, a univariate multinomial logistic regression treatment model was constructed by fitting each baseline covariate (i.e., age, sex, BMI, healthcare coverage scheme, diabetes duration, FPG, HbA1c, eGFR, TC, LDL-C, HDL-C, triglycerides, HT, CVD, PVD, CKD, DR, history of UTIs, DLP, statins, ACEis/ARBs) separately by treatment prescription. Then, covariates with a p-value < 0.1 or of previously reported clinical significance (OR > 2.0 or < 0.5) were simultaneously included in a multivariate multinomial logistic regression model. Stepwise forward selection with likelihood ratio testing (p-value < 0.05) and clinical significance was applied to select covariates for the final fully adjusted model.

A propensity score reflecting the probability of receiving each treatment was created for the four treatment groups (i.e., different propensity scores for different treatment groups). An outcome model was constructed using Cox proportional hazards that was weighted by 1/propensity score for each treatment group, by fitting treatment with baseline covariates (i.e., age, sex, BMI, healthcare coverage scheme, diabetes duration, FPG, HbA1c, eGFR, uric acid, TC, LDL-C, HDL-C, triglycerides, HT, CVD, PVD, CKD, DR, history of UTIs, DLP, statins, ACEis/ARBs, insulin, thiazides, and furosemide). Model selection was performed using the same criteria as the treatment model. Finally, model assumptions were assessed as follows: First, a balance of covariates between treatment groups was assessed by estimating weighted standardized mean differences (SMD) and the variance ratio between the treatment and control groups for each covariate. A weighted SMD close to 0 or < 0.1 with a variance ratio of 1 was taken as indicating well-balanced covariates between treatment groups [28]. Second, the treatment overlap assumption was checked to ensure that each patient had a positive probability of being assigned to each treatment option, which can be checked by graphically plotting propensity scores between treatment and control groups on the x-axis and a propensity score density on the y-axis. Third, the proportional hazard assumption was checked by a global Chi-square test and by visual inspection of failure curves.

Hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for nephrolithiasis and UTI outcomes were estimated. To confirm the robustness of the main analysis, sensitivity analyses were performed as follows: (1) an analysis that included only patients who had previously received metformin; (2) an analysis for which a history of metformin use was further adjusted in the outcome model; (3) a modified ITT analysis that included censoring patients when treatment switching/changing occurred; and (4) a time-varying analysis that allowed all variables including treatment to be varied with time. (5) an E-value was calculated to estimate the effect of unmeasured potential confounding on the risk of nephrolithiasis. The E-value represents the minimum strength of association that an unmeasured confounder would need to have with both the treatment and the outcome to explain away the association observed, conditional on the measured covariates [29].

All analyses were performed using STATA version 18.0. (Stata Corp., College Station, TX, USA). A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 107,942 adult patients with T2D were identified from the hospital databases from 2010 to 2023. Of these, patients were excluded for the following reasons; 53,973 did not receive any of the designated treatments of interest (SGLT2is, DPP4is, SUs, or TZDs); 16,987 received either DPP4is, SUs, or TZDs before 2015, which was the date at which SGLT2is were first available in this setting; 2,721 patients had nephrolithiasis before the study index date; 10,183 had insufficient data for analysis or imputation; 308 had nephrolithiasis within one month after the index date; and 5,949 had an initial prescription date of DPP4is, SUs, or TZDs that could not be matched to the initial SGLT2i prescription date. Finally, 17,821 patients were eligible for inclusion in this study (see Fig. 1); they received the following treatments: 5,626 received SGLT2is, 4,999 received DPP4is, 4,887 received SUs, and 2,309 received TZDs. Among those in the SGLT2i group, 3,175 received empagliflozin, 1,934 received dapagliflozin, 443 received luseogliflozin, and 74 received canagliflozin. Most patients (82.1%) had previously received metformin. Baseline characteristics between groups differed, i.e., patients prescribed SGLT2is were more likely to be male and obese, have longer diabetes duration, a lower FPG, more comorbidities (i.e., HT, CVD, CKD, and DLP) with higher use of ACEis/ARBs, and statins. Patients who received DPP4is were more likely to be older and have a lower eGFR, while patients who received SUs had a higher FPG and HbA1c. Patients who received DPP4is and SGLT2is were more likely to be covered under the CSMBS, which is the only health coverage scheme in Thailand that reimburses the costs of these medications, see Table 1. Treatment model

Baseline characteristics at treatment initiation were compared between treatment groups, identifying 13 covariates significantly associated with treatment prescription (i.e., age, sex, BMI, healthcare coverage schemes, diabetes duration, HbA1c, eGFR, DLP, CVD, PVD, DR, history of UTIs, and ACEis/ARBs), see Supplementary Table S1. The propensity score for each treatment group was then calculated. Weighted SMDs for all covariates were less than 0.1, suggesting they were well-balanced between treatment groups, see Supplementary Table S2. An overlap plot was constructed indicating that the positive probabilities for the receipt of SGLT2is, DPP4is, SUs, and TZDs overlapped well across patient groups, see Supplementary Fig. 1.

Nephrolithiasis

The median (IQR) follow-up time for nephrolithiasis was 1.8 (0.8, 3.5), 1.7 (0.7, 3.5), 1.8 (0.7, 3.4), and 2.7 (1.0, 4.5) years, in the SGLT2i, DPP4i, SU, and TZD groups, respectively. The corresponding incident nephrolithiasis was 7.7, 18.5, 20.5, and 12.1 per 1000 person-years. Univariate assessment identified 18 covariates significantly associated with nephrolithiasis (age, sex, BMI, FPG, eGFR, uric acid, LDL-C, HDL-C, HT, CVD, PVD, CKD, DR, history of UTIs, DLP, use of statins, insulin, and furosemide, see Supplementary Table S3). Of these, eight variables remained significantly or clinically associated with nephrolithiasis in the multivariable analysis including sex, BMI, history of UTIs, eGFR, uric acid, HDL-C, and use of insulin and statins (see Table 2). The multivariable Cox proportional hazard model with IPW by propensity score estimated a 55%, 63%, and 40% lower nephrolithiasis risk for patients prescribed SGLT2is compared to those prescribed DPP4is (HR = 0.45; 95% CI: 0.35, 0.58), SUs (HR = 0.37; 95% CI: 0.28, 0.48), and TZDs (HR = 0.60; 95% CI: 0.43, 0.85), respectively, see Table 3; Fig. 2. Sensitivity analyses showed similar results for the model that included only patients with previous use of metformin (HRs ranging from 0.36 to 0.62) and the model further adjusted for metformin (HRs ranging from 0.37 to 0.59), with stronger protective effects of SGLT2is in the modified ITT analysis (HRs ranging from 0.26 to 0.55) and the time-varying analysis (HRs ranging from 0.26 to 0.45), see Table 3. Estimated E-values were 3.90, 4.88, and 2.70 for the comparisons of SGLT2is versus DPP4is, SUs, and TZDs, respectively, suggesting that substantial unmeasured confounding would be required to explain away the observed protective effect of SGLT2is on nephrolithiasis, supporting the robustness of the main findings, see Supplementary Table S4.

Discussion

A target trial approach, i.e. using observational data to emulate a clinical trial, was performed using data from a retrospective cohort of 17,821 patients with a median follow-up duration of 1.8 years. Our study identified a 55%, 63%, and 40% lower risk of nephrolithiasis in patients prescribed SGLT2is relative to those who had received DPP4is, SUs, and TZDs, respectively, with additional sensitivity analyses further supporting the robustness of the associations observed. In addition, we also assessed the risk of UTIs following SGLT2i treatment as a secondary outcome which identified reduced risk in the SGLT2i group, although this only reached statistical significance for the SU comparison.

Our findings support previously reported protective effect of SGLT2is against nephrolithiasis in population-based cohort studies [13, 14, 19]. Kristensen et al. [13] reported a lower risk of nephrolithiasis in Danish patients who received SGLT2is with a HR of 0.51 (95% CI: 0.37, 0.71) compared to patients who had received GLP1-RAs, with a follow-up time of 2 years. Paik et al. [14] reported lower nephrolithiasis incidence in patients from the United States prescribed SGLT2is with HR of 0.69 (95% CI: 0.67, 0.72) and 0.74 (95% CI: 0.71, 0.77) compared to patients treated with GLP1-RAs and DPP4is, respectively. Shin et al. [19] also demonstrated lower nephrolithiasis incidence in Korean T2D patients with and without a history of nephrolithiasis, following SGLT2i treatment with HR of 0.64 (95% CI: 0.59, 0.69) and 0.43 (95% CI: 0.39, 0.48), respectively, compared to DPP4is. One previous meta-analysis of RCTs identified a reduced risk of nephrolithiasis in patients prescribed SGLT2is (odds ratio [OR] of 0.85; 95% CI: 0.57, 1.26) although this failed to reach the significance threshold [30]. A more recent meta-analysis of RCTs revealed a significant protective effect associated with empagliflozin with an incidence rate ratio of 0.64 (95% CI: 0.48, 0.86) compared with placebo [15]. These results support the use of SGLT2is as a preferential treatment option for reducing the risk of incident nephrolithiasis in patients with T2D. Several biological mechanisms have been proposed, including increased urinary flow from osmotic diuresis leading to lower risk of stone formation [31], decreased serum uric acid [32], increased urine bicarbonate and urine pH [33] which prevents the formation of uric acid stones, and the downregulation of osteopontin, an important inflammatory factor for calcium oxalate stone formation [34]. The overall effects of SGLT2is on reducing the risk of nephrolithiasis may be due to its influence on multiple pathways and additional investigations are needed to clarify this further.

Although glucosuria is a recognized effect of SGLT2is that could potentially increase the risk of UTIs [35], we found no evidence of this in our study, in line with previous meta-analyses of RCTs and observational studies in patients with diabetes [24, 25, 36, 37]. A meta-analysis of 72 RCTs identified a slightly increased UTI risk following SGLT2i use compared to placebo and other antihyperglycemic medications (relative risk [RR] of 1.03; 95% CI: 0.96, 1.11, and 1.08; 95% CI: 0.93, 1.25, respectively), although neither were significant [24]. Similarly, another meta-analysis of 77 RCTs reported a pooled RR of 1.05 (95% CI: 0.98, 1.12) for SGLT2is versus placebo or other antihyperglycemic medications [25]. Furthermore, data from a large population-based cohort study that used two US databases found no increased risk of severe UTIs following SGLT2i use compared with DDP4is (HR of 0.98; 95% CI: 0.68, 1.41), with a significantly reduced UTI risk when compared to GLP1-RAs (HR of 0.72; 95% CI: 0.53, 0.99) [36]. A Canadian cohort study of elderly participants also reported no association between SGLT2is and UTIs (HR of 0.89; 95% CI: 0.78, 1.00) [37].

Our study identified low BMI as a potential risk factor for both nephrolithiasis and UTIs. This may reflect underlying comorbidities, malnutrition, and dietary factors such as high oxalate vegetable consumption (commonly consumed locally) and low fluid intake, which can predispose stone formation. Similarly, the Nurses’ Health Study I and II reported a higher risk of kidney stones among women with BMI < 21 kg/m² compared to those with BMI of 21–22.9 kg/m2, albeit this was not significant (RR of 1.15; 95% CI: 0.95, 1.38, and RR of 1.13; 95% CI: 0.94, 1.36) [38]. Furthermore, a Swedish nationwide cohort reported an increased risk of cystitis and pyelonephritis (HR of 1.11; 95% CI: 1.09, 1.13, and HR of 1.13; 95% CI: 1.06, 1.21) [39, 40], potentially due to malnutrition and shorter anogenital distance in underweight individuals [41, 42].

In addition, our results showed that patients with insulin use had a higher risk of both nephrolithiasis and UTIs. This is consistent with previous observational studies linking insulin use with both kidney stones (OR of 3.31; 95% CI: 2.02, 5.45) [43] and UTIs (RR of 1.41; 95% CI: 1.26, 1.58) [44]. The underlying mechanism may be related to greater disease severity and insulin resistance that reduces renal ammonium production and lowers urinary pH, favoring uric acid stone formation [45]. Insulin resistance may also increase UTI susceptibility via impaired insulin receptor signaling that reduces antimicrobial peptides and increases susceptibility to uropathogenic Escherichia coli (UPEC) [46].

Interestingly, statin use was associated with a 30% lower risk of nephrolithiasis in our study. This aligns with two large cohort studies which reported lower risk of nephrolithiasis associated with statin use (OR of 0.51; 95% CI: 0.46, 0.57 and OR of 0.57; 95% CI: 0.53, 0.61); the protective effect may be due to the anti-inflammatory and antioxidative effects of statins [47, 48].

A major strength of our study is that we applied a propensity score-based method (IPW) to balance covariates associated with treatment assignment similar to the randomization process used in RCTs [49], with adjustment for potential confounding variables significantly associated with nephrolithiasis through regression analysis. We excluded patients with a previous nephrolithiasis diagnosis prior to the index date based on the increased likelihood of recurrence, as well as those who developed nephrolithiasis within one month after the index date, to more strongly ascertain the causal relationship between nephrolithiasis following SGLT2i treatment. The date of initial SGLT2i prescription was matched to the initial prescription date of the other comparators using a 6-month caliper to avoid immortal time bias. These methodologies and the sensitivity analyses undertaken reduced the opportunity for potential bias and increased the robustness of our findings. Previous investigations of SGLT2i use and incident nephrolithiasis have been largely restricted to Caucasian populations and this Thai hospital-based study confirmed similar effects in real clinical practice in an Asian cohort.

Nevertheless, our study also had several limitations. Fluid intake and diet are established risk factors for nephrolithiasis, but these data were not available in our cohort and therefore could not be considered in our analysis. Data on the severity of nephrolithiasis or UTIs was also not available, precluding assessment of the clinical burden or a potential dose-response relationship with SGLT2i use. In addition, recurrent nephrolithiasis could not be reliably assessed, and thus was not considered as a potential outcome. Distinguishing residual stones from new stone formation requires post-treatment imaging, which was not consistently assessed/available in our setting. Similarly, recurrent UTIs were uncommon in our database as only a small number of patients (15%) experienced recurrent UTIs and was therefore not evaluated. Furthermore, GLP1-RAs were not included in this study due to their limited use (n = 909 patients) during the study period. The median follow-up time in the SGLT2i group was relatively short, as SGLT2is were not available as a treatment option in our setting prior to 2015. Finally, further research with longer follow-up time, larger sample sizes, and more granular clinical and image data is warranted to evaluate outcome severity, recurrence, and potential heterogeneity across individual SGLT2is, and to better identify patient subgroups most likely to benefit from nephrolithiasis prevention with SGLT2i therapy.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our real-world study indicated a lower risk of nephrolithiasis in Thai patients with T2D who were prescribed SGLT2is as a treatment option, with no significant increased risk of UTI. These findings inform prescribing practice of SGLT2is in T2D patients to prevent nephrolithiasis in clinical practice.
