Whole exome sequencing: a new era in prenatal diagnostics
Authors: Panlai Shi, Conghui Wang, Yanjie Xia, Zili Li, Zhi Gao, Xiangdong Kong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAdvances in bioinformatics have revealed the potential of whole exome sequencing (WES) for copy number variations (CNVs) detection. This study aimed to evaluate whether WES can replace low pass copy number variation sequencing (CNV-seq) for the detection of CNVs in clinical prenatal diagnosis.MethodsBased on a cohort of 1055 fetuses tested concurrently with trio-WES and CNV-Seq, we performed a systematic comparison of both methods for the detection of aneuploidies, mosaicism, and pathogenic/likely pathogenic (P/LP) CNVs.ResultsBoth WES and CNV-Seq concordantly identified 11 aneuploidies, 4 mosaicism, and 29 P/LP CNVs. The aneuploidies comprised eight cases of trisomy 21, two cases of 45,X, and one case of 47,XXX. Four mosaicisms consisted of two whole-chromosome mosaicism (WCM) and two segmental aneuploid mosaicism. The two platforms demonstrated comparable accuracy in copy number (CN) detection for both aneuploidies and whole-chromosome mosaicism. However, WES showed comparatively lower precision than CNV-Seq in detecting CN alterations within segmental aneuploid mosaicism. For P/LP CNVs, both platforms demonstrated full concordance in the quantity and pathogenicity classification, although minor discrepancies were observed in their genomic locations and sizes determination. The Jaccard similarity coefficient values ranged from 57.9% to 99.7% (mean±SD, 90.2%±9.5%). Notably, only three cases fell below the 80% threshold, 1q21·1q21.2 deletion (66.0%), 1q21·1q21.2 duplication (57.9%) and 2q32·2q32.1 deletion (78.2%). After excluding these three samples, the similarity improved to 81.3%-99.7% (mean± SD, 92.7%±5.4%).ConclusionWES shows comparable detection of aneuploidies, WCM, and P/LP CNVs to CNV-Seq, supporting its use as a first-tier prenatal diagnostic approach. However, caution remains for segmental mosaicism and certain regions with reduced precision.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-026-07899-4.Keywords: Whole exome sequencing, Copy number sequencing, Copy number variations, Aneuploidies, Mosaicism

Background

Copy number variations (CNVs) are defined as DNA sequences of 1 kb or larger that appear in variable copy numbers, leading to microdeletion, microduplication, or more complex genomic rearrangements [1, 2]. If classified by size, CNVs can be categorized into aneuploidy, mosaicism, and submicroscopic CNVs. And, it is well-established that these pathogenic or likely pathogenic aberrations mentioned above collectively account for over 80% of the genetic causes of birth defects [3]. Therefore, accurate detection of CNVs is critical for genetic counseling and prenatal diagnosis.

Currently, both chromosomal microarray analysis (CMA) [4, 5] and copy number variant sequencing (CNV-seq) [6, 7] are recommended as first-tier prenatal diagnostic methods in clinical practice, with CMA as the gold standard for CNVs detection. Recently, accumulating evidence suggests that whole exome sequencing (WES) should be a complementary [8–10], routine [11], or even a first-tier approach [12] by incorporating CNV analysis. The reason is that WES offers a significant advantage by enabling simultaneous detection of single nucleotide variants (SNVs) and CNVs through bioinformatic re-analysis without incurring additional experimental costs, thereby providing a comprehensive and cost-effective genetic testing approach. And, the increase in overall diagnostic yield through WES-based CNV analysis varies across different study populations. Daniel et al. [8]. showed that additional analysis of WES data yields an additional 1.6% pathogenic/likely pathogenic (P/LP) CNVs of patients with a broad range of phenotypes. Furthermore, Beryl et al. [10]. found that employing WES-based CNV analysis increased the overall diagnostic rate by 5.9% in a cohort across diverse disease presentations. Notably, Xiang et al. [13] reported that the increasing diagnostic yield could be as high as 18% in children with unexplained developmental delay and/or intellectual disability.

Moreover, several studies have compared the performance of WES-based CNV detection with established techniques such as CMA, CNV-seq, SNP array, or multiplex ligation-dependent probe amplification (MLPA) [9, 12]. Rajagopalan et al. [14]. demonstrated 100% equivalence between WES and SNP array. And, the study from Zeng et al. [9] showed the overall concordance rate of 88.5% between WES and other validation methods, with complete consistency for large CNVs (deletion > 100kb, duplication > 500kb). However, there is currently a lack of performance evaluation between WES and CNV-Seq in CNV detection. In China mainland, most clinical institutions utilize CNV-Seq rather than CMA for CNV detection due to its cost-effectiveness, high throughput, and compatibility with non-invasive prenatal testing (NIPT).

Therefore, our study aimed to evaluate the performance of WES and CNV-Seq in detecting CNVs. Using a cohort of 1055 fetuses undergoing simultaneous trio-WES and CNV-Seq testing, we systematically compared the two methods in detecting aneuploidies, mosaicism, as well as P/LP CNVs, to evaluate whether WES can effectively replace CNV-Seq in clinical prenatal diagnosis.

Materials and methods

Participants

This retrospective cohort study encompassed 1055 parent-offspring trios who were enrolled at the first affiliated hospital of Zhengzhou university between January 2023 and May 2025. Primary clinical indications included ultrasound abnormalities (772, 73.2%), a poor gestation and birth history (267, 25.3%), and other indications such as a high-risk result from noninvasive prenatal test or advanced maternal age (16, 1.5%). Among 772 ultrasound abnormalities group, 51 fetuses presented with both ultrasound abnormalities and poor gestation and birth history. The pregnant women were aged 19–44 and the average age was 30.7 years, with 195 (18.4%) cases of advanced maternal age (AMA, ≥35 years). Gestational weeks ranged from 11 to 36, with a mean of 24.5 weeks. All parents signed informed consent for next-generation sequencing. This study conformed to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Zhengzhou University Ethics Committee (Grant No. 2019-KY-418).

Sample preparation

In total, 1055 fetal samples and 2110 parent peripheral blood samples were prepared as previously described [6]. Of the 1055 fetal samples, 94.3% were amniotic fluid, and 5.7% were chorionic villi. In brief, genomic DNA was extracted using DNeasy Blood &Tissue Kit(Qiagen, Venlo, The Netherlands). And, all fetal samples were performed Quantitative fluorescent PCR (QF-PCR) to measure level of maternal cell contamination (MCC) by Goldeneye®DNA Identification System 20A (Base point Cognitive Technology (Beijing) Co., LTD). Genescan analysis on the GeneMapper® ID-X platform were used to resolve all marker-specific QF-PCR products. Samples met quality control criteria when the MCC value was below 10%.

Copy number variant sequencing

Copy number variation sequencing (CNV-seq) was performed as previously describedI [6]. After genomic DNA extraction, DNA fragmentation and library construction were performed (end-filling and adapter ligation, ChroSure kit, Berry Genomics, Beijing). Then, massively parallel sequencing was performed using the NextSeq CN500 platform (Illumina). After the sequencing was completed, the obtained data were filtered to detect copy number variant changes using the human genome (Version GRCh37) as a reference. Identified CNVs were evaluated according to guidelines outlined by American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) guidelines [15], and public databases, such as DGV, Decipher, OMIM, ClinGen, UCSC, published papers, and the internal database of our laboratory, were cited. The pathogenicity of CNVs was classified as one of 5 different assignments: pathogenic (P), likely pathogenic (LP), variant of uncertain significance (VUS), likely benign (LB), and benign (B). For clinical reporting of patient results and the datasets analyzed in this study, only P or LP CNVs were considered.

Whole exome sequencing

WES was performed as previously described [16]. Simply, it was performed on the NovaSeq6000 platform (Illumina, paired-end, 150bp) after being captured with a commercial enrichment kit (SureSelect, V6 kit, Agilent Technologies Inc., Cedar Creek, TX). Low-quality reads and adapters were filtered by the Trimmomatic version, and high-quality reads were blasted against the hg19 by Burrows-Wheeler Aligner MEM version 0.7.17 [17]. Then, the duplicates were deleted by Picard version 2.9.0, and small variants were called by Genome Analysis Toolkit version 3.8 [18]. Quality control (QC) information provide a mean sequence depth for nuclear exome regions was over 100X, with more than 98% of the target bases having at least 20×coverage. Copy number variation (CNV) analysis based on WES data was also analyzed using the Efficient Genosome Interpretation System (EGIS; Sierra Vast Bio-Medical, Shanghai, China), which is a software jointly developed by our laboratory and the company. The background library was constructed by calculating the correlation coefficient (R > 0.95) based on the average sequencing depth and exon fragment length of the target samples and reference samples (8 random examiners in the same batch). The CNV analysis was performed as previously described [19]. Simply, exome hidden Markov model(XHMM) was used for exon CNV calling. First, build reference baseline (≥50 same-flow-cell libraries, copy number’s correlation coefficient > 0.94). Second, calculate copy number ratio (sample’s reads per kilobase per million (RPKM) value/reference mean RPKM). Last, allowing a 20% standard deviation (normal, 1.8–2.2, deletion, 0.8–1.2, duplicaiton, 2.8–3.2). Identified CNVs were classified as above [2]. Identified variants were classified using ACMG guidelines [20] and were filtered against public databases, such as gnomAD, Clinvar, OMIM, HGMD, and the internal database of our laboratory. Identified SNVs or CNVs were classified as one of 5 different assignments: P, LP, VUS, LB, and B. Consistent with the reporting criteria for CNV findings, only P and LP SNVs or CNVs were considered.

Statistical analysis

A chi-square or Fisher’s exact test was cited to analyze difference between the groups. The Mean±standard deviation was used to express measurement data, and count data expressed by the ratio p < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Study design and preliminary analysis of results

From January 2023 to May 2025, 1129 parent-offspring taken Trio-WES testing at our center (Fig. 1). After excluding 11 trios that were voluntarily requested without clear clinical indications and 63 trios that did not undergo CNV-Seq testing, a total of 1055 trios who underwent concurrent both WES and CNV-Seq testing were enrolled.

As shown in Fig. 1, preliminary analysis indicates concordant CNV detection capabilities between WES and CNV-Seq. Both of them identified 11 aneuploidies, 4 mosaicism, and 29 P/LP CNVs (Fig. 1, Table 1, 2). Additionally, WES detected 105 P/LP SNVs beyond the CNVs findings. Consequently, the initial assessment revealed that the sensitivity and specificity of WES and CNV-Seq were comparable in detecting clinically significant chromosomal abnormalities.

Aneuploidy and mosaicism contrast

Among the 11 aneuploidies, eight cases were trisomy 21, two cases were 45,X (Turner syndrome), and one case was 47,XXX (Triple X syndrome). And, four mosaicisms contain two whole-chromosome mosaicism and two segmental aneuploid mosaicism (Table 1). We further assessed the quantitative accuracy of both platforms in determining copy number (CN) states.

Representative image of aneuploidies were showed in Figures 2A and 2B. WES and CNV-Seq consistently detected CN states of 3 or 1 for chromosome 21 and X, confirming equivalent precision in aneuploidy CN quantification. As consistent with the precision in aneuploidy detection, both platforms exhibited equivalent detection accuracy for whole-chromosome mosaicism (Figure 2C, Table 1). Specifically, WES and CNV-Seq consistently detected CN states of 1.30 or 1.85 for chromosome X in case F12 and F13.

Representative image of aneuploidies and mosaicism detected by WES and CNV-Seq. A. Trisomy 21, B. 45,X, C. Mosaic X, D.Segmental mosaic X

However, notably divergent performance was observed between the two platforms in detecting segmental aneuploid mosaicism (Figure 2D, Table 1). In cases F14 and F15, CNV-Seq reported segmental aneuploid mosaicism (e.g.,case F15, seq[GRCh37] Xp22.33p11.22(160000 -52100000)x1; Xp11.22q21.1(52560000-82520000)x1.39; Xq21.1q28(82520000-154940000)x1), while WES indicated whole-chromosome aneuploid mosaicism (e.g.,case F15, seq[GRCh37] Xp22.33q28(2700088-154741453)x1.2). Although probes in the Xp11.22q21.1 region showed a higher mean CN values compared to other genomic segments, the analytical pipeline ultimately reported whole-chromosome X mosaicism. Hence, WES demonstrates comparatively lower precision than CNV-Seq in detecting CN alterations within segmental aneuploid mosaicism.

Pathogenic and likely pathogenic CNVs contrast

For P/LP CNVs, we systematically evaluated the concordance between the two methods with respect to four key parameters: detection quantity, pathogenicity classification, genomic location, and size determination (Fig. 3, Table 2, Supplementary Table 1). First, both methods identified an identical set of 29 P/LP CNVs, also with complete concordance in pathogenicity classification for each variant (Table 2). Second, we assessed genomic position and size using the Jaccard similarity coefficient combined with direct segment-to-segment comparison. As Table 2 showed, the similarity values ranged from 57.9% to 99.7% (mean±SD, 90.2%±9.5%). Notably, only three cases fell below the 80% threshold, F31 (1q21.1q21.2 deletion, 66.0%), F33 (1q21.1q21.2 duplication, 57.9%), and F37 (2q32.2q32.1 deletion, 78.2%). After excluding these three samples, the similarity improved to 81.3%-99.7% (mean±SD, 92.7%±5.4%).

Representative image of 1q21.1q21.2 deletion (case F31) detected by WES and CNV-Seq. C. Representative image of 2q32.2q32.1 deletion (case F37) detected by WES and CNV-Seq

As shown in Figure 3A, when using CNV-Seq derived CNV sizes as the gold standard reference, the relative ratios of WES-determined CNV sizes to their corresponding reference values ranged from 66% to 125% (mean±SD, 98.6%±12.0%) (Supplementary Table 1). It showed that 65% (20/31) of CNVs exhibited size deviations within ±10% of the reference standard, 90% (28/31) fell within±20% deviation range, while 97% (30/31) showed variations within ±30% boundaries. Consistent with the similarity values, the largest discrepancies were observed in samples F31, F33, and F37, which exhibited 66.0%, 125.6%, and 78.2% discordant calls, respectively. If excluding these three samples, the relative ratios improved to 81.3%-120.3% (mean±SD, 99.5%±8.9%).

To investigate the underlying causes of subtle discrepancies in size determination and genomic localization, we analyzed the genomic characteristics of each case. Notably, 17 CNVs were localized to recurrent genomic regions, nine CNVs resided in subtelomeric regions, and remian five occurred within an interstitial chromosomal segment. 17 Recurrent CNVs includes 1q21.1q21.2 (two cases), 7q11.23 (three cases), 15q11.2 (one case), 16p11.2 (two cases),17q12 (five cases), and 22q11.21 (four cases). Subtelomeric regions CNVs includes 2q37.1, 4p16.3p16.1, 5q35.3, 6q27, 8p23.3p11.22, 12p13.33, 17p13.3, 20p13 and Xq28. Among CNVs within these recurrent and subtelomeric regions, substantial fluctuation was observed specifically at 1q21.1q21.2 (case F31, 66.0%, and case F33, 125%), whereas all other size ratios remained within the ±20% concordance threshold (88.1%-120%). As depicted in Figure 3B, the 1q21.1q21.2 recurrent region resides in close to the centromere and contains extensive heterochromatic domains (red line), which significantly impedes precise breakpoint delineation in short-read sequencing technologies. Hecne, it results in subtle alignment discrepancies between the two techniques due to reduced sequencing mappability finally.

Among five interstitial CNVs, the observed size ratios were 78.2%, 98.1%, 99.4%, 99.7%, and 101.2%, respectively. With the exception of 2q32.2q32.1 deletion (case F37, 78.2%), all demonstrated high concordance (±2%) relative to the reference standard (2p23.3p13.3, 10q23.1q25.1, 12q24.21q24.31 and 22q12.1q13.2). As shown in Figure 3C, 2q32.2q32.1 region is devoid of recurrent and subtelomeric region. And, discordant size estimations were observed between WES and CNV-Seq at the 2q32.1q32.2 region (4.64 Mb vs 5.94 Mb). However, both CNVs harbored an identical complement of RefSeq protein-coding genes within the implicated genomic interval. The discordant genomic interval (Seq[GRCh37]:2:184,560,000–185,800,509, 2:190,500,000–1 90,445,268) contained zero protein-coding genes annotated in the RefSeq database.

In summary, WES and CNV-Seq demonstrated concordance in both detection quantity and pathogenicity classification, albeit with slight divergence in CNV size determination and genomic localization.

Discussion

In this study, we retrospectively assessed the detection efficacy of Trio-WES and CNV-Seq for aneuploidies, mosaicism, and P/LP CNVs in a cohort of 1055 fetuses in which both tests were performed concurrently. The results revealed that both platforms demonstrated equivalent sensitivity for detecting aneuploidies and whole-chromosome mosaicisms (achieving 100% concordance in CN status), and exhibited strong concordance with respect to both the detection quantity and pathogenicity classification of P/LP CNVs. However, WES showed suboptimal precision compared to CNV-Seq in detecting segmental aneuploid mosaicism, with slight discrepancies in CNVs size estimation and genomic localization. This is primarily due to inherent limitations in WES-based CNV detection, resulting from the discontinuous nature of exome capture and the fact that most CNV breakpoints fall outside exonic regions [8, 10, 21–25].

Comparative performance of WES and CNV-Seq in CNVs detection

WES probe design specifically targets exonic regions comprising only 1%-2% of the genome [22, 23], whereas > 98% of CNV breakpoints reside in non-coding areas [8, 10, 24, 25]. Therefore, this results in underdetection of non-coding variants, particularly deep intronic and extragenic sequences, which has been proven by many previous literatures [8, 10, 24, 25](e.g., missed a deep intronic variant (c.3873+727A > G) of PTPRQ gene [25]) and our study (e.g., case F37, missed non-coding region). Also, it’s well known that uneven sequencing depths in GC-rich regions and difficulty identifying variants in homopolymeric regions are draw backs of WES [26]. However, even so, compared with other detection methods, the similarity values (Jaccard similarity coefficient) of WES in these regions exceeded 80% in these regions [8, 9, 11, 12], with a exception of 1q21.1q21.2 region (<70%) [8, 10]. As Daniel et al. showed, the similarity value of 1q21.1q21.2 deletion detected by WES is 63.1% compared to Affymetrix CytoScan [8]. And, Royer-Bertrand et al. showd that the similarity value of 1q21.1q21.2 duplication detected by WES is 31.4% compared to MLPA results [10]. In our study, the similarity of 1q21.1q21.2 deletion and duplicaiton are 66.0% and 57.9%, respectively.

In the detection of aneuploid and mosaicism, studies have reported that NGS technology can detect both whole and mosaic segmental aneuploidies with efficiency comparable to array comparative genomic hybridization (aCGH) [27–29]. María Vera-Rodríguez et al. [29] demonstrated that among 126 cases of segmental aneuploidy detection, the concordance rate between NGS and aCGH was 124 cases (98.4%), while among 50 cases of whole-chromosome aneuploidy detection, the concordance reached 48 cases (96.0%). The overall consistency rate was 99.8% (2,276 out of 2280 chromosomes evaluated). In preimplantation genetic testing, NGS-based methods have also shown potential in detecting segmental mosaicism within samples, however, these studies emphasize the necessity of balancing sensitivity and specificity in such detection [27]. In our study, WES and CNV-Seq showed comparable sensitivity in detecting aneuploidies and whole-chromosome mosaicisms; however, WES exhibited lower precision than CNV-Seq in identifying segmental aneuploid mosaicisms.

Feasibility and limitations of WES as a replacement for CNV-Seq

As a comprehensive genetic testing method, the major advantage of WES lies in its ability to simultaneously detect single-nucleotide variants (SNVs), small insertions/deletions (indels), and CNVs, providing a “one-stop” diagnostic solution for genetically heterogeneous disorders. Furthermore, as a parental carrier screening tool, WES helps parents ascertain whether they carry pathogenic variants and facilitates precision genetic and reproductive counseling. The most apparent limitations of WES are its restriction to exonic regions and its imprecise resolution in mapping CNV breakpoints.

Studies have shown that WES demonstrates strong performance in detecting coding CNVs involving three or more exons, with a successful identification rate of 88%, achieving resolution comparable to medium-resolution genomic microarrays commonly used in clinical settings [30]. This multi-faceted detection capability makes WES an attractive first-tier diagnostic tool for genetically heterogeneous conditions. Recently developed algorithms and tools—such as OFF-PEAK, which innovatively leverages typically discarded off-target sequencing reads—have further improved WES-based CNV detection, reporting over 95% sensitivity and 80% specificity in cancer and inherited retinal disease samples [31]. These technological advances support the potential use of WES as an alternative to dedicated CNV detection methods in prenatal diagnosis. However, in critical clinical scenarios, WES results still require cautious interpretation and additional validation to ensure diagnostic accuracy.

Conclusion

Through a systematic evaluation of CNV detection performance in 1055 fetuses tested with both WES and CNV-seq, it found that the two methods demonstrated comparable capabilities. Our study reveals the emerging potential of WES in CNV detection. With continued advancements in bioinformatic techniques, further research is also needed to thoroughly assess and clarify the value of WES-based CNV calling. These studies will ultimately demonstrate the advantage of WES as an all-in-one diagnostic approach, providing families with more comprehensive, rapid, and cost-effective testing, thereby offering timely and precise guidance for pregnancy management.
