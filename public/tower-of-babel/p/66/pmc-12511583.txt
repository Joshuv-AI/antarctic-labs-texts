Preoperative prediction of lymph node metastasis risk in papillary thyroid carcinoma based on multiple model comparisons
Authors: Yuan-yuan Zhou, Yu-zhi Zhang, Juan Li, Zhi-qiang Li, Wen-bo Ding, Mei Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The clinical necessity of lymph node dissection in papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC) surgery remains contentious. This study compared four logistic regression (LR) models (with distinct feature selection strategies) and four machine learning (ML) models to preoperatively predict lymph node metastasis (LNM) risk in PTC patients, with emphasis on multidimensional evaluation and cross-populational generalizability. Data from 3,175 PTC patients (2021 cohort) were randomly split into training (70%) and testing (30%) subsets, with external validation performed using a Chinese (2024, n = 104) and a Canadian (2019–2022, n = 412) cohort. Twelve predictors were screened, and models were evaluated using metrics of discrimination (AUC), calibration (Brier Score), classification accuracy, and clinical utility. The prevalence of LNM was 34.48%, 36.54%, and 30.10% in the internal, Chinese, and Canadian cohorts, respectively. Among ML models, Random Forest achieved the highest internal AUC (0.767), whereas XGBoost demonstrated superior generalization (external AUCs: 0.785 and 0.725). LR models, particularly BestSubset-GLM, outperformed these ML models with an internal AUC of 0.770 and external AUCs of 0.831 and 0.785. Notably, BestSubset-GLM exhibited high specificity (0.86), precision (0.59), favorable calibration (Brier Score < 0.20), and robust clinical utility across the approximately 15–90% threshold probability range. Extrathyroidal extension, tumor size above 1.00 cm, younger age, and male gender were identified as key LNM risk factors. Bethesda classification and molecular aberrations were integrated into models. BestSubset-GLM balanced parsimony, interpretability, and generalizability, thereby supporting clinical decision-making through dynamic nomograms. Introduction

The clinical necessity of surgical intervention for papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC), particularly regarding the extent of lymph node dissection, remains a subject of debate. A conservative treatment strategy, “active surveillance” instead of immediately aggressive treatments, has been proposed to minimize unnecessary surgery or overtreatment for low-risk papillary thyroid microcarcinoma1. Nonetheless, accurately identifying the presence of lymph node metastasis (LNM) before surgery, as well as prior to the subsequent radioactive iodine therapy post-surgery, is crucial for clinical management and prognosis assessment2. Existing studies have predominantly focused on developing prediction models. Several clinical institutions have utilized their institutional datasets to evaluate risk factors for lymph node metastasis in thyroid cancer patients and subsequently developed clinical prediction models3. To enhance clinical application, these models should be presented in user-friendly formats, such as web-based calculators, simplified scoring systems or nomograms derived from scoring frameworks. Such risk scores are widely integrated into clinical decision-making and are typically generated using conventional logistic regression (LR) models. However, many of these models are developed using small sizes or traditional statistical databases (e.g., SEER)4,5, with restricted collection of targeted variables. Furthermore, due to the use of diverse modeling methods and variable selection strategies, substantial heterogeneity exists among prediction models. These models also exhibit limitations in their predictive performances.

In recent years, the rapid advancement of artificial intelligence has established machine learning (ML) as a powerful tool across diverse fields, particularly in medical research. The primary focus of ML is not on model interpretability, but in improving predictive performance and generalizability via rigorous cross-validation protocols and iterative algorithm optimization. Thus, ML often surpasses explanatory models in the realm of managing intricate, high-dimensional data sets5. Nonetheless, this “black-box” characteristic hinders its clinical translation. The SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) algorithm has been employed to quantify the importance of each input predictor and clarify the impact of individual features on overall model performance6. Furthermore, to enhance clinical application, researchers have investigated the development of interpretable ML models. These models enhance transparency and interpretability, allowing medical professionals to understand the decision-making process of algorithms, thereby increasing trust and acceptance. For instance, AutoScore, an ML-based automatic clinical scoring generator, has been used to develop risk scoring tools using interpretable point-based systems7.

In constructing clinical prediction models for tumor-related diseases, the integration of demographic, pathological, and molecular testing data is often employed to enhance diagnostic accuracy. To predict the risk of malignancy in thyroid nodules with atypia of undetermined significance, some researchers compared three conventional LR models and two ML models, finding that the LR model developed via backward stepwise regression outperformed the ML models8. In differentiating follicular thyroid carcinoma from adenoma, other researchers developed a predictive model using ML algorithms after analyzing diagnostic parameters through least absolute shrinkage and selection operator regression (LASSO) and multivariate LR screening methods, identifying the XGBoost model as the optimal one among seven ML methods6. Similarly, researchers have also optimized the XGBoost model from six ML models to build a predictive model for central LNM in PTC patients from the SEER database5. However, there is a lack of studies comparing the predictive performance of conventional LR and ML models for LNM in PTC, particularly regarding the evaluation of extrapolative and generalizability using external validation datasets.

Therefore, in this study, we collected predictors from independent data sets, including training, internal and external validation sets. We screened features using various variable selection methods after conducting unified univariate analysis and evaluated the performance of prediction models generated by conventional LR algorithms against those produced by four different ML algorithms. Our primary aim is to develop a comprehensive prediction model for assessing LNM risk in PTC patients. The secondary objective is to identify key risk factors associated with LNM in PTC, thereby offering clinicians more personalized decision-making support and a practical, visualized tool for the management of thyroid cancer. We used the TRIPOD checklist when writing our report9.

Results

Study cohorts

Figure 1 depicts the study flowchart, which outlines the screening of 525 cases with confirmed LNM status (km_dataset) from a cohort of 3175 PTC patients. This cohort was then divided into training and testing subsets at a 7:3 ratio to develop LNM predictive models. Additionally, two external validation datasets were enrolled: a 2024 Chinese cohort (ch_valid, n = 104) and a 2019–2022 Canadian cohort (ca_valid, n = 412). Of these patients, 181 cases were found LNM (34.48%) in km_dataset, while 38 cases (36.54%) and 124 cases (30.10%) were reported LNM in ch_valid and ca_valid cohorts respectively.Fig. 1Flow chart of patients selection and study design.

Flow chart of patients selection and study design.

Sample size adequacy was validated using events-per-variable (EPV) analysis: the training cohort (n = 367, 128 events) had an initial EPV of 10.7 (128/12 variables) and a final EPV of 12.8–25.6 after feature selection (5–10 variables). External validation incorporated the 2024 Chinese cohort (n = 104, 38 events) for preliminary calibration and the 2019–2022 Canadian cohort (n = 412, 124 events, EPV = 12.4–24.8), both aligning with the recommended 10–15 EPV threshold10.

Preliminary features selection

Referring to previously reported risk factors11,12, predictors were grouped by LNM status, with 18 variables initially retained after excluding empty categories and constructing dummy variables. These encompassed demographic characteristics (age, gender), clinicopathological features including extra-thyroidal extension (ETE) and tumor size, cytopathological classification according to The Bethesda System for Reporting Thyroid Cytopathology (TBSRTC) (categories I-VI), and molecular profiles [eight genomic aberrations, including BRAF p.V600E mutation, RAS mutations (KRAS, NRAS, or HRAS), RET rearrangement, TP53 mutation, BRAF non-p.V600E mutations, PIK3CA mutation, PTEN mutation, and undetected molecular alterations]. These molecular tests involved fine-needle aspiration (FNA) samples collected under ultrasound guidance30. Notably, all variables incorporated into the models are derived from routine preoperative examinations, requiring no additional invasive procedures or specialized tests, thus aligning with clinical workflows in both primary care facilities and tertiary medical centers.

After univariate LR analysis, twelve potential variables remained for subsequent feature selection. As shown in Table 1, LNM was significantly associated with age, female gender, ETE, Bethesda categories III to VI, tumor size, BRAF p.V600E mutation, RAS mutation, RET aberration, and undetected molecular alteration (all P < 0.05). Multivariate LR analysis further identified ETE (odds ratio [OR] 2.063, 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.946, 4.501) and tumor size exceeding 1.00 cm (OR 4.609, 95% CI 2.225, 9.548) as independent positive predictors of LNM, while age (OR 0.957, 95%CI 0.937–0.978) and female gender (OR 0.639, 95%CI 0.374–1.090) were independent negative predictors of LNM. Figure 3 shows comparative visualization of four LR models in the form of ROC curve, forest plots representing conventional regression analyses alongside dynamically interactive nomograms depicting multivariate LR model outputs. All four LR models demonstrated comparable predictive performance on the test dataset, notably for features selected through univariate analysis, BestSubset, and LASSO algorithms (Fig. 3A,D,G). The RFFilter_GLM achieved an AUC of 0.775 (95% CI 0.704–0.846) on km_test, surpassing the other models; however, its performance was the least impressive in external validation sets. In contrast, BestSubset_GLM exhibited the highest performance, with AUC values of 0.831 (95% CI 0.755–0.907) and 0.785 (95% CI 0.738–0.833), surpassing Full_GLM’s corresponding values of 0.827 (95% CI 0.749–0.904) and 0.777 (95% CI 0.728–0.826) in ch_valid and ca_valid. This finding corresponds with Brier Score trends, indicating that the LR model incorporating RF-identified features achieves enhanced predictive accuracy relative to its counterparts (see Supplementary Table S2). Nevertheless, in terms of data generalization, UniFilter_GLM, BestSubset_GLM, and LASSO_GLM demonstrated superior discrimination performance in both cohorts. To enhance clinical accessibility, the BestSubset_GLM-based dynamic nomogram incorporates routine preoperative variables (age, gender, tumor size, BRAF p.V600E mutation status, etc.) to calculate real-time LNM risk probabilities. To elucidate the discriminative capacity of our dynamic nomograms, we conducted matched-case analyses in PTC patients. Two treatment-naïve female PTC patients (both aged 41 years, Bethesda V, BRAF p.V600E-positive) with temporally synchronized diagnoses were rigorously matched. Both demonstrated left lobe localization with divergent pathophenotypes: Case 11 from km_test (Code: 2589) exhibited a 1.10 cm lesion with transcapsular invasion (gross ETE), whereas Case 131 from km_test (Code: 1856) presented a 0.4 cm nodule maintaining intact capsular integrity. Postoperative histopathology confirmed LNM in Case 11 but not in Case 131. This aligns with the clinical need to balance therapeutic efficacy and overtreatment avoidance.

Discussion

Previous studies have demonstrated that LNM is associated with increased mortality in PTC patients and may impact prognosis related to local recurrence and distant metastasis14,15. Accordingly, prophylactic central lymph node dissection is recommended to ensure comprehensive treatment16. However, the potential complications, such as hypoparathyroidism and recurrent laryngeal nerve injury, should not be overlooked. Accurate prediction of LNM before surgery effectively allows clinicians to determine the extent of surgical resection. Prediction models have been employed to forecast preoperative LNM in this field. A meta-analysis of 107 studies involving 134 predictive models for LNM in PTC patients found that approximately 80% of these models were based on LR, with the remainder consisting of gradient boosting machines (GBM, a ML technique similar in principle to the XGBoost algorithm), RF, SVM, and others3. This meta-analysis reported a C-index of 0.773 (95% CI 0.754–0.791) for LNM predicting in the validation set. Out of the 107 studies, 86 provided validation details, whereas only 9 studies were dedicated to external validation, including 4 multi-center external validation studies3. The interpretation of the results for most researches was considered limited due to the lack of independent external validation. Thus, our study aimed to develop a clinical prediction model for LNM risk in PTC patients. Through preliminary screening of potential variables and algorithm-specific feature selection, we established a framework to develop an accurate, interpretable, and generalizable model. Multi-dimensional validation metrics were employed to systematically evaluate performance, including cross-population generalizability analysis. Ultimately, the LR model was translated into clinical practice via dynamic nomogram visualization, enhancing its practical utility in clinical decision-making.

Many LR models have been constructed using variables directly selected from univariate and multivariate analysis results. This data-driven approach may excessively depend on statistical criteria and lack integration of clinical expertise17. In this study, therefore, we identified 12 risk factors associated with LNM in thyroid cancer based on clinical expertise and univariate analysis results alone, ensuring the predictors are clinically accessible and applicable. Extra-thyroidal extension, pathological tumor size exceeding 1.00 cm, younger age, and male gender were found to be associated with an increased risk of LNM, consistent with previous findings12. Bethesda classification and molecular alterations were also included as potential variables, considering that most suspicious thyroid nodules undergo ultrasound-guided FNA cytology and molecular testing prior to surgical decision-making.

Numerous variable selection methods are available for constructing prediction model classifiers, and clinicians may find it challenging to select optimal methods for different datasets and algorithms. In our study, a comparative analysis was conducted on various variable selection methods, including univariate analysis, BestSubset, LASSO, RF, RFE, and PFI, across independent data sets with binary outcomes (LNM or non-LNM in PTC patients) for different types of classification algorithms. To reduce data collection burden and improve efficiency, the objective is to minimize the number of variables required for prediction36. Consequently, when model performance is comparable, feature reduction is preferred (aiming to achieve the best possible model prediction performance with the fewest features). We constructed predictive model classifiers for the selected features using various LR and ML algorithms, and simultaneously built both a full-variable model for reference and a simplified model for control. The LR models constructed using features selected by four algorithms (univariate analysis, BestSubset, LASSO, and RF) exhibited minimal differences in AUC values on the test cohort (km_test), with RF slightly outperforming the others. This could be associated with the preliminary feature selection conducted through univariate analysis. However, in two validation cohorts (ch_valid and ca_valid), the LR models built with features further selected by the BestSubset algorithm showed significantly improved extrapolation and generalization capabilities. Best subset regression identifies the optimal model from all possible subsets based on goodness-of-fit criteria, including Mallow’s Cp (ideally close to the number of model parameters), the Bayesian information criterion (BIC, lower values indicate better fit), and R-squared and adjusted R-squared (Adjr2, higher values are more favorable)18. In this study, we chose the adjusted R-squared statistic to screen out the optimal feature combination19, which includes six variables: age, gender, ETE, tumor size, BRAF p.V600E mutant, and RET aberration. Notably, BRAF V600E was significantly associated with LNM in univariate analysis (Table 1, P < 0.05) but not retained in multivariate analysis (P = 0.998). The discrepancy possibly due to correlation with tumor size (> 1.00 cm) and ETE: VIF decreased from 3.36 (full model) to 1.52 (optimized after BestSubset Regression), aligning with 2024 ATA Guidelines (contextual interpretation with clinicopathological factors).

This study utilizes a multidimensional evaluation framework to comprehensively assess model performance, including discrimination (ROC AUC, ΔAUC), calibration (Brier Score, calibration curves), classification metrics (sensitivity, specificity, F1 score, accuracy), clinical utility (DCA curves), and model interpretability (SHAP values, forest plots, and dynamic nomograms)20,21. This approach overcomes the limitations of traditional studies that overly rely on a single AUC metric22. The results demonstrate that BestSubset_GLM exhibits the best generalization ability in two independent validation sets, with AUC values (0.831 in ch_valid and 0.785 in ca_valid) consistent with the trend of the Brier Score. Additionally, the calibration curve presents clinical reliability in testing cohort, indicating that this model achieves precise alignment between predicted probabilities and actual risks while maintaining high discrimination. Notably, although RFFilter_GLM achieves the highest AUC (0.775) in the test set (km_test), its significant performance degradation in two validation sets (lowest AUC) reveals limitations in generalization ability. Meanwhile, Full_GLM and Ctrl_GLM exhibit expected performance differences (with the simplified model performing the weakest), further highlighting the effectiveness of the BestSubset algorithm in feature selection.

Traditional external validations are often restricted to multicenter studies within the same country or city, or datasets collected across different time periods, which lack sufficient geographic and ethnic diversity to fully evaluate model applicability across institutions with varying clinical practices. Moreover, fully external validation results are typically less favorable than internal validation results23. To address these limitations, this study not only adopts a 7:3 data split for internal validation and cross-validation but also integrates external validation datasets comprising cohorts from different years within the same ethnic group and populations of distinct ethnicities, systematically assessing the extrapolation generalization capability of the models across diverse clinical and demographic contexts. DCA analysis shows significant differences in clinical net benefits across different threshold ranges (15–90%) for models constructed using different algorithms, with RFFilter_RF and RFE_SVM demonstrating stable performance across a wide threshold range, suggesting that clinical decision-making should consider specific risk stratification needs when selecting models. Through cross-validation using F1 score and accuracy, the study finds that the LR model constructed using features selected by BestSubset achieves 74.04% accuracy and an F1 score of 0.690 in the validation set, outperforming the full-variable model and models constructed using LASSO/RF feature selection. This confirms the critical role of feature selection in balancing model parsimony and predictive performance. These findings collectively indicate that relying solely on AUC evaluation may overestimate the practical value of models, whereas an integrated evaluation framework incorporating classification accuracy, calibration, feature parsimony, and clinical decision curves can more reliably guide the selection and optimization of clinical prediction models.

The results of Full_GLM compared with Ctrl_GLM indicate that in predictor screening with a limited number of candidate variables, redundant variables impair model generalizability, while oversimplified models directly reduce predictive accuracy by omitting critical predictors24. Our study demonstrates that combining BestSubset with LR yields more reliable predictive models for biomedical data compared to other methods (e.g., LASSO, XGBoost). BestSubset thoroughly evaluates all possible variable combinations, retaining key biomarkers that independently or jointly influence outcomes, which is critical for clinical datasets with interconnected biological factors25. Unlike methods that oversimplify variable selection (e.g., LASSO) or overfit noise (e.g., complex ML models), BestSubset_GLM balances accuracy and stability, especially when sample sizes are limited26. The linear nature of LR aligns well with biological systems where variables often have additive effects, avoiding the pitfalls of overly complex models in external validation27,28. Historical success of BestSubset in biochemical studies further supports its suitability for biomedical research29. The superior performance of BestSubset_GLM stems from its comprehensive variable selection, compatibility with biological data patterns, and avoidance of overfitting. This approach is recommended for studies with limited samples or when biological relationships are primarily linear25,26,29.

This study validated the effectiveness of four LR prediction models using two PTC cases with similar clinical characteristics (age, gender, Bethesda V, BRAF p.V600E mutation) but opposite LNM outcomes. For Case 1 (Code: 2589, tumor size 1.10 cm with capsular invasion, LNM-positive), the predicted probabilities were as high as 0.887–0.943, while for Case 2 (Code: 1856, tumor size 0.40 cm without capsular invasion, LNM-negative), the predicted probabilities were only 0.362–0.402, demonstrating strong alignment between model predictions and actual outcomes. Key factor analysis revealed that tumor size exceeding 1.00 cm and capsular invasion were the primary drivers of high LNM risk, while BRAF p.V600E mutation required evaluation in conjunction with other pathological features. The four models showed consistent predictions (standard deviation of 0.024 for Case 1 and 0.018 for Case 2), particularly exhibiting greater stability in high-risk cases, indicating that the models could effectively differentiate LNM risk by integrating key variables such as tumor size and invasiveness. These results support the value of the model in preoperative individualized risk assessment, such as aiding decisions on surgical extent or the necessity of lymph node dissection, providing a quantitative basis for clinical decision-making.

This study has limitations: (1) the small sample size and reliance on literature-based external validation cohorts may introduce potential racial or geographical biases, highlighting the need for multi-center prospective validation; (2) moderate predictive accuracy (approximately 70%) is attributed to incomplete variable capture (e.g., rare genetic variants, cytological subtypes) due to data gaps; (3) exclusion of LNM cases with short follow-up (< 2 years) and incomplete data collection may lead to false-positive predictions—wherein patients predicted to develop metastasis did not exhibit clinical evidence within the study’s follow-up window but might do so later; and (4) the lack of regional lymph node partitioning data (e.g., central vs. lateral cervical nodes) in external validation cohorts, particularly the Canadian dataset, precluded subgroup analyses of metastatic patterns, which limits the model’s specificity for regional nodal risk stratification. Future efforts should prioritize cohorts with granular regional lymph node data (e.g., central vs. lateral neck nodes), extended follow-up, and enriched variables to enhance model generalizability and precision, particularly for subtype-specific LNM prediction.

Methods

Study design and datasets preparation

This single-center retrospective cohort study recruited patients with thyroid nodules diagnosed via ultrasound at Jiangsu Province Hospital of Integrated Traditional Chinese and Western Medicine (February2021–December 2021), based on a similar patient cohort to our previous study but with updated inclusion criteria30. Inclusion criteria: (1) Bethesda III–VI nodules confirmed by fine-needle aspiration cytology (FNAC); (2) pathologically confirmed PTC after surgery with documented lymph node metastasis status; (3) age 18–75 years. Exclusion criteria: (1) history of radioactive iodine or radiofrequency ablation; (2) incomplete clinical data (e.g., missing molecular testing results); (3) concurrent malignancies. This study strictly followed the 2024 ATA Guidelines for case selection (Recommendation 11), ensuring alignment with clinical practice standards. The dataset (km_dataset, with raw data presented in Supplementary Table S4) was randomly stratified into 70% training (km_train) and 30% testing (km_test) subsets.

We identified publications investigating thyroid nodules or carcinoma with lymph node metastasis, screened for open-access datasets with clear diagnoses and metastasis-related outcomes, extracted variables of interest and excluded cases with incomplete records. Two independent external validation cohorts were established: the ch_valid cohort, which contains 104 Chinese cases derived from 2024 publications and serves as a pilot-scale dataset31; and the ca_valid cohort, which integrates three temporally staggered Canadian cohorts (2019–2022) from a single research team and ultimately yielded 412 analyzable cases following deduplication and data cleaning32–34.

Preliminary features selection

Features were selected based on clinical relevance and adherence to 2024 ATA Guidelines: molecular markers (e.g., BRAF V600E, RET fusion) were included per Recommendation 10 (preoperative FNA-confirmed only); clinicopathological factors (extrathyroidal extension, tumor size) aligned with Recommendation 28 for risk stratification; lymph node assessment followed histopathological standards in Recommendation 27. Variables were integrated with clinicopathological factors and filtered for multicollinearity using variance inflation factor (VIF < 5). Dummy variables were constructed by excluding empty categories, and potential variables with P < 0.05 in univariate logistic regression analysis were selected as related risk factors for LNM.

LR modeling with four feature selection algorithms and visualization

For the following development of conventional LR model, four variable selection algorithms were applied to the training data set for the best predictor features, including univariate analysis (UniFilter), Best Subset Regression (BestSubset), LASSO, and RF (RFFilter), with all twelve potential variables as input35. Feature selection for univariate analysis uses the “glm” package in R to fit models and select variables by univariate analysis with FDR-adjusted odds ratios and significance levels (adjusted_p < 0.05). BestSubset employs the “regsubsets” function from “leaps” package to choose variables that maximize the adjusted R-squared value36. LASSO analysis was conducted using the “glmnet” package, with the tuning parameter set to lambda.1se37. RF algorithm was implemented using the “randomForest” package, identifying the number of trees corresponding to the minimum error in the total sample, and selecting variables with the highest “Mean Decrease Gini” value based on variable importance scores and five rounds of ten-fold cross-validation.

Four LR prediction models were constructed for the km_train data using the “glm” package with selected features from univariate analysis, BestSubset, LASSO, and RF algorithm, as well as a 12-variable full model for reference and a 3-weaklink-variable simplified model (gender_Female, Bethesda_IV, and RAS_mutant) for control. A dynamic nomogram was developed to visualize individualized risk prediction, where each predictor was systematically assigned a point-based weighting proportional to its clinical impact, contributing collectively to a comprehensive risk score for LNM in PTC patients. Concurrently, forest plots were implemented to graphically present the multivariable-adjusted odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) and statistical significance levels (p-values) for selected variables retained in the logistic regression models.

Diverse ML models development and explanation

Following initial univariate analysis feature screening, three distinct reduction strategies were applied: mean decrease Gini values guided RF predictor selection, recursive feature elimination (RFE) optimized support vector machine (SVM) inputs, and permutation feature importance (PFI) via the “hstats” R package for extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost) feature curation38. Three parallel ML architectures were subsequently developed for comparative assessment. RF classifiers constructed via the “randomForest” R package with 66 decision trees and considered three random features at each split (mtry = 7). This configuration was fine-tuned based on testing and adjustments, ultimately resulting in the lowest estimation error rate of out-of-bag samples (0BB = 28.95%). SVM models employed the “e1071::svm” function with radial basis function (RBF) kernel on RFE-filtered features. The XGBoost model was optimized for log loss minimization through “caret::train” hyperparameter tuning and “xgboost::xgb.train” implementation, utilizing PFI-selected predictors39. Similarly, these feature-optimized models were benchmarked against a 12-variable full model and a 3-weaklink-variable simplified model. Feature importance was quantified through model-specific metrics: Gini index (randomForest::varImpPlot), gain values (xgboost::xgb.importance), and RFE rankings (caret::rfe). Unified interpretability analysis employed SHAP values (shapviz::sv_importance) supplemented by accelerated Shapley approximations (fastshap::explain for SVM-RFE) and individualized prediction decomposition (DALEX::predict_parts)40.

ML model development with AutoScore framework

Except three common ML models, a ML-based clinical score generation algorithm, AutoScore, was also introduced to develop LNM risk scoring models for PTC41. Following the methodological descriptions of the six modules as previously detailed 7, AutoScore automates the development of parsimonious sparse-score risk models for predefined outcomes. The training data set (km_train) served as the foundational resource for generating prediction models within the AutoScore framework, employing variable ranking methodologies based on Area Under the Curve (AUC) or RF approaches. Subsequently, the testing data set (km_test) was instrumental in assessing a variety of candidate clinical models, facilitating parameter tuning, and model selection. Ultimately, the performance metrics of the final model was calculated based on the validation cohorts (ch_valid and ca_valid). The visualization of predicted probabilities were generated corresponding to risk scores with an interactive figure.

Model performance comparison

As the measures of performance previously reported42, ML and LR models were evaluated in terms of discrimination, calibration, accuracy, and clinical utility metrics. The receiver operating characteristic area under the curve (ROC AUC) was used to evaluate the discrimination capabilities in assistance with “pROC” package. Model performance was evaluated using confusion matrix with “confusionMatrix” function in “caret” package, including several commonly used evaluation indicators, such as sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, F1-score, and Brier Score. The predicted probabilities and the actual outcomes were matched by calibration curves with “val.prob” function from “rms” package43. Decision curve analysis (DCA) was used to evaluate the net benefit of the models at different thresholds. We selected the best prediction model on the performance of the above evaluation indicators in the testing and validation sets. The optimal model was selected via multi-criteria evaluation across testing and validation cohorts, prioritizing clinical interpretability and feature compatibility.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics of categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Continuous variables with non-normal distribution were described as median with interquartile range (IQR) and compared using the Kruskal–Wallis test, while categorical variables were compared between LNM and non-LNM groups by chi-square tests. The OR and the 95% Cl for the relationships between each variable and LNM (positive or negative) were calculated using binary LR. All statistical analyses were conducted using R (version 4.3.3) and RStudio (version 2023.06.0 + 421) software, including baseline tables, LR analysis, ML algorithms, model performance comparison, explanation, and visualization. AutoScores models were built using R (version 4.3.3) and Python (version 3.12). Statistical significance levels were set at 0.05.
