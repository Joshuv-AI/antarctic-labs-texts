Three-dimensional imaging of subepithelial tumors: feasibility and utility in preoperative assessment—a retrospective single-center observational study in Japan
Authors: Eriko Koizumi, Osamu Goto, Yumiko Ishikawa, Tsugumi Habu, Hiroto Noda, Shun Nakagome, Kazutoshi Higuchi, Katsuhiko Iwakiri
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background/AimsEndoscopic ultrasound and conventional computed tomography (CT) are useful for preoperative assessment of subepithelial tumors (SETs). However, surgical approaches are sometimes changed intraoperatively owing to unexpected discrepancies between the planned and actual visualizations of tumors because preoperative images are typically two-dimensional.MethodsIn this study, we evaluated the feasibility of morphological evaluation using three-dimensional (3D) reconstruction of SETs and its utility in preoperative assessments. We included 15 lesions with a diameter of 1 to 5 cm that were evaluated by CT and pathologically diagnosed as mesenchymal tumors. We examined the feasibility of 3D reconstruction of lesions by evaluating sphericity using CT images with reference to circularity, which was measured from endoscopic ultrasound still images. Furthermore, the predictability of planned surgery determined using 3D images was investigated.ResultsThe median lesion diameter was 22 mm. There were 10, 3, and 2 lesions of gastrointestinal stromal tumors, leiomyomas, and schwannomas, respectively. 3D reconstruction was feasible for all lesions, with a median sphericity of 0.85, aligning with the median circularity (0.88). The predictability of the 3D-based surgical approach was 90%.Conclusions3D reconstruction of SETs is feasible and useful for preoperative determination of the surgical approach.

Graphical abstract

INTRODUCTION

During surgical treatment of gastric subepithelial tumors (SETs), endoscopic ultrasonography (EUS) and computed tomography (CT) are generally performed to confirm the anatomical characteristics of the lesion. However, sometimes, there are discrepancies between the expected and actual visual images during surgery, leading to changes in resection methods. This is partly because of the two-dimensional (2D) nature of the EUS and CT evaluations.

For localized gastric SETs, several approaches can be considered: laparoscopic local resection (LLR), laparoscopic and endoscopic cooperative surgery (LECS), and endoscopic full-thickness resection (EFTR) at selected institutions. These approaches can be flexibly determined depending on the location of the lesion during surgery, provided that both laparoscopists and endoscopists always participate in every surgery, which is difficult, considering cost-effectiveness. If the anatomical information of the lesion can be precisely obtained preoperatively, the surgical approach can be determined more efficiently.

Currently, three-dimensional (3D) imaging has been introduced in clinical settings, and its usefulness has been reported.1-4 Compared with 2D imaging, 3D evaluation is considered more appropriate for understanding the morphology and location of lesions, particularly when the anatomical characteristics of the target lesion affect clinical practice. However, whether 3D images of SETs are clinically useful has not been discussed.

In this study, we aimed to evaluate the feasibility of 3D reconstruction of gastric SETs using preoperative CT images. Furthermore, we investigated the validity of 3D reconstruction of gastric SETs as a preoperative evaluation modality by assessing the predictability of the surgical approach, which could be suggested based on the 3D images.

METHODS

This single-center, retrospective, observational study was conducted between January 2019 and January 2024. We selected consecutive patients with 1 to 5 cm sized gastric SETs, observed on EUS images and thin-slice CT scans in coronal and sagittal sections and pathologically diagnosed as mesenchymal tumors (gastrointestinal stromal tumors [GISTs], leiomyomas, or schwannomas). Because the statistically required number of cases could not be calculated owing to a lack of referrable previous studies, we recruited 15 patients.

3D imaging and measurement of sphericity

After 4 hours of preoperative fasting, thin-slice abdominal CT scans were acquired without any preprocedural preparation, such as oral intake of a foaming agent or water. The CT images of the lesions were outsourced to an external company (CrossMedical Inc.), where 3D images were created from the 2D CT images using the Mimics software (Materialise NV). The volume and surface area of the reconstructed tumor were measured, and we calculated the sphericity, which is the surface area of a sphere with the same volume as the object divided by the surface area of the object (range, 0–1; 1=a true sphere). It is a concept similar to circularity, which is four pi times the area divided by the perimeter squared (range, 0–1; 1=a true circle).5 We also calculated the circularity of each lesion as a reference in the same manner as in our previous study,5-8 using an EUS still image, which showed the maximum surface of tumors.

Outcome measurements

As the primary endpoint, we investigated whether 3D images could be reconstructed, and objectively evaluated whether sphericity could be measured as accurately as circularity. Additionally, we assessed whether sphericity could differentiate leiomyomas from the other two mesenchymal tumor types. As the secondary endpoint, we investigated the predictability of the preferred surgical approach using 3D images and compared it with that of the preoperatively planned surgical approach. Surgery was planned without referring to the 3D images, and information on the actual surgical methods was retrospectively obtained from medical records. In determining the surgical approach, we observed the following principles: LLR or a combination of laparoscopic and endoscopic approaches to neoplasia with non-exposure technique (CLEAN-NET) for the extraluminal type or any type on the fornix, classical LECS for the intramural type, and EFTR with or without one laparoscopic port for the intraluminal type or any type on the center of the lesser curvature.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were presented as median and interquartile range, and differences were analyzed using the Mann-Whitney U-test. Pearson’s correlation coefficient was used to evaluate correlations. A p-value <0.05 was considered indicative of a statistically significant difference. All data management and analyses were performed using IBM SPSS ver. 25.0 (IBM Corp.).

Ethical statement

The study was performed according to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Review Boards of Nippon Medical School Hospital (approval number: B-2021-462). Written informed consent was waived owing to the retrospective nature of the study, and approval to participate in this study was obtained via an opt-out method on the web.

RESULTS

The clinicopathological characteristics of all 15 patients and their lesions are shown in Table 1. The median age was 61 (range, 53–70) years, and 47% of the patients were male. The median lesion diameter on the EUS images was 22 (20–25) mm. There are 10, 3, and 2 lesions of GIST, leiomyoma, and schwannoma, respectively.

3D reconstruction and sphericity measurements were feasible for all lesions (Fig. 1). The median sphericity and circularity were 0.85 (range, 0.82–0.88) and 0.88 (range, 0.50–0.94), respectively (Table 1). Figure 1A shows a scatter diagram of the correlation between sphericity and circularity. Sphericity was linearly correlated with circularity (r=0.461, p=0.084). The median sphericities of GISTs, leiomyomas, and schwannomas were 0.85, 0.73, and 0.87, respectively (Fig. 2B). The sphericity of leiomyomas tended to be lower than that of the combined group of GISTs and schwannomas (0.73 vs. 0.85, p=0.149) (Fig. 2C).

Of the 15 patients, 10 underwent surgery (Table 1). Regarding the surgical approach, in cases 1, 2, 3, 4, 11, 13, and 14, the approach was determined based on the tumor growth pattern. In case 7, although the 3D image suggested an extraluminal growth type, EFTR was chosen because of the lesion being located at the center of the lesser curvature, where a full-thickness defect caused no deflation of the lumen. In case 15, LLR was selected because the lesion was located in the gastric fornix. Accordingly, the predictability of the 3D image-based preferable surgical approach was 90%. In one case (case 12), classical LECS was finally performed to avoid gastric deformity via a unidirectional external approach. The predictability of the planned surgical approach was 80%. In case 4, classical LECS was initially planned based on the assessment of the lesion as an intramural growing type by EUS examination. However, when the stomach was inflated to a certain extent during surgery, the lesion unexpectedly protruded extraluminally, leading to the selection of CLEAN-NET.

DISCUSSION

This study revealed that 3D reconstruction using CT data was possible in all 15 patients with 1 to 5 cm sized gastric SETs and that the images seemed to show the morphology of the lesions well, considering the potential differentiation ability of sphericity, which could be calculated from the 3D images. This imaging modality also appears helpful for preoperatively determining the surgical approach efficiently.

For removing gastric SETs, an optimal method can be chosen from amongst several techniques according to the tumor size, location, and growth type. However, the final decision is made by directly visualizing the tumor. We anticipate that the surgical style can be determined more efficiently by utilizing 3D images preoperatively. In this study, we confirmed that 3D images could be accurately reconstructed to understand lesion shape and calculate sphericity. Unlike vessel reconstruction using contrast agents, no technique enhances SETs; however, 3D images can successfully isolate the tumor from the surrounding tissue, possibly owing to thin-slice CT images. With further advancements in imaging modalities, 3D reconstruction is expected to become increasingly refined.

To assess whether the 3D images could be accurately reconstructed according to the morphological nature of the lesion, we used circularity, which represents the morphology of SETs, accurately and efficiently enough to differentiate one mesenchymal stromal tumor (leiomyoma) from two other major tumors (GIST and schwannoma). The results revealed that sphericity and circularity were lower in leiomyomas than in other mesenchymal tumors, which is consistent with our previous study.5 However, there was no significant difference owing to the small number of cases studied. A larger study may confirm the superiority of sphericity over circularity in differentiating SETs, particularly borderline lesions between roundish leiomyomas and bumpy GISTs/schwannomas. Unfortunately, similar to circularity, sphericity also appeared to have no ability to isolate GIST, which is worth differentiating clinically.

In this study, the predictability of the 3D image-based preferred surgical approach was favorable, at 90%, exceeding that of the clinically determined surgical approach using conventional 2D modalities. This suggests that 3D reconstruction provides detailed information on the morphology and growth type of SETs, potentially aiding in the selection of surgical approach. However, it seems unnecessary to perform 3D reconstruction in all cases because of the associated costs and time consumption if the tumor is small or sufficiently well-protruded to guess the entire shape. In particular, 3D reconstruction is useful when delineating the lesion using EUS is difficult, or when understanding the tumor morphology is difficult because the 2D image shows various shapes depending on the direction and slice. A real model can also be created using 3D printing, which can facilitate the explanation of the current condition of the lesions and the optimal surgical approach for a patient. Furthermore, it could be a useful follow-up tool for patients who prefer CT over more invasive endoscopic examinations.

This study has some limitations. First, this is a single-center retrospective observational study with a small sample size. Due to the retrospective nature of case accumulation in this study, the distribution of GISTs, leiomyomas, and schwannomas was statistically suboptimal. Second, for extraluminal lesions, although 3D images may help determine the need for preoperative endoscopic intervention, selecting the surgical method between LLR and CLEAN-NET remains difficult. Moreover, accurate preoperative prediction of the surgical approach cannot be achieved by 3D reconstruction alone, as it also depends on clinical factors, such as tumor location. Therefore, a prospective study with a large sample size is needed. Third, the generalizability of the findings of this study may be limited because 3D imaging may be less beneficial in institutions with fewer surgical options. Fourth, the circularity and sphericity measured using EUS and 3D-CT imaging may vary depending on factors such as image quality and degree of gastric inflation.

In conclusion, this study suggests that 3D morphological evaluation of gastric SETs is feasible and useful for selecting surgical procedures preoperatively. Our findings may contribute to the development of a novel imaging modality for SETs.
