Physician payment models and preventive cancer screening: a population-based retrospective cohort analysis from Ontario, Canada
Authors: Yihong Bai, Rose Anne Devlin, Steven Habbous, Liisa Jaakkimainen, Sisira Sarma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOntario's primary care reforms have introduced three blended physician payment models: (i) blended fee-for-service (BFFS), (ii) blended capitation without interprofessional teams, and (iii) blended capitation with teams. Each model includes the same pay-for-performance incentives, yet their impact on cancer screening, including that during the COVID-19 pandemic, remains unclear.MethodsWe used linked administrative data (2018–23) to examine the associations between these models and colorectal, cervical, and breast cancer screening rates. Fractional probit regression models, adjusting for physician and patient characteristics, estimated the effects of each payment model relative to the BFFS. Stratified analyses explored heterogeneity by physician sex, age, practice size, rurality, and socioeconomic deprivation.ResultsCompared with the BFFS model, the blended capitation models were associated with higher screening rates, although initial differences were modest. By 2022, nonteam and team capitation models had colorectal screening rates 3.0% and 3.6% higher, respectively, than those of the BFFS. Similar but smaller increases were observed for cervical and breast cancer screening. These advantages persisted through COVID-19 disruptions and were most pronounced among physicians serving rural or socioeconomically disadvantaged populations. Stratified analyses indicated that female, younger, and higher-volume physicians performed better in capitation-based models.ConclusionsBlended capitation arrangements, especially those integrating interprofessional teams, appear more effective than the BFFS in delivering preventive cancer screening. Strengthening team-based primary care and targeted incentives could bolster preventable cancer screening rates in the population, even under pandemic-related challenges. These findings can inform policy decisions aimed at improving population health through optimized primary care provisions.

Introduction

Primary health care improves population health by preventing illness, effectively managing chronic conditions, and providing preventive care [1]. Ontario is Canada's most populous province, with over 15 million residents as of 2024, and operates within a single payer, publicly funded and privately delivered healthcare system. Primary care is delivered by ∼15 000 family physicians who serve as the first point of contact and coordinate access to all health services across the province [2]. By the early 2000s, ensuring timely access to primary care had emerged as a significant concern in Canada [3]. In response to increasing concerns about primary care access and preventive care, the government of Ontario, Canada, implemented wide-ranging primary care reforms that transformed the primary care landscape from pure fee-for-service (FFS) physician payments to retrospective and prospective payment models. These models integrate FFS, capitation, and pay-for-performance financial incentives to encourage preventive care and chronic disease management; and interprofessional primary care teams [4, 5]. It is well-known that the method of physician remuneration influences practice patterns and the delivery of health services to the population [6–8]. FFS models may encourage higher service volumes, whereas capitation and salary lead to fewer services, but incentivize physicians to focus more on preventive care [9].

In response to challenges stemming from the over-provision of services inherent in pure FFS and the under-provision of services inherent in pure capitation, policymakers have introduced blended payment schemes in various jurisdictions, including the United States, Denmark, the United Kingdom, and Sweden [10–12]. In Ontario, the introduction of patient enrollment models (PEMs) within blended payment models in the early- to mid-2000s represented a key policy initiative to reorganize primary care delivery. The comprehensive care model (CCM) and Family Health Group (FHG) are enhanced or blended FFS (BFFS) that combine traditional FFS payments with additional incentives for preventive services. In contrast, the Family Health Network (FHN) and Family Health Organization (FHO) employ blended-capitation models, providing physicians with age- and sex-adjusted capitation payments for enrolled patients, limited FFS payments for certain services, and financial incentives for preventive care such as those in CCMs/FHGs [3, 5].

By 2010/2011, the majority of Ontario's primary care physicians practiced under PEMs, with the FHG (blended-FFS) and FHO (blended-capitation) models being the most prevalent [5, 13]. The FHG model pays physicians primarily through FFS, supplemented with small capitation amounts and preventive care incentives. The FHO model uses capitation as the main payment mechanism, with additional pay-for-performance (P4P) bonuses focused on preventive care and chronic disease management. Furthermore, since 2005, blended capitation physicians (FHNs and FHOs) have been able to form Family Health Teams (FHTs), interdisciplinary groups consisting of physicians, nurses, and allied health professionals who collaborate to provide comprehensive, patient-centered care, subject to availability and approval by the Ontario Ministry of Health [14].

We are interested in whether the type of blended model (capitation versus FFS) affects the ability of P4P incentives to influence preventive cancer screening uptake. Both types of models are likely to increase preventive services in response to financial incentives, however, the stable income of physicians inherent to the blended capitation model may prompt more or more effective preventative services than FFS physicians dependent on service volume [15]. Moreover, compared with blended models, interprofessional team-based capitation (i.e. FHTs) may enhance care coordination and continuity, thereby improving adherence to recommended screening guidelines [16].

Cancer screening represents a critical preventive function of primary care, and primary care physicians play a pivotal role in its delivery [17, 18]. Regular screening facilitates the early detection of cancer in asymptomatic individuals, improving treatment outcomes and reducing mortality [19]. Ontario's organized cancer screening programs with financial incentives facilitate early cancer detection and intervention, reducing the burden of cancer and cancer-related mortality [20, 21]. Understanding how different physician payment models influence cancer screening rates is, therefore, essential, given their implications for population health.

The existing evidence on the impact of Ontario's primary care reforms on cancer screening uptake has been mixed. Using data from 1999 to 2009, one cross-sectional study reported no significant changes in cervical, breast, or colorectal cancer screening rates following the introduction of preventive care incentives [22]. Another study reported that patients in team-based capitation models had higher screening rates for mammography and colorectal cancer screening relative to enhanced FFS in 2011, and experienced greater improvements in cervical cancer screening over time [23]. Differences in screening rates have been noted across various PEMs: in 2011, cervical cancer screening rates were highest in FHG (81.9%), followed by FHO (79.6%) and the traditional FFS (74.2%) [24]. Transition from the traditional FFS to the BFFS is associated with increased screening uptake, although these gains sometimes widen screening inequities for more vulnerable population groups [25]. Multiple studies, including those in Canada [16] and the USA [15, 26, 27], have linked capitation models to higher screening rates.

These findings underscore the influence of physician payment models on cancer screening rates. Nevertheless, critical gaps remain in our understanding of how these payment reforms have affected screening rates over time, particularly amidst the disruptions caused by the COVID-19 pandemic. The pandemic has exposed significant vulnerabilities in health systems that rely heavily on face-to-face contact and the FFS system to deliver services [28]. The present study seeks to address these gaps by examining the association between physician payment models and preventive cancer screening uptake in Ontario. By drawing on recent administrative physician-level data (2018–22), this analysis aims to inform policymakers, healthcare providers, and other stakeholders about the link between physician payment models and preventable cancer screenings.

Method

Data and variables

The study utilized data extracted from health administrative databases maintained at ICES, Ontario. By linking multiple data sources, comprehensive physician-level and patient-level information was obtained, including information on eligible patients who underwent cancer screening, their demographic characteristics and the practice characteristics of physicians across Ontario. Data were gathered from 1 April 2018 to 31 March 2023. We selected 2018 as the starting point because, by this time, the major physician payment models in Ontario introduced in the early to mid-2000s had stabilized. This period also allows for the inclusion of 2 years before the COVID-19 pandemic (2020) and 2 years following its onset, enabling us to assess how different payment models performed under both typical and disrupted contexts.

Information on physicians' payment models and years of medical school graduation was obtained from the Corporate Provider Database. The ICES Physician Database provides physician demographic and practice-related data, including age, sex, and geographic location. Patient age, sex, and residential postal codes were obtained from the Registered Persons Database, which is Ontario's health care registry. The material deprivation quintile was derived from census-based dissemination-area-level data, whereas the Client Agency Program Enrollment database facilitated the linkage of patients to their enrolled family physicians, thus enabling the identification of enrolled and nonenrolled patients. Enrollment documentation requires patients to seek primary care services from their enrolled physician or affiliated group/team, and physicians, in turn, commit to providing comprehensive care to their enrolled patients. Physicians maintain control over their patient rosters, which is subject to approval by the Ministry of Health.

Cancer screening outcomes were defined annually as the proportion of each physician's eligible patient population who met the screening criteria. For breast cancer screening, the denominator included all female patients aged 52–69 years who were alive during the year of assessment, had no recorded history of breast cancer, and had not undergone a mastectomy in the last two years. The numerator tracked those patients within the denominator who had received a mammogram within the past 30 months. For cervical cancer screening, the denominator consisted of female patients aged 35–69 years who were alive during the assessment year, had no previous diagnosis of invasive cervical cancer, and had not undergone a hysterectomy prior to the index date. We chose the 35–69 age range to ensure comparability with earlier studies [16, 22, 24]. The numerator included those among the denominator who had received a Pap test within 36 months preceding the index date. As a robustness check, we conducted sensitivity analyses using the new recommended screening age range of 21–69 years. Finally, for colorectal cancer screening, the denominator was composed of patients aged 52–74 years who were alive within the assessment year; had no prior diagnosis of colorectal cancer, inflammatory bowel disease, or colectomy; and were not receiving palliative care. The numerator included patients who had completed a fecal occult blood test (FOBT) or fecal immunochemical test (FIT) within 2 years before the index date, a sigmoidoscopy within the preceding five years, or a colonoscopy within the preceding 10 years.

The exposure variable categorized physicians into three groups based on their payment model: (i) blended FFS (BFFS), encompassing CCMs and FHGs; (ii) blended capitation without teams (i.e. FHNs FHOs without forming FHTs); and (iii) blended capitation with teams (i.e. FHNs and FHOs who formed FHTs). The BFFS is predominantly an FFS-based model but includes additional incentives and bonuses for certain preventive care activities. Blended capitation without teams receives a set payment per rostered or enrolled patient (adjusted for patient age and sex) alongside some FFS payments for nonenrolled patients and services outside the capitation basket. While preventive care incentives are similar across all three groups, the organizational structure in the BFSS and blended capitation does not include the extensive allied health team support found in FHTs. Blended capitation with FHT relies on age- and sex-adjusted capitation payments for physicians but integrates allied health professionals on a salary basis (e.g. nurse practitioners, dietitians, social workers, etc.) into the practice setting. The presence of team-based support is hypothesized to promote proactive population health management and potentially enhance the delivery of preventive care services, including cancer screening.

All three payment models studied include preventive care incentives (see Supplementary Table S1). Under the capitation models, physicians are also eligible for an “access bonus” (up to 18.59% of the base capitation, or 20.65% for long-term care patients), which is reduced if patients receive in-basket services from primary care physicians outside of the patient's physician practice providers. This bonus encourages continuity and coordination of care in teams. In addition, capitation physicians receive 15% of the FFS billing as shadow-billing premium for in-basket services. These features may incentivize proactive, and guideline-concordant care. In contrast, blended FFS physicians rely primarily on service-based payments but are also eligible for identical preventive care incentives and a small capitation amount. While preventive care incentives are present across all the models, their interaction with the base compensation structure and organizational features may differentially influence physicians' capacity and motivation to meet screening targets [16].

Empirical analysis

To study the associations between physician payment models and cancer screening rates, we employed a fractional regression model. Since the outcome variable is the proportion of eligible patients screened for cancer, a linear regression model may yield predicted proportions exceeding the possible range of zero to one and fail to capture the distributional characteristics of the outcome variable. Thus, we used fractional response models—specifically a fractional probit regression model is better suited for this type of fractional outcome variable. Table 2 presents the marginal effects derived from the adjusted fractional probit models, illustrating the associations between physician payment models and cancer screening rates over time (Supplementary Tables S7-S9 present the full regression results). In 2018, for colorectal cancer screening, physicians practicing under nonteam capitation and team capitation models exhibited screening rates of approximately 0.6% (P = 0.06) and 0.4% (P = 0.28) higher, respectively, than those practicing under the BFFS. The associations were not significant. However, the differences increased in subsequent years, reaching 3.0% (P < 0.001) and 3.6% (P < 0.001), respectively, by 2022. A similar trend was evident for cervical cancer screening: in 2018, nonteam capitation and team capitation models were associated with 2% (P < 0.001) and 2.5% (P < 0.001) higher screening rates than the BFFS, increasing to 3.1% (P < 0.001) and 4.4% (P < 0.001) by 2022. For breast cancer screening, the initial differences were also substantial. In 2018, nonteam capitation was associated with a 2.3% (P < 0.001) higher cancer screening rate, and team capitation was associated with a 4.3% (P < 0.001) higher screening rate than the BFFS. By 2022, these differences had increased to 3.4% (P < 0.001) and 4.7% (P < 0.001), respectively. Supplementary Table S10 presents findings from the unadjusted models. In addition, we conducted our analysis using the updated recommended screening age range of 21–69 years. The results were largely unchanged (see Supplementary Table S11), confirming the robustness of our conclusions.

Discussion

This study contributes to the growing body of evidence suggesting that physician payment models affect preventive cancer screening uptake within the primary care setting. Consistent with prior research [15, 16, 23, 24], we found that capitation payment combined with team-based care was associated with higher screening rates for colorectal, cervical, and breast cancers than the BFFS model. Although the disparities in screening rates in 2018 were relatively small, these gaps expanded considerably over time. For colorectal cancer screening, modest prepandemic differences between capitation and BFFS widened considerably by 2022. Team-based capitation, in particular, resulted in greater relative gains than BFFS, suggesting that the pandemic intensified existing payment-model disparities. The widening differences may be partially explained by structural differences in how these models responded to the pandemic. Fee-for-service models rely heavily on in-person encounters for revenue and thus may have experienced service disruptions when practices were closed and/or in-person patient visits declined during lockdowns. Capitation-based models—especially those with team-based care—had greater financial and organizational flexibility to pivot toward virtual care and maintain screening-related outreach. Additionally, the relational continuity combined with dedicated staff facilitated to resume cancer screening during the recovery phase. Notably, the 2022 results indicate that these differences persisted even after the peak pandemic years. The gap in colorectal cancer screening rates may be partially attributed to the transition from the FOBT to the FIT in June 2019. FIT offers several advantages over FOBT, including higher sensitivity for detecting colorectal cancer and precancerous polyps, no dietary restrictions, and the requirement of only a single stool sample, which can increase patient compliance [31, 32]. Practices operating under capitation-based models, especially those with integrated team-based care, may have been better equipped with the necessary infrastructure and resources to adapt to these changes effectively. In contrast, fee-for-service models might face more challenges in accommodating the new screening protocol, potentially leading to disparities in screening uptake [33]. While cervical and breast cancer screenings exhibited a broadly similar pattern, the incremental increases during and following the pandemic were somewhat smaller than those observed for colorectal cancer screening.

These findings corroborate earlier studies showing that team-based capitation models in Ontario frequently outperform BFFS models in delivering preventive services [23]. They also align with evidence from other jurisdictions demonstrating that the capitation reimbursement system is correlated with higher cancer screening rates [15]. By integrating allied health professionals and fostering coordinated care, team-based models appear to facilitate more proactive population health management and improved adherence to cancer screening guidelines [16]. Moreover, the observed differences persisted during the COVID-19 pandemic, in which the resilience of primary care systems was tested. The relative stability of capitation-based models, including those employing team-based approaches, may be attributable to their reduced reliance on in-person encounters, more stable funding mechanisms, and inherent flexibility in adapting to alternative care delivery methods.

Stratified analyses further revealed that the positive effects of capitation models, especially those with team-based care, were more pronounced among physicians and patient populations facing greater barriers to care. Both female and younger physicians, as well as those practicing in rural, socioeconomically deprived, or high-volume settings, generally experienced greater gains. These findings underscore the importance of considering physician, patient, and area-level factors when evaluating the effectiveness of physician payment models on cancer screening rates. This also suggests that payment models supporting collaborative, interdisciplinary approaches to care may be especially beneficial in contexts where resource constraints and patient complexity pose challenges to preventive care uptake.

Team-based care was significantly associated with approximately two percentage points higher for breast cancer screening rates and 1.5% points higher for cervical cancer screening rates, whereas the difference in colorectal cancer screening was not statistically significant (Supplementary Table S12). These modest improvements may reflect the complexity of preventive care performance, including demand-side factors. Our findings highlight the need for future work examining how specific team-based processes influence screening outcomes.

Our study makes several contributions. First, we provide the most recent evidence on the associations between physician payment models and preventive cancer screening uptake in Canada's most populous province, Ontario, and extend previous work that focused on earlier time periods [22–24]. Second, we examine trends over time, including during the COVID-19 pandemic, thereby offering insights into the relative effectiveness of different remuneration models under conditions of substantial healthcare disruption and recovery periods. Third, our stratified analyses highlight important heterogeneity in cancer screening outcomes across physician and patient characteristics, emphasizing the need to consider contextual factors when designing and evaluating primary care reforms. Future research could explore the relationship between physician payment models and adherence to follow-up care after abnormal test results, as screening is only effective when followed by timely and appropriate diagnosis and treatment. In addition, Ontario recently introduced an organized lung cancer screening program. Assessing whether similar patterns in screening uptake emerge for lung cancer and screening for chronic disease under different payment models would be a valuable extension of this work.

Despite these strengths, our study is not without limitations. As a retrospective observational analysis using administrative data for the most recent years, we cannot establish causality. In particular, there may be selection effects whereby physicians choose to participate in specific payment models based on preferences over wok, leisure and patient health [34, 35]. While we adjusted for a broad range of observed physician and practice characteristics, residual confounding may remain.

Conclusion

Our findings underscore that blended capitation models, especially those featuring interprofessional teams, are associated with higher preventive cancer screening rates than the fee-for-service arrangements. By fostering proactive, population-oriented care, these models show particular promise under challenging conditions, including the COVID-19 pandemic. Policymakers may thus consider expanding the team-based capitation framework and strengthening incentives for preventive care while also providing targeted support to rural or socioeconomically disadvantaged regions. Such measures could help optimize primary care delivery and achieve more equitable health outcomes.
