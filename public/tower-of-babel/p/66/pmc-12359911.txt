Analysis of adaptation processes and anxiety levels of university students staying with earthquake survivor families in state dormitories
Authors: İbrahim Dağ, Kadriye Olğaç
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aimed to examine the adaptation processes and anxiety levels of university students living with earthquake survivor families placed in state dormitories after recent major earthquakes.Materials and MethodsA descriptive, cross-sectional quantitative study was conducted in a state dormitory between May and August 2023. A total of 108 students participated using the snowball sampling method. Data were collected through a Descriptive Characteristics Form and the Beck Anxiety Scale. Statistical analyses included descriptive statistics and linear regression using SPSS 25.0.ResultsAmong the participants, 49% reported increased frequency of contact with their families after the earthquake. Sharing the same dormitory space with earthquake-affected families led to limited personal space for 56%, emotional impact for 51%, and benefits such as emotional support for 56%. Additionally, 45% noted changes in their attitudes, 46% in their social lives, and 56% in their social responsibility awareness. Regression analysis showed that sharing the same environment with families explained 33.7% of the variance in Beck anxiety scores. A significant positive relationship was found between cohabitation with families and anxiety levels (B=0.337, p<0.001), where each unit increase in cohabitation was associated with a 5.379-point increase in anxiety scores.ConclusionsThe findings indicate that post-earthquake family cohabitation significantly affects students' psychological and social well-being. Living in close proximity to affected family members increases anxiety levels. Providing psychological support services for students is crucial to reduce anxiety and facilitate post-disaster recovery and adaptation.

Introduction

Natural disasters, particularly earthquakes, cause not only extensive damage to physical infrastructure but also long-lasting impacts on mental health [1]. University students are among the vulnerable populations whose psychosocial needs may be neglected in post-disaster recovery efforts. These students, already coping with academic and social pressures, may also experience emotional strain when living in environments shared with individuals who have been directly affected by traumatic events [2, 3].

Following the 2023 earthquakes in Turkey, many university students were placed in dormitories alongside earthquake survivor families [4]. This situation created a unique social environment in which students were indirectly exposed to trauma, faced limitations in privacy, and had to adjust to altered living conditions.

While much of the existing literature has focused on the mental health outcomes of direct disaster victims, little is known about the psychological and social effects experienced by those who share living spaces with them. This study aims to address this gap by exploring both the adaptation experiences and anxiety levels of university students living with earthquake survivor families in state dormitories.

Existing studies show that earthquake survivors experience high levels of stress, post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) and anxiety [1–3]. For example, according to the Psychiatric Association of Turkey (TPD) 2023 data, anxiety symptoms were observed in approximately 40% of the affected individuals and PTSD symptoms were observed in 25% [4]. However, the psychological effects of university students sharing the same living space with these families have not yet been sufficiently investigated.

Earthquake survivor families face a serious risk of isolation due to the loss of their social environment and material resources. According to the 2023 report of the Ministry of Youth and Sports, approximately 10,000 families were accommodated in state dormitories after the earthquake. These dormitories are environments where different socio-cultural dynamics interact and where young individuals are predominantly located. This can have a direct effect on the social lives and academic achievements of students living in dormitories. Students who witness the traumatic experiences of earthquake survivors may feel emotional burden and this may increase their anxiety levels [5].

Adaptation is the process of individuals adapting to new environmental conditions. For earthquake survivors, this process involves not only getting used to temporary accommodation conditions, but also uncertainty and anxiety about the future. While social interaction and existing support networks in state dormitories play an important role in the adaptation process of earthquake survivors, the change in the social order that students are used to may cause disruptions in their daily routines. For example, in Anadolu University’s 2023 survey, 35% of students who shared a dormitory with earthquake survivor families reported high levels of anxiety [6].

Anxiety is a state of intense worry that occurs in situations of uncertainty and loss of control. While earthquake survivors experience intense anxiety due to insecurity of shelter, economic problems and uncertainty about the future, students may experience anxiety due to academic pressures and changes in social environment [7, 8]. According to World Health Organisation (WHO) data, the incidence of mental disorders in individuals who live in post-disaster regions is 20–30% higher than normal averages [9]. In this context, students who share the same environment with earthquake survivor families may face additional psychological burden brought by traumatic experiences.

A better understanding of the adaptation processes of earthquake survivor families and students living in state dormitories is critical for the planning of social and psychological support services. The present study aims to examine the adaptation processes and anxiety levels of university students living in dormitory with earthquake survivor families placed in state dormitories after the recent earthquakes in Turkey. The present study aims to contribute to the development of more effective social policies for such crisis situations in the future by providing important data on social solidarity, social cohesion and post-traumatic recovery processes.

Materials and methods

Type of research

The present study is a descriptive, cross-sectional quantitative research.

Setting and time of research

The study was conducted in a state dormitory between May and August 2023.

Sample and population of research

The population of the study consisted of 156 university students residing in the state dormitories of a public university. The sample of the study comprised 108 students, selected through the snowball sampling method, who met the inclusion criteria and voluntarily agreed to participate. Snowball sampling is a non-probability sampling technique commonly used when it is difficult to access all members of the population.

The inclusion criteria for the study were being a university student residing in a state dormitory, volunteering to participate, experiencing adaptation problems, and not having any disability that might impair communication. The exclusion criteria were not having adaptation problems or having any barrier to communication.

Measures

Two measurement instruments, ‘Descriptive Characteristics Form’ and ‘Beck Anxiety Scale’ were used to collect the data.

Descriptive characteristics form

This form was developed by the researchers and includes 17 questions about socio-demographic information including age, gender, height, weight, number of siblings, parents’ education level and employment status, income status, physical activity and dormitories of students. The Descriptive Characteristics Form included several items designed to explore students’ adaptation experiences after the earthquake. These included emotional impact, limitations in space, perceived advantages, and changes in social life or responsibility (for example: ‘Has staying in the same environment as your family affected your social life?’).

Since the form contains open-ended and descriptive questions specific to the research rather than standard scales, validity and reliability analyses have not been performed.

Adaptation-Related Items: The Descriptive Characteristics Form included a number of questions designed to explore students’ adaptation processes to post-disaster dormitory life. These items covered aspects such as emotional responses, limitations in personal space, social life changes, and perceived benefits or disadvantages of cohabiting with earthquake survivor families. These variables were used as indirect indicators of students’ adaptation levels.

Beck anxiety scale

Beck Anxiety Scale was developed by Beck et al. in 1988 to assess the severity of anxiety symptoms [10]. The Turkish validity and reliability study of the scale was conducted by Ulusoy, Şahin, and Erkmen in 1998, demonstrating strong psychometric properties [11]. The scale is a self-report, Likert-type tool consisting of 21 items, each scored between 0 (not at all) and 3 (severely). It evaluates the frequency and severity of anxiety symptoms experienced by the individual over the past week. Total scores range from 0 to 63, with 0–7 points indicating minimal anxiety, 8–15 points mild anxiety, 16–25 points moderate anxiety, and 26–63 points severe anxiety. In the Turkish adaptation study, the internal consistency of the scale was found to be high (Cronbach’s α = 0.93), with item-total correlations ranging from 0.45 to 0.72, and the two-factor structure of the scale (somatic and cognitive symptoms) was confirmed. While the scale is practical, quick to administer, and suitable for both clinical and research settings, it should be interpreted in conjunction with clinical evaluations due to its self-report nature and the potential overlap of somatic symptoms with medical conditions.

Data collection

The data were collected by the first author (İD) in the training rooms of the university. Questionnaires were distributed to the participants, and instructions were provided on how to complete them. The researcher remained present to clarify any questions, but the forms were completed by the students independently. This method ensured consistency in administration while allowing participants to respond privately. It took approximately 20–25 min to administer the forms.

The datasets generated and analyzed during the current study are not publicly available due to ethical restrictions and the protection of participants’ confidentiality, but are available from the corresponding author on reasonable request.

The questionnaires form was developed specifically for this study and is available as Supplementary File 1.

Statistical analysis

The analyses of the data included in the study was carried out with SPSS (Statistical Program in Social Sciences) 25 programme. The Kurtosis and Skewness values were checked whether the data included in the study conformed to normal distribution [12]. Significance level of (p) < 0.050 was taken for comparison tests. Since the variables were normally distributed (p > 0.05), the analyses were continued with parametric test methods. Descriptive values of the variables were presented as number, percentage, mean and standard deviation. Linear regression test was used to explain the Beck anxiety scale.

Discussion

Natural disasters such as earthquakes not only cause physical destruction. but also deeply affect the mental health of individuals. In the aftermath of the 2023 earthquakes families of earthquake survivors had to struggle with the psychological effects of traumatic experiences in addition to their needs for shelter and safety. In this process state dormitories played a critical role in terms of psychosocial support as well as providing temporary accommodation. However university students staying in these dormitories also had to adapt to the new social environment continue their academic life and cope with the indirect effects of the trauma experienced. Although there are studies on the adaptation states and anxiety levels of earthquake on individuals there are not enough studies examining the adaptation processes and anxiety levels of university students who live with earthquake survivor families placed in state dormitories after the earthquake. Our study was conducted to examine the adaptation processes and anxiety levels of university students living with earthquake survivor families in state dormitories. This study focused on two key variables: (1) the adaptation processes of university students living with earthquake survivor families in state dormitories and (2) their levels of anxiety following the post-disaster cohabitation experience. The discussion explores both factors in light of the findings and relevant literature.

In a study conducted by Kirişçi and Atalmış (2017) on the satisfaction of university students staying in state dormitories regarding their housing conditions. The reasons for staying in dormitories were as follows: dormitory is financially convenient, most of their friends live in dormitories, dormitory is safe, dormitory life is disciplinar, the family wants them to stay in dormitories and since there is no housework such as cleaning and food preparation there is more time for studying [13]. In a study conducted by İbili and Uyanık (2018) it was stated that the first reason why students preferred dormitories was that dormitories were financially affordable. Family choice ranked the third. In the present study when the reasons why students preferred dormitories were analysed economic reasons ranked first with a rate of 30%. The family’s choice ranked second with a rate of 19% [14]. According to these results the most important factor why students prefer dormitories as a place of accommodation is economic reasons. Moreover the fact that family choice is also an important reason shows that family choices can be an important factor in students’ personal preferences.

It was found that 27.8% of the students saw their families once a month and 25.9% saw their families 2–3 times a month. After the earthquake an increase of 49.1% was observed in the frequency of seeing their families. This increase shows that the earthquake created a tendency for students to strengthen their family ties. In a study conducted by Uygur (2024) on university students who were survivors of the earthquake it was found that the majority of the students became more resilient thanks to the emotional support they received from their families and experienced a decrease in post-traumatic stress symptoms [15]. In different studies it was reported that significant changes occurred in family relationships after the earthquake [16, 17]. This shows that traumatic events such as earthquakes may leave lasting effects on social relationships and may cause families to play a greater role in students’ search for trust.

24.1% of the students stated that they had problems in dormitories due to friendship relations and 23.1% due to cultural differences. In Demirbilek’s study (2012) the friendship relations of students staying in dormitories and the problems related to these relations were examined and it was found that 27.7% of the students had low rates of friendship relations and cultural characteristics were among the important factors in this [18]. These results suggest that students living in dormitories experience difficulties in social relationships and that social diversity can sometimes lead to conflicts.

While some students rely on their own internal resources to cope with the challenges they face others prefer to receive support from their social environment. In a study conducted by Tang et al. (2018) it was stated that young people used coping methods by sharing their feelings [19]. In the study conducted by Bahar and Çuhadar (2023) it was found that university students used coping strategies effectively after the earthquake [20]. In the present study it was reported that 26.9% of the students found solutions to the problems they encountered on their own while 21.3% of them consulted their friends. Social support functions as a problem- and emotion-focused coping strategy that reduces stress and alleviates negative effects.

After the earthquake 55.6% of the students who shared the same dormitory environment with families stated that their living spaces were restricted and 50.9% stated that they were emotionally affected. In addition 55.6% stated that they benefited from the advantages of this situation. 45.4% stated that there was a change in their attitudes. 46.3% stated that there was a change in their social lives and 56.5% stated that their social responsibility awareness was affected. In a study conducted by Tekin Babuç and Gökçe (2024) it was stated that relational problems arising from the need for privacy emerged as a result of the participants living with other families after the February 6 earthquake. Also in the present study participants started to respond more positively to these changes in their relationships in the post-disaster period by showing flexibility due to factors such as adapting to the new lifestyle and experiencing new roles [21]. These findings show how a major disaster such as an earthquake can profoundly affect the lives of individuals and how this interaction can lead to significant changes in their psychological and social behaviors. It can be understood that students developed a different perspective after the earthquake especially on issues such as safety and living together. In addition increased awareness of social responsibility shows that students became more sensitive to social events and realized the necessity of social solidarity.

No statistically significant difference was found between the mean age, height and weight of the students and their Beck anxiety scores. This finding shows that students’ physical characteristics do not have a direct effect on their anxiety levels. In contrast to our study there is a significant relationship between age and scores obtained from the Beck anxiety scale in the study conducted by Algur (2019) for university students. The anxiety levels of students who participated in the study and who were in the ‘23 and over’ age group were found to be significantly higher than those of students in the ‘20 and under’. ‘21’ and ‘22’ age groups [22]. In a study conducted by Baş et al. (2016) it was found that students with low weight had high scores on the Beck anxiety scale [23]. Considering these findings it can be suggested that the effects of students’ physical characteristics on their emotional and psychological states may vary and anxiety is affected by many factors such as environmental social and psychological factors.

Studies show that natural disasters such as earthquakes are a serious risk factor causing stress in individuals and various psychological symptoms (depression. anxiety) may occur [24–26]. In a study conducted by Şam et al. (2025) it was emphasized that 43.3% of the students were psychologically affected by earthquakes and experienced increased depression, anxiety negative self-concept somatization and hostility [27]. In the present study it was found that staying in the same dormitory environment with survivor families after the earthquake explained 33.7% of the Beck anxiety scores. These findings suggest that traumatic events such as earthquakes can increase students’ anxiety levels and that being with families in the same environment shapes this effect. After an earthquake especially safety and survival concerns can deeply affect the psychological state of individuals. Although family support provides reassurance and comfort in stressful situations it can also lead individuals to feel more stress. Social interactions and constraints in the dormitory environment may increase students’ anxiety levels.

Despite the importance of social support and family ties after the earthquake. the tension and anxiety caused by staying in the same environment have negative effects on students. In particular trauma and uncertainty experienced after a disaster may lead to an increase in anxiety levels of individuals. In the present study a significant positive correlation was found between Beck anxiety scores and this situation in students who shared the same dormitory environment with survivor families (B = 0.337. p < 0.001). A one-unit increase in staying in the same environment with families leads to a 5.379-unit increase in Beck anxiety scores. In previous studies sharing the same environment with individuals who experienced the earthquake was associated with various psychological problems [28–30]. This relationship suggests that being in the same environment with families is a factor that increases anxiety levels of individuals especially after a disaster. Although being in the same environment with families can meet the emotional needs of students at the same time the restriction of the student’s own space and constant close communication can create tension and uncertainty which can increase anxiety and stress levels in students.

The findings suggest that adaptation was not solely an individual process. but was shaped by the emotional climate of the shared dormitory environment. Limited personal space and increased exposure to others’ trauma appear to have influenced both the social cohesion and psychological states of students. The study shows that students experience changes not only in their anxiety levels but also in their perceptions and attitudes towards social responsibility. This highlights the need to evaluate adaptation not only in psychological terms but also in social terms.

Limitations of the study

The fact that the knowledge attitudes and behaviors of the participants were obtained from their statements may have created some limitations for these data. The fact that the study was conducted in a single region may be a limitation. One limitation of this study is that adaptation was not measured using a standardized scale. Instead students’ self-reported experiences and perceptions related to dormitory life were used as indirect indicators of adaptation which may have limited the generalizability of the findings. Finally it would be more appropriate to examine the relationship between anxiety levels and whether participants lost a family member during the earthquake rather than age, height and weight. However such data were not collected in this study which is acknowledged as a limitation.

Conclusion

The study found that cohabitation with earthquake survivor families influenced students’ psychological and social experiences leading to increased anxiety and changes in social behaviors. These findings indicate that university students’ family relationships social lives and the changes they experienced after the earthquake had significant effects on their psychological and social development.

This study revealed that living in shared dormitory environments with earthquake survivor families had a significant impact on university students’ psychological well-being and adaptation experiences. Students reported emotional strain changes in social life. and increased awareness of social responsibility. These results highlight the importance of considering the indirect psychological effects of disaster response strategies on unaffected individuals living in close contact with trauma survivors.

Universities and dormitory administrations should prioritize psychosocial support services in the aftermath of disasters not only for directly affected individuals but also for students who live in shared spaces with them. Interventions should aim to create supportive. inclusive environments that promote both emotional well-being and community resilience.

The problems students face in the dormitory their ability to find solutions on their own. and the changes they experience after a disaster emphasize the importance of student support systems in universities. Such findings suggest that future studies should focus more on the social support needs of university students and the emotional and psychological difficulties they experience in times of crisis.

Disasters such as earthquakes can leave lasting effects on the psychological health of individuals. In the present study it was determined that staying in the same environment with families after the earthquake caused a significant increase in anxiety scores of students. Post-disaster trauma can threaten not only students’ physical safety but also their emotional and psychological health. It can be seen that being in the same environment with families after the earthquake is an important factor that increases anxiety levels of students. It is emphasized that traumatic events such as earthquakes can cause permanent effects on psychological health of students and that family support and social environment play an important role in this situation. In this context universities and dormitory administrations should strengthen post-disaster psychological support services and create environments to support students’ psychological well-being. It is important for universities and dormitory administrations to provide psychological support services to reduce anxiety levels of students and help them recover after disasters and to improve their ability to cope with stressful situations.
