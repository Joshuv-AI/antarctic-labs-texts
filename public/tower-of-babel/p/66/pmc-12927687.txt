The Effect of Education on Evidence‐Based Practice Implementation Among Nurse Leaders: A Mixed‐Methods Systematic Review
Authors: Dawn M. Horsom, Natasha Keogh, Geraldine Lee, Mohamad M. Saab
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimThis mixed methods systematic review explored the effect of education on the implementation of evidence‐based practice among nurse leaders within healthcare settings.IntroductionEducation influences nurse leaders’ ability to implement evidence‐based practice. Successful implementation requires multifaceted strategies, including organisational support and leadership engagement, to overcome persistent challenges and ensure evidence‐based knowledge is translated into high‐quality, person‐centred care.MethodsThis review followed Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) guidance and was reported using the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses checklist. CINAHL, PubMed, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials, Web of Science, ClinicalTrials.gov, and Google Scholar were searched without language or year limits to June 2025. Studies were double screened, appraised using the Mixed‐Method Appraisal Tool, and synthesised using convergent parallel synthesis.ResultsA total of 3,646 records were identified, with nine studies included. Education positively influenced nurse leaders’ knowledge, attitudes, beliefs, clinical skills, and leadership behaviours regarding evidence‐based practice. Perceptions of leadership support improved following interventions. However, evidence on long‐term sustainability was limited. Organisational culture, resourcing, communication, and collaboration acted as both barriers and facilitators to implementation.DiscussionEducation strengthens nurse leaders’ capacity to support evidence‐based practice but is most effective when embedded within supportive organisational contexts. Education alone is insufficient without alignment with culture, resources, and leadership structures.ConclusionEducation enhances nurse leaders’ ability to implement evidence‐based practice, but sustained impact requires organisational readiness and ongoing support.Implications for nursing and nursing policyTargeted education, combined with organisational support, can strengthen leadership capacity and improve care quality and patient outcomes.Policies that invest in structured evidence‐based practice education for nurse leaders, alongside organisational infrastructure and protected time, may enhance sustainable evidence‐based care delivery.

Introduction

Evidence‐based medicine has long been recognised through the recording and learning from patient outcomes (Claridge and Fabian 2005). The evolution of evidence‐based practice (EBP) in the 1990s built on this foundation, shifting healthcare towards conscious, critical decision‐making rooted in research, clinical expertise, and patient values to deliver standardised, quality, person‐centred care (Claridge and Fabian 2005; Lancaster and Rhodes 2020). The main goal of EBP is to improve patient outcomes and ensure safe, effective care (Lancaster and Rhodes 2020). However, EBP implementation faces challenges, and addressing these can enhance the translation of clinical insights into policy and foster supportive environments for best practices (Bullock et al. 2021).

Establishing standards is crucial for quality improvement in healthcare services (Kelly et al. 2023). Political, economic, sociocultural, and technological factors influence the provision of safe, high‐quality, affordable, person‐centred care in an equitable manner (Wang et al. 2016). The primary goal is to enhance the health and well‐being of populations (National Institute for Health and Care Excellence [NICE] 2018). Healthcare settings, where nurses and their leaders provide care, range from acute services to increasingly within homes and communities, aligning with the “treat at home first” care strategy (World Health Organization [WHO] 2021, 2023). Implementing EBP in healthcare systems with conflicting agendas is complex and challenging (Bullock et al. 2021). Oh et al. (2021) highlighted the gap between the development of EBP and its actual implementation, a problem particularly noticeable in low‐ and middle‐income countries (WHO 2023).

Implementing EBP at a local, national, and international level is crucial (Lancaster and Rhodes 2020). Nurses, who make up over 60% of the global healthcare workforce, are central to EBP delivery (Government of Ireland 2021). Nurse leaders are especially important for driving change and implementing EBP (WHO 2020). Nurse leadership can occur at any stage in a nurse's career (Ting et al. 2024). In the current review, a nurse leader is considered a registered nurse who uses clinical expertise, communication, and leadership skills to influence individuals and teams to improve patient care and organisational outcomes (American Nurses Association 2015). Examples of nurse leader roles include nurse manager, clinical nurse specialist, advanced nurse practitioner, director of nursing, and chief nursing officer (Cummings et al. 2018).

Clinical competence among nurses and their leaders not only is essential for high‐quality care but can also create barriers to EBP by fostering overreliance on experience, resistance to change, and scepticism toward research (Lee and Choi 2022). Conversely, insufficient competence can result in lack of confidence, inadequate knowledge, and difficulty integrating theory into practice (Wang et al. 2016). Optimal care requires combining clinical expertise with current EBP (Kelly et al. 2023). Overcoming barriers requires targeted education, leadership development, and organisational support (Authement and Dormire 2020).

Nurse leaders play a significant role in ensuring EBP is implemented into practice, making it critical to consider practical and impactful educational strategies to aid them in doing so. Education among nurse leaders is an essential lifelong commitment to maintain the highest standard of knowledge (Lehane et al. 2019). Strategies to implement EBP through education should yield high‐quality, auditable outcomes and be impactful and cost‐effective (Authement and Dormire 2020).

Nurse leaders must be equipped with practical, impactful educational strategies to implement EBP (Lehane et al. 2019). Educational strategies should produce high‐quality, auditable, and cost‐effective outcomes (Authement and Dormire 2020). While face‐to‐face education was once the standard, online learning now offers accessible, affordable, evidence‐based options (Portela Dos Santos et al. 2022). Yet, challenges such as budget constraints, competing priorities, and resource shortages persist, especially in lower‐income settings (WHO 2021). Flexible education, including blended onsite and online approaches, is optimal for nurse leaders, accommodating irregular shifts and providing both practical and theoretical learning (Portela Dos Santos et al. 2022; Authement and Dormire 2020).

However, education alone is insufficient for successful EBP implementation (Lehane et al. 2019). Multifaceted approaches incorporating organisational support, leadership engagement, and practice‐reinforcing strategies are required to achieve meaningful change (Ayoubian et al. 2020). Therefore, educational strategies must address these challenges to ensure that EBP, deeply embedded in nursing education, translates effectively into practice (Portela Dos Santos et al. 2022). It is imperative to explore the effect of education on EBP implementation among nurse leaders, recognising both its strengths and limitations.

Aim

The aim of this mixed methods systematic review is to explore the effect of education on the implementation of EBP among nurse leaders within healthcare settings. This systematic review sets out to address the following questions: What methods are used in the literature to educate nurse leaders about the implementation of EBP in healthcare settings?What mechanisms are in place to measure the effect of education on the implementation of EBP among nurse leaders in healthcare settings?What is the effect of education on nurse leaders’ ability to implement EBP in healthcare settings?What are the barriers and facilitators to education and/or the implementation of EBP among nurse leaders in healthcare settings?

Methods

Design

This review was guided by the JBI mixed methods systematic reviews guidance (Lizarondo et al. 2020; Stern et al. 2020) and reported using the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses checklist (Page et al. 2021). The protocol for this review is registered in Open Science Framework: https://osf.io/32jbu.

Eligibility Criteria

Eligibility criteria were developed using the population, exposure, and outcomes (PEO) framework, which was a modified version of the population, intervention, comparison, and outcome (PICO) framework (Richardson et al. 1995; Hosseini et al. 2024). All included studies met the following criteria: Population: Registered nurses occupying formal leadership or advanced practice roles with responsibility for clinical, managerial, educational, or strategic decision‐making within healthcare organisations. Eligible roles included nurse managers, clinical nurse specialists, advanced nurse practitioners, directors of nursing, and chief nursing officers (Cummings et al. 2018). Studies with mixed professional samples were included only where data specific to nurse leaders could be clearly disaggregated from those of other participants.

Exposure: Structured educational interventions aimed at developing knowledge, skills, or competencies related to EBP. Eligible educational approaches included, but were not limited to, formal training programmes, workshops, academic courses, blended or online learning, mentorship, coaching, or continuing professional development initiatives. To be included, the educational intervention had to explicitly target EBP principles (e.g., critical appraisal, research utilisation, guideline development, or implementation strategies) and be delivered to nurse leaders working in healthcare settings (Melnyk and Fineout‐Overholt 2019). Acknowledging that education is often delivered as part of a broader EBP implementation strategy, studies in which education constituted one component of a multifaceted strategy were included only where data specific to the educational component could be isolated.

Outcomes: The implementation of EBP in healthcare settings, defined as the active integration of best available research evidence with clinical expertise and organisational or patient preferences to inform practice change (Melnyk et al. 2014). Outcomes included, but were not limited to, changes in clinical practice, adoption or use of evidence‐based guidelines or protocols, implementation behaviours, organisational practice change initiatives, or leadership actions that facilitated EBP uptake among nursing staff. Moreover, studies conducted in primary, secondary, and tertiary locations in healthcare settings were all considered for inclusion. No limitations were applied to the year of publication, geographical areas, or language to minimise the risk of study selection bias (Higgins et al. 2017).

Dissertations and theses were excluded due to variable methodological reporting, limited peer review, and challenges related to accessibility and reproducibility, which may compromise consistent appraisal and synthesis (Paez 2017). Review articles were excluded to avoid duplication of evidence and double counting of findings, as this review focused on synthesising primary empirical studies examining educational interventions and EBP implementation among nurse leaders (Aromataris and Munn 2020). Case studies were excluded because their context‐specific and descriptive nature limits transferability and does not permit systematic assessment of implementation outcomes at organisational or leadership levels. The full eligibility criteria are detailed in Supplementary File S1.

Search Strategy and Study Selection

A scoping search of Google Scholar, CINAHL, PubMed, and the University College Cork's library discovery search (OneSearch) was carried out initially in all fields to obtain key terms, synonyms, subject headings, and terminology relevant to the aim of the current review (Bramer et al. 2018). Search strategies were developed and adapted for each database using subject headings related to three key concepts: ‘nurse leaders’, ‘education’, and ‘evidence‐based practice’. These terms were then applied to the databases CINAHL, PubMed, CENTRAL, and Web of Science.

ClinicalTrials.gov was searched to seek further studies, and Google Scholar was searched using the advanced search filter. Due to the high volume of results and as Google Scholar ranks hits in terms of relevancy, the first ten pages were reviewed (Haddaway et al. 2015; Saab et al. 2022). Hand searches of the included studies’ reference lists were also carried out. The search strategy and the choice of databases were reviewed by an experienced academic librarian to ensure rigour in line with Peer Review of Electronic Search Strategies guidelines (McGowan et al. 2016). Key terms were combined with Boolean terms ‘AND’ and ‘OR’ with the proximity operator ‘N’ being used as appropriate. The resulting keywords were searched under the limits of title or abstract using the PEO framework as a guide. Records were searched from their inception to June 2025 (Figure 1). The full search strategy for all data sources is presented in Supplementary File S2.

Study Identification, screening, and identification process.

All records identified in the search were uploaded and screened using Covidence systematic review online software (Veritas Health Innovation 2024). Duplicates were automatically removed in Covidence. A second reviewer was provided with the review aims, questions, and eligibility criteria. Title and abstract screenings were then carried out independently by both reviewers, and potentially eligible studies were moved to the full‐text screening and reviewed. At this stage, the reference lists of the included studies were hand‐searched. These processes were again carried out independently by both reviewers, with any conflicts being resolved through discussion at all stages. A third reviewer was involved when consensus was not reached.

A total of 3,646 studies were identified from the searches. Following deduplication, 3,103 records were screened based on title and abstract, and 3,029 irrelevant records were excluded. There were 74 studies brought forward for full‐text screening. Of those, nine were included in the current review (Figure 1).

Of note, one included study (Sheffer et al. 2011) focused on advanced practice nurses and other nursing disciplines, but it was directly and identically extracted from an earlier, broader study involving multiple healthcare professionals (Sheffer et al. 2009). For the purpose of this systematic review, these studies were treated as a single source, as only the data related to advanced practice nurses met the eligibility criteria.

Data Extraction

Data were extracted using two tables. The first table was based on the work of Fineout‐Overholt et al. (2010) and adapted further with guidance from Saab et al. (2018) and Dillon et al. (2020) (Supplementary File S3). Headings were derived from the characteristics of the included studies and constructed in the table as follows: Author(s), year and country, aims, research design and setting, sample, educational intervention, data collection, relevant outcomes, results, and barriers and facilitators. The second table (Supplementary File S4) was adapted from O'Connell et al. (2024) to help further elaborate on the relevant findings. All data were extracted by the first author and cross‐checked for validity and accuracy by the second and fourth authors.

Data Synthesis

Quantitative, qualitative, and mixed methods studies included in a mixed methods review are either synthesised sequentially through sequential synthesis or concurrently through convergent synthesis (Hong et al. 2017; Coffey et al. 2019). Convergent synthesis is often used for mixed methods systematic reviews and is further divided into three sub‐categories: data‐based convergent synthesis, results‐based convergent synthesis, and parallel results convergent synthesis (Hong et al. 2017; Stern et al. 2020). Where there are two or more complementary review questions, which is the case in the current review, Hong et al. (2017) recommended using parallel results convergent synthesis, where the evidence from the included studies is analysed and presented separately. The integration then takes place during the interpretation of the results, which occurred in the discussion section of this review (Hong et al. 2017; Stern et al. 2020).

Quantitative and qualitative findings were grouped and thematically synthesised under two categories in line with the pre‐defined review questions and outcomes. Following data extraction, findings were sub‐categorised for each of the two categories (O'Connell et al. 2024). Therefore, findings were synthesised as follows: (i) The effect of education on nurse leaders’ implementation of EBP (nurse leaders’ knowledge, attitudes, and beliefs about implementing EBP; nurse leaders’ clinical skills; and leadership among nurse leaders) and (ii) barriers and facilitators to the implementation of EBP among nurse leaders (resources for the implementation of EBP; collaboration and communication among nurse leaders; and organisational culture and readiness for EBP). Data synthesis was carried out by the first author and reviewed and approved by all co‐authors to help reduce the risk of selective outcome reporting (Stern et al. 2020). Quality Appraisal

The quality of mixed methods studies, randomised controlled trials (RCTs), non‐RCTs, and qualitative studies was assessed using the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (Hong et al. 2018). Five criteria relevant to each study design were used (Hong et al. 2019). Each criterion was applied to the corresponding study, with responses rated as ‘Yes’, ‘No’, or ‘Cannot Tell’. To reduce the risk of reporting and study selection bias, all eligible studies were included irrespective of their methodological quality (Saab et al. 2018). Quality appraisal was conducted by the first author and cross‐checked by the second and fourth authors. Any disagreements were discussed until a consensus was reached.

All nine studies had clear research questions. All but one study (Simmons‐Fields et al. 2020) collected data appropriate to address their research question. Among the mixed methods studies (n = 4), three (Caramanica and Spiva 2018; Gifford et al. 2011, 2012) received a ‘Yes’ rating across all five qualitative criteria. In contrast, Simmons‐Fields et al. (2020) received ‘Unclear’ ratings, with only the sampling strategy and sample representativeness rated positively. Overall, methodological rigour for this study was unclear, particularly regarding adherence to both qualitative and quantitative quality criteria.

The non‐RCT component of Caramanica and Spiva (2018) rated well, except for uncertainty about whether confounders were accounted for in design and analysis. Gifford et al. (2011) rated positively in the quantitative descriptive domain, though it was unclear if their sample was representative. Gifford et al. (2012), which included an RCT, was methodologically strong, though blinding of outcome assessors was unclear. All three studies rated positively in mixed ‐methods quality appraisal.

Hooge et al. (2022), Patton et al. (2024), and Sheffer et al. (2009, 2011) used non‐RCT designs and demonstrated good methodological quality. However, the completeness of outcome data in Hooge et al. (2022) was unclear.

The qualitative study by Kanerva et al. (2017) used an appropriate data collection method that effectively addressed the research question. There was strong coherence across data sources, collection, analysis, and interpretation.

In their RCT, Tucker et al. (2022) used appropriate randomisation with comparable groups at baseline. Outcome data were complete, and participant adherence to the intervention was evident. However, it was unclear whether outcome assessors were blinded. The full quality appraisal is available in Supplementary File 5.

Results

Study Characteristics

The majority of the included studies (n = 6) were conducted in the United States. Settings ranged from home and community care organisation (n = 2) to acute hospitals (n = 2). Nurse managers, nurse leaders, and advanced practice nurses were featured in all nine studies. Four studies used a mixed‐method design, three studies were non‐RCTs, one study was an RCT, and one study was qualitative. The sample size varied from 10 (Kanerva et al. 2017) to 75 participants (Simmons‐Fields et al. 2020).

In‐person educational interventions were most utilised either alone (n = 3) or in combination with other modes of delivering education (n = 5). Online education with podcasts, blogs, and video tutorials was the sole educational intervention in one study (Hooge et al. 2022).

Different data collection tools were utilised with questionnaires and surveys featuring strongly (n = 6). Outcome measures were heterogeneous and included both subjective and objective indicators. Most studies used self‐report scales assessing EBP knowledge, beliefs, leadership behaviours, self‐efficacy, and organisational readiness (Caramanica et al. 2018; Hooge et al. 2022; Patton et al. 2024; Tucker et al. 2022). Fewer studies assessed objective practice outcomes, including completion of EBP projects (Caramanica et al. 2018), chart‐audited guideline adherence (Gifford et al. 2011; Gifford et al. 2012), implementation of structured tools or programmes (Sheffer et al. 2009, 2011, Kanerva et al. 2017), and integration of advanced care planning into clinical practice (Simmons‐Fields et al. 2020). Several studies combined audits or interviews with self‐report measures, although tool characteristics and validation were inconsistently reported (Gifford et al. 2011, 2012).

Follow‐up periods varied considerably across studies, ranging from immediate post‐intervention assessment to two years. Five studies measured outcomes immediately post‐intervention only, limiting insight into sustainability (Caramanica and Spiva 2018; Sheffer et al. 2009, 2011; Hooge et al. 2022; Kanerva et al. 2017). Short‐term follow‐up at one to three months suggested maintained improvements in implementation‐related behaviours and practice indicators (Patton et al. 2024; Simmons‐Fields et al. 2020). Only one study assessed outcomes at two years and reported that improvements in EBP knowledge and beliefs were not fully sustained over time (Tucker et al. 2022). Overall, evidence regarding the durability of intervention effects beyond the immediate post‐intervention period was limited or unclear. The full study characteristics can be found in Table 2.

The Effect of Education on Nurse Leaders’ Implementation of Evidence‐Based Practice

Nurse Leaders’ Knowledge, Attitudes, and Beliefs About Implementing Evidence‐Based Practice

In all nine studies, the effect of education on the implementation of EBP among nurse leaders was positive when measured in terms of knowledge, attitudes, and beliefs. Sheffer et al. (2009, 2011) found that advanced practice nurses who received in‐person education on managing tobacco dependence showed significantly enhanced knowledge and attitudes and improved patient assistance (P < .01). Patton et al. (2024) also reported EBP knowledge increased post the in‐person programme (P < .001). Caramanica and Spiva (2018) educated nurse managers through a blend of online programmes and in‐person workshops with learning objectives of environmental support, leadership behaviours, education and clinical support. It was found that beliefs and attitudes improved significantly from 3.86/5 to 4.69/5 (P < .001), with clinical nurses actively contributing to project success (Caramanica and Spiva 2018).

Six studies found that educational interventions for nurse leaders improved knowledge, attitudes, and beliefs, positively influencing quality care and patient outcomes (Caramanica and Spiva 2018; Gifford et al. 2011, 2012; Hooge et al. 2022; Kanerva et al. 2017; Simmons‐Fields et al. 2020). Kanerva et al. (2017) reported that nurse leaders’ active involvement in safe patient care improved outcomes and addressed challenges to safe care. Tucker et al.’s (2022) RCT showed immersive education improved EBP knowledge and beliefs in cancer care for the first cohort, but improvements were not maintained at one year. The second cohort, receiving the intervention in year two, showed greater EBP implementation knowledge, possibly due to COVID‐19's impact on the study's initial stages (Tucker et al. 2022). Previous education and EBP exposure among nurse leaders may have influenced the positive findings, particularly regarding knowledge, attitudes, and beliefs (Sheffer et al. 2009, 2011; Tucker et al. 2022).

Nurse Leaders’ Clinical Skills

All nine studies demonstrated that educating nurse leaders significantly improved their EBP implementation skills. Patton et al. (2024) in their pre‐ and post‐test analysis found an improvement in the nurse manager EBP competency scale, overall rising from 1.03 pre‐test to 1.70 post‐test (P < .001). Caramanica and Spiva (2018) found that after implementing an EBP educational programme for nurse managers, clinical nurses’ EBP‐related skills increased significantly from 3.20/5 to 4.38/5 (P < .001). Following this training, nurse managers were better able to support and foster an EBP environment, which contributed to the successful implementation of 11 EBP projects through targeted interventions.

All nine studies reported positive impacts on clinical skills from educational interventions, leading to successful EBP projects, increased nurse engagement, and improved patient outcomes. Gifford et al. (2011) found nurse managers have better translation skills to their teams, connecting clinical guidelines to patient benefits in practice. Gifford et al. (2012) noted that while the control and experimental groups performed similarly in most task‐oriented leadership behaviours, the experimental group excelled in ongoing support, planning, establishing clear roles, and providing feedback post‐intervention. Nurse managers in this group consistently followed up on team progress to meet target outcomes. Simmons‐Fields et al. (2020) reported a significant 37% increase in completed advanced care directives, resulting in more effective communication of patients’ preferences, values, and goals to the multidisciplinary care team. This improvement demonstrated the practical impact of the educational interventions on patient‐centred care practices.

Leadership Among Nurse Leaders

Five studies reported improvements in leadership post‐education. Within the experimental group of nurse managers in the study by Gifford et al. (2012), it was reported that their ability to lead effectively was enhanced post‐intervention. This resulted in positive changes in their ability to engage with staff around guideline use and led to the adoption of new EBP practices (Gifford et al. 2012).

Effective leadership was reported as a vital component in the implementation of EBP in the study by Gifford et al. (2011), especially in relation to supporting practice change post‐intervention, with one nurse manager stating: This put a whole different light on making change … you don't realise how important it is to have your manager and the people in the office, the resource nurse and ET … backing the nurses. I don't think the nurses realise how important it is but sometimes we ourselves don't realise how important our support is to them. So that brought that piece to light. Caramanica and Spiva (2018) found that enhanced leadership post‐intervention improved staff participation in EBP, with nurse managers’ support of nurses increasing from 3.39/5 to 4.5/5 (P < .001). Kanerva et al. (2017) reported that consistent practices and nurse leadership are crucial for advancing patient safety. Post‐intervention, leadership facilitated systematic use of the identify, situation, background, assessment, and recommendation (ISBAR) tool during patient transfers, improving consistency in documentation, oral reporting, work division, and medication administration (Kanerva et al. 2017). Patton et al. (2024) in their implementation leadership scale reported a marked improvement post their educational programme, with the overall pre‐test score being 2.08, increasing to 2.52 post‐test (P < .001). While clinical perceptions of this reported change differed, showing no statistically significant difference (P = .49), it must be noted that their pre‐test perception of nurse leader's implementation of the EBP scale was high at 2.81, showing a moderate to high score (Patton et al. 2024).

Barriers and Facilitators to the Implementation of Evidence‐Based Practice Among Nurse Leaders

Seven studies reported barriers to EBP implementation, while six identified facilitators. These findings are categorised under resources for the implementation of EBP, collaboration and communication among nurse leaders, organisational culture, and readiness for EBP. Some overlap existed between barriers and facilitators. In certain cases, pre‐intervention barriers became facilitators or had no impact post‐intervention. None of the included studies reported on barriers and facilitators to education per se.

Leadership and Barriers to the Implementation of Evidence‐Based Practice Among Nurse Leaders

Resources for the Implementation of Evidence‐Based Practice

Three of six studies reporting on resourcing identified it as a barrier to EBP implementation. Gifford et al. (2011) cited heavy workload and a lack of equipment as challenges. Similarly, Caramanica and Spiva (2018) noted staff shortages, time constraints, and lack of supportive resources as barriers. Kanerva et al. (2017) added ineffective use of available resources as an impediment to EBP implementation.

Collaboration and Communication Among Nurse Leaders

Three studies highlighted the negative impact of poor collaboration and communication on EBP implementation (Caramanica and Spiva 2018; Gifford et al. 2011; Kanerva et al. 2017). Gifford et al. (2011) reported persistent poor team communication, with teleconferencing identified as a barrier to effective communication. Caramanica and Spiva (2018) noted a lack of collaborative practices and communication pre‐intervention, which hindered a shared vision for EBP. Kanerva et al. (2017) found fragmented EBP implementation due to poor collaboration and ineffective communication among nurse leaders and teams, with only partial improvement post‐intervention.

Organisational Culture and Readiness for Evidence‐Based Practice

Five studies identified organisational culture and readiness as barriers to EBP implementation, primarily at the local level. Caramanica et al. (2018) found nurse managers faced resistance to the implementation of EBP pre‐intervention, reflected in low leadership support ratings (3.39/5). Kanerva et al. (2017) reported embedded communication norms hindered ISBAR tool implementation, with some staff found to be using oral reporting as a means of demonstrating their excellence when transferring patients, leading to lengthy handovers. Hooge et al. (2022) noted a lack of protected time for EBP processes, indicating poor organisational commitment, though pre‐ and post‐intervention results showed no significant difference (P = .423). The desire to change was found to be lacking within the organisation in many of the studies (Caramanica and Spiva 2018; Gifford et al. 2011; Gifford et al. 2012; Kanerva et al. 2017).

Leadership and Facilitators to the Implementation of Evidence‐Based Practice

Resources for the Implementation of Evidence‐Based Practice

Six studies highlighted the positive impact of resourcing on EBP implementation post‐intervention. Tucker et al. (2022) noted a strong organisational commitment to resourcing EBP from their study's inception. They reported a strong existing willingness to engage with and resource EBP implementation within the organisation studied (Tucker et al. 2022). Caramanica and Spiva (2018) found nurse managers prioritised providing EBP mentors to staff after the educational intervention. Multiple studies identified key facilitators: learning tools, continuous education, and resources for EBP implementation (Gifford et al. 2011, 2012; Hooge et al. 2022; Kanerva et al. 2017; Tucker et al. 2022).

Collaboration and Communication Among Nurse Leaders

Kanerva et al. (2017) reported improved communication and collaborative practices following the implementation of the ISBAR tool through nurse leader interventions. Multiple studies identified communication, engagement, networking, and collaboration as key facilitators of EBP implementation and maintenance (Caramanica and Spiva 2018; Gifford et al. 2011, 2012; Hooge et al. 2022; Kanerva et al. 2017; Simmons‐Fields et al. 2020). Simmons‐Fields et al. (2020) noted the creation of a bidirectional communication system post‐intervention, enabling safe patient transfers and effective communication of advanced care planning needs. Hooge et al. (2022) found that collaborative practices enhanced advanced practice nurses’ competence and confidence in EBP use, while improved networking among multidisciplinary teams and nurse leaders led to better patient outcomes.

Organisational Culture and Readiness for Evidence‐Based Practice

Several studies identified strategies to overcome barriers to EBP implementation. Gifford et al. (2011) involved senior administrators to demonstrate organisational commitment, while Simmons‐Fields et al. (2020) aligned team‐based advanced care planning with organisational goals. Addressing barriers throughout the interventions served as a facilitator (Gifford et al. 2011, 2012), and nurse manager support for clinical nurses increased significantly post‐intervention, from a mean of 3.39/5 to 4.5/5 (P < .001) according to Caramanica and Spiva (2018). Tucker et al. (2022) noted that pre‐existing organisational commitment to EBP culture among nurse leaders facilitated implementation. These strategies collectively promoted motivation, enthusiasm, and cultural change, supporting successful EBP implementation (Tucker et al. 2022).

Discussion

Education is fundamental to nursing practice, from undergraduate level through the career lifecycle of nurses (Lehane et al. 2019). Findings from this mixed methods systematic review indicate that educating nurse leaders positively influences the implementation of EBP across diverse healthcare settings. Implementing EBP is essential for safe, high‐quality service, as recognised in the literature (NICE 2018), and is crucial for optimal patient care and improved outcomes (WHO 2023). However, translating EBP into practice remains challenging (Lehane et al. 2019).

All nine studies indicated that educational interventions with nurse leaders positively influenced EBP implementation regarding knowledge, attitudes, and beliefs. Hooge et al. (2022) highlighted the success of an EBP e‐mentoring programme in enhancing knowledge and beliefs among advanced practice registered nurses, suggesting that increased EBP beliefs predict better implementation. The significance of nurse leaders’ knowledge and beliefs in EBP implementation is widely accepted (Haavisto et al. 2023). The review results underscored a connection between enhancing nurse leaders' knowledge and attitudes about EBP through education and the quality of care and patient outcomes (Caramanica and Spiva 2018; Gifford et al. 2011, 2012; Hooge et al. 2022; Simmons‐Fields et al. 2020; Tucker et al. 2022).

Gifford et al. (2011) noted that EBP education not only improved nurse leaders’ knowledge but also increased guideline adherence, leading to better patient care. The goals of providing quality care and improving patient outcomes are universal in healthcare (Wang et al. 2016; WHO 2023). Thus, continuous engagement of nurse leaders in educational interventions promoting EBP implementation is vital within healthcare organisations. This finding is particularly relevant to current practice as implementing EBP within a growing healthcare system poses an increasing challenge to meet patient needs in a safe way and improve patient outcomes (Melnyk et al. 2018).

It was found within this review that maintaining the momentum and level of competence post‐intervention was a challenge (Kanerva et al. 2017; Tucker et al. 2022). Melnyk et al. (2018) reported similar findings, noting that the education nurse leaders typically receive to attain their positions tends to emphasise research writing rather than the integration of EBP into clinical settings. This educational focus may limit their ability to effectively implement and promote EBP in practice. Elsheikh et al. (2023) proposed ongoing supportive education in EBP practices throughout nurse leaders’ careers. Improvement in clinical skills was consistently reported across all studies, crucial for successful EBP project implementation (Caramanica and Spiva 2018; Gifford et al. 2011, 2012; Kanerva et al. 2017; Patton et al. 2024; Sheffer et al. 2009, 2011; Simmons‐Fields et al. 2020; Tucker et al. 2022).

Caramanica and Spiva (2018) documented that eleven EBP projects were successfully implemented following a significant increase in EBP skill levels due to training and support from nurse managers. Enhancing clinical skills among nurse leaders benefited their teams and patient outcomes (Haavisto et al. 2023). The ability of nurse leaders to mentor frontline staff in EBP implementation is essential for success (Cassidy et al. 2021). Improved leadership was noted by Caramanica and Spiva (2018) and Patton et al. (2024), emphasising the value of strong leadership in EBP success.

While this review aimed to explore barriers and facilitators related to education and/or EBP implementation, none of the included studies attributed these factors to education; they were linked exclusively to EBP implementation. Barriers to EBP implementation included resource issues identified in seven studies. Lack of time and resources due to competing priorities was a pre‐intervention finding by Caramanica and Spiva (2018) and Kanerva et al. (2017), continuing to challenge post‐intervention efforts. Conversely, Tucker et al. (2022) found that a committed approach to resourcing led to successful implementation even during a pandemic. A lack of resources can jeopardise care quality and patient safety (Melnyk et al. 2018; WHO 2023).

Substandard communication within teams was another barrier reported by several studies. Improvements in communication skills among nurse leaders through shared governance positively impacted patient outcomes (Caramanica and Spiva 2018; Hooge et al. 2022; Kanerva et al. 2017; Tucker et al. 2022), although poor communication challenges persisted post‐intervention due to ingrained practices (Kanerva et al. 2017). Barriers related to organisational culture and readiness for EBP were also noted.

Resistance to change by nurse leaders was cited as a barrier in multiple studies (Caramanica and Spiva 2018; Gifford et al. 2011, 2012; Kanerva et al. 2017), highlighting the need for alignment with organisational goals to incentivise change (Gifford et al. 2011, 2012; Simmons‐Fields et al. 2020). Undoubtedly, organisational support is critical for successful EBP implementation (Tucker et al. 2021).

The prominence of organisational culture and readiness in the included studies aligns with contemporary implementation frameworks, such as the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR), which highlight “inner setting” factors—including leadership engagement, culture, and resource availability—as key determinants of successful implementation (Reardon et al. 2025). These findings suggest that educational interventions for nurse leaders are most effective when embedded within organisations prepared and committed to change, reinforcing the need to consider both individual competencies and the broader organisational context when implementing EBP.

Finally, it is important to acknowledge that, across the included studies, educational interventions were frequently embedded within broader, multi‐component implementation strategies that also incorporated organisational change, mentorship, leadership development, and team‐based structures. As such, while positive outcomes were associated with education, it was not always possible to attribute improvements in EBP implementation solely to the educational component. This reflects the complexity of implementation processes in real‐world healthcare settings (Grol and Wensing 2013; Powell et al. 2015), and is consistent with implementation science literature, which emphasises the synergistic effects of combined strategies in achieving sustainable practice change (Nilsen 2015; Melnyk et al. 2018).

Implications

Education positively influenced EBP implementation, leading to successful EBP projects that ensured safe, quality services and improved patient outcomes. Nurse leaders, who oversee the largest healthcare populations, play a vital role in this process (WHO 2023). Their involvement in implementing quality practices based on current EBP fosters positive patient outcomes locally while aiming to enhance population health at a macro level (NICE 2018; WHO 2023).

Melnyk et al. (2018) emphasised that optimal treatment and population health outcomes depend on nurses developing proficiency in EBP, with effective educational strategies being essential. The influence of organisational culture and readiness for EBP on intervention outcomes highlights the need for further research in this area (Tucker et al. 2021). Successfully incorporating EBP can enhance care quality and standardise healthcare delivery to achieve desired patient outcomes (Melnyk et al. 2012). Future research should include nurse leaders as standalone practitioners and part of multidisciplinary teams, given the collaborative nature of these practices (Elsheikh et al. 2023). The inclusion of diverse healthcare settings, particularly community environments, is recommended as part of the “treat at home first” vision for care (Government of Ireland 2021; WHO 2021, 2023).

There is a paucity of literature on the effectiveness of education on EBP implementation among nurse leaders that warrants attention (Elsheikh et al. 2023). Incidental findings suggest that previous education may have positively influenced results in some studies, with successful outcomes achieved post‐intervention regardless of education modality. These findings require further exploration. There is also a paucity of evidence on the barriers and facilitators to education, an area that warrants further exploration in future research.

The review primarily included mixed methods studies. While combining quantitative and qualitative methodologies yields rich insights, more longitudinal RCTs are needed to evaluate the full impact of education on EBP implementation among nurse leaders (Saab et al. 2018). Future research should incorporate standardised educational interventions and measurement tools to produce meaningful results that align with organisational goals, enhancing care quality and patient‐centred outcomes (Melnyk et al. 2018).

There is limited evidence on the sustainability of the effect of educational interventions over time. While most studies reported positive immediate effects on EBP knowledge, skills, leadership behaviours, and practice outcomes, follow‐up beyond the short term was rare. Only one study examined outcomes at two years and demonstrated diminished retention of EBP knowledge and beliefs over time (Tucker et al. 2022), while other studies relied on immediate or short‐term follow‐up only (Kanerva et al. 2017; Patton et al. 2024). This highlights sustainability as a critical gap in the evidence base. Future research should prioritise longitudinal designs with repeated follow‐up and objective implementation outcomes to determine whether educational gains translate into long‐term practice change.

All the included studies were conducted in high‐income countries. Resource constraints, differences in healthcare infrastructure, staffing levels, and organisational support in low‐ and middle‐income countries may influence the feasibility and effectiveness of educational interventions for nurse leaders. Therefore, while the review demonstrates the potential of education to enhance EBP implementation, adaptation of strategies to local contexts and careful consideration of resource availability are necessary to achieve similar outcomes in settings with different healthcare challenges (World Health Organization 2023; Melnyk et al. 2018).

Strengths and Limitations

To the authors’ knowledge, this is the first mixed methods systematic review examining the impact of education on implementing EBP among nurse leaders in healthcare settings. Strengths include consultation with an academic librarian, comprehensive searches across databases and grey literature, inclusion of various study designs, and independent screening and quality appraisal; all of which enhance rigour and reduce study selection bias (Higgins et al. 2017).

Of note, the databases were selected to ensure comprehensive coverage of nursing, biomedical, and interdisciplinary health research relevant to nurse leadership, education, and EBP. CINAHL was prioritised for its strong indexing of nursing and allied health literature, while PubMed and CENTRAL were included to capture biomedical and intervention‐focused studies. Web of Science was used to identify interdisciplinary and implementation‐related research not always indexed in discipline‐specific databases. Google Scholar and ClinicalTrials.gov were searched to enhance sensitivity and identify potentially relevant grey or ongoing studies, and hand‐searching of reference lists further supported coverage. Despite these measures, it is acknowledged that limiting the search to these databases may have resulted in the omission of relevant studies indexed in other discipline‐specific (e.g., Embase and PsycINFO) or regional databases. This represents a potential limitation of the review and may affect the breadth and representativeness of the evidence base; however, the selected databases were deemed sufficient to capture the most relevant and methodologically robust literature aligned with the review aim and questions.

The diversity of outcome measures within the included studies limited cross‐study comparability. While subjective outcomes (e.g., beliefs, perceived leadership, and self‐efficacy) captured important precursors to EBP implementation (Hooge et al. 2022; Patton et al. 2024; Tucker et al. 2022), they did not adequately demonstrate practice change. Objective outcomes, such as guideline adherence or completed implementation activities, provided stronger evidence of impact but were measured inconsistently and using different approaches (Gifford et al. 2011, 2012; Simmons‐Fields et al. 2020). This heterogeneity limits the ability to draw firm conclusions and highlights the need for greater standardisation of outcome measures, particularly through the inclusion of objective, practice‐level indicators alongside validated self‐report scales.

Acknowledging positionality is important in interpreting review outcomes (Higgins et al. 2017). Therefore, the first author's role in developing and implementing EBP and healthcare policies may represent a potential source of bias. None of the included studies addressed barriers or facilitators to education, highlighting a gap in the literature. The limited number of studies, some with small sample sizes and high attrition rates due to COVID‐19 (Hooge et al. 2022; Tucker et al. 2022), affects generalisability. Additionally, heterogeneity across study designs, settings, interventions, and outcome measures limited options for data synthesis and further impacts generalisability (Linden and Hönekopp 2021). Finally, although studies were not excluded based on methodological quality, findings from studies with methodological concerns should be interpreted with caution.

Conclusion

This review highlights the critical role of educating nurse leaders in the successful implementation of EBP, with important implications for policymakers, future research, and the creation of high‐quality, safe healthcare environments aimed at improving patient outcomes. As leaders of the largest healthcare workforce, nurse leaders are well‐positioned to align EBP implementation with national and international organisational goals.

Education at the ward level positively impacts nurse leaders’ knowledge, attitudes, and beliefs about EBP, while also strengthening clinical and leadership competencies. However, the literature notes some limitations, and successful EBP implementation remains a multifaceted challenge. Key barriers include limited resources, poor communication, lack of collaboration, and insufficient organisational readiness. Without a supportive culture, competing priorities can overshadow EBP initiatives.

Although educational interventions demonstrate consistent short‐term benefits, evidence regarding the sustainability of these effects over time is limited. This review contributes to the existing evidence base but highlights a clear need for longitudinal research with standardised outcome measures to determine whether educational gains are maintained and translated into sustained practice change. Addressing contextual barriers and facilitators alongside education will be essential to empowering nurse leaders to drive durable, evidence‐based improvements in clinical practice.
