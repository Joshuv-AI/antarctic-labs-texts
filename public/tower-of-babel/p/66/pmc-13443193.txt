Brain-Dead Organ Donation in Neurosurgical Care: Roles of Neurosurgeons and Collaboration With the Korea Organ Donation Agency (KODA)
Authors: Kyoungmin Kim, Jaesook Oh, Hyunjin Kang, Samuel Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOrgan transplantation is the ultimate treatment that extends and improves the quality of life for patients with end-stage organ failure, and among these, brain-dead organ donation enables life-sustaining heart and lung transplants. Since the first kidney transplantation from a brain-dead donor in Korea in 1979, the Organs Transplant Act was enacted in 1999 and comprehensively revised in 2010. This amendment marked a shift in the national framework from “Preventing Organ Trafficking and Ensuring Fair Allocation” toward “Establishing an Active Organ Procurement System” to promote donation. The number of brain-dead donors increased every year from 2010 to 2016 following the comprehensive amendment of the Organs Transplant Act. Since 2017, however, this trend has plateaued and subsequently declined, raising concerns about the widening gap between organ supply and demand, increasing reliance on living donation, and prolonged waiting times and mortality among transplant candidates. The major underlying causes of brain death, including traumatic brain injury, intracranial hemorrhage, and hypoxic brain injury, are frequently encountered in neurocritical care. Therefore, neurosurgeons play a crucial role in the brain-dead organ donation pathway. This review examines the institutional framework and current status of brain-dead organ donation in Korea. It also discusses the major clinical conditions leading to brain-dead organ donation and explores the role of neurosurgeons, as well as points of collaboration with the Korea Organ Donation Agency.Keywords: Brain death; Tissue and organ procurement; Organ transplantation; Brain injuries, traumatic; Neurosurgery

INTRODUCTION

Organ transplantation is the only treatment that extends life and improves quality of life for patients with end-stage organ failure. In Korea, the first kidney transplantation from a brain-dead donor was performed in 1979, followed by a liver transplantation from a brain-dead donor in 1988. Subsequently, the enactment of the Organs Transplant Act in 1999 brought organ transplantation under institutional regulation, and the comprehensive amendment in 2010 established an independent organ procurement organization (IOPO) and introduced a reporting system for potential brain-dead (PBD) donor to alleviate the imbalance between organ supply and demand, thereby shifting the organ procurement system from a hospital-based model to a national public framework. The brain-dead organ donation system has continued to evolve, including the integration of ‘the organ procurement organization’ and ‘the Human Tissue donation support institutions’ in 2017, and the inclusion of the PBD reporting system in the Quality Assessment of Medical Institutions in 2023.16,20)

When comparing national and international trends in organ donation and transplantation, living donor organ transplantation accounted for an average of 61.5% of all organ transplants in Korea over the most recent five-year period (2020–2024),29) markedly higher than the proportions reported in the United States (14.7%) and Spain (6.7%).12) In 2024, the number of brain-dead donors per million population (PMP) in Korea was 7.15, substantially lower than that in Spain (32.32) and the United States (28.39). These findings indicate that, in Korea, where the number of brain-dead donors has remained stagnant despite a continued increase in transplant candidates, organ transplantation remains highly dependent on living donation.12) Because brain-dead organ donation enables transplantation of life-sustaining organs, including the heart and lungs, for which there are no alternatives, promoting brain-dead organ donation has emerged as an important priority directly related to saving lives.

Severe head trauma, intracranial hemorrhage, and hypoxic brain injury are representative conditions that, despite intensive treatment, can progress to brain death and are closely associated with the neurotrauma and neuro-intensive care. Therefore, even when neurosurgical staff have provided the best possible treatment to save a patient’s life, if the patient unfortunately progresses to brain death, their efforts should include reporting to the Korea Organ Donation Agency (KODA) and explaining the PBD notification system, ensuring that the family receives appropriate counseling regarding organ donation.

This article aims to provide an overview of the evolving legal and institutional frameworks surrounding organ donation in Korea, the current status of brain-dead organ donation and transplant waitlists, and the clinical background leading to brain-dead donor organ donation. Furthermore, it will delineate the crucial role of neurosurgical teams in the management of neurotrauma and critically ill neurological patients, emphasizing the importance of their collaboration with the KODA.

LEGISLATIVE AND REGULATORY CHANGES PERTAINING TO ORGAN DONATION

Organ transplantation in Korea began in 1969 with a living-donor kidney transplant, and the history of brain-dead organ transplantation began in 1979 when a kidney was donated from a 41-year-old man who became brain-dead due to subarachnoid hemorrhage. Subsequently, in 1988, a successful liver transplantation from a brain-dead donor for a 14-year-old girl with Wilson’s disease expanded medical and social interest in brain death and organ donation, and with the success of pancreas and heart transplants in 1992 and lung transplants in 1996, all solid organ transplants became possible in Korea.16,20)

At that time, there was not yet sufficient social consensus in Korea on whether to recognize “brain death” as death, and there was no national legal framework existed to comprehensively regulate the brain-dead organ donation and transplantation process.

Accordingly, discussions to establish medical criteria and procedures for determining brain death proceeded, centered on the Korean Medical Association. In 1983, the first brain death determination criteria were drafted by the “Committee on the Definition of Death” under the Korean Medical Association, and in 1989 the “Special Committee on Brain Death Research” specified the criteria for brain death determination by defining death as “the irreversible loss of cardiac and respiratory function, or of whole-brain function including the brainstem.” Subsequently, in 1993, the Korean Medical Association proclaimed the “Declaration on Brain Death” and established the “Brain Death Determination Criteria and Requirements for Medical Institutions for Brain-Dead Organ Transplantation,” and in 1998 announced a “Amendment of Brain Death Determination and Brain-Dead Organ Transplant Criteria.”15,27) Before the enactment of the “the Organs Transplant Act,” brain death determinations for organ donation in clinical practice were made based on the brain death criteria approved by the Korean Medical Association.

With the advancement of organ transplantation, concerns such as illegal organ trafficking and the legal and ethical challenges surrounding the recognition of brain death have emerged, alongside growing social awareness of the necessity for clear criteria to objectively and fairly select transplant recipients, as well as for institutional mechanisms to support and regulate organ transplantation. In light of this awareness, the “the Organs Transplant Act” was enacted in February 1999 and came into effect one year later, in February 2000.18) The enactment of the law marked a significant turning point by institutionalizing organ donation and transplantation, thereby transitioning the practice from being centered within individual medical institutions to a publicly managed system. This led to the establishment of the national organ transplant management system, currently the National Institute of Organ, Tissue and Blood Management, known as the Korean Network for Organ Sharing, and the implementation of legal standards governing the prohibition of organ trafficking, the selection of transplant recipients, equitable organ distribution, as well as the determination of brain death and procedures for organ donation.16,18,20)

However, because the initial the Organs Transplant Act primarily addressed transplant management issues, such as prohibiting organ trafficking and ensuring equitable distribution, it was relatively inadequate in actively identifying and systematically managing PBD. Following the enactment of the law, fairness in organ distribution improved; however, brain-dead organ donations did not increase as anticipated and, in fact, experienced a temporary decline.16,20) The number of brain-dead donors, which had risen to 162 in 1999, declined to 36 by 2002 (TABLE 1), leading to a significant imbalance between organ supply and demand that has become a serious social issue. In response, the Hospital-based Organ Procurement Organization (HOPO) system was introduced in 2003, designating institutions responsible for managing PBD. HOPO designated hospitals that functioned as both organ transplant centers and brain death determination institutions, enabling them to comprehensively manage brain death assessments, organ recovery, and transplant-related activities at the institutional level. However, medical institutions that did not conduct organ transplants demonstrated inherently low interest in identifying and managing PBD, and because this responsibility was distributed across multiple institutions, there were limitations to achieving consistent nationwide growth in donations.16,20) Indeed, the number of brain-dead donors remained at 68 in 2003, 86 in 2004, and 91 in 2005 (TABLE 1).

As the organ supply-demand imbalance persisted, the Ministry of Health and Welfare determined that fundamental measures were needed to promote donation. In April 2005, a “Task Force for Improving the Organ Donation Management System,” comprising relevant academic societies, legal professionals, and civic organizations, was established, and in November of the same year, the “Comprehensive Plan for Improving Organ Donation Management” was announced.16) This plan aimed to promote a culture of donation to increase the number of PBD, while also establishing an active brain-dead organ donation and transplant management system capable of effectively identifying PBD and facilitating organ donation. In particular, as it became evident that the existing hospital-centered procurement system was limited in identifying PBD and facilitating donation procedures, the implementation of a donor-centered IOPO system was advocated. As a result, The Korea Organ Donation Agency (KODA) was established in 2009, leading to the creation of a domestic IOPO system designed to professionally manage the identification of PBDs and the coordination of family donation discussion, and to support brain death determination and organ procurement procedures.

Subsequently, the comprehensive amendment of the “Organs Transplant Act” in 2010, which took effect in 2011, brought another important change to the domestic organ donation system. The core elements were the legal establishment of the PBD reporting system and the organ procurement organization.16,22) The PBD reporting system requires medical institutions to report to the organ procurement organization when PBDs are identified. This moved away from the previous method of proceeding with the donation process only when the family first expressed their intent to donate to medical staff,22) to a system in which the medical institution recognizes the PBD and connects them with the IOPO. With the legal basis for the organ procurement organization established, KODA was formally launched as the nationwide organ procurement organization, and when a medical institution reported a PBD, a coordinator could be dispatched to explain donation, conduct family donation discussions, and support brain death determination and donation procedures. Additionally, institutional improvements were made, such as allowing the donation process to proceed at the hospital where the PBD was located—reducing the burden of transfer—clarifying the time of death as the end time of the brain death determination committee rather than the time of organ procurement, and simplifying the consent requirement from two senior-priority consenters to one.16,22)

To enhance the effectiveness of the revised system in clinical practice, KODA benchmarked the European-developed Donor Action Program in 2012 and applied it to hospitals in Korea. In 2014, the program was further developed into the Donation Improvement Program (DIP), a Korea-adapted program tailored to the domestic organ donation process and medical environment, thereby enhancing its effectiveness.10,16)

The DIP focuses on establishing in-hospital committees to promote donation through agreements with hospitals where PBD are likely to occur, developing protocols, assessing strengths and weaknesses at each stage of the donation process via medical records of intensive care unit (ICU) deaths, conducting surveys on medical staff awareness, and providing education; it has been reported to have positive effects on enhancing medical staff recognition rates of PBD, donation rates, and overall awareness of donation.10)

The integrated operation of the organ procurement organization and the Tissue donation support institutions was also an important change. Since 2005, the field of human tissue donation has operated as a distinct system under the “Safety and Management of Human Tissue Act,” yet there has been overlap between the responsibilities of the tissue donation support institutions—such as providing education to medical institution staff and disseminating information for identifying tissue donors—and those of the organ procurement organization. Accordingly, the need for integrated operation to promote both organ and human tissue donation was recognized, and in April 2017, the Korea Organ Donation Agency and the Korea Foundation for Tissue Donation were integrated to launch the current KODA.16) This served as the foundation for the integrated management of both ‘PBD donors’ and ‘potential tissue donors,’ facilitating consistent communication of donation information to medical institution staff and families, and expanding the support system focused on donors and their bereaved families.

Looking at the national awareness survey report on organ donation, over 80% of respondents indicated that recognition and honors for organ donors are necessary,25) and the ongoing call for appropriate social honors, alongside national support such as funeral expense coverage, persists as a means to promote organ donation.13) Accordingly, following the partial amendment of the Organs Transplant Act in 2023, KODA expanded the responsibilities of the organ procurement organization to include the management and support of organ donors and their bereaved families. It has since been working to foster a culture of social honor that respects donors’ noble decisions and supports the grieving process of their families by organizing “Life-Sharing Week,” facilitating donor transport, holding donor memorial services, establishing bereaved family self-help groups, and providing emotional support counseling.

Recently, policies have been reinforced to institutionally encourage the participation of medical institutions, alongside the establishment of a national mid- to long-term plan to promote donation. In 2021, the government formulated a fundamental plan to promote organ donation, which included intensifying public awareness and education on life-sharing, increasing support for medical institutions handling brain-dead donors, and enhancing recognition for donors.16,23) Specifically, considering that the timely identification and reporting of PBD within medical institutions constitute the initial step in activating brain-dead organ donation, efforts were also made to integrate this process with the medical institution evaluation system. In 2023, the pilot indicator “number of reported PBD” was introduced as part of the Quality Assessment of Medical Institutions.9) This indicator assesses the annual number of reported PBD donors relative to the bed capacity of emergency rooms and adult and pediatric ICUs. This provided medical institutions with an opportunity to understand that reporting PBD to the organ procurement organization is not merely optional cooperation but an integral component of enhancing medical quality and promoting the practice of life-sharing.

Subsequently, through the amendment of the “the Organs Transplant Act” In June 2023, the foundation for developing a comprehensive plan for organ and tissue donation and transplantation was established, followed by the announcement of the first Comprehensive Plan for Organ and Tissue Donation and Transplantation (2026–2030) in November 2025.24) This is the government's first comprehensive plan aimed at promoting organ donation, notable for outlining policy directions and goals for the next five years and for preparing extensive support measures to encourage donation.

In this way, the Organs Transplant Act evolved from its starting purpose of “eradicating organ trafficking and fairly distributing donated organs” toward “activating donation,” through the establishment of an IOPO and a PBD reporting system as a public brain death management system, strengthened donor-centered procedures, and the establishment and implementation of a comprehensive plan for organ and tissue donation and transplantation on a five-year cycle.

Additionally, the integrated operation of the organ procurement organization and the human tissue donation support organization (2017) to improve efficiency in organ and human tissue donation work, and the pilot operation of the “number of reported PBD” as a medical quality indicator (2024) to improve reporting rates of PBD, were also implemented. These changes show that, in line with the international recommendation (World Health Organization) to introduce programs to achieve national self-sufficiency to meet organ transplant demand,37) beyond securing fairness and ethics in organ transplantation, the system has expanded into one where medical institutions, medical staff, and the organ procurement organization cooperate to identify PBD donors and manage the process through to donation.

CURRENT STATUS OF BRAIN-DEAD ORGAN DONATION

Annual trends

The number of brain-dead organ donors, which began at 64 in 2000 following the enactment of the “the Organs Transplant Act,” declined to 36 by 2002 but subsequently increased to 141 in 2006 due to institutional improvements such as the implementation of a hospital-based organ procurement system responsible for managing PBD, and further rose to 261 in 2009, influenced by notable cases including the organ donation of boxer Choi Yo-sam, who was declared brain-dead in 2008, and the corneal donation of Cardinal Kim Sou-hwan in 2009 (FIGURE 1).

Annual trend in brain-dead organ donors in Korea (2000–2025).

The comprehensive amendment of the “Organs Transplant Act,” the introduction of the PBD reporting system in 2010 and the legal establishment of the organ procurement organization in 2011 marked a significant turning point in the increase of brain-dead organ donations. The number of brain-dead organ donors increased from 268 in 2010 to 368 in 2011, just six months after the revised law took effect.16) As efforts to promote donation continued—including the implementation of the DIP, expanded collaboration with medical institutions, and enhanced donor recognition and bereaved family support—brain-dead organ donations reached a peak of 573 in 2016.

However, after 2017, brain-dead organ donation showed a repeating pattern of decline and stagnation. After declining to 515 in 2017, the number of brain-dead donors further decreased to 405 by 2022, influenced by factors such as the implementation of the Life-Sustaining Treatment Decision system3) and the spread of coronavirus disease 2019. After the pilot indicator “number of reported PBD” was introduced as part of the Quality Assessment of Medical Institutions in 2023, the number of donors temporarily increased to 483; however, due to a shortage of hospital medical staff caused by the prolonged medical conflict in 2024, this number declined to 397 in 2024 and further to 370 in 2025. Taken together, the overall trend of brain-dead organ donation in Korea can be characterized by an institutional settlement phase in the early 2000s, a growth phase from the late 2000s through 2016, and a period of decline and stagnation from 2017 onward. Specifically, following the implementation of the PBD reporting system and the establishment of the organ procurement organization in 2011, the number of brain-dead donors increased rapidly, peaking in 2016; however, despite subsequent efforts to strengthen the institutional framework to increase donation rates, has not continued to increase steadily.

Distribution by sex and age groups

Over the nine years from 2017, when the integrated organization combining the organ procurement organization and tissue donation support institutions (KODA) was launched, through 2025, the number of brain-dead donors was 3,989. The sex distribution of brain-dead donors was 2,642 men (66.2%) and 1,347 women (33.8%), with male donors twice as numerous as female donors (TABLE 1).

By age distribution, those aged 50–59 were most numerous at 1,075 (27.0%), followed by 60–69 at 791 (19.8%) and 40–49 at 788 (19.8%). Those in their 20s and 30s accounted for 9.3% and 11.6% respectively, individually lower than the 40s–60s age groups, but combined, those in their 20s and 30s accounted for 833 (20.9%), the second-largest share after those in their 50s. Those aged 19 and under and those aged 70 and over accounted for 5.1% and 7.5%, respectively (TABLE 1). The average age of all brain-dead donors was 48.8 years, with the youngest donor being 73 days old and the oldest 86 years old. While brain-dead donors are mainly concentrated in the middle-aged and older group of 40–69 years (66.5%), the fact that donation is possible across all age groups, from 2 months old to those in their 80s, shows that it is important to assess donation eligibility based on the patient’s medical condition rather than age alone.

CLINICAL BACKGROUNDS LEADING TO BRAIN-DEAD ORGAN DONATION

The major causes of brain death leading to brain-dead organ donation include severe head trauma, intracranial hemorrhage, hypoxic brain injury, and central nervous system tumors. Although these disease groups differ in their underlying mechanisms, they commonly exhibit a high risk of progression to severe brain injury, which may result in increased intracranial pressure, decreased cerebral perfusion, severe brain edema, brainstem compression, or herniation and, despite intensive treatment, can lead to brain death due to irreversible brain damage.6,36,42) Therefore, the field responsible for treating patients with neurological injuries occupies a critical clinical juncture: assessing the likelihood of progression to brain death when recovery is deemed impossible despite aggressive life-saving interventions, reporting to KODA, and facilitating the process of referral for a family donation discussion. This section explores the clinical backgrounds of the principal disease groups responsible for brain death, which can directly result in organ donation.

Head trauma and intracranial hemorrhage

Severe head trauma resulting from incidents such as traffic accidents and falls, as well as intracranial hemorrhages, including subarachnoid and intracranial hemorrhages, constitute major categories of brain injuries that can progress to brain death. The typical clinical progression leading to brain death has been reported in the following order: cardiopulmonary arrest, traumatic brain injury, subarachnoid hemorrhage, and intracranial hemorrhage, with 2.8%–6.1% of patients with traumatic brain injury, 8.5%–10.7% of those with subarachnoid hemorrhage, and 6.1%–9.6% of patients with intracranial hemorrhage progressing to brain death.42) In the actual domestic statistics on brain-dead donors, these two disease groups have also been identified as the primary causes of brain death, accounting for approximately 62.4% of all cases (TABLE 3).

In this patient cohort, prognostic assessment at the initial treatment stage may influence subsequent therapeutic decisions and the PBD for proceeding to brain-dead organ donation. Patients with acute severe brain injury are often initially assessed as having a poor prognosis due to decreased consciousness, abnormal pupillary responses, and diminished brainstem reflexes2,19); however, if prognosis is prematurely determined or withdraw life-sustaining treatment (WLST) is decided without adequate hemodynamic stabilization and repeated neurological evaluations, opportunities to reassess PBD progression to brain death and organ donation may be limited.

In countries such as the United States, the United Kingdom, and Spain, the devastating brain injury (DBI) protocol is applied as an approach to more carefully judge the prognosis of patients with acute severe brain injury and to timely confirm the possibility of organ donation. A significant proportion of deceased organ donations reportedly originate from DBI, such as intracranial hemorrhage and severe head trauma. Within the emergency and ICU, protocols recommend delaying the WLST in DBI patients for up to 72 hours while continuing conservative management and reassessing the patient every 24 hours. If the patient progresses to brain death during this period, consultation with an organ donation coordinator is advised to facilitate brain death determination and organ donation. Conversely, if WLST is being considered, consultation with the organ donation coordinator regarding donation after circulatory death (DCD) is recommended.8,33) In Spain, for patients with DBI for whom treatment is deemed futile and death is anticipated based on neurological criteria (death by neurological criteria), a protocol is implemented in which organ donation is considered as an integral component of end-of-life care planning during the initiation or continuation of intensive care treatment. According to the report, 74.2% of patients with DBI progressed to brain death, of whom 77.8% subsequently donated organs.35) This approach does not imply prolonging treatment solely for the purpose of organ donation; rather, it serves as a clinical basis for adequately assessing the recovery PBD and progression to brain death in DBI, as well as for promptly confirming donation eligibility by facilitating referrals to specialized organizations for donation discussions when patients advance to brain death.

Hypoxic-ischemic brain injury caused by cardiac arrest

Hypoxic brain injury may occur after cardiac arrest, which can result from various causes including heart disease, respiratory failure, hanging, and drowning. As of 2024, the survival rate after cardiac arrest in Korea was reported to be 9.2%, and 9.37% of patients were reported not to recover brain function.21) Severe hypoxic-ischemic brain injury caused by cardiac arrest can lead to the loss of whole-brain function, that is, brain death.39) For the patient and their family, this irreversible medical condition represents a deeply unfortunate outcome; However, organ donation may provide an opportunity to save the life of another patient awaiting transplantation. Although the temporary cessation of blood circulation during cardiac arrest is likely to impair organ function, including that of the brain, organs retrieved from patients who progressed to brain death following cardiac arrest have been reported to exhibit transplant success rates comparable to those of organs donated by brain-dead patients due to other causes.1,34,38) The 2025 American Heart Association guideline for post-cardiac arrest care11) recommends that all patients resuscitated following cardiac arrest who subsequently progress to brain death be evaluated for organ donation.

Tumors of the central nervous system

Central nervous system tumors are not a leading cause of brain death; however, they constitute one of the disease categories that can progress to brain death. However, in this patient group, the potential for organ donation necessitates more careful judgment than in patients with severe brain injuries in general. Unlike other malignant tumors, primary brain tumors rarely metastasize to other organs, which permits consideration of organ donation. However, several risk factors have been identified that may increase the likelihood of tumor cell transmission, including high-grade central nervous system tumors (grades III–IV), metastasis to other organs, the presence of a ventriculoperitoneal or ventriculoatrial shunt, and a history of radiation therapy and chemotherapy.26,44) Meanwhile, overseas cohort studies have reported that no tumor metastasis was observed in recipients who received organs from donors with primary intracranial malignant tumors or high-grade brain tumors, and that transplant survival rates did not differ significantly from those of the control group.7,43) Therefore, when risk factors are identified in donor groups with central nervous system tumors, the tumor grade and metastatic potential should be thoroughly evaluated instead of categorically excluding the possibility of donation.

Spinal cord injury

Unlike intracranial hemorrhage, head trauma, and hypoxic brain injury, spinal cord injury is not typically classified as a primary cause directly leading to brain death. Rather than spinal cord injury directly causing the irreversible loss of whole-brain function that defines brain death, it should be understood that upper cervical spinal cord injury can induce respiratory center paralysis and autonomic nervous system dysfunction, leading to hypoxic-ischemic brain injury that may result in brain death, as well as influencing the process of brain death determination.14,31,40)

When patients with spinal cord injury exhibit symptoms such as loss of consciousness, absence of brainstem reflexes, and cessation of spontaneous breathing, these manifestations should not be solely attributed to neurological deficits resulting from the spinal cord injury; rather, the potential coexistence of brain injury and the risk of progression to brain death should be concurrently evaluated. Since spinal cord injury can result in quadriplegia and respiratory failure, it is essential during the brain death determination process to differentiate whether the absence of motor response or apnea arises from brainstem dysfunction or from loss of spinal function caused by cervical cord injury. Since spinal cord injury may confound the clinical diagnosis of brain death, ancillary testing should be performed when necessary to confirm the absence of cerebral blood flow.14)

Meanwhile, although spinal cord injury does not result in brain death, it holds significant clinical relevance in terms of broadening the scope of organ donation. In countries where DCD is already practiced, patients with spinal cord injuries are identified as a potential donor group following death after WLST within the controlled DCD category.5) In Korea, where only brain-dead organ donation is currently permitted, this holds significant importance in the context of integrating organ donation into end-of-life care, particularly with regard to the prospective introduction of a DCD system.

THE ROLE OF NEUROSURGEONS AND COLLABORATION WITH KODA

Brain-dead organ donation is closely associated with the management of severe neurological injuries and the care of patients in neuro-ICUs. Neurosurgeons are often the first physicians to evaluate patients at risk of progressing to brain death and to communicate the patient’s condition and prognosis to their families. In this process, the timely identification of a PBD and coordination with KODA are essential to ensure that donation suitability assessments and family donation discussions are conducted appropriately, thereby providing families with sufficient information about organ donation and supporting informed decision-making.

Identification of PBD and reporting to KODA

Neurosurgical staff are responsible for promptly identifying the potential progression to brain death during the intensive care of patients with severe neurological injuries and reporting this to KODA. despite intensive treatment, brain death should be considered when the patient meets the legal criteria for reporting, such as the absence of spontaneous breathing while maintained on a ventilator, the presence of a definitive brain lesion unamenable to treatment, or the loss of five or more brainstem reflexes. At this stage, the assessment and determination of a PBD state is a medical judgment grounded in the course of treatment and clinical criteria, rather than a decision based on organ donation, and must be reported to the organ procurement organization, KODA, in accordance with the PBD reporting system.

Timely recognition and reporting of a PBD allows the patient's family sufficient time to consider the option of organ donation. In Spain, an analysis of 206,000 medical records of ICU deaths from 1999 to 2010 indicated that brain death accounted for 2.3% of all hospital deaths and 12.4% of ICU deaths.4) When applied to the scale of deaths in domestic medical institutions, the estimated number of PBD is approximately 5,000; however, it can be observed that the actual number of reported PBD constitutes only 42% of this estimate (TABLE 8). Since 2024, following the introduction of the “number of reported PBD” as the Quality Assessment of Medical Institutions, the implementation of an EMR-based SMS reporting system, and the education of medical staff, the number of reports concerning PBD has increased annually; however, enhanced cooperation remains necessary throughout the various processes, including the early recognition of patients at risk of progressing to brain death in clinical practice and timely reporting to KODA.17)

In clinical practice, even when brain death is suspected, reporting may be delayed or omitted due to concerns about family reactions, the burden of explaining organ donation, a heavy workload from treatment and surgery, and discomfort with the reporting process. In such cases, delays in subsequent procedures, such as family donation discussions, may lead to deterioration in the patient’s condition and thereby complicate the brain death determination process, even if the family consents to donation, potentially leaving the family with regret after making such a difficult decision. In neurosurgical care, it is essential to promptly report patients suspected of progressing to brain death to KODA and collaborate closely to ensure that subsequent procedures, including family donation discussions, are conducted in a timely manner.

Facilitating the family approach by KODA coordinators

Another important role of medical staff is to explain the patient’s medical condition and prognosis to the family and to facilitate referral to a KODA coordinator so that a family donation discussion can be conducted appropriately. An analysis of family approaches following the reporting of PBDs from 2021 to 2025 showed that, although the number of reported PBDs increased annually, the family approach rate steadily declined from 55.2% in 2021 to 45.1% in 2025 (TABLE 9). This suggests that promoting organ donation requires not only increasing the number of reports of PBD but also supporting the subsequent process leading to family donation discussions.

For a donation discussion to proceed effectively, the family must first understand and accept the possibility that the patient may be brain-dead. Families are often confronted with the sudden and unexpected situation in which the patient has become dependent on intensive care, including mechanical ventilation, following an accident, intracranial hemorrhage, or cardiac arrest. Because the patient may appear outwardly alive, with a heartbeat and maintained body temperature, the family may find it difficult to accept the possibility of brain death. If organ donation is discussed before the family has adequately understood the patient’s condition and poor prognosis, the discussion may be misunderstood as an indication that the medical team is giving up on treatment or as an attempt to encourage organ donation. Therefore, rather than merely stating that “there is a possibility of death,” medical staff should consistently and repeatedly explain that “the patient is suspected of being brain-dead and has a very low likelihood of recovery despite ongoing active treatment.” Medical staff should also explain that “patients suspected of being brain-dead are required to be reported to the national organ procurement organization, after which a KODA coordinator may provide information and guidance regarding organ donation.” Providing these explanations in advance may help prevent the coordinator’s approach from being perceived by the family as sudden or unexpected.

The importance of this explanatory process has been emphasized in both domestic and international studies. A higher level of family understanding of brain death has been reported to be significantly associated with a higher consent rate for organ donation.32,41) This finding suggests that explanations provided by medical staff before a family donation discussion with a KODA coordinator are essential to helping families adequately understand the patient’s condition and the possibility of progression to brain death. In other words, neurosurgeons play an important role in assessing the family’s understanding of the PBD state and providing repeated explanations tailored to their level of comprehension, which are essential preceding steps that enable the family to consider organ donation.

This process must also be carefully considered in communication regarding decisions to WLST. Since the implementation of the ACT ON HOSPICE AND PALLIATIVE CARE AND DECISIONS ON LIFE-SUSTAINING TREATMENT FOR PATIENTS AT THE END OF LIFE in 2018, WLST has become part of clinical decision-making for patients with severe brain injury, including PBD, following Patient Assessment in the End-of-Life Process. According to 2019 statistics on the life-sustaining treatment decision, among 48,884 patients for whom Patient Assessment in the End-of-Life Process was completed, 2,444 patients (5%) belonged to the brain disease group without contraindications to organ transplantation.28) In addition, among PBD reported to KODA, the proportion with a preference for WLST increased annually, reaching 29.1% in 2025 (TABLE 10).

In overseas literature and guidelines, organ donation is addressed as an area to be considered as part of end-of-life care for patients with severe brain injury who have a low likelihood of recovery. This does not mean that organ donation should become the ultimate goal of treatment. Rather, it emphasizes the importance of a structured process in which the patient’s condition and prognosis are sufficiently assessed, the family understands the medical situation, and specialized organ donation personnel participate in family donation discussions. In the United Kingdom, specialist nurses for organ donation (SNOD) were involved in 97% and 90% of family approaches for patients considered for donation after brain death (DBD) and donation after circulatory death after WLST (cDCD), respectively.30) These findings indicate that the burden of conducting family donation discussions is not placed solely on individual medical staff but is shared within a planned system involving specialized personnel.

Therefore, the possibility of referring families to a KODA coordinator for a donation discussion should not be excluded solely because WLST discussions are taking place. Rather, based on sufficient explanation of the patient’s condition and prognosis, medical staff should help ensure that patients and families receive information about organ donation together with WLST discussions and have the opportunity to deliberate on donation.

CONCLUSION

Opportunities for DBD begins in the course of acute clinical care for patients with severe brain injury, particularly in emergency departments and ICUs. Despite intensive treatment, some patients may progress to irreversible brain damage and brain death. In this process, neurosurgeons play a central role in evaluating neurological changes, explaining the patient’s condition and prognosis to the family, and identifying PBD in a timely manner. Therefore, promoting brain-dead organ donation requires close collaboration between neurosurgeons and KODA so that potential donors can be reported appropriately and families can receive accurate information and sufficient time to deliberate on donation.

The role of neurosurgeons is not to obtain consent for organ donation directly, but to help families sufficiently understand the patient’s medical condition and to support appropriate referral to a KODA coordinator for a family donation discussion. The importance of this linkage has also been emphasized in recent discussions on life-sustaining treatment decisions. In future discussions on the implementation of controlled DCD in Korea, it will be important to establish clinical criteria for referring patients with severe brain injury who are not brain-dead but have a low likelihood of recovery to KODA, as well as the appropriate timing and procedural pathway for linkage to family donation discussions. In this process, the neurosurgical field can play an important role based on its expertise in neurological prognosis and clinical course.

Brain-dead organ donation is a noble process in which the last moment of one life becomes an opportunity for another. Stable collaboration between neurosurgeons and KODA will help establish organ donation not as a separate burden on medical staff, but as a process of life-sharing that takes place as an extension of clinical care by supporting families’ deliberation and informed decision-making.
