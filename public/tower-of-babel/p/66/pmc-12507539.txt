Navigating Uncertainty: Family Experiences and Coping Strategies During Critical Care Hospitalisation: A Descriptive Qualitative Study
Authors: Melissa Riegel, Monica Rückholdt, Sue Randall, Geoffrey Tofler, Victoria Whitfield, Kristy Washington, Thomas Buckley
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundFamily members of patients admitted urgently to adult intensive care or coronary care units face significant psychological stress due to the sudden and severe nature of the illness. Having a greater understanding of their stressors and coping mechanisms is essential for developing interventions to support family members during and after critical care admissions.AimThis study aimed to explore the experiences and unique challenges faced by family members following an unplanned emergency hospitalisation to an adult critical care setting and to identify the coping strategies they employed.Study DesignUsing a descriptive qualitative design, we employed inductive reflexive thematic analysis on a purposeful sample of 27 participants, whose loved ones underwent unplanned admissions to an adult intensive or coronary care unit at a tertiary referral hospital.ResultsFindings generated four themes: moving forward and coping with the early unknowns, communication contributions to family crisis, challenges and emotional struggle of supporting a loved one in hospital and navigating and redefining normal.ConclusionThis study reveals the substantial psychological stress experienced by family members of patients following unplanned admissions to intensive or coronary care units. Participants emphasised the critical importance of effective communication, noting that uncertainty and a lack of updates heightened feelings of isolation and anxiety. Findings highlight the need for healthcare professionals to enhance communication and support strategies to promote a more supportive environment for families. Prioritising these aspects can enhance the experience of family members and contribute to better outcomes for both patients and their families.Relevance to Clinical PracticeRecognising the complex dynamics of family support and effective communication can help healthcare providers better address families' needs during critical medical events.Keywords: coping skills, critical care, family, intensive care, psychological, stress

Introduction and Background

For many individuals, the onset of illness necessitating intensive or critical care hospitalisation can be very rapid. This situation often challenges family members' ability to cope and maintain hope for their loved one, given the seriousness of the illness and the uncertainty surrounding recovery. In such critical moments, hope can manifest in various forms—whether it be the hope for a treatment breakthrough, the hope of even a small improvement in the patient's condition or the hope that the healthcare team will provide compassionate and effective care [1, 2].

To navigate stressful situations, earlier theorists have proposed two primary coping strategies: emotion‐focused coping and problem‐focused coping. Emotion‐focused coping involves cognitive processes aimed at modifying emotional responses, which may include strategies such as positive reframing, acceptance, venting, rumination or avoidance. In contrast, problem‐focused coping involves active problem‐solving strategies aimed at addressing the source of the stressor and reducing its psychological impact [3]. However, certain emotional coping responses, such as rumination, avoidance, suppression of emotional expression and difficulties with situational reappraisals, can be maladaptive and are strongly associated with depression and anxiety [4]. These responses can impede individuals from considering or implementing more adaptive coping strategies such as problem‐focused coping [5].

Individuals with significant relationships to a person admitted to adult intensive care are at substantial risk of developing new or worsening symptoms of depression, anxiety and post‐traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), with these heightened psychological responses potentially persisting after the hospitalisation [6]. Some researchers have proposed the term ‘postintensive care syndrome‐family’ (PICS‐F) to encompass these responses [7]. Critical care hospitalisation is recognised as a multidimensional stressor that can elicit both immediate and prolonged psychological responses in family members [8]. Spouses, in particular, have been reported to face considerable psychological challenges, influenced by concerns about their loved one's ongoing health issues and the potential responsibilities associated with providing care [9]. These heightened psychological responses may be exacerbated by maladaptive coping mechanisms, such as suppression of emotional expression [4], which have been observed between former ICU patients and family members, contributing to misunderstandings and hindering reintegration into everyday life following hospitalisation [10]. However, some evidence indicates that family members often employ greater amounts of problem‐focused coping strategies, such as positive reframing, which are considered an adaptive coping responses and may help mitigate psychological burden [11]. Nevertheless, this does not fully explain the family's risk of developing heightened psychological responses, making it challenging to design and implement appropriate interventions.

Although efforts have been made to support family members through coping skills training following hospital discharge, such interventions have not been shown to improve symptoms of anxiety, depression or PTSD [12]. These findings suggest that current knowledge and interventions might be insufficient to support family members during and after critical care admissions. A deeper understanding of the stressors and coping strategies related to the critical care admission is essential, and gaining insights directly from family members can help identify more effective approaches to minimise their psychological burden.

Aim

This study aimed to explore the experiences and unique challenges faced by family members following an unplanned emergency hospitalisation to a critical care setting and to identify the coping strategies employed by those affected.

Design and Methods

Study Design

This study is part of a larger investigation into the psychological, behavioural and physiological effects on family members visiting their loved one during an emergency, unplanned adult intensive care unit (ICU) or coronary care unit (CCU) hospitalisation [8]. For this study, a qualitative descriptive design was utilised, as it enables a rich, contextual understanding of participants' experiences, capturing their perspectives of a phenomenon [13].

Setting and Sample

A purposeful, convenience sample of English‐speaking spouses, partners or parents of patients with an emergency, unplanned admissions to the adult ICU or CCU at a tertiary referral hospital in Sydney, Australia between January 2017 and December 2018 was invited to participate by a member of the research team within 48 h of visiting their hospitalised loved one. Potential participants were excluded if they were unable to speak or read English or if they were less than 18 years of age. From the larger study of 40 family member participants, three withdrew after the initial in‐hospital assessment [8]. Two of these participants were lost to follow‐up, and the third declined to continue in the study due to the death of their spouse following hospital discharge. This resulted in a sample of 37 participants for the second timepoint assessment at 2 weeks post‐discharge from the hospital, when the interviews were conducted. In‐person interviews were conducted with the first 27 participants of the larger study, and interviewing ceased at this number as the data obtained was deemed sufficiently rich and complex to develop meaningful insights for this study [14]. All participants who were approached consented to participate in the interviews.

Data Collection

A semi‐structured interview guide was used to guide the interviews (see Appendix A: Family members interview guide). Due to the researcher–participant interactions as part of the larger study, a relationship was established prior to data collection for this study between second author MRü and study participants through study recruitment and the initial in‐hospital timepoint assessment. Through these interactions, participants gained an understanding of MRü's professional goals as a PhD candidate conducting research in the field of preventive cardiology. All interviews were conducted by MRü, audio‐recorded and transcribed verbatim. The transcripts were verified against the original audio recordings for accuracy by first author MRi, and no transcripts or findings were returned to participants. The interviews occurred an average of 17.4 days (SD = 5.4) after hospital discharge, and no repeat interviews were performed. All interviews were conducted in the participant's home, in accordance with the study protocol, to avoid the potential stress of returning to the hospital. In three interviews, other family members were present in the background, such as the previously hospitalised partner or spouse, but they did not participate in the interview. Five of the interviews included more than one person in the interview, most commonly the spouse or partner who was previously hospitalised. However, the previous patient's contributions were not included in the data analysis, which focused solely on the responses from the family member participant. The average interview was 24 min in length (range 6–51 min).

Data Analysis

A pragmatist epistemology was adopted for this study [15], and the entire data set was analysed following Braun & Clarke's 6‐phase process of inductive reflective thematic analysis [16, 17]. Inductive reflective thematic analysis was chosen for its flexibility and ability to generate insights providing rich descriptions of the data set [16]. For phase 1 of the data analysis process, the audio recording of the data was listened to, and the transcripts were read and verified by first author MRi to support data immersion and familiarisation. In phase 2, MRi generated the initial codes from the entire data set and used NVivo to assist with data organisation [18]. During phase 3, MRi arranged the codes into initial themes according to the study's aim. In phase 4, these themes were reviewed and further developed by first author MRi, second author MRü and TB, with additional refinement in phase 5 before the final phase 6 of report writing [16, 17].

Rigour

At the time of the interviews, the second author MRü was a PhD candidate with significant experience as a female registered nurse with an interest in the impact of hospitalisation on family members' psychological and physical health and well‐being. Interviews were conducted by MRü, under the direction of TB, a male registered nurse who has extensive expertise conducting research related to bereavement and intensive care, and SR, a female registered nurse with expertise in qualitative research. MRü, TB and SR met regularly to discuss and debrief about the interviews, and a personal reflective diary was maintained by MRü. The first author MRi is a highly experienced female registered nurse in intensive care with expertise in qualitative research. The consolidated criteria for reporting qualitative research (COREQ) and Braun & Clarke's guide for the assessment of thematic analysis were utilised for reporting for this manuscript [17, 19]. See Data S1 for the COREQ checklist.

Ethical Considerations

Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the local health district's Human Research Ethics Committee (HREC/16/HAWKE/330), and it was conducted in accordance with the principles set forth in the Declaration of Helsinki. Interested eligible participants received a study information sheet and provided written informed consent prior to the interview. Investigators MRü, TB, SR and MRi did not provide direct patient care during the hospital admission to any of the patients or families interviewed in this study. During the interviews, second author MRü was attentive to participants, pausing the interview if a participant became significantly emotionally distressed. On one occasion, the interview was ended early and not restarted to prioritise the participant's well‐being. Following ethical guidelines, support was provided to all participants post‐interview as needed, including offering reassurance and referring them to their primary healthcare provider if required. All files were securely stored on a password‐protected computer, and participants' data were anonymised and assigned pseudonyms.

Findings

Study participants were predominantly female (85.2%), with an average age of 59.6 years (range 34–80 years). The majority were born in Australia (74.1%) and reported being a spouse or a partner to the hospitalised person (85.2%). Study participants reported cardiovascular conditions, such as myocardial infarction (59.3%), as the primary reasons for hospitalisation, with trauma, such as burns, being the least common (3.7%). The most frequent length of hospital stay was between 4 and 7 days (40.7%). See Table 1 for sociodemographic characteristics.

Moving Forward and Coping With the Early Unknowns

One theme generated was moving forward and coping with the early unknowns. Participants described significant physical responses to the stress experienced while their loved one was admitted to hospital:

My heart was pounding. I was shaking. I couldn't stop tapping my foot and I was crying a lot. (HOSF012)

While others described physical signs of stress related to degrees of frustration with their loved one's prior lifestyle decisions, which they believed contributed to the current hospitalisation:

And so, he's had a stroke previously. And it was only a minor. They didn't know a TIA [transient ischemic attack], they don't know. And so, I got really angry. I was angry then because it was his lifestyle, and he hasn't altered his lifestyle enough. (HOSF017)

However, a few participants described having more stoic‐like responses to the stress experienced:

But I am one to sort of bottle things up. I don't talk about things because I know I get too upset. (HOSF016)

Participants described their coping strategies for managing the initial stress to include physical activities, reliance on religious beliefs and activities that facilitated self‐distractions such as reading or shopping in order to think less about the hospitalisation.

I just kind of went around. I just kind of, yeah, I just kind of went outside. I don't know where. I went. There was a church there, (laugh) a little chapel. I didn't go into the chapel, but I did see it on the [sic]. I was gonna go there, but, yeah, I was just enjoying the sunlight really. Just, yeah just doing stuff like that. (HOSF018)

A few participants described finding ways to be actively involved in patient care which facilitated coping with the hospitalisation, such as helping with eating, easing the sense of thirst by providing interventions such as ice chips, applying lotion, being present to watch and warn about falls safety or assisting in changing their surroundings. Other participants described supporting their loved one in hospital by ensuring the household was cared for:

I think we coped; I coped alright. What we did was just, did what you need to do to make sure things were done. So that was to try and be there for (husband), but also keep the family doing with what they were needing to be doing at home. (HOSF020)

Previous experience in managing health‐related uncertainties was also described by some to have assisted in their ability to cope with the current hospitalisation, however, many participants had difficulty identifying specific coping strategies and instead described having a more passive approach and coping with the hospitalisation as the situation evolved:

I had breast cancer three years ago, and I had double mastectomy. And I think that taught me a lot. How to cope. And that you get through things. (HOSF034)

Ah, it's a matter of having to cope with it. (HOSF022)

Communication Contributions to Family Crisis

The next theme generated was communication contributions to family crisis, as both having and not having communication from a variety of sources contributed to the experiences of the hospitalisation. Communication deficiencies were described by participants to include waiting for patient updates, not being updated at all, feeling excluded and emotionally isolated, and inconsistent communication between hospital departments:

I was standing out in the hallway of the ICU bawling my eyes out, and then some nurse saw me and said come with me, and took me to the end of the hall and put me in a waiting room and left me for 20 min. Handed me a box of tissues and left me alone. And all I could hear was yelling and beeping. … And then finally after 20 min, she came back and said he's okay. He's conscious. … The doctor will come and speak to you soon. And then about 10 min later, the doctor came and a chat for me to explain what happened. (HOSF012)

Several participants described being the point of contact for other family and friends as a source of stress. This stress was related to the frequency of having to update multiple people, managing others' communication expectations, dealing with family or friends who were not perceived as supportive and being required to adopt extrovert characteristics to increase communication with others:

And then the other thing I felt really stressful was trying to manage other people that want to come and don't understand. When you say your child has a brain injury, that they need little stimulus, they don't get it. They want to come and visit regardless. And I felt rude when I used to have to say to people, even my own family, no, you can't come. You need to understand that you can't come. And there's a reason for it. We're not telling you you can't come for no reason. And that became very stressful as well. (HOSF019)

While a few participants described supressing their feelings in order to portray optimism as a way to cope with the hospitalisation and continue providing support to those around them:

My 15‐year‐old at home, he very early on, he showed a few tears, but he had said to my stepdaughter, look, I've got to be strong for mum. I was being strong for him. I think we're all trying to be strong for each other, and maybe at times we just didn't let it go. And I didn't. I had a. [sic] I had a couple of moments with (husband) where, I just, you know, couldn't help myself and had a few tears. But I thought it was important for him to always feel like, everything's okay. You're going to be fine. (HOSF006)

The perceived ability and willingness of healthcare professionals to answer participants' questions was also described to contribute to their stress experienced during hospitalisation, particularly if participants felt that important details were not fully explained:

I think one of the scariest moments, I had been signing forms for the authorities for the DSAs [digital subtraction angiography]. And they'd said, you know, it's quite a serious procedure. And one of the doctors, on an occasion just sort of slipped in that one of the, um, potential risks was death. And I was like, whoa, whoa, stop. (HOSF006)

Some participants perceived the transition from an acute level of care such as ICU, where patients' real‐time data are continuously monitored and communicated to healthcare professionals, to a less acute area, such as a hospital ward, to be abrupt. Additionally, when patients were discharged home with pending diagnostic tests or uncertainties about the causes of their acute illness, it also contributed to some participants' stress experience:

And then he went to [nursing ward] where there's almost no monitoring. Like, you press the button and ten minutes later someone would come. And I just thought, how can you go from ICU where you're being so closely monitored where they're worried that you're not completely stable to then, hardly any monitoring. (HOSF012)

However, other participants described that receiving detailed and frequent explanations and updates by healthcare professionals contributed to easing the stress of the hospitalisation and facilitating coping:

the previous nurse, she was wonderful, she was actually telling me what was going on, the drugs, what she was doing. And she was actually repeating herself, and she kept apologising, ‘Oh, look, I'm sorry I'm repeating myself’. I said, ‘no, no’. But that's good that you do repeat yourself, because I'll forget, you know. She kept me [sic], I actually felt like, even though it was quite gruesome news and whatever it was, I felt comforted by that, because I knew, you know, I had more of an understanding. (HOSF018)

Challenges and Emotional Struggle of Supporting a Loved One in Hospital

The next theme generated was challenges and emotional struggle of supporting a loved one in hospital. Many participants reported spending long hours at the hospital and described a sense of obligation to be near their loved one. A few participants also expressed the need to be present at the bedside to advocate for greater care and support for their loved one:

And to get somebody, I know, pain killers team [acute pain service]. I don't know how many times I was almost wanted to strangle somebody. Get them now. We told them yesterday, and she can't sleep second day. I said, just call them now. I don't care where they are. I mean 24 h, 48 now. She can't sleep, she's in pain, and you can't get the pain killers there. … Call them. And so, I was kind of policing everyone together. So, if I wasn't there, who would do it? (HOSF005)

Others described the stress of feeling unable to provide substantial assistance regarding their loved one's medical condition:

…I think it's always stressful seeing someone that you care about in a lot of pain and you're trying to provide comfort and you know you're in the road [in the way]. And you know that they want you to be there, I guess, to provide a [sic] support and to distract the person. Yeah. You are very much in the road. (HOSF039)

For some participants, being asked to make medical‐related decisions was described as source of stress due to fears of potentially making the wrong decision. Some described coping with these situations by utilising input from other family to help support or make the decisions:

I gave them [hospital staff], really, my son's phone. I said, look, call him. Um. He'll understand things much better than I understand. And, um, if he feels that I should know something about it, he'll call me and explain to me. Including if they need approval for some other procedure or operation. (HOSF007)

While a few participants described that believing their loved one was being cared for helped ease their stress, several others described stress related to their loved ones' critical illness, which contributing to personality changes during the hospitalisation, despite concurrently expressing an understanding of the situation:

And then the other thing that really. (pause) I found really difficult, him [son] being restrained all the time. And I know why he had to be restrained, but to see him, um, it was awful. And then from there, and then when he started to come out of it and he was so aggressive, it was like, I couldn't. Because he's not like that. It was, it was. He was so aggressive. So, so aggressive. He kicked me underneath the neck, and it was my fault. I was too close to him. I should have known better. (HOSF019)

Navigating and Redefining Normal

The final theme generated was navigating and redefining normal as participants described the aftereffects of the critical illness and coping through readjusting to lifestyle changes. For some, the hospitalisation created experiences of vulnerability, as well as highlighted a perceived necessity to learn new roles, skills or to make lifestyle changes:

…it's the thoughts of, are you going to be on your own? You know, like it's a forever thing. … And then I'd think the silly things, you know, because it's the first thing I said to him. Where are the deeds to the house? So, it's things like that, the practicality of stuff. Where do I look for this, or where do I go for that? So, yeah, and that's when, you know, you get a bit teary then. (HOSF024)

Many participants described ongoing stressors after hospitalisation related to concerns about the future. For some, the uncertainty of the medical emergency's cause or the need for lifestyle changes to prevent future recurrences contributed to their stress:

He's had some readmissions, so that's been stressful. And, um. … And he has been very much acting like nothing's happened in terms of him being admitted to hospital, everything's okay. (HOSF039)

Some participants also described having reduced resilience and emotional capacity to manage concerns that might have been mundane before the hospitalisation experience:

And I said well, I've just had a whole hour of stressing that I'd locked you out and I couldn't leave Pappy because he needs me with him. … So anyway, she [daughter] said it's all right, Mum. Don't worry about it. So, then we went [sic] and we got the medication. And then we went [sic] and we had a cup of tea. And I calmed down. But I was really stressed, and it was really silly, but it was unusual for me to get so uptight about something like that. (HOSF014)

To cope with the change and uncertainties caused by the hospitalisation, most described reliance on friends and family to assist with both emotional and instrumental supports. However, for some participants, also needing to provide care and support for other family members was described as increasing their stress:

I was extremely well supported, um. After (daughter) had to go back. My stepdaughter to the US [United States of America]. My mom came here. Um. My network around was providing meals. I had people offering help. … And so, my mother‐in‐law, in particular, she's 90 [years old]. And she was, she was absolutely beside herself, hysterical. She lives on her own. She wasn't coping. So, I had then had [sic] to set up a whole network of support. So, I had my mom calling her. I had some of my close friends, one friend … to go over and read the Bible and say some prayers with her. … I had to help her cope. I had to ring her every day. I was just exhausted. And I had to call her to check in because, she wasn't eating, and she wasn't sleeping. … So, I had to set up around my mother‐in‐law, this whole network. (HOSF006)

But for many, proximity of family and friends impacted their perception of available supports to assist in coping with the hospitalisation:

Our daughter. She's supporting us, because she just lives around, not very far away. I mean, it's hard for our other daughter in Canada, because she couldn't come over, and she can't. So therefore, you almost feel that you need to be supporting her as well. (HOSF033)

Discussion

The aim of this study was to explore the experiences and unique challenges faced by family members following an unplanned emergency hospitalisation to a critical care setting as well as to identify the coping strategies utilised by those affected. Findings generated four themes: moving forward and coping with the early unknowns, communication contributions to family crisis, challenges and emotional struggle of supporting a loved one in hospital and navigating and redefining normal.

Many family members expressed that they learned how to cope with this unplanned hospitalisation from their prior knowledge and experiences with aging and health issues, but described it as a more passive approach, managed as the situation evolved. For example, waiting for medical updates was as a significant source of their stress, such as when a loved one was in surgery longer than anticipated or became haemodynamically unstable in the ICU. This often resulted in family members being removed from the bedside and placed in a waiting room, exacerbating their anxiety. The concept of “waiting” has been explored in the context of healthcare, where individuals engage in various strategies to cope with the waiting period. This includes seeking information, communicating with healthcare providers and managing their own emotional responses [20]. Critical care communication interventions commonly report formal family meetings and incorporating families into ICU medical rounds, and less frequently, the use of a communication navigator [21]. One systematic review emphasised the importance of family communication support in preventing or reducing family members' psychological burden, noting that family conferences involving a physician and proactive participation from a nurse were associated with lower levels of anxiety, although it did not reduce anxiety prevalence [22]. Additionally, improved communication with family members has been linked with higher family‐perceived quality of care [23]. This suggests that while certain communication interventions may effectively reduce anxiety, they do not necessarily address all aspects of family members' psychological well‐being, highlighting the need for a multifaceted approach to family support in critical care settings.

Findings in our study highlight that family members experience substantial stress related to communication during critical micro‐moments and changes in a loved one's health condition, indicating that pre‐arranged family meetings may not always be the best solution. The communication navigator, a role described to facilitate structured meetings with scheduled times varying from daily to weekdays, has the potential to facilitate communication [24]. However, this individual may not always be available to support family members during urgent micro‐moments of communication needs, which might further help explain its reported limited impact on reducing family members' post‐ICU psychological stress [24, 25, 26].

Alternatively, others have advocated for unrestricted ICU family visitation, including during invasive procedures or medical emergencies such as cardiopulmonary resuscitation [27, 28]. When supported by a healthcare professional, this approach may address many of the family members' communication needs and reduce stress, as well as potential feelings of isolation and loneliness caused by uncertainties while waiting in areas away from the bedside, such as in waiting rooms.

Family members with responsibilities for younger children and cohabitating young adult children described experiencing an additional layer of stress beyond the hospitalisation of their spouse or partner, a phenomenon also reported in partners and spouses following bereavement in ICU [29]. The exchange of support between family members is reportedly influenced by several factors, including life stages, marital status, family structure (such as stepfamilies) and the quality of the parent–child relationship [30, 31]. Family members in this study were primarily spouses or partners to the person hospitalised, and many described having social supports available to assist with the stress of the hospitalisation, which often included midlife‐aged children. Family members commonly support each other for altruistic reasons based on cultural norms and informal family rules that dictate intergenerational obligations [30], and adult children often provide support and companionship for their older parents [32, 33]. The reliance on ongoing support from other family members was described in this study to depend on proximity to the family home, as well as their child's lifestyle and personal family commitments. Consequently, family members' stress from assuming or learning new roles, such as managing home repairs or driving a car, might have been reduced if their midlife‐aged child cohabitated, lived in closer proximity or had perceived fewer personal family obligations, allowing them to provide more instrumental support to their parents.

Interestingly, some family members in our study described needing to continue providing considerable amounts of emotional support to their young‐adult and midlife‐aged children who lived further away and could not visit regularly. Others have reported that older adults providing emotional support to their adult children can experience improvements in their own well‐being [32, 34]. Our findings suggest that while older age and experience might enhance coping responses and minimise psychological stress, the relationship between an older parent and their adult child likely has a significant impact on the older parent's experiences and stressors regarding the unplanned hospitalisation.

Despite the positive aspects of coping gained from social support, our study found that acting as the primary communicator of information heightened stress among family members. Many felt compelled to project an image of ‘strength’ by suppressing their own emotions, which contributed to their experience of stress. This need to maintain a façade of ‘being strong’ for others often leads to inauthentic emotional suppression rather than using other coping mechanisms, such as venting [35]. Similar findings have been reported by others regarding ICU family members who struggle to communicate illness details with others, and family members with anxiety often resort to suppression during their communication [36]. This suppression can lead to rumination, prolonged periods of negative emotions, and symptoms of depression [35]. However, a recent scoping review reported that ICU nurses proactively participating in family meetings and rephrasing medical information for family members can enhance their understanding, reduce their anxiety and depression, and improve their effectiveness in relaying information to other family members [21, 37]. Further research is needed to determine whether the most stressful aspects of family members communicating information relate to the number of individuals to involved, the nature of their relationships, the management of others' emotional responses or if education and skills training on the risk of suppression could improve their communication and reduce the likelihood of negative psychological outcomes.

Strengths and Limitations

The strength of this study includes successful recruitment of family members following an unplanned emergency admission of their family member to a critical care setting, during a time of significant stress. Although providing unique insights into family members' experiences, this study was conducted at one tertiary referral hospital, so findings might not be transferrable to other critical care areas. Recruitment relied on family members visiting the critical care areas and agreeing to participate, which might have introduced selection bias, and their experiences might differ from those who were unable to visit or chose not to participate. However, findings include quotations and contextual details about the participants, enabling readers to reflect on how this study's insights might apply to other contexts through transferability and naturalistic generalisation methods [38].

Recommendations for Practice and Further Research

Recognising the intricate dynamics of family support, including factors such as age, relationship and living proximity, along with the essential role of effective communication, can help healthcare professionals can better address the needs of families during critical medical events. Increased opportunities for unrestricted family visitation may help reduce stress during critical micro‐moments, especially those caused by uncertainty while waiting away from the bedside, such as in waiting rooms. Future research should focus on enhancing communication and support for family members, with the aim of reducing stress and improving their overall well‐being.

Conclusion

This study provides valuable insights into the complex experiences of family members during the sudden hospitalisation of a loved one. Participants emphasised the critical role of communication, noting that uncertainty during critical micro‐moments and a lack of updates from healthcare professionals heightened feelings of isolation and anxiety. The emotional toll of supporting a loved one in critical care was significant, with many participants feeling a heavy sense of responsibility for both their own well‐being and that of other family members. This burden was often intensified by the pressure to project strength, which led to emotional suppression and potential long‐term psychological effects. The study also revealed that families must navigate changes in roles and expectations post‐hospitalisation, while continuing to confront ongoing uncertainty.
