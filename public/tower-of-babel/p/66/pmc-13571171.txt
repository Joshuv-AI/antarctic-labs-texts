Long‐Term Discharge Records from United States and European Karst Springs: Data Limitations in Trend Analysis
Authors: Laura Toran, Teresa Baraza, Kübra Özdemir Çallı, Carly R. Finegan, Elizabeth A. Hasenmueller, Jonathan Nyquist, Shishir K Sarker, Patricia Spellman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractA cross‐site comparison of 49 springs from the United States and Europe with more than a 30‐year record of spring discharge was conducted to look for response to climate change (temperature and precipitation). The dataset included both carbonate and non‐carbonate springs across urban and rural settings, covering a wide range of discharge (0.001 to 25 m3/s) and discharge variability (45 to 6500% variability index) to encompass aquifers with a variety of hydrologic regimes and degrees of karstification. Relatively small changes in discharge trends (i.e., change in discharge over the long‐term time record) and similar responses across the diverse spring dataset were observed, which suggested that groundwater storage buffers hydrologic stresses in these aquifers despite varying degrees of karstification. Although decreases in discharge were associated with an observed increase in air temperature, larger air temperature trends did not lead to larger discharge trends. Spring discharge trends over time could not be categorized by spring behavior as measured by mean discharge, variability in discharge, or rural vs. urban setting. The detection of trends was sensitive to the time period being considered, with selected time periods yielding different and sometimes opposite discharge trends, highlighting the importance of time period selection when making cross‐site comparisons. This study benefited from cross‐site comparison by finding similarities in spring discharge trends across varied karst regimes.

Introduction

Karst landscapes contain open channels, conduits, and fractures (Atkinson 1977; White 1988) that facilitate the rapid flux of solids, solutes, and gasses into and through the subsurface such that these landscapes may be more vulnerable to climate and land use changes than siliciclastic counterparts (Hartmann et al. 2014; Covington et al. 2023; Zhang et al. 2023; Leins et al. 2025). Karst springs are often characterized by their discharge variability (Meinzer 1923; Bakalowicz 2005; Goldscheider and Drew 2014). However, the inherent heterogeneity of karst aquifers due to conduit development, diverse sources of recharge such as direct inputs from sinkholes and diffuse input, and transient capture areas for springs (Worthington 2009; White 2017; Çallı et al. 2025) present challenges to understanding drivers of response to stresses.

Nonetheless, karst aquifers also have a storage component, which can be estimated through hydrograph recession analysis where the time component of slope changes is evaluated (Mitrofan et al. 2015; Fatoni et al. 2024), by calculating memory using autocorrelation (Kovács 2021), using hysteresis loops (Ashjari et al. 2025), and by tracer tests (Ryan and Meiman 1996; Worthington et al. 2000). Higher primary porosity characteristic of some eogenetic karst could allow for more groundwater storage in the carbonate bedrock matrix (Vacher and Mylroie 2002), which was corroborated by Florea and Vacher's (2006) observation of higher normalized peak discharge in telogenetic karst springs and higher aquifer inertia (due to the higher storage) in eogenetic karst springs of Florida. By examining the long‐term behavior of spring discharge, the role of rapid flow paths vs. storage can be evaluated.

Furthermore, limited data across karst landscapes, particularly cross‐site comparisons, has made it difficult to develop unifying conceptual models. Knowledge integration through cross‐site comparisons enhances the ability to make decisions about water resources (Van Beynen and Townsend 2005; Musgrove et al. 2016; Ficco and Sasowsky 2018) as climate and population changes stress water resources. However, most cross‐site comparisons are regional in nature (e.g., Pennsylvania's Valley and Ridge Province in Shuster and White 1971; the Pyrénées in Labat et al. 2000a, and Labat et al. 2000b; Texas in Scanlon et al. 2003, Southern Italy in Fiorillo 2009; and Florida in Work 2020). Florea and Vacher (2006) was an exception with a study of 59 karst spring sites across North America, Europe, and Africa. They found that spring discharge patterns differ between eogenetic and telogenetic karst and hypothesized that springs in eogenetic karst exhibit more subdued responses due to the higher primary porosity resulting from limited burial and compaction. However, Florea and Vacher (2006) compared datasets varying from 1 year to 82 years in length. Ashjari et al. (2025) compared 42 springs in the United States, Europe, and China for up to 7 flood events with both specific conductivity and discharge data to evaluate sources of stored water. Other researchers also rely on truncated datasets of only a year or even a few months, although a few long‐term regional studies have been published (e.g., almost 70 years of sub‐hourly to hourly data in Fan et al. 2023; ~100 years of daily data in Fiorillo et al. 2021; up to 120 years of daily data in Fiorillo and Guadagno 2012; and almost 50 years of daily to weekly data in De Vita et al. 2012). These studies typically focused on five or fewer springs within the same region.

To facilitate easier comparisons among karst regions, Olarinoye et al. (2020) compiled the World Karst Spring (WoKaS) Hydrograph database, which is a repository for long‐term spring discharge. The WoKaS database identified karst springs and collected spring discharge data from across the globe, creating an accessible repository for acquiring and utilizing spring data for comparative studies. Giese et al. (2025) compared long‐term karst spring discharge data from the WoKaS database in four European countries over 20‐year (54 springs) and 40‐year (22 springs) periods. They attempted to detect signals of climate change in their spring discharge data using monthly data, although most springs saw no significant change. The authors attributed the decreasing discharge trends they saw in a limited number of European karst spring systems to increasing temperature (e.g., via evapotranspiration at higher temperature leading to decreased recharge and flow), while precipitation trends were not predictive.

In this study, we examined long‐term (≥30 years) records of spring discharge, temperature, and precipitation to see how karst springs respond to climate trends. By comparing karst springs with non‐carbonate springs, a wide variety of discharge rates and variability, two continents, and both urban and rural springs, the aim was to identify which of these factors were drivers of change in spring discharge response. Another goal was to evaluate how data limitations can affect results. Specifically, we compared trend analyses across different time periods and measurement frequencies rather than assuming that mixing data of variable lengths and measurement intervals (to extend the number of springs in the dataset) would produce consistent results. In addition, we examined whether discharge variability decreased or increased over time for a measure of storm response to climate trends. While researchers are often encouraged to highlight the broader relevance of their work, making meaningful cross‐site comparisons in karst remains a significant challenge. Despite the challenges associated with cross‐site comparisons, the ubiquity of karst landscapes demonstrates their global importance as water resources (Stevanović 2019; Goldscheider et al. 2020) and, therefore, the need for more of these types of studies.

Materials and Methods

Data Acquisition

For long‐term karst spring discharge trends and variability, the data retrieval criteria were set to require a minimum of 30 years of daily discharge records. The search for springs meeting this threshold included global, national, and local databases. The WoKaS hydrograph database, a global compilation of spring data (Olarinoye et al. 2020), was the first source. At least 20 springs were desired for a large enough data set to compare trend data within a continent. Only springs in North America (all of which were in the United States) and Europe had a sufficient number of springs with more than 30 years of coverage. Few springs outside of Europe or the United States had daily data in the WoKaS database as shown in Figure S1.

Next, additional springs were obtained from national databases, including the U.S. Geological Survey's (USGS) National Water Information System (NWIS) database (USGS 2024), Austria's eHYD database (eHYD 2024), and the United Kingdom's National River Flow Archive (NRFA; UK NRFA 2024), and a local database for the St. Johns River Water Management District in Florida, USA (St. Johns River Water Management District 2024). However, sub‐daily (e.g., hourly) timesteps were not available in the WoKaS database. Hourly data were available for a limited number of springs in the United States from the USGS's database (nine springs for a shorter time period of 15 years). A further constraint in identifying spring datasets for use in the study was that some springs were categorized by their relevant monitoring agencies as “river” or “stream” gauging locations (e.g., for Royal Spring, Kentucky, USA, and Comal Spring, Texas, USA). Some springs were eliminated after checking whether these gauges were monitoring springs using a combination of personal knowledge, confirmation with the monitoring agencies, and comparison with satellite imagery to identify locations on rivers that would receive discharge from other sources.

Long‐term discharge data for the springs were downloaded from their respective data sources. Discharge data for the European springs were obtained from the open‐source website figshare.com provided in Olarinoye et al. 2020 as a downloadable zip file for the WoKaS database. Rather than using WoKaS for the listed U.S. springs, the original USGS NWIS source was used. These springs were supplemented with additional U.S. springs in the NWIS database, and data were retrieved using the dataRetrieval package in R, which provides direct access to the NWIS database (USGS 2024). The remaining springs were downloaded from their respective online databases listed previously. Discharge rate was used as a proxy for spring size due to inconsistencies in the availability and methodologies for estimating catchment size. Land use was classified for each spring site using the Global Urban Heat Island (UHI) Data Set (CIESIN 2016). Specifically, springs located within 305 m of an urban heat island were categorized as urban, while springs outside of that range were categorized as rural. To confirm these classifications, the ESRI Sentinel‐2 10‐m Land Use Land Cover (LULC) map was referenced, which showed that the spring land use categories were consistent with local land use.

Even when spring sites met the length and timestep requirements, plotting data from each spring showed that the data records frequently had gaps (e.g., Alley Spring, Missouri, USA), changing baselines (e.g., Bistricka Spring, Slovenia), or site‐specific factors that impacted the data quality (Figure S2). For example, Sulfur Spring (Florida, USA) features an abrupt drop in discharge that resulted from pumping activities (Scharping et al. 2018). Also, Stansted Springs (United Kingdom) had changes in data pre‐processing steps that made the data look flashier for a period of time. Despite some springs having large gaps in data, most of the springs had daily data for at least 85% of the total monitoring period (36 of 49 springs). However, some springs had as low as 15% coverage while still providing records encompassing more than 30 years of data. Plotting each individual spring and assessing the springs for data issues was necessary to avoid erroneously including springs in the analysis, highlighting the importance of the manual review of each individual spring discharge record (Figure S2). These data issues are summarized in Table S1 (along with more detailed spring location information), but springs with sufficient length for their discharge records were still included to assess trends over time due to the already limited number of qualifying springs.

In all, data were tabulated for 49 springs with at least 30 years of daily discharge data, including 42 carbonate springs (with 17 located in the United States and 25 located in Europe) and 7 non‐carbonate springs (all located in the United States; Table 1; Figure 1). More springs in Europe had long‐term data than the United States. Average discharges among the spring sites ranged from 0.001 to 25 m3/s.

To analyze long‐term trends in precipitation and temperature at each spring location, climate data were obtained from the Global Data Land Assimilation System (GLDAS; Beaudoing and Rodell 2020). GLDAS integrates satellite and observation data to simulate three land surface models, providing various land surface states and fluxes, including precipitation and temperature (e.g., Rodell et al. 2004). This data offers robust spatial and temporal coverage for the study sites and allows standardization of the climate data used in the analysis. Using Google Earth Engine (GEE), precipitation and temperature data were downloaded from 1948 to 2024 at each spring location. Coordinates for each spring were uploaded to the GEE interface, and time series for precipitation and temperature were downloaded. Climate data were aggregated to monthly means using the GEE script as daily data from GLDAS has poorer correlation in some regions with daily observed precipitation (Muche et al. 2020), while monthly data captures the overall statistics of the long‐term data. Furthermore, additional analysis presented in the results showed monthly and annual trends were comparable. Thus, when comparing climate and spring discharge trends, spring discharge data were aggregated to an annual mean, and the time period was clipped to the available records for precipitation and temperature to ensure uniform comparison of time periods and data frequency.

Time Periods

The time intervals over which spring discharge datasets were available had differences in the lengths of the discharge records (e.g., a 75‐year dataset vs. a 30‐year dataset). Time periods for discharge also needed to overlap with climate data to provide meaningful comparisons with precipitation and temperature data. To find overlapping time periods, a narrower set of springs was compared. However, not all springs had complete data for these intervals due to gaps and different start and end dates in the datasets. The time interval with the most overlapping data from the initially selected 49 springs was 1977 to 2014 (38 years). For this period 33 springs had available data, although two were eliminated due to significant changes in trends during this period, reducing the number to 31 springs. This set of 31 springs included 13 from the United States (10 carbonate springs and 3 non‐carbonate springs) and 18 from Europe. This overlapping data period was also split into two shorter time periods of roughly 20 years (1977 to 1997 and 1995 to 2017) for a separate comparison of early and late data in the 1977 to 2014 period. The period overlapped slightly to ensure that at least 24 springs had data available for both the beginning and end of each time period for analysis. Comparing these time periods enabled evaluation of how dataset length can affect the results. A Python script allowed the user to select specific intervals so that trends could be compared over both shorter and longer periods.

Discharge and Temporal Trend Analysis

Discharge and climate trends were calculated initially using linear regression over specified monitoring periods. The change in discharge rate (reported as cubic meters per second or m3/s) per day was multiplied by 365 days to obtain an annual rate of change; climate trends were also reported as an annual rate of change. Discharge trends were calculated for the longest time period available (Table 1) and selected time intervals discussed in the previous section. For data used in climate comparisons, the normality assumption for linear regression was tested by evaluating quartile‐quartile plots of model residuals and by comparing trends with a non‐parametric analysis (i.e., a modified Mann‐Kendall with Sen's slope). Discharge records were also used to calculate the average discharge values.

Discharge trends and climate trends were calculated using the same frequencies and time periods. Most discharge data were available at daily resolution and aggregated to monthly and annual means to compare trends across frequencies. A subset of nine U.S. springs had hourly data, which were used to compare trends calculated with daily data. Three springs in Germany had weekly data for the initial portion of the monitoring period, which was aggregated to monthly and annual data then concatenated with daily aggregations. Trends based on daily data were used for cross‐continent discharge comparisons, whereas trends based on annual data were used for comparisons with climate trends. At annual resolution, storm response‐related discharge outliers were smoothed, making linear regression trend estimates comparable to those obtained using more robust methods such as Sen's slope. Because monthly and annual trend estimates were similar, seasonal trend analyses were considered unnecessary.

Inflection points were identified in the long‐term datasets to determine changes in trends and further evaluate the suitability of applying linear regressions to annual data. Inflection points in daily data with at least 85% coverage (n = 36) were calculated using the R script “Segmented” package (Muggeo 2008). Inflection points were estimated by placing a breakpoint at the dataset's median date, then iteratively fitting linear regressions to find the best‐fit segmented lines. After determining the optimal inflection point, trends before and after this point were calculated and compared, and the date of the inflection point was recorded. The method identifies an inflection point regardless of significance.

Discharge Variability Index

To compare discharge variability across the sites, the springs were classified based on their discharge characteristics using the discharge variability index (VI) percentage. In addition to serving as a site characteristic, discharge variability provides an indicator of hydrologic response to climatic forcing, particularly through short‐term responses to precipitation events. VI was calculated following Meinzer (1923): (1) VI=Qmax−QminQavg×100% where Q max, Q min, and Q avg are the maximum, minimum, and average discharge values (in m3/s) for the selected spring discharge dataset, respectively. According to Meinzer's classification, springs with a VI < 25% are “constant,” springs with VI = 25 to 100% are “intermediate,” and springs with VI > 100% are “variable.” However, daily discharge data were used in the calculations for this study, while Meinzer's work was based on shorter datasets without high data resolution, which limited peak flow detection. With modern, continuous datasets, VI values as high as 6500% were observed using daily discharge data. Based on breakpoints in the distribution of the VI values, the Meinzer “variable” category was further subdivided into “variable” (VI = 100 to 500%), “highly variable” (VI = 500 to 1000%), and “extremely variable” (VI > 1000%).

Another measure of discharge variability is the flashiness indicator (or flashiness index; FI; Florea and Vacher 2006), calculated as follows: (2) FI=QmaxQmean The breakpoints for both FI and VI were similar, supporting the validity of the new categories. Plots of Q max vs. Q mean illustrate this ratio as the slope. In this analysis, VI was calculated both for the entire dataset and for each individual year to assess potential changes in variability over time

Results

Discharge Variability Index (VI) and Temporal Trends in Discharge Variability

Discharge VI calculations and FI Q max/Q mean showed the springs fell into four categories (Table 1): extremely variable (VI > 1000%), highly variable (VI = 500 to 1000%), variable (VI = 100 to 500%), and intermediate (VI = 25 to 100%). Only the non‐carbonate springs had intermediate variability (VI < 100%). These categories were confirmed by a plot of FI (Q max vs. Q mean), which showed R2 of 0.84, 0.999, and 0.63 for the variable, highly variable, and extremely variable springs (Figure S3). There were not enough springs within the intermediate variability category to perform a regression analysis.

Most springs (44 out of 49) did not show a significant change in the annual VI over time (Table S2). Only five of the springs showed significant VI trends over time (p < 0.01) with R2 between 0.29 and 0.45. This trend was increasing for one spring (Stansted, 17% per year), but trends were much less pronounced for the other four springs, with two springs just under 1% per year and two non‐carbonate springs less than 0.5% per year. Stansted had an abrupt shift in variability, which may have been related to a change in averaging frequency. For the springs with significant VI trends, two of the springs were in Europe and three were non‐carbonate springs in the United States. None of the carbonate springs in the United States showed a trend in variability over time. The five springs with significant VI trends over time do not share a common discharge variability, ranging from the highest to the lowest observed variability categories. They include both constant and decreasing discharge trends over time, one of the two lowest mean discharge rates, and the highest discharge rate of any non‐carbonate spring. All but one is a rural spring; however, rural springs are more common in the dataset.

Comparison of Data Frequencies and Time Periods

The discharge trends were compared for different data frequencies and time periods to evaluate whether these factors change the interpretation of spring discharge trends over time. This analysis was conducted to ensure that observed changes in discharge over time were not due to bias introduced by comparing datasets that do not have the same time coverage or collection frequency.

Daily and annual frequencies produced similar discharge trends (Figure 2). These discharge trends fall close to a 1:1 line with little offset, which provided justification to use annual trends for comparison with climate data. The annual data had better R2 (Table S3) because there are fewer outliers. Hourly data were available for shorter periods (ranging between 1991 and 2023) but showed similar discharge trends to daily data for all but one spring in the United States (Big Spring, Missouri; Figure S4). This spring is the largest of the U.S. springs but not the largest spring in the full dataset. Because daily, monthly, and annual trends are equivalent, annual trends were used when comparing to climate to reduce variability in the long‐term trends from storm events. Furthermore, using high‐frequency data can make a trend significant based on the p values, even as the range of trends based on the confidence interval is large. Daily discharge data were used when considering discharge trends alone and for the VI calculations.

Comparison of 1977 to 2014 dataset for daily and annual aggregation to calculate change in discharge trends shows similar trends.

Temperature data were not strongly affected by data collection frequency either. Temperature data from GLDAS also showed a strong correlation (R2 = 0.998) between monthly and annual trends for the 1977 to 2014 period (Figure S5). The trend was close to the 1:1 line, but with a temperature offset of 0.004°C/year. Thus, the monthly and annual trends were comparable.

The same time period needs to be used when comparing spring discharge behavior because the time period selected can affect trends, based on deviations from R2 = 1. The discharge trends were not the same for two time periods within the longest consistent period of record (1977 to 1997 and 1995 to 2017). In particular, two springs had a significant change in trend, and thus were considered outliers: Silver Spring in the U.S. and Unica Springs in Europe. Even excluding these two springs, the two 20‐year time periods produced a weak relationship with R2 = 0.56 and a slope of only 0.63 instead of the expected 1.0 (Figure 3), showing disparity in results for two time periods. A comparison of the 1977 to 2014 period with the longest time period for each spring produced a relationship with R2 = 0.72 and slope of 0.69 (Figure S1) with the same two springs removed as outliers plus one additional spring. Without conducting the time period comparison, the outlier springs would not have been identified. Most of the springs had the same direction for discharge trends (either increasing or decreasing) over different time periods, but Unica Spring changed directions from a negative trend to a positive trend for the 20‐year time period comparison (Figure 3). Given that the trends were not the same for selected time periods and the outliers showed that some springs can have large differences in their discharge trends depending on the time period, matching time periods for all springs were used when comparing to climate data.

Comparison of change in discharge trends for two ~20‐year time periods: 1977‐1997 and 1995‐2017. Trend line calculated without the labeled outliers.

The period used for comparison with temperature and precipitation data was 1977 to 2014, and these springs were evaluated for normality in residuals and their linear regression trends were compared with modified Mann‐Kendall and Sen's slope, which are non‐parametric techniques. The quartile‐quartile plots of residuals showed straight slopes and the linear regression closely matched Sen's slope for trends that were deemed significant (Figure S7). The similarity between linear regression and Sen's slope estimates was also noted by Fiorillo et al. (2021).

Discharge Trends

The discharge trends of the U.S. carbonate springs were distinct from the European springs in that there were more springs with trends near zero or showing negative trends for Europe and more springs with a positive trend in the U.S. carbonate springs (Figure 4a). There were not as many springs in the U.S. non‐carbonate set, but they also had near zero or negative trends. Half of the rural springs showed constant or nearly constant discharge trends, whereas the urban springs were more spread out in values (Figure 4b). These patterns applied to both the data comparing springs across the longest time period available (blue dots in Figure 4) and the overlapping time period from 1977 to 2014 (orange dots in Figure 4). However, for the shorter time period of 1977 to 2014, there were two springs with positive discharge trends in Europe, and the discharge trend for Silver Spring in the United States increased from −0.09 to −0.27 m3/s per year.

Swarm plots to show the distribution of discharge trends for each spring type, including (a) Europe, U.S. carbonate, and U.S. non‐carbonate springs and (b) rural vs. urban springs. Two time periods are included in the swarm plot: the total discharge record (longest time period, blue dots) and the truncated period (1977 to 2014, orange dots).

Discharge showed small changes over time. The mean absolute value of the discharge trend was 0.02 m3/s per year, and the median was 0.01 m3/s per year for the 38‐year period with the most overlapping data. The range in discharge trend values for the longest time period was from 0.02 to −0.05 m3/s per year with one outlier at −0.09 m3/s per year in the U.S. carbonate springs, whereas the European spring discharge trends ranged from 0.002 to −0.05 m3/s per year. The ranges for rural and urban springs overlapped, except for the outliers. Silver Spring is the spring with the largest discharge trend (−0.09 m3/s per year), and it discharges from a confined aquifer in Florida. Increased pumping in northern Florida has led to declines in this aquifer, which is exacerbated in part by variable aquifer confinement for the recharge area to the spring (Shoemaker et al. 2004).

Discharge variability as measured by VI was not a predictor of discharge trends (Figure 5). Discharge trend values overlapped for all of the variability classes except intermediate variability, which only had three points and the outlier lowest trend in the variable class. Variable to extremely variable springs each had discharge trends around 0.02 m3/s per year as well as trends in the −0.004 range. Thus, there was overlap in discharge trends for each variability class. Similarly, the size of the spring as measured by mean discharge did not predict variability. There are similar discharge trends for high and low mean discharge, and there are both increasing and decreasing trends for the same mean discharge (Figure 6).

The inflection points that were used to evaluate changes in discharge trends did not occur in the same year, even for springs in the same region (Figure S8, Table S4). Inflections, particularly short‐term trends at the beginning or end of the dataset, were evaluated when considering long‐term trends because they can steepen the overall trend. Most of the springs had inflection points outside the 1977 to 2014 period for evaluating climate effects, the springs did not have a significant change in trend, or the R2 for the trend was too low for the trend to be used to compare with climate data. Only two of these springs had trend changes that deviated from their overall trends (Ombla and Mammoth), and these two springs were eliminated from the comparison with climate data.

Comparison between Discharge and Climate Trends (Temperature and Precipitation)

Comparing annual discharge trends with annual air temperature trends showed that, for most springs, warmer temperatures also corresponded to decreased discharge, but the relationship included springs with little change in discharge. Furthermore, higher temperature trends did not lead to higher discharge trends (Figure 7a, which only includes trends with R2 > 0.2). The dataset adding trends with R2 < 0.2 included 11 springs that had an increase in air temperature but nearly constant discharge and three springs with increased discharge and air temperature trends (Figure 7b). The outlier with a negative discharge trend is Silver Spring, which is a confined aquifer with increased pumping as mentioned previously. The 95% confidence intervals of the discharge and air temperature trends showed that the European springs with R2 > 0.2 had little or no change in discharge, and both United States and European springs had large uncertainties in the temperature trends (Figure S9).

Relationship between air temperature trends and discharge trends over time using annual aggregation. In (a), only discharge trends with R2 > 0.2 are shown, while (b) shows all data (1977‐2014). Although decreasing discharge trends were associated with increasing temperature trends, higher temperature trends did not predict higher discharge trends.

The relationships between precipitation and discharge trends were evaluated to see if they could explain cases when the air temperature and spring discharge trends were not inversely correlated as expected because an increase in precipitation could potentially offset warmer air temperatures (Figure S10). For the three springs with a positive discharge trend in the 1977 to 2014 dataset, the two U.S. springs have a negative precipitation trend, so increased precipitation does not explain the increasing discharge trend. Furthermore, the precipitation changes are small (<1 mm/year), and the amount of precipitation does not predict the degree of discharge trend (Figure S10). Additional factors besides lower temperature or increased precipitation are needed to explain the positive discharge trends in both the United States and Europe.

Discussion

Observed Trends in Spring Discharge

Although discharge variability is commonly used to characterize karst springs, it did not predict their long‐term discharge responses in this study. The concept tested was that highly variable springs might be more susceptible to climate change or other anthropogenic alterations because of fast flow paths (Hartmann et al. 2014; Fiorillo et al. 2015; Xanke and Liesch 2022). Discharge variability is just one of several indicators of the component of fast flow in karst aquifers, but it has been linked to void development and shorter memory (Fatoni et al. 2024). This study found that trends in discharge (either increasing or decreasing) were not observed when sites were grouped by discharge variability or mean discharge despite the distributed, long‐term spring discharge records (Figures 6 and 7).

This overlap in discharge trends for springs across two continents with varying discharge suggested that even springs with fast flow paths may have sufficient contributions of discharge from storage to buffer climate trends, much like non‐carbonate springs. The generally small trend in discharge trends over time (Figure 4) further suggested that the degree of karstification does not impair the ability of groundwater storage to buffer hydrologic stresses. The spring with the largest change in discharge, Silver, was in a confined aquifer with lower storage, supporting this premise. Similarly, Giese et al. (2025) observed increasing and decreasing discharge trends across a range of values for memory, which is a measure of storage in karst aquifers. Worthington et al. (2000) estimated over 95% of storage in four karst aquifers was in the rock matrix, and storage has long been recognized as an important component of karst transport models (Birk et al. 2006; Çallı et al. 2025).

This comparison of United States and European springs revealed different discharge trends between the continents, but the distinction was not correlated with air temperature trends. A relationship between air temperature and discharge trends has been observed before in European springs (Fiorillo et al. 2021; Giese et al. 2025) and was related to increased evapotranspiration. However, in this study, large air temperature trends did not lead to larger discharge trends. Although Europe had larger air temperature trends over time than the United States, spring discharge trends overlapped for the two continents, particularly for U.S. non‐carbonate springs (Figures 5 and 8). The positive discharge trends in the United States were not associated with increased precipitation. Bonacci (2001) speculated that conduit constructions can reduce the potential for increased precipitation to impact discharge rates. Fan et al. (2023) saw trends in peak discharge were affected by precipitation in certain seasons, but not annual discharge. Brookfield et al. (2017) modeled the effect of cool weather precipitation on dampening air temperature effects, which suggests that the timing of precipitation can be a factor in karst aquifer response to air temperature changes.

Map of discharge trend patterns for the long‐term dataset.

Land use can impact spring discharge by reducing recharge in the case of urbanization (Sharp Jr et al. 2009; Toran et al. 2009; Bittner et al. 2018), but the expected effect was not observed in this study. What is considered here is not land use change, but the comparison of two land use types: a land use with potentially less recharge (urban) and a land use with unrestricted recharge (rural). Thus, we tested whether restricting the recharge could make a spring less sensitive to changes in climate. In this study, urban springs showed more change in discharge over time, rather than less, compared to rural springs, where more of the rural springs showed nearly constant discharge (Figure 4). Although land use change can also be a factor in decreasing discharge trends (Zhang et al. 2023), only a small number of the studied springs showed a change in discharge trend for selected time periods, suggesting that land use change was not a widespread factor.

Some trends may be driven by local factors, but this study emphasized global trends and moved beyond considering each spring as a separate case study. Nonetheless, there were some regional patterns (Figure 8), which illustrate the need to do cross‐site comparisons beyond a single region. All three springs in Florida showed a decreasing trend in discharge over time, which may be associated with a change in pumping rate (Verdi et al. 2006; Scharping et al. 2018). Furthermore, a drought in 1998 to 2004 likely led to further water level declines in the aquifer, and Silver Spring, among other springs across Florida, showed significant discharge reduction (Verdi et al. 2006). The three springs in Missouri with longer discharge records (1948 to 2024) showed an increasing trend in spring discharge, likely associated with a long‐term (1921 to 2020) increase in precipitation for the region (Marti and Heimann 2024) that was not captured in the 1977 to 2014 dataset. Although Padilla et al. (1994) found Baget Spring in France had a higher quickflow component than Aliou Spring (91% vs. 40%, respectively) using hydrograph separation, in this study, the long‐term discharge trend was nearly the same. Slovenia and Croatia had the highest temperature trends in Europe (Figure 7, all springs above 0.04 °C per year) along with low discharge trends, but also including high discharge trends (Figures 7 and 8). Regional decreases in spring discharge have been observed due to reduced snowpack (Moniewski 2015; Kovačič et al. 2020; Lorenzi et al. 2022), but none of the U.S. carbonate springs analyzed are in a region impacted by mountain snowmelt. In Europe, several springs are in watersheds associated with mountain snowmelt, but these springs include both decreasing and nearly constant discharge trends (Table S5).

Sometimes local factors explain outliers in spring trends. Unica Spring showed a shift from a negative to positive trend for the earlier 1977 to 2014 and later 1995 to 2017 periods, which likely reflects deforestation and increased recharge in the watershed (Kovačič et al. 2020). Another spring with an inflection point that demonstrated a strong change in discharge trend, Ombla, was impacted by an engineering project from 1968 to 1980 to fill a local river with concrete and reduce recharge (Roje‐Bonacci and Bonacci 2013). This change makes Ombla sensitive to the selected start date for trend calculations. Mammoth in Arkansas also has an inflection point with a strong change in discharge trend toward the middle of the assessment period (2003). Although temporal variation in recharge to the Ozark Aquifer could affect spring discharge (Clark et al. 2019), this mechanism does not explain why Mammoth Spring would respond differently than other major springs in the same aquifer system (e.g., Big Spring and Greer Spring). Heterogeneity in hydraulic conductivity or regional groundwater use may contribute to this stronger inflection at Mammoth. For Ombla and Mammoth springs, the two discharge trends effectively canceled leading to an overall flat slope, so these two springs were not used in climate comparisons. In summary, even considering local factors, there is considerable overlap in long‐term spring response for most springs.

Lessons Learned: Key Considerations for Cross‐Site Comparison Research Efforts in Karst Aquifers

A large dataset of karst spring discharge records spanning two continents revealed dissimilar karst environments exhibited the same (or no) trends. Including non‐carbonate springs in this comparison showed that not all trends can be attributed to the fast flow paths in karst aquifers, since the response in these systems overlapped with karst springs. Indeed, the karst spring responses were not correlated with discharge variability, a common indicator of fast flow paths.

However, continuity of datasets can limit cross‐site comparison. The initial dataset included 49 springs but was pared down to 31 springs when it was established that overlapping time periods are required for unbiased comparison. Trends in spring discharge from overlapping time periods revealed outliers, including two springs that changed their trend from negative to positive when evaluated over a different time period. Two additional springs were eliminated when a significant change in trend at the midpoint of monitoring was identified.

It is important to plot the data to evaluate data quality (Figure S2) and report the monitoring period and monitoring frequency when conducting cross‐site comparisons. Data gaps reduced the number of springs with overlapping time periods and limited the use of some statistical techniques that require continuous data. In some cases, changes in monitoring frequency limit the discharge dataset size. Discharge trends over time for hourly, daily, monthly, and annual frequencies were compared. In this dataset, only one spring showed a notable change in discharge trends for a finer frequency, which suggests that aggregating to a common frequency is a good approach. However, daily data are needed to aggregate to coarser intervals to establish a representative trend.

Larger datasets and comparisons across varied karst regions are needed to improve the identification of drivers of discharge change. Understanding of karst behavior is limited by a lack of long‐term records across multiple sites. Longer records could allow us to evaluate the influence of cyclical weather patterns, land use change, and water management. Water quality and temperature datasets should also be included in monitoring programs to enhance interpretation of discharge trends (e.g., Luhmann et al. 2011; Toran et al. 2018; Husic et al. 2023; Spellman et al. 2025). The lack of integrated data creates a “black box” in understanding of karst spring discharge behavior and limits the accuracy of future predictions. Thus, there is a need for publicly available data, especially outside of the United States and Europe (as previously noted by Olarinoye et al. 2020), and for continued monitoring at existing sites. Evaluating anthropogenic factors remains a challenge and can devolve into site‐specific studies, but larger datasets can help overcome the tendency to view every karst aquifer as unique and create a more comprehensive picture of spring discharge dynamics in karst aquifers.

Conclusions

This study benefitted from examining long‐term data from two continents and including non‐carbonate springs to illustrate trends that are common across varied spring types. The occurrence of increasing, decreasing, or no trend in discharge among springs with high variability in discharge was surprising because it suggests that fast flow paths do not necessarily make springs more vulnerable to long‐term trends, and contributions from storage may buffer climate trends even in karst springs. The majority of springs in a dataset of 49 springs from the United States and Europe (including seven non‐carbonate springs) with a 30 to 75‐year record of spring discharge showed either decreasing or little change in discharge over time. Decreases in discharge were associated with an observed increase in air temperature over the same time period, but larger values for air temperature trends did not lead to larger discharge trends. Furthermore, air temperature increases were observed for springs showing nearly constant or increasing discharge trends over time. Even with a large dataset, spring discharge trends over time could not be categorized by spring behavior as measured by mean discharge, variability in discharge, or land use, suggesting that the vulnerability to change is a result of a combination of drivers.

Because some springs do not show the same trends for different time periods, monitoring periods need to be matched for climate data and spring discharge. Data limitations such as gaps or changes in monitoring techniques can hinder the documentation of spring discharge trends. The long‐term data set was reduced from 49 springs to 31 springs when matching time periods and data quality constraints were imposed. Thus, developing large datasets to evaluate trends over time is hampered by a lack of publicly available continuous data and uneven monitoring periods. The karst community has recognized the influence of indirect drivers such as climate change, land use change, and water abstraction, requiring a quantitative, data‐driven understanding of these impacts. To plug this data gap, it is vital to continue research efforts to build more comprehensive, long‐term monitoring networks globally.
