Curcumin inhibits glycolysis via EP300 in oral squamous cell carcinoma
Authors: Wanrong Tang, Chao Zhang, Li Han, Haoxuan Fu, Zhouhong Mao, Yongheng Zhang, Ying Liu, Lihua Li, Jie Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Curcumin, a natural polyphenolic compound known for its antitumor efficacy, has been shown to modulate glycolytic pathways in various cancers. However, its specific role and underlying mechanism in oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) remain insufficiently explored. This study systematically investigated the impact of curcumin on glycolysis in OSCC cells, with particular focus on EP300, a key epigenetic regulator, as a target of curcumin’s action. Human oral squamous cell carcinoma cell lines (UM-SCC-1 and HSC-3) and normal human oral keratinocytes (HOK) were treated with curcumin at various concentrations. Cell proliferation, clonogenic ability, and migration were assessed using CCK-8, colony formation, and wound-healing assays, respectively. Glycolytic activity was evaluated by measuring glucose uptake and lactate production. An EP300-overexpressing UM-SCC-1 cell line was established via plasmid transfection. Protein-protein interaction (PPI) network analysis through the STRING database identified potential EP300-regulated glycolytic enzymes. Expression levels of EP300 and key glycolytic markers (PKM2, LDHA, GLUT1) were quantified using qRT-PCR and Western blot. Curcumin significantly inhibited proliferation, clonogenic growth, and migration of OSCC cells (UM-SCC-1 and HSC-3) in a concentration-dependent manner. Curcumin treatment markedly reduced glucose uptake and lactate production in OSCC cells, indicating effective suppression of glycolytic activity. At the molecular level, Curcumin downregulated EP300 expression. EP300 overexpression enhanced glycolytic activity and increased the expression of key glycolytic enzymes, including PKM2, LDHA, and GLUT1, and partially reversed the inhibitory effects of curcumin on glycolysis and enzyme expression. Bioinformatic analysis confirmed interactions between EP300 and these glycolytic enzymes. Curcumin suppresses glycolysis in OSCC by modulating EP300 expression and downregulating key glycolytic enzymes such as PKM2, LDHA, and GLUT1. These findings are consistent with curcumin’s potential role as a metabolic modulator in OSCC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-44496-3.

Introduction

Oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC), the most prevalent malignancy of the head and neck, accounts for approximately 90% of all oral cancers1. Its persistently high incidence and mortality rates pose a significant global health challenge2. Beyond its lethality, OSCC is frequently associated with disfigurement and severe functional impairments, including dysphagia, speech disorders, and loss of taste, all of which substantially diminish patients’ quality of life3,4. These limitations underscore the urgent need for novel therapeutic strategies.

Natural compounds have gained increasing attention for their potential in cancer prevention and treatment, with curcumin emerging as one of the most extensively studied candidates due to its pronounced anticancer activity5. Curcumin, a polyphenolic compound derived from the rhizome of Curcuma longa, exhibits a wide range of pharmacological properties, including anti-inflammatory6, antioxidant7, and anticancer effects8. Extensive studies have demonstrated that curcumin can inhibit tumor cell proliferation, invasion, and metastasis by modulating multiple signaling pathways9–11.

A defining hallmark of cancer cells is their reprogrammed energy metabolism. Even in the presence of oxygen, tumor cells preferentially convert glucose to lactate through glycolysis, a phenomenon termed the “Warburg effect”12.This metabolic shift not only ensures a continuous supply of energy and biosynthetic intermediates but also contributes to the establishment of a tumor-promoting microenvironment13,14. Aberrant metabolic reprogramming has been closely linked to OSCC progression, suggesting that targeting glycolysis and related metabolic pathways may provide promising therapeutic avenues15. Recent studies have highlighted the ability of curcumin to regulate glycolysis through multi-target and multi-pathway mechanisms16,17.

EP300 (p300), a member of the KAT3 family of histone acetyltransferases, regulates gene transcription through acetylation of histone and non-histone proteins. Beyond its canonical transcriptional role, accumulating evidence indicates that EP300/CBP serves as a critical epigenetic regulator of tumor metabolic reprogramming. In hepatocellular carcinoma, inhibition of p300/CBP disrupts glycolysis by reducing histone acetylation at promoters of metabolism-related genes18, while p300 has also been shown to directly regulate the activity of glycolytic enzymes through non-histone post-translational modifications19. In OSCC, however, the metabolic role of EP300 remains insufficiently explored. Our previous studies further demonstrated that EP300 is aberrantly overexpressed in oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC), and that EP300 depletion markedly suppresses glucose consumption and lactate production, accompanied by reduced invasive and migratory capacities, highlighting a pivotal role of EP300-driven glycolytic reprogramming in OSCC progression20.

However, the effects of curcumin on glycolysis in OSCC and its mechanistic association with EP300 remain incompletely understood. Therefore, the present study aimed to systematically investigate the effects of curcumin on glycolytic activity in OSCC cells, with particular emphasis on the regulatory role of EP300. These findings provide mechanistic insights into curcumin-mediated metabolic regulation and may offer a theoretical basis for metabolic-targeted therapy in OSCC.

Materials and methods

Reagent preparation of curcumin stock solution

Curcumin (1.84 mg) (GlpBio, USA) was accurately weighed using an analytical balance and dissolved in 50 μL of cell culture-grade dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) to obtain a 100 μmol/L stock solution. The stock was aliquoted, protected from light, and stored at −80 °C. Working concentrations were freshly prepared by diluting the stock in complete culture medium, ensuring that the final DMSO concentration did not exceed 0.1% in any treatment group. In all subsequent experiments involving curcumin, the 0 μmol/L group corresponded to cells treated with an equivalent concentration of DMSO (<0.1%, v/v), which served as the vehicle control.

Cell culture

Human oral keratinocytes (HOK) and two oral squamous cell carcinoma cell lines (UM-SCC-1 and HSC-3) were used in this study, and all cell lines were commercially obtained and authenticated by short tandem repeat (STR) profiling. All cell lines were confirmed to be mycoplasma-free. UM-SCC-1 cells were generously provided by the West China School of Stomatology, Sichuan University, and was purchased from Wuhan Procell Life Technology Co., Ltd. HOK and HSC-3 cells were purchased from Guangzhou Jennio Biotech Co., Ltd. Cells were maintained in high-glucose Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM) (BDBIO, China) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (BDBIO, China) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (BDBIO, China) under standard culture conditions (37 °C, 5% CO₂). Cells were passaged at approximately 90% confluence and used for experiments at 70% confluence.

CCK-8 cell proliferation assay

Oral squamous cell carcinoma cell lines (HSC-3 and UM-SCC-1) and HOK cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 3 × 103 cells per well, with five replicate wells. After 24 hours of attachment, the medium was replaced, and cells were treated with Curcumin at 0, 10, 20, or 40 μmol/L. After 24 hours of treatment, 10 μL of Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; APExBio, USA) solution and 90 μL of serum-free medium were added to each well, including blank control wells. Following incubation at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ for 2 hours, absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (Bio-Rad, USA). Cell viability was calculated using GraphPad Prism software.

Colony formation assay

HSC-3 and UM-SCC-1 cells were seeded into 6-well plates at 1,000 cells per well, , with five replicate wells. After 24 hours of attachment, cells were exposed to the indicated concentrations of Curcumin, and the medium was refreshed after 48 hours. Cells were cultured for 2–3 weeks until visible colonies formed. When most colonies contained ≥50 cells, cultures were terminated. Colonies were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 minutes and stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 30 minutes. Plates were photographed, and colony numbers were quantified.

Wound healing assay

HSC-3 and UM-SCC-1 Cells were seeded in 6-well plates at 5 × 105 cells per well and grown to full confluence. A straight scratch was created using a sterile 1 mL pipette tip, and detached cells were removed by PBS washing. Baseline images were captured with an inverted microscope, followed by treatment with serum-free medium containing the indicated concentrations of Curcumin. After 24 hours, wound closure was imaged and quantified using ImageJ. Migration was calculated as: Wound healing rate (%) = (cell-free area at 0 h − cell-free area at 24 h) / (cell-free area at 0 h) ×100%.

Glucose uptake assay and lactate production assay

Log-phase HOK, UM-SCC-1, and HSC-3 cells were collected after trypsinization, counted, and seeded into 6-well plates at 1 × 105 cells per well (three replicates per group). After 12 hours of attachment, cells were washed twice with pre-warmed PBS and treated with Curcumin at 0, 5, 10, 15, or 20 μmol/L. Culture supernatants were collected at 0 and 24 hours, centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 5 minutes at 4°C.Glucose and lactate levels were quantified using glucose oxidase and lactate assay kits (Abbkine, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Absorbance was measured at 630 nm for glucose and 450 nm for lactate, and concentrations were determined from standard curves. Glucose uptake was calculated as: [Glucose uptake (mg/105 cells/24 h) = (Initial concentration − Final concentration) × Medium volume / Cell number]. After recording the OD value at each time point, the lactate production (μmol) of 1×105 cells after 24 h was converted according to the standard curve.

Cell transfection

EP300 overexpression lentiviral vectors and corresponding empty vectors were purchased from Genechem (China), which contained puromycin (Solarbio, China) resistance sequences.UM-SCC-1 cells at 70–80% confluence were transfected using the corresponding transfection reagent according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Lipofectamine™ 3000 transfection reagent was purchased from OriScience (China). After incubation at 37°C with 5% CO₂, stable transfectants were selected with puromycin (Biosharp, China) for 7 days and maintained in puromycin-containing medium. qRT-PCR and Western blot were performed to verify the transfection.

qRT-PCR

Total RNA was isolated from log-phase cells using an RNA extraction kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), and RNA quality was verified with a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). One microgram of RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA (Vazyme, China). Quantitative PCR was performed using SYBR Green, with GAPDH as the reference gene. Each 20 μL reaction (Vazyme, China) contained 2× SYBR Green Master Mix, primers (10 μM), cDNA, and nuclease-free water. Cycling conditions were 95°C for 5 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95°C for 15 s and 60°C for 30 s. Melt-curve analysis confirmed amplification specificity. Relative expression was calculated using the 2⁻ΔΔCt method, and assays were run in biological triplicate. Primer sequences are provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Western blot

Cells at approximately 80% confluence were washed with cold PBS and lysed on ice using RIPA buffer supplemented with protease inhibitors (EpiZyme, China). Lysates were sonicated, centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 15 minutes at 4°C, and supernatants were collected. Protein concentrations were measured using a BCA assay (Solarbio, China), adjusted with loading buffer (EpiZyme, China), and denatured at 100°C for 10 minutes. Equal amounts of protein (30 μg per lane) were resolved on 7.5% SDS-PAGE gels (EpiZyme, China) for EP300 or 10% gels for PKM2, GLUT1, and LDHA, and transferred to 0.45 μm PVDF membranes (EpiZyme, China). Membranes were blocked and incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against EP300 (1:1000) (EpiZyme, China), PKM2(1:1000) (EpiZyme, China), LDHA (1:1000) (EpiZyme, China), GLUT1 (1:1000) (Affinity, USA), and β-tubulin (1:2000) (Abmart, China). After washing, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:5000) (EpiZyme, China) for 1 hour at room temperature, followed by ECL detection. Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ and normalized to β-tubulin.

PPI Network construction

EP300 and glycolytic enzymes (PKM2, LDHA, GLUT1, HK2, PFKFB3) were imported into the String database (https://string-db.org/cgi/input.pl), and the species was set as “Homo Sapiens”. The confidence level of the selected parameters was medium confidence (0.04), and the unrelated target proteins were removed to obtain the protein interactions and screen the potential core targets.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 9. All experiments were performed independently in biological triplicate. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Differences between two groups were evaluated using an unpaired Student’s t-test. For comparisons among multiple groups, one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple compasisons test was performed. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Curcumin inhibits proliferation, clonogenicity and migration of OSCC cells

To evaluate the effects of Curcumin on human normal keratinocytes (HOK) and oral squamous cell carcinoma (HSC-3, UM-SCC-1) cells, cell viability assays were first conducted. However, at 40 µmol/L, curcumin markedly decreased HOK cell viability. This suggests that low concentrations of curcumin markedly inhibit the proliferation of HSC3 and UM-SCC-1 cells without exhibiting significant toxicity toward HOK cells. Consistently, colony formation was markedly suppressed by curcumin. Both UM-SCC-1 and HSC-3 cells displayed a pronounced reduction in viable colony numbers following exposure to 10 or 20 μmol/L curcumin compared with untreated controls (Fig. 1B). Moreover, curcumin inhibited cell migratory ability. Wound-healing assays showed that curcumin significantly delayed scratch closure in HSC-3 and UM-SCC-1 cells, with large residual wound areas remaining after 24 h of treatment at 10 or 20 μmol/L (Fig. 1C).

Together, these results indicate that curcumin suppresses key malignant behaviors of OSCC cells, including proliferation, clonogenic growth, and migration.

Curcumin suppresses glycolytic activity in OSCC cells

This study evaluated the effects of curcumin on glycolytic processes by measuring glucose uptake and lactate production in human oral keratinocytes (HOK) and two oral squamous cell carcinoma cell lines (HSC-3 and UM-SCC-1) treated with varying concentrations of Curcumin (0, 5, 10, 15, 20 μmol/L). Under the same treatment conditions, no significant alterations in glycolytic parameters were observed in HOK cells. As shown in Fig. 3A, EP300 expression was significantly upregulated at both the mRNA and protein levels in EP300-oe cells compared to negative control (NC) and wild-type (WT) groups, confirming the successful generation of the model. We then examined the effect of curcumin. Pharmacological intervention experiments revealed that curcumin treatment markedly reduced EP300 expression in UM-SCC-1 cells (Fig. 3B). Notably, this curcumin-induced downregulation of EP300 was abolished when EP300 was forcibly overexpressed (Fig. 3B). Glycolytic function assays revealed that EP300 overexpression markedly enhanced glucose metabolic activity in UM-SCC-1 cells. As shown in Fig. 3C, glucose uptake rates in the EP300-oe group were significantly elevated compared to the NC group (p<0.01). Notably, enforced EP300 overexpression reversed the inhibitory effect of curcumin on glucose uptake (compare EP300-oe+CUR with NC+CUR). Consistently, lactate production assays showed that curcumin-induced suppression of lactate release was also significantly rescued by EP300 overexpression (Fig. 3D). Taken together, these results indicate that EP300 acts as a positive regulator of glycolysis in OSCC, promoting glucose uptake and lactate production. Conversely, curcumin exerts its inhibitory effect on glycolysis primarily through downregulating EP300 expression.

EP300 positively regulates key glycolytic enzymes in OSCC cells

To investigate how EP300 regulates glycolysis in OSCC, we combined bioinformatics analysis with experimental validation to examine its relationship with key glycolytic enzymes. Consistent with the transcriptional upregulation, Western blot analysis confirmed elevated protein expression of PKM2, GLUT1, and LDHA following EP300 overexpression (Fig. 4C). Consistent results were observed at the protein level. Western blot analyses demonstrated that Curcumin markedly decreased PKM2, GLUT1, and LDHA protein abundance, whereas EP300 overexpression led to notable upregulation. Discussion

Recent studies have demonstrated that curcumin can directly modulate tumor glycolysis through multiple mechanisms. In hepatocellular carcinoma, curcumin suppresses glycolysis by downregulating the key glycolytic enzyme hexokinase 2 (HK2), thereby reducing metastatic potential both in vitro and in vivo21 Similarly, in colorectal cancer, curcumin inhibits glycolytic flux by decreasing HK2 expression and activity and disrupting its mitochondrial association, leading to impaired glucose metabolism22. In pancreatic cancer models, curcumin has been shown to attenuate hypoxia-induced glycolysis by suppressing HIF-1α signaling and downregulating glycolysis-related genes, including GLUT1, HK2, LDHA, and PDK1, accompanied by reduced ATP production23. In the present study, we demonstrate that curcumin markedly suppresses glycolytic activity in oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) cells while exerting minimal metabolic effects on normal oral keratinocytes.

EP300 is a multifunctional epigenetic regulator that modulates gene expression through acetylation of histone and non-histone proteins and has been increasingly implicated in tumor metabolic reprogramming. Consistent with previous reports in other malignancies, our results support that EP300 functions as a positive regulator of glycolysis in OSCC cells, as EP300 overexpression significantly enhanced glucose consumption and lactate production. Curcumin reduced glucose uptake and lactate production in OSCC cells in a concentration-dependent manner, accompanied by downregulation of EP300 expression. Moreover, enforced EP300 overexpression largely reversed these metabolic effects. Our study confirmed that Curcumin significantly down-regulated the expression level of EP300 in OSCC, a finding that corroborates with Wang et al.24. who reported that Curcumin exerts anti-tumor effects in bladder cancer through epigenetic regulation. Collectively, these results suggest that EP300 may act as a critical mediator linking curcumin treatment to glycolytic inhibition in OSCC.

In terms of molecular mechanisms, EP300 regulates tumor metabolism through multiple pathways.EP300 can stabilize HIF-1α protein and enhance its transcriptional activity through acetylation modification25, thereby activating HIF-1α signaling and upregulating the expression of key glycolytic enzymes, including PKM2, GLUT1, and LDHA26,27. Notably, Siddiqui et al.28 reported that Curcumin could down-regulate PKM2 expression by inhibiting the mTOR-HIF1α signaling pathway, which is highly consistent with the findings of the present study. In addition to HIF-1α–dependent regulation, EP300 can also affect tumor metabolism by regulating c-Myc protein stability29. Recent studies have shown30 that stable expression of c-Myc significantly promotes the transcription of glycolysis-related genes such as LDHA, SLC2A1 and PKM2, further supporting the role of EP300-associated transcriptional networks in metabolic reprogramming.

To further elucidate the downstream metabolic circuitry regulated by EP300, we integrated bioinformatics prediction with experimental validation. Protein–protein interaction network analysis revealed close associations between EP300 and key glycolytic enzymes, including PKM2, GLUT1, and LDHA. These enzymes orchestrate sequential steps of glycolysis, ranging from glucose uptake to lactate generation, and constitute essential molecular components of the Warburg effect. Consistently, EP300 overexpression increased, whereas curcumin treatment decreased, the transcriptional and protein expression levels of PKM2, GLUT1, and LDHA. Importantly, EP300 overexpression partially rescued curcumin-induced suppression of these enzymes, supporting the notion that curcumin attenuates glycolysis primarily through EP300-dependent regulation of glycolytic gene expression.

However, there are several limitations to this study. Firstly, the research is entirely in vitro, and future studies using in vivo models, such as xenografts or orthotopic OSCC models, are required to validate these findings. Moreover, the pharmacokinetics of curcumin and achievable tissue concentrations should be explored to evaluate its clinical applicability. Secondly, although our data suggest that curcumin suppresses glycolysis in OSCC cells in association with EP300 modulation, the precise molecular mechanisms underlying EP300-mediated regulation of glycolytic enzymes remain unclear. While EP300 has been reported to regulate glycolysis through transcription factors such as HIF-1α and c-Myc, the present study does not conclusively determine whether EP300 directly acetylates the promoter regions of glycolytic enzyme genes or indirectly regulates their expression via these transcription factors. Further mechanistic investigations, including chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assays and acetylation profiling, will be required to clarify the direct epigenetic mechanisms involved in this regulatory process.

Conclusion

This study suggests that curcumin inhibits glycolysis in oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) cells by modulating EP300 expression, which in turn reduces the expression of key glycolytic enzymes such as PKM2, LDHA, and GLUT1. These findings provide insights into the potential role of curcumin as a metabolic modulator in OSCC. However, further in vivo studies and validation are needed to confirm these results and evaluate its clinical applicability.
