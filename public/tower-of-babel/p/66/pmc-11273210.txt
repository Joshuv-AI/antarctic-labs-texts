Eagle syndrome presentation and outcomes in a large surgical case series
Authors: Michael E Held, Soroush Farsi, Erin R Weatherford Creighton, Kyle P Davis, Deanne L King, James Y Suen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe purpose of this study is to describe both the common and uncommon symptoms associated with Eagle syndrome and share our experience treating a large group of patients with surgical intervention, primarily intraoral excision of the calcified stylohyoid ligament.MethodsThis retrospective case series included 56 patients at least 18 years of age or older with a diagnosis of Eagle syndrome. All operations were conducted by a single surgeon at a tertiary medical center from 2015 to 2022. Charts were reviewed for demographics, prior medical/surgical history, symptoms, imaging results, operative details, and follow‐up history. A phone survey inquired about presenting symptoms and symptom resolution following surgery.ResultsThe most common areas of pain were the ear (64.3%), underneath the angle of the mandible (50%), throat (46.4%), and neck (30.4%). Over 70% of patients reported tinnitus, dysphagia, and pain that were exacerbated by head rotation. Fifty‐one of the 56 patients underwent surgical treatment, 92.2% via intraoral and 7.8% via cervical approaches. All patients (100%) reached in a phone survey stated that their symptoms resolved or improved after surgery.ConclusionEagle syndrome typically presents with common symptoms. However, healthcare providers must also be vigilant for less common manifestations, such as seizures or episodes of dizziness/fainting. These may be caused by calcification of the stylohyoid ligament. Intraoral surgical resection of the calcified ligament is a safe and effective treatment for most patients.Level of EvidenceLevel 4.Keywords: Eagle syndrome, ear pain, neck pain, stylohyoid ligament calcification, stylohyoid syndrome, surgical treatment

INTRODUCTION

Eagle syndrome, sometimes known as stylohyoid syndrome, is a rare and poorly characterized condition caused primarily by calcification of the stylohyoid ligament within the neck. 1 , 2 This abnormal complex interferes with the function of vital neighboring neurovascular structures and can result in a variety of symptoms that clinicians should be aware of for correct diagnosis and treatment. Eagle syndrome is classified with other facial pain syndromes with symptoms caused by an abnormal styloid complex; however, the pathogenesis of this syndrome along with the reason for onset of symptoms is not fully understood. 3 , 4 , 5

Although reports of the calcified stylohyoid ligament date back to 1870 (Lucke) and 1907 (Dwight), Watt Eagle first delineated the syndrome into two groups based on presenting symptoms—the Typical Eagle syndrome and Carotid Artery syndrome. 6 While some clinicians may initially use medical treatments for these patients, the cure rates are much higher following intraoral or cervical approach surgical resection. 7

At our institution, a tertiary, academic medical center, we see a high volume of patients with orofacial or head and neck pain, and most have seen multiple providers before coming to our center for diagnosis and/or treatment. The purpose of this study is to describe both the common and uncommon symptoms associated with Eagle syndrome and share our experience treating a large group of patients with surgical intervention, primarily intraoral excision of the calcified stylohyoid ligament.

METHODS

The described study is a retrospective case series from a single surgeon at a tertiary medical center between January 2015 and August 2022. The study protocol was approved by the institutional review board at the University of Arkansas for Medical Sciences (IRB No. 273405). Inclusion criteria included patients 18 years of age or older with a diagnosis of Eagle syndrome. Patients were identified by searching the electronic medical record (EMR) for the following International Classification of Disease (ICD) codes: facial pain (784.0), throat pain (784.1), neck pain (723.1) and dysphasia (784.5), other disorders of muscle, ligament, fascia (728.89), and other disorders of bone and cartilage (733.99). Current Procedural Terminology (CPT) code 21499, the unlisted code for the musculoskeletal procedures of the head, was used to query the EMR for patients undergoing surgical intervention for Eagle syndrome.

A thorough medical history and clinical examination were performed on each patient along with a radiographic examination with computed tomography (CT) of the neck to reach a diagnosis of Eagle syndrome. Clinically manifesting as a slowly progressing chronic pain at or near the angle of the mandible often aggravated by cervical rotation with associated symptoms such as ear pain, tinnitus, dysphagia, and globus sensation in the throat. Typical CT findings show calcification of the stylohyoid ligament (typically 3–3.5 cm) predominantly on the symptomatic side but can also be present bilaterally. Furthermore, CT with contrast may show indentation of the Carotid or Jugular vein that can help with the diagnosis. The diagnosis was explained, imaging reviewed, and treatment options discussed. Surgical intervention in the form of a cervical or intraoral approach for the excision and/or release of the calcified ligament was discussed. Patients who wished to proceed with surgical intervention were scheduled per our institution's policies.

The following surgical procedure was performed for patients receiving intraoral approach for resection of the calcified stylohyoid ligament. Under general anesthesia, a Crowe Davis or Dingman retractor was used to retract the endotracheal tube to allow exposure of the oropharynx. After sterile preparation of the oropharynx with a betadine solution, drying time, and proper draping, there was palpation of the oropharynx to try to palpate the calcified styloid–hyoid ligament. The surgical procedure becomes significantly easier and shorter when the calcified stylohyoid ligament is palpable, as it allows for easier identification and excision of the calcification.

A 3‐cm vertical incision was then made near the anterior tonsillar pillar. Dissection was carried down through the mucosa and submucosa and the tonsil capsule, if present, and medial pterygoid muscle was identified. Dissection was done between the muscle and tonsil posteriorly until a fat pad was identified. The calcified ligament is typically located in the fat pad or positioned posterior to it. The typical styloid process is typically not encountered; however, the calcified stylohyoid ligament can be palpated and exposed through careful blunt and sharp dissection using bipolar and monopolar electrocautery. Caution must be exercised to avoid the internal jugular vein, internal carotid artery, and Vagus nerve.

The calcified ligament can be hidden by a large stylohyoid muscle, and this can be seen on the axial CT scan. Once identified, the calcified ligament was dissected circumferentially as far superiorly and inferiorly as possible and then removed with a Kerrison Rongeur. The distal ligament was divided to release any tension on neurovascular structures. After meticulous hemostasis using the bipolar cautery and saline irrigation, the wound was closed in layers. Patients with bilateral symptoms and/or CT findings undergo the same procedure on the contralateral side. We usually inject 5 mL of 0.5% Bupivacaine for postoperative pain relief.

When standard discharge criteria are met in the post‐anesthesia care unit, patients are discharged the same day with oral antibiotics and pain medications. Patients are scheduled to be seen in our clinic ~2 weeks after surgery for postoperative visit and thereafter as needed. There is significant throat pain for 7–10 days post‐op.

A chart review was conducted for all patients who met inclusion criteria and the following data was collected from the EMR: age, gender, race, prior medical and surgical history, symptoms, imaging results, operative details, and follow‐up history. To gather additional information regarding the presenting symptoms and efficacy of the surgical treatment, a phone survey was conducted. We attempted to contact all patients who underwent surgery. Once verbal consent was obtained and recorded, the survey was conducted over the phone (see Data S1 [phone survey]) Patients were queried about their initial symptoms and how they compared with their current state post‐surgery, categorized as resolved, improved, unchanged, or worsened. The number of physicians and/or dentists the patient had seen before presenting to our center was recorded along with any procedures or medical interventions that the patient underwent for these symptoms before resection of the calcified ligament. Study data, including survey consent and responses, was collected and managed using REDCap electronic capture tools hosted at UAMS.

There were 16 patients (16 of 56; 28.6%) who reported undergoing previous procedural interventions for their complaints without any relief from symptoms. These procedures included craniotomy with microvascular decompression, radiofrequency ablation, nerve blocks, tonsillectomy, submandibular gland excision, TMJ and dental procedures, and Botox injections.

From the phone survey, 31 patients (31 of 56; 55.4%) were willing to participate in the survey however, 1 patient was unable to provide answers concerning symptom relief due to concurrent trigeminal neuralgia. When asked on the survey, pain was exacerbated by cervical rotation (23 of 30: 76.7%) and chewing food (19 of 30: 63.3%). Associated symptoms were tinnitus (22 of 30; 73.3%), dizziness or fainting (16 of 30; 53.3%), seizures (2 of 30; 6.7%), and trouble swallowing (23 of 30; 76.7%) as seen in Figure 2. When asked further about the trouble swallowing, 15 patients (15 of 23; 65.2%) described a sensation of something being stuck / a foreign body sensation in the throat, 15 patients (15 of 23; 65.2%) described difficulty swallowing, and 3 patients (3 of 23; 13%) described pain with swallowing, with several patients identifying with more than one of the above options.

All 30 patients (30/30; 100%) reported seeing another physician and/or dentist for their complaints before presentation to our clinic seeing an average of 4 physicians and/or dentists (range = 1–10, median = 4), of which 5 (5 of 30; 16.4%) patients reported prior unsuccessful medical or surgical interventions.

In our cohort, 51 of 56 patients underwent surgical intervention. Non‐surgical patients were offered but declined surgery. Of these cases, 92.2% utilized an intraoral approach for the release and excision of the calcified stylohyoid ligament, distinct from the choice of styloidectomy. The other 7.8% had a cervical approach. Complications include hypernasality or nasopharyngeal reflux in five patients, all of which resolved with time, wound dehiscence, and facial artery pseudoaneurysm that presented 2 weeks post‐op as intraoral hemorrhage requiring interventional radiology embolization.

All the patients who responded to the survey (n = 30) reported that in general their symptoms were resolved or improved after surgery (18 of 30 resolved; 60%, 12 of 30 improved; 40%, 30 of 30 combined resolution or improvement; 100%). Specifically, pain exacerbation with cervical rotation (17 of 23 resolved; 73.9%, 5 of 23 improved; 21.7%, 22 of 23 combined resolution or improvement; 95%) or chewing food (12 of 19 resolved; 63.2%, 5 of 19 improved; 26.3%, 17 of 19 combined resolution or improvement; 89.5%), tinnitus (5 of 22 resolved; 22.7%, 9 of 22 improved; 40.9%, 14 of 22 combined resolution or improvement; 63.6%), dizziness or fainting (12 of 16 resolved; 75%, 3 of 16 improved; 18.8%, 15 of 16 combined resolution or improvement; 93.8%), seizures (2 of 2 resolved; 100%, 0 of 2 improved; 0%, 2 of 2 combined resolution or improvement; 100%), trouble swallowing (14 of 23 resolved; 60.9%, 7 of 23 improved; 30.4%, 21 of 23 combined resolution or improvement; 91.3%), and ear pain (12 of 23 resolved; 52.2%, 9 of 23 improved; 39.1%, 21 of 23 combined resolution or improvement; 91.3%) as seen in Figure 3. No patients reported that any of their symptoms worsened after surgery. The patient, who had a several‐year history of frequent grand mal seizures, reported being completely seizure‐free since the surgery, for a total of 6 years and 3 months.

DISCUSSION

Eagle syndrome can be a debilitating disease often being undiagnosed for years due to its complex symptomology leading to poor management and outcomes. 4 , 7 The varying symptoms occur due to impingement or compression of the surrounding anatomy by a calcified stylohyoid ligament. 1 , 6 The styloid process is a protuberance from the inferior surface of the temporal bone that functions as a site of attachment for several ligaments and muscles, importantly, the stylohyoid ligament. These attachments help with the movement of the mandible, hyoid bone, tongue, pharynx, and larynx to aid in a myriad of daily functioning. The styloid process and its attachments are surrounded by several important neurovascular structures. Medial to the styloid process are cranial nerves (CN) IX, X, and XI, the internal jugular vein, and the internal carotid artery and its branches. Lateral to the styloid process are CN VII and XII and the external carotid artery. 3 , 4 , 8

The direct pathogenesis of a calcified stylohyoid ligament has not been determined although multiple theories exist. Some suggest that ossification of the stylohyoid ligament is due to congenital variables displaying autosomal dominant inheritance. 9 Others state ossification and bone formation at the tendonous attachments to the styloid process happen naturally later in life. 5 Traumatic injuries leading to scarring, tonsillectomies, metabolic imbalances of calcium, vitamin D and phosphorus, menopause, and endocrine disorders have also been discussed as contributors. 2 , 7 , 10 , 11 Whatever the exact mechanism is, Eagle syndrome causes significant morbidity, specifically affecting middle‐aged women three times more often than men 3 , 12 This is similar to findings in our case series. It is important to be aware that patients may have excess calcification of the stylohyoid ligament.

Eagle syndrome was originally separated into two types depending on the symptom manifestation thought to be derived from which adjacent neurovascular structures were compressed by the calcified stylohyoid ligament. 6 The first type, known as classic Eagle syndrome, is more prevalent. It manifests with symptoms such as facial, neck, and throat pain, odynophagia, dysphagia, globus sensation, referred ear pain, seizures, and tinnitus, all stemming from irritation of cranial nerves V, VII, IX, and X. 13 , 14 These symptoms were also the most common symptoms in our series, as well. The second subtype, known as the carotid type, results from compression of the internal and external carotid arteries, sympathetic nerve plexus, and jugular vein. This subtype manifests symptomatically with dizziness, carotid dissection, syncope, stroke, sudden death, headache, Horner syndrome, cerebral venous sinus thrombosis, transient visual loss, as well as facial and neck pain. 3 , 6 , 7 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 , 21

Due to its wide array of possible symptoms, Eagle syndrome is often misdiagnosed causing patients to undergo unnecessary treatments and prolonging their symptoms. 22 In the cohort of patients seen at our center, an average of four physicians and/or dentists were seen by patients for these symptoms before being diagnosed with or treated for Eagle syndrome. Sixteen patients underwent previous interventions or procedures, ranging from minimal to very invasive, that did not resolve their symptoms. A detailed history of present illness, physical exam, diagnostic imaging with neck CT, and heightened clinical suspicion are crucial to achieving a correct diagnosis of Eagle syndrome. Diagnostic clues such as ear or throat pain that is made worse by head rotation and jaw movements along with globus sensation or dysphagia should prompt further examination for Eagle syndrome. Palpation of the calcified stylohyoid ligament in the lateral tonsillar fossa may illicit pain. 3 , 22 , 23

The definitive diagnostic tool for Eagle syndrome involves identifying an elongated styloid process or abnormally calcified stylohyoid ligament through CT imaging of the neck, 24 a correlation that aligns with our observations in all of our patients. This imaging modality provides optimal visualization of abnormal structure and its relation to nearby nerves and vessels. 3 , 11 , 25 It should also be used to rule out other differential diagnoses such as tumor or infection. 7 The “LAM” classification was created to describe abnormal styloid processes via 3D CT imaging by looking at the length, angulation, and morphology, and it is a valuable tool for distinguishing problematic styloid complexes. 24 In the literature, an elongated styloid process is characterized as exceeding 2.5 cm in length. 25 Confirmation of Eagle syndrome diagnosis is established when this elongation is observed in conjunction with symptoms. 7 In our experience, we classify patients as high risk for Eagle syndrome if the styloid process extends below the inferior alveolar canal of the mandible, even if it falls short of the 2.5‐cm threshold. Furthermore, we believe that the calcified ligament is more likely the primary cause of the symptoms. This is supported by positive outcomes observed after releasing and excising the calcified ligament, without the necessity of removing the actual styloid process.

While surgical excision is our preferred method for patient treatment, management and treatment of a calcified stylohyoid ligament is possible non‐operatively. Medical treatments include non‐steroidal anti‐inflammatory drugs, analgesics, antiepileptics, muscle relaxants, and anti‐depressants. These, along with local injections of various medications, have been successful for a short duration with their effects diminishing with time. 4 , 7

Two main approaches exist for surgical resection of the calcified ligament or styloid process: intraoral or transcervical. Intraoral has been the traditional method first described by Eagle 1 though it has been adapted to no longer include a tonsillectomy. 26 This approach allows for a better cosmetic outcome and shorter operating times. 3 , 4 In the literature, proposed downsides of this approach are increased risk of infection, difficulty controlling bleeding, and restricted operative field leading to impaired complete resection; however, evidence of these complications is limited. 7 Complications in our cohort were limited to temporary hypernasal speech, minor wound dehiscence, and one intraoral bleeding episode 2 weeks post op which was found to be due to the residual calcified bone being sharp and hitting the facial artery. The bleeding was controlled with an arteriogram and embolization of that artery. Other intraoral techniques describe robotic and endoscopic assistance for resection but have limitations in cost and training. 13 , 27 , 28 The preferred operative approach by the senior author is intraoral with preservation of the tonsils, if present. Our experience shows that there is less chance for bleeding, less pain, and quicker healing time if tonsils are preserved.

Traditionally, the external approach has been thought to benefit from increased exposure of the styloid process and calcified ligament, leading to high success rates of treatment. 23 While a preferred technique for many that have its own benefits, from our experience, this approach is more difficult, requires a cervical scar, have a longer operative duration and hospital stay post‐surgery. It also risks injury to the marginal mandibular branch of the facial nerve. 4 In our study, the intraoral approach, which we adopted after a few cervical approaches, was successful in resolving or improving symptoms in all of our patients for a majority of their symptoms. Furthermore, we feel that complete resection of the styloid process proper up to the mastoid is almost impossible, dangerous, and unnecessary, from either approach. Our patients do well with releasing the distal ligament and excising 1–3.5 cm of calcified ligament without a portion of the styloid process. The goal is to prevent compression of the nearby neurovascular structures, and we have found our technique to be successful.

The limitations of this study are multifaceted. First, the retrospective nature of the research introduces a discrepancy between symptoms and aggravating factors collected from the chart review and those obtained through our phone survey. This incongruence may stem from a lack of documentation in the records and whether the information was reported to us or not. Furthermore, the reliance on participants' recall abilities poses a substantial limitation, as there is a risk of both recall and confirmation bias. Patients may inadvertently report postoperative symptoms not initially noticed or attributed to Eagle syndrome on presentation. Additionally, despite having a broad referral base for complex head and neck pain cases, this study is a single‐institution/single‐surgeon case series, limiting its generalizability. These limitations underscore the importance of cautious interpretation and emphasize the need to complement retrospective data with prospective studies for a more comprehensive understanding of the research questions.

CONCLUSION

When symptoms develop from a calcified stylohyoid ligament, a thorough and proper history, physical examination, and neck CT scan can lead to a diagnosis of Eagle syndrome. It must be in a clinician's differential diagnosis when seeing a patient with orofacial pain and or cerebrovascular symptoms that may seem idiopathic. Once diagnosed, intraoral surgical resection of the calcified ligament is a safe and effective surgical treatment which improves or resolves symptoms of Eagle syndrome. If a patient has abnormal calcification of the stylohyoid ligament but no symptoms compatible with Eagle syndrome, surgery would not be indicated.
