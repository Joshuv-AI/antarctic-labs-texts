Epidemiology, treatment patterns, and survival outcomes of patients with mantle cell lymphoma in Germany: a retrospective analysis of administrative claims data
Authors: Heiko Friedel, Nora Hennies, Jürgen Zschocke, Sabrina Khageh Hosseini, Sebastian Bölz, Derya Bocuk, Stefan Wilhelm, Okan Zaba, Christian Buske
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractUntil the early 2020s, standard mantle cell lymphoma (MCL) treatment in Germany primarily included chemoimmunotherapy, autologous stem cell transplantation (autoSCT) for eligible patients in first line, and covalent Bruton tyrosine kinase inhibitors (cBTKis; ibrutinib at the time) for relapsed/refractory disease. However, real-world data on MCL treatment patterns and outcomes in Germany remain scarce. This retrospective observational study analyzed administrative claims data between 2015 and 2020. Annual incidence and prevalence, treatment patterns, healthcare visits, and overall survival (OS) were evaluated. Extrapolated to the German statutory health insurance population, annual MCL prevalence and incidence rates (per 100,000 individuals) ranged from 6.64 to 10.02 and from 1.28 to 2.06, respectively, showing an upward trend. Among 369 patients with MCL in the database (2015–2020) receiving at least one anti-cancer treatment, median age at diagnosis was 71 years and average Charlson Comorbidity Index was 2.9. In first-line, patients mainly received chemoimmunotherapy (77.2%; n = 285); 13.8% (n = 51) underwent autoSCT, and 30.9% (n = 114) received rituximab maintenance; in second-line (n = 193), 45.6% received chemoimmunotherapy and 22.3% a cBTKi. Median OS from diagnosis was 6.3 years. In cBTKi-treated patients (n = 82), median OS from first cBTKi therapy initiation (> 75% of the patients received cBTKi in second or third line) was 11.2 months, decreasing to 3.0 months from cBTKi discontinuation (n = 45). This study presents the first comprehensive analysis of MCL epidemiology, treatment patterns, and survival outcomes in Germany using claims data. Findings indicate rising MCL incidence and prevalence, a high treatment burden and poor survival outcomes, underscoring the need for treatment advancements.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00277-026-06915-3.Keywords: BTK inhibitors, Real-world, Relapsed/refractory MCL

Introduction

Mantle cell lymphoma (MCL) is a rare B-cell non-Hodgkin lymphoma (B-NHL) subtype, accounting for approximately 3–10% of B-NHL in Western countries [1]. Global incidence rates range from 0.1 to 1.27/100,000 individuals [2]. Although some patients have indolent MCL at diagnosis, the majority experience an aggressive disease course and require multiple lines of therapy, usually associated with a poor prognosis [3–10].

Treatment choice for patients with MCL depends on multiple factors, including age and overall health status. Until the early 2020s, standard front-line treatment for younger, fit patients with MCL in Germany was centered around anthracycline- and cytarabine-containing immunochemotherapy, followed by autologous stem cell transplantation (autoSCT) and anti-CD20 antibody (rituximab) maintenance [1, 6–13]. For elderly patients, guideline recommendations included conventional immunochemotherapy followed by rituximab maintenance [11, 12]. In the relapsed or refractory (R/R) setting, the covalent Bruton tyrosine kinase inhibitor (cBTKi) ibrutinib became the standard of care (SOC) [13–18]. However, the duration of response to cBTKi therapy was often limited, and treatment discontinuation because of toxicity or intolerance remained a challenge [19–23]. No clear SOC existed for patients who progressed on or discontinued cBTKi therapy [18, 24–31], and prognosis remained particularly poor [32].

Recent therapeutic advances have begun reshaping the MCL treatment landscape. In the R/R setting, novel agents such as the CD19-directed chimeric antigen receptor T-cell (CAR-T) therapy brexucabtagene autoleucel (approved in late 2020) and the non-covalent BTKi pirtobrutinib (approved in 2023) have emerged [33, 34]. Additionally, off-label use of ibrutinib in the first-line setting and findings from the TRIANGLE trial have begun to challenge the role of autoSCT in younger patients [35].

This study aims to characterize MCL care in Germany prior to these recent therapeutic advances to help contextualize their potential impact and provide a foundation for future research. Although real-world data analyses are essential for capturing epidemiological trends and treatment patterns in routine care [36, 37], evidence on MCL epidemiology and management approaches in the German real-world setting remains scarce. This study presents a comprehensive analysis of epidemiology, treatment patterns, and overall survival (OS) in patients with MCL receiving anti-cancer therapy in Germany, using administrative claims data up to 2020. As nearly 90% of the German population is insured through the Statutory Healthcare Insurance (SHI) system, these data offer a robust foundation for healthcare and epidemiological research, with only a minor share of services (such as over-the-counter medications and privately funded treatments) excluded [38–41].

Materials & methods

Data source

This retrospective observational study utilized anonymized claims data from a German Sickness Fund Database, covering the period from January 1, 2011, to December 31, 2020. The database comprises longitudinal data on approximately 4.5 million publicly insured German residents, all enrolled with a single type of SHI, the Betriebskrankenkassen (BKK).

Ethical approval for this study was not required since it was based on anonymized claims data. The health insurance companies were informed about the study, and the necessary approvals were granted.

Study objectives

The study aimed to (i) evaluate the annual prevalence and incidence of MCL from 2015 to 2020 within the German BKK Sickness Fund Database and extrapolated to the German SHI population and to describe (ii) patient characteristics, (iii) longitudinal treatment patterns, (iv) the frequency of healthcare visits, and (v) OS in patients with MCL who received anti-cancer treatment between January 1, 2015, and December 31, 2020.

Study populations and statistical analyses

In this study, adult patients in the database with a confirmed diagnosis of MCL between January 1, 2015, and December 31, 2020, were identified. MCL was considered confirmed if two diagnoses (inpatient or outpatient) of MCL (as identified by the International Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems, Tenth Revision, German Modification [ICD-10-GM] C83.1 code) were recorded within a four-quarter period, with the second diagnosis occurring within the three quarters following the quarter of the first diagnosis. This criterion was utilized to increase diagnostic specificity.

Distinct study populations were used for objective (i) and objectives (ii)–(v).

Objective (i)

MCL prevalence and incidence (per 100,000 individuals and individuals at risk, respectively) were assessed annually from 2015 to 2020, stratified by age group and gender. For each study year, all adult individuals in the database—who were continuously insured during both the respective year and the preceding year—with consistent data on age and gender (except in the case of death) were included, and patients with a confirmed MCL diagnosis were identified. An MCL diagnosis was classified as incident if it represented the first recorded diagnosis within the study period. Results were extrapolated to the German SHI population by age and sex using the annual demographic data from the German Federal Ministry of Health. Differences between the sample and the SHI population in age and gender distribution were < 0.7% across all age and gender groups, allowing for unbiased extrapolations. Since the four-quarter confirmation criterion may have led to underestimation of both the prevalence and the incidence of MCL in the final study year (2020), analyses primarily focus on the period 2015–2019.

Objectives (ii) to (v)

Objectives (ii) to (v) were evaluated for three cohorts. Cohort A included all patients with MCL who received anti-cancer treatment (defined as one or more prescription for anti-cancer therapy) between January 1, 2015, and December 31, 2020, and had the initial MCL diagnosis recorded in the database. The initial diagnosis (defined as index event for cohort A) was the first recorded MCL diagnosis during the study period, with ≥ 1 year of baseline data free of MCL diagnosis or anti-cancer treatment. Continuous insurance coverage was required from the first available data record within the study period through the end of follow-up. Cohort B included patients from Cohort A who received a cBTKi, defined as one or more prescription of ibrutinib between January 1, 2015, and December 31, 2020 (as ibrutinib was the only cBTKi approved for MCL in Germany during this period). The first cBTKi prescription was defined as the index event for cohort B.

Cohort C included patients from Cohort B who discontinued cBTKi treatment, defined as no further cBTKi prescriptions, with available post-cBTKi follow-up data. The end of cBTKi treatment (defined as index event for cohort C) was estimated as the date of the last prescription plus the calculated treatment duration based on pack size and defined daily dose.

Results are reported descriptively. Index events were defined separately for each cohort (see the Online Resource).

Patient characteristics, including year of initial MCL diagnosis, age at index, sex, and comorbidities (objective ii), and longitudinal treatment patterns (objective iii) were described for each cohort. Medical treatments were classified into therapy regimens and analyzed by line of treatment (LOT). A new LOT was defined as the start of a new therapy regimen. AutoSCT and rituximab maintenance were considered part of the preceding LOT (see the Online Resource).

Inpatient and outpatient healthcare visits were described over a 1-year period following the index event for each cohort. Inpatient visits and outpatient consultations were considered MCL-related if a primary MCL diagnosis was recorded or a confirmed MCL diagnosis was documented during the respective quarter, respectively.

OS was evaluated for each cohort using Kaplan–Meier methodology. OS was measured from index event until all-cause death. Patients were censored at the end of the study period (December 31, 2020) or at the end of insurance (loss to follow-up), whichever occurred first. All statistical analyses were conducted using SAS Software, version 9.4.

Results

Prevalence and incidence of MCL

The prevalence of MCL (per 100,000 individuals) increased over time from 7.31 (95% CI, 6.47–8.26) in 2015 to 11.32 (95% CI, 10.27–12.47) in 2019. When extrapolated to the German SHI population, the prevalence increased from 6.64 (95% CI, 6.45–6.83) in 2015 to 10.25 (95% CI, 10.02–10.49) in 2019 (Table 1). Across all study years, the prevalence of MCL was more than double in men than in women (Online Resource Table 1). The highest prevalence was observed in the older age groups, particularly those aged 70 years and older. This age group also exhibited the largest increase in prevalence between 2015 and 2019 (Online Resource Fig. 1A).

Incidence generally increased over time, except for in 2016 and 2020. It ranged from 1.43 (95% CI, 1.08–1.89) to 2.27 (95% CI, 1.83–2.82) per 100,000 individuals at risk in the database and from 1.28 (95% CI, 1.20–1.37) to 2.06 (95% CI, 1.96–2.17) per 100,000 individuals at risk in the German SHI population (Table 1). The age distribution of incidence showed an increase in the 80 years and older age group across the years, driving the overall increase in the incidence of MCL between 2015 and 2019 (Online Resource Fig. 1B).

The median age at initial diagnosis was 71 years (Cohort A). The median age was 72 years at the start of cBTKi treatment (Cohort B) and 74 years at cBTKi discontinuation (Cohort C). In all cohorts, the majority of patients were male (> 75%). The average Charlson Comorbidity Index was 2.9 at initial diagnosis (Cohort A), 3.7 at the start of cBTKi (Cohort B), and 4.2 at the time of cBTKi discontinuation (Cohort C).

The most common comorbidity across all cohorts was essential hypertension, with more than 60% of patients affected; disorders of lipoprotein metabolism/other lipidemias (> 30%), type 2 diabetes mellitus (> 25%), and chronic ischemic heart disease (> 20%) were also common (Online Resource Table 3).

MCL treatment patterns

By definition, all patients in Cohort A had received at least one LOT between 2015 and 2020. Of these patients, 52.3% had received two lines, 21.7% had received three lines, and 10.3% had received four lines. Time to treatment for each cohort is shown in Online Resource Table 4.

Of the 369 patients in Cohort A, 285 (77.2%) received chemoimmunotherapy as first-line treatment, 51 (13.8%) proceeded to autoSCT consolidation, and 114 (30.9%) continued first-line treatment with rituximab maintenance (Fig. 1A). Among the 193 patients who received second-line treatment, 43 (22.3%) received cBTKi therapy and 88 (45.6%) received chemoimmunotherapy (Online Resource Table 5).

Of the 82 patients in Cohort B, 43 patients (52.4%) received cBTKi as second-line therapy, 18 patients (22.0%) as third-line therapy, and 13 patients (15.9%) as fourth-line therapy. Figure 1B shows the switches to and from cBTKi treatment, irrespective of the LOT in which it was received. The most common prior treatments before initiating cBTKi were chemoimmunotherapy (39.7%) and rituximab maintenance (27.4%).

Of the patients who received cBTKi (Cohort B), 37 (45.1%) had no follow-up data beyond cBTKi treatment available, either due to death (n = 14; 17.1%) or end of study period (n = 23; 28.0%); 45 (54.9%) patients discontinued cBTKi and had follow-up data available. Of those, 20 patients (24.4% of Cohort B, 44.4% of Cohort C) received further anti-cancer treatment, with chemoimmunotherapy and immunomodulatory agents being the most frequent treatments (chemoimmunotherapy, n = 6; immunomodulatory agent, n = 5; allogeneic SCT, n = 2; B-cell lymphoma 2 inhibitor, n = 2; other, n = 5). The remaining 25 (55.5% of Cohort C) patients received no further systemic anti-cancer treatment.

Overall, the median duration of anti-cancer treatments decreased with treatment line (Online Resource Table 6). Median duration of first-line treatment was 150 days. Median treatment duration of cBTKi was 133.7 days (Cohort B), and median treatment duration of post-cBTKi anti-cancer treatment (Cohort C) was 18.0 days. However, the variance between patients was high (Online Resource Table 6).

Healthcare visits

In the year following diagnosis (Cohort A), nearly all patients (99.7%) had at least one outpatient visit to a non-hospital, office-based practitioner, with a median of 59 visits/patient (Online Resource Table 7). Nearly half (51.5%) of patients had an outpatient consultation at a hospital, with a median of one consultation/patient, and 86.7% of patients had at least one inpatient stay (median four inpatient stays/patient). The median length of inpatient stay was 28 days/patient.

When considering only visits attributed to MCL, 88.9% of patients had at least one outpatient visit to a practitioner (median: 17 visits per patient), 35.8% had an outpatient hospital consultation (median: 0 per patient), and 69.9% had at least one inpatient stay (median: 2 per patient).

In the first year following cBTKi initiation (Cohort B), patients had a median number of 36, 0, and 1 non-hospital outpatient visits, hospital outpatient consultations, and inpatient stays, respectively. In Cohort C, the respective median values were 8, 0, and 1 during the first year post-cBTKi follow-up (Online Resource Table 7).

Survival analyses

Patients in Cohort A had median OS of 75.3 (95% CI, 58.8-NE) months from their initial MCL diagnosis (Fig. 2A). Here, the upper confidence limit was not estimable (NE), indicating that the available follow-up data were insufficient to reliably determine it. In Cohort B, median OS after cBTKi initiation was 11.2 (95% CI, 7.6–42.8) months (Fig. 2B). For patients in Cohort C, median OS from cBTKi discontinuation was 3.0 (95% CI, 1.9–15.6) months (Fig. 2C).

Discussion

This study provides a comprehensive analysis of the real-world treatment landscape for patients with MCL in Germany, utilizing administrative claims data from 2011 to 2020. To our knowledge, this is the first population-level evaluation of MCL epidemiology, treatment patterns, and survival outcomes in Germany. Claims data serve as a valuable resource, as MCL typically necessitates continuous medical care, which is fully reimbursed by the German SHI system. This ensures comprehensive capture of MCL-related healthcare services across all care sectors, generating longitudinal data and offering a representative overview of MCL management within the SHI-covered population, which accounts for approximately 90% of the German population [42–45].

Consistent with previous reports, MCL incidence and prevalence increased with age, peaking in patients aged ≥ 70 years, and were higher in males than females (male-to-female ratio: 2.0–2.8:1) [2, 46, 47]. The median age at initial diagnosis (71 years) aligns with findings from the literature [2]. Furthermore, increasing incidence and prevalence over time—particularly among elderly patients—was observed from 2015 to 2019, a trend also reported in other countries, including the USA, Sweden, and Denmark [2, 48]. This underscores the growing clinical burden of MCL among the elderly, highlighting the importance of therapeutic strategies that consider age-related comorbidities and frailty [49, 50].

Although SCHOLAR-2 provides a European benchmark for patients in whom cBTKi therapy failed [32], this study provides a broader perspective by examining all patients with MCL undergoing systemic treatment in Germany during this period. In line with guideline recommendations at the time [12], most patients (77.2%) received chemoimmunotherapy as first-line therapy, with 30.9% continuing on rituximab maintenance. Only 13.8% of patients underwent autoSCT consolidation, which had a long-standing role as SOC for fit patients in first-line treatment [11]. This possibly reflects the advanced age and multiple comorbidities of the patient population and is similar to findings in other countries, where a significant proportion of patients with MCL are ineligible for intensive treatment regimens [51]. Metabolic disorders were among the most common comorbidities, indicating a high cardiovascular risk profile, which may pose clinical limitations on the use of cBTKi [52].

In the second line, most patients received chemoimmunotherapy (45.6%), whereas 22.3% were treated with a cBTKi. Although no real-world data from Germany are currently available for comparison, a US-based retrospective cohort study [53] using the Flatiron Health electronic health record-derived database (2011–2021) similarly reported chemoimmunotherapy as the predominant second-line regimen (52%), with 37% of patients receiving a cBTKi. Notably, although ibrutinib was the only cBTKi approved for MCL in Germany during the study period, the US study also included acalabrutinib and zanubrutinib.

Among patients receiving cBTKi in the current study, the majority received it as monotherapy (89.5%); 22.0% initiated cBTKi treatment in the third line and 15.9% in the fourth line, aligning with SCHOLAR-2. In line with the literature, treatment patterns showed that ~ 44% of patients who discontinued cBTKi therapy received subsequent anti-cancer therapy; in addition, treatment duration decreased with treatment line and was particularly short following cBTKi discontinuation [32, 54]. The analysis of healthcare visits revealed that MCL management required extensive medical care, with frequent outpatient visits to non-hospital-based practitioners, and significant inpatient treatment, underscoring the complexity of disease management and patients’ ongoing medical needs.

The median OS from initial MCL diagnosis was approximately 6.3 years, closely aligning with the 6.7-year OS reported in a Finnish/Spanish cohort study [51]. Survival outcomes for patients who received cBTKi (~ 75% received as second or third line) were poor, with a median OS of 11.2 months from cBTKi initiation, similar to SCHOLAR-2 (14.6 months). Post-cBTKi survival outcomes were particularly poor, with a median OS of 3.0 months from cBTKi discontinuation; however, these estimates should be interpreted with caution due to the small sample size in this cohort. Although multiple studies have highlighted the substantial unmet need in MCL, particularly for patients with R/R disease following cBTKi failure [2, 32, 47, 51, 55], our results align with these findings and offer additional insights into the treatment landscape in Germany.

Importantly, since the study period (2011–2020), significant therapeutic advancements, including brexu-cel, acalabrutinib, and pirtobrutinib, have emerged [18, 29, 34, 56–58]. CAR-T therapy has demonstrated considerable efficacy in patients with R/R MCL [29]. However, its wide application remains challenging [59]. Pirtobrutinib has demonstrated favorable efficacy among patients with MCL who had prior cBTKi treatment, and it showed good tolerability and a favorable safety profile [33, 34, 60]. Moreover, the recently published results of the phase III TRIANGLE study have challenged the previous SOC for younger, fit patients with MCL by demonstrating that incorporation of ibrutinib into first-line treatment during induction and maintenance overcomes the need for autoSCT [35, 61]. These innovations have begun to reshape clinical practice in Germany.

By offering a comprehensive overview of the epidemiology, treatment patterns, and survival outcomes of MCL in routine care in Germany before the introduction of these therapeutic advancements, this study establishes a foundation for future research on the German MCL treatment landscape. Further real-world studies will be essential to evaluate how these new treatment options shape clinical practice and patient outcomes.

Limitations

This study is based on claims data, which is limited to individuals insured by the German SHI. Consequently, inherent limitations of claims-based data constrain interpretation of the findings, and the results may not be fully generalizable to other healthcare systems or the private sector, where treatment practices and access to therapies may differ.

Claims data do not include detailed clinical information such as disease stage, performance status, or biomarker profiles, resulting in unmeasured confounding that may influence treatment decisions, healthcare utilization, and survival outcomes. Claims data may also be subject to coding variability. Treatment lines and durations were reconstructed from prescriptions and therefore represent approximations rather than exact clinical sequences. Because claims data do not capture clinical intent, reasons for treatment changes, or regimen details, misclassification of therapy lines and inaccuracies in estimated treatment durations are possible. In addition, the required 1-year diagnosis-free baseline period, beginning January 1, 2011, may have resulted in incomplete capture of treatments and diagnoses occurring prior to 2011, potentially affecting the completeness of longitudinal treatment histories in the early study years. Attribution of healthcare visits to MCL based solely on primary diagnosis codes or confirmed diagnoses within a quarter may also be imprecise, and some degree of misclassification cannot be excluded.

Censoring at the boundaries of the observation period introduces several constraints for both incidence and survival analyses. Patients diagnosed near the beginning of the study period may not have had sufficient observable history to meet the mandatory 1-year diagnosis-free baseline, which may contribute to underestimation of incidence in the early study years. Conversely, for patients diagnosed near the end of the observation period there may not have been enough time to complete the four-quarter confirmation window requiring two MCL diagnoses in separate quarters. Although this criterion increases diagnostic specificity, its interaction with limited observable time may result in underestimation of incidence in the final study year. Survival estimation may be similarly affected: right-censoring may reduce the precision of survival estimates. Additionally, informative censoring cannot be excluded and small sample sizes—especially in the post-cBTKi cohort—may limit the precision and generalizability of survival results. As no comparative analyses between treatment subgroups were undertaken, the survival findings are purely descriptive and should not be interpreted as evidence of comparative effectiveness or causality.

A source of selection bias may arise from the inclusion of only treated patients in the study cohorts; individuals who remained untreated—whether managed with watch-and-wait strategies or not initiating treatment for other reasons—were not captured. This may limit the generalizability of the study population to the broader MCL population.

These limitations should be taken into account when interpreting treatment patterns, healthcare utilization, and survival outcomes, although the observed treatment patterns align with published real-world evidence [53–55].

Conclusions

This study provides the first comprehensive analysis of MCL epidemiology, treatment patterns, and survival outcomes in Germany using claims data, providing valuable real-world insights into routine clinical practice. The findings underscore the substantial treatment burden of MCL and the significant unmet need, particularly among patients with R/R disease following cBTKi discontinuation. As novel therapies begin to reshape the MCL treatment landscape, further real-world studies will be essential to evaluate their impact on treatment patterns and patient outcomes.
