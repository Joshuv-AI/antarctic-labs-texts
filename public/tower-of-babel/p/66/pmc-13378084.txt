Idiopathic Iliopsoas Bursitis: Diagnostic Utility of Aspiration and Culture
Authors: Yuki Asada, Ryuichi Minoda Sada, Yuria Umemura, Koichi Yamamoto
Published: 2026

A 78‐year‐old man without immunosuppression or other significant medical conditions experienced a sudden, stabbing pain in the right groin while moving his leg 1 month before presentation. The pain was relieved at rest and exacerbated by leg movement. He visited a local clinic, where no cause was identified; despite persistent pain, he remained active. Two weeks prior to presentation, he noticed swelling of the right lower extremity and revisited the clinic. He had no history of fever or weight loss. On arrival, he was alert and afebrile, and his vital signs were stable. Physical examination revealed right groin pain without erythema or local warmth. Deep palpation of the right inguinal region revealed a tender, deep‐seated mass‐like lesion without overlying skin changes. Hip flexion was limited by pain, interfering with activities of daily living such as putting on trousers. The psoas sign was negative, and he was able to bear weight. Cardiac auscultation revealed no murmurs. Laboratory tests showed a white blood cell count of 9700/μL and a C‐reactive protein level of 3.28 mg/dL. Rheumatoid factor and anti‐cyclic citrullinated peptide antibodies were both negative.

Contrast‐enhanced computed tomography (CT) revealed a low‐attenuation fluid collection with rim enhancement around the right hip joint, raising suspicion of a psoas abscess (Figure 1). Because unilateral lower leg swelling was observed, contrast‐enhanced CT of the chest, abdomen, pelvis, and lower extremities showed no evidence of deep vein thrombosis or neoplastic lesions. The patient was admitted for further evaluation, and blood cultures were negative. Ultrasound‐guided bursal aspiration was performed by the interventional radiology team to identify the causative organism. No findings suggestive of crystal deposition were observed on ultrasonographic examination. The aspirated fluid was pale yellow and serous, and both routine bacterial cultures and acid‐fast bacilli tests were negative. Due to the limited volume of the specimen, polarized light microscopy could not be performed to assess for crystal deposition. As an infectious etiology was considered unlikely, antibiotics were withheld, and conservative treatment with oral nonsteroidal anti‐inflammatory drugs was initiated, resulting in rapid symptom improvement. The patient was ultimately considered to have idiopathic iliopsoas bursitis after exclusion of infectious, rheumatologic, and other potential causes. Follow‐up ultrasonography performed approximately 3 weeks after discharge showed complete resolution of the fluid collection and no evidence of calcification from the right iliac region to the proximal thigh (Figure 2), suggesting that crystal‐induced bursitis, including CPPD, was unlikely.

Bursitis is an inflammatory condition affecting bursae throughout the body and causes pain during movement of surrounding muscles and tendons. The iliopsoas bursa is located between the iliofemoral and pubofemoral ligaments and facilitates smooth gliding of the iliopsoas muscle; therefore, inflammation can present as groin pain and may be difficult to distinguish from a psoas abscess [1]. When rim‐enhancing fluid collections are observed on contrast‐enhanced CT, differentiation between infectious and non‐infectious lesions based solely on imaging findings is challenging, as rim enhancement is not specific for infection. Approximately 10% of bursitis cases are reported to be infectious [2], demonstrating the importance of culture studies in the differential diagnosis. Image‐guided aspiration is an effective method for obtaining diagnostic specimens [3]. Ultrasonography, as a real‐time imaging modality, is particularly useful for guiding aspiration, with the potential to improve diagnostic accuracy and reduce false‐negative results [4]. A cross‐sectional study conducted in primary healthcare hospitals in southwestern China reported that only 8.7% of antibiotic prescriptions were considered appropriate [5]. This case highlights the need to prioritize aspiration culture over imaging findings alone to avoid unnecessary antibiotic use.
