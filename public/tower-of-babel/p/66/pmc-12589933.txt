Advancing Wrist‐Worn Accelerometry for Measurement of Infant Motor Behavior
Authors: Catherine E Lang, Jana M Iverson, Jeffery D Konrad, Audrey S Lorence, Izza Choudhry, Marghuretta D Bland, Meghan Campbell, Stormi Pulver White, Dorothy H Balser, Motunrola B Adebogun, Nailah Islam, Moira L Pileggi, Savannah C Davis, Madeline S Dieffenbacher, Keith R Lohse, Natasha Marrus
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTWearable sensor technology, particularly accelerometry, is an emerging tool to measure infant motor behavior. We evaluated (1) differences and relationships between accelerometry‐derived variables in structured versus unstructured contexts and (2) the convergent validity of accelerometer‐derived variables with coded motor behavior during structured motor tasks and with caregiver reports of infant motor behavior. Typically developing infants (n = 176, 6 months adjusted age) were evaluated via bilateral wrist‐worn accelerometers during structured, laboratory toy play tasks and at home for 2 days, unstructured. Eleven accelerometer variables quantifying duration, intensity, and variability of movement were extracted from each context and analyzed with respect to five variables from the video‐coded structured toy play task and caregiver report (Ages and Stages Questionnaire motor subscales). Infants and parents tolerated the devices as indicated by a usable data rate equivalent to the questionnaire response rate. Accelerometry values from the structured context were generally larger than those from the unstructured context, except for the variance variables. Values across the contexts were moderately and consistently related. Accelerometry variables were related to video‐coded variables but were only minimally related to caregiver reports of fine motor behavior. Upper limb accelerometry is a feasible, valid addition to the infant motor measurement toolbox.Keywords: infant, measurement, motor behavior, movement, upper limb, wearable sensors

Introduction

Movement is one of the earliest forms of human communication, and limb movements are one of the earliest outputs of the developing central nervous system (Cainelli et al. 2025). Measurement of motor behavior in the first years of life is important for research on typical and atypical development and for clinical care, as it can inform assessment and intervention. There is a range of current tools used to measure motor behavior, all of which have strengths and drawbacks. Caregiver report is quick and economical but subject to various biases (e.g., overly positive reporting, demographic characteristics) (Rice et al. 2013, Zheng et al. 2024, Yepez et al. 2024). Clinical assessments can provide detailed information about motor capabilities; these assessments, however, can be time‐consuming, require a trained/licensed professional for administration, and often have age ranges that necessitate switching tools as children age. In the research space, much has been learned from video‐recorded and video‐coded laboratory tasks (Corbetta and Thelen 1996, Koterba et al. 2014), but these are extremely time‐ and labor‐intensive and challenging to scale beyond the research realm. Further, while such assessments are important for demonstrating motor capabilities in a specific context and setting, they do not necessarily describe an individual's motor behavior in the day‐to‐day environment. The increased availability of wearable movement sensors, particularly accelerometers, suggests these devices may be a scalable option for feasibly and efficiently quantifying a range of movement behaviors starting in the first year of life and extending through adulthood.

The development of accelerometry methods and applications to pediatric populations is still in the early stages but is increasing rapidly (Lang et al. 2024, Ricardo et al. 2018). A strength of accelerometry is that it can provide an objective, quantitative, continuous measurement over extended durations (i.e., hours or days instead of minutes) in an individual's natural environment (Hoyt et al. 2020, Lang et al. 2020). Accelerometry in early childhood could yield multidimensional data that could be relatively easy to collect over time, and has the potential to detect subtle objective markers correlated with risk status and/or emerging delays. While many pediatric accelerometry applications have been focused on general physical activity, there are growing options for quantifying other characteristics of movement behavior, particularly upper limb behavior (Lang et al. 2024, J. Konrad et al. 2022, J. D. Konrad et al. 2024, Shida‐Tokeshi et al. 2018, Airaksinen et al. 2022, Saudino 2012). We focus here on accelerometer measures of the upper limbs because upper limb component movements (i.e., reach, grasp, manipulate, release) remain similar throughout life and upper limb motor skills emerge in the first few months of life. This allows sensor‐based tracking and the variables derived from sensors on the upper limbs to be used across the lifespan (Lang et al. 2024). For the promise of accelerometry as a tool in developmental research and clinical care to be realized, more work is required. It is critical to investigate two key gaps related to accelerometry: (1) the influence of context on the recorded data and variables extracted and (2) relationships of the variables extracted with other types of measures reflecting aspects of motor function.

The first research gap is the context in which the accelerometry is recorded. Accelerometry can yield variables derived from structured contexts, such as standardized activities in a clinic or laboratory, or from unstructured contexts such as the home and community environments (J. D. Konrad et al. 2024, Saudino 2012). Differentiating between these two contexts is important because behavior in a laboratory, which may provide conditions intended to optimize performance on a specific task or activity, may not generalize to behavior in the real world, especially during development (Lang et al. 2020, Iverson et al. 2023). A study in 2‐ to 3‐year‐old twins demonstrated that overall motor activity level indexed with accelerometry at both the wrist and the ankle was higher in the structured context of the laboratory compared to the unstructured context of home, with a correlation between contexts of 0.3–0.4 (Saudino 2012, Saudino and Asherson 2013). These results suggest that toddler motor behavior in structured contexts may not be fully representative of behavior at home. In another study in children ages 6–12, multiple wrist accelerometry variables from the structured context (laboratory visit) were more closely related to overall motor competence (as measured by the Movement Assessment Battery for Children v2 [Wuang et al. 2012]) compared to the unstructured context (at home) (J. D. Konrad et al. 2024). These results suggest that school‐aged motor behavior in structured contexts may be a closer match to motor abilities than motor behavior in unstructured contexts. Behaviors from unstructured contexts might be particularly useful to quantify and understand from a developmental perspective, as skills emerge and provide a foundation for advancing abilities (Iverson et al. 2023). It is plausible that the influence of context on accelerometry recordings could vary by age. Thus, here we evaluate how context influences accelerometry variables in infants, who have a much smaller repertoire of possible motor behaviors and daily activities compared to toddlers and school‐aged children.

A second research gap in the accelerometry literature is the current understanding of how accelerometer variables relate to other measures of motor behavior, especially in infants. Accelerometry can measure unique aspects of movement not detected with standardized assessments. For example, variables derived from accelerometry can quantify the duration, intensity, and smoothness of movement throughout the day (Lang et al. 2024). While accelerometry can provide a direct quantification of movement in the infant's own environment, it does not measure the acquisition of motor skills (e.g., rolling, sitting, reaching) that are quantified via clinical assessments. The complementary information gained from accelerometry can enhance our understanding of infant development, when signs of atypical motor development may first appear. While a growing number of studies support a variety of feasible approaches for accelerometry in infants, investigations of relationships between accelerometry and motor behavioral or developmental features in infants have been relatively limited. A 2012 investigation showed that caregiver report of activity level was related to the unstructured (at‐home) accelerometry measures of overall activity level, with r = 0.30 (Saudino and Eaton 1991). Only a few other investigations have extended beyond the quantification of overall activity‐level measures. In one report, unstructured wrist accelerometry variables quantifying duration and intensity of arm movement were shown to relate to clinical rating scales of motor and nonmotor development in a small, longitudinal sample of infants (Shida‐Tokeshi et al. 2018). Another group has used a custom wearable jumpsuit with arm sensors to detect infant movements and postures in structured (Airaksinen et al. 2020) and unstructured contexts (Airaksinen et al. 2022), with variables extracted showing relationships with age, growth, clinical assessments, and caregiver report (Airaksinen et al. 2022, 2023). This small number of papers with complementary purposes and methods shows the great promise of wearables while simultaneously highlighting the value of multiple approaches and the tremendous need to learn more.

Our overall goal is to investigate low‐cost, scalable, in‐home accelerometry measurement as a means by which to reliably capture critical indices of variation in infant motor behavior. Using an ongoing observational cohort of infant twins evaluated around 6 months of age, the purposes of this report were to evaluate (1) differences and relationships between accelerometry‐derived variables in structured versus unstructured contexts and (2) the convergent validity of accelerometer‐derived variables with simultaneously coded motor behavior in structured motor tasks (Koterba et al. 2014) and with caregiver reports of infant motor behavior. We hypothesized that (1) accelerometry variables would have greater magnitudes in the structured context, since infants are intentionally engaged in movement play; (2) accelerometry variables from the two contexts would be modestly related, consistent with measurements across contexts in motor and other domains; (Rice et al. 2013, Sanchez and Constantino 2020, B. N. Wilson et al. 2009, Zysset et al. 2018, Agarwal et al. 2024); and (3) small to moderate correlations would exist between the three measurement types, also consistent with the pediatric literature (Gudmundsson 2015, Sulek et al. 2022). This report extends previous work measuring overall activity level (Saudino 2012, Saudino and Asherson 2013, Saudino and Eaton 1991, Ilott et al. 2010, Bruijns et al. 2020) to measures of multiple movement characteristics related to duration, intensity, and complexity (Lang et al. 2024). Further, we deployed commercially available devices to derive variables that are not limited to measurement in infancy or toddlerhood and do not rely on machine learning classification of specific movements and postures (Airaksinen et al. 2022, Airaksinen et al. 2020, Airaksinen et al. 2023), which change over development. The goal of the analyses presented here is to help advance accelerometry as a measurement tool for a broad range of infant neurodevelopmental research studies and ultimately for clinical care.

Methods

This report is an analysis of infant motor behavior collected during the Wearable Accelerometry To CHaracterize Motor Development (WATCH) study. The larger WATCH study is a prospective longitudinal cohort examining the heritability, stability, and potential predictive validity of infant motor, social, and attentional metrics for Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD)‐related outcomes in toddlers. Data in this report were obtained from community‐ascertained twin pairs who participated in the initial assessment at 6 months of age between July 2021 and September 2024. The 6‐month assessment data from the WATCH study used were developmental questionnaires completed electronically by caregivers, in‐person motor play tasks conducted in the laboratory, and bilateral, wrist‐worn accelerometer recordings for two consecutive days. This report includes data from 176 infants with successful collection of at least one type of data available, that is, questionnaires, accelerometry, or video‐coded motor play tasks. Note that participants were recruited in twin pairs, but the twin aspect of the design is not a focus of the current report, and statistical dependence of twin pairs was accounted for in the analyses.

Participants

Participants were recruited at two sites, WashU Medicine in St. Louis, MO, and Emory University/Marcus Autism Center in Atlanta, GA. Recruitment sources included university‐affiliated obstetrics, newborn, and pediatric services; community pediatricians; area child‐care centers; social media (e.g., Facebook ads); and word of mouth. Healthy twin pairs were included in the 6‐month assessment if they were between 5.5 and 7.5 months of age, adjusted for gestational age, and had at least one adult primary caregiver who was a biological parent of the twins, was over 18 years of age, and spoke English as the primary language. Twin pairs were excluded if they had (1) a known medical condition (including neurologic or genetic syndromes) that could significantly impact motor function; (2) extreme prematurity, as indicated by a gestation age <30 weeks; (3) birth weight <1500 g in either twin; or (4) twin–twin transfusion syndrome that required surgery. This study was approved by the Washington University Human Research Protection Office, using a single IRB across sites. Informed consent was obtained from an adult birth parent (typically the adult primary caregiver) via a verbal consent initially, followed by an electronic written consent administered through REDCap (Harris et al. 2009, Harris et al. 2019, Lawrence et al. 2020).

Study Questionnaires

The adult primary caregiver completed the sociodemographic inventory, pregnancy and birth medical history, and developmental questionnaires. The Ages and Stages Questionnaire (ASQ) Version 3 is a 38‐item, multiple‐choice, developmental milestone screening survey across five domains; it was used here to assess developmental progress in fine and gross motor abilities (Squires et al. 2009). Because it was a twin population, we attempted to control for caregivers scoring twins exactly the same by separating the timing of the questionnaires for each twin by at least 1 week. Caregivers completed the age‐appropriate version of the form around the time of the visit, which accounted for gestational age; age‐normed standardized scores were used in analyses. Items were scored by the caregiver as Not Yet, Sometimes, or Yes Observed, for a possible range of scores from 0 to 60 (higher = better). The Fine Motor Skills subscale (six items) was of primary interest for this analysis, but we also examined relationships with the Gross Motor Skills subscale (six items). The fine motor items at 6 months are (1) grabbing and holding an offered toy for ∼1 min; (2) reaching or grasping bilaterally; (3) reaching for and touching a crumb or cheerio; (4) picking up a small object with a gross grasp; (5) attempting to pick up a crumb or cheerio with a raking movement; and (6) picking up a small toy with only one hand.

Toy Play Task and Video‐Coded Variables

The “caregiver–infant toy play task” assessed the development of manual exploratory behaviors via spontaneous use of the hands for object exploration (Koterba et al. 2014). Infants and a primary caregiver were observed in a quiet room in the laboratory. The infant sat in a supportive chair to optimize trunk stability at a rectangular table; the table edge was parallel and adjacent to the infant's chest. A nonslip material was taped down to the table to prevent toys from sliding away. The caregiver sat on the side of the table at a right angle to the infant. Three age‐appropriate toys were placed on the table surface: a plastic ring stacking toy, a textured ball, and a larger activity ball containing rattles and rings. The caregiver was given general instructions to play with the infant “as they would at home” for 10 min. The interaction was recorded from the frontal and lateral views, which were later synchronized offline. To allow for warm‐up and potential loss of interest with time, the middle 5 min of the 10‐min recording were used for coding. After the 10‐min caregiver play period, the assessor returned to the room and engaged with the infant in additional play with a range of objects (Fagard and Peze 1997, Kimmerle et al. 2010) for a total play time of around 30 min.

Six primary observers from Boston University completed coding for this study using Datavyu (Datavyu.org), a software that allows for identification and annotation of user‐defined behaviors. Prior to initiating coding, coders were trained via an iterative process to a criterion of at least 85% agreement on three consecutive videos. Following training, a primary coder scored 100% of each infant's video data, and a second coder independently scored 25% of each video (during a randomly chosen time window) to assess interrater reliability. Disagreements were resolved through discussion.

Two target behaviors were coded: (1) touching—the infant's fingers were in contact with a toy, and (2) grasping—the infant's fingers were curled around the object, with at least one corner of the object lifted off the table. If infants touched a toy and then went on to grasp it, it was coded as a grasp and not a touch. Each touch or grasp of an object was considered an engagement with an object; onset and offset times were identified on a frame‐by‐frame basis (30 Hz resolution). Engagements were further coded as unilateral (infant engaged with one object with one hand) or bilateral (infant engaged with one object with both hands or two objects with two hands). The bilateral variables (both engagements [touches] and grasping) included instances of both bilateral interaction with one object and simultaneous bilateral interaction with two objects because both represent prerequisite object exploration skills that underlie the development of role‐differentiation bilateral manipulation (Taylor et al. 2024). Interobserver reliability, based on agreement between coders prior to discussion, was high for identifying onsets and offsets of engagements (94%) and for identifying engagement type (touch vs. grasp, 87%) and hand (unilateral vs. bilateral, 94%).

Key variables derived from the observational coding quantified the amount of spontaneous, object‐directed activity (engagement count and duration) and fine motor skills (grasping, bilateral engagement, and bilateral grasping). These variables are shown in the top half of Table 1. Consistent with increasing motor control demands, bilateral grasping was considered more advanced than unilateral grasping (Corbetta and Thelen 1996, Goldfield and Michel 1986).

Accelerometry Data Collection and Variables

The wearable devices used were the GT9X‐Link Activity Monitors (Actigraph, Pensacola, FL). These devices have three‐axis, solid‐state accelerometers with a dynamic range of ±8 gravitational units. Infants wore the devices on both wrists, just above the radial and ulnar styloid processes. Sampling rate was set at 30 Hz. A research team member placed the devices on the infants at the start of the in‐person visit. A snug but comfortable fit was achieved via reinforced, coated paper or plastic, disposable bands. Families were asked to have their child wear the devices for two consecutive days (Ricardo et al. 2018). The devices are waterproof, but caregivers were permitted to remove the devices for bathing or for changing dirty or wet wristbands. During the two consecutive wearing days, caregivers completed an hourly log to mark any removal of the devices (e.g., bath time), infant sleep times (naps and nighttime sleep), and any other events that might affect the recordings; caregivers wrote in the activities in an open‐ended manner and did not use a closed‐ended list to prompt them. Other events that might affect the recordings, as noted by caregivers, include times when an infant was in a stroller, swing, and so forth. Devices and logs were returned to the study team after the wearing period, with shipping materials provided to the caregiver; dropped off by the caregiver or a family member; or picked up from the participant's home.

Once devices were returned to the lab, data were downloaded using ActiLife 6 software (Actigraph, Pensacola, FL), plotted, and visually inspected to confirm wearing time. We extracted the recorded 30‐Hz data, along with downsampled 1‐Hz data. The 30‐Hz data were bandpass filtered between 0.2 and 12 Hz to remove constant linear acceleration components, such as gravity and nonhuman accelerations (e.g., riding in a car or elevator) (Winter 1990, Robertson et al. 2014). Similarly, via Actigraph's proprietary software, the 1‐Hz data were bandpass filtered between 0.25 and 2.5 Hz and converted to activity counts, where each 1‐Hz frame equates to the sum of the activity over the 1‐s epoch (Neishabouri et al. 2022). Accelerometer data from each axis were then transformed into vector magnitudes by taking the square root of (x 2 + y 2 + z 2).

Accelerometry data were separated into (1) structured recordings, taken during the in‐person play tasks, and (2) unstructured recordings, taken from the end of the last in‐person assessment on Day 1 through the end of the wearing period on Day 2, with sleep time excluded. Sleep time was determined from the sensor worn on the participant's left wrist using the Sadeh method (Sadeh et al. 1994), with additional adjustments for infants as described by Schoch et al. (2019). Overall accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity of the algorithm in comparison to caregiver logs were 0.88, 0.89, and 0.87, respectively. Additionally, any non‐wearing times noted on the log were also removed. Stroller, swing, and bouncer time were infrequent within individuals and across the sample and therefore were not removed. Times when infants were carried by other people were not logged and thus could not be removed. All 11 accelerometer variables were then calculated from the time‐series vector magnitude from both the structured and unstructured recordings. For the variables from the structured recordings, we used the full duration of the motor tasks, which typically ran 25–30 min. The full duration was used because the accelerometer variables tended to be highly unstable over only 5–10 min of recording (inadequate sampling of arm movement to sample behavior); durations of >20 min produced stable values.

Extracted accelerometry variables quantified various aspects of upper limb movement as described previously (Lang et al. 2024, J. Konrad et al. 2022, J. D. Konrad et al. 2024, Barth et al. 2021, Miller et al. 2024). Several variables calculated here are a conceptual match to previous literature quantifying activity duration and intensity (Shida‐Tokeshi et al. 2018) but do not rely on the computational challenges of using machine learning techniques to define and label bouts of movement from heterogeneous behaviors. Eleven variables are included (Table 1, bottom half), measuring duration, intensity, and complexity of motor behaviors. Duration variables quantify the amount of time that one or both limbs are moving. Note that the threshold for determining movement versus no movement (≤2 activity counts or ∼0.003328 gravitational units based on the filtering used here) was validated in adults with stroke, individuals who tend to accelerate slowly (Uswatte et al. 2005, 2000). Simultaneous infant accelerometer versus video data from others suggest that this threshold may be reasonable in infants also, where accelerations rapidly exceed 1.0 gravitational unit (1 m/s2) as infants develop reaching skills (Trujillo‐Priego et al. 2017). Within our data, the range of accelerations for most infants spanned from 0 to 200 or 300 activity counts, with wider ranges during intended play and slightly narrower ranges at home. Until the threshold has been validated in infants specifically, values here should be considered estimates of movement versus no movement, not absolutes. Intensity variables quantify the magnitude of the movement accelerations for one or both limbs, where faster movements of the upper limb generally produce higher values. Complexity variables form a heterogeneous category that quantifies different aspects of the variability (e.g., in magnitude or in frequency) of the acceleration data. Duration variables are presented here as a percentage of the recording time for comparison between contexts. Both left‐ and right‐sided data were analyzed to look for consistency across limbs and because infants at this age do not yet show hand dominance. Jerk and the variance of the frequency spectrum required greater time resolution and were derived from 30‐Hz data. All other variables were computed from the 1‐Hz time‐series data.

Statistical Analyses

Statistical analyses were performed in the R environment version 4.3.2 (R Core Team 2023, Wickham et al. 2019). Descriptive statistics were computed for demographics, questionnaires, toy play video codes, and accelerometry variables. More data were available for the accelerometry and questionnaires, given the additional time required to code task‐based data (around 50% of available videos coded) (see Tables 2 and 3). The success rate for usable data collection was 86% and 92% for the ASQ and accelerometry, respectively.

Our first purpose was to evaluate differences and relationships between accelerometry variables in structured versus unstructured contexts. To do this, we ran a series of mixed‐effect models (Bates et al. 2015) with random intercepts of participant and family to account for the within‐subject nature of the context variable (structured versus unstructured) and for participants nested within families (Lohse et al. 2023). These models calculated differences between contexts using the Satterthwaite approximation to the degrees of freedom (Kuznetsova et al. 2017).

Next, for each accelerometry measure, we calculated the correlations between structured and unstructured contexts. However, because twins are nested within families, we included a random intercept of family to account for the statistical dependency between pairs. Accounting for this statistical dependency yields more accurate estimates of the r‐ and p‐values; that is, families are treated as a random sample of possible families, and individual errors are now independent after family‐specific variance has been removed (Raudenbush et al. 1995, Snijders 1995). Z‐transformed pairs of variables from each recording context were regressed onto each other, with a random deviate for each family and a slope that can be interpreted equivalently to a Pearson correlation coefficient (i.e., the standardized change in Y for a 1‐standard deviation change in X). In the main results, we present correlations and p‐values that have been adjusted for the random intercept of family. We recognize that this calculation may be less familiar/intuitive to many readers; therefore, for comparison, we also present unadjusted Pearson correlations and confidence intervals in Table SI.

Our second purpose was to evaluate the convergent validity of accelerometer variables with video‐coded variables from the caregiver–infant toy play task and with caregiver‐reported Ages and Stages Fine and Gross Motor Skill subscales. Using the same analytic approach as above, we calculated correlations between accelerometry variables (structured and unstructured) and video codes of the toy play task (which are taken concurrently with the structured accelerometry recording) and between accelerometry variables and caregiver‐reported behavior on the ASQ. Given our large sample size and the roughly equal nature of the intervals on the ASQ, our models are sufficiently robust to treat these outcomes as interval data (Bishara and Hittner 2012). Adjusted r‐ and p‐values accounting for the random effect of family are reported in the text; unadjusted Pearson correlations and 95% confidence intervals are given in Tables SII–SV.

For all analyses, alpha was set to 0.05, and no adjustment for multiple comparisons was made across analyses. Note that (1) all analyses are reported, so the proportion of statistically significant results is clear, and interested readers can be more conservative at either the family‐wise or experiment‐wide level; and (2) given the early‐phase nature of this exploratory work, we are first interested in the magnitude of effect sizes and concerned about “misses” (i.e., decreasing the alpha level would necessarily increase the likelihood of Type II errors). The associations presented here can inform the design of future work for a range of situations, depending on the specific accelerometry, toy play, or caregiver report measures in which other researchers are interested.

Results

The overall sample included 176 infants. The demographics of the sample are provided in Table 2. Participants were largely White and non‐Hispanic from middle‐income homes with college‐educated caregivers. Demographically, we did see differences between study sites with respect to race (p < 0.001), household earnings (p < 0.001), maternal education (p = 0.002), and paternal education (p = 0.002). We generally found no significant differences (p > 0.05) between sites with respect to our motor behavior variables: unstructured accelerometer, structured accelerometer, video‐coded toy play, or Ages and Stages variables. Only one of the structured accelerometer variables, the right variance of the frequency spectrum, was different between sites (p = 0.023), and two of the unstructured variables, the left and right variance of the frequency spectrum, were different between sites (p = 0.005 and p = 0.042). Descriptive statistics for the variables, including the number of available participants, are provided in Table 3. The highest numbers of available participant data were for the accelerometry variables, an intensive measurement that can be obtained mostly passively. The average accelerometry recording times were 32 ± 9 and 647 ± 90 min/day (sleep and non‐wearing time removed) for the structured and unstructured contexts, respectively. Thus, duration variables are presented as a proportion of the recording time.

Accelerometry Variables From the Structured Versus Unstructured Contexts

Boxplots in Figure 1 compare each accelerometer variable in the structured and unstructured contexts, with estimates of the differences and p‐values in the upper right corners of each panel. Accelerometry values from the structured context were generally larger than those from the unstructured context, except for the left and right variance variables (Figure 1C), which showed the opposite pattern (i.e., variances were larger in the unstructured context), and the left‐side variance of the frequency spectrum (Figure 1E, left panel), which showed a negative but not statistically significant difference (p = 0.131). Visual inspection of Figure 1 suggests that the range of values is broader in the structured condition compared to the unstructured condition. It is likely that this arises from the smaller sampling time in the structured context (∼30 min [∼1800 data points] vs. >10 h [36,000 data points] on average). Recorded accelerations from any one specific activity over a longer sampling time will have less mathematical influence on the value of the accelerometry variable calculated in the unstructured setting compared to the structured setting.

Convergent Validity Between Accelerometry Variables and Other Motor Behavior Measurements

Correlation matrices showing relationships between the video‐coded toy play task (structured context) and the accelerometry variables are shown in Figure 3. More relationships reached significance within the same context (Figure 3A) than in different contexts (Figure 3B). Simultaneous time, the accelerometry variable quantifying the duration during which both limbs were moving, was correlated with bilateral engagements and bilateral grasping during the motor task, as conceptually anticipated. Jerk and magnitude variables maintained their relationships across contexts. The toy play variable bilateral grasping was related to multiple accelerometer variables, indicating that those infants who most frequently grasped objects with both hands tended to move more (time variables), move at higher intensities (magnitude variables), and move with less smooth movements (jerk). Full details of the correlations are given in Tables SII and SIII for accelerometry during structured toy play and accelerometry in the unstructured home setting, respectively.

Heatmaps show the correlation between toy play video codes and accelerometry variables during either structured toy play (A) or unstructured daily life (B). (C) Scatterplots show candidate associations in more detail, specifically the relationship between simultaneous time during toy play and engagement counts, proportion of bilateral engagements, and proportion of bilateral grasping.

Correlations between caregiver report using the Ages and Stages and the accelerometer variables are shown in Table SIV for the structured setting and Table SV for the unstructured setting. We did not find any statistically significant correlations between the structured accelerometry variables recorded during toy play and caregiver reports of fine motor or gross motor behavior (ps between 0.093 and 0.950). We did find a few statistically significant correlations between accelerometry measured at home and caregiver reports of fine (left jerk r = 0.15, p = 0.021; left time r = 0.14, p = 0.032) and gross (right jerk r = 0.17, p = 0.030) motor behavior. The same variables related to fine motor behavior trended toward relationships with gross motor behavior (left jerk r = 0.15, p = 0.054; left time r = 0.14, p = 0.063).

Discussion

This study investigated upper limb accelerometry measurement of infant motor behavior at the 6‐month assessment to examine feasibility and convergent validity across contexts and measurement types. Consistent with previous reports (Ricardo et al. 2018), infants and parents tolerated wearing the devices on the wrists as indicated by a usable data rate approximately equivalent to the rate for the questionnaires (see Section 2). Accelerometry values from the structured context were generally larger than from the unstructured context, except for the left and right variance variables. Accelerometry variables from the structured context were consistently and moderately related to values from the unstructured context. When analyzed with observational task‐based codes from the structured context, more relationships with accelerometer values reached significance in the same context than in different contexts. Thus, upper limb accelerometry is a feasible, valid addition to the infant motor measurement toolbox; accelerometry's strengths (efficient, scalable quantification over longer durations) complement the strengths of other existing tools.

Accelerometry values in the structured context were generally larger than and moderately related to accelerometry values in the unstructured context, as hypothesized. During the structured context in the lab, a concentrated period of motor activity was created as the infant engaged in play with objects while seated. It was anticipated that the overall level of motor activity during this structured play would be greater than the overall level of activity during the unstructured period in the infant's home environment, where caregivers and infants are not constantly engaged with toys. This is nicely illustrated by the difference in duration variables (Figure 1A) and the relatively lower associations between the duration variables across contexts (Figure 2A). The left and right magnitude variables were also higher in the structured context (Figure 1B), albeit with a more robust relationship across contexts (Figure 2B). It was interesting that the left and right variance variables were larger in the unstructured context (Figure 1C). Less variable accelerations, quantifying less variable movement in the structured context, may arise from the requirements of the motor task (e.g., spatial constraints of sitting at a single table, access to three toys), resulting in limited exploratory or spontaneous movement above and below levels that could occur during unstructured time. The magnitidue of the relationships between contexts (coefficients between 0.26 and 0.54) are consistent with, or perhaps slightly higher for some variables than, those reported previously in children beyond infancy (Rice et al. 2013, J. D. Konrad et al. 2024, Saudino 2012, B. N. Wilson et al. 2009). The similarities on the right and left sides seen here in infants match what is seen from bilateral wrist accelerometry in pediatric (Lang et al. 2024) and adult (Bailey and Lang 2013) samples, reinforcing that the use of a single sensor on one side may be sufficient to capture motor behavior across the lifespan in people who are not suspected to have asymmetric motor deficits (Lang et al. 2024, Smith and Lang 2019). The data overall suggest that decisions to deploy accelerometry in the structured versus unstructured context in any given study may be dictated by the scientific questions, since the accelerometry variables appear to be capturing overlapping aspects of infant motor behavior across contexts.

Relationships between the video‐coded and the accelerometry variables (Figure 3) demonstrate convergent validity of the measurement tools, as hypothesized. Given that individual infant movement patterns fluctuate (Corbetta and Thelen 1996), it is noteworthy that significant relationships were found between the five coded minutes of toy play and accelerometer recordings. Conceptually, each variable, either from the accelerometer recordings or from the coded video recordings, quantifies a single aspect of movement behavior. Modest, significant correlations were found for many pairs of variables, even though the quantified movement aspects are quite different (e.g., left magnitude compared to bilateral engagements). The magnitude of the relationships is consistent with those reported in other studies across the literature investigating different types of measurement to assess human developmental behaviors (Sanchez and Constantino 2020, Agarwal et al. 2024, Gudmundsson 2015, Sulek et al. 2022). A lack of correlation between some pairs of variables (e.g., no relationships between grasping and the accel variables) is harder to interpret and may reflect a wider difference between the specific aspects quantified by those variables. Future work could be targeted toward investigating the accelerometry variables in different tasks and/or different accelerometry variables in the same task to fully understand the shared variances between the two types of measurement.

A larger number of significant correlations of task‐based codes with accelerometry in the same (i.e., structured) versus unstructured contexts (Figure 3) is logical when both tools are deriving variables from the same, concurrent behavior. The persistence of some task–accelerometry relationships across contexts (i.e., video‐coded bilateral grasping with left and right jerk and magnitude in Figure 3B) suggests that accelerometry can capture generalizable aspects of motor behavior. With respect to relationships of coded motor behaviors to specific accelerometry metrics, more movement (longer durations) and more intense movement (higher magnitudes), but less smooth movement (higher jerk), were all positively associated with video‐coded skilled motor behavior (more bilateral grasping). Longer durations, higher magnitudes, and higher jerk measured via accelerometry also appear collectively in typically developing children (Lang et al. 2024) and in neurotypical adults (Miller et al. 2024) (see https://langlab.shinyapps.io/harmonized_data/, Cross Sectional Upper Limb tab, to manipulate data); part of this association arises from the mathematical link between acceleration and its time derivative, jerk. Our team, as part of its interest in identifying accelerometry variables relevant to typical and atypical development, has applied analogous protocols to school‐aged children with ASD, a group enriched for atypical motor development. Those studies show that (1) children with ASD had less smooth (higher jerk) but not more movement or more intense movement compared to neurotypical peers in one sample (J. Konrad et al. 2022) and (2) better motor competence and coordination (Wuang et al. 2012) were associated with lower peak intensities and with less variable and smoother movement in another sample (J. D. Konrad et al. 2024). All together, these findings align with the idea that as development progresses and motor control increases, motor skills are executed with smoother and less variable movement (Konczak et al. 1997, Konczak et al. 1995, Yan et al. 2000, Stergiou and Decker 2011). In this study, relationships between these accelerometer variables and the developing skill of bilateral grasping support the idea that more movement in infancy may facilitate improved motor skills and exploratory learning (Corbetta and Thelen 1996, Iverson et al. 2023). This study, however, explores these relationships at one point in time, not longitudinally as the skill is developing. It is possible that more movement occurs as motor skills improve or as new skills begin to emerge, and that the way infants move affects their motor skill learning.

Only a few relationships were found between the unstructured accelerometry variables and the caregiver reports of infant motor skills via the ASQ. While it is plausible that these two measurement types lack convergence in infants, we consider it more likely that the limited range of scores on the ASQ, designed to screen for delay rather than quantitatively index motor function (Squires et al. 2009, Radecki et al. 2011), constrained the ability to identify relationships, which were hypothesized to be in the moderate range. We have previously found relationships between accelerometry variables and caregiver report using the Developmental Coordination Disorder Questionnaire in school‐age children (J. Konrad et al. 2022, J. D. Konrad et al. 2024). Consistent with the ASQ as a screening tool, the typically developing infants in this sample had scores distributed almost entirely in the upper half of the scoring range, which further limited our ability to detect relationships between the measurement types.

Limitations

Beyond the limitation of using the ASQ discussed above, there are three other limitations that influence the interpretation of our data. First, the overall study design from which these data arose did not include any in‐lab standardized assessments of motor behavior at the 6‐month time point; thus, we could not evaluate the convergent validity of the accelerometry variables with that type of measurement. Second, despite our broad recruitment efforts, our sample was relatively homogenous with respect to demographic variables. A more diverse sample may capture more robust and generalizable relationships between the accelerometry, toy play, and caregiver‐reported variables. Third, these data are from infants in a limited age range, around 6 months adjusted age. Future work will need to replicate findings and examine relationships across motor metrics longitudinally.

Conclusions

The ability to capture variation in motor behavior during infancy can inform our growing knowledge of typical development and early markers of vulnerabilities for atypical motor development. As hardware and software technologies improve over time, accelerometry is emerging as a scalable in‐home measurement tool to objectively quantify aspects of infant motor behavior across settings. Our study demonstrates the ease of use of this technology in infants and provides evidence that upper limb accelerometry captures convergent aspects of motor behavior across structured and unstructured contexts while correlating with performance on a motor task. The findings support the potential for future application of accelerometry during the first year of life as a complementary tool to expand options for measurement of functionally relevant motor behavior. Such approaches in infancy, a period of rapid motor development, could be especially valuable for detecting subtle deviations from normative trajectories that are relevant to future outcomes, with implications for timely intervention. As these approaches continue to be developed, our accelerometer data from typically developing infants can provide a foundation of referent data for comparison with clinical and at‐risk populations going forward.
