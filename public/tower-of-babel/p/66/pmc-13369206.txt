Synergistic effects of nicotine and hyperlipidemia induce cardiac damage via dynamic cardiomyocyte-based biosensing
Authors: Cheng-Wen He, Xiaohuan Guo, Tao Liang, Pan Chen, Kaiqiang Li, Aibo Xu, Ke Hao, Ning Hu, Zhen Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCigarette smoking and hyperlipidemia are major risk factors for cardiovascular disease. However, the mechanisms underlying their synergistic cardiotoxic effects remain insufficiently elucidated, particularly at the cellular electrophysiological level. To address this, we developed an electrophysiological biosensing platform to establish a real-time co-exposure model that simulates the combined stress of nicotine and palmitic acid-induced hyperlipidemia on the primary rat cardiomyocytes. This model enabled continuous, non-invasive monitoring and quantitative analysis of field potential under individual and combined conditions. Our results revealed a distinct concentration-dependent effect of nicotine on cardiomyocytes: low-dose nicotine induced transient and reversible electrophysiological suppression, while high concentrations triggered severe arrhythmias and cell death. Critically, the co-exposure model revealed that a hyperlipidemic state significantly sensitizes cardiomyocytes to nicotine, markedly exacerbating arrhythmogenesis and compromising cell viability compared to nicotine alone. Moreover, nicotine withdrawal experiments showed that the electrophysiological damage induced by moderate nicotine exposure was reversible, whereas the adverse effects of high-dose nicotine exposure were largely irreversible. This study establishes an in vitro model to investigate the multifactorial cardiotoxicity, and provides a predictive platform for risk stratification and therapeutic intervention in smoking- and/or diet-related cardiovascular diseases. Subject terms: Electrical and electronic engineering, Biosensors

Introduction

Cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) are responsible for a leading cause of death worldwide, accounting for over 17 million fatalities annually1, and with expectations of surpassing 20 million by 20252. Smoking and an unhealthy diet are key factors in inducing heart disease and may lead to sudden death. Numerous current studies have shown that smoking is a known risk factor for CVDs, including coronary heart disease and stroke3,4. The probability of sudden death in smokers is significantly higher than that in non-smokers, with the risk being about three times higher than that in those who have never smoked5. In addition, smoking serves as an independent risk factor for atrial fibrillation, particularly among current smokers, with a more prominent risk and a clear dose–response relationship6. Nicotine, the main component of tobacco smoke, plays a critical role in these processes. On the other hand, obesity is also a significant contributor to the development of heart disease. Due to various factors, such as changes in cardiac structure and function, and dysfunction of the autonomic nervous system, individuals with obesity are prone to arrhythmias7–9. Arrhythmias can disrupt the normal rhythm of the heart, thereby affecting the heart’s pumping function, increasing the risk of thrombus formation, and eventually leading to serious complications such as stroke and heart failure. In addition, obesity often contributes to hyperlipidemia, a major risk factor for heart disease. In individuals with obesity, the excessive accumulation of fat leads to abnormal increases in blood lipid levels, such as increases in cholesterol, triglycerides, and other components10. These lipids can accumulate on the vascular walls, forming atherosclerotic plaques, leading to vascular stenosis or even occlusion, and affecting myocardial blood perfusion11,12, thereby triggering a series of cardiac disease problems such as myocardial ischemia. Additionally, obese patients may also experience impaired coronary microvascular function13,14, further disrupting myocardial blood supply and increasing the risk of heart disease11,15. The latest research shows that both nicotine exposure and hyperlipidemia can lead to cardiac structural and functional abnormalities through various mechanisms. However, the synergistic effect of the two on cardiomyocyte damage has not yet been studied in detail.

Nicotine significantly disrupts the electrophysiological homeostasis of cardiomyocytes by inducing multiple pathways, including oxidative stress, impairment of mitochondrial function, and calcium homeostasis imbalance.16–18. Similarly, hyperlipidemia has also been established as an independent risk factor for arrhythmias. Free fatty acids can directly affect the ion channel function of cardiomyocytes, prolong the action potential duration, and increase arrhythmia susceptibility19–21. Noteworthily, existing evidence indicates that both nicotine exposure and lipotoxicity converge on oxidative stress injury and intracellular calcium handling dysfunction as core downstream drivers of cardiomyocyte electrophysiological disorder. However, most existing studies focus on single-factor analysis, and there is a lack of systematic research on the real-time, dynamic electrophysiological responses under the combined action of nicotine and hyperlipidemia. In particular, no long-term in vitro models have been established to dissect the synergistic pro-arrhythmic effects of these two risk factors, nor to clarify whether their shared pathological pathways form an amplifying cascade to exacerbate cardiac damage.

Conventional methods for evaluating cardiac toxicity largely depend on live animal studies. While these methods can provide physiological and pathological endpoint parameters, they suffer from limitations such as being time-consuming and resource-intensive, having low throughput, and difficulty in achieving dynamic monitoring at the cellular level22. Furthermore, they are inconsistent with the evolving trend of the “3 R” (Replacement, Reduction, Refinement) ethical principles23,24. In recent years, electrophysiological biosensing technologies such as microelectrode arrays (MEAs) have emerged as a critical tool for elucidating electrophysiological mechanisms, due to its non-invasive nature and high-throughput capabilities for recording the field potentials and firing frequencies of cardiomyocytes25,26. This technology is characterized by high sensitivity, good biocompatibility, capacity for dynamic real-time monitoring, and potential for integration27. Consequently, it is gradually replacing traditional animal experiments and becoming an important tool in precision medicine and drug development28,29. Although this technology has been applied to studies on the neurotoxicity of nicotine30,31, using it to establish a dynamic model of cardiac injury induced by the synergistic effect of nicotine and high fat, as well as to analyze the associated electrophysiological phenotypes and recovery potential, remains an unaddressed gap. Despite these advances, a critical gap remains: current MEA-based cardiotoxicity studies have been largely restricted to single-factor acute exposure models, whereas real-time dynamic monitoring of cardiomyocyte electrophysiology under combined metabolic and chemical stressors has not been explored28,32. Prior studies have individually characterized the electrophysiological impact of nicotine and lipotoxicity using endpoint-based techniques such as patch clamp and fluorescence imaging, yet no integrated in vitro model has been established to simultaneously capture the progressive electrophysiological deterioration resulting from co-exposure to nicotine and hyperlipidemia over extended time courses33–35. Consequently, it remains unknown whether nicotine and hyperlipidemia act synergistically on cardiomyocyte electrophysiological function through a shared “Oxidative Stress–Calcium Dyshomeostasis” cascade, and whether a MEA-based real-time biosensing platform can capture the dynamic progression, dose-response relationships, and reversibility of this synergistic cardiotoxicity to serve as a predictive model for risk stratification.

Based on the known cardiotoxic profiles of tobacco components and lipid metabolites, and their shared downstream pathological pathways, we herein propose a synergistic “Oxidative Stress-Calcium Dyshomeostasis” cascade hypothesis to explain the synergistic cardiomyocyte dysfunction induced by nicotine and hyperlipidemia. We hypothesize that nicotine acts as the primary trigger to induce mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (ROS) overproduction, initiating oxidative injury in cardiomyocytes, while the palmitic acid (PA)-induced lipotoxic environment creates a “priming effect” that lowers the cellular antioxidant defense threshold, thus sensitizing cardiomyocytes to nicotine exposure. The burst of ROS triggered by nicotine and the lipotoxic stress induced by PA act in concert to cause damage to key calcium-handling proteins such as ryanodine receptor 2 (RyR2), thereby leading to irreversible intracellular calcium homeostasis imbalance. This molecular cascade ultimately manifests as the breakdown of cardiomyocyte electrophysiological stability, characterized by diminished field potential amplitudes, aberrant arrhythmic firing, and even cell death, which our MEA-based biosensing platform is uniquely positioned to monitor dynamically and non-invasively in real time.

Therefore, this study utilizes the MEA electrophysiological platform to establish an in vitro cardiomyocyte model that simulates the pathological state of smoking combined with hyperlipidemia, and enables long-term, real-time dynamic monitoring of cellular electrophysiological activity under this complex condition. (Fig. 1). By systematically administering nicotine and PA at different concentrations, we established a synergistic injury model with well-defined dose-effect relationships and time courses for the first time, and focused on evaluating the characteristics and reversibility of cardiomyocyte rhythm disturbances under the combined action of these two factors. The establishment of this model provides a reliable and controllable research system for real-time elucidation of multi-factor synergistic arrhythmogenic mechanisms at the cellular level, overcoming the limitations of traditional methods in dynamic monitoring and mechanism elucidation. Furthermore, by means of this innovative experimental model and evaluation strategy, this study aims to verify the above hypothesis, clarify the synergistic effects and underlying basis of nicotine and hyperlipidemia in inducing myocardial electrophysiological disorders, thereby providing a new theoretical basis and methodological support for the assessment of related cardiotoxicity risks and the development of intervention strategies.

Materials and methods

Fabrication of the MEA device

The manufacturing process of the MEA device is basically the same as that described in earlier research28,29. Utilizing a 6-inch quartz wafer as the base material, we applied photoresist through spin-coating to create the designs for electrodes, leads and contact pads. Subsequently, a thin layer of titanium/gold (measuring 10 nm and 100 nm, respectively) was coated through magnetron sputtering and then elevated to form conductive designs. The SU-8 photoresist functioned as an insulator and underwent spin-coating on the specimen to define the operative electrode zone (Fig. 2a). After processing, the quartz wafer was divided into 25 separate segments, with each segment measuring 2 × 2 cm2. Each device was affixed to a printed circuit board (PCB) for electrophysiological analysis and fastened to a glass circle measuring 1.5 cm in diameter and 1.0 cm in height, functioning as a cell culture chamber. Sterilization of the MEA devices was achieved by the use of 75% ethanol and ultraviolet light. Prior to cell seeding, they received a coating of 10 μg/mL fibronectin and underwent incubation at 37 °C in an atmosphere containing 5.0% CO2 for a duration of 4 to 6 h to enhance cell adhesion.

Isolation of primary cardiomyocytes from neonatal rats

Sprague-Dawley rats within 24 h of birth were used for primary cardiomyocyte isolation. First, the abdominal area was disinfected with 75% ethanol. Then, in a sterile environment, the rats underwent dissection along the sternum’s midline to reveal the thoracic cavity, followed by the removal of the heart’s apex and its immediate placement in DMEM for blood removal. The heart apex was subsequently transferred into a 3 mL glass vial containing HBSS and cut into tissue pieces approximately 1 mm³ in size. Digestion of the tissue samples was performed 10 times with 1 mL of an enzymatic mixture containing 0.08% trypsin and 0.1% type II collagenase, for 8 min each time. After every cycle, the enzymatic activity was halted by removing the digestion solution and mixing it with DMEM medium containing 10% FBS. Each tissue segment was meticulously dissected into separate cells. Following a 10-min centrifugation of the cell suspension at 100 × g, the cell pellet was delicately reintroduced into the culture medium. To minimize fibroblast impurities, the mixture was filtered through a 200-mesh cell strainer, followed by its placement in a culture flask to ensure varied adhesion. Around 3.5 × 105 cells in each well were planted on MEA chips or in 24-well plates after counting (refer to Fig. S1). All protocols adhered to the guidelines set by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Zhejiang University (AP CODE: ZJU20240103).

CCK-8 assay

After counting, a total of 50,000 separate primary rat cardiomyocytes were reconstituted in 100 μL of culture medium and distributed into every well of a 96-well plate. Following a 2–3 day cultivation period and the verification of regular spontaneous beating, the experimental groups were administered drug therapies, while the control group did not receive any treatment. After the designated incubation phase, each well was added with 10 μL of CCK-8 reagent (Beyotime). A control group was also incorporated, comprising wells with merely 100 μL of culture medium and 10 μL of CCK-8 reagent, devoid of cells. The 96-well plate was placed in a cell incubator for 30 minutes, then its absorbance was gauged at 450 nm through a microplate reader. The formula for determining cell viability (%) is as follows: Cell viability (%) = (OD treatment – OD blank)/(OD control – OD blank) × 100%. Every group was tested three times.

Live/dead cell staining

Calcein-AM, a fluorescent dye, distinctly marks living cells with a green glow. While propidium iodide (PI) is unable to infiltrate the unbroken membranes of living cells, it can infiltrate dead cells via impaired membrane areas, attach to nuclear DNA and produce red fluorescence. In this study, cardiomyocytes, placed in 24-well plates, underwent a three-day cultivation period followed by treatment based on the experimental classifications. Each well received 250 μL of the ready-made Calcein-AM/PI working solution (Beyotime) and then the plate was incubated in darkness at 37 °C for a duration of 15 min. Subsequently, the stained cells were examined using a ThermoFisher fluorescence microscope (EVOS M7000).

Immunofluorescence staining of cardiomyocytes

Cell coverslips (14 mm in diameter) were sterilized by soaking in 75% ethanol, then placed into 24-well plates and rinsed with PBS. Each well was added with a 500 μL mixture of cardiomyocytes (3 × 105 cells per well), followed by incubation at 37 °C for three days. Post-culturing, the medium was discarded, and cells were washed gently 3 times with PBS. Subsequently, each well was added with 200 μL of 4% paraformaldehyde to stabilize the cells at ambient temperature for half an hour. Post-fixation, the cells underwent five PBS washes, each lasting 5 min. The process of permeabilization involved introducing 200 μL of 0.1% Triton X-100 into every well, followed by a 10-min room temperature incubation. Subsequently, the cells underwent a triple rinse with PBS, each lasting 5 min. To block non-specific binding, 200 μL of 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA) blocking solution was added for incubation at 37 °C for 30 min. After removing the blocking solution, 200 μL of diluted fluorescent antibody (Abcam, Alexa Fluor® 488 Anti-Cardiac Troponin T) was added and cells were incubated for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. Subsequently, cells were washed three times with PBS (5 min each). The coverslips were carefully removed and gently blotted to remove excess liquid. Then, 2 μL of DAPI (Beyotime) and 5 μL of anti-fade mounting medium (SlowFade™ Diamond, Invitrogen) were dropped onto a microscope slide. The coverslip was inverted onto the slide and sealed around the edges with nail polish. A confocal laser scanning microscope (Leica, STELLARIS STED) was employed to capture the images.

Cell treatment

After four-day cultivation on MEA chips, the cardiomyocytes underwent treatment with drugs. To reach concentrations of 1 mM, 2 mM, 5 mM, and 10 mM, the nicotine solution was thinned out in 1 mL of culture medium. Every concentration was individually introduced into the cardiomyocytes for storing in a cell incubator, and the MEA system was utilized to capture electrophysiological signals. In a distinct process, 55.68 mg of sodium palmitate was weighed and dissolved in 10 mL of Dimethyl Sulfoxide (DMSO) to prepare a 20 mM sodium palmitate stock solution. The stock solution was then added to 1 mL of cell culture medium to achieve a final concentration of 200 μM. Thereafter, this culture medium containing sodium palmitate was added to the cells and incubated for 0.5 h to induce a hyperlipidemic state in the cells. Subsequently, the same wells were supplemented with nicotine stock solutions to reach final concentrations of 1 mM, 2 mM, 5 mM, and 10 mM. Real-time changes in cardiomyocyte electrophysiological activity were continuously monitored using the MEA recording system. During the recovery experiment, the culture medium was replaced twice. Cardiomyocytes were first exposed to nicotine-containing medium at 0.5 h, followed by replacement with fresh nicotine-free medium after 4 h to initiate recovery. The total culture volume was maintained at 1 mL per well throughout the experiment.

Transmission electron microscopy

Cardiomyocytes were fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde in 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.4) at 4 °C overnight, washed thoroughly with phosphate buffer, and post-fixed with 1% osmium tetroxide for 1–2 h. After rinsing with phosphate buffer, the samples were dehydrated through a graded ethanol series (30 vol%, 50 vol%, 70 vol%, 80 vol%, 90 vol%, 95 vol%, 100 vol%), transferred to pure acetone, and infiltrated with epoxy resin using acetone–resin mixtures of increasing resin content (1:1 to 3:1) followed by pure resin overnight. The infiltrated specimens were embedded and polymerized at 70 °C overnight. Ultrathin sections (70–90 nm thick) were cut with a Leica EM UC7 ultramicrotome, counterstained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate, and examined with a transmission electron microscope.

Western blot analysis

Cultured cardiomyocytes in 24-well plates were lysed on ice for 10 min with 50 μL RIPA buffer supplemented with a protease inhibitor cocktail. The lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C, and the resulting supernatants were collected. Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA assay kit. Equal amounts of protein were mixed with 5× loading buffer and denatured by boiling for 10 min. The samples were resolved by SDS-PAGE and electrotransferred onto nitrocellulose membranes at 120 V for 1.5 h in a tank transfer system filled with 1× transfer buffer and an ice pack to maintain low temperature. After a brief TBST wash, the membranes were blocked with 5% BSA in TBST for 1.5 h at room temperature, followed by overnight incubation at 4 °C with appropriately diluted primary antibodies on a shaker. The membranes were then washed with TBST, incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 2 h at room temperature, and washed extensively with TBST. Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) substrate and imaged with a chemiluminescence detection system.

Intracellular Ca2+ transient measurement

Intracellular Ca2+ transients were measured using Fluo-4 AM (Beyotime). After the designated treatments, cardiomyocytes were washed once with PBS and loaded with Fluo-4 AM staining solution at 37 °C for 30 min in the dark. Following incubation, fluorescence images were acquired under a fluorescence microscope (Ex/Em = 490/525 nm) in video mode at 66 frames per second for 5 s. The video sequences were converted into 330 consecutive frames, and the time-dependent fluorescence intensity at a fixed region of interest was quantified using ImageJ software. Ca²⁺ transient amplitude was expressed as ΔF/F₀, where F₀ represents baseline fluorescence and ΔF represents the change in fluorescence relative to baseline.

Data analysis

Electrophysiological signals recorded by the MEA system were processed offline using a custom-developed LabVIEW program. Raw signals were first band-pass filtered (0.1 Hz–2 kHz) to remove baseline drift and high-frequency noise. Spike detection was performed using an amplitude threshold set at 5 times the root-mean-square noise level of each recording channel. Results and discussion

Construction and characterization of the cardiomyocyte-based sensing platform

The development of a cardiomyocyte-based sensing system, both self-constructed and constructed, includes four primary elements: a MEA, an electrical signal processing unit, a module for gathering data, and a module for interpreting data using LabView software (Fig. 2b). Equipped with 32 electrode sets, the MEA device facilitates the prolonged, instantaneous, and non-intrusive observation of cells grown on its surface. When inserted into the signal conditioning module, the MEA can transmit recorded signals for cascade amplification and filtering. The amplified signals are then sampled at a frequency of 20 kHz via a data acquisition card. These electrical signals are ultimately transmitted to a PC through a USB connection for data analysis and processed using LabView software. The signal conditioning module containing the MEA device can be placed inside the incubator, allowing for stable, long-term cell culture. Cardiac troponin T (cTnT) is a muscle contraction regulatory protein specifically expressed in cardiomyocytes, that is primarily located on the thin filaments28,39. To verify the purity of the isolated cardiomyocytes, an immunofluorescence assay targeting cTnT was performed (Fig. S2). The results confirmed that the isolated cells from the apex of rat hearts were predominantly cardiomyocytes. Figure 2c displays the growth state of primary cardiomyocytes on the MEA. The bright-field images show that the cells are uniformly distributed and form a monolayer on the chip. The survival of cells was evaluated through a live/dead staining test utilizing Calcein-AM and propidium iodide (PI). In the fluorescence images, most cells are showing green and only a small number are showing red, suggesting that the primary cardiomyocytes not only adhered well to the MEA surface but also maintained good viability. Based on bright-field imaging and Calcein-AM staining, the average coverage of cardiomyocytes on MEA chips exceeded 90%, and the vast majority of microelectrodes established effective contact with viable cells. Meanwhile, the signal acquisition performance of the MEA system was evaluated according to the number of active channels. After cellular stabilization, more than 24 out of 32 recording channels consistently captured clear action potential signals on average. These findings confirm favorable cell–electrode coupling, as well as stable and reliable signal detection capacity of the MEA system. Figure 2d shows the electrophysiological recordings from cardiomyocytes on three different devices over the course of four days. Normal electrophysiological signals could be observed as early as day 2 after cell adhesion and continued rhythmically through day 4. Subsequently, a statistical evaluation of signals from various electrodes was conducted to determine the most suitable duration for culture. As shown in Fig. 2e, the average signal amplitudes across all three devices increased to approximately 155.11%, 2.56% and 120.37% on day 4 compared to day 2. The variation in the percentage increase in amplitude between devices reflects inherent differences in initial cell-electrode coupling and is accounted for in all pharmacological experiments by normalizing responses to the pre-treatment baseline for each individual electrode. Meanwhile, the firing rate increased over the first three days and stabilized by day 4 (Fig. 2f), indicating that cell growth and electrophysiological performance gradually stabilized over time, and that the connection between cells and MEA electrodes was enhanced. To confirm the platform’s steadiness, cardiomyocytes underwent continuous observation for four days after seeding, with the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) being documented. As shown in Fig. S3, the SNR was approximately 36 dB on the first day and stabilized at around 48 dB from day 2 to day 4, demonstrating that the system can reliably record signals over an extended period without significant signal degradation. These results indicate that the cells reached optimal growth conditions on day 4, exhibiting stable and robust electrophysiological outputs. Therefore, all subsequent experiments were initiated on the fourth day after primary cell extraction.

Additionally, since DMSO is used as a solvent in the PA-induced hyperlipidemic state, it is necessary to investigate its toxicity. As shown in Fig. S4a, at a specific electrode site, the cardiomyocytes treated with 1% DMSO maintained a relatively stable firing rate within 12 h, while the amplitude decreased at the end of the period. After conducting a statistical analysis of data from all electrode sites, it was observed that the amplitude showed a slight increase in the first 8 h, and the firing rate maintained a small fluctuation of approximately 10% (Fig. S4b, c). Therefore, in subsequent studies, it can be concluded that treating cells with 1% DMSO for 12 h does not have a significant impact on the electrophysiological state of the cells.

Evaluation of effects of nicotine on cardiomyocytes

To evaluate the effects of nicotine, four different nicotine concentrations (1–10 mM) were applied to cultured cardiomyocytes (Fig. 3a), followed by continuous monitoring of their electrophysiological activities over a 24-h period. As shown in Fig. 3b, only a slight decrease in firing rate was observed under 1 mM nicotine, without any signs of arrhythmia. When treated with 2 mM or 5 mM nicotine, the signal amplitude exhibited a significant drop within the first hour, but later recovered to near-normal levels. However, by 24 h, the signal amplitude dropped again, with near-total signal loss in the 5 mM group. For cells exposed to 10 mM nicotine, the signal amplitude disappeared within the first hour and showed no signs of recovery thereafter, maintaining a persistent arrhythmic state. Figure 3c, d present the changes in signal amplitude and firing rate within the first hour of nicotine treatment, with electrophysiological data recorded every five minutes. These figures allow for a more detailed observation of the short-term effects of nicotine exposure on cardiomyocyte electrophysiology. As illustrated in Fig. 3c, the signal amplitude decreased immediately upon exposure, with the degree of reduction positively correlated with nicotine concentration. Specifically, the 1 mM concentration resulted in a ~ 40% decrease in amplitude, while concentrations of 5–10 mM led to near-complete signal loss. Over time, cells exposed to 1 mM nicotine nearly fully recovered their amplitude within 40 min, those exposed to 2–5 mM recovered approximately 20% of their original amplitude within 60 minutes, whereas the 10 mM group showed no recovery. In contrast, changes in the firing rate followed a different pattern. At 1 mM, only a slight, temporary decrease in frequency was observed immediately after nicotine exposure, followed by a return to stable activity. However, significant cardiac arrhythmia was observed in the 2–10 mM groups, characterized by frequent fluctuations in firing rate in the 2 mM group, pronounced tachycardia in the 5 mM group, and an initial sharp increase followed by complete signal loss in the 10 mM group. It is noteworthy that cardiomyocytes exhibited divergent response patterns in signal amplitude and firing rate under identical nicotine concentrations, which can be attributed to their distinct electrophysiological determinants: while signal amplitude primarily reflects the peak depolarization current driven largely by the availability of fast Na+ channels and L-type Ca2+ channels40, the firing rate is governed by the pacemaking rhythm, which depends on the diastolic depolarization rate and the kinetics of repolarizing K+ currents41. Our results demonstrate that at low nicotine concentrations (2–5 mM), the observed decrease in amplitude without a corresponding permanent shift in firing rate suggests a transient inhibition of inward depolarizing currents that can be initially counteracted by the cell’s homeostatic mechanisms. However, at higher concentrations (10 mM), the significant decline in amplitude was accompanied by severe rhythm disturbances, such as tachycardia or total signal loss, a divergence that likely stems from nicotine-induced oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction, which progressively impair intracellular Ca2+ handling. Looking again at the 24-h time frame, Fig. 3e summarizes the average signal amplitude across multiple channels at various time points. In the 1 mM group, the amplitude remained relatively stable throughout the monitoring period, whereas in the 2–10 mM groups, the amplitudes initially dropped sharply, peaked at 6 h, and then gradually declined again. The recovery level was inversely correlated with nicotine concentration. Figure 3f details the statistical analysis of firing rate over 24 h. In the 1 mM group, frequency gradually declined over time. The 2 mM group saw a slow increase in frequency during the first 12 h, followed by a drop to a level similar to that of the 1 mM group by 24 h. In contrast, the 5 mM group maintained elevated firing rate throughout, with significant tachycardia observed at 24 h. However, this seemingly elevated firing rate does not indicate enhanced physiological function, but instead reflects pathological tachyarrhythmia induced by high-dose nicotine. In this state, severe disruption of cellular ion homeostasis accelerates repolarization while rendering it incomplete, ultimately leading to a characteristic electrophysiological pattern featuring low amplitude, high firing rate, and disrupted rhythmicity. These findings suggest that low concentrations of nicotine cause only temporary disturbances in cardiomyocyte electrophysiology, from which the cells can recover. However, exposure to high concentrations leads to sustained reductions in signal amplitude and increases in firing rate, indicative of arrhythmia, with the severity of damage being positively correlated with nicotine concentration. To further clarify the effects of nicotine on the rhythmic stability of cardiomyocytes, we adopted Poincaré plots to analyze the nonlinear dynamic characteristics of cardiac electrical activity (Fig. 3g, h). As a classic nonlinear analytical approach, the Poincaré plot generates two-dimensional scatter diagrams by plotting each inter-spike interval (ISIn) against the subsequent interval (ISIn+1). The spatial distribution of scattered points enables comprehensive evaluation of the stability and variability of the electrophysiological system29,42. Following 1 mM nicotine intervention, data points formed a compact elliptical distribution along the identity line (ISIn = ISIn+1). This highly ordered pattern indicates robust temporal coupling between consecutive action potentials, reflecting well-maintained ion homeostasis and intact automaticity regulation. In contrast, exposure to 10 mM nicotine completely destroyed the regular elliptical contour, resulting in highly dispersed and random scatter distribution, which denoted the loss of rhythmic periodicity. Such irregular firing patterns are closely correlated with cardiac triggered activity and primarily arise from intracellular calcium cycling dysfunction and excessive oxidative stress. Moreover, the diffuse shift of scattered points toward the coordinate origin further verified the occurrence of tachycardia and shortened inter-spike intervals. Collectively, the distinct transition from a tightly clustered elliptical shape to a disordered, scattered distribution directly characterizes the pathological progression from physiological spontaneous pacing to lethal arrhythmia in cardiomyocytes at the nonlinear dynamic level. To explore the molecular mechanism underlying nicotine-induced electrophysiological dysfunction in cardiomyocytes, we performed comprehensive validation from four perspectives: mitochondrial morphology, oxidative stress, ion channel expression, and calcium homeostasis. Transmission electron microscopy results (Fig. 3j) revealed densely arranged and intact mitochondrial cristae in the control group. After 24 h of 2 mM nicotine treatment, partial inner mitochondrial membrane swelling and disorganized cristae were observed, while the 5 mM nicotine group exhibited extensive mitochondrial vacuolization and cristae rupture, indicating severe mitochondrial structural damage. Consistent with these morphological alterations, ROS fluorescence detection demonstrated that intracellular ROS levels increased markedly in a nicotine concentration-dependent manner (Fig. S6). Further Western blot analysis confirmed concentration-dependent downregulation of key ion channel proteins, including the calcium release channel RyR2, sodium channel Nav1.5, and potassium channel KCNH2 (Fig. 3k, l). In the 10 mM group, the expression levels of these three proteins decreased by approximately 70.7%, 39.4%, and 64.1%, respectively, compared with the control group. The reduced abundance of ion channel proteins ultimately contributed to disrupted calcium homeostasis. Calcium transient recordings (Fig. 3m) showed regular and synchronized calcium transients in control cardiomyocytes, whereas elevated nicotine exposure gradually attenuated calcium transient amplitude and induced obvious rhythmic disorders. In summary, nicotine exposure triggers mitochondrial structural impairment and excessive ROS accumulation, which inhibits the expression of critical ion channel proteins and eventually leads to intracellular calcium homeostasis collapse. This cascade forms a complete mechanistic pathway linking molecular damage to adverse electrophysiological phenotypes, thereby strongly supporting the synergistic pathogenic hypothesis proposed in this study.

These findings lead to the inference that nicotine levels under 1 mM result in only temporary harm to cardiomyocytes, long-term exposure (24 h) to 2 mM nicotine leads to clear electrophysiological impairment, while exposure to 5–10 mM causes a rapid onset of arrhythmia and significant cell death. It is important to acknowledge that the nicotine concentrations used in this in vitro study (1–10 mM) are significantly higher than the typical plasma levels found in smokers (approximately 6–100 nM or 1–16 ng/mL)43,44. However, the application of supra-physiological doses is a well-established strategy in in vitro toxicological research, and previous studies have reported the treatment of neutrophils with nicotine at concentrations exceeding 3 mM45. This is because in vitro systems lack systemic metabolic clearance, and the experimental duration is considerably shorter compared with the decades-long chronic exposure in humans. Accordingly, elevated doses are required to compensate for these discrepancies. Higher concentrations act as a “stress test” to accelerate and clarify the concentration-dependent mechanistic pathways leading to electrophysiological dysfunction and cell death, which might otherwise remain occult at lower doses within a 24-h window46. This high-dose model provides a robust baseline for identifying the cellular limits of compensatory homeostasis and offers insights into extreme exposure scenarios.

Synergistic effects of nicotine and hyperlipidemic state on cardiomyocytes

In order to investigate the combined damage of nicotine and hyperlipidemic conditions on cardiomyocytes, a hyperlipidemic cardiomyocyte model was first established, with the addition of PA to simulate a hyperlipidemic state. To confirm that cardiomyocytes were in a hyperlipidemic state, the accumulation of intracellular lipid droplets in cells treated with PA was observed via an Oil Red O staining assay. As shown in Fig. S7, some cells were stained red after 0.5 h of incubation with PA. Among the groups, at the concentration of 100 μM, some cells remained unstained, while at concentrations of 200 μM and 500 μM, nearly all cells were stained. With the passage of time, the staining gradually deepened. Subsequently, electrophysiological detection was performed on cells in the 200 μM and 500 μM groups, where the vast majority of cells had entered a hyperlipidemic state. As shown in Figure. S8a, cardiomyocytes treated with 200 μM PA maintained stable electrical signals for up to 8 h, with signal attenuation occurring at 24 h. In contrast, cells treated with 500 μM PA lost their electrical activity within 8 h. According to the average electrophysiological indicators across multiple channels, in the 200 μM group, the signal amplitude remained stable at 8 h and only showed a significant decline at 24 h, whereas in the 500 μM group, a marked reduction was already evident at the 8-h mark (Fig. S8b). Correspondingly, the firing rate followed a similar trend (Fig. S8c): in the 200 μM group, 50% of the initial frequency was still retained at 24 h, while in the 500 μM group, the frequency nearly disappeared. To assess cell viability after PA treatment, a CCK-8 assay was conducted at 24 h (Fig. S8d). The results indicated that cells in the 200 μM group maintained over 80% viability, whereas those in the 500 μM group dropped to just 50%, suggesting potential cytotoxicity at this higher concentration. These findings demonstrate that while 200 μM PA exerts an effect on cardiomyocytes, it induces relatively low cytotoxicity and was therefore selected for simulating a hyperlipidemic state in subsequent experiments.

In order to evaluate the synergistic effects of nicotine and hyperlipidemic conditions, cells were first incubated with 200 μM PA for 30 min to achieve a hyperlipidemic state (Fig. 4a). Throughout this timeframe, there were no notable alterations in the electrical behavior (Fig. 4b). Similar to nicotine treatment alone, cardiomyocytes co-treated with PA and nicotine showed an immediate decrease in electrical signal upon nicotine addition, followed by partial recovery within the next 2 h. However, unlike single nicotine treatment, the recovery of signal amplitude under co-treatment was limited. Over longer exposure durations, specifically at 24 h, electrical signals in cardiomyocytes co-treated with all tested nicotine concentrations were completely undetectable. It should be noted that the representative electrophysiological traces (Fig. 4b) reflect signals from individual electrodes, whereas the quantitative amplitude analysis presented in Fig. 4c is based on averaged data from multiple active channels. Due to the inherent heterogeneity in cell–electrode coupling and cellular stress responses under hyperlipidemic conditions, minor discrepancies between individual representative traces and population-level statistical results may occur. The partial recovery of signal amplitude observed at approximately 2 h likely represents a transient compensatory response of cardiomyocytes to acute nicotine-induced stress. Importantly, this compensatory effect is not sustained, and is followed by progressive electrophysiological deterioration at longer exposure times. No selective data inclusion or exclusion was applied in this analysis; all active channels with detectable action potential signals were included in the final statistics after normalization to their respective baseline values. As shown in Fig. 4c, the amplitude of cardiomyocytes co-treated with 1 mM nicotine and PA did not significantly decline within the first 2 h, compared to PA treatment alone. However, after 24 h of exposure, the amplitude dropped to less than 10% of the original value. For cells co-treated with 2 mM and 5 mM nicotine after PA pre-incubation, the amplitude declined rapidly and almost completely abolished by 24 h. Figure 4d illustrates the changes in firing rate under co-treatment with nicotine and PA. Short-term PA treatment alone had negligible effect on firing rate of cardiomyocytes; however, upon addition of 1–2 mM nicotine, the firing rate gradually decreased over the monitoring period. In the hyperlipidemic state, cells exposed to 5 mM nicotine exhibited an immediate complete loss of firing activity, which showed a transient recovery at 2 h but was fully abolished again by 24 h. Figure 4e, f assess cardiomyocyte viability under the combined influence of nicotine and PA using the CCK-8 assay and live/dead staining. The CCK-8 experiment was independently repeated four times, with four parallel replicates per group in each experiment. The results showed high reproducibility across all independent experiments. The data demonstrate a dose-dependent decrease in cell viability with increasing nicotine concentration under hyperlipidemic conditions, which is consistent with the live/dead staining results. Compared to nicotine alone (Fig. 3i, Fig. S5), the combined effect of PA and different concentrations of nicotine increased the number of PI-positive cells (dead cells) by 366%, 205%, 29%, and 15%, respectively (Fig. 4f and Fig. S9). To quantitatively determine whether nicotine and PA act synergistically, we calculated Q values based on the inhibition of field potential amplitude. The results are summarized in Table S1. At the early exposure phase (1 h), all tested nicotine concentrations combined with PA exhibited synergistic effects, with Q values of 4.59 (1 mM), 1.25 (2 mM), and 1.44 (5 mM), all exceeding the synergy threshold of 1.15. Synergy persisted at 2 h, with Q values of 3.34, 1.40, and 1.22, respectively. At 24 h, the combination of 1 mM nicotine with PA remained synergistic (Q = 1.31), whereas the 2 mM and 5 mM groups shifted into the additive range (Q = 1.04 and 1.11, respectively), likely because the near-complete signal abolition under these severe co-treatment conditions reaches the detection ceiling of the inhibition assay. Collectively, these Q value data provide rigorous quantitative evidence that PA-induced hyperlipidemia synergistically amplifies nicotine-induced electrophysiological suppression, especially during the early phase of co-exposure. These findings suggest that PA exacerbates the electrophysiological disturbances and viability loss induced by nicotine exposure. Compared to nicotine treatment alone, the presence of PA significantly intensifies cardiomyocyte dysfunction under hyperlipidemic conditions.

Recovery of electrophysiological activity in cardiomyocytes after nicotine exposure

Previous experimental data indicates that prolonged nicotine exposure leads to a progressive decline in the electrophysiological function of cardiomyocytes. To investigate whether this decline is reversible, we conducted recovery experiments following nicotine exposure. After 4 h of nicotine exposure, the culture medium was replaced with fresh medium to observe the recovery process (Fig. 5a). As established earlier (Fig. 3), only nicotine concentrations above 1 mM exerted a significant effect on cardiomyocyte electrophysiology; thus, concentrations greater than 1 mM were used to stimulate the cells. Figure 5b shows representative real-time electrophysiological signals from each treatment group at various time points. The 2 mM group demonstrated a full recovery of electrical activity after nicotine removal. The 5 mM group recovered its signal amplitude by 12 h, but the firing rate remained unstable until 24 h into the experiment, at which point the waveform became relatively normalized. In contrast, the 10 mM group, although showing a stable frequency at 12 h, had extremely low peak amplitudes and nearly vanished signals by 24 h. Figure 5c shows the normalized amplitude curves over 24 h. After nicotine withdrawal at 4 h, the signal amplitude in all treatment groups began to recover gradually immediately. The 2 mM nicotine group showed a steady and continuous recovery trend, while the 5 mM and 10 mM high-concentration groups exhibited slower recovery and weaker recovery capacity. At 10 h post-exposure (i.e., 6 h after nicotine withdrawal), the signal amplitudes in the 2 mM and 5 mM groups both recovered to the pre-treatment baseline level and then fluctuated above this level. In contrast, the 10 mM group remained at approximately 60% of the baseline level. More specifically, as shown in Fig. 5d, f, the amplitudes of the 2 mM and 5 mM groups had fully recovered by 12 h, whereas the 10 mM group had only recovered to approximately 64% at 12 h, dropping again to about 50% by 24 h. Regarding the firing rate (Fig. 5e, g), all three groups initially recovered to normal levels at 12 h. However, by 24 h, the 2 mM group was 86% higher than the normal level, indicating the presence of tachycardia. Meanwhile, the 5 mM group dropped to 68% of the normal rate, and the 10 mM group fell to 50%. The initial recovery of electrophysiological activity at 12 h post-nicotine withdrawal reflects the cardiomyocytes’ rapid compensatory attempt to restore ionic homeostasis and electrical function. However, the tachycardia that occurred in the 2 mM group at 24 h indicates that this compensatory response may have become maladaptive. Although nicotine removal may stop the primary injury, the oxidative stress and mitochondrial damage it caused may not be fully repaired within the 24-hour timeframe. This lingering cellular stress could perpetuate aberrant electrical activity and impair normal pacemaking function, ultimately leading to rhythm instability. Figure 5h presents a heatmap showing the recovery of signal amplitudes across 32 recording channels after exposure to different concentrations of nicotine. The data visually reinforce the conclusion that cells exposed to lower nicotine concentrations are able to recover their amplitudes after returning to normal culture conditions, while those exposed to higher concentrations struggle to regain normal activity. In conclusion, the long-term, continuous and non-invasive monitoring via an electrophysiological platform allowed us to assess the recovery of cardiomyocyte function after varying degrees of nicotine exposure. The cells treated with 2 mM nicotine could basically recover within 12 h, but tachycardia occurred at 24 h. Those exposed to 5 mM nicotine showed partial recovery, and those treated with 10 mM suffered irreversible electrophysiological damage. This study demonstrates a strong negative correlation between nicotine concentration and the reversibility of cardiomyocyte damage, offering critical experimental evidence for determining the cardiotoxic threshold of nicotine and elucidating its mechanisms of action.

Conclusions and perspectives

This research established a real-time, dynamic monitoring system for cardiomyocyte electrophysiology, employing advanced biosensing technology to systematically evaluate both the independent and synergistical effects of nicotine and PAon the electrophysiological functions of cardiomyocytes, with a particular focus on their long-term dynamic effects. Experimental findings have elucidated that nicotine exerts a concentration-dependent effect on cardiomyocyte electrophysiology. Specifically, low concentrations (≤2 mM) caused only transient and reversible suppression of electrical activity, whereas high concentrations (≥5 mM) induced pronounced arrhythmias and cell death. Furthermore, prolonged exposure exacerbated the irreversibility of the damage. In a PA-induced hyperlipidemic state, cardiomyocytes exhibited significantly heightened sensitivity to nicotine, indicating a synergistic damaging effect between the two factors. Subsequent drug withdrawal experiments revealed that cardiomyocytes damaged by moderate nicotine could gradually restore electrophysiological function after the removal of the stimulus. In contrast, cells exposed to higher nicotine concentrations exhibited limited recovery, highlighting the importance of timely intervention. The proposed electrophysiological monitoring platform facilitates the long-term, dynamic and non-invasive real-time evaluation of cardiomyocyte function, thereby underscoring its pivotal role in drug screening, toxicity assessment, and disease mechanism research.

The field potential amplitude recorded by the MEA system in this study is a comprehensive functional parameter coregulated by multiple factors. Key influencing elements include single-cell excitability gap junction mediated intercellular electrical coupling and electrode cell sealing impedance. The pronounced reduction in amplitude observed in the PA plus nicotine combined group reflects the synergistic collapse of electrophysiological integrity within the cardiac syncytium. Nicotine triggered oxidative stress inhibits fast sodium currents and L type calcium currents47,48. This change directly weakens cellular depolarization capacity and suppresses cardiomyocyte excitability. Lipotoxicity induced by lipid metabolic disturbance disrupts the normal distribution of connexin 4349. The disorder will further impair electrical signal propagation between adjacent cardiomyocytes. The current MEA recording system cannot separately quantify the individual contribution of each influencing factor. Even so the progressive decline in field potential amplitude serves as a sensitive functional indicator of collective dysfunction across the cardiac cellular network. Coordinated alterations contain decreased signal amplitude and elevated arrhythmic firing frequency. These changes constitute an integrated functional signature. They also reveal how metabolic stress and chemical stress synergistically aggravate cardiac conduction dysfunction.

The CCK 8 assay is a widely adopted classic approach for cytotoxicity evaluation. It detects the activity of cellular dehydrogenases and mitochondrial dehydrogenases act as the main detection object. This method cannot directly count viable cell numbers. The reduced absorbance in nicotine and PA treated groups does not exclusively indicate cell death. Such declines may also be confounded by early metabolic perturbations. Common manifestations cover drug induced mitochondrial dysfunction and metabolic reprogramming. These abnormal changes typically occur prior to irreversible cellular necrosis. Nicotine disrupts the mitochondrial electron transport chain. The damage will lead to artificially low CCK 8 readings even in surviving cardiomyocytes. We adopted multiple means to address this methodological limitation. We supplemented CCK 8 results with propidium iodide staining to verify plasma membrane damage. Real time MEA recording was also used to dynamically track the progression of cardiac functional impairment. Strong consistency exists among metabolic suppression, membrane leakage, and electrophysiological deterioration. The correlation solidifies the reliability of our conclusion. The combined insult of nicotine and the hyperlipidemic state can induce obvious synergistic cardiotoxicity.

The present study adopts multiple experimental methods. Research contents contain mitochondrial ultrastructural observation, ROS measurement, ion channel protein detection, and calcium transient recording. This work preliminarily uncovered a sequential oxidative stress calcium dyshomeostasis cascade mechanism. The pathway can well explain the occurrence and development of arrhythmia under combined insult of nicotine and hyperlipidemia. Future research will carry out more in depth exploration on this basis. The core research direction covers mitochondrial energy metabolism dysfunction, fine regulation of oxidative stress signaling pathways, and their crosstalk with intracellular calcium handling networks. Subsequent research can adopt diversified technical means. Researchers can apply Seahorse metabolic analysis to dynamically evaluate mitochondrial respiratory function. Gene editing technology can be used to selectively regulate key proteins such as RyR2 and connexin 43. Small molecule pharmacological interventions are also feasible to verify potential therapeutic targets. Integrating these molecular and metabolic analytical techniques with the established MEA biosensing platform will help construct a comprehensive research framework. The new system can effectively connect molecular mechanisms and macroscopic electrophysiological phenotypes. Relevant research findings will provide more systematic theoretical evidence and technical support. The final achievement is to promote the development of targeted therapeutic strategies. These schemes are mainly applied to treat arrhythmias associated with smoking and metabolic disorders.
