Professionalization of an Undergraduate Teaching Assistant program to increase participation and build career skills
Authors: Aisling S. Dugan, Kristina Monteiro, Toni-Marie Achilli
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTUndergraduate Teaching Assistant (UTA) positions offer valuable benefits for student learning, leadership development, and resume building. Here, we share the multi-year transformation of our biology UTA program, which was designed to increase awareness and visibility of UTA positions while making the roles and responsibilities more transparent to students. Professionalizing the hiring process by requiring resume, applications, training, and contract signing aims to better prepare students for the workforce. Collectively, these efforts have doubled the number of UTA applicants, indicating that students are now more aware of these opportunities and have a clearer idea of how to pursue them. Our program also includes learning objectives for UTAs, many of which focus on professional development. Finally, we share our ideas for future directions to refine and expand our UTA program.

PERSPECTIVE

Undergraduate biology programs have made strides in integrating novel pedagogical practices using student-centered approaches such as value-affirmation teaching, active learning, fostering social belonging, and research-based programs (1–4). Many of these changes have improved student learning, narrowed opportunity gaps, and increased retention of historically underrepresented students. Effective curricular and co-curricular initiatives are still needed to effectively train future biologists and retain diverse talent.

One effective way to support all students is to develop departmental employment opportunities with critical skill training and resume building in the students’ field of study. Strong STEM career growth is projected for the next decades (5, 6), and robust and meaningful changes should aim to educate, train, and retain students in these fields. Moreover, budgetary concerns in institutions of higher education further incentivize a move toward the use of Undergraduate Teaching Assistants (UTA) over Graduate Teaching Assistants. Strong and thoughtfully developed UTA programs will build greater capacity in the biological sciences, while developing student confidence, leadership, and collaborative skills that lead to success in the workforce or graduate programs.

UTA programs provide multiple benefits to the UTAs, including enhanced scientific knowledge, lab skills, communication, and leadership (7). UTAs report the experience to be personally rewarding by building their self-confidence, developing better time management skills, and providing a stronger sense of community (8). The benefit extends to the students these UTAs serve to enhance student learning, improve the student experience, support group conflict resolution, and even increase faculty productivity (9, 10).

Here, we share the programmatic interventions implemented at Brown University’s Biology Program university over 4 years to improve our UTA program. These scaffolded changes provide students with competitive compensation, a transparent, structured, and inclusive application process, clear expectations about UTA roles and responsibilities, and training in best teaching practices. In addition to mastery of a subdiscipline of biology, our UTA program promotes skills in career development, such as resume writing, teaching training workshops, and contract agreements. UTAs are an integral part of the undergraduate Biology Program at Brown University, supporting 58% of Biology courses and employing over 200 students each year. Collectively, this large footprint offers an opportunity to build professional skills in this group, while tapping into the power of near-peer mentoring. Moreover, a streamlined and transparent onboarding process may also support and encourage students with less social capital and/or knowledge of how to navigate within the scientific community.

As UTA initiatives at other universities demonstrate clear positive outcomes, our goal is to professionalize our UTA recruitment and hiring process to better reflect the job market by embedding a more streamlined application process with transparent expectations. This clearer understanding of the application steps, deadlines, and expectations of the position has resulted in tremendous growth in UTA applications.

METHODOLOGY

Initial state: Pre-2021

Before Spring 2021, the UTA hiring process in Biology faced several challenges that created strain for both faculty and students. Without a standardized process or timeline, faculty independently selected UTAs for their courses, leading to variability in how and when positions were filled. This lack of a unified hiring structure sometimes caused confusion for students, as opportunities were shared primarily through informal channels such as in-class announcements, office hours, or emails, leaving many unaware of available roles. As a result, some students with existing faculty connections may have had an advantage, unintentionally limiting accessibility for others.

Additionally, the absence of standardized role descriptions meant that UTAs often began their positions with varying levels of understanding about their responsibilities and expected outcomes. Without a formalized process to guide hiring or set clear expectations, both students and faculty experienced challenges in creating consistent and productive working relationships. These issues underscored the need for a more transparent, equitable, and structured approach to ensure that the process served the needs of all stakeholders effectively.

Phase 1: Posting positions in workday (Spring 2021)

The first step toward improving the UTA hiring process was the implementation of a requirement for all UTA positions to be posted in Workday, the university’s employment platform. This change aimed to create a more accessible and transparent system by centralizing information about available roles. By encouraging faculty to share postings broadly with their students, the initiative sought to increase awareness of opportunities and move away from informal networks and subjective selection processes that could inadvertently limit access for some students. While this adjustment significantly broadened the visibility of UTA opportunities and created a central application for faculty to evaluate applicants, it also highlighted areas that still needed attention. Application timelines varied between courses, which meant students didn’t know when to search Workday for opportunities, and students occasionally expressed confusion regarding the requirements and expectations of the UTA position once hired. Nevertheless, the transition to Workday represented a critical first step in addressing these challenges, laying the groundwork for a more equitable and consistent hiring process in the future.

Phase 2: Introducing UTA expectation sheets (Fall 2021)

To further improve equity and clarity, standardized UTA expectations were introduced in Fall 2021. This document, “UTA Guidelines and Expectations,” outlined the specific responsibilities, learning objectives, and professional development goals for each UTA position (Fig. S1). In addition, it requires students to enroll in the University Teaching Center’s UTA orientation and compensates students upon completion. Both instructors and UTAs collaboratively created and signed this document, formalizing their mutual understanding and commitment to the role and serving as a contract.

The inclusion of learning objectives was particularly helpful, as it shifted the UTA position from a task-based role to one with clear educational outcomes. UTAs were now expected to develop skills such as effective communication, critical thinking, and leadership. This elevated the role beyond routine duties such as grading papers or assisting with logistics, turning it into a valuable learning experience for both the students in the UTA role and the students they served. By incorporating professional development goals, the UTA position became a more robust opportunity for students to enhance their academic and career skills, fostering a deeper sense of purpose and engagement.

The implementation of the UTA written guideline and expectations document brought greater clarity and professionalism to the process. UTAs entered their positions with a clear understanding of their duties, while instructors benefited from a structured way to communicate expectations, which also reflected in employment contracts used in the workforce. This alignment reduced miscommunication and helped standardize UTA roles across courses. However, while these measures improved the experience for selected UTAs, they did not lead to a significant increase in the number of applications. Additionally, some students remained unaware of the hiring process, highlighting the need for further improvements in accessibility and awareness.

Phase 3: Streamlined centralized hiring process (Spring 2024)

In Summer 2024, a task force was formed with key stakeholders from across the biology department, including faculty, staff from Biology Undergraduate Education (BUE), who managed payroll, and Student Employment. This collaborative group was instrumental in developing a fully centralized hiring process for UTA positions, addressing lingering challenges related to the UTA process. The task force’s efforts ensured that all aspects of the process were carefully considered.

As part of this effort, a dedicated webpage was created on the Biology program’s website, providing a central hub for all UTA opportunities and real-time updates. While Workday remained the platform for posting positions, this webpage acted as an accessible and comprehensive resource for students, consolidating information about available roles, application timelines, and required materials. The centralized process established a unified timeline for posting, interviewing, and hiring UTAs across all biology courses, eliminating confusion caused by varying schedules. Centralized communications were sent to all Biology students via email listservs and directly to declared concentrators, ensuring consistent messaging and broad awareness of opportunities, rather than relying on channels through individual courses.

For faculty, the centralized process eliminated the need for managing disparate hiring timelines and individual course-specific procedures. This streamlined approach saved time and reduced administrative burdens, allowing faculty to focus more on teaching and mentoring. The centralized process also led to a significant increase in UTA applications, providing faculty with a larger pool of qualified candidates to choose from.

Outcomes

Number of UTA candidates

Data for the initial phase of the UTA hiring process was not available, as students did not use Workday to apply for positions and therefore data were not in a centralized location. After the second phase was initiated, the number of UTA candidates received could easily be tracked in Workday and resulted in an average of 361 candidates across three academic years (Fig. 1). Importantly, we saw a significant 139% increase in total applications in the third phase of the UTA hiring process. Our Introductory Biology course showed a 187% increase in applicants, and this was mirrored in other foundational courses, suggesting that underclassmen are more knowledgeable and empowered to apply.

Overall, the results presented here highlight two main findings. Second, the selection of UTA learning objectives highlights that many faculty and students are using the UTA written guidelines and expectations document as a contract that focuses on the development and refinement of career development knowledge and skills.

FUTURE DIRECTIONS

Phase 4: Exploring additional opportunities for engagement

As interest in the UTA program continues to develop, the number of qualified applicants exceeds the available positions. To continue to engage and develop as many applicants as possible, the BUE Office is considering several initiatives to ensure that all students who apply for UTA positions—whether selected or not—have access to meaningful opportunities to engage, develop skills, and contribute to the academic community. In addition to highlighting other existing opportunities such as peer advising or the group tutoring program, some ideas currently being explored include:

Creating a Feedback Process: The BUE Office is exploring how to provide constructive feedback to UTA applicants on their applications. This could help students better understand how to improve their materials and highlight relevant experiences in future cycles.

Applying the model to other institutions

Our model is readily adaptable to a range of institutional settings beyond Brown University. Its core components, such as transparent job postings, standardized contracts, and professional development–focused learning objectives, can be implemented by departments or institutions of any size. Centralizing the hiring process, whether through a campus-wide employment platform or a departmental webpage, increases access and reduces reliance on informal faculty networks. Formalizing expectations through contracts and collaborative learning goals supports equitable student development and mirrors authentic workforce experiences. A staged approach allows programs to build capacity and stakeholder support over time. For example, institutions might begin by centralizing job postings to increase visibility and equity, then introduce standardized contracts or expectation documents to clarify roles, followed by targeted training and learning outcomes aligned with workforce preparation. Community colleges, which often operate with fewer resources and serve highly diverse student populations, could adapt this model by integrating elements into existing peer mentoring or academic support programs, offering resume-based applications or training workshops that double as career development. Each phase provides an opportunity to gather feedback and refine implementation before scaling up. By tailoring both the content and pacing of reform to local infrastructure and student needs, other colleges and universities can enhance student engagement and broaden access to professional opportunities in the students field of study to better prepare them for future careers.

Conclusion

Professionalizing the UTA application and onboarding process by synchronizing the hiring timeline and emphasizing transparency and communication has led to a significant increase in UTA applicants. All candidates gain valuable experience in preparing an application and resume, while selected UTAs further develop their skills by participating in a teaching orientation training, reviewing professional contracts, deepening content mastery, and serving as student leaders. The UTA Guidelines and Expectations contract fosters collaboration between faculty and UTAs, ensuring the intentional development of learning goals and outcomes. Collectively, these changes create a more streamlined process that mirrors an authentic hiring experience and prioritizes student learning.
