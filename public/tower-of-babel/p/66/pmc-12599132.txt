Measurement Error in Osteometric, Photographic, and Virtual Methodologies to Quantify the Torsional Profile of the Lower Limb
Authors: Jessica S. Wollmann‐Reynolds, Audrey Mees, Bence Viola, Michelle E. Cameron
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesThe torsional profile of the lower limb consists of femoral torsion, tibial torsion, and talar neck angle. Due to high levels of inter‐individual variation and a lack of defining landmarks, these variables are difficult to precisely measure. It is important to ensure torsional profile measurement methodologies are repeatable, so that studies evaluating these variables can be compared.Materials and MethodsTwo observers collected torsional profile and linear measurements from the femur, tibia, and talus of 20 individuals using osteometric, photographic, and virtual methodologies. Intra‐ and interobserver error were assessed using the technical error of measurement (TEM), %TEM, and coefficient of reliability. Comparability between methods was evaluated using correlations, reduced major axis regression, and reduced mean squared error. Two methods for measuring the torsional profile were compared: a landmark method and a shape‐fitting method.ResultsObserver error was low for linear measurements. Torsional profile measurements have higher intra‐ and interobserver error and lower comparability between methods than linear measurements. Shape‐fitting methods for femoral torsion lowered observer error but did not improve methodological comparability. Shape‐fitting methods for tibial torsion did not substantially alter observer error but improved method comparability. Shape‐fitting methods for talar neck angle greatly improved method comparability, but not observer error.DiscussionLinear measurements have low observer error and are highly comparable between osteometric and virtual methods. There is greater observer error and lower comparability between measurement modalities for angular measurements. Shape‐fitting is a promising way to reduce observer error when measuring the torsional profile.

Introduction

The torsional profile of the lower limb includes femoral torsion, tibial torsion, and talar neck angle, which function together to influence the mobility of the hips, the rotation of the knees, and the angle of the feet during walking and standing (Bleck and Minaire 1983; Radler et al. 2010; Hudson 2016; Uemura et al. 2018). These three measurements are likely developmentally plastic in humans (Hellier and Jeffery 2006; Tardieu 2010; Scorcelletti et al. 2020) and, as such, have been used in behavioral reconstructions (Wescott et al. 2014) and to explore potential variability in locomotor mode in hominins (Pontzer et al. 2010; Marchi et al. 2017). However, these variables are often difficult to measure precisely due to a high level of morphological variation among individuals (Rittmeister et al. 2006; Mahato and Murthy 2012; Decker et al. 2013; Ries et al. 2021). This inter‐individual variability makes standardizing landmarks to measure any of these three variables in the torsional profile difficult, which has resulted in many studies seeking to evaluate the torsional profile (or individual aspects of it) utilizing different landmarks to define axes, or different positioning of subjects or skeletal elements depending on the imaging technique used (see Scorcelletti et al. 2020 for an outline of methodological variability for measuring femoral torsion in in vivo studies alone).

This variation in measurement methodologies presents a problem because it affects the reliability of conclusions and reduces the comparability of studies exploring the same measurements. For example, femoral torsion, the most well‐studied out of the three variables of the torsional profile, has had many definitions depending on the method of measurement (e.g., computed tomography, ultrasound, from skeletal remains using a goniometer or protractor, from 3D models using virtual methods), which inherently limits the ability to compare conclusions drawn from prior studies as the methodologies might utilize different landmarks and could therefore be capturing different aspects of shape variation (Kingsley and Olmsted 1948; Eckhoff et al. 1994, 2016; Scorcelletti et al. 2020). Femoral torsion can also be referred to as femoral version, femoral anteversion when the femur projects anteriorly relative to the diaphysis, or femoral retroversion when the femur projects posteriorly relative to the diaphysis (Kate 1976; Scorcelletti et al. 2020). These terms are often used interchangeably, despite using different reference axes in their definitions, which can further complicate this issue (Child 2017).

In the past, studies involving human skeletal remains have quantified the torsional profile using a goniometer or protractor for femoral torsion (Kingsley and Olmsted 1948; Child 2017) or photographs for tibial torsion and talar neck angle (Pontzer et al. 2010; Mahato and Murthy 2012). With the increasing availability of 3D surface scanners to generate virtual reproductions of skeletal elements, new options for quantifying the torsional profile of the lower limb with virtual methods are arising. Virtual methods are those which use digital data to quantify or analyze anatomy in three dimensions, often using methods which are not possible to apply to the physical objects themselves (e.g., segmentation of internal structures; Weber and Bookstein 2011). Virtual methods have been successfully used to take standard long bone measurements (Reynolds et al. 2017) as well as to measure femoral torsion (Eckhoff et al. 2016; Hartel et al. 2016). Particularly for measures of torsion, where photographs can only capture the twist along the longitudinal axis in two dimensions, the application of virtual methods is promising.

Given the issue of repeatability in measuring the torsional profile, it is prudent to ensure that the methodologies used when measuring femoral torsion, tibial torsion, and talar neck angle (1) are consistent across studies to improve comparability of results and (2) have acceptable levels of intra‐ and interobserver error to ensure that any variation among individuals included in analyses is not due to measurement error. Here, we explore measurement error in femoral torsion, tibial torsion, and talar neck angle, as well as in standard linear measurements such as bone lengths. We examined measurement error across three methodologies (osteometric, photographic, and virtual) and explored how results obtained from each methodology compared to each other. For the torsional profile, we compared two photographic and virtual measurement techniques, one which uses landmarks to determine reference axes and a method the authors created which employs shape‐fitting to determine reference axes.

Materials and Methods

Ethics approval to complete this research was granted by the University of Toronto Research Ethics Board (#41434). Thirty individuals were chosen for measurement from the J.C.B. Grant Collection at the University of Toronto, which houses the skeletal remains of individuals who lived in the Greater Toronto Area, Canada and had years of death between 1928 and 1953 (Albanese 2018). These individuals largely derive from hospitals and welfare institutions and were rehoused from the Department of Anatomy to the Department of Anthropology at the University of Toronto in the late 1980s (Albanese 2018). Community consult for these individuals was not possible, as the descendants of these individuals are unknown. All individuals were adults, with epiphyses fully fused, and were between the ages of 20 and 59 at the time of death based on records curated with these individuals. Individuals were excluded from measurement if they had pathological features or fractures affecting the lower limb identifiable by macroscopic observation. For each individual, the femur, tibia, and talus from both the right and left sides of the body were measured using osteometric, photographic, and virtual methods. The right and left sides of the body of 30 individuals were measured. Of the 30 individuals, only three were female. Data from the right and left sides of the first 10 individuals measured with each methodology were excluded from analyses to give the observers the opportunity to become comfortable with data collection protocols, various software used to process data, and the definitions used in each methodology. These practice trials were always completed on the same 10 individuals, and the measurements taken in these trials were discarded. This resulted in a final sample size of 20 individuals, with the right and left sides of the body pooled in analysis, equaling 40 measurement trials total per observer. Of those final 20 individuals, only one was female and the rest were males. Both the right and left sides of the body were included after initial analyses found substantial side biases for measurement error in methodologies (Mees et al. 2024). While we do not explicitly discuss side biases in measurement error in this paper, including both sides of the body accounted for the total level of measurement error, including any side bias.

Two observers (JWR and AM) completed data collection. JWR took osteometric measurements and photographs, and generated the 3D models for analysis. JWR completed every measurement with every methodology two times to assess intraobserver error. To assess interobserver error, AM completed all measurements with photographic and virtual methods once for each method. All measurement trials were taken from the same previously generated photograph or 3D model. Having one observer complete the same measurements with all three methodologies allowed for the comparison of results obtained from different methodologies without the influence of interobserver error (i.e., methodological comparability).

Osteometric Methods

The measurements collected with osteometric methods are outlined in Table 1 and include all measurements except tibial torsion and talar neck angle, which currently do not have established osteometric methods that we are aware of. Measurement definitions are given in Table 2 and visualized in Figure S1. Femoral torsion and femoral neck‐shaft angle were measured with a goniometer. Femoral bicondylar angle was measured with an osteometric board and a protractor. Long bone lengths were measured with an osteometric board. Femoral head and talar dimensions were taken with sliding calipers. One observer, JWR, collected osteometric measurements in two trials, separated by a minimum of 1 h. Due to the timing of data collection, it was not possible for a second observer to complete osteometric measurements.

Photographic Methods

Only the variables in the torsional profile were measured with photographic methods (Table 1). Other linear measurements were not taken from photographs because the focus in photographic measurements was on the variables in the torsional profile. All photographs were taken by a single observer, JWR, using a Nikon D7100 SLR with a 60 mm Micro Nikkor 1:2.8D macro lens (effective focal length 90 mm). Long bones were placed posterior‐side‐down on a table with the long axis of the bone aligned to a straight line on the table. The femur rested with the posteriormost points of the distal condyles and greater trochanter on the table. The diaphysis was aligned to a sagittal plane defined by Ruff and Hayes (1983), which passed under the mediolateral midpoint of the diaphysis just distal to the lesser trochanter and the deepest point on the intercondylar notch. The tibia rested with the posteriormost points of the tibial plateau and distal end on the table, and the diaphysis was aligned to a sagittal plane that passed under the mediolateral midpoint of the proximal and distal articular surfaces (Ruff and Hayes 1983). The camera was set on a tripod at a height of 92 cm, at a distance of 50 cm from the table. Photographs were taken of the proximal and distal ends of each long bone, with the diaphyseal axis of the bone centered in the field of view. The talus was set on the table inferior‐side‐down, with a tripod of support between the talar head, lateral process, and posterior process. A photograph of the talus was taken from above, with the camera set at 50 cm away from the talus, with the center of the trochlea in the center of the field of view. Photos were processed using the open‐source photo analyzing software FIJI (Schindelin et al. 2012).

Two photographic methods were compared (Table 3, Figure 1). The first method (Photographic Landmark Method) uses visual landmarks and measured midpoints to determine reference axes from which angular measurements are taken. The second method (Photographic Shape‐fitting Method) uses a combination of measured midpoints and centroids of best‐fit shapes determined from skeletal geometry, primarily articular surfaces, to establish reference axes. Full definitions of each measurement are in Table 3 and graphical depictions are in Figure 1. Full protocols for the Photographic Shape‐fitting Method are available in the Tables S1–S3.

Two observers, JWR and AM, completed measurements with the Photographic Landmark Method and Photographic Shape‐fitting Method to assess interobserver error for both methods. One observer, JWR, completed two measurement trials for each methodology to assess intraobserver error. Each measurement trial was completed with the same photograph of a previously oriented bone.

Virtual Methods

All 11 measurements were obtained from whole‐bone 3D models using virtual methods (Table 1). Surface scans were captured by one observer, JWR, using a DAVID SLS‐2 structured light scanner with a resolution of 0.06 mm (DAVID 3D Solutions GbR). Surface scans were stitched together to generate whole‐bone 3D models using the software DAVID 3D Scanner Pro (version 4.5.3, DAVID 3D Solutions GbR). All analyses using virtual methods were completed in a CAD modeling program, either RapidFormXOR 3 64 SP1 (version 3.1.0.0, INUS Technology Inc.) or Geomagic Design X (version 2023.3.0, Oqton Inc.). Geomagic Design X is a lateral iteration of RapidFormXOR 3 after RapidFormXOR 3 was acquired by the parent company of Geomagic Design X, and these programs are considered interchangeable.

Prior to measurements, one observer, JWR, aligned 3D models to reference axes. Both programs generate X, Y, and Z reference axes in a global coordinate system. Using reference geometry such as planes, vectors, and points, 3D models can be oriented to this global coordinate system to establish reference axes. Long bone 3D models were aligned to anatomical axes as defined by Ruff and Hayes (1983). For the femur, the coronal plane passes through the anterior–posterior midpoints of the diaphysis at a point just distal to the lesser trochanter and just proximal to the distal condyles (Ruff and Hayes 1983). The sagittal plane is defined by the mediolateral midpoint on the diaphysis just distal to the lesser trochanter and the deepest point on the intercondylar notch (Ruff and Hayes 1983). For the tibia, the coronal plane passes through the anterior–posterior midpoints of the proximal and distal articular surfaces, while the sagittal plane passes through the mediolateral midpoints of the proximal and distal articular surfaces (Ruff and Hayes 1983). The talus was aligned to a horizontal plane, so that the inferiormost points of the talar head, lateral process, and posterior process contacted the plane.

Virtual measurement methodology was designed to mimic osteometric methods as closely as possible (Table 2). For linear measurements, this typically involved using point‐to‐point or plane‐to‐point measurements. For femoral neck‐shaft angle and femoral bicondylar angle, axes were established with reference planes and a plane‐to‐plane method was used to measure the angle between the axes. Two virtual methods for measuring the torsional profile were compared (Table 4, Figure 2). The first method (Virtual Landmark Method) is based on the Photographic Landmark Method and uses similar landmarks and measured midpoints to establish reference axes. The second method (Virtual Shape‐fitting Method) uses a combination of measured midpoints and CAD solid primitives or 2D mesh sketches generated from skeletal morphology to generate reference geometry. Solid primitives in CAD modeling are the simplest geometric shapes a program can create, such as spheres, cylinders, and cubes. Similarly, 2D mesh sketches are simple entities such as lines, arcs, and splines, which can be extracted from the silhouette of a 3D mesh. This method generates best‐fit solid primitives or 2D sketches from articular surfaces or diaphyseal sections and uses those shapes to generate reference geometry such as center points, vectors, and planes. That reference geometry can then be used to measure the angles of the torsional profile. Full definitions of each measurement are in Table 4 and graphical depictions are in Figure 2. Full protocols for the Virtual Shape‐fitting Method are available in the Tables S4–S6.

All measurements were taken from 3D models that had been previously aligned to reference axes by JWR. Two observers, JWR and AM, completed all measurements with virtual methods and JWR completed these measurements twice. For the torsional profile variables, AM completed trials for both the Virtual Landmark Method and the Virtual Shape‐fitting Method and JWR completed two measurement trials for each methodology. For measurements of long bone torsion only, the femur and tibia were realigned from anatomical axes to a horizontal plane in the global coordinate system that was in contact with the posteriormost points of the bones (see Tables S4 and S5 for more detail). Each observer completed this realignment as part of their measurement trial for torsion. All other measurements were taken from previously aligned bones to minimize error due to positioning.

Statistical Methods

Analyses were conducted with measurements from the right and left sides of the body pooled together. Intra‐ and interobserver error was assessed using the technical error of measurement (TEM) and the coefficient of reliability (R) as defined by Ulijaszek and Lourie (1994), which are commonly used metrics in anthropometry. TEM describes the differences between observations relative to the total number of observations made (Ulijaszek and Lourie 1994). TEM to calculate intra‐ or interobserver error from two measurement trials is calculated using Equation (1), where D is the difference between observations made for each individual and N is the number of individuals measured (Ulijaszek and Lourie 1994). (1) TEM=ΣD22N

The coefficient of reliability (R) describes what proportion of the variation between individuals is free from measurement error (Ulijaszek and Lourie 1994). R ranges from 0.00 to 1.00 and is calculated using Equation (2), where SD is the standard deviation for all subjects, including measurement error (Ulijaszek and Lourie 1994). SD is calculated from all measurements taken by both observers (Ulijaszek and Lourie 1994). (2) R=1−TEM2SD2

We further determined %TEM by calculating TEM as a percentage of the mean of the measurement, as defined by Pomeroy et al. (2018). There are no accepted standards for reliability in measurements, but a measurement is considered to be more reliable when TEM and %TEM are low and R is high (approaching 1.00; Ulijaszek and Lourie 1994; Pomeroy et al. 2018). TEM, %TEM, and R were calculated in Microsoft Excel (version 2508).

Results obtained using different methodologies were compared by first correlating measurements taken with different methodologies against each other and calculating Pearson's r and the p‐value for the correlation. Statistical significance was determined at α = 0.05. If a correlation was significant, reduced major axis (RMA) regressions and root mean squared error (RMSE) were calculated. Virtual measurements were regressed against osteometric and photographic measurements. Photographic measurements were regressed against osteometric measurements. Measurement methodologies were considered to be more comparable the closer R 2 got to 1.00, if the correlation was significant at α = 0.05. Methods would also be considered more comparable the closer RMSE got to 0, which would indicate lower levels of error in the model. Correlations, regressions, and RMSE were calculated in R (version 4.2.2; R Core Team 2022) using packages stats (R Core Team 2022), lmodel2 (Legendre 2018), and Metrics (Hamner and Frasco 2018), and plots were generated with the package ggplot2 (Wickham 2016). All data analysis with R was completed using RStudio (v. 2024.04.2; Posit Team 2024).

Only intraobserver error was evaluated for osteometric measurements. Intra‐ and interobserver error was determined for all other measurements. All methodological comparisons were made using data collected by the same observer (JWR). Osteometric and virtual methods were compared for all measurements. Methodological comparisons were also made between osteometric and photographic methods for femoral torsion, and photographic and virtual methods for all variables in the torsional profile. For photographic and virtual methods, measurement error in the landmark methods was compared to error in the shape‐fitting methods. We qualitatively compared TEM, %TEM, and R for the landmark and shape‐fitting methods. We interpreted a decrease in TEM and %TEM and an increase in R as evidence of improved methodological reliability. We also evaluated r, R 2, and RMSE for the relationships between the photographic and virtual methods to determine if the shape‐fitting methods affected the comparability of different modalities relative to the landmark methods. If R 2 was higher and RMSE was lower in one method relative to the other, we interpreted this as evidence of better comparability.

Osteometrics Results

We found very low levels of intraobserver error in osteometric measurements, particularly for linear measurements using well‐defined landmarks, such as bone lengths. %TEM for intraobserver error of osteometric linear measurements was always less than 1%, except for talar width, which was less than 2%, and R ranged from 0.95 to 0.99 (Table S7). Of the angular measurements, femoral neck‐shaft angle had the lowest R (0.75), and femoral torsion measured with a goniometer had the highest R (0.94; Table S7). We were not able to assess interobserver error for osteometric measurements.

Photographic Results

In photographic methods, the shape‐fitting method had lower intra‐ and interobserver error than the landmark method for all three measurements of the torsional profile (Table 6). Femoral torsion measured with the landmark photographic method had %TEM over 20% for both intra‐ and interobserver error and R of 0.76 and 0.80 respectively. Femoral torsion with the shape‐fitting photographic method had 10% or less %TEM and R above 0.97 for both intra‐ and interobserver error. In tibial torsion, intraobserver error in %TEM lowered from 15% to 7% and interobserver error lowered from 20% to 16% with the shape‐fitting method. R for intraobserver error improved from 0.91 to 0.98 and for interobserver error from 0.87 to 0.90. Talar neck angle with the landmark method had a complete lack of agreement in interobserver data, with R at −0.03, indicating more measurement error than the variation present in the data. With the shape‐fitting method, R in interobserver error improved to 0.69, and R in intraobserver error improved from 0.42 with the landmark method to 0.81 with the shape‐fitting method.

Similarly to osteometric measurements, we found very low levels of intraobserver error in virtual linear measurements excluding the torsional profile. For all measurements except maximum tibial length, intraobserver error was lower than interobserver error.

Method Comparability

Standard linear measurements taken from osteometrics and virtual methods were very comparable (Table S9, Figure S2). R 2 for the regressions was 0.99 for all long bone lengths and was 0.95 or above for maximum femoral head diameter and talar length. Talar width and angular measurements, namely femoral neck‐shaft angle, and femoral bicondylar angle, had the lowest R 2 values, at 0.75, 0.63, and 0.31 respectively.

For torsional profile measurements, femoral torsion was the most comparable across methods, tibial torsion was slightly less comparable, and talar neck was not comparable (Table 8, Figures 3 and 4). R 2 was between 0.80 and 0.88 for all femoral torsion comparisons. The highest R 2 was between photo data and virtual data using the landmark method at 0.88, which also had the lowest RMSE at 2.47. In all comparisons for the femur, the landmark method had a higher R 2 and lower RMSE than the shape‐fitting method. For tibial torsion, the shape‐fitting method increased R 2 from 0.61 to 0.79 and decreased RMSE from 9.23 to 3.68 compared to the landmark method for comparisons between photographic and virtual data. For talar neck angle, the correlation between data from virtual methods and data from photographic methods generated using the landmark method was not significant, indicating a complete lack of agreement. Shape‐fitting methods for talar neck angle improved the relationship to a significant correlation and an R 2 of 0.34, with RMSE of 7.75.

Discussion

We explored measurement error in the torsional profile of the lower limb and linear measurements across osteometric, photographic, and virtual methodologies. For torsional profile variables we compared landmark measurement techniques to shape‐fitting techniques. Finally, we examined how the results obtained from each of these measurement methodologies compare to each other.

Linear measurements with well‐defined landmarks, such as long bone lengths, had the lowest intra‐ and interobserver error out of all measurements for both osteometric and virtual methods. These measurements taken using osteometric and virtual methods were also highly comparable, with high R 2 values. This indicates that virtual measurement methodology is very precise and accurately mirrors more standard osteometric measurement methodologies. Of the standard linear measurements, talar width had the highest %TEM and the lowest coefficient of determination between osteometric and virtual methods. This is likely due to some level of subjectivity in determining the location of the “midpoints” of the medial and lateral borders of the superior articular surface of the trochlea.

Compared to standard linear measurements, angular measurements, namely femoral bicondylar angle and femoral neck‐shaft angle, had higher levels of intra‐ and interobserver error and were less comparable between osteometric and virtual methods. This is likely due to a greater degree of subjectivity in the selection of axes for these angular measurements. For virtual methods, alternative strategies using best‐fit shapes, such as those we applied to measure variables in the torsional profile, could be utilized to measure femoral bicondylar angle and neck‐shaft angle. This would decrease the subjectivity of axis selection and improve intra‐ and interobserver error. Such methodologies have been pursued for measuring femoral neck‐shaft angle, where successive cross‐sectional ellipses are fit to the femoral neck to determine the neck axis (Bonneau et al. 2012; Bonneau 2013) and could easily be translated to bicondylar angle. As these methods already exist, we did not test new shape‐fitting methodologies for those measurements in this study.

Torsional Profile: Photographs

In photographs, shape‐fitting methods had lower intra‐ and interobserver error than landmark methods for all variables in the torsional profile, particularly for femoral torsion and talar neck angle. Previous landmark methods for these variables did not expressly define a method for determining the center of the femoral head and neck to measure femoral torsion or for determining the head–neck axis to measure talar neck angle, which introduced subjectivity when defining the axes that passed through these regions. Shape‐fitting methodology for photographic methods generates reference geometry to more clearly define how the reference axes for the femur and talus can be established and thereby reduces observer error. This is particularly true for the talus, which had more measurement error than variation in the data for the photographic landmark method. Photographic shape‐fitting methods for tibial torsion only slightly reduced measurement error. Between landmark and shape‐fitting methods, the difference in methodology was in determining the proximal articular angle and the methods to determine the distal articular angle remained constant across both methods. These minor improvements in observer error suggest that shape‐fitting at the proximal end is more repeatable between observers compared to measuring to midpoints in photographs. Future analyses could consider how measurement error might differ at the proximal and distal ends of the tibia to find more targeted methods of improvement in measuring tibial torsion from photographs.

Torsional Profile: Virtual Methods

Shape‐fitting in virtual methods reduced observer error in femoral torsion, but did not substantially alter measurement error in tibial torsion and talar neck angle. As with photographic methods, the addition of best‐fit shapes to measure femoral torsion virtually decreased subjectivity in axis selection, meaning shape‐fitting represents a repeatable method to measure femoral torsion from virtual models. Shape‐fitting for tibial torsion increased intraobserver error but did not substantially change interobserver error. As with photographic methods, it would be interesting to explore if error is arising at the proximal or distal end for both the shape‐fitting and landmark methods. In landmark methods, the proximal articular axis was assumed to be set at 0 degrees after the tibia had been aligned to anatomical axes. When completing their trials, each observer only had to establish the distal articular axis, as the proximal articular axis was already set. Therefore, the only source of error is from measurements taken to establish the distal articular angle. In shape‐fitting methods, both the proximal and distal articular axes were determined by each observer. These additional steps introduce more opportunities for error to arise compared to the landmark method, yet the reliability of the methods did not change substantially. This could suggest that the shape‐fitting methods to measure the distal articular axis decrease measurement error compared to the landmark methods, but this should be explored further. Shape‐fitting methods for talar neck angle improved intraobserver error but not interobserver error. This is likely due to subjectivity between observers in selecting the articular surface areas to which shapes will be fit. For example, to determine the center point of the talar head and neck, a sphere is fit to the talonavicular articular surface. However, the talonavicular articular surface has substantial overlap with the subtalar facet and very rarely has a clearly defined border between these two surfaces. This introduces subjectivity in the selection of the articular surface that should be included in generating the best‐fit shape, which could account for the lack of agreement between observers.

Torsional Profile: Method Comparability

In methodological comparisons, femoral torsion measurements across all modalities have similar levels of comparability. Shape‐fitting and landmark methods had similar levels of comparability across all modalities, meaning that, although shape‐fitting methods have lower observer error than landmark methods, measurements derived from photos and 3D models are not any more or less comparable using shape‐fitting methods. Shape‐fitting methods for measuring tibial torsion did not substantially reduce the error of the measurement taken from photos or 3D models, but did improve the comparability of these methodologies. This is an important consideration for determining the level of comparability between studies that utilize different methodologies for measuring long bone torsions. Shape‐fitting methods for talar neck angle substantially improved the comparability of photographic and virtual measurements, but not to a high level. Based on this low level of comparability, talar neck angles measured using virtual and photographic methods are measuring almost completely different aspects of talar morphology. We believe this is related to the amount of superior articular surface area that is visible on a photograph relative to a 3D model. In either the landmark or shape‐fitting methods, when determining the midpoints of the lateral and medial borders of the superior articular surface, a photograph taken from above is not capturing the entire surface area of the talar trochlea, so the trochlear axis is placed in a position on a photograph that is not comparable to its position on a 3D model. Therefore, while shape‐fitting might have reduced subjectivity in determining the head–neck axis, it is still not capturing the same aspects of talar anatomy between photographs and virtual methods due to differences in defining the trochlear axis.

Limitations

Shape‐fitting methods, while promising, are not without limitations. While our updated methodologies improved measurement error for femoral torsion, there remain issues with determining appropriate borders of articular surfaces in the tibia and talus, as well as with what portions of articular surfaces should be included in the regions created when generating best‐fit shapes. The methods presented here could still be improved upon to aid with these issues, even by simply utilizing additional tools in Geomagic Design X. Further, a particularly important point in applying these methods to anthropological questions is the issue of preservation. These methods currently require that an entire bone be preserved, with minimal damage to any articular surface, which will inherently limit the applicability of these methods to fragmentary or poorly preserved collections. Finally, there are additional sources of measurement error that we did not explore in depth in this paper; for example measurement error in alignment. Measurements were always taken from the same pre‐aligned photographs and pre‐aligned scans. Differences in the alignment of bones could adjust the perspective from which the measurement is taken, meaning that the alignment of the bones or models is an additional source of measurement error not accounted for here.

Conclusion

Linear measurements with well‐defined landmarks, such as long bone lengths, have low error and high comparability between osteometric and virtual methods, affirming that virtual measurement methodologies accurately simulate traditional osteometric measurement methodologies. Angular measurements, including femoral bicondylar angle, femoral neck‐shaft angle, and the torsional profile, have greater subjectivity in the selection of axes and therefore higher degrees of measurement error. Shape‐fitting methodology to measure the torsional profile reduced observer error for femoral torsion in both photographs and scans, but did not greatly alter error when measuring tibial torsion. Both the landmark and shape‐fitting method for measuring tibial torsion are still fairly reliable and have similar degrees of error between methods for both photographic and virtual methods. Shape‐fitting for the talus improved intraobserver error, but worsened interobserver error. For all three measurements in the torsional profile, it is important to consider what methodologies were used to measure these variables, as the methodology selection will affect the comparability of results obtained with different methods. These shape‐fitting methods represent a promising way to measure the torsional profile from skeletal elements and could be used in the future in interpretations of behavior or locomotor mode.
