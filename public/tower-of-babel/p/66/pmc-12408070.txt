Exploring Changes in Body Composition Before and After a National Collegiate Athletic Association Division I Football Season of Marching Artists
Authors: Noelle J. Smith, Brett S. Nickerson, Ronald L. Snarr, Matthew P. Brancaleone
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The purpose of this study was to observe the changes in body composition of collegiate marching band artists after a competitive season NCAA Division I football season. Thirty-seven marching artists (7 females, 31 males; age: 21.5 (1.4) years; height: 177.2 (9.5) cm had body fat percentage (BF%), fat mass (FM), android adiposity, gynoid adiposity, bone mineral content (BMC), and lean soft tissue (LST) measured before and after a competitive season of Division I football. Body composition measures were conducted using single-frequency bioelectrical impedance analysis device (Quantum IV, RJL systems, Clinton MI). Pre- and post-season differences in anthropometrics and body composition were evaluated using repeated measures analysis of variance with Cohen’s d statistics to estimate the magnitude of effect. Over the 15 weeks, participants experienced an average body mass loss of −0.4 (2.5) kg (d=0.17). BF% and FM increased on average by 1.4 (1.8)% (d=0.78) and 0.95 (1.8) kg, respectively. There were small increases in both android (0.08 (0.2) kg; d=0.36) and gynoid (0.10 (0.3) kg; d=0.38) fat throughout the season. Additionally, BMC showed a trivial increase of 0.002 (0.1) kg (d=0.02), despite a large decrease in mean LST (−1.40 (1.3) kg; d=1.06). Nutrition and physical activity level may have led to physical changes. Interventions designed to maintain adiposity throughout a season may be necessary. Additionally, resistance training programs may be appropriate to help maintain or increase LST throughout a season. Healthcare providers should be aware of changes in body composition which may lead to higher prevalence of injuries.

Introduction

Marching bands are often an integral part to American collegiate athletics as it is common for bands to perform during their competitions, primarily football. Preseason marching band rehearsals often begin in August and continue until the American football season has concluded.1 During this time, marching artists remain active for up to nine hours on a football game days,1 with varying levels of physical activity that may peak at the moderate to intense levels.2 Young adults aged 18–29 years take approximately 5,250 steps to 6,250 steps a day,3 whereas marching artists may take approximately 13,260 steps on a rehearsal day and that number increases to approximately 16,174 steps on game days.4 Additionally, marching performers bear the external weight of their instruments, such as a sousaphone or bass drum, which can weigh as much as 35 pounds and 20 pounds, respectively.1

The volume and intensity of activity5 in addition to carrying an external load6 may evoke body composition changes in marching artists throughout the season. Unfortunately, longitudinal changes in body composition of marching artists have yet to be investigated despite the known prolonged activity duration, intensity, and volume that they experience throughout a marching season.7 Accordingly, a study focused on changes in body composition of marching artists is warranted.

Electrical impedance analysis (BIA) technology has emerged over the last decade and provides numerous advantages when employed to monitor changes in body composition over time. For instance, traditional BIA models provide whole-body analyses while newer models can also provide segmental body composition measurements. BIA is also a more practical technique for measuring body composition than sophisticated body composition methods often found in research facilities such as dual energy X-ray absorptiometry and air displacement plethysmography. Specifically, BIA is easy to use, time efficient, and requires minimal training. Moreover, previous authors have found BIA devices to be valid and reliable in diverse populations as well as college-aged individuals.8–10 Altogether, the benefits of BIA make it a desired tool for monitoring alterations in body composition longitudinally.

Despite BIA being available on college campuses in a variety of settings (e.g., recreational faculties, athletic facilities, research laboratories), research has yet to utilize this technique in collegiate marching artists. Consequently, longitudinal changes in marching artists’ body composition throughout a collegiate football season have yet to be determined. Examining body composition changes in marching artists throughout the season may lead to a better understanding of the effects of marching-related activities on body composition. Moreover, information obtained from a longitudinal study in this area may lead to improved interventions such as strength and conditioning programs or nutritional interventions from health care providers during the season. Therefore, the purpose of this study was to observe the changes in body composition of collegiate marching band artists after a competitive season.

Methods

Participants

Marching artists from a single National Collegiate Athletics Association (NCAA) Division I university were recruited to participate in this study. Eligible participants were 1) at least 18 years of age; 2) reported no cardiac, pulmonary, or metabolic diseases; and 3) a member of a collegiate marching band. Exclusion criteria included persons with non-disease related conditions that may affect body composition or intra- and extra-cellular fluid. Sample size was determined via G*Power software, which suggested that a sample size of 30 participants would be sufficient for a power of 0.80, a significance level of 0.05, and a moderate effect size of 0.60 in a paired samples t-test. During the marching season, marching artists rehearsed five days a week for 1.5 hours each day and performed during six home football game days and two away football game days. All participants provided written informed consent and completed a medical history questionnaire before the study. This study was conducted according to the guidelines laid down in the Declaration of Helsinki and all procedures involving human participants were approved by the Institutional Review Board of the host university (IRB# 2018H0334) and the study is aligned with the ethical policies of this journal.

Protocol

Pre-season data collection occurred two weeks the first football competition of the season, and post-season testing occurred within one week following the last regular season football competition which totaled 15 weeks. All research participants reported to the laboratory for data collection following pre-testing guidelines, which included 1) no high-intensity exercise for 24 hours, 2) fasting less than or equal to 8 hours, 3) no alcohol or caffeine for greater to or equal to 24 hours, 4) no water intake for less than two hours. Once pre-testing guideline adherence was verified by participants’ self-report, hydration (i.e., urine specific gravity), anthropometrics (i.e., height and body mass), and single frequency bioimpedance analysis (SFBIA) assessments were completed. Prior to all anthropometric and body composition measurements, shoes, jewelry, and metallic objects were removed to minimize measurement error. Hydration was assessed via urine specific gravity using a hand-held refractometer (Atago SUR-NE, Atago Corp Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). Participants exceeding a urine specific gravity of 1.029 were excluded from participation.11 Standing height was measured to the nearest 0.1 cm using a stadiometer (SECA 213, Seca Ltd., Hamburg, Germany), while body mass (BM) was measured to the nearest 0.1 kg using a digital scale (Tanita BWB-800, Tanita Corporation, Tokyo, Japan).

Single-Frequency Bioimpedance Analysis

Participants had body fat percentage (BF%), fat mass (FM), fat free mass (FFM), LST, bone mineral content (BMC), total body water (TBW), intracellular water (ICW), and extracellular water (ECW) measured with a single-frequency BIA (SFBIA) device (Quantum IV, RJL systems, Clinton MI). For SFBIA testing, participants were asked to lay supine on an athletic training table with shoes and socks removed, and arms placed ≥ 30° away from the body with legs separated and not touching. Excess hair at electrode sites was removed and skin was cleaned with alcohol pads and dried prior to electrode placement. Surface electrodes were placed on the right and left wrist beside the ulnar head and on the first joint of the middle finger. Surface electrodes were also placed on the right and left foot beside the medial malleolus and on the base of the second toe. Once the electrodes were placed, leads were attached and a single frequency (i.e., 50 kHz) whole-body impedance measurement was obtained for each participant.

Statistical Analysis

All data was analyzed using SPSS version 28.0 (IBM©, Somers, NY). Prior to statistical analyses, data were assessed for normality using histogram analyses and Shapiro-Wilks tests. Outliers were removed if the reported value exceeded a z-score ≥ ±3.0. Cohen’s d to estimate the magnitude of effect between body mass and body composition outcomes during pre- and post-season. 12 To interpret the Cohen’s d values, Hopkin’s scale of magnitude for small sample sizes was used as follows: trivial effect <0.20, small effect 0.20 – 0.59, moderate effect 0.60 – 1.19, large effect 1.20 –1.99, and very large effect ≥ 2.0.11 In addition to effect size, the smallest worthwhile change was calculated to display the minimal value needed to denote a practical, meaningful change. Smallest worthwhile change was estimated for each dependent variable using an effect size of 0.3 multiplied by the baseline standard deviation. Descriptive statistics for all data were reported as mean (standard deviation) unless otherwise noted, along with minimum, maximum, and mean change values. Pearson’s product moment correlations (r) and coefficients of determination (R2) were also estimated to examine the relationships between individual changes in body mass and body composition over the course of the band season. Correlation coefficient (r) values with a value of 0.00 to 0.19 were deemed very low, 0.20 to 0.39 were low, 0.40 to 0.59 were moderate, 0.60 to 0.79 were high, and 0.80 to 1.00 were very high.

Results

Fifty-seven marching artists were recruited, however, twenty were removed and excluded from data analyses due to incomplete data (i.e., did not complete both pre- and post-season data collection sessions; n=16), inaccurate assessments (i.e., negative android fat estimations; n=2), or exceeded a z-score of ±3.0 (n=2). Thirty-seven marching artists (age: 21.4 (1.5) years; height: 177.1 (9.5) cm) completed the testing for both pre- and post-season and were included within the statistical analyses. Mean change values and individual plots of difference are displayed in Table 1 and Figures 1–2, respectively. Over the 15 weeks, the group experienced a trivial average body mass loss of 0.5 (2.5) kg (d=0.18), ranging from a 4.9 kg gain to a 4.6 kg loss. While the mean change in body mass was deemed trivial, FM increased on average by 1.0 (1.8) kg (d=0.54; r=0.85; R2=0.72), while FFM exhibited a moderate decrease of 1.4 (1.4) kg (d=1.05; r=0.73; R2=0.53). The largest increases in FM and FFM were 5.0 kg and 1.1 kg, respectively; whereas the largest decrements in FM and FFM were 3.4 kg and 4.4 kg, respectively. Based on small and moderate changes in FM and FFM, mean BF% increased for the group on average by 1.4 (1.8)%, with a maximum gain of 6.5% and loss of 2.9% (d=0.78). The changes in BF% showed a positive, moderate-strength relationship to changes in body mass (r=0.65, R2=0.42). Regarding BF distribution, there were small increases in both android (0.1 (0.2) kg; d=0.34; r=0.89; R2=0.79) and gynoid (0.1 (0.3) kg; d=0.38; r=0.71; R2=0.50) fat throughout the season. Additionally, BMC showed a trivial increase of 0.001 (0.1) kg (d=0.02), despite a moderate decrease in mean LST (1.4 (1.3) kg; d=1.11; r=0.69; R2=0.48). Lastly, moderate reductions in TBW (1.0 (1.2) kg; d=0.86; r=0.63; R2=0.40) and ECW (0.7 (0.7) kg; d=0.99) were observed, with only a small mean decrease in ICW (0.3 (0.7) kg; d=0.47).

Discussion

The purpose of this study was to observe the changes in body composition in collegiate marching band artists over a competitive Division I football season. Results revealed that BF% moderately increased (1.4%; d=0.78) in the marching artists, despite a small loss in body mass (0.5 kg; d=0.18). The changes in BF% were accompanied by an increase in FM (1.0 kg; d=0.54) and a moderate decrease in FFM (1.4 kg; d=1.05). A large portion of the decrease in FFM is attributed to the loss of TBW (1.0 L). Consequently, the loss in FFM, comprising of TBW and other components, such as muscle and bone, totaled 1.5 kg, with 1.0 kg attributed to water loss and the remaining 0.5 kg is likely due to reductions in other tissues.

In-season changes in body composition has primarily focused on collegiate athletes throughout a competitive season. Moreover, the current findings are contrasting with many studies conducted in athletes throughout a competitive season. Summer et al13 found significant decreases in FM and increases in LM (i.e., arms, legs, trunk, gynoid, and total) when examining the body composition of NCAA Division I female field hockey athletes. This conflicts with current study findings, which observed an increase in FM and decrease in LM of marching artists. Fields et al14 reported no changes in body composition of NCAA Division I men’s basketball athletes. However, NCAA Division I women’s basketball experienced reductions in FFM,14 which is similar to the present study results. Lastly, Stanforth et al15 observed decreases in FM and BF% in NCAA Division I women’s basketball athletes, and increase in women volleyball LM during a competitive, which both conflict with current study findings.

The reasons for conflicting results between the present study and previous research is worth discussion. First, the use of different body composition methods should be carefully considered. For instance, previous research has used dual energy x-ray absorptiometry (DXA) and air displacement plethysmography (ADP) to track changes in athletes’ body composition throughout a competitive season,13–15 whereas the current study utilized SFBIA. Although many authors have validated SFBIA against DXA and multi-compartment models,8,16,17 it is conceivable that results may have been different with a more sophisticated laboratory-based method, such as a DXA. Another reason for the discrepancy may be differences in the physical demands between marching artists and other collegiate athletics. For instance, the physical activity of marching artists was measured at 4.5 to 6.0 metabolic equivalents of task (MET),18,19 whereas engaging in high-intensity sports such as maximum sprinting or basketball demands significantly higher physical exertion, typically requiring MET values well above 6.0.20 Altogether, future research may seek to monitor physical activity in marching artists during a competitive football season.

Clinically, sports medicine health care professionals should recognize the body composition changes that may occur to marching artists during a competitive NCAA Division I football season. Being cognizant of these changes may help sports medicine professionals cater nutritional interventions which may assist in optimizing health and performance of marching artists. Additionally, the utilization of strength training interventions for marching artists may assist in maintaining LST and therefore, decreasing the likelihood of injury throughout the season.

Although the current study has many strengths, there are limitations worth mentioning. First, the use of one body composition device (i.e., SFBIA) may be viewed as a limitation. Ideally, a more sophisticated body composition technique such as DXA used within the study. SFBIA has been shown to under estimated percentage of fat and fat mass and overestimate lean mass.21 Additionally, SFBIA cannot detect small changes in body composition that DXA is able to quantify.21 However, testing was restricted to the marching band facilities. Therefore, the current research team was limited to utilizing a portable body composition device. Accordingly, future research may seek to utilize a more accepted criterion method for body composition measures. Second, a comparison group was not evaluated in the current study. Therefore, it is unknown whether the body composition changes are similar between marching artists and their peers. Thirdly, the current study did not record objective physical activity or nutrition. Consequently, the current research study team are unable to determine whether changes in body composition are attributable to changes in physical activity, nutrition, or other potential factors. Lastly, only 65% of the eligible participants had complete data. Participants likely did not complete post-season testing due to scheduling conflicts between academic courses and scheduled data collection times. Scheduling conflicts were limited during pre-season testing due to academic courses not beginning prior to initial testing. Altogether, future research might control for these areas of limitations and further explore the changes in body composition of marching artists.

In summary, there was an increase in BF% and a decrease in LST% over the football season in collegiate marching band artists. These findings were small and may have been within the standard error of measure and normal fluctuations of marching artists. However, these findings may also be due to the physical demands required of marching artists and a lack of nutritional and resistance training intervention throughout the season. These outcomes highlight that collegiate marching artists experienced adverse changes in body composition throughout a competitive season.
