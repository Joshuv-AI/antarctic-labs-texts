IL-6 in Checkpoint Myocarditis
Authors: Daniel A. Zlotoff, Karishma Rahman, Steven M. Blum
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corresponding Author

More than one-half of the patients in the United States with advanced malignancies are eligible to receive immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICIs) for approved indications.1 ICIs offer improved outcomes to these patients but can cause significant immune-related adverse events (irAEs), such as ICI-associated myocarditis (ICI-My). ICI-My is fortunately uncommon, occurring in approximately 1% of ICI recipients, but it unfortunately is among the most clinically concerning irAEs because of its high morbidity and mortality rate.2 Making a definitive diagnosis of ICI-My can be challenging,3 as endomyocardial biopsies carry procedural risk and are not available at all centers, and cardiac magnetic resonance imaging can have limited sensitivity.4

The identification of reliable blood-based biomarkers to help diagnose, risk-stratify, and treat patients with ICI-My is an attractive goal. Circulating troponin T can identify cardiac injury and provide both diagnostic and prognostic information for patients with suspected or confirmed ICI-My,5 but it is not specific to inflammatory cardiac damage. Studies of human ICI-My and mouse myocarditis models have implicated cytokines such as CCL4, CCL5, CXCL9, CXCL10, CXCL16, IL-1B, IL-6, and TNF-α in disease pathogenesis,6, 7, 8, 9, 10 but none of these has yet been implemented in routine clinical care. Similarly, there are no rigorous prospective data for the management of ICI-My, and consensus guidelines recommend a backbone of high-dose corticosteroids followed by secondary agents that use a wide range of therapeutic mechanisms (eg, JAK inhibitors, CTLA-4 Ig, intravenous immunoglobulin, mycophenolate, TNF-α inhibition, IL-6 receptor antagonism) for refractory cases.11,12

In the present study, Daoudlarian et al13 sought to identify peripheral biomarkers of ICI-My and translate them into therapeutic opportunities. They performed serum cytokine analysis on a cohort of 33 patients with ICI-My (7 severe, 26 nonsevere) and 68 ICI-treated control patients without ICI-My. They identified increased levels of CCL3, CCL4, CCL5, CXCL9, CXCL10, CXCL13, IL-6, and VEGF-A in patients with ICI-My. They also performed mass cytometry analyses in 16 patients with ICI-My and 72 ICI-treated control patients without ICI-My, finding that ICI-My was associated with increased abundance of HLA-DR+CD38+ CD4+ and CD8+ T cells along with decreased CXCR3 expression in circulating T cells. They extended their cytokine analysis with clinical data and reported a case series of 8 patients with steroid-refractory ICI-My treated with tocilizumab, a monoclonal antibody that antagonizes the IL-6 receptor. The study has notable strengths, including the relatively large sample size in the context of the ICI-My literature, particularly of the cytokine analysis, and the use of an ICI-treated control group. These ICI-treated control subjects are important, as ICI exposure leads to nonspecific changes in circulating cytokines and immune cell composition.10 The case series also adds to the previously limited body of reports regarding the use of IL-6 receptor blockade for the treatment of ICI-My.14

Some of the limitations of this work reflect underlying challenges of studying ICI-My, which is difficult to diagnose and often co-occurs with irAEs affecting other organs. Eighty-eight percent of the studied ICI-My patients had co-occurring noncardiac irAEs that could have affected that peripheral cytokine and immune cell milieu. Additionally, only 4 patients in this study were diagnosed by biopsy, and many of the remainder were considered only “possible” ICI-My cases by the widely used Bonaca criteria.3 A large majority of the identified cases were not considered to be severe ICI-My, and although the group’s use of routine screening is commendable in the interest of preventing devastating outcomes, it may have identified patients with less severe biology than other prior reports that relied on samples collected solely from symptomatic patients. In fact, recent work has shown that patients with ICI-My and certain low-risk features may not need therapeutic intervention15; as such, there may be subphenotypes of ICI-My biology that correspond to varying clinical outcomes. Unfortunately, the present study did not identify biomarkers that could distinguish severe from nonsevere ICI-My. Larger cohorts will be required to discern these potentially valuable features.

The study focuses on peripheral blood specimens, which are widely accessible in routine clinical care, but it is unclear how they reflect tissue biology or distinguish irAEs from antitumor responses. For instance, the investigators report no change in circulating complement, but studies have identified complement deposition in ICI-My-affected myocardial tissue on the basis of immunohistochemical staining.6 Similarly, the investigators note lower expression of CXCR3 on circulating T cells. CXCR3 is the receptor for the chemokines CXCL9, CXCL10, and CXCL11, which are associated with ICI-My pathology.7 It is possible that these shifts in circulation may represent the recruitment of CXCR3hi cells from blood to heart tissue, but this chemokine axis is broadly implicated in both irAE processes across tissues and antitumor responses.16 Importantly, the investigators also associate ICI-My with an increased abundance of HLA-DR+CD38+ CD4+ and CD8+ T cells. Increased abundance of HLA-DR+CD38+ CD8 T cells have been previously associated with ICI-associated arthritis,17 ICI-associated hepatitis,18 and ICI resistance.19 These populations have been shown to be induced by type I interferon or long term T-cell receptor stimulation and may be a useful target for treatments seeking to boost antitumor responses. Given the life-threatening nature of severe ICI-My, the safety of the patient and need for aggressive immunosuppression is paramount, but in lower risk cases and more manageable irAEs, seeking to balance irAE mitigation while maximizing antitumor responses will be crucial.

The present study serves as a helpful launching point for future efforts that can help identify biomarkers, targets, and treatments of ICI-My. Although the cohort is robust, considerably larger and multi-institutional efforts are needed to tease out competing signals from factors such as varying ICI-My severity, concurrent noncardiac irAEs, different anticancer therapies, and immunosuppressive treatments. Additional signaling axes anticipated to contribute to ICI-My, such as the recently described role of the CXCL16/CXCR6 pathway,8 were not assessed in this study but merit follow-up in future work. The investigators focus on blocking IL-6 signaling, which is attractive as a potential treatment modality for irAEs. Mouse models20 and observational studies in patients treated with IL-6 pathway blockade for irAEs affecting a range of organs14 suggest that this approach can treat irAEs without mitigating antitumor responses. These and other studies inspired clinical trials that block IL-6 signaling in parallel to combination ICI therapy to potentially decrease irAE incidence (eg, NCT03999749, NCT05428007). These studies are limited in size and are unlikely to demonstrate a signal to prevent an uncommon event like ICI-My, but they are promising attempts toward prevention, rather than treatment, of irAEs.

With the increasing use of ICIs and novel immunotherapy combinations, the number of patients at risk for developing ICI-My is large and continuing to grow. Daoudlarian et al13 make a meaningful contribution toward efforts seeking to identify noninvasive biomarkers and therapeutic targets for managing ICI-My. Ultimately, we need randomized trials to best identify effective prevention and treatment options for ICI-My, and these are emerging (eg, NCT05335928, NCT05195645). The future will hopefully pair novel diagnostics and risk-stratifying biomarkers with treatments that can optimally treat or even prevent ICI-My.
