Successful Tarlatamab Rechallenge After Recurrent High-Grade Cytokine Release Syndrome in Chromothripsis-Mediated SCLC: A Case Report
Authors: Ryan Cheng, Sarah Reddy, Kendra Gee-Rodriguez, Rogelyn Harlan, Chetan Vakkalagadda, Jeremy Cetnar, Rebecca Silbermann, Richard T. Maziarz, Rajat Thawani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Chromothripsis-mediated SCLC (cSCLC) is a distinct subtype of SCLC characterized by wild-type TP53 and RB1, arising in light- or never-smokers. Although data are limited, cSCLC may be associated with poor response to first-line platinum-based chemotherapy. We report the case of a 60-year-old woman with extensive-stage cSCLC who had rapid disease progression after platinum-based chemoimmunotherapy. Second-line therapy with tarlatamab was initiated, which was complicated by high-grade cytokine release syndrome (CRS) after both the initial and second step-up doses. Repeat imaging after these doses demonstrated a substantive early response to tarlatamab. A patient-involved decision was made to continue treatment, and CRS did not recur. This case illustrates the promise of delta-like ligand 3–directed therapy in cSCLC. High baseline disease burden may predispose subjects to more severe CRS without necessitating treatment discontinuation. Early reimaging in these situations may reveal marked treatment response and inform clinical decision-making.

Introduction

SCLC is an aggressive neuroendocrine malignancy marked by rapid proliferation, early metastatic spread, and poor outcomes. Classically, it exhibits near-invariable inactivation of TP53 and RB1 and is strongly associated with tobacco exposure.1 A few patients, however, present without a smoking history while retaining wild-type TP53 and RB1. This emerging entity, now recognized as chromothripsis-mediated SCLC (cSCLC), is defined by complex chromosomal rearrangements.2 cSCLC is known to be chemotherapy resistant, and optimal treatment for this subtype has yet to be determined.

Tarlatamab, a bispecific T-cell engager targeting delta-like ligand 3 (DLL3) on tumor cells, received Food and Drug Administration approval in May 2024 as a second-line therapy for SCLC. Early tarlatamab clinical trials reported an overall response rate of 40% in patients with SCLC who had progressed after at least two lines of therapy.3 Cytokine release syndrome (CRS) is the most frequently observed treatment-related adverse event with tarlatamab.

Here, we report the clinical course of a patient with extensive-stage cSCLC who developed high-grade CRS with the first two doses of tarlatamab and achieved a rapid partial response.

Case Presentation

A 60-year-old never-smoking woman with a remote history of multiple myeloma in remission after autologous hematopoietic cell transplant presented with abdominal pain and transaminitis. A needle biopsy of the liver revealed high-grade neuroendocrine carcinoma with small cell morphology. Immunohistochemistry (IHC) result was positive for synaptophysin, chromogranin, and INSM1 and negative for TTF-1 and CK20. Next-generation sequencing was performed to rule out de novo oncogenic transformed SCLC and revealed wild-type TP53 and RB1 and amplifications of CCND2 (∼57 copies), CDK4 (∼16 copies), and MDM2 (∼85 copies). Cytogenetics was not done. Tumor mutation burden was low (3 mutations/Mb), and the Ki-67 rate was 50% to 60%. Notably, DLL3 IHC revealed high expression in 80% of tumor cells. Because the primary site of origin could not be identified based on morphology and IHC alone, tissue-of-origin testing was performed, with results indicating lung carcinoid. These findings, taken together with this patient’s lack of smoking history, wild-type TP53 and RB1, and amplifications in CCND2, CDK4, and MDM2, were strongly suggestive of cSCLC.

The patient initiated first-line treatment with carboplatin and etoposide. Durvalumab was added at cycle 2, but post-cycle 2 imaging revealed disease progression in the liver and bone in parallel with a decline in her clinical condition with anasarca and abdominal pain. She was then transitioned to second-line tarlatamab. Shortly after completion of the initial 1 mg infusion, she developed chills minutes after the infusion ended, which gradually became grade 3 CRS with hypotension requiring transfer to the intensive care unit for vasopressor support, dexamethasone, and three doses of tocilizumab, with resolution after approximately 4.5 days. Per guidelines, tarlatamab was resumed at 10 mg on cycle 1 day 8. Nonstandard premedications—acetaminophen, diphenhydramine, and famotidine—were added to dexamethasone, given initial concern for concomitant infusion-related hypersensitivity. She developed recurrent grade 4 CRS with hypotension requiring intensive care unit transfer, vasopressor support, and respiratory failure requiring high-flow oxygen. CRS resolved with dexamethasone and three doses of tocilizumab treatment after 24 hours.

Per the tarlatamab label, we discussed permanent discontinuation of treatment because of two episodes of high-grade CRS. However, the patient had clinically improved with reduced abdominal pain and improved hepatic function. Early imaging demonstrated marked regression of the left hilar mass, hilar and mediastinal adenopathy, and liver lesions, improvement in abdominal distension, and resolution of anasarca (Fig. 1B). The patient and care team elected to continue tarlatamab. The patient was admitted for the third treatment dose to facilitate enhanced monitoring, and montelukast was added to the premedications listed previously. Notably, there were no further episodes of CRS, and the patient continues to tolerate tarlatamab treatment in the outpatient setting into cycle 7 with continuing systemic response (Fig. 1C and D). Premedications with diphenhydramine, acetaminophen, dexamethasone, famotidine, and montelukast were maintained for utmost caution, with a plan to taper in subsequent cycles. Of note, the first magnetic resonance imaging of the brain on tarlatamab revealed multiple punctate lesions concerning for metastasis; thus, a gamma knife radiosurgery was performed after cycle 2 concluded.

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report of a patient with SCLC who experienced tarlatamab-associated high-grade CRS yet had a responsive disease course, justifying consideration of treatment rechallenge.

CRS occurs when activated T cells release proinflammatory cytokines, including TNF-α, IFN-γ, IL-2, and IL-8, which can further recruit and activate additional immune cell populations. The severity of fever, hypotension, and hypoxia grades the resulting inflammatory response.4 CRS with tarlatamab is found in 57% of patients and usually occurs during the first treatment cycle. Most events are grades 1 and 2, and only less than 2% of patients are reported to experience grade 3 or higher CRS.5 CRS after bispecific T-cell therapy is typically treated with steroids, with escalation to anti–IL-6 therapy.

Risk factors for the development of CRS in patients receiving tarlatamab remain poorly understood. However, insights may be drawn from chimeric antigen receptor T-cell (CAR-T) therapy, another form of T-cell–mediated immunotherapy that relies on T-cell recognition and activation of cancer cells and is similarly associated with CRS. In CAR-T therapy, tumor burden has emerged as a key predictor of CRS risk. Prospective and retrospective studies in patients with B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia and non-Hodgkin lymphoma have demonstrated that baseline tumor burden—measured by bone marrow blast percentage or metabolic tumor volume on positron emission tomography imaging—correlates with increased incidence and severity of CRS.6, 7, 8 Although such correlations have not yet been rigorously validated in SCLC, our patient's clinical course suggests a potential parallel. Our patient presented with the following exceptionally high disease burden: extensive liver metastases, bulky mediastinal and hilar adenopathy, and widespread osseous lesions on baseline positron emission tomography scan. This quantitative assessment was consistent with the aggressive presentation typical of cSCLC, as described by Rekhtman et al.2 where more than half of patients had liver metastases at diagnosis and most presented with extensive-stage disease. The development of high-grade CRS in our patient, combined with similar case reports describing severe early CRS in patients with SCLC with extensive liver involvement, suggests that high baseline tumor burden may be a risk factor for CRS with tarlatamab in SCLC.9,10

Our patient had frank disease progression after two cycles of chemotherapy, a poor prognostic sign in SCLC, which is predicted to be chemotherapy sensitive in more than 70% of cases. There is no clear precedent for managing chemotherapy-refractory cSCLC. Rekhtman et al.2 found that all instances of cSCLC belonged to the ASCL1-high molecular subtype and exhibited high DLL3 expression. Our patient's tumor sample expressed high DLL3 by IHC in 80% of cancer cells, providing a strong biological rationale for DLL3-targeted therapy. Despite tarlatamab's longer median time to response compared with first-line chemotherapy in canonical SCLC, our patient with cSCLC experienced a notably early partial response. An interval scan may therefore be useful for identifying hyper-responders and weighing the risks and benefits of continuation.

The successful response to tarlatamab in our chemotherapy-refractory patient highlights the therapeutic potential of DLL3-targeted bispecific antibodies in cSCLC, a subset with limited treatment options and poor outcomes with conventional therapy. This approach may be particularly relevant given recently presented results from the phase 1B DeLLphi-303 (safety and activity of tarlatamab in combination with a PD-L1 inhibitor as first-line maintenance therapy after chemo-immunotherapy in patients with extensive-stage small-cell lung cancer) trial, which demonstrated a median overall survival of 25.3 months with tarlatamab incorporated into first-line maintenance treatment.11 These findings suggest that earlier integration of DLL3-targeted therapy may substantially improve outcomes for patients with SCLC, including those with difficult-to-treat cSCLC. As experience with bispecific T-cell engagers grows, cases like ours will help define optimal management strategies, including approaches to rechallenge and patient selection criteria that balance the potential for durable disease control against the risks of serious immune-related toxicities.

Conclusion

In summary, our case is an example of cSCLC that demonstrated an early disease response to tarlatamab after progression on first-line chemotherapy. Furthermore, successful rechallenge with tarlatamab is feasible after high-grade CRS in select patients with adequate inpatient support. Early repeat imaging may enable identification of patients with exceptional response to bispecific T-cell therapy and inform shared decision-making regarding treatment continuation despite early toxicity and standard recommendations to discontinue therapy.
