Can we rebuild what has been taken? A narrative review of biological and functional consequences of hamstring tendon harvest in ACL surgery
Authors: Johan Högberg, Mathias Wernbom, Eric Hamrin Senorski
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis narrative review aimed to summarize the consequences of hamstring tendon (HT) harvest for anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction (ACLR), with reference to tendon regeneration, knee flexor morphology, clinical assessment of knee flexion strength, and rehabilitation strategies for optimizing recovery. The available evidence suggests that the harvested semitendinosus (ST) and gracilis (GR) tendons regenerate in ∼70-86% of cases after ACLR. Despite tendon regeneration, ACLR with HT autograft results in morphological alterations in both the ST and GR. These changes include tendon elongation, proximal migration of the musculotendinous junction, muscle atrophy, shortened fascicles, and increased fatty infiltration, which may persist for several years after ACLR. Growing evidence also suggests that assessment of knee flexion strength after ACLR cannot rely on a single test position, and that knee flexion strength should be evaluated in both hip-flexed and hip-extended positions to provide a more comprehensive evaluation of the recovery. We propose that optimizing the recovery of knee flexion strength may benefit from a multifaceted approach: (1) early, symptom-guided initiation of isometric and low-load exercises to minimize early strength deficits, (2) progressive loading of the knee flexors across multiple hip-and knee-joint angles, including the seated leg-curl to promote a high ST activation, (3) subsequent incorporation of eccentric loading as part of progressive strengthening strategies, with the aim of enhancing ST and GR muscle volumes and fascicle lengths, and (4) strength assessments across multiple hip and knee-joint positions to monitor recovery, as testing in a seated position may underestimate residual deficits in the ST and GR.Keywords: anterior cruciate ligament, HT autograft, rehabilitation, strength testing, reconstruction

1. Introduction

Reconstructive surgery after anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) injury is frequently performed in young and physically active individuals. 1 In approximately 50% of primary ACL reconstructions (ACLR), a hamstring tendon (HT) autograft; using either the semitendinosus (ST) tendon alone or in combination with the gracilis (GR) tendon, is the autograft of choice. 2 Tendon harvest may be considered equivalent to a complete distal tendon rupture, which raises important questions regarding the long-term consequences for the knee flexor muscle-tendon morphology and function.

The knee flexors serve as important synergists to the ACL by contributing to the restraint of anterior tibial translation through reflex-mediated mechanisms. 3 In addition, the medial knee flexors have been reported to enhance medial knee-joint stability by increasing contact pressures within the medial compartment. 4 Against this background, it is important to understand the extent to which the harvested ST and GR tendons regenerate and to clarify how tendon harvest influences knee flexor morphology, strength, and overall function in individuals after ACLR.

Accordingly, the aim of this narrative review was to synthesize current evidence regarding the consequences of HT harvest for ACLR, with emphasis on tendon regeneration, knee flexor morphology, clinical assessment of knee flexion strength and function, and rehabilitation strategies to facilitate recovery.

2. Methods

The Scale for the Assessment of Narrative Review Articles (SANRA) was adopted to enhance methodological rigor. 5 Due to the broad scope of the present study, a narrative review design was considered appropriate.

A literature search was performed by two authors (J.H and M.W) using the Web of Science, PubMed, and AMED databases. The search included studies published from database inception until April 2026. The search strategy combined keywords such as “anterior cruciate ligament”, “anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction”, “hamstring tendon autograft”, “tendon regeneration”, “knee flexion strength”, “hamstring morphology”, “tendon harvest morbidity”, “electromyography”, “magnetic resonance imaging”, and “ultrasound” using Boolean operators. To specifically identify studies on knee flexor muscle morphology, the search strategy used terms such as “biceps femoris”, ”semitendinosus”, “semimembranosus”, “gracilis”, ”sartorius”, ”popliteus”, ”fascicle length”, ”fiber length”, “muscle volume”, and “physiological cross-sectional area” using Boolean operators. Reference lists of included studies, as well as textbooks on skeletal muscle morphology and physiology,6,7 were also hand-searched to identify additional relevant publications.

Only studies published in English were considered. All study designs were eligible for inclusion. Studies investigating either healthy athletes or individuals after ACLR with HT autograft were considered. Studies were selected based on their relevance to the following topics: a) morphology and function of knee flexors, b) tendon regeneration, c) knee flexor morphology after ACLR, d) clinical assessment of knee flexion strength after ACLR, and e) rehabilitation after ACLR.

3. Brief overview of the morphology and functions of the knee flexors

The muscles which perform knee flexion include the biceps femoris (BF), semimembranosus (SM), ST, sartorius (SAR), GR and popliteus (POP) (Figure 1). 8 The gastrocnemius also contributes to knee flexion, however, its contribution is greatest near full knee extension and decreases with increasing knee flexion angles. 9 Collectively, the BF, SM and ST are commonly referred to as the hamstrings.8,10

An anatomical illustration of the knee flexor muscles.

The two functionally most important architectural parameters of a muscle are its physiological cross-sectional area (PCSA) and muscle fiber length.11,12 A large PCSA corresponds to a greater force-generating capacity, whereas longer muscles are designed more for excursion, particularly when the fibers span the entire muscle length. 12 In the following sections, the morphology and function of the hamstrings and other key knee flexors are discussed, with emphasis on PCSA, fiber length, and the overall architectural organization of each muscle.

3.1. Morphology of the knee flexors

The hamstrings cross both the hip and knee-joints, with the exception of the short head of the biceps femoris (BFsh), which spans only the knee-joint. 10 They are commonly classified into a medial hamstring group, comprising the SM and ST, and a lateral hamstring group, consisting of the long head of the BF (BFlh) and the BFsh. 13 The BFlh has been described as a bipennate muscle which contains two distinct regions, a slightly larger superficial region and a deeper region. Cadaver studies have reported mean fascicle lengths of the BFlh ranging from approximately 6.5 to 10 cm (Supplementary file 1).

The BFsh has a parallel pennate architecture, 14 and is anatomically divided into two regions. Woodley & Mercer 10 reported that the mean fascicle length of the BFsh was the longest among the hamstrings muscles (12.4 cm). Cadaver studies further indicate that BFsh fascicles are substantially longer than those of BFlh, with mean lengths ranging from approximately 10.5-14 cm (Supplementary file 1). However, in vivo measurements using ultrasound and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) are less conclusive, and one study even reported longer fascicle lengths for the BFlh than for the BFsh. 15 This suggests that the fascicle lengths of BFlh reported in cadaver studies may often underestimate BFlh fascicle lengths in adult individuals, possibly due to age- and inactivity-related changes in older individuals (for discussion, see Supplementary file 3).

The SM has been reported to have three distinct regions, with the proximal and middle portions exhibiting a unipennate architecture, and the distal portion being bipennate. 10 The fascicle lengths vary slightly along the muscle, with the proximal region generally exhibiting longer fascicles. 10 Cadaver studies report mean SM fascicle lengths ranging between 5-7 cm, representing the shortest among the hamstring muscles (Supplementary file 1). In vivo measurements using ultrasound indicate slightly greater variability, with mean fascicle lengths ranging between 5-9 cm and longer fascicles observed in the proximal relative to the distal regions 16 (Supplementary file 2).

The ST is a long, thin, straplike muscle, and it is considered to be the longest muscle belly within the hamstrings. 10 Notably, the proximal and distal portions of the ST are separated by an inverted V-shaped tendinous inscription,10,13,17–19 also referred to as the raphe. 20 The muscle fibers of the proximal compartment insert into this inscription, which also serves as the origin for the muscle fibers of the distal compartment. 20 This structural arrangement is often overlooked in both cadaver and in vivo investigations, likely contributing to the wide range of reported ST fascicle lengths (∼15-20 cm) (Supplementary file 1). Cadaver studies report that average fascicle lengths may not differ substantially between compartments,10,13 however, other studies have observed distal compartment fascicles to be 1.7-5 cm longer than those of the proximal compartment.17,18,21 Consistent with the latter, a MRI study of young individuals after ACLR with HT autografts indicated that the proximal compartment was ∼ 17% shorter than the distal compartment in the non-injured leg. 22 Taken together, these findings suggest that the distal compartment is generally longer, although further in vivo investigations are warranted, particularly given the potential influence of aging on fascicle lengths (Supplementary file 3).

The GR is the most superficial muscle of the medial thigh.23,24 It has a spiral unipennate architecture and a long, flat, tapering morphology.17,25 Notably, many muscle fibers in the GR do not span the entire fascicle length, a feature consistently observed across studies (Supplementary file 3).

The SAT is both the longest muscle in the human body 26 and the only knee flexor to originate superior to the acetabulum. 8 Its muscle fascicles run in a nearly parallel course. 17 Cadaver studies report fascicle lengths of approximately 40-50 cm (Supplementary file 1). However, as observed in the GR, many fibers do not extend the entire length of the fascicles (Supplementary file 3). In contrast, the POP has the shortest fascicles among all knee flexors, with mean lengths between 2.2-3.6 cm in cadaver studies (Supplementary file 1).

With reference to PCSA and muscle volume, data from both cadaver studies and in vivo investigations using MRI and ultrasound indicate that the SM has the largest PCSA and the greatest muscle volume among the hamstring muscles (Supplementary file 1 and 2). The BFlh typically ranks between the SM and ST in both parameters (Supplementary file 1 and 2). The BFsh consistently exhibits the smallest PCSA of the hamstrings. 10 Although the SAR and GR are relatively long muscles, their muscle volumes are smaller than those of the biarticular hamstring muscles (BFlh, SM, ST). The SAR generally has a slightly smaller volume than the BFlh, whereas the GR is comparable in size to the BFsh. The PCSAs of the SAR and GR are usually reported as lower than those of the hamstring muscles (Supplementary file 1 and 2), however, the uncertainties regarding their mean fiber lengths raise the possibility that their PCSAs may be underestimated (Supplementary file 3).

Interestingly, the ST muscle volume in sprinters sometimes exceeds that of BFlh and approaching that of the SM, 27 whereas, the POP displays only marginally greater muscle volumes in sprinters compared to controls. 27 Notably, the ST also has functionally longer fascicle arrangements than both the SM and the BFlh when considering that its two compartments are serially linked via the tendinous inscription.10,13,17–19 Consequently, even at comparable muscle volumes, the PCSA of the ST remains considerably lower than that of the SM and the BFlh.

3.2. Functions of the hamstrings and other knee flexors

At the knee-joint, the BFlh and BFsh contribute to knee flexion and lateral rotation of the tibia, whereas the SM and ST perform knee flexion and medial rotation of the tibia. 28 The biarticular muscles BFlh, SM and ST additionally produce hip extension. 29 The SAT and GR are active during both knee and hip flexion, although the GR is primarily active during the initial phase of hip flexion. 29 The GR also contributes to hip adduction, and medial rotation of both the femur and tibia,24,29 while the SAT assists in lateral rotation of the hip and medial rotation of the knee, and is active in hip flexion regardless of knee position.26,29 The POP acts as a medial rotator of the tibia, and is active at the onset of knee flexion, contributing to the unlocking of the knee-joint. 29

Interestingly, the GR and the ST were the two muscles that underwent the greatest hypertrophy of all knee flexors after knee flexion resistance training (∼20-24% increases in muscle volume), regardless of whether the exercises were performed in a prone or seated position. 30 The SAT also hypertrophied to a similar extent as the other hamstrings muscles (∼8-12%). 30 These findings underscore the importance of the GR and SAR as synergists in knee flexion against resistance.

3.3. Knee flexors as knee-joint stabilizers

Landmark studies in the 1980s provided evidence from both human and animal experiments supporting a protective role of the hamstrings in counteracting excessive loading of the ACL and other knee ligaments.3,31–33 Notably, Solomonow et al. 3 identified not only an ACL-hamstring reflex arc but also an ACL-independent reflex pathway, characterized by increased hamstrings activation and decreased quadriceps activation, as measured by electromyography (EMG). The same group also reported hamstring co-activation during knee extension. 34

Roentgenographic studies by Yasuda & Sasaki32,33 provided compelling in vivo evidence that hamstring co-contraction reduces ACL loading and counteracts the anterior drawer force at knee angles up to 7° from full extension. In vitro cadaver studies by Renström et al. 31 and Draganich & Vahey 35 largely corroborated the results of Yasuda & Sasaki.32,33 Subsequent simulation and in vitro studies indicate that the lateral hamstrings play a prominent role in decreasing ACL load resulting from anterior tibial translation.36,37 This is further supported by the finding that isolated electrical stimulation of the BFlh during walking in healthy individuals reduces tibiofemoral anterior shear force and tibial internal rotation. 38

However, anterior tibial translation is not the only movement that loads the ACL. Current consensus suggests that the greatest ACL loading during single-leg athletic maneuvers performed near full knee extension arises from a combination of knee-joint compression and resistance to a knee flexion moment, together with anterior tibial shear force, internal tibial torque, and a knee abduction moment. 39 As a knee adductor, the ST may function as an antagonist to externally applied knee abduction and internal rotation moments by increasing compression of the medial knee-joint compartment. 40 In support of this notion, a combined EMG and fluoroscopy study demonstrated that co-activation of the medial hamstrings and the vastus medialis was associated with a shorter ACL length during a step-up task. 41 In vitro evidence indicates that among the medial knee flexors, the ST is the primary contributor in reducing anterior tibial translation and external rotation. 23 Conversely, several studies have reported associations between increased BFlh-vastus lateralis co-activation, greater knee abduction angles, and ACL elongation.42,43 In line with this, an in vivo modeling study suggested that the vastus lateralis may be a primary contributor to ACL loading in healthy females. 43

Taken together, the BFlh appears to be important in limiting anterior tibial translation, while the activation of the medial hamstrings, particularly the ST, may play an important role in mitigating ACL strain associated with high activation of the lateral thigh muscles. In support of the latter view, Zebis et al. 44 reported that reduced ST pre-activity during side-cutting was associated with an increased risk of first-time ACL injury. Similarly, a study of adolescent alpine skiers reported that female skiers who subsequently sustained an ACL injury had a smaller pre-injury ST CSA than non-injured skiers. 45

A counterargument against a prominent role of the ST in unloading the ACL is its relatively small PCSA.36,46 However, both the ST and the GR readily undergo hypertrophy after knee flexion resistance training performed in conventional seated and prone leg-curl machines. 30 In support of their substantial growth potential, the ST and GR are also two of the muscles that display the greatest hypertrophy in sprinters, 27 with absolute muscle volumes of the ST and GR being up to ∼2 and ∼1.8 times greater, respectively, in elite sprinters than in controls. 47 In addition, accumulating evidence suggests that the PCSA of the GR may be up to twofold greater than previously assumed (Supplementary file 3). The significance of greater force-generating capacities of the ST as well as the GR is supported by the findings of Toor et al., 23 which indicate that the combined force of the ST, GR and SAT muscles contribute to limiting anterior tibial translation for a given applied load. If true, this could have clinical implications for knee function and stability after ACLR with HT autografts, as well as for injury prevention.

4. ST and GR tendon regeneration after ACLR with HT autografts

There is consensus across systematic reviews that in the majority of cases, the harvested ST and GR tendons regenerate after ACLR.48–50 However, the definition of tendon regeneration is heterogeneous and lacks standardization,48,50 ranging from descriptions of partial regrowth to classifications of incomplete or complete regeneration. Incomplete regeneration typically refers to tendon regrowth located around the medial knee joint that does not reach the native insertion site at the pes anserinus, whereas complete regeneration is defined by reattachment at the native anatomical location. 50 Irrespective of the definition applied, reported regeneration rates range from approximately 70 to 86%,48,49 with one review reporting regeneration in ∼79% of ST tendons and ∼43% of GR tendons. 50 The likelihood of tendon regeneration appears to decrease with increasing age and in individuals who smoke. 51 Most regeneration occurs within the first year after ACLR, 48 although both the extent and degree of tendon regeneration are highly variable. 50 While several MRI, ultrasound and computed tomography (CT) studies have reported continuity of tendon-like structures within the harvest tract which could be seen as regeneration of the native tendon,52–54 these imaging modalities may have limited ability to differentiate organized tendon tissue from scar tissue or partial structural reconstitution alone. However, studies which have included macroscopic visualization, arthroscopic evaluation and histological analyses performed between 1 and 8 years after ACLR have demonstrated regenerated tissue with gross and microscopic characteristics closely resembling native tendon morphology, although focal fibrosis and structural alterations may persist.55–59 The available evidence supports that biological tendon regeneration can occur after HT harvest, while the degree of restoration and its functional equivalence to the native tendon likely vary between individuals.

With regard to the time course of tendon regeneration, longitudinal MRI assessments up to 32 months after ACLR have demonstrated progressive maturation in a proximal to distal direction. 60 Magnetic resonance imaging studies indicate that no tendon is visible at approximately 2 weeks postoperatively,54,60 but tendon-like structures (neo-tendons) become visible by 6 weeks at the level of the superior pole of patella and are identifiable near the knee-joint line by 3 months. 60 By 12 months, the regenerated tendon insertions are typically located 1-3 cm above the pes anserinus, 60 and at this stage, the tendons generally demonstrate clearer margins and increased thickness compared with the early postoperative period. 61 At 32 months, the regenerated tendon has been reported to be located 1-2 cm above its tibial attachment. 60 Interestingly, some data suggests that regenerated tendons may reach their final anatomical location as early as 3-6 months after ACLR, with subsequent changes being predominantly morphological rather than positional. 62 However, in some cases, the regenerated ST tendon forms a conjoined structure with the GR tendon approximately 3 cm distal to the knee-joint line before inserting at the pes anserinus, whereas in the absence of anatomical regeneration, the ST tendon may fuse with the SM tendon proximal to the knee-joint. 63

In agreement with MRI-based investigations, 3D CT studies also support a high overall tendon regeneration rate.52,64,65 In the studies by Nakamae et al.,52,65 nearly all tendons (90-97%) had regenerated by 6-12 months after ACLR. Some tendons, particularly at 6 months, exhibited hypo-or hyperplastic morphology, whereas 55% had a normal appearance at 12 months. 65 Ultrasound findings align with MRI data, showing no detectable tendon at 2 weeks, the appearance of an irregular hypoechoic structure by 1 month, and substantial increases in tendon thickness and CSA between 2-6 months. 54 By 12 months, tendon edges become more distinct, and by 18-24 months, echogenicity closely resembles the preoperative state. 54

Taken together, the available data indicate a high rate of regeneration of the harvested ST and GR tendons. Regenerated tendons are typically not visible during the first 2 weeks but reappear between 1 and 6 months, with most reaching near-anatomical position by 6-12 months. Maturation continues into the second year, with progressive normalization of structure and appearance (Figure 2).

5. Changes in knee flexor morphology

5.1. Changes in the morphology of the ST and GR

Despite the capacity for tendon regeneration, both the harvested ST and GR tendons and their associated muscle bellies often undergo morphological changes after ACLR.66,67 Magnetic resonance imaging studies assessing muscle volume and anatomical CSAs have consistently reported pronounced atrophy of both the ST and GR.53,68,69 Williams et al. 68 reported marked decreases in muscle volumes (30-40%), CSAs (20-30%), and lengths of the ST and GR 6 months after ACLR, with tendon regeneration occurring to varying degrees in nearly all individuals. Konrath et al. 53 reported significantly shorter muscle lengths in the ACLR leg compared with the non-injured leg for both the ST (21.3 vs. 31.5 cm) and GR (27.6 vs. 32.7 cm) at a minimum of 2 years after ACLR. Between-leg deficits in muscle volume were 40% for the ST and 50% for the GR, with peak muscle CSA deficits of 30% and 40%, respectively. 53 In cases where tendon regeneration did not occur, atrophy of the ST and GR was even more pronounced. 53 Importantly, the degree of muscle shortening is highly dependent on the success of tendon regeneration. In the MRI study by Kositsky et al. 22 regenerated tendons were associated with ST muscle length deficits of ∼4 cm in the ACLR leg, whereas non-regenerated tendons were associated with deficits of nearly 12 cm.

In a follow-up at 6.8 years after ACLR, Speedtsberg et al. 70 observed an average ST muscle shortening of 8.1 cm (25%) in the ACLR leg compared with the non-injured leg, with side-to-side differences significantly exceeding those observed in healthy controls (∼1 cm side-to-side difference). A long-term follow-up (9-11 years after ACLR) reported persistent deficits in the muscle volumes, with the ST and GR reaching only 58% and 54% of the non-injured leg, respectively, accompanied by fatty infiltration. 71 Considerable variability in tendon and scar tissue morphology was also noted, with many individuals demonstrating tendons that blended into the SAR or medial gastrocnemius fascia proximal to the knee-joint line. 71

Shape-analysis approaches have revealed that the greatest morphological deviations occur in the distal ST of the ACLR leg, whereas decreases in CSA and muscle volume are more pronounced in the proximal and mid-muscle regions. 72 In line, Kositsky et al. 22 reported that, in contrast to the non-injured leg, where the distal compartment exhibited greater volume and length than the proximal compartment, no such differences were present in the ACLR leg. Furthermore, both compartments of the ACLR leg displayed substantially smaller muscle lengths, volumes, and peak CSAs, 22 indicating disproportionately greater atrophy and remodeling in the distal ST after HT harvest. 22 These shape alterations may affect force transmission, length-tension relationships, and load distribution within the ST, potentially contributing to persistent deficits in knee flexion and internal rotation strength. 22

A systematic review and meta-analysis 66 confirmed that the ST and GR typically exhibit reduced muscle volumes, smaller CSAs, and reduced muscle thickness compared with the non-injured leg and healthy controls. These changes coincide with shorter ST muscle length and a longer tendon, consistent with proximal migration of the distal musculotendinous junction. 66

5.2. Changes in the morphology of other knee flexors

Across the hamstring muscle group (ST, SM, and BF), overall muscle volume has been reported to be approximately 12% lower in the ACLR leg at 2 years after ACLR. 53 Conversely, increases in BFlh volume and peak CSA of 7.7% and 10.6%, respectively, as well as a 4% larger SM volume, have also been observed in the ACLR leg. 53 The hypertrophy of the BFlh and the SM have been interpreted as evidence of compensatory hypertrophy in the remaining hamstring muscles. 53 The evidence of whether compensatory hypertrophy occurs after ACLR is conflicting. In their systematic reviews, Sherman et al. 66 and Dutaillis et al. 67 concluded that BF and SM muscle volumes and CSAs did not differ between the ACLR and non-injured leg. Nevertheless, several studies examining muscle volumes and CSAs have reported evidence of compensatory hypertrophy in the BF and/or the SM of the ACLR leg.53,71,73–75

Several factors may explain why hypertrophy of BFlh and/or SM is observed in some studies but not others. First, many studies may have been underpowered to detect minor increases in muscle size. Second, differences in the extent of ST and GR tendon regeneration appear to play a role. Eriksson et al. 73 reported a 26% greater SM muscle CSA in the ACLR leg in individuals without ST tendon regeneration, compared with a non-significant 3% increase in individuals with ST tendon regeneration. Similarly, Irie & Tomatsu 69 reported greater SM and BF CSAs in the ACLR leg in individuals whose ST and GR muscles were reduced to less than 50% of the non-injured leg at 12-16 months postoperatively. Third, a lack of compensatory hypertrophy may also reflect reduced physical activity. A systematic review reported that, on average, individuals remained less physically active than their non-injured peers for up to 35 months postoperatively. 76

Collectively, compensatory hypertrophy of the BF and/or the SM may occur, which could be dependent on factors such as the severity of morphological changes in ST and GR, and the level of physical activity.

6. The possible impact of iatrogenic saphenous nerve injury

In addition to morphological alterations of the knee flexors, injury to the terminal branches of the saphenous nerve has been reported in up to 88% of individuals undergoing ACLR with a HT autograft. 77 Such injuries may result in hypoesthesia, dysesthesia, or the formation of painful neuromas along the anteromedial aspect of the knee and lower leg.78,79 Distal to the adductor canal, the saphenous nerve divides into two terminal branches: the sartorial branch and the infrapatellar branch. The infrapatellar branch courses vertically along the medial aspect of the knee, passing between the SAR and GR tendons, and is particularly susceptible to injury during HT harvest for ACLR. 79 Saphenous nerve injury is therefore considered as iatrogenic. The risk of damage to the infrapatellar branch is influenced by the surgical technique used for HT harvest, with vertical incisions associated with higher risk compared with oblique or horizontal incisions.78,79 Sensory disturbances may resolve within approximately one year in about one-fifth of individuals, however, persistent symptoms are reported in approximately one-third of individuals. 80 Importantly, saphenous nerve injury may not only induce hypoesthesia but also increase the risk of re-rupture of the ACL, based on reported higher rates of re-rupture in individuals with SN injury. 80 However, as stated by these authors, this finding needs to be further supported by studies with a larger number of individuals. 80

7. Assessment of knee flexion strength and overall function

7.1. The importance of testing position and contraction mode for strength outcomes

Knee flexion strength recovery is commonly expressed as the strength of the ACLR leg relative to the non-injured leg, reported as a limb symmetry index (LSI). 81 Systematic reviews and meta-analyses indicate that average knee flexion strength LSI values typically approach ∼90% at 1 year after ACLR with HT autografts.82,83 Although this rate of knee flexion strength recovery appears encouraging, the interpretation of strength outcomes is highly dependent on the testing method used. 84 The most frequently used test is seated concentric knee flexion using an isokinetic dynamometer. 85 However, when compared with the Nordic hamstring exercise (NHE) assessed on a NordBord, LSI values are consistently ∼10% lower, both within first postoperative year 86 and up to 5 years after ACLR with HT autografts. 87 Fewer than half of the individuals achieve an LSI ≥90% on the NordBord, even up to 5 years after ACLR.86,87 Even greater LSI asymmetries are observed when knee flexion strength is assessed at deep knee flexion angles in prone or supine positions, particularly when the hip is extended.61,84

Testing knee flexion strength at different joint angles highlights substantial angle-specific asymmetries.61,88 Nikose et al. 88 investigated isometric knee flexion torque and LSI in the prone position 1-year after ACLR at knee angles between 30° and 105°. They reported that isometric knee flexion strength in the ACLR leg was roughly 116% of body weight (91% LSI) at 30° of knee flexion, but only ∼44% of body weight (73% LSI) at 105°. Similarly, Murakami et al. 61 reported that knee flexion strength in a prone position appeared symmetrical at moderate knee flexion angles (e.g., 45°) but LSI declined markedly at deeper angles, reaching 65% in LSI at 90° and 56% in LSI at 120° of knee flexion. These angle-dependent deficits may partly reflect incomplete or absent tendon regeneration, as supported by the finding that individuals without ST tendon regeneration exhibit greater knee flexion strength deficits than individuals with regeneration. 53 Even among individuals with tendon regeneration, a moderate negative correlation has been observed between the proximal migration of the ST musculotendinous junction and knee flexion peak torque, particularly at 6 months postoperatively. 65

When the hip is extended and the knee is deeply flexed, neither the BFlh nor the SM appears able to compensate effectively due to mechanical disadvantages in their moment arms. 89 In addition, both the BFlh and SM have shorter fascicle lengths than the ST. Accordingly, the sarcomere lengths in the BFlh are likely shortened in these positions, 90 and the sarcomere lengths in the SM are probably even more shortened given the inherently shorter fascicles. In contrast, the ST has a long excursion range. 12 Consequently, when the ST is compromised by the tendon harvest, the BFlh and SM muscle fibers are too short to effectively compensate at deeper knee flexion angles, particularly with the hip extended. Since the ST may remain considerably shortened after ACLR, even after successful tendon regeneration, 53 the capacity of the ST to generate force in these positions is further diminished.

Simulation modeling based on postoperative MRI of knee flexor morphology demonstrates that the ST exhibits a reduced knee flexion moment arm and a lower torque-generating capacity in the ACLR leg. 91 Notably, these alterations persist when the tendon regenerates at the pes anserinus, as ongoing changes in muscle morphology continue to modulate strength deficits. 91 Another simulation study confirmed that interlimb asymmetries are most pronounced at deeper knee flexion angles. 89 Interestingly, ST atrophy, both radial and longitudinal, does not fully account for the strength deficits observed at deep knee flexion angles, suggesting that additional factors, such as sarcomere shortening, may contribute even in the presence of tendon regeneration. 89 Furthermore, decreases in muscle quality, for example due to increased intramuscular fat deposition, 75 may also exacerbate strength deficits. Taken together, the combination of muscle atrophy, shorter fascicles, potentially shortened sarcomeres, decreased muscle quality, and reductions in the knee flexion moment arm of the ST help explain why torque deficits can persist for several years postoperatively (Figure 3).

Another reported finding is a shift in the knee flexion angle at which peak torque occurs during seated isokinetic concentric testing.92,93 In the ACLR leg, the knee flexion angle tends to shift toward extension, likely reflecting an increased relative contribution from synergistic knee flexor muscles. The shift could be attributable to the relatively shorter muscle fibers in BFlh and SM,11,12,94 which reinforces the notion that knee flexion strength assessment after ACLR cannot rely on a single test position. Given the biarticular nature of the knee flexors and the heterogeneous morphology and function of individual knee flexor muscles, strength should be evaluated in both hip-flexed and hip-extended positions to provide a comprehensive assessment of knee flexion strength recovery.

Finally, the contraction mode may also influence the strength outcomes. Egerci et al. 95 demonstrated lower LSI in isokinetic eccentric knee flexion (70% LSI) than in concentric knee flexion (87% LSI) in a group of male individuals at 12 months post-ACLR with HT autografts, although the test position was not specified. Further studies with larger samples are needed to confirm this finding.

7.2. ST alone versus ST and GR for outcomes after ACL-R

The HT autograft can consist of the ST tendon alone or of both the ST and the GR tendons. The harvest of the GR tendon in addition to the ST tendon has led to the hypothesis that knee flexion strength could be more adversely affected compared with using the ST tendon alone. An early systematic review by Ardern et al. 96 suggested that HT autografts comprising both ST and GR tendon may result in reduced knee flexion strength at knee flexion angles ≥70° compared with the ST alone. In a subsequent study by the same group, 97 isokinetic and isometric strength were evaluated in a seated position at 30°, 90° and 105°, with no differences observed between graft configurations. Furthermore, evidence from a large registry study of ∼7,000 individuals, 98 a randomized controlled trial, 99 and a systematic review with meta-analysis 100 indicates no significant differences in knee flexion strength between ST alone or ST together with the GR for ACL-R. Nevertheless, as discussed in Section 3.3, the GR may contribute to limiting anterior tibial translation. Cristiani et al. 98 reported that individuals receiving HT autograft which consisted of both the ST and GR had greater mean side-to-side anterior laxity compared to individuals in whom the ST alone was harvested. Despite this, inclusion of the GR tendon in the HT autograft was not associated with an increased risk of revision ACL-R after primary ACL-R compared with the use of the ST tendon alone. 98

7.3. Assessment of muscle function by MRI and EMG

Functional MRI assessment of muscle activation via exercise-induced T2 signal changes has demonstrated reduced ST activation during the NHE in the ACLR leg compared with the non-injured leg, which was interpreted as suggesting a potential neural inhibition component. 74 Similarly, a functional MRI study during prone weighted leg-curl exercise with the hips at 60° of flexion revealed increased BFlh activation alongside reduced ST activation in the ACLR leg compared with the non-injured leg. 101 These findings support the notion that synergistic muscles partially compensate for ST deficits during knee flexion tasks, particularly when the hip is flexed and at low-to-moderate knee flexion angles.

However, it is important to note that evidence for impaired neural activation of the ST is limited, and that there is also contrary evidence. Takeda et al. 102 reported that post-exercise T2 signal changes in the ST and GR were comparable between the ACLR and non-injured leg after 10 sets of 10 maximal concentric knee flexion contractions at 120°/s in an isokinetic dynamometer, although the test position was not explicitly stated. 102 Similarly, Makihara et al. 103 reported that EMG activity of the ST at 50% of a maximal voluntary isometric contraction in prone knee flexion at 45° was comparable between the ACLR and non-injured leg. In contrast, at 90° of knee flexion, ST EMG was significantly lower in ACLR leg compared with the non-injured leg. 103 Notably, BFlh and SM EMG activities were also reduced at 90° of knee flexion compared to 45° in both legs. 103 Makihara et al. 103 suggested that at deeper knee flexion angles, the ST in the ACLR leg was unable to generate adequate torque due to atrophy and shortening of the muscle. The results of Makihara et al. 103 and Takeda et al. 102 argue against a generalized neural inhibition of the ST and highlight the critical importance of test position, joint angle, and contraction type in assessing knee flexion strength after ACLR.

8. Early phase rehabilitation: To load or not to load?

As HT harvest may lead to altered knee flexor morphology and deficits in knee flexion strength, a graft-specific rehabilitation protocol is likely needed. However, the components of such a protocol remain unclear. A scoping review 104 found inconclusive evidence regarding the optimal timing for initiating knee flexor strengthening. The authors reported insufficient data to determine whether isometric knee flexor exercises can be safely introduced before 6 weeks postoperatively, although they recommended starting them after 6 weeks. 104 For dynamic knee flexor loading, the review advised avoiding such exercises during the first 4 weeks, with inconclusive data from 4 to 12 weeks, and recommended initiating dynamic exercises only after 12 weeks postoperatively. 104 This uncertainty raises a central question: Does early loading promote tendon regeneration, mitigate morphological changes, and enhance knee flexion strength recovery, or does it risk impairing these processes? The underlying hypothesis is that in the absence of a distal tendon attachment, the muscle belly may retract during contraction. Greater retraction could create a larger gap for the regenerating tendon to span to reach the pes anserinus, potentially resulting in a longer, thinner, and more proximally positioned tendon. Based on this assumption, a period of postoperative immobilization may limit muscle retraction and theoretically support more favorable tendon regeneration.

Nakamae et al. 52 tested the immobilization hypothesis by comparing two postoperative protocols. One group wore a brace locked at 20° of knee flexion for 3 days, followed by passive range of motion exercises. Active range of motion and partial weight-bearing were permitted at 10 days, with full weight bearing at 4 weeks. 52 The second group remained immobilized for 10-14 days before initiating passive range of motion, with active range of motion and partial weight-bearing introduced at 3 weeks, and full weight-bearing at 5 weeks. 52 At 6-12 months follow-up, no differences were observed between the groups in ST morphology, as assessed by CT, or in knee flexion strength. 52 These findings suggest that extended immobilization does not meaningfully influence tendon regeneration or strength outcomes. If immobilization does not influence tendon regeneration, early loading may represent an alternative strategy. Labanca et al. 105 investigated early loading combined with neuromuscular electrical stimulation (NMES) beginning on postoperative day 15. The NMES was applied to the ST during squat exercises, progressing to step-ups after 2 weeks for an additional 4 week-period. 105 Compared with a standard rehabilitation protocol, no significant differences in ST morphology were observed at 1 year after ACLR using MRI. 105 Overall, the limited available data suggests that morphological changes in the ST muscle and tendon appear to occur regardless of early management strategy.

Given that the early morphological changes may be largely unavoidable, the focus of rehabilitation shifts toward optimizing overall knee flexion strength recovery. Based on clinical experience, Buckthorpe et al. 106 suggested that isometric loading at short to mid-muscle lengths can be introduced as early as 4 weeks postoperatively, guided by symptom response. Supporting this approach, an exploratory study in individuals undergoing ACLR with HT autograft implemented twice-weekly seated isometric knee flexion at 60° and 90°, as well as an isometric long-lever glute bridge, starting at 3 weeks postoperatively. 107 Exercises were performed for 5 sets of 45-second holds, with pain limited to ≤3/10 on a numerical pain rating scale, and training progressed to seated isotonic leg-curls at 6 weeks. 107 No adverse events were reported, and mean improvements in isometric knee flexion strength LSI from postoperative weeks 6 to 12 weeks were ∼ 10% at 60° of knee flexion, and ∼20% at 90° of knee flexion. 107

Similarly, early exposure to the NHE produced no adverse events during maximal eccentric testing at 10 weeks postoperatively. 86 An accelerated rehabilitation program allowed bridging variations at 2 weeks, good mornings at 3 weeks, seated leg-curl at 4 weeks, sliding eccentric leg-curls at 6 weeks, and NHE at 10 weeks. 108 At 6 months, individuals achieved an average LSI of ∼97% for knee flexion strength, significantly greater than the control group, again without adverse events. 108 Additionally, Takeda et al. 102 introduced leg-curl exercise already at 3 weeks postoperatively, and at a mean follow-up for 12.7 months, all 11 individuals had regeneration of the semitendinosus tendon with normal topography at or below the knee-joint line, with no significant difference in the anatomical CSA of the ST between the ACLR and non-injured leg (8.0 ± 2.6 cm2 vs. 8.5 ± 2.1 cm2).

Collectively, it remains unclear whether postoperative morphological changes of the ST can be prevented or minimized, and current data is insufficient to resolve this question. Moreover, even if formal knee flexor strengthening is delayed, individuals will inevitably contract their knee flexors during daily activities, such as stair climbing. Unless complete immobilization is maintained until full tendon regeneration, which is neither feasible nor desirable, some morphological changes are likely to occur regardless. Given this reality, rehabilitation may be better directed toward optimizing strength recovery despite these changes. Current evidence suggests that early, symptom-guided loading can be performed safely and may promote superior knee flexion strength recovery.86,105–108 However, most assessments to date have used seated knee flexion testing, which raises the possibility of compensation from other knee flexor muscles, which likely will mask the true extent of ST and GR recovery.86,101 Whether early loading improves medial knee flexor strength and structure, particularly in the ST, remains unknown and warrants further investigation. In this context, the importance of controlled loading must also be emphasized, given case reports of sudden sharp pain after too aggressive loading in the first 3-4 weeks of rehabilitation. 109 Such ‘ruptures’ may in turn lead to unsuccessful tendon regeneration. 109

9. Optimizing long-term knee flexion strength recovery

To optimize knee flexion strength recovery after ACLR using HT autograft likely requires an approach that acknowledges the unique morphological and functional consequences of tendon harvest. Long-term follow-up studies (up to ∼7-11 years) have demonstrated persistent reductions in ST muscle volume, proximal migration of the musculotendinous junction, shortened fascicles, and fatty infiltration,70,71 collectively depicting a scenario in which full restoration of native knee flexor structure may appear unlikely. Given that regeneration is common yet anatomically variable, and that substantial muscle–tendon remodeling has been reported regardless of postoperative management,52,105 rehabilitation may need to shift from attempting to fully restore preoperative morphology toward strategies that maximize knee flexion strength recovery within these altered constraints, across multiple hip and knee angles.

The biarticular nature of the knee flexors and their heterogeneous activation patterns during various knee flexion and hip extension exercises can inform strategies to optimize knee flexion strength recovery. Based on functional MRI and EMG assessments, the BFlh is more active relative to the ST during hip-dominant tasks, whereas the ST exhibits greater activation during knee-dominant tasks. 110 Using high-density surface EMG, Hegyi et al. 111 demonstrated distinct intermuscular and proximal-distal activation patterns that varied by exercise. Within the ST, distal activation was lower than mid/proximal activation during bent-knee bridge and leg-curl exercises, but not during straight-knee bridging, upright hip extension on a conic pulley, 45° hip extension, cable pendulum, unilateral Romanian dead-lift, or good mornings. 111 Whether targeted exercises that increase distal ST activation can reverse shortening of the distal muscle is currently unknown, but these findings underscore the need to vary knee flexors exercises to target both individual muscles and different intramuscular regions. Additionally, the extent to which preferential activation patterns observed in healthy individuals translate to individuals after ACLR remains largely unexplored. One exercise reported to elicit high ST activation in individuals after ACLR is the seated leg-curl, 30 which produces substantial MRI T2 signal intensity increases in the ST of both the ACLR and non-injured legs. 102

The persistent deficits observed at deep knee flexion angles and with the hip extended 84 may partly be attributable to shortening of the ST and GR fascicles as well as muscle atrophy. Accordingly, it could be argued that once the tensile strength of the tendon permits, the rehabilitation should perhaps progressively emphasize exercises that load the medial knee flexors at long muscle lengths, such as seated dynamic knee flexions. Furthermore, the gradual introduction of eccentric loading, including exercises such as sliding leg-curls and the NHE, appears to be well tolerated early and may drive meaningful improvements in LSI at 6 and 12 months postoperatively. 108 However, whether such loading specifically restores the specific contribution of the ST, as opposed to promoting compensatory hypertrophy or neural substitution from other knee flexors, remains largely unknown. This distinction is clinically important, as compensation by synergists may restore torque in mid-range positions while leaving deficits at deeper knee flexion angles unresolved.

Nevertheless, a study that combined ultrasound and microendoscopy provided direct evidence that muscle fascicles can lengthen in healthy individuals after 9 weeks of NHE through the addition of more sarcomeres in series within the muscle fibers. 112 This contrasts with shorter-term NHE training, where increases in fascicle length are primarily attributable to sarcomere lengthening. 113 In the study by Andrews et al., 112 BFlh fascicle lengths increased by 19% in the central muscle region and 33% in the distal region, corresponding to absolute increases of ∼1.8-2.6 cm. In a subsequent report from the same project, 114 DT-MRI demonstrated increases in fiber tract lengths across all hamstring muscles after 9 weeks of NHE training, with the ST exhibiting the greatest increase (19%). These findings are of potential clinical relevance, as they imply that it may be possible to lengthen the shortened fascicles in the ST and GR, even in individuals after ACLR with HT autograft through extended training periods of knee flexor resistance exercise that include eccentric contractions. Accordingly, future studies should investigate the long-term effects of exercise such as the NHE and seated knee flexions on ST and GR fascicle lengths in individuals after ACLR.

Interestingly, maximal EMG activity during common knee flexor exercises has been reported to reach only 40-65% of peak values during sprinting for the ST, 18-40% for the BFlh, and 40-75% for the SM.115,116 However, the EMG reflects neural drive rather than the underlying tissue loading. Tissue loading is influenced by several additional factors, including muscle length and contraction velocity. Accordingly, combining EMG data with measures of muscle length and contraction velocity through musculoskeletal modeling may provide more accurate estimate of forces experienced by the hamstring. Breed et al. 117 reported that sprinting elicited the greatest peak ST force, although this was not significantly different from the NHE, unilateral hamstring bridge or unilateral eccentric slider. The Romanian deadlift produced greater BFlh and SM forces than sprinting when the perceived exertion was ≥8/10. 117 Importantly, activation of both the BFlh and ST increases exponentially at sprint speeds above 85% of the maximal velocity, with substantially greater ST activation (EMG data) compared with the BFlh. 118 These findings suggests that sprinting imposes high forces and strain on the hamstrings which may have implications for hamstring retraining. Therefore, a controlled symptom-guided implementation of sprinting during the later rehabilitation phases may be important to optimize improve knee flexor function prior to return to sport.

10. Limitations

This review has several limitations. First, as a narrative review, the literature search was not exhaustive, which increases the risk of missing relevant studies. In addition, the absence of predefined inclusion criteria and formal risk of bias assessment introduces a potential selection bias. Second, much of the available data on knee flexion strength and muscle morphology relies on comparisons with the non-injured leg. While commonly used, this approach may be confounded by bilateral adaptations after injury and surgery, and comparisons with healthy controls may be a better reference. Third, there is a lack of intervention studies which investigates the effect of targeted knee flexion strength training on muscle morphology after ACLR. Such studies are essential to inform future rehabilitation strategies. Consequently, the rehabilitation section within this narrative review is mainly clinical reasoning based on the current available data. Finally, heterogeneity in study populations and rehabilitations protocols limits comparability across studies. The absence of quantitative synthesis further limits the ability to draw firm conclusions regarding the magnitude of observed effects.

11. Conclusion

Morphological alterations in the knee flexors are commonly observed after ACLR with HT autografts and may be associated with deficits in knee flexion strength and function. To improve knee flexion strength recovery likely requires a multifaceted rehabilitation approach. Early, symptom-guided initiation of isometric and low-load exercises may help minimize early strength loss, followed by progressive loading of the knee flexor muscles across multiple hip-and knee flexion angles. Knee flexion exercises performed in hip-flexed positions may be useful to achieve a high ST activation and potentially address both muscle-tendon unit adaptations and length-related changes. Subsequently, eccentric loading may be incorporated as a part of a progressive rehabilitation strategy, with the aim of enhancing longer fascicle lengths and neuromuscular recruitment. Regular assessment across multiple hip and knee-joint positions can be considered to monitor knee flexion strength recovery, as testing in a seated position may mask residual deficits in the ST and GR (Figure 4). However, whether these strategies can meaningfully restore medial knee flexor structure, strength and overall function remains a critical area for future research.
