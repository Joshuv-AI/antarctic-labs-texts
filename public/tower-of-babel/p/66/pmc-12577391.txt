Knowledge, attitudes, and practices toward mpox among university health science students in Türkiye: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Ömer Ataç, Ayşe Nur Balcı Yapalak, Osman Hayran
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe aim of this study was to examine the level of knowledge, attitudes and preventive practices regarding mpox among health science students.MethodsThis cross-sectional study was conducted among a group of students from four schools of a university in İstanbul, Türkiye. All students were invited to participate voluntarily, and the final sample represented convenience sampling based on classroom attendance. A total of 1,466 students participated with a participation rate of 31%. Data were collected between September 20 and October 15, 2024, using a paper-based self-administered questionnaire that was distributed and retrieved in person during class sessions. The questionnaire included questions to describe demographic characteristics and to measure the level of knowledge. Attitudes and practices were evaluated by measuring the agreement level on a 5-point Likert scale of various statements. Group differences were assessed using chi-square tests for categorical variables and t-tests or ANOVA for continuous variables.ResultsHealth science students had limited knowledge about mpox, with a mean score of 10.09 out of 22 (45.9%) correct responses. Just over half of participants (51.0%) reported agreement with favorable attitude items, while only 19.3% reported agreement with preventive practice items. Social media was the most frequently reported source of information (56.3%), followed by TV channels (35.8%). Most students (74.9%) expressed hesitancy toward vaccination, and 60.5% of hesitant respondents cited fear of side effects as their main concern.ConclusionOur study highlights gaps in mpox knowledge, attitudes, and preventive practices among university health sciences students. Low willingness to receive a mpox vaccine was the most prominent finding, and many students reported concerns about side effects as their main reason for reluctance. Reliance on social media for information was also common, underscoring the need for targeted educational campaigns that address vaccine safety and promote accurate information dissemination.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-025-08090-x.

Introduction

Mpox is a zoonotic disease caused by the monkeypox virus and first identified in humans in the Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC) in 1970, has primarily been endemic to Central and West Africa [1]. The outbreak that began in May 2022 globally spread and the World Health Organization (WHO) declared mpox as a Public Health Emergency of International Concern (PHEIC) in July 2022. Although the emergency status was removed in May 2023 due to the noteworthy progress for controlling the outbreak globally, the situation worsened. Between January 2022 and November 2024, 117,663 confirmed cases and 263 deaths were reported from 127 countries, and upsurge of mpox in the DRC with a growing number of countries in Africa, leading WHO to declare mpox as a PHEIC for the second time in August 2024 [2].

Mpox mostly presents as a self-limiting disease but complications, including encephalitis, pneumonia, and secondary bacterial infections, can occur especially among children, pregnant women, and immunocompromised individuals. The primary modes of transmission include close contact with infected individuals, respiratory droplets, and contaminated objects. Current evidence also shows potential sexual transmission pathways [1].

Türkiye reported 12 mpox cases in 2022 and 20 cases between April 2024 and March 2025, indicating the emergence of disease in non-endemic regions [3]. Türkiye is an intersection point of Europe, Asia, and the Middle East, along with its role for international travel and migration, increases the potential for exposure to infectious diseases like mpox. Although the confirmed case numbers are low, some factors such as migration and cross-border movement, highlight the need for enhanced public health measures to monitor and control the disease.

Prevention of mpox mostly depends on surveillance, contact tracing, improved awareness about the disease, and preventive measures such as hygiene practices and vaccination [4, 5]. Modified vaccinia Ankara-Bavarian Nordic (MVA-BN) have demonstrated efficacy in preventing mpox, yet hesitancy and misinformation remain as barriers [6]. Public knowledge and adherence to preventive measures are essential for effective disease control among the population.

Health science students are one of the target groups for interventions. They are typically young, socially active, and engaged in environments available for rapid information dissemination. Beyond their future responsibilities as healthcare providers, they may already influence peer norms and public awareness during health crises through their social networks. However, recent studies suggest some gaps in knowledge about mpox among students, particularly in non-endemic countries [7–12].

In Türkiye, health science students will play an important role in epidemic preparedness and response as future healthcare providers who will be directly involved in patient education, vaccination counseling, patient management, and outbreak control. Their position also makes them key actors in shaping public opinion and countering misinformation during public health challenges. This is especially relevant in Türkiye, which faces ongoing challenges related to international mobility and infectious disease risks. Strengthening the preparedness of health science students is essential for both their professional development and for improving health literacy among the population.

To date, research on mpox awareness in Türkiye has been limited to specific subpopulations, such as men who have sex with men and individuals living with HIV [13, 14]. No studies have examined knowledge, attitudes and practices (KAP) regarding mpox among health science students. Our study aims to contribute to filling this gap by assessing the KAP of health science students regarding mpox, as they are among the future stakeholders in public health preparedness and response. Specifically, we aim to: (1) assess the level of knowledge about mpox among health science students; (2) evaluate their attitudes toward mpox prevention and vaccination; (3) examine their self-reported practices; and (4) identify sociodemographic and educational factors associated with differences in knowledge, attitudes, and practices.

Methods

Study design and population

This cross-sectional study was conducted at the Istanbul Medipol University between September-October 2024. Students from four schools (Medicine, Dentistry, Pharmacy, Nursing) were the target population of the study (n = 4,673). All students were invited to participate, and the final sample represented a convenience sample of volunteers based on classroom attendance, yielding a 31% response rate.

Because the study aimed to include all students in the four schools, no a priori sample size calculation was performed. Ater data collection, a post-hoc power analysis was conducted based on the number of correct knowledge responses across schools. The effect size was calculated as 0.18, and power of the study was estimated at 99% at a significance level of 0.05 using G*Power [15].

Data collection

Data were collected using a paper-based, self-administered questionnaire prepared by the researchers (see Supplementary File 1). Questionnaires were distributed and retrieved in person before lectures, with prior permission from the relevant lecturers and distribution were carried out by final-year (6th year) medical students. The survey was anonymous, and no incentives such as course credit or gifts were offered. Responses were not audited during completion. Students who were absent, unwilling to participate, or who submitted incomplete questionnaires were excluded.

Age, gender, school and presence of a health professional in the family were the independent variables and level of knowledge, attitudes and preventive practices regarding mpox were the dependent variables of the study. The year-of-study variable was collected to examine whether the distribution of participants across class levels within each school was homogeneous or concentrated in a specific year (Supplement Table 1). However, because the number of available year-of-study differs substantially across schools (e.g., four years in nursing versus six years in medicine), this variable was not included as an independent predictor in analyses to avoid structural imbalance and biased estimates.

The questionnaire was developed based on a review of previous studies addressing knowledge, attitudes, and practices regarding mpox [1, 7–11]. Rather than adopting a single validated scale, we synthesized commonly assessed domains from the literature to design the items. The instrument consisted of five sections: (1) demographic characteristics, (2) knowledge statements, (3) attitudes, (4) preventive practices and (5) sources of information and reasons for vaccine hesitancy.

Knowledge level was measured by the number of correct responses to 22 statements about mpox. Knowledge scores ranged from 0 to 22, with each correct response awarded 1 point; incorrect and blank responses were scored as 0. Participants who left more than half of the knowledge statements blank were excluded from the analysis (n = 21).

Attitudes and practices were evaluated using a 5-point Likert scale (strongly agree, agree, not sure, disagree, strongly disagree) to 17 attitude statements and 5 preventive practice statements. The term ‘practices’ in this study refers to personal preventive behaviors (e.g., mask use, following outbreak news), rather than clinical practices. These personal behaviors are important in their own right and may also influence how students approach clinical training before graduation. For analysis, response options were collapsed into agreement (strongly agree/agree), disagreement (strongly disagree/disagree), and not sure. To avoid interpretive bias, we report agreement, disagreement, and not sure categories directly in tables and text. For vaccine-specific items, we additionally distinguish between vaccine-accepting (agreement with pro-vaccination statements) and vaccine-hesitant (agreement with anti-vaccination statements) responses to preserve directionality at the item level. Some attitude items were positively worded (e.g., “Health workers should be vaccinated against mpox”), whereas others were negatively worded (e.g., “There is no need to be vaccinated, as mpox is mild”). For summary measures, agreement with positively worded items and disagreement with negatively worded items were both counted as favorable responses; the reverse was counted as unfavorable. Participants were classified according to whichever response type was most frequent across all items: favorable, unfavorable, or unsure. This classification was used only for participant-level comparisons (e.g., gender, school), whereas item-level results are presented as agreement/disagreement percentages for transparency. Participants who left attitude or practice items unanswered were classified as “not sure” and those who left more than half of the attitude or practice items blank (n = 60 and n = 96 respectively) were excluded from the analysis.

The study was conducted in adherence with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval no. 920 was obtained from Istanbul Medipol University Non-Invasive Research Ethics Committee on 26 September 2024. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Statistical analysis

The normality of continuous variables (age and the number of correct knowledge responses) was assessed using skewness (1.748 and − 0.266, respectively), kurtosis (7.788 and − 0.496, respectively), and visual inspection of histograms. Formal tests such as the Shapiro–Wilk or Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests were not applied, as these tests tend to become overly sensitive with large sample sizes and often indicate statistically significant deviations even for minor departures from normality [16].

Descriptive statistics were reported as means and standard deviations for continuous variables and as frequencies and percentages for categorical variables. Univariate analyses were performed using chi-square tests for categorical variables and t-tests or ANOVA for continuous variables. Post-hoc analyses were conducted using Bonferroni correction to adjust for multiple comparisons.

As a supplementary sensitivity analysis, linear regression was used to explore predictors of knowledge scores, and logistic regression was applied for attitudes and practices. For these analyses, responses were dichotomized as agreement versus disagreement, with “not sure” responses excluded to ensure clearer group comparisons. Full regression results are presented in the Supplementary File 2.

All statistical analyses were performed with IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 22), and figures were created using Flourish Studio [17, 18]. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

However, more than half of respondents expressed uncertainty about vertical transmission, the availability of specific treatment, the possibility of long-term immunity and incorrectly believed mpox could not be transmitted via respiratory droplets (Supplementary Table 2).

Discussion

In this cross-sectional study, health science students demonstrated limited knowledge about mpox, with a mean score of 10.09 out of 22 (45.9%) correct responses. Just over half of participants (51.0%) reported agreement with favorable attitude items, while only 22.9% reported agreement with preventive practice items. Social media was the primary source of information for mpox and followed by TV channels. Vaccination for mpox was a concern, with side-effect concerns cited most frequently as the reason for reluctance.

Our findings are consistent with the findings of similar studies from different countries. For example, studies from Egypt and Pakistan found that only a small proportion of the university students demonstrated good knowledge [6, 19]. Similarly, studies from Algeria and Bangladesh reported that fewer than half of respondents correctly answered knowledge questions and that agreement with favorable attitudes and practices was limited [7, 11]. Even among healthcare workers and medical students’ knowledge levels are only moderate in some studies [9, 11, 20]. In a study including 11,929 medical students from 27 countries, it was found that only 55.3% had good knowledge [21] and another study reported slightly higher knowledge scores for medical students, but still lower than the sufficient knowledge level [22] both showing that education in related fields does not necessarily ensure a high level of knowledge. All these findings indicate the necessity of information provision to enhance awareness among health sciences students who are the key group for prevention efforts.

These findings should also be considered within the Turkish context. Mpox case numbers have remained very low in Türkiye compared to some other countries, which may partly explain the limited awareness and knowledge observed among students. In addition, the Turkish healthcare system is strongly centralized, with a tradition of receiving health information through official institutions and academic training. However, younger populations often rely more heavily on social media, which may account for the high proportion of students citing social media as their main source of information. Attitudes toward mpox vaccination may also reflect patterns observed in Türkiye during other emerging infections, where trust in official health authorities and community influences shape preventive behaviors and vaccine decisions. Together, these contextual factors provide important insight into why knowledge gaps and vaccine hesitancy were evident in our sample.

An interesting finding was that students with a health professional in the family scored lower on knowledge questions compared to those without. This counterintuitive result may reflect reliance on informal conversations with family members rather than scientific studies or other reliable sources, which might not provide accurate or up-to-date information. It is also possible that these students might feel more confident in their existing knowledge and therefore engage less actively with formal learning opportunities. Further research is needed to clarify this relationship and explore the factors behind it.

In our study, gender was not a determinant of knowledge level since no difference is found between males and females. Conflicting results have been reported regarding gender in the relevant literature. Some studies have found no difference in knowledge level by gender [6, 7, 11] while others have reported higher levels of knowledge for females than males [19, 21]. However, considering all these findings we cannot conclude gender as a significant determinant of knowledge level.

Agreement with favorable attitude items was more frequent among females in our study group, similar to the findings of some other studies [6, 7, 21]. Nursing students also reported higher agreement with favorable attitude items compared with other schools. While nursing is traditionally more preferred by females in Türkiye, our regression analyses controlling for gender showed that the difference between nursing and medical students remained significant. Both gender and professional training may play a role, and further research is needed to disentangle these effects.

The nature of preventive practices was not different by gender and presence of a healthcare professional in the family while agreement with preventive practice items was lowest among dentistry students. The most common practices included monitoring mpox-related news, modifying personal hygiene habits, and taking preventive precautions. Practices specific to mpox among health science students were less documented in the literature, presenting a research gap. However, willingness to get vaccinated, which can be considered a practice, provides some insight. In our study, 74,9% of the participants were not willing to receive the mpox vaccine, primarily with concerns about its side effects. This is higher than the findings of other studies [6, 11]. Fears of unknown side effects and insufficient information remained key barriers to vaccination. Hussein et al.’s multi-country study also reported moderate confidence in the vaccine, yet only 32% were willing to get vaccinated to prevent transmission [8]. These findings indicate the necessity of clear public health messaging to address vaccine safety concerns to increase acceptance rates when needed.

Social media was the most common information source for mpox in our study group, while healthcare organizations and official institutions are used less frequently. This aligns with previous studies showing that social media is the most preferred source of health information among students [11, 21], with a great majority searching for health-related content online [22]. In contrast, Nassar found WHO reports to be a key source, but his study group has included only pharmacists and pharmacy students [10]. A multi-country study of medical students also found that information from social media or scientific websites, together with higher knowledge levels predicted more favorable attitudes toward mpox [21]. These findings highlight both the widespread reliance on social media and the variability in trusted sources across student groups.

Our findings on mpox vaccine hesitancy should also be interpreted within the broader vaccine confidence literature emerging after the COVID-19 pandemic. Post-COVID studies have documented widespread uncertainty regarding new vaccines, where safety concerns, rapid development processes, and exposure to misinformation all played central roles. Similar dynamics may help explain why side-effect concerns dominated in our study, even among health science students. The role of social media is especially critical. While it can increase access to information, it also represents a major channel for misinformation [23]. This dual role underscores the public health risks of unregulated health content online and highlights the importance of strengthening digital health literacy and promoting accurate communication through trusted institutions.

Given these knowledge gaps, vaccine hesitancy, and reliance on social media, targeted interventions are needed to better prepare health science students for emerging infectious diseases. Incorporating mpox and outbreak awareness modules into health science curricula would provide structured, evidence-based training. Partnerships between universities and official health organizations could help disseminate accurate content on social media platforms, while campaigns that directly address vaccine safety concerns may reduce hesitancy and strengthen trust in vaccines.

Our study has many strengths. A primary strength is that it is the first assessment of knowledge, attitudes, and preventive practices on mpox among university students in Türkiye. The relatively large sample also provided comparative insights across different health science student groups. In addition, data were collected soon after the second WHO PHEIC declaration, capturing student perspectives during a critical stage of global attention. Finally, the use of a structured, literature-based questionnaire allowed us to explore key domains of knowledge, attitudes, and practices in a systematic way.

This study has several limitations. First, it was based on a voluntary, classroom-based survey with a participation rate of 31%. Although all students were invited, this effectively constitutes a convenience sample rather than a representative random sample. This low response rate raises concerns about selection bias, as students absent from class or less willing to participate may differ systematically from those who responded. Furthermore, the study was conducted at a single university in İstanbul, which limits the generalizability of the findings to other institutions or cultural settings. Second, the data relied on self-reported responses, which may be subject to social desirability bias, particularly for items on attitudes and preventive behaviors. Responses were collected in a classroom environment, which might have reinforced tendencies to provide socially acceptable answers. Third, the study instrument has not undergone formal psychometric validation, and while it was developed based on existing literature, this lack of validity and reliability testing is a limitation. In addition, coding all “not sure” responses as incorrect may underestimate partial or evolving knowledge of mpox. Fourth, as a cross-sectional design, the study cannot assess causal relationships. We did not adjust for potential confounding variables through multivariable analyses, which may affect interpretation of subgroup differences. Fifth, our assessment of ‘practices’ focused on personal preventive behaviors rather than clinical procedures, which may limit comparability but remains relevant given their influence on students’ clinical training. Finally, the timing of data collection between September and November 2024 coincided with the second declaration of mpox as a Public Health Emergency of International Concern, when media coverage was high. This may have influenced both awareness levels and attitudes of the participants.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study highlights gaps in mpox knowledge, attitudes, and preventive practices among university health sciences students in Türkiye. The most prominent finding was low willingness to receive a mpox vaccine, with nearly three-quarters of students expressing hesitancy, most often due to concerns about side effects. Reliance on social media for information was also common, underscoring both the opportunities and risks of this medium for health communication. Future studies should examine the role of social media in shaping attitudes toward emerging infectious diseases. These findings align with other studies, emphasizing continuing challenges in mpox awareness. From a public health perspective, our results underline the urgent need for educational interventions that address vaccine safety concerns, promote accurate information through trusted channels, and prepare university students—future health professionals—for mpox and other emerging infections. Strengthening such initiatives will not only enhance outbreak preparedness in this group but also contribute to broader public health readiness. preparedness.
