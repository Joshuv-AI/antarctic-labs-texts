Modified atmosphere under cold storage does not reduce phytosanitary irradiation efficacy for Drosophila suzukii (Diptera: Drosophilidae)
Authors: Inajara Viana Gomes, Maria Teresa Vera, Alessandro Migliori, Polychronis Rempoulakis, Christian Stauffer, Scott W Myers, Vanessa Simoes Dias, Aruna Manrakhan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPhytosanitary irradiation (PI) has been advancing consistently in recent years, partly due to its broad applicability to fresh produce and insect pests. Internationally adopted PI treatment schedules cover all plant hosts associated with a target pest. Thus, it is crucial to evaluate the impact of commodity-related postharvest practices on PI efficacy. For instance, modified atmospheres (MA) used to extend the shelf life of fresh produce might increase insect radiotolerance. Hence, we tested the extent to which combining atmospheric (severe hypoxia–N2, severe hypoxia–CO2, hypoxia, and normoxia) and storage conditions (25 and 0 °C) affects PI efficacy against Drosophila suzukii (Matsumura) (Diptera: Drosophilidae). Late pupae of D. suzukii were irradiated at nominal doses of 52 Gy (suboptimal dose) and 72 Gy (PI dose). Prevention of egg laying was considered the treatment endpoint. Our results showed that egg laying was prevented in PI-treated females at 72 Gy, regardless of atmospheric or storage conditions. Females irradiated with the suboptimal dose of 52 Gy under severe hypoxia laid significantly more eggs than in normoxia or hypoxia. Ovary dissections and scanning electron microscope (SEM) imaging confirmed irreversible damage in the reproductive tract of PI-treated D. suzukii females. We conclude that a minimum absorbed dose of 80 Gy is effective for D. suzukii, preventing egg laying even under modified atmosphere and cold storage conditions.Keywords: spotted wing drosophila, postharvest treatment, low temperature, cobalt-60, low oxygen

Introduction

Ionizing radiation has been used as a phytosanitary measure to disinfest fresh commodities and prevent the spread of regulated pests by impairing their development or rendering them infertile (IPPC 2023). Unlike phytosanitary temperature treatments, which are limited to specific pest species and hosts, phytosanitary irradiation (PI) is broadly applicable and can cover all hosts of the target pest as well as higher insect taxa through generic treatments (Hallman et al. 2016, Barkai-Golan and Follett 2017, USDA-APHIS 2024).

Phytosanitary irradiation is compatible with other postharvest practices and can be applied for various agricultural commodities. This integration supports the commercialization of high-quality and safe fresh produce (Mahajan et al. 2014, Lacroix and Follett 2015, Golding et al. 2024). For instance, modified atmosphere (MA) and cold storage are postharvest practices used to increase the shelf life of fresh commodities and reduce food waste (Porat et al. 2018). Due to concerns about the potential increase in insect radiotolerance under treatment combinations, the extent to which commodity-related postharvest technology applications, such as MA and cold storage, affect PI efficacy warrants systematic evaluation (Hallman et al. 2010, Follett 2014, Lacroix and Follett 2015, Hallman 2017).

While the simultaneous application of MA, cold storage, and PI is understudied, evidence exists on how they can affect PI efficacy for several insect pests (Table 1). For instance, robust and independent studies indicated that MA does not reduce the efficacy of phytosanitary irradiation for Tephritid fruit flies (Hallman 2004a, Follett et al. 2013, Srimartpirom et al. 2018, Dias et al. 2020, Zhan et al. 2020). These findings contributed to include ink amendments in several annexes phytosanitary treatments for fruit flies adopted by the International Plant Protection Convention (IPPC) under the International Standard for Phytosanitary Measures (ISPM) 28 (Phytosanitary treatments for regulated pests) (IPPC 2016). The amendments removed the disclaimer that the irradiation treatment should not be applied to fruits and vegetables stored in modified atmospheres (IPPC 2021a). Furthermore, 20 h of exposure at 1 ± 0.5 °C immediately before irradiation had no effect on the prevention of pupation in Rhagoletis pomonella third instars irradiated with 20 Gy (Hallman 2004a). However, exposure to 4 °C for 3-13 days post-irradiation consistently reduced pupation and adult emergence in Ceratitis capitata and Zeugodacus cucurbitae (Follett and Snook 2013).

Drosophila suzukii, commonly known as spotted-wing drosophila (SWD), is a widely distributed pest with the potential to infest over 80 plant hosts, including rosaceous crops (e.g., cherry, strawberry, raspberry) and blueberries (Vaccinium spp.) (Asplen et al. 2015), threatening free areas of this pest like Australia and New Zealand (Cini et al. 2012, Dos Santos et al. 2017, Abeijon et al. 2025). Scientific evidence supports a PI treatment of 80 Gy for D. suzukii (Follett et al. 2014, Gomes et al. 2024), and a treatment submission is currently under evaluation by the IPPC Technical Panel on Phytosanitary Treatment (TPPT). Soft-skinned and stone fruit, considered some of the preferred hosts of D. suzukii (Walsh et al. 2011, Kenis et al. 2016), are often cold-stored and treated with MA (Thompson 2010). The efficacy of irradiation treatments for D. suzukii third instars and late pupae exposed to MA conditions through gas flushing and modified atmosphere packaging (MAP), respectively, was not reduced for doses greater than 60 Gy (Follett et al. 2018, Chen et al. 2023).

Considering the commercial relevance of the simultaneous application of postharvest treatments for D. suzukii hosts, we assessed the potential effects of MA and cold storage on PI efficacy for the species. To our knowledge, this is the first simultaneous evaluation of combining effects from MA, cold storage, and PI for an insect pest. Additionally, we compared the radiation-induced ovarian damage in D. suzukii when PI was applied alone or combined with MA and cold storage. Our results, in conjunction with the existing scientific data (Follett et al. 2018, Chen et al. 2023), might be used to support a harmonized PI treatment without MA restrictions for D. suzukii.

Materials and Methods

Insects and Rearing

The D. suzukii colony utilized in this study was established in 2014 from flies collected in San Michele All ‘Adige, Trento, Italy. The colony was maintained at the Insect Pest Control Laboratory (IPCL) of the Joint FAO/IAEA Centre of Nuclear Techniques in Food and Agriculture, Seibersdorf, Austria, without the addition of wild insects. Immature stages of D. suzukii were reared on a wet artificial diet composed of 25.7% wheat bran, 6.4% brewer’s yeast, 11.9% sugar, 0.4% sodium benzoate, 0.4% nipagin, and 55.1% water (FAO/IAEA 2022). Sexually mature females were offered the artificial diet in Petri dishes for oviposition over a 24-h period. Following this period, Petri dishes containing the diet with newly laid eggs were removed from the cage and placed individually in plastic containers (38 × 37 × 21 cm) to facilitate larval development. After twelve days, larvae developed into the late pupal stage. The Petri dishes were then washed with fresh water for pupae collection. All collected pupae were carefully screened to confirm the lack of contamination with Drosophila melanogaster. Screened pupae were transferred into rearing cages (30 × 30 × 30 cm) to complete their development to the adult stage. Emerged adults were maintained in cages provided with water and a dry artificial diet consisting of a mixture of hydrolyzed yeast (MP Biomedicals) and sucrose (1:3). Rearing conditions were kept at 23 ± 1 °C, 65 ± 5% relative humidity, and 12 L:12D photoperiod.

Fruit Preparation and Infestation

Blueberries produced in Spain, Morocco, and Peru were obtained from a local market and used in our study. Prior to the experiment, the fruits were washed, rinsed, and soaked for 15 min in an antifungal solution (4% sodium benzoate and 1% sodium hypochlorite), followed by rinsing, drying, and cold storage. Fruit was placed in room temperature at 23 ± 1 °C 1-h before infestation, weighed, and portioned into 50 g samples for each experimental treatment. The fruit samples were naturally infested by their exposure to approximately ∼3,000 sexually mature D. suzukii adults in rearing cages (30 × 30 × 30 cm) for 24 h. After the infestation, the blueberries were transferred into separate plastic containers. To prevent mold contamination, the infested fruit was elevated using a piece of voile fabric secured with an elastic band to prevent contact with any leaked juice during maturation. The containers were individually labeled and sealed with a lid covered with voile fabric for ventilation. The infested fruit was incubated at 25 °C in a chamber (Forma Reach-In Incubator, Model 3951, Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA, USA) until the insects reached the late pupal stage, 11 days post-infestation, which is considered the most tolerant life stage of D. suzukii found in the commodity (Follett et al. 2014).

MA Conditions

Once the insects reached the late pupal stage, infested blueberries were placed in a 330-ml air- and liquid-tight polypropylene (PP) chamber (7.7 × 11.3 cm) with a screw-top lid. The chamber contained two plastic Luer-lock valves (Cole-Parmer, Vernon Hills, IL, USA) positioned on the top and bottom parts of the chamber to facilitate gas flushing and air expulsion. A 0.6-cm hole sealed with hot-melt adhesive (Pattex, Henkel, Austria) was created on the upper part of the jar and covered with a 1.5-cm adhesive septum to enable gas concentration measurements within the chamber. To ensure an airtight seal and prevent leaks, the chamber’s lid was further secured with transparent grease (Liqui Moly 3312, Germany) and self-adhesive plastic wrap (Toppits, Poland). Before irradiation, MA conditions mimicking commodity packaging were achieved by flushing certified gas mixtures (Linde Gas kGmbH, Stadl-Paura, Austria) into the chamber. Three specific gas mixtures were used: a hypoxic atmosphere with 5% oxygen, 10% carbon dioxide, and 85% nitrogen (Hypoxia) and two anoxic atmospheres, either with nearly 0% oxygen, 21% carbon dioxide, 1% argon, and 78% nitrogen (Severe-hypoxia CO2) or with nearly 0% oxygen and 100% nitrogen (Severe-hypoxia N2). Infested blueberries were held under each modified atmosphere treatment or a normoxia control (Nx) for 6 h. Gas concentrations inside each chamber were monitored hourly with a CheckMate 3 gas analyzer (Dansensor, Ringsted, Denmark) to confirm O2 and CO2 levels with uncertainties of ±0.01 (0–0.999% O2) or ±1.0% (1—100% O2) for oxygen and ±0.5% for carbon dioxide. Whenever the oxygen or carbon dioxide levels deviated by 1.0%, the certified gas mixture was flushed again to restore the desired atmosphere.

Cold Storage Conditions

The cold storage temperature was selected based on the standard postharvest cooling process for berries, where temperatures ranging between 0 and 3 °C are used to reduce respiration rates and extend shelf life of the fruit (Sargent et al. 2006). Accordingly, infested blueberries under each MA condition described above were either subjected to a cold storage temperature of 0 °C for 6 h or kept at room temperature at 25 °C. To reach and maintain the cold storage condition desired, a Percival chamber (model LT-36VL; Percival Scientific, Inc., Perry, IA, USA) with a temperature range of −10 to 44 °C (±0.5 °C) was used.

Irradiation Treatment

A nominal dose of 72 Gy, which effectively impairs the further development of D. suzukii by preventing egg laying after the irradiation treatment (Gomes et al. 2024), and a suboptimal nominal dose of 52 Gy was included to show a dose-response model, confirming that the recommended dose of 72 Gy, provides secure phytosanitary efficacy. In addition, for each combination of MA and temperature, a non-irradiated control was included. Following the 6-h MA and cold storage treatments, containers holding infested blueberries storage in cold were transported in insulated coolers (25.5 × 19.5 × 11.5 cm) (Chemglass Life Sciences LLC) containing ice to the irradiation room, located approximately 150 m away from the controlled cold chamber room. Irradiation was conducted using a Gamma Foss Model 812 self-contained Co-60 irradiator (Foss Therapy Services Inc., California, USA) at the IPCL in Seibersdorf, Austria. Samples were positioned on a 10-cm-high platform constructed from stacked Petri dishes to standardize dose distribution. Prior to irradiation, 10% of the infested fruit were randomly allocated as untreated controls.

The absorbed dose for all the replicates was verified through the Gafchromic dosimetry system (FAO/IAEA 2022) with 95% confidence limits that varied between ± 1.7% and ± 3.91% of uncertainty for different batches of film used for this work. Three pieces of Gafchromic HD-V2 films (1.0 × 1.0 cm each) were individually packed in paper envelopes (2.5 × 2.5 cm, FWT-80, Far West Technologies, Goleta, CA, USA) and then sealed in plastic bags (3.0 × 3.0 cm) to avoid its contact with the juice leaked from the fruit. One plastic bag containing the three packed films was placed on top, and another bag containing 3 films was placed on the bottom of the infested fruit to measure the absorbed dose. A portable densitometer (DoseReader 4, RadGen, Budapest, Hungary) read the dosimeter films. HD-V2 films were read approximately 24 h after gamma radiation exposure, and when read after 24 h the correction factor for time was used according to the Standard Operating Procedures for Gafchromic Film Dosimetry System for Gamma Radiation v1.0 of the FAO/IAEA (FAO/IAEA 2022).

Post-Irradiation Routine

To evaluate female fertility after irradiation, both control and treated fruit were placed in a Petri dish inside a plastic cage (20 × 14 × 20 cm) containing water and a dry artificial diet. The cage was enclosed in a black voile bag (100 × 60 cm) to prevent contamination by Drosophila flies. Five days after adult emergence, non-infested blueberries were introduced into the cages as oviposition resource for sexually mature females. Egg collection was performed three times per week for two weeks, with fruit remaining in cages for 24 h each time. However, due to scheduling constraints, egg collection was conducted for only one week in some blocks. The number of eggs laid by control and treated females was recorded. If no eggs were found, the fruit was discarded. If eggs were present in the fruit, viability (hatching) was assessed daily for three days. These fruits, were placed in an incubator at 25 °C to allow immature development. Once pupariation occurred, fruit-containing puparia were transferred to cages and observed for two weeks to track adult emergence. Evaluation of Radiation-Induced Ovarian Damage in D. suzukii

To evaluate the damage caused by the PI treatment in the female reproductive system, a sample of pupae from blueberries irradiated with nominal doses of 52 and 72 Gy, and their respective controls, were placed in emergence cages until adult emergence. Fourteen-day-old females were killed using the gas mixture of nearly 0% oxygen, 21% carbon dioxide, 1% argon, and 78% nitrogen gas, and immersed in a 0.70% Phosphate-buffered Saline (PBS) solution to make the dissection. The ovary dissection procedures followed Thompson et al. (2015). After removing the reproductive system, the ovaries were transferred to a clean microscope slide for examination and imaging. Ovary images were acquired using a scanning electron microscope (SEM) Zeiss EVO LS15 (Carl Zeiss Microscopy GmbH, 07745 Jena, Germany) equipped with a tungsten filament. Imaging parameters included an electron high tension (EHT) of 20.00 kV, a working distance (WD) of 8.5–10.5 mm, and a magnification of 78×–130×. The samples were not treated prior to the placing on the SEM stubs since the device can work in a variable pressure mode, separating the vacuum of the column from the one of the chamber though a dedicated pinhole; the pressure in the chamber used for collecting these images was 50 Pa. Secondary electron signals were captured using the variable pressure secondary electron detector (VPSE G3). To ensure consistent and accurate imaging, all procedures strictly followed standardized protocols. Damage to individual ovaries, resulting from the combined effects of PI, MA, and cold storage, was assessed by semi-qualitative evaluation by observing the presence or absence of oocytes.

Data Analysis

To evaluate female fecundity (egg collection), a bias-reduced binomial generalized linear model (Firth 1993) was explored via the brglm2 package (Kosmidis et al. 2020). Radiation dose, MA, and cold storage conditions were included as fixed factors. The significance of the fixed effects and their interactions was evaluated using the Type III Wald chi-square. Post-hoc pairwise comparisons of estimated marginal means were conducted for the treatment conditions, applying Bonferroni correction (Holm 1979). All analyses were performed in RStudio (R version 4.3.1), and the following packages were used: lme4 (Bates et al. 2015), brglm2 (Kosmidis et al. 2020), bbmle (Bolker 2025), and emmeans (Lenth 2025) packages.

Dosimetry Routine

Our dosimetry system’s level of uncertainty was 3.91%. The insects used in our experiments were irradiated with dose rates ranging from 40.6 to 43.9 Gy/min−1. Table 2 shows the average, minimum, and maximum absorbed doses.

Effect of MA, Cold Storage, and Irradiation Treatment on the Number of F1 Eggs

The evaluation of ovarian damage showed significant main effects and interactions among irradiation dose, modified atmosphere (MA), and cold storage. Irradiation was the primary factor causing ovarian atrophy, with damage severity increasing with dose. Exposure to a phytosanitary dose of 72 Gy consistently induced the most severe damage across all experimental conditions. The use of MA, particularly severe hypoxia, independently caused ovarian atrophy compared to normoxia. Similarly, cold storage at 0 °C alone resulted in increased atrophy relative to storage at 25 °C.

These factors interacted to modulate the overall level of damage. The 72 Gy treatment caused significantly more ovarian damage than the 52 Gy treatment under every combination of MA and temperature. However, the extent of this dose-dependent effect was significantly influenced by the MA and cold storage conditions. For instance, at 25 °C, the damage effect of 72 Gy was most pronounced in normoxic conditions, while severe hypoxia–CO2 at this dose resulted in marginally less severe damage than other MA treatments. The suboptimal 52 Gy dose showed a wider range of damage outcomes dependent on the environment. At 25 °C, ovaries from D. suzukii irradiated with 52 Gy under severe hypoxia (both CO2 and N2) exhibited less damage than those treated under normoxia or severe hypoxia. This protective effect of severe hypoxia was altered by cold storage. At 0 °C, the severe hypoxia–N2 condition uniquely resulted in more damage with 52 Gy irradiation dose compared to the other MA treatments, indicating a significant three-way interaction (Cold storage, Atm × Cold × Irradiation; Tables 3 and 4). Ultimately, while MA and cold storage modified the damage response, the phytosanitary 72 Gy dose induced complete and irreversible ovarian atrophy that overrode all other conditions (Irradiation; Tables 3 and 4).

Effect of MA, Cold Storage, and Irradiation Treatment on the Number of F1 Adults Emerged

The recovery of F1 adults was primarily affected by irradiation dose, with significant effects imposed by modified atmosphere (MA) and cold storage. Irradiation was the most important factor, as the phytosanitary dose of 72 Gy completely prevented adult emergence across all treatments. The suboptimal 52 Gy dose showed minimal F1 adult recovery, though at drastically reduced numbers of adults emerged compared to non-irradiated controls (Irradiation; Tables 4 and 5). Independently, MA condition also significantly affected fecundity and viability. Among non-irradiated treatments, exposure to severe hypoxia–N2 at 25 °C resulted in the lowest number of F1 adults, indicating a suppressive effect of this atmosphere on reproductive output (Atmospheric conditions; Tables 4 and 5). Cold storage alone generally reduced the number of recovering adults compared to constant 25 °C across comparable treatments (Cold storage; Tables 4 and 5).

The interaction of these factors determined the final outcome. As expected, all 52 Gy treatments yielded more F1 adults than any 72 Gy treatment, where recovery was zero. However, the level of recovery following 52 Gy was contingent upon the specific environmental conditions. The 52 Gy irradiation dose, when combined with cold storage at 0 °C, resulted in lower adult recovered at 25 °C. Discussion

This study evaluated the efficacy of PI for D. suzukii under MA and cold storage conditions. Our results demonstrate that the efficacy of PI against D. suzukii was not affected by modified atmosphere (MA), cold storage, or their combination. This result is evidenced by the complete absence of egg-laying in females irradiated at the nominal dose of 72 Gy under all tested conditions. We discuss the implications of these findings, considering the relative role of each stressor and the practical relevance of our results.

Egg-laying suppression in D. suzukii has been reported in normoxic conditions for nominal doses of 72 Gy (Gomes et al. 2024), 75 Gy (Krüger et al. 2018, Sassù et al. 2019), 80 Gy (Follett et al. 2014), 90 Gy (Lanouette et al. 2017), and 100 Gy (Kim et al. 2018). In hypoxic conditions, D. suzukii females irradiated as pharate adults under 0.3% O2 (measured at the end of a 5-h treatment) and chilling (18 °C) conditions, laid eggs for doses up to 90 Gy when crossed with non-irradiated males in the context of the sterile insect technique (SIT); however, these eggs did not hatch (Sassù et al. 2019). Furthermore, complete sterility of D. suzukii females was induced at 75 Gy in normoxia and 85 Gy in hypoxia (Sassù et al. 2019). In the context of phytosanitary irradiation, no radioprotective effects were observed for D. suzukii late pupae exposed to MAP with oxygen levels ranging from 3.2 to 15.4% O2 combined with a suboptimal radiation dose of 60 Gy in cherries (Follett et al. 2018).

In our study, regardless of cold storage and MA conditions, egg laying was significantly reduced at 52 Gy and entirely suppressed at 72 Gy when females were maintained with their irradiated male counterparts, as expected for phytosanitation. Considering that male fertility can be reduced sixfold when irradiated at 75 Gy (16.3% fertility) compared to non-irradiated males (90.0% fertility) (Krüger et al. 2018), we can assume that complete sterility might be induced at lower radiation doses, as observed in previous studies and ours, due to synergistic effects of crosses between irradiated males and females. The TPPT recommended the prevention of egg laying as the treatment endpoint of an irradiation treatment for D. suzukii (IPPC 2019—TPPT meeting report). Therefore, our findings indicate that this may be achieved even if the treatment is applied under MA and cold storage conditions.

Extensive ovarian damage and atrophy were observed in our study for D. suzukii females irradiated with 52 and 72 Gy under all MA and cold storage conditions. The length and width of D. suzukii ovaries were sharply reduced after pharate pupae irradiation at 75 Gy, leading to complete atrophy of the female reproductive system and its consequent inability to produce eggs (Krüger et al. 2018). The effectiveness of ionizing radiation on inducing severe ovarian damage has been shown for multiple fruit fly species, including Anastrepha fraterculus (Bartolucci et al. 2008, Mastrangelo et al. 2018, Krüger et al. 2021), Anastrepha obliqua (Costa et al. 2020), Anastrepha suspensa (Walder and Calkins 1992), Bactrocera dorsalis (Yusof et al. 2019), Bactrocera zonata (Younes et al. 2009), C. capitata (Williamson et al. 1985, Negm et al. 2022), and Zeugodacus tau (Cai et al. 2018). The ovarian atrophy reported in our study for females irradiated in normoxia, hypoxia, and severe hypoxia at either 25 °C or 0 °C reinforces the robustness of the proposed irradiation treatment with a minimum absorbed dose of 80 Gy to prevent reproduction in D. suzukii (Follett et al. 2014, Gomes et al. 2024).

Ovary size evaluation has been suggested as a method to distinguish between wild and sterile trapped females in the context of SIT for D. suzukii, particularly when trapped insects lack the fluorescent dye used to mark released sterile insects (Walder and Calkins 1992, Bartolucci et al. 2008, Krüger et al. 2018). Considering that the available studies supporting a PI treatment for D. suzukii propose prevention of egg laying as an endpoint (Follett et al. 2014, Gomes et al. 2024), live adults may be found in the traded commodities during the inspection process without being considered a treatment failure. Thus, like for the SIT application, ovary dissection might be used as a tool for inspectors to confirm that the irradiation treatment was properly applied.

Our data also showed that cold storage, whether combined with MA or not, does not reduce the efficacy of the proposed PI dose for D. suzukii. Applied before the irradiation treatment, cold storage at 1 ± 0.5 °C for 20 h did not reduce PI efficacy for R. pomonella (Hallman 2004a). It has been hypothesized, however, that the transcription, translation, and thermal kinetics of the repair enzymes are not favorable under low temperatures, potentially making irradiation treatment more effective under cold storage (Follett and Neven 2020). A synergistic effect of the subsequent application of irradiation treatment and cold storage was reported for Z. cucurbitae (Follett and Snook 2013) and G. molesta (Hofmeyr et al. 2016). For instance, cold storage (4–11 °C) for 11 days after irradiation treatment with 30 Gy significantly reduced survivorship by more than 99% in Z. cucurbitae infesting papayas (Follett and Snook 2013).

Considering that the simultaneous application of MA and cold storage before, during, and after PI application typically involves prolonged exposure periods under commercial conditions, enhanced pest control can be expected (Follett and Neven 2020). Additionally, cold (IPPC 2021b) and modified atmosphere (IPPC 2022) alone can be used as phytosanitary treatments. Solid evidence, including the irradiation of several thousand insects, supports the use of an irradiation treatment with the minimum absorbed dose of 80 Gy to completely disrupt D. suzukii reproduction (Follett et al. 2014, Gomes et al. 2024). Based on additional information from our study, it is evident that the recommended PI dose for D. suzukii is efficacious enough to override any potential protective effect induced by MA, particularly under cold storage. Thus, restrictions limiting PI application under MA for D. suzukii are not needed, as MA did not reduce PI efficacy using either gas flushing (Chen et al. 2023 and this study) or MAP systems (Follett et al. 2018). The commercial application of MA, cold storage, and PI may be used to maintain the quality of fresh produce while ensuring quarantine security against D. suzukii.
