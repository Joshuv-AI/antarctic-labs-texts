Cranial Base Characteristics in Growing Patients With Class III Skeletal Pattern: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis
Authors: Zhuoying Li, Junqi Liu, Tingting Wei, Kuo Feng Hung, Yuxiong Su, Zhiyi Shan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroduction and aimsThe treatment of skeletal Class III malocclusion presents challenges due to heterogeneous craniofacial growth patterns. The cranial base is a craniofacial growth centre, while its role in skeletal malocclusion remains unclear. This systematic review aimed to investigate the relationship between the cranial base characteristics and skeletal Class III malocclusion in growing patients.MethodsObservational studies investigating cranial base characteristics among growing skeletal Class III patients were included. Six databases (PubMed, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, Embase, MEDLINE, and Scopus) were searched up to May 5, 2026. Risk of bias and evidence certainty were assessed using the AXIS tool, and the GRADE framework. Meta-analysis was performed using RevMan 5.4 software. Subgroup meta-analyses were conducted by growth stage and gender if applicable.ResultsEleven studies were included, of which eight were eligible for meta-analysis. Compared to Class I patients, Class III patients exhibited a smaller NSBa angle (−1.84°, 95% CI: −2.80°, −0.89°, P < .001) and NSAr angle (−2.02°, 95% CI: −3.26°, −0.78°, P = .001). Subgroup analyses revealed shorter SN and SBa lengths among Class III patients before or during the growth spurt (SN: −1.59 mm, 95% CI: −2.69 mm, −0.50 mm, P = .004; SBa: −1.56 mm, 95% CI: −2.88, −0.24, P = .004). Additionally, SN length was significantly shorter in Class III males (−1.68 mm, 95% CI: −3.11mm, −0.25 mm, P = .020).ConclusionsGrowing skeletal Class III patients presented significantly smaller cranial base angles. Moreover, those evaluated before or during the growth spurt showed shorter anterior and posterior cranial base lengths, with differences in anterior cranial base length demonstrating gender-specific variation. However, the preliminary findings necessitate cautious interpretation given the very low certainty of evidence.Clinical relevanceA better understanding of the etiology of skeletal malocclusion may inform individualized orthodontic diagnosis and treatment planning.Keywords: Skeletal Class III malocclusion, Cranial base, Growth, Radiographic imaging, Meta-analysis

Introduction

Skeletal Class III malocclusion is a sagittal skeletal discrepancy characterized by maxillary retrusion and/or mandibular prognathism. Its prevalence varies across populations, affecting 0.48% to 4% of Caucasians and 13% to 19% of Asians.1,2 Clinically, patients with skeletal Class III malocclusion display a concave facial type, and often experience impaired oral health, unfavourable facial aesthetics, compromised psychosocial well-being, and deteriorated oral health-related quality of life.3,4 In selected cases, timely intervention during the growth period, such as early orthodontic or orthopaedic treatment, can mitigate the necessity of orthognathic surgery in adulthood.5 Unfortunately, decision-making on early treatment of Class III patients can be challenging due to unpredictable individual responses, high compliance demands, growth-related relapse, and irreversible consequences of tooth extractions. Considering most of these difficulties arise from the variability in craniofacial growth, understanding craniofacial growth patterns is essential in selecting the appropriate candidate and determining the intervention timing in growing patients with skeletal Class III malocclusion.6

Cranial base, forming the interface between the neurocranium and the facial skeleton, has been regarded as the ‘engine’ of the craniofacial complex, playing a pivotal role in determining facial growth patterns.7,8 Externally, it articulates with the maxilla via sutures and with the mandible through the glenoid fossa of the temporomandibular joint (TMJ), whereas internally, the cranial cavity is formed by the fusion of the occipital, sphenoid, temporal, frontal, and ethmoid bone.9 Postnatal cranial base growth is driven primarily by specialized cartilaginous growth centres, known as synchondroses, in coordination with the growth of the surrounding facial skeleton.10, 11, 12

Among these growth centres, the spheno-occipital synchondrosis (SOS) is of particular clinical interest. Its timing of growth and fusion influences the position of the sella turcica and the morphology of the sphenoid sinus, both of which are anatomically connected with nasomaxillary and mandibular changes during adolescence.13,14 Premature or delayed closure of SOS may lead to midfacial hypoplasia, as observed in various types of craniosynostosis syndromes with different genetic bases, including mutations in the Fibroblast Growth Factor Receptor genes (FGFR1-3).14,15 Previous studies have indicated that the fusion at the SOS starts superiorly and extends inferiorly, occurring as early as 8 to 11 years old and essentially completed by the age of 17 in both sexes, while a fusion scar was found to persist by 25 years of age.16,17 Given the close relationship between SOS maturation and craniofacial growth, cranial base morphology may serve as a valuable indicator for identifying and potentially predicting facial growth patterns in adolescents.

In growing Class III patients, reliable predictors for early orthodontic treatment outcomes are still limited. Targeting the cranial base, particularly those related to the mechanisms of craniofacial growth pattern, is therefore a critical strategy for improving clinical decision-making. Previous studies have evaluated the relationship between cranial base morphology and facial pattern, however, the results remains controversial. Kucukkaraca et al18 observed a smaller cranial base angle in prepubertal and pubertal patients with skeletal Class III malocclusion, but other studies failed to show the significant difference between facial patterns.19, 20, 21, 22 Additionally, a shorter posterior cranial base length in growing patients during the growth peak with skeletal Class III malocclusion was observed in a study,22 while another study did not find the reduction, but did find the difference in the cranial base inclinations in patients after the growth peak.21

Previous reviews have assessed the cranial base dimensions in skeletal Class III malocclusion; however, they did not specifically focus on growing patients or comprehensively evaluated distinct cranial base structures such as the sella turcica and SOS.23,24 Evidence-based guidance for early orthodontic diagnosis and treatment planning in skeletal Class III adolescents remains limited. Therefore, this systematic review aims to qualitatively and quantitatively synthesize current evidence on cranial base characteristics in skeletal Class III patients, including overall cranial base dimensions and specific structural features to clarify their association between cranial base characteristics and skeletal Class III malocclusion in growing individuals.

Methods

Protocol and registration

The protocol of the systematic review was registered in the Prospective International Registration of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO; No. CRD42024560213). The review was conducted and reported according to the checklist of Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA).25

Eligibility criteria

The eligibility criteria were designed according to the PECOS (Participants, Exposure, Comparison, Outcome, Study design) strategy.

Inclusion criteria: (1) Participants: non-syndromic growing participants under 19 years old with skeletal Class III malocclusion; (2) Exposure: diagnosis of skeletal Class III malocclusion using recognized skeletal diagnostic criteria; (3) Comparison: individuals with skeletal Class I malocclusion; (4) Outcome: the measurements on radiographic images of the cranial base regarding its dimension and density were the main outcome. The characteristics of specific anatomical structures including sella turcica, sphenoid sinus, glenoid fossa and SOS were the secondary outcomes; (5) Study design: observational studies.

Exclusion criteria: (1) subjects with cleft lip and palate, temporomandibular disorders (TMD), systemic diseases, a history of orthodontic or orthopaedic treatment, trauma, orthognathic surgery, or other jaw reconstructive procedures; (2) studies only relying on the diagnosis of dental relationship; (3) studies lacking a skeletal Class I control group; (4) animal experiment, in-vitro experiment, review articles, case reports, case series, and letters, as well as studies only available in non-English versions.

Information sources and search strategy

The search procedure was carried out independently by two review examiners (Z.L. and J.L.) with no restrictions on the date published. Six electronic databases (PubMed, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, Embase via Ovid, MEDLINE via Ovid, and Scopus) were systematically searched for eligible studies based on a combination of three key concepts – cranial base, malocclusion, and radiography – with their synonyms using Boolean operators. The search strategy was further refined using MeSH and Emtree terms. The detailed search strategies for each database were listed in the supplementary material (Tables S1-S6). In addition, the reference lists of relevant reviews and included articles were hand-searched to avoid omission. The pooled results were imported to EndNote 21 software (Clarivate Analytics, Philadelphia, PA, USA) for duplicate removal and screening. The initial search was performed on 30 July 2024 and last updated on 5 May 2026.

Study screening and data extraction

The studies were screened based on titles and abstracts, followed by the full text by the two examiners independently. Inter-rater agreement was evaluated using Cohen’s kappa statistic. Any disagreements were resolved by discussion with the third examiner (Z.S.). Studies that fulfilled the eligibility criteria were included for data extraction, which include author names, publication year, the country, ethnicity, age, gender and sample size of participants, image type, diagnosis criteria for malocclusion, and outcome measurements.

Risk of bias assessment

The risk of bias was evaluated using the Appraisal tool for Cross-Sectional Studies (AXIS tool) (Table S7).26 Developed based on a combination of reporting quality, study design quality and the risk of bias, the AXIS tool comprises 20 items. Each item was rated as ‘yes’, ‘no’, or ‘don’t know’. One point was awarded for each ‘yes’, except for items 13 and 19, where a point was awarded for a ‘no’ response. Based on the six risk-of-bias core items (Q6, Q7, Q9, Q13, Q14, Q15), studies were categorized as having low risk of bias (≥5 points), moderate risk of bias (3-4 points), or high risk of bias (≤2 points).The assessment was carried out independently by two review authors (Z.L. and J.L.). Cohen’s weighted kappa statistic was used to assess the reliability between two reviewers. Any disagreements were resolved by discussion until consensus was reached for the final analysis.

Meta-analysis

Studies with the same outcome measurements were involved in a quantitative synthesis using RevMan 5.4 software. For continuous variables with a normal distribution, pooled results were expressed as the mean difference (MD) with a 95% confidence interval (CI). For categorical variables, the odds ratio (OR) with a 95% CI was used. Heterogeneity among the studies was assessed using Tau2 statistic, Cochran’s Q test, and I2 index. The statistical significance of pooled estimates was determined by Z-test at the significant level of P < .05.

In accordance with the Cochrane Handbook, the non-reporting bias would be conducted only when at least ten studies were available for a given outcome, due to the limited reliability of these methods with smaller samples.

According to the Cochrane Handbook,27 for pooled outcomes with I2 statistic exceeding 30% and sufficient data, subgroup meta-analyses were performed based on participants’ growth stage and gender, as these factors were considered critically related to growth. The growth stage was primarily classified according to either the cervical vertebral maturation (CVM) stages or hand–wrist films, depending on the method used in each included study. CVM stages 1-4 were defined as ‘before or during the growth spurt’, while stages 5-6 were defined as ‘after the growth spurt’.28 For studies using hand–wrist radiographs, the classification followed the definition of the original study. In studies where no growth phase definition was reported, we assigned patients to CVM-based growth stages using chronological age cut-offs established from previous studies.29

In the meta-analysis, studies with questionable statistical methods or estimates would be included in the sensitivity analysis to assess the robustness of the primary findings. This involved recalculating the aggregate estimates with the specific studies re-excluded or re-included, then comparing these recalculations with the original meta-analysis. The changes in MD, 95% CI, I², and P value for the Z test and Q test for heterogeneity in all sensitivity analyses would be clearly documented.

The Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluations (GRADE) tool was used to evaluate and rank the outcome evidence certainty of included records and meta-analysis based on five domains: risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias.30

Results

Study selection

The initial search yielded 5016 records from all databases. After removing 2830 duplicates, 2113 were excluded following title and abstract screening, with a high level of inter-reviewer agreement (κ = 0.821). The remaining 73 records underwent full-text assessment, resulting in the exclusion of 62 articles (κ = 0.833) for the following reasons: 25 did not focus on growing patients, 6 lacked the skeletal criteria for diagnosis of malocclusion, 12 did not report outcomes for growing patients with skeletal malocclusion, and 19 were not available of full-text in English. A manual screen of the reference lists from included studies and relevant reviews23,24 did not identify any additional eligible reports. Ultimately, 11 records were included in this systematic review. Of these, eight studies were included in the quantitative synthesis (meta-analysis). The study selection process is detailed in the PRISMA flowchart (Figure 1).

Characteristics of included studies

The characteristics of included studies, including participants, skeletal malocclusions with diagnostic criteria, and outcome measurements were summarized in Table 1. These studies were published between 1989 and 2023. Sample sizes ranged from 60 to 262 participants, with ages varying from 6 to 19 years. All studies included both males and females with varying sex ratios, except for one that included only female subjects20; four studies did not clearly report gender distribution.19,22,31,32 According to the criteria for classifying growing patients,29 six records focused on patients before or during the growth spurt,19,22,31, 32, 33, 34 three on those after the growth spurt,18,20 and two on patients both before/during and after the growth spurt.18,35 Most studies analysed lateral cephalograms (LCs), with only one study focusing on the cone beam computed tomography (CBCT) scans. The majority applied the ANB angle or Wits appraisal as diagnosis criteria for skeletal malocclusion, while two studies used other angular measurement. Regarding outcome variables, five studies reported the angular and linear measurement of the cranial base based on cephalometric analysis,18,20, 21, 22, 23 three compared the characteristics of sella turcica,31,34,36 two analysed the glenoid fossa,32,33 and one evaluated the fusion pattern of the SOS.35

Risk of bias assessment

The AXIS assessment revealed that four records were evaluated as moderate risk, while the remaining seven records were judged as high risk of bias (Table 1, Table S8), primarily due to the concerns on sampling clarity, and non-response bias. The agreement between the two authors is satisfying (κ = 0.792).

Results of meta-analysis

Five studies on the cranial base measurement18,20, 21, 22 and three studies on the characteristics of sella turcica31,34,36 were included in the meta-analysis. Given the heterogeneity in populations across the included studies, a random-effects model was employed for all meta-analysis.

Figure 2 presented the results of meta-analysis on the cranial base measurement, including the NSBa angle, NSAr angle, the length of SN and SBa. For the NSBa angle, the pooled mean difference (MD) between the Class III and Class I groups was −1.84° (95% CI: −2.80° to −0.89°, P = .0002, I² = 3%). Regarding the NSAr angle, the MD between two groups was −2.02° (95% CI: −3.26° to −0.78°, P = .001, I² = 14%). For the SN length, the MD between groups was −0.94 mm (95% CI: −2.27 mm to 0.38 mm, P = .16, I² = 49%). For the SBa length, the MD between groups was −0.81 mm (95% CI: −2.03 mm to −0.40 mm, P = .19, I² = 62%). For sella turcica dimensions, the pooled MDs ranged from 0.14 mm to 0.50 mm (95% CI: −0.45 mm to 1.09 mm; all P > .05), with heterogeneity from low to moderate (I² = 0%-27%; Figure 3). For the sella morphology, the pooled odds ratio (OR) of non-normal sellar shape between groups was 2.73 (95% CI: 0.56-13.22, P = .21), with high heterogeneity (I² = 84%; Figure 3).

In one study,18 the NSAr angle and the SN length were analysed using the non-parametric test, and the authors failed to provide sufficient raw statistics for normality or skewness assessment. Consequently, these two variables were excluded from the primary meta-analysis.

Non-reporting bias was not assessed as the numbers of included records was insufficient to support a reliable funnel plot or more advanced regression-based assessments.

Given the heterogeneity in the outcome variable of the SN and SBa length, the subgroup analysis regarding age and gender was performed (Figures S1 and S2). The significant differences were only found in the SN and the SBa among patients before or during the growth spurt (SN: MD: −1.59 mm, 95% CI: −2.69 mm to −0.50 mm, P = .004, I² = 0%; SBa: MD: −1.56 mm, 95% CI: −2.88 mm to −0.24 mm, P = .02, I² = 46%) and the SN in males (MD: −1.68 mm 95% CI: −3.11 mm to −0.25 mm, P = .02, I² = 0%).

As for the two excluded variables in one study due to the questionable distribution characteristics,18 sensitivity analyses were performed by re-incorporating the study. First, for the NSAr angle, the pooled MD, 95% CI, P value and I² remained stable, and the direction of the effects did not change, indicating that the single study did not significantly affect the results (Table S9). Second, for the SN length, the sensitivity analysis led to the decreased heterogeneity (I² = 49%-32%) and the altered pooled MD of −1.12 mm (95% CI: −2.11 mm to −0.12 mm, P = .030). This shift, from non-significant in the primary meta-analysis to statistically significant, demonstrated that the primary conclusion was not robust and critically contingent upon the questionable normality assumption of the excluded study (Table S9).

Since a study contributed an outlier standard deviation (SD), which was visualized via forest plot inspection,19 we repeated the analyses after excluding the study. The results showed that the pooled estimates remained stable, demonstrating the robustness of the pooled records (Table S9).

Discussion

The inherent variability of craniofacial growth patterns presents a significant challenge in the treatment of skeletal Class III malocclusion. This systematic review, therefore, synthesized existing evidence concerning the relationship between skeletal Class III malocclusion and cranial base characteristics in growing patients, comparing cranial base angle, cranial base length, sella turcica, SOS and the glenoid fossa characteristics between skeletal Class III and Class I growing patients. These group-level comparisons provide valuable insights into the underlying etiology of skeletal malocclusion. The pooled estimates generated from this meta-analysis may serve as reference ranges for future diagnostic test studies aimed at identifying Class III patterns, which may ultimately contribute to individualized orthodontic diagnosis and treatment planning.

In the meta-analysis, the cranial base angle, measured by the NSBa and the NSAr angle, was found to be significantly sharper in Class III patients with low heterogeneity, which was consistent with the findings in the previous review.23 According to Björk and Weidenreich, a reduced NSBa angle could be one of the causes for mandibular prognathism.37,38 A longitudinal study showed that a sharper NSBa angle resulted in a forward displacement of the foramen magnum, leading to an anterior position of the glenoid fossa due to the requirement for the natural balance of the head on the spinal column.37 As one of the components of TMJ on the external surface of the cranial base, the anteriorly-displaced glenoid fossa causes the protrusion of the mandibular condyle by affecting both its bodily displacement and growth direction.37,39 The anterior position of the condyle directly resulted in the forward displacement of point Ar, causing a sharper NSAr angle in skeletal Class III patients. These findings suggest that a sharper cranial base angle may clinically assist in predicting a more pronounced mandibular prognathic growth pattern and may indicate a less favourable orthopaedic treatment outcome in growing patients. However, compared with the result in Gong et al’s review,23 the estimated mean difference in the NSBa angle (MD: −1.84°, 95% CI: −2.80° to −0.89°) was less negative, whereas the NSAr angle (MD: −2.02°, 95% CI: −3.26° to −0.78°) was more negative in this meta-analysis. Ursi et al40 previously observed that the NSBa angle experienced a gradual reduction during the growth period.41 The difference in this angle between Class III and Class I might become larger with the cranial base growing, and thus it is less negative in our meta-analysis focusing on growing patients rather than adults. As for the NSAr angle, it is more likely affected by the skeletal pattern, especially the mandibular protrusion, which leads to a more pronounced difference in our study emphasizing the skeletal malocclusion. In general, our model demonstrated satisfying robustness in these two angles, since the results mostly remained unchanged irrespective of the inclusion of the questionable studies (Table S9).

Although Björk observed that the decreasing anterior and posterior length of the cranial base, measured by the SN and SBa length, can help to predict the skeletal Class III malocclusion,37 no significant difference was found in the length between Class III and Class I patients in this meta-analysis. However, the result of SN length should be interpreted with caution due to the inconsistency with or without including the questionable data as shown in the sensitivity analysis (Table S9). Currently, insufficient evidence exists to determine whether there is a difference in the SN length in growing patients between groups. Similarly, no significant difference in the SBa length was found, which agreed with the previous review.23 Nevertheless, moderate to substantial heterogeneity was observed in these pooled outcomes, which may be attributed to variations in diagnostic criteria for Class III malocclusion (Table 1), growth stage assessment, and the origins and gender distribution of the study populations.

Interestingly, the subgroup meta-analysis revealed a shorter anterior and posterior cranial base length in Class III patients before or during the growth spurt, despite the lack of difference in the whole group of growing patients (Figures S1 and S2). According to the previous longitudinal studies, the anterior and posterior cranial base length was observed to reach its completion at different velocities across time, and its growth spurt coincides with the general physical growth spurt.17,42,43 Thus, the difference in younger patients instead of those post-growth spurt could be attributed to the elevated growth velocity of cranial base length, rather than the growth increment in Class III growing patients. Besides, Henneberke et al43 found that boys showed a more pronounced growth spurt for SN length than girls, which could explain our findings that a shorter anterior cranial base length in Class III patients in males rather than females (Figure S1). As for the length of posterior cranial base, its increase is driven by the growth activity of SOS, the growth centre of the cranial base.43 Yang et al35 investigated the time and fusion pattern of SOS in Class III and Class I patients and observed no significant difference between the two groups. Nevertheless, they found that the mean age when the osseous cores appeared within the SOS, tended to be earlier in the Class III groups, suggesting a condensed growth period of SOS which ultimately resulted in a shorter SBa length as shown in our study. In this context, for Class III patients treated during the active maxillary growth period (ie, before or during the growth spurt), a shorter cranial base length may serve as a predictor of maxillary retrognathism and indicate the necessity of maxillary protraction therapy, consistent with recent evidence emphasizing the role of appropriate early intervention in children, alongside considerations of treatment sequencing.44,45 It should be noted, however, that the exploratory findings in the subgroup analysis need to be interpreted with caution due to the limited studies in the subgroup.

Affected by the growth activity of SOS in the posterior cranial base, dimensional changes of sella turcia were found until 25 years old, with varied growth patterns in the length, depth and diameter.46,47 The relationship between sella turcica dimensions and morphology and the facial pattern in adults has been investigated in various studies.11,48 However, there is currently a lack of evidence regarding the relationship between sella turcica characteristics and facial patterns in growing patients, which could be attributed to the limited accuracy of sellar dimension measurements on the LCs and the restricted availability of CBCT images in adolescents due to radiation concerns. Moreover, the limited number of studies and age variations among study populations may also lead to the considerable heterogeneity in sella morphology.

Some concerns of the present study should be considered and require further improvement in the future. Firstly, the evidence was assessed as very low certainty by GRADE, due to the cross-sectional study designs, risk of bias, and the limited number of included studies. Most included studies had limitations in sampling clarity and non-response bias. A constrained number of studies also raises concerns about the robustness of some pooled estimates, which were reflected in the results of the sensitivity analyses. Future high-quality longitudinal studies are necessary to increase the certainty of evidence and enable robust comparative analyses. Secondly, subgroup analyses were limited by incomplete original data. For gender-specific analyses, several studies could not be incorporated due to the absence of gender-stratified data. For growth stage analyses, some studies provided only chronological age rather than skeletal maturity indicators, which may have reduced the precision of growth stage classification. Thirdly, this review was restricted to predominantly Caucasian populations from the available studies, leading to limited generalizability of the findings. Therefore, future studies across different ethnic populations are needed to validate these findings.

Conclusion

This systematic review and meta-analysis highlighted differences in cranial base characteristics, including cranial base dimensions, sella turcica dimensions and morphology, timing and fusion pattern of SOS, and the position of the glenoid fossa, between growing patients with skeletal Class III malocclusion and skeletal Class I malocclusion. The analysis demonstrated sharper cranial base angles (NSBa and NSAr), in Class III patients, while no significant relationship was observed between the anterior/posterior cranial base length and facial pattern in the full sample of growing patients. However, subgroup analysis revealed that shorter anterior and posterior cranial base lengths (SN and SBa) were presented in Class III individuals before or during the growth spurt. Currently, evidence remains insufficient to support a significant relationship between sella turcica characteristics and skeletal malocclusion. Limited by the very low certainty of evidence, the pooled results should be interpreted with caution. Future high-quality longitudinal studies are anticipated to improve the evidence strength.
