Severity Prediction of Traumatic Cervical Spinal Cord Injury With an AI Model Based on MRI Radiomics
Authors: Chunshuai Wu, Chaochen Li, Guanhua Xu, Jiajia Chen, Liangliang Wang, Haiyan Gu, Jinlong Zhang, Hongxiang Hong, Chunyan Ji, Zhiming Cui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundTraumatic cervical spinal cord injury (TCSCI) often leads to significant patient paralysis. Current clinical diagnosis relies heavily on empirical interpretation of magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and the American Spinal Injury Association Impairment Scale (AIS) grade, lacking robust quantitative markers to precisely reflect injury severity. This study aimed to build an artificial intelligence (AI) pipeline for AIS grade prediction based on radiomic features extracted from manually defined regions.MethodsWe included 189 patients with TCSCI who underwent MRI within 48 h post‐injury. MRI images from 130 patients were used for developing an AI model encompassing image segmentation. Radiomic features were extracted from manually delineated volumes of interest (VOIs). T2‐weighted imaging (T2WI) sagittal images were randomly divided into training (n = 104), validation (n = 13), and test (n = 13) sets for segmentation. A total of 183 patients (excluding AIS E) were included in the AIS grade prediction task. Model performance was evaluated using mean dice similarity coefficient (mDICE), mean intersection over union (mIOU), mean specificity, and mean sensitivity.ResultsAn optimized UCTransnet network, leveraging a Transformer architecture for formal training, combined with a U‐Net++ network for pretraining, achieved promising results in segmenting the spinal cord injury site on T2WI sagittal images (mDICE: 0.777 ± 0.021, mIOU: 0.646 ± 0.025, mean specificity: 0.998 ± 0.001, mean sensitivity: 0.895 ± 0.015). Subsequently, an ensemble model (we named Em‐En) constructed using selected radiomic features from the manual VOIs demonstrated superior performance for predicting AIS grades in terms of sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, and clinical decision‐making benefit compared to other tested models.ConclusionsThis study presents an AI‐assisted pipeline for predicting the severity of TCSCI. The developed resources provide a theoretical foundation for the clinical application of AI‐assisted diagnostic methods, potentially lowering the interpretation barrier for MRI and offering clinicians preliminary quantitative indicators of injury severity. The source code is publicly available.

Introduction

Global epidemiological reports indicate an incidence rate of traumatic cervical spinal cord injury (TCSCI) of approximately 10.5 per 100 000, with patient mortality rates as high as 60%. TCSCI damages neurological function, leading to limb paralysis, loss of normal living capacity, and substantial economic burdens on families and society [1]. A critical aspect of the clinical diagnosis and treatment of TCSCI is the timely and accurate assessment of the severity of neurological functional damage to identify effective treatment targets and formulate rapid intervention plans [2]. Current diagnosis primarily relies on the American Spinal Injury Association (ASIA) Impairment Scale (AIS) grade and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) [3], both of which require extensive clinical experience. Treatment often involves surgical decompression of the injured spinal segment [4], but predicting functional recovery and individual patient prognosis remains challenging [5, 6].

Conventional MRI is the current gold standard for visually assessing tissue injury, compression, and the internal structure of the spinal cord [7, 8]. Some studies have shown that MRI may be a useful prognostic tool [9], with features like hemorrhage and canal diameter at the compression site potentially serving as predictors of neurological outcome [10, 11]. However, other studies indicate MRI primarily reveals the initial trauma site rather than the driving factor of injury progression [12]. Advanced quantitative MRI techniques (e.g., diffusion imaging, relaxation mapping) can improve the detection and quantification of microscopic structural pathology at and beyond the injury site [13]. Nevertheless, accurate diagnosis by surgeons using MRI still largely depends on accumulated experience [14, 15], highlighting the need for advanced, objective MRI feature identification technologies in clinical practice.

The AIS grade, derived from the International Standards for Neurological Classification of Spinal Cord Injury (ISNCSCI) exam, classifies injury severity based on standardized motor and sensory function assessments [16, 17]. The AIS grade is one component of a comprehensive assessment that also includes the neurological level of injury (NLI). The ISNCSCI exam provides a full characterization, with the AIS grade specifically reflecting impairment severity [18]. Furthermore, it lacks agreed‐upon thresholds for surgical intervention, and the neurological assessment is inherently subjective, conducted independently of radiological data.

With advancements in medical imaging technology and computer technology, artificial intelligence (AI)‐assisted medical image analysis has become indispensable for clinical disease diagnosis and treatment [19]. In recent years, deep learning (DL), particularly deep convolutional neural networks (CNNs), has rapidly emerged as a research hotspot in medical image analysis [20]. CNNs are a multistage, trainable neural network capable of automatically identifying numerous diagnostic features from medical imaging datasets. With sufficient data, DL models can learn abstract, essential features from minimally preprocessed data and achieve stable performance [21]. AI excels at feature extraction during image segmentation, achieving significant success in segmenting medical images (e.g., MRI, CT, and x‐ray) and aiding diagnosis in recent years. Several studies have begun exploring AI for spinal cord injury analysis, including automated segmentation [7] and outcome prediction [22], demonstrating the potential of these approaches.

This study developed an optimized UCTransNet network for the semantic segmentation of patient MRI images. Imaging features were then extracted from manually defined volumes of interest (VOIs) using PyRadiomics. An ensemble model was developed to predict AIS grades based on these radiomic features. This AI‐assisted approach aims to explore automated target area delineation and establish links between MRI characteristics and AIS grades.

Materials and Methods

Patients and Group

This study was approved by the ethics committee of Nantong First People's Hospital (2022KT251). Trial registration: ClinicalTrials.gov, NCT06436716. Informed consent was obtained from all participants. We collected MRI data from patients within 48 h after TCSCI at Nantong First People's Hospital between May 2016 and April 2025. Inclusion criteria are as follows: (1) Diagnosis of TCSCI confirmed by MRI examination or by surgery with clinic signs of TCSCI, (2) the injury site is cervical spinal cord, (3) MRI images of T2‐weighted image (T2WI) within 48 h after injury, (4) complete and available imaging data, clinical data and transcriptome data, including MRI, sex, and age. The exclusion criteria are as follows: (1) Spinal cord concussion with no obvious positive findings indicative of spinal cord structural injury on MRI scans, (2) poor MRI quality or severe motion artifacts. Initial screening identified 189 patients (Table 1). A total of 183 patients (excluding AIS E) were assessed for eligibility (see CONSORT diagram in Figure S1). For the image segmentation task, high‐quality manual VOIs were available for 130 of these patients. The AIS grades were determined by trained clinicians following the ISNCSCI protocol during the acute phase (within 48 h of injury).

MRI Protocol and Lesion Area Delineation

The conversion of the original MRI image format DICOM to Nii was based on Python (Version: 3.10.6), and those would be loaded into the MRIcroGL software (Version: 12.2). Three spinal surgeons (with 5, 8, and 18 years of experience in interpreting spinal MRI, respectively) manually depict the region of interest (ROI) of the lesion area layer by layer, to form three‐dimensional (3D) VOI. After the primary spinal surgeon finished depicting the injured spinal cord, the senior spinal surgeon checked the quality of ROI and made some adjustments. Retrospective review indicated adjustments were made in approximately 15% of cases.

Neural Network Architecture for Segmentation

In the task of medical image segmentation, the U‐Net backbone was proven to be an effective solution in common use [23]. In the traditional U‐Net model, the single connection between encoder and decoder may cause the loss of some important features [24]. The optimized U‐Net model (such as U‐Net++) has few of the shallower features about semantic information, which may damage the final performance due to the semantic gap between the shallower encoder and decoder, resulting from their simply skipping the connection [25]. Transformer backbone, originated from natural language processing (NLP), has excellent global connectivity, which we based on to optimize U‐Net architecture in this study [26]. In Python (version: 3.8.15), we constructed UCTransNet based on the PyTorch Framework (version: 1.11.0), and trained it for the semantic segmentation of injured spinal cord in MRI images. The network input is PNG image of each layer of T2WI. After N4 bias field correction and Z‐score standardization, we resample the input image to a 224 × 224 × 3 size to provide consistent input for the attention neural network.

The attention neural network is composed of four layers, each of which has three modules. The first module has a convolution layer with a core size of 33 and a step size of 1. The second module is batch normalization and a corrected linear unit. The third module is the channel‐wise cross‐fusion transformer (CCT) between the vanilla U‐Net encoder and decoder. In CCT, there are four tokens (Ti) for search and one concatenated token (Tc) for integration. The keys and values of Multi‐head cross‐attention include Qi = TiWQi, K = TcWk, and V = TcWv, where WQi, Wk, and Wv are the weights of different inputs. The location of the injury in the spinal cord accounts for a small part of the MRI image, and the size of the lesion area is not consistent between different patients, there is an imbalance between the front and back scenes and the segmentation content. To better guide the feature extraction and information filtering of the network, we have determined the final BCE‐DICE loss function (BDLF) based on binary cross entropy loss (BCE) and dice similarity coefficient (DICE): BDLp,t=1/n∑iwiti−1×logpi−1+s−ti×logpi+s+1−2×ti×pi+sti2+pi2+s where p is the predicted value of the model, t is the tag value, w is the readjustment weight of the loss of each batch element, where the value is [0.5, 0.5], i is the batch element, n is the number of samples, and s is the smooth value (here is 1e−5).

Pretraining and Transfer Learning for Target Area Segmentation

In the first round of pretraining, we used the U‐Net++ network for its superiority in retaining information. We obtained the pretraining network from the public data set MoNuSeg Dataset (https://github.com/McGregorWwww/UCTransNet.git), and transferred the parameters to the U‐Net++ network, in which the input was the full‐slice T2WI images (13 layers) of TCSCI patients in the training set group. To obtain better migration parameters for subsequent training, we input the full‐slice image of T2WI, including some layers without VOIs. The artificial action of increasing noise may be beneficial to the robustness of the network.

To mitigate potential limitations of U‐Net++, we transferred the parameters from the pretraining model to the UCTransnet network. For formal training, the input was restricted to T2WI slices containing manually defined VOIs to reduce noise and enhance sensitivity. Formal training was conducted with the following hyperparameters: batch size = 28, learning rate = 1e−4, embedding dropout rate = 0.1, and attachment dropout rate = 0.1. These hyperparameters were selected based on common practices for similar architectures and preliminary experiments; extensive tuning was not performed due to computational constraints. Performance was evaluated using mean dice similarity coefficient (mDICE), mean intersection over union (mIOU), mSpecificity, and mSensitivity to evaluate the network performance. The value calculation formula is as follows: mDICEp,t=1n×∑is+2×∑pi×ti/s+∑pi+∑ti mIOUp,t=1n×∑is+2×∑pi×ti/2×s+∑pi+∑ti−1 mSpecificityp,t=1n×∑i∑pi−1×ti−1∑ti−pi+1/2+∑pi−1×ti−1+s mSensitivityp,t=1n×∑i∑pi×ti/∑ti+s where p is the prediction target, t is real target, i is the a single sample, n is the number of samples, s is the smooth value (here is 1e−5). Our research is implemented in Python 3.8.15 and runs on a Ryzen 5800X CPU, RTX3090 GPU, and 128GB memory system.

Feature Extraction and Consistency Analysis

Imaging features were extracted from the manually defined VOIs of each patient using PyRadiomics. The open source package (Pyradiomics, https://pypi.org/project/pyradiomics/) was used to extract imaging features from the VOIs of each TCSCI patient. To obtain isotropic voxels, VOIs were resampled to 3 × 3 mm. N4 bias field correction and intensity normalization (0–600 range) were applied to reduce imaging differences. Extracted features included first‐order statistics, shape‐based, gray level co‐occurrence matrix (GLCM), gray level dependence matrix (GLDM), gray level size zone matrix (GLSZM), gray level run length matrix (GLRLM), and neighboring gray tone difference matrix (NGTDM) features (854 total) (Table S1). Features were z‐score normalized. To ensure reproducibility and mitigate segmentation subjectivity, the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was calculated for each feature across annotators; 235 features with ICC > 0.70 were retained (Tables S2 and S3).

Radiomic Features Selection for AIS Prediction Models

AIS grades have four class labels (A–D) in our study, constituting a multi‐classification problem. We first used the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression (L1 regularization) for feature selection, resulting in 88 features. Models including Random Forest (RF), XGBoost, LightGBM (LGBT), support vector machine (SVM), and multinomial logistic regression (MLR) were constructed using fivefold cross‐validation. However, these models performed poorly in direct four‐class prediction.

Therefore, a stratified embedded feature selection approach was adopted using L1‐regularized logistic regression combined with the simulated annealing (SA) algorithm [27] to avoid local optima. First (Tier 1), features distinguishing AIS‐high (Grades A–C) from AIS‐low (Grade D) were selected (51 features) (Table S4). Second (Tier 2), within the AIS‐high group, features distinguishing Grades A–C were selected (50, 26, and 18 features, respectively) (Tables [Link], [Link], [Link]). In total, 221 unique strong features were identified (Table S8). SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) values [28] were calculated to evaluate feature impact.

Construction of an Ensemble Model for AIS Prediction

An ensemble model (Em‐En) was constructed. First, a binary logistic regression model (Em‐2Class) using the 51 Tier 1 features predicted AIS‐high versus AIS‐low; “AIS‐low” predictions were assigned as AIS D. For samples predicted as AIS‐high, three separate models (Em‐C, Em‐B, and a bagged ensemble Em‐A) were developed to predict Grades C, B, and A within the AIS‐high group using their respective Tier 2 features. The bagged Em‐A model combined 15 decision trees (DT) using bootstrap aggregating (Bagging) to improve robustness. Predictions from these sub‐models, combined with the original strong features, served as input to a final DT model (Em‐3Class) for definitive A/B/C prediction within AIS‐high. Em‐En integrates Em‐2Class and Em‐3Class. To further assess the robustness of Em‐En, a fivefold cross‐validation was performed on the entire cohort of 183 patients, yielding a mean accuracy of 0.832 ± 0.024.

To clarify the decision logic of the model, we used Matplotlib to visualize the decision boundary. We used the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve to evaluate the comprehensive performance and generalization ability of the ensemble model. The precision‐recall (PR) curve was used to evaluate the robustness of the model. To clarify the actual prediction performance and generalization performance of the model, we used Matplotlib to draw the confusion matrix heat map. According to the confusion matrix, we calculate true positive (TP), true negative (TN), false positive (FP), and false negative (FN). The accuracy, specificity, sensitivity, and F1‐score of Em‐En are calculated using the following formula: Accuracy=1C×∑TP+TNTP+FP+TN+FN Specificity=1C×∑TNTN+FP Sensitivity=1C×∑TPTP+FN F1‐score=1C2×∑∑TP/TP+FP×∑TP/TP+FN×2∑TP/TP+FP+∑TP/TP+FN where C is the number of multiple categories of the model (here is 4).

At the same time, the ROC curve was used to compare the prediction performance and generalization performance of multiple models (RF, XGBoost, LGBT, SVM, MLR, and Em‐En), and the decision curve analysis (DCA) was used to compare the clinical decision benefits of multiple models.

Statistics Analysis

All statistical tests were performed using R software 4.2.0, Python 3.10.6, and Python 3.8.15. Continuous and categorical variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation and counts, respectively. The normality of continuous data was checked using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Continuous variables were analyzed using one‐way ANOVA, Mann–Whitney U test, or Kruskal–Wallis test. Categorical variables were compared using the chi‐square or Fisher's exact test. Pearson correlation analysis was used for continuous variables. A p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Pretraining of U‐Net++ Network for Target Area Segmentation of T2WI Images

AI is now a vital technology for medical image segmentation, with U‐Net being a common choice. However, the single connection between the encoder and decoder in the U‐Net can lead to the loss of important features. U‐Net++ addresses this by connecting all encoder and decoder subnetworks through nested skip pathways, though this can sometimes impact final performance. Transformer architectures, employing multi‐head attention modules for channel‐wise cross‐fusion between encoder and decoder, excel at capturing global context but may retain less detailed shallow information compared to U‐Net++. To leverage the advantages of both, we employed a UCTransNet network, which combines U‐Net++ for pretraining with a transformer architecture for formal training (Figure 1).

(B) Pretraining and transfer learning for target area segmentation of T2WI images. (C) Radiomic features selection for AIS prediction models. For pretraining, inputs were the full set of T2WI sagittal image slices (13 layers) from TCSCI patients in the training set. Pretraining required 140 h on an RTX 3090 GPU, involving five complete training runs using “dropout” and “early stopping” to identify the optimal model. The U‐Net++ network processed each T2WI slice in 0.048 s, completing the segmentation for one patient (13 slices) in 0.63 s. The five best‐performing networks from pretraining were applied to the test set, and their mIOU, mDICE, mSpecificity, and mSensitivity were calculated (Table 3). Results indicated excellent specificity (0.999) but relatively poor sensitivity (0.713) for U‐Net++, suggesting a conservative segmentation approach.

Transfer Learning With Attention Neural Network for Target Area Segmentation

In the formal training phase, parameters from the pretrained model were transferred to the UCTransNet network. To reduce noise and enhance model sensitivity, only T2WI image slices containing manually defined ROI regions were used as inputs. Formal training lasted 95 h on an RTX 3090 GPU for five complete runs. The resulting networks were evaluated on the test set (Table 3). The optimized UCTransNet used a combined binary cross entropy and dice loss function (BDLF) to address class imbalance and improve segmentation consistency. Specificity remained high (0.998 vs. 0.999), while sensitivity significantly improved (0.895 vs. 0.713). Both mDICE and mIOU also increased compared to the pretraining model. Furthermore, UCTransNet was computationally more efficient, processing each slice in 0.024 s and a full patient scan in 0.31 s, advantageous for potential deployment.

To validate the network's segmentation performance, two test patients were randomly selected (Figure 2). Visual assessment showed excellent and robust prediction accuracy (Figure 2A–E,G). To elucidate the segmentation logic, a hook algorithm captured attention weights, visualized as heatmaps (Figure 2F,H). The attention field formed a rectangular area around the injury site, smaller than the input image. While higher attention weights were often observed near the endpoints of the manually segmented lesion, the model also attended to adjacent anatomical structures (e.g., vertebrae), which is consistent with leveraging contextual information for the segmentation task.

Radiomic features were extracted from manually defined VOIs. Initially, 854 features were extracted per patient. After ensuring reproducibility (ICC > 0.70), 235 features were retained for subsequent analysis. Targeting the four AIS grade labels (A–D), features were normalized using z‐scores. LASSO regression selected 88 features with nonzero coefficients for the multi‐classification problem (Figure 3A). Using fivefold cross‐validation, various models, including RF, XGBoost, LGBT, SVM, and MLR, were constructed. Discussion

TCSCI is a common but severe disease, frequently accompanied by compression and edema of the spinal cord. Sixty percent of traumatic SCIs occur in the cervical spine [30], frequently resulting in paralysis. Early surgical decompression is considered to be the most effective treatment for patients with TCSCI [31], but relies on timely and accurate diagnosis, for which limitations remain in precisely characterizing injury extent. MRI is currently an effective method for visualizing compression and internal structure of the spinal cord [7, 8], while the AIS grade, determined via the ISNCSCI exam according to the professional neurological examination, classifies impairment severity [16, 32]. Nevertheless, both of these two common methods need accumulation of clinical experience, creating a great need for a convenient and objective technology in clinical practice.

AI has become an indispensable technical means for clinical diagnosis and treatment in recent years [4]. Especially in aspect of image segmentation and feature extraction, AI performs much faster and more accurately for TCSCI severity classification. This study developed an AI‐assisted pipeline based on T2WI MRI (Figure 1), incorporating TCSCI target area identification, radiomic features selection, and AIS grade prediction.

T2WI can indicate hyper‐intensity areas for TCSCI, with sagittal images being particularly valuable for spinal surgeons in the preliminary stage. We optimized a UCTransNet network, combining U‐Net++ (pretraining) and Transformer (formal training) based on T2WI sagittal images from TCSCI patients. This hybrid approach retained U‐Net++'s information preservation benefits while incorporating the Transformer's global contextual awareness via multi‐head attention. Using full slices (including noise) as inputs in pretraining enhanced robustness, while using ROI slices in formal training improved sensitivity. Meanwhile, we compiled a new loss function algorithm to optimized UCTransnet network, which greatly increased the specificity. A custom loss function (BDLF) further optimized performance. The final model achieved good segmentation accuracy of T2WI sagittal images from TCSCI patients (mDICE: 0.790, mIOU: 0.663) and high specificity (0.998) and sensitivity (0.895). It only takes 0.31 s for the whole segmentation process of one patient (13 slices), potentially aiding surgeons in quickly locating injuries.

To explore the MRI‐AIS severity relationship, an ensemble model (Em‐En) was built using radiomic features from manually defined VOIs. A stratified prediction approach (first AIS‐high/‐low, then A/B/C within high) was necessary due to the complexity of direct four‐class prediction. A series of strong features served as labels to restructure an integration model (Em‐En). The Em‐En model demonstrated superior performance for predicting AIS grades on our dataset compared to other models we tested. Series and parallel of each AIS grade is compatible in the Em‐En model, which combines elements of boosting (sequential focus on errors across tiers) and bagging (for Em‐A). The Em‐En AIS prediction model could potentially assist surgeons in estimating injury severity from MRI, particularly when patient communication is impaired. This will contribute to the surgical opportunity for TCSCI patients with some complications, such as coma, limb fracture, traumatic pain, post‐traumatic psychological dysfunction, or other communication disabilities [30].

Several limitations must be acknowledged. The Em‐En prediction model relied on radiomic features extracted from manually defined VOIs rather than from automatically segmented injury areas. In approximately 15% of the 189 cases, the senior surgeon made minor adjustments to the ROI boundaries delineated by the primary surgeon. These adjustments were typically refinements of a few pixels to better align with the perceived edges of the hyperintense signal on T2WIs. The frequent need for adjustments during the senior‐review process highlights the experience‐based variability in manual MRI interpretation, thereby underscoring the clinical need for the objective, AI‐assisted diagnostic pipeline developed in this study.

While our DL model showed good segmentation performance, it was trained on a relatively small dataset (n = 130 for segmentation). A fully automated pipeline integrating the segmentation and prediction models is a key future objective. We anticipate that directly using features from automated segmentations with current performance levels would likely reduce prediction accuracy, highlighting the need for further improvement in segmentation robustness before seamless integration. Future multicenter studies with larger datasets are planned to improve the DL segmentation and enable fully automated prediction. This study is based on data from a single center, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. The cohort also had a relatively high median age (males: 62 and females: 60), which may not be fully representative of the broader TCSCI population; the impact of this on model generalizability should be further investigated. The sample sizes for certain AIS grades (particularly B and C) in the test set were limited, which could affect the stability of performance estimates for those classes; this is an inherent challenge in clinical datasets, but should be noted. The assessment of AIS grades itself can be subject to inter‐rater variability and clinical subjectivity; our model's performance is relative to this clinical standard. Finally, the timing of the AIS grade assessment relative to the MRI (both within 48 h) suggests the assessed grades represent acute injury severity; the model's applicability to chronic stages may differ.

Conclusions

This study reported an AI‐assisted pipeline for relatively objective diagnosis and severity prediction in TCSCI disease. Standardized code, T2WI sagittal image data, and clinical baseline data were released, with a complete analysis process accompanied by detailed descriptions and algorithms. These resources will serve as a theoretical foundation for the clinical application of AI‐assisted diagnostic methods for TCSCI disease and lower the barrier of interpretation of MRI with AIS grades, offering some quantitative indicators of the severity for clinicians. Future work will focus on multicenter validation, full automation integrating segmentation and prediction, and exploration of longitudinal changes.
