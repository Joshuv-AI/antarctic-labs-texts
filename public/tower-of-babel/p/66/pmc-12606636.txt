A Comprehensive Machine Learning Model for Metal–Ligand Binding Prediction: Applications in Chemistry and Biology
Authors: Erandika Karunaratne, Federico Zahariev, Marilú Pérez García
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
A machine-learning (ML) model that predicts metal–ligand binding constants was developed using the open-source Chemprop software. The model was trained on over 30,000 experimental log K 1 values, which include both protonation and metal–ligand stability constants, comprising over 3500 ligands and 102 metal ions from 73 total elements, thus generalizing beyond existing limited approaches, which focus only on specific metals or ligand families. The best-performing model included a combination of SMILES-based molecular representations along with descriptors for the metal ion and experimental conditions. It had an external test R 2 value of 0.942, and MAE value of 0.834. A “SMILES-only” simpler version also produced accurate predictions and preserved the binding trends, serving as a quick and easily accessible alternative for users without computational expertise. The SMILES-only model performed comparably to density functional theory (DFT) calculations but utilized a fraction of the computational resources. The model was successfully applied across diverse domains, including bioinorganic chemistry, heavy metal remediation, and sensor development and demonstrated its effectiveness as a rapid and reliable screening tool for both academic and industrial uses.

Introduction

Metal–ligand affinity constants, often termed stability constants, quantify the strength of the interaction or binding between a metal ion and a ligand in a complex formed through coordination bonding and provide insights into the thermodynamics of complex formation. A stability constant is the equilibrium constant (K 1) for the formation of a metal–ligand complex in a solution. It is expressed as the quotient of the metal–ligand complex concentration [MLig] and the product of the metal and ligand concentrations, [M] and [Lig], respectively, as shown in eq . 1 M(aq)+Lig(aq)⇋MLig(aq),⁣K1=[MLig][M][Lig]

Similarly, proton association constant (K P) describes the equilibrium between a ligand (Lig) and a proton (H+) and can be expressed as, 2 Lig(aq)+H2O(aq)⇋LigH+(aq)+OH−(aq),⁣KP=[LigH+][OH−][Lig]

Proton association constants are important in understanding and predicting the degree to which a ligand donates a proton in solvent extraction and complex formation. Both protonation and metal–ligand complexation are governed by interaction between electron-rich donors within the ligand and electron-deficient metal ion or proton acceptors.

Stability constants play a crucial role in understanding and predicting the behavior of metal–ligand complexes in various chemical, biological, analytical, and environmental contexts such as catalysis, drug design, − enhancing drug stability, MRI imaging, , and metal ion quantification, extraction, and purification. − To enhance the efficiency of these processes, we are actively seeking novel ligands that are cost-effective and environmentally friendly. Employing experimental methods to conduct a ligand search and ascertain metal–ligand affinity constants, including techniques like spectrophotometry, , potentiometry, and calorimetry, , can be labor-intensive, impractical, and subject to interpretation. Given the vast chemical space involved, obtaining the stability constants without a predictive model is virtually impossible.

Various models have been introduced to predict metal–ligand binding constants, including quantum mechanics calculations, − Quantitative Structure–Property Relationship (QSPR) − models, and machine learning (ML) − models. Although quantum mechanics approaches can offer high accuracy, their wide use is impractical due to the associated computational cost and inaccessibility to nonexperts. While QSPR approaches can offer computationally efficient interpretable models, they are often designed for limited types of ligands or metals. The specificity of these approaches prevents or restricts their transferability and thus broad applicability. Recently, data-driven approaches such as ML techniques emerged as a promising alternative for estimating metal–ligand log K 1 values, enabling high-throughput screening of much larger chemical space. These models can capture complex nonlinear relationships between the chemical properties of ligands, metals, and their binding affinities. ML techniques, such as Artificial Neural Networks, Support Vector Machines, and Random Forests, have been employed to predict metal–ligand binding constants. , For example, Chaube et al. developed models to predict the first stability constant (log K 1) for lanthanide–ligand complexes, employing various traditional ML algorithms. Similarly, Kanahashi et al. applied Gaussian Process Regression (GPR) models to predict both the first (β1) and higher-order (β n ) overall stability constants for a diverse set of metal–ligand complexes. These models rely on various physicochemical and topological features of the metal ions and ligands as input features. In our previous study, we extended ML models by integrating ML predictions with computer-aided molecular design software (HostDesigner) to rank and identify ligands with optimal metal ion selectivity, a feature not explicitly addressed in the aforementioned studies. While all these studies leverage ML techniques to predict metal–ligand stability constants, they differ in their specific algorithms, data diversity, and scope.

In contrast to earlier studies, we have developed a more generalized ML model capable of predicting both the protonation constant and the metal–ligand stability constant. Specifically, while Chaube et al. included only 15 lanthanide ions, and Kanahashi et al. included only 57 metal ions for β1 and 50 for β n (n = 2–6), our current model is built on a data set consisting of 102 metal ions from 73 elements, nearly double the number used in previous models. This substantially broader chemical space coverage enhances the model’s generalizability.

To construct our ML framework, we relied on experimental data obtained from the IUPAC Stability Constants Database. Models were built to predict both metal–ligand binding constants (log K 1) and ligand protonation constants using the directed message-passing neural network architecture − implemented in the Chemprop software. , Separate models were trained on the stability constant and protonation constant data sets, along with a combined model trained on the merged data set, enabling analysis of the benefits of integrative learning. Overall, our ML approach is a scalable and accessible alternative to traditional computational approaches, allowing for rapid exploration of chemical space and supporting the rational design of ligands for use in catalysis, coordination chemistry, drug discovery, and other applications.

Results

Data Set Analysis

The experimental data for this study are from the IUPAC Stability Constants Database (SC-database), which comprises metal–ligand stability constants, protonation constants, and related experimental data published from 1950 to 2013. The detailed method used to prepare the data for this study is described in the Methods Section. The ligands belong to a wide range of chemical classes, including N-donors, N–O donors, S-donors, indicators, inorganic oxyacids, halides, pseudohalides, and phosphines. The experimental log K 1 values of metal–ligand stability constants span from −3.8 to 52 (Figure S4a), while the molecular weights of the ligands in this data set range from 17 to 1537 Da with an average of 203 Da (standard deviation = 126 Da, Figure S4b). Chemprop Workflow and Model Architecture

We employed the Chemprop software to develop a predictive model for log K1. Chemprop is a graph neural network model belonging to the message passing neural network (MPNN) family. It uses a directed message-passing neural network (D-MPNN) architecture that converts molecular SMILES strings − into graph representations, enabling the model to learn atom- and bond-level features. In this framework, atoms are represented as nodes with features such as element type, number of bonds, formal charge, hybridization, and aromaticity, while covalent bonds are represented with features such as directed edges with features including bond type, conjugation, and stereochemistry. Through iterative message passing along these directed edges, the model propagates atom- and bond-level information to generate molecular embeddings, which are subsequently aggregated and passed through a feed-forward neural network to predict the stability constant. Model Training with Different Subsets of the Data Set

In this study, we utilized a data set of 32,459 metal–ligand stability constants and 11,521 protonation constants, both extracted from the IUPAC database. The metal–ligand data set includes 3585 ligands, while the protonation data set includes 3864 chemically diverse organic and inorganic ligands. To evaluate model performance under varying conditions, we trained separate models on multiple data subsets, including: (i) stability constants at 25 °C, (ii) stability constants at various temperatures, (iii) protonation constants at various temperatures, and (iv) a combined data set of metal–ligand and protonation data at various temperatures.

Molecular Representation Using SMILES

The ligand and metal chemical structures represented as SMILES (simplified molecular-input line-entry system) were used as the input data for the model training with experimental log K 1 as the target variable. For example, a nickel–polyamine complex was represented as “NCCNCCN·[Ni+2]”. Different metal oxidation states are represented using bracketed notation, such as [Fe+3] for Fe­(III) and [Fe+2] for Fe­(II). It is important to note that this SMILES representation does not explicitly specify which ligand atoms are coordinated to the metal ion, limiting its ability to capture detailed coordination information. As a result, when constructing the molecular graph, atoms are treated as nodes and only explicit covalent bonds are encoded as edges, leaving the metal ion as an isolated node without coordination edges to donor atoms. This reflects a current limitation of the representation for coordination complexes.

Feature Selection and Model Evaluation

While the D-MPNN model performs well using only SMILES strings, we evaluated whether incorporating additional features, such as metal ion descriptors, ligand-based properties, and experimental conditions, could improve its accuracy. The effect of these features was not uniform across data sets. In most subdata sets, particularly those involving metal–ligand stability constants, adding metal ion descriptors and experimental conditions yielded modest but measurable gains. By contrast, RDKit-derived ligand features or Morgan fingerprints alone did not improve performance, though in the protonation constant data set, RDKit features contributed modestly when combined with other feature types. These results suggest that the benefit of additional features is data set dependent, since the D-MPNN already captures much of the relevant chemical information through its message-passing framework. Detailed evaluations and feature selection results are provided in Section C of the Supporting Information (Tables S3–S6).

Model Performance by Metal Ion Characteristics and Ligand Type

The M4 model was trained and evaluated on metal ions with oxidation states ranging from +1 to +6. As shown in Figures S13 and S14, the model performs strongly for ions with charges of +1 to +3 (R 2 >0.93 on both test and external test sets), consistent with their high representation in the training set (Figure S7). In contrast, performance declines for +4 and +5 ions and data for +6 ions were insufficient to draw meaningful conclusions. These trends suggest that model accuracy is closely linked to representation in the training data.

Figure S15a,b further explores model performance by individual metal ions. In the test set, ions such as Ni3+, Zr4+, Ti3+, Pt2+, Pu3+, Mn3+, and Cr3+ exhibited the highest prediction errors (MAE >3 log K units). In the external test set, no ion exceeded this error threshold, although Be2+ showed the largest deviation. Although ions such as Cr3+, Be2+, and Zr4+ have moderate representation in the training set, their elevated prediction errors may reflect ligand-specific factors or variability in experimental data quality. For ions with very limited representation, such as Mn3+ and Pu3+, sparse data likely contribute to reduced model accuracy. These findings highlight the importance of both data quantity and quality in driving predictive performance for specific metal ions.

To further understand the sources of prediction error, model performance was analyzed by the ligand type (Figure S15). Ligands were classified using RDKit based on their SMILES representations into five categories: aliphatic chains, aromatics, macrocycles, macromolecules, and others. Figure S15a shows that aliphatic chains account for the largest portion of the training data set (51.9%), followed by aromatics (36.5%), macrocycles (6.6%), others (4.9%), and macromolecules (0.1%). Notably, macrocyclic ligands yielded the highest prediction errors in both the test and external test sets (Figure S15a,c). Since macrocycles are not the least represented class, this suggests that the elevated errors may stem from challenges in accurately encoding metal coordination environments using 2D SMILES representations rather than data imbalance alone.

Discussion

The results above confirm that our ML model delivers a strong predictive performance across diverse ligands and metal ions. To demonstrate broader applicability, we next explore its use in practical scenarios where accurate metal–ligand affinity prediction is critical, for example, in metal extraction processes, as well as in biological and environmental applications. Use cases shown below and in Supporting Information Section F include peptides, proteins, drugs, and probes, that highlight the model’s agreement with experimental affinities and its potential as a faster, scalable alternative to DFT-based methods.

To highlight the practicality and accessibility of our approach, we also evaluated the SMILES-only version of the model (M4-S) for these application-driven predictions. While the inclusion of atomic and experimental features can enhance accuracy, the SMILES-only model yields qualitatively similar trends. Importantly, this version facilitates broader use, as SMILES strings can be readily generated by using common molecular editors such as Avogadro, ChemDraw, or OpenBabel. A comparison of predictions from M4 and M4-S is shown in Figures S17.1 and S17.2. Based on performance on the external test set (Table S6.1), the M4-S model exhibits an approximate 3% decrease in R 2 compared to the full-featured M4 model, representing a modest trade-off for improved usability.

Binding Affinity Predictions for Small Proteins and Peptides

To demonstrate applicability in biologically relevant systems, we evaluated the model on high-affinity lanthanide-binding proteins, focusing on Lanmodulin (LanM), − a well-characterized protein with four EF-hand metal-binding motifs. LanM shows over 100-million-fold selectivity for Ln3+ and Y3+ over Ca2+.

We compared predicted and experimental log K values using the full LanM sequence, individual EF hand domains (EF1–EF4), and linker regions (non-EF). As shown in Figure (a), predictions from the full sequence closely match experimental values. The model also differentiates among EF hands, predicting higher log K for EF1–EF3 than EF4 consistent with EF4 being the weakest site (Figure (b)). Additionally, EF-hand sequences showed stronger predicted binding than non-EF regions (Figure c), indicating that the model captures sequence-level effects beyond individual amino acid contributions.

DFT vs ML Predictions

Density Functional Theory (DFT) is a well-established computational method for estimating the thermodynamics of metal–ligand binding, offering insights into the coordination site and electronic structure. However, DFT-based predictions are often limited by high computational cost, especially for large or electron-rich systems, and the challenges of accurately modeling solvation and entropy contributions. While DFT can effectively capture relative trends in binding affinities among similar ligands, it frequently struggles to reproduce absolute stability constants without extensive thermodynamic corrections.

To benchmark our machine learning (ML) model against DFT-based approaches, we compared model predictions with experimental data and DFT-calculated binding energies from previous studies on two systems: (i) lanthanide complexes of bis-lactam phenanthroline (BAPhen) ligands and (ii) uranyl (UO2 2+) complexes with 13 oxygen-donor ligands.

In the case of BAPhen–lanthanide complexes, Healy et al. employed DFT calculations at the M06/LC/6–31+G­(d) level to compute relative aqueous selectivities (ΔΔG aq(La/Ln)) for various lanthanides using preorganized and nonpreorganized ligands. Their approach modeled 1:1 metal–ligand complexes in solution to evaluate the lanthanide selectivity. The uncorrected DFT-calculated ΔΔG aq values, as shown in Figure S1 of ref , deviate substantially from the corresponding experimental stability constants in some cases by more than 2 kcal/mol. These calculations required hours to days per complex, depending on the system size and metal identity.

Similarly, Vukovic et al. were able to provide approximate log K1 values for UO2 2+ complexes with various negatively charged oxygen-donor ligands using the B3LYP and IEF-PCM solvation models. While this approach demonstrated good trends for relative affinities, it overestimated absolute log K 1 values by 6–30 units, which was largely due to the limitations of modeling solvation energies. To address the problem, linear corrections based on empirical relationships were used to ultimately provide predictive utility.

In contrast, our trained ML model produces virtually real-time estimates of metal–ligand binding affinities (<1 s per complex), whereas density functional theory (DFT) calculations typically require 24–48 h per complex and involve computationally intensive geometry optimization, solvation modeling, and electronic structure calculations. For example, the SMILES-only version of the D-MPNN model (M4-S) accurately predicts the stability constants of the BAPhen–lanthanide complexes with lower deviations from experimental values (see an example in Figure ). Additionally, the ML model is applicable to different metals and classes of ligands, including charged actinides, which are particularly challenging for DFT due to their electron count and solvent environments.

Comparison of DFT-calculated and ML predicted log K values from the D-MPNN (M4-S) model against experimental log K values for (a) lanthanide ions with BAPhen and (b) 13 ligands forming uranyl (UO2 2+) complexes.

While DFT remains indispensable for understanding electronic structure and mechanistic pathways, our results demonstrate that machine learning offers a scalable and computationally efficient alternative for screening large chemical libraries. Ultimately, once the ML model is trained, predicting binding affinities requires milliseconds rather than hours to days necessary for a DFT calculation, making it especially advantageous for high-throughput applications.

In summary, these examples highlight the model’s capability to replicate experimentally observed binding affinities and approximate DFT-level predictions while offering significant computational savings. This makes it well suited for screening across vast chemical spaces.

Oversampling to Address Class Imbalance

As an exploratory attempt to mitigate the class imbalance observed for higher oxidation states (Figure S7) and the deviations noted for +4 ions in the application examples (Figure ), we applied a moderate oversampling of underrepresented oxidation states using SMILES randomization. Augmentation was carried out only after data set splitting to prevent leakage, with a cap on randomized SMILES per parent ligand to avoid overamplification. This procedure increased the representation of +4 ions from ∼1.7% to ∼12% in the training set while maintaining the original distributions in the test and external test sets.

Despite this adjustment, the performance of the model remained essentially unchanged (Tables S7 and S8). For selected +4 complexes highlighted in Figure (e.g., Sn4+, Th4+, Pu4+), oversampling produced modest shifts (Table S9), but improvements were not systematic. These findings suggest that while oversampling can reduce the severity of class imbalance, that alone is insufficient to substantially enhance predictive performance for high oxidation states. Future work will therefore focus on integrating curated high-valence data from external sources and exploring cost-sensitive or class-weighted learning approaches to improve robustness for these challenging cases.

Novel Ligand Design for Efficient Metal Extraction

Building on the predictive capabilities demonstrated above, our approach can be extended to the rational design of new ligands for selective metal extraction. Starting from seed molecules with high binding affinity identified using the IUPAC data set (Table S2, Figures S8–S10), libraries of candidate ligands can be constructed with attention to both binding strength and synthetic accessibility. For this, a variety of approaches may be employed, including combinatorial chemistry, evolutionary algorithms, and other data-driven methods. In particular, generative AI models offer a powerful means of designing novel ligands by leveraging structural patterns and binding affinities of known complexes. The resulting candidates can then be rapidly screened by using our trained D-MPNN models to predict binding affinities and prioritize ligands with improved selectivity and stability for target metal ions.

Limitations and Future Work

While our machine learning model demonstrates robust predictive performance across diverse applications, it is essential to acknowledge several limitations.

First, data set representation is a key factor in model accuracy. The model struggles with metal ions of charge +4 and higher oxidation states, because these ions are underrepresented (∼4%) in the initial training data. Similarly, most ligands in the training data are less than 1500 g/mol, and only 1% of ligands exceed 500 g/mol, which restricts the applicability to larger biomolecules and macromolecular systems.

Second, structural information is missing from the SMILES representations. SMILES strings can be computed and easily used, but they do not include 3D information on coordination geometry, specific sites of donor atoms, or conformational dynamics that can greatly influence binding affinity. This information loss is especially concerning for making predictions about the class of macrocyclic ligands, which exhibited higher-than-average prediction error even with sufficient data set representation. Developing tailored models or incorporating macrocycle-specific descriptors may help address this challenge. More generally, incorporating 3D- and coordination-aware features represents an important direction for future work, including workflows for reliable 3D structure generation, refinement, and subsequent training of 3D graph neural networks, with particular attention given to macrocyclic ligands.

Third, although some experimental conditions are included, other critical environmental factors are not explicitly modeled. By default, our models do not consider solvent effects, ionic strength, pH or matrix effects, all of which can have significant effects on the stability constants. These contextual factors are critical for translating predictions to real-world experimental conditions.

Finally, consideration should be made about the variation in the experimental data quality. Differences in measurement methods (e.g., potentiometric, spectroscopic, and volumetric titrations) and how stability constants are reported may add variation/noise that can influence model training and likely explain residual errors for certain ligands or metal ions.

In summary, future developments should focus on (i) enriching data sets with higher oxidation states and larger ligands, (ii) developing 3D- and coordination-aware molecular representations, and (iii) integrating environmental parameters such as solvent, pH, and ionic strength into the modeling framework. The integration of our approach with generative AI and automated molecular design platforms offers an exciting pathway to alleviate limitations and further accelerate the discovery of ligands across metal extraction, environmental remediation, and biomedical applications.

Conclusion

We have developed a comprehensive machine learning framework that significantly advances metal–ligand binding prediction across a wide chemical space encompassing a variety of ligands and metal ions. By expanding coverage beyond previous models and achieving strong predictive accuracy, this approach enables scalable and accessible affinity prediction across a broad range of chemical systems. Using only SMILES representations as input, the model allows researchers to rapidly screen chemical libraries without requiring specialized computational expertise. Its prediction speeds are several orders of magnitude faster than those of DFT, significantly reducing the computational cost and enabling more feasible early-stage ligand discovery.

The broad applicability of the ML model has been demonstrated across diverse applications, including protein binding, contrast agents, chelation, and sensing, with predictive affinities closely aligned with experimental values. These results facilitate the rational design and prioritization of ligands for drug discovery, environmental science, and materials development. Combined with generative design and automated synthesis, our ML model could enable the rapid development of selective metal-binding ligands with applications in various scientific and technological applications.

Methods

Data Collection, Cleaning, and Curation

The experimental data used to train the machine learning models were derived from the IUPAC Stability Constants Database (SC-database), which compiles metal–ligand complexation and protonation constant data published between 1950 and 2013. A curated subset of metal–ligand complexes was generated by filtering out nonmetal complexes, polymeric ligands, ambiguous charge states, and entries lacking log K1 values. Additional filtering based on the electrolyte medium and temperature was applied to improve consistency. Ligand structures were represented as SMILES strings, retrieved using CAS numbers or IUPAC names. Detailed data curation procedures are provided in Supporting Information Section A.

Data Splitting and Sub Data Sets

Initially, an external test set composed of 20–40 ligands with all metals complexed with those ligands was removed from the data set according to the method explained in the Supporting Information. It is important to note that this external test set was excluded from the k-fold validation process to ensure the integrity of the model assessment. The remaining data set was then randomly divided into training and test sets at a ratio of 90:10. The training set was utilized for model training and underwent 5-fold cross-validation. This process involved further splitting the training set into training and internal validation subsets in an 80:20 ratio. Subsequently, the performance of the model was evaluated using the test set and external test set. This procedure was applied to all subdata sets used in this study, namely, the stability constant data set, the protonation constant data set, and the combination of these two subsets. See Supporting Information for additional details.

Features

The chemical structures of ligands and metal ions were represented using SMILES (Simplified Molecular Input Line Entry System) and served as input for model training with experimental log K 1 values as the target. Each input SMILES string consisted of the ligand structure followed by a period (indicating a disconnected system) and then the SMILES representation of the metal ion. In this format, inorganic anions are typically represented in their anionic forms, while most organic ligands appear in their neutral state. This input data was further augmented by additional features that describe the ligand, metal, and experimental conditions. Ten features are used for each metal ion, including atomic number, density, dipole polarizability, van der Waals radius, atomic weight, covalent radius, electronegativity, ionization energy, charge, and number of valence electrons. The ligands, represented by SMILES, were fed into the RDKit cheminformatics toolkit to generate 208 molecular physicochemical features for each ligand. Out of the RDkit features, redundant or sparse features were removed. This screening resulted in 181 RDKit features. The RDKit features were combined with the Morgan fingerprints, along with the ligand, and complex charges for a more detailed representation of the ligand. Experimental conditions: temperature, electrolyte concentration, and physical properties of the electrolyte (molecular weight and density) were also included. In total, 1221 features were used for each data set entry.

Hyperparameters for D-MPNN

Hyperparameter optimization of D-MPNN models was performed via Bayesian optimization using the hyperopt package of Chemprop. The best configuration of hidden size, depth, dropout, and number of feed-forward layers was determined through a 10-iterations search. The optimized hyperparameters are listed in Table S1.

D-MPNN Model Training and Evaluation

Model training was performed using 5-fold cross-validation (CV) on the training set, with performance reported as the average across folds. Within each fold, the best checkpoint was selected based on the lowest validation loss. Test set performance was averaged across folds to provide a robust estimate of generalization. For the external test set, predictions were generated by ensembling outputs across all folds. Model performance was evaluated using mean absolute error (MAE), root mean squared error (RMSE), and the coefficient of determination (R 2), calculated by comparing predicted and experimental values.

Software for Model Training

Chemprop 1.5.2 was used in the D-MPNN model training. KNN, Linear Regression, Random Forest Regression, Lasso Regression, and Gradient Boosting Regression were implemented using Scikit Learn.
