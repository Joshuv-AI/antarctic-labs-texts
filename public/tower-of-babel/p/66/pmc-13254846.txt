Assessing a method for single- and multidomain biofilm growth in a novel biofilm reactor: shear stress, ruggedness, repeatability, and reproducibility
Authors: Kylie B. Bodle, Ghazal Vahidi, Erick Johnson, Diane Walker, Al Parker, Christopher Jones, Darla Goeres, Brent M. Peyton, Heidi J. Smith
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Numerous lab-scale bioreactor systems exist to cultivate biofilms under desired growth conditions. However, existing systems have not been able to simultaneously deliver low shear stress, high gas transfer, and intermittent wetting. The industrial surfaces biofilm reactor (ISBR) was specifically developed to overcome these limitations by simultaneously providing these conditions. This study presents the first standard operating procedure (SOP) for the growth of a model biofilm-forming organism, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, within the ISBR, providing a reproducible framework for investigating biofilm development under conditions that closely mimic industrial environments. Statistical analyses confirmed the SOP's repeatability, ruggedness, and reproducibility using biofilm density and biovolume data, supported by an analytical shear stress model for ISBR coupons. Biofilm densities were rugged to recycle and rotation rate deviations but increased with influent feed rate. Conversely, biovolumes were rugged to influent feed and rotation rate changes but declined with higher recycle rates. Both metrics demonstrated excellent repeatability and reproducibility, underscoring the need to assess these characteristics for consistent biofilm formation. Additionally, as a proof-of-concept, two multidomain biofilms (a Legionella pneumophila-Vermamoeba vermiformis model and a Rhodotorula mucilaginosa yeast-P. aeruginosa bacterial coculture) were cultivated and analyzed. Both exhibited high repeatability, and the coculture remained stable over a month-long growth period.

Introduction

Biofilms, or communities of microorganisms living in a self-secreted polymeric matrix often attached to surfaces, can provide numerous benefits and detriments to human health and the environment. Widespread industrial and clinical problems, including microbially influenced corrosion, chronic wounds that require long-term medical attention, and various other infections can be linked with detrimental biofilm formation and growth [1,2]. Conversely, biofilms are essential for contaminant removal during wastewater treatment, nutrient uptake by plants and thus food production, ecosystem function, and bioremediation efforts [[2], [3], [4], [5]]. As such, harnessing or mitigating the effects of biofilms is a major research topic, and biofilm reactors are essential tools for in vitro cultivation and study of biofilms.

The ability to cultivate biofilms under diverse growth conditions in laboratory settings has been accomplished by developing and validating multiple bench-scale biofilm reactor models. For example, high fluid shear stresses and turbulent conditions can be modeled using a Center for Disease Control (CDC) biofilm or rotating annular bioreactors [6,7], whereas low or moderate shear conditions can be replicated using drip-flow or rotating-disk bioreactors, respectively [8,9]. Growth conditions and biofilm properties are inextricably linked, and fluid flow conditions are particularly influential on biofilm characteristics [[10], [11], [12]]. In short, simulating real-world biofilms in vitro requires bioreactors that faithfully emulate biofilm growth conditions.

Outdoor statues or architecture [13], household appliances (e.g., dishwashers [14], washing machines [15], showers [16], or toilets [17]), or industrial equipment (e.g., heat exchangers [18] or cooling towers [19]) are all environments with intermittently wet, low shear, high gas transfer conditions. Biofilms are also prevalent in these systems, but available bioreactors have, to date, failed to recapitulate these conditions. The industrial surfaces biofilm reactor (ISBR, BioSurface Technologies) was developed to fill this gap and specifically model cooling tower environments, which are known to foster biofilms containing the pathogen Legionella pneumophila [19,20]. Up to 84% of legionellosis outbreaks have been linked with aerosols from cooling towers [19]; thus, optimized biocide dosing strategies are needed and may be facilitated by ISBR-based testing. Since the ISBR is a novel reactor and has yet to be employed in any published biofilm study, the overarching goals of this study were to develop a standard operating procedure (SOP) for biofilm growth of a model biofilm-forming organism (Pseudomonas aeruginosa) in the ISBR and to evaluate the method's repeatability, reproducibility, and ruggedness. A method is repeatable if repetition of the same experiment in the same lab gives similar outcomes and reproducible if the same experiment, conducted in different labs, gives similar outcomes [22]. A rugged method will produce similar outcomes despite minor operational changes [23]. To identify if small changes to SOP variables result in substantially different outcomes, SOP ruggedness was evaluated with a 23 factorial experimental design in which rotation, recycle flow rate, and influent feed rates were varied by approximately 50-150% of SOP values. Variables were specifically selected because all are likely to deviate slightly from the SOP when implemented: for example, rotational speed is expected to vary slightly between different stir plates; likewise, fluid flow rates may shift due to pump calibration inconsistencies. Quantifying the effect of these operational variables is therefore essential for repeatable and reproducible biofilm growth and testing as well as standard method development [22]. Additionally, to improve understanding of shear stress and fluid film thicknesses across ISBR coupons under standard operating conditions, a computational fluid dynamic (CFD) model of both parameters was developed.

Lastly, we demonstrated the ISBR's functional applicability and research utility through two distinct use cases. First, a method was established to repeatably grow model cooling tower biofilms containing L. pneumophila and Vermamoeba vermiformis and tested to confirm the ISBR could accurately cultivate and model cooling tower growth conditions. Second, a method for repeatable multidomain biofilm (MDB) growth was developed and tested with one fungal (Rhodotorula mucilaginosa) and one bacterial (P. aeruginosa) species. MDBs were cultivated and the stability of each population was assessed over time because, despite MDBs' prevalence in real-world environments [24,25], methods for sustained MDB cultivation in vitro are lacking in the literature. Both use cases are important examples of how the ISBR can be used to cultivate diverse biofilms. This work represents an important step in the ability to cultivate both single- and multidomain biofilms from low shear stress, high gas transfer, and intermittently wet environments.

Materials and methods

The following sections are organized by each type of biofilm cultivated in the ISBR, followed by a description of numerical modelling methods.

Standard operating procedure development and ruggedness tests

Inoculum preparation

All ruggedness tests were conducted with P. aeruginosa ATCC strain 15442. Before each test, frozen stocks (used no more than five times) were streaked onto R2A agar plates (BD Difco, prepared per manufacturer's instructions), followed by incubation at 35 °C for 24 h. A single colony was then transferred to 50 mL of sterile 3 g/L tryptic soy broth (TSB, MP Biomedicals) and shaken at 150 RPM for 24 h; the 24-h-old aqueous culture served as the inoculum for all ruggedness experiments. This process was repeated prior to all SOP and ruggedness tests.

Coupon and reactor cleaning procedure

Before each test, ISBRs were disassembled and washed with soap and water. Polycarbonate coupons (15.3 mm × 76.2 mm x 1 mm thick) were removed from the coupon rotor, and both coupons and rotor were sonicated in a 1% soap solution for 15 min. The coupons and rotor were then rinsed in tap water, and sonicated for another 15 min in fresh, soap-free tap water. ISBRs were then reassembled with clean tubing, and cleaned coupons were visually inspected for flaws before they were returned to the coupon rotor. To ensure surface irregularities did not influence biofilm growth distributions and thereby impact microscopy analyses, brand new coupons were placed in rotor slots 3, 6, 9, 12, 15, and 18 prior to each test, and only new coupons were sampled for the analyses described below. All coupons were set at an 80° angle from the horizontal (Fig. 1).

Cleaned ISBRs and tubing were autoclaved for 15 min prior to use. A diagram of flow directions within the ISBR system is shown in Fig. 1. Coupons and ISBRs were purchased from Biosurface Technologies Corp., Bozeman, MT.

Standard operating protocol parameters for rotation rate and influent feed rate were based on those in the ISBR Operator's Manual provided by Biosurface Technologies [20,26]. The SOP recycle flow rate was set at 1 mL/min/coupon (i.e., 18 mL/min). Rotational speed was controlled with RT Touch Series Magnetic Stirrers (ThermoScientific), and recycle and influent media were pumped with Masterflex L/S Digital Drive peristaltic pumps (Avantor) pre-calibrated to the flow rates listed in Table 1. Influent media consisted of sterile deionized water and 3 g/L TSB, fed to achieve a final influent TSB concentration of 300 mg/L. On day zero, effluent ISBR tubing was clamped shut and reactors were inoculated with 1 mL of the aqueous culture (Section 2.1.1) and 350 mL of sterile 3 g/L TSB via the sampling port. The optical density at 600 nm (OD600) of the 24-h-old culture was measured prior to each inoculation and verified to be between 0.74 and 0.8 (corresponding to approximately 2.8E9 CFU/mL, drop plate method detailed in Section 2.1.4). Stir plates and recycle pumps were then turned on, and ISBRs were operated in batch phase for 24 h. Influent pumps were then turned on, and the effluent port was unclamped to begin the continuous phase. Sterile water and TSB medium for each operating condition were fed at the rates listed in Table 1. Rotation and recycle rates remained identical to those in the batch phase. The continuous phase lasted an additional four days, after which ISBRs were shut down and six coupons were sampled: three for biofilm density and three for biovolume (Sections 2.1.4, 2.1.5).

SOP ruggedness to small changes in rotational speed, recycle flow rate, and influent feed rates was determined using a 23 factorial experimental design in which the test variables were purposefully varied by ∼50-150% of SOP values (Table 1). Each ruggedness test consisted of a unique combination of each variable at its minimum or maximum level, detailed in Table S1. Three independent ISBR runs under SOP conditions were also performed to assess the ISBR's repeatability variance across independent runs and the variability across coupons within the same run. All tests were randomly ordered as listed in Table S1.

Biofilm cell density enumeration

At the end of the continuous phase (day 5), ISBR pumps and rotation were turned off and aerosols were allowed to settle for at least 15 min prior to opening the reactor. A flame-sterilized hemostat was used to remove triplicate coupons from equally spaced rotor positions (3, 9 and 15). Once removed, coupons were gently rinsed once via immersion into sterile dilution water (0.0425 g/L KH2PO4 and 0.405 g/L MgCl·6H2O, both Fisher). Rinsed coupons were then placed into new centrifuge tubes containing 40 mL sterile dilution water.

Following rinsing, biofilm cells were removed from coupons and disaggregated by a series of three 30-s vortex-sonication cycles [22]. Disaggregated samples (i.e., the 100 dilution) were then serially diluted (10−1-10−7) with dilution water in a 96-well plate [27]. Five drops from each dilution were drop-plated onto R2A agar plates in duplicate. Plates were incubated at 35 °C for 24 h, after which colonies were counted from the dilution with drops containing 3-30 colonies per drop. The arithmetic mean of colonies on duplicate plates was calculated, and the log density of biofilm coverage per coupon was calculated as detailed in Equation (1) [28]:(1)log10(CFUcm2)=log10[(XB)(VA)(1D)]where X = mean colony forming units (CFU) across all drops at the lowest countable dilution D; B = drop volume plated; V = volume cells were disaggregated into (i.e. 40 mL); A = coupon surface area; and D = dilution factor (e.g., 10−5). The area of one side of the coupon (11.62 cm2) was used in this calculation.

Microscopy

Triplicate coupons from equally spaced rotor positions (6, 12, and 18) were sampled for biomass quantification via confocal scanning laser microscopy (CSLM). Following the initial rinse, coupons were placed biofilm-side up in sterile Petri dishes and stained with the LIVE/DEAD™ BacLight™ Bacterial Viability Kit (Invitrogen) following the manufacturer's instructions for quantification of live and membrane-compromised cells. For imaging, three distinct regions of interest (ROIs) were identified (Fig. S1); ROI 1 at the droplet mark (“top”), ROI 2 at 2 cm below (“middle”), and ROI 3 at 4 cm below (“bottom”). For staining, coupons were incubated in the dark at room temperature for 15 min, then rinsed with sterile MilliQ water to remove excess stain before immediate imaging.

Stained biofilms were imaged using a Stellaris 8 DIVE upright CSLM with a 25× Leica HCX APO L U–V–I water immersion objective (NA 0.90, WD 220 μm) and a digital zoom to achieve 63× magnification. Fluorescent stain SYTO9 was excited at 488 nm and propidium iodide at 568 nm with an emission range of 498-550 and 578-650, respectively. For each coupon, three randomly selected 161 μm × 160 μm fields of view (FOVs) were imaged per ROI. All images were acquired as z-stacks, spanning the full visible depth of the biofilm.

CSLM images were analyzed for live biofilm biovolume using Beer's analysis method, detailed elsewhere [29,30]. This method estimates the surface of the biofilm by fitting the 3D image intensity data with a regression model, then integrates under the surface to calculate biovolume.

Statistical methods

The ISBR's ruggedness in terms of biofilm log densities (log10(CFU/cm2)) and biovolume (μm3) was evaluated with respect to operational factors using separate linear mixed effects models (LMMs). Covariates in the LMMs included the operational factors rotation rate, influent rate, and recycle rate for both CFU and imaging data. The LMMs included experiment as a random block effect to account for triplicate coupons from each reactor run. The LMM for biovolumes also included ROI (i.e., the location of imaging data relative to the droplet mark on the coupons) as a covariate and coupon as a random effect to account for the three FOVs from each ROI on each coupon. All two-way and three-way interactions among the operational factors and ROI were considered. For all models, residuals were checked to verify assumptions of normality and homoscedasticity. Tukey post hoc tests were performed to maintain a family-wise significance level of 5%. Analyses were performed with Minitab v. 21. Correlation between biovolume and log density responses was assessed by averaging imaging and CFU data for each coupon and fitting a linear regression model to the resulting paired values.

The repeatability of the ISBR SOP in terms of log densities was assessed by fitting an LMM to the three SOP runs with a random effect for experiment. Repeatability is evaluated by independently repeating the same experiment in the same lab. Good repeatability, i.e. similar experimental results, is indicated by a small repeatability standard deviation (SDr) [31]. The repeatability in terms of the biovolume was assessed by an LMM that had an additional random effect for coupon and a covariate for ROI [22].

Reproducibility is evaluated directly by performing the same method in different laboratories by different personnel. If similar results are observed across labs, the reproducibility standard deviation (SDR) is also small [31]. The ISBR SOP was not conducted across multiple labs in this study. Instead, reproducibility was estimated by assuming that the nine unique factor level combinations (Table S1) represented nine faux labs. An LMM with nested random effects for faux lab and experiment were then used to estimate SDR for the log density, and an LMM with an additional random effect for coupon to estimate SDR for the biovolume.

Use case 1: cooling tower biofilm cultivation

A detailed description of cooling tower biofilm inoculum preparation, ISBR operation, and cell density enumeration can be found in Section S1.2 of the SI. In brief, L. pneumophila BAA-74 (ATCC strain F1724) and Vermamoeba vermiformis strain CDC-19 cultures were grown and maintained in ATCC 1034 medium [32]. L. pneumophila requires a eukaryotic host for replication outside of laboratory media; V. vermiformis, a wide-spread, free-living amoeba, was used for this purpose [33,34]. Both cultures were maintained as detailed in Section S1.2.1. ISBRs were cleaned and sterilized (Section 2.1.2), followed by inoculation with an undefined heterotrophic bacterial mixture from the effluent of a biologically activated carbon filter (Section S1.2.2). Biofilm was grown for three days to ensure sufficient biomass for V. vermiformis grazing. Following this period, V. vermiformis and L. pneumophila cultures were inoculated and the ISBR was returned to batch phase for 24 h (Section S1.2.2). The V. vermiformis and L. pneumophila representative cooling tower biofilms were then grown for an additional three days under continuous flow before coupons were sampled for enumeration (Section S1.2.3).

Use case 2: multidomain biofilm cultivation

Multidomain biofilms consisting of one fungal species (Rhodotorula mucilaginosa strain IF6SW-F1 [35]) and one bacterial species (P. aeruginosa ATCC strain 15442) were cultivated in the ISBR as a second use case demonstration. Rhodotorula mucilaginosa was the selected fungal species because it is commonly found in both human and environmental microbiomes [36,37] and known to cohabitate with P. aeruginosa [38,39]. P. aeruginosa was selected for further study because ruggedness tests demonstrated its fast and adaptable growth in the ISBR, and therefore we anticipated it would tolerate growth well with another species. Details on MDB inoculum preparation, ISBR operation, and biofilm enumeration are provided in Section S1.3. Briefly, ISBRs were first inoculated with R. mucilaginosa and operated with a 5-day batch phase followed by a 10-day continuous phase to establish fungal biofilms. P. aeruginosa was then added to the ISBR, and MDBs were grown for an additional 13 days (Section S1.3.2). Coupons were sampled for microscopy and cell density enumeration on days 5, 15, 21, and 28 (Section S1.3.3).

Analytical fluid film and shear stress model development

To support our experimental observations, we constructed a numerical model to simulate fluid flow within the ISBR. To accomplish this, the ISBR, was represented as a 40° pie-slice (as viewed from above, Fig. 2A), where the wedge encompasses two coupons and provides the smallest axisymmetric version of the reactor required to capture coupon rotation under the recycle port. The coupons, and the rotor that carries them, are further enclosed by a smaller cylindrical wedge enabling coupon rotation while the surrounding ISBR chamber and ports remain stationary. For the CFD simulations, both the coupons and rotor were held stationary and coupons' location under the recycle port was varied. The justification for this design is three-fold: (i.) it allows droplets to run along each coupon's centerline, which improves comparability and model simplicity. Preliminary simulations showed negligible changes in the wall shear stress and only minor changes to the propagation path of droplets when droplets did not hit the center of coupons or when coupons were rotating. For this reason, including either parameter added unnecessary complexity to the model without significantly impacting model results, and therefore both were neglected. (ii.) Given that recycle flow rate is not guaranteed to synchronize with coupon rotation, it was not appropriate to model continuous coupon rotation without completing computationally prohibitive simulations; therefore, coupons were limited to discrete positions. (iii.) For model interpretation, the analysis was intentionally restricted as the intrinsically high spatial and temporal complexity of the fluid motion became increasingly difficult to interpret as model details increased in specificity (e.g., contact angle or changing surface properties due to biofilm, evaporation, or fluid atomization). Restricting the analysis allowed major trends to be highlighted while avoiding overemphasizing potentially inaccurate outcomes driven by minor or uncertain aspects of the model setup.

To characterize the range of wall shear stress coupons experience during ISBR operation, our model examined coupons at three distinct positions under the recycle port. As coupons spin, the preceding coupon creates the equivalent of a “rain shadow” on the following coupon, thereby only allowing droplets to fall directly on the upper ∼40% of each coupon. Coupons were therefore evaluated across this un-shadowed portion to approximate the maximum spread in wall shear stress. Coupons were modeled at 6°, 9°, and 12° about the origin, or where water droplets hit each at approximately 30.5, 16, and 1.5 mm from the top, respectively (Fig. 2B). These positions represent the complete range over which recycle droplets may impact coupons and correspond to droplets travelling 45.7, 60.2, and 74.7 mm, respectively, down a coupon surface. The model was set at SOP conditions, i.e. 30 RPM rotation rate (equivalent to coupons passing under the recycle port every 0.0875 s) and 80° coupon angle.

CFD simulations were performed in Siemens’ SimCenter Star-CCM+ (version 2502). Water (1000.0 kg/m3 density, 8.8871E-4 Pa s viscosity) and air (1.18415 kg/m3 density, 1.85508E-5 Pa s viscosity) phases were treated as incompressible and laminar. The interface between the two fluids was captured with two multiphase formulations using the volume-of-fluid (VOF) method [[40], [41], [42]]. The thin film left as the droplet moved down the coupon was modeled with the fluid-film formulation [43,44] and coupled to the VOF method (0.072 N/m surface tension). The front and back of the modeled ISBR wedge were periodic, allowing water and air to move across both boundaries appropriately. Coupon surfaces were approximated as clean and smooth.

To maximize computational efficiency, the reactor was discretized with a polygonal mesh with an average element size of 1 mm. Regions under the recycle port and surrounding the coupon were further refined with an average element size of 0.175 mm to enhance model precision in areas experiencing bulk flow. To adequately resolve droplet shapes, there were at least 10 elements per droplet diameter (per established practice [45,46]). A prismatic layer was applied to the coupon surface to improve resolution of droplet and fluid-film development. This configuration also ensured a uniform first-layer thickness adjacent to the surface, thereby reducing noise in the wall-shear-stress calculations. In total there were five prism layers growing with a rate of 110% away from the surface with a first layer thickness of approximately 0.049 mm. For the 6°, 9°, and 12° coupon positions, the total mesh had 3,102,510, 3,085,220, and 3,018,229 elements, respectively.

In addition to modeling the baseline shear stress, or that occurring under SOP recycle flow rates (18 mL/min), the shear stresses occurring at the minimum (9 mL/min) and maximum (27 mL/min) recycle flow rates was also modeled to aid interpretation of ruggedness results. Changing rotation rates were not evaluated, as preliminary simulations showed that rotation negligibly impacted shear. Instead, rotation mainly impacted the “rain shadow” between coupons, with shadows declining with lower rotation rates. Likewise, since influent flow droplets do not hit the coupons, changing influent flows were not modeled.

In total, six simulations were performed that evaluated each coupon position under the SOP recycle flow rate. As the coupon with the longest droplet flow path, the 12° coupon was also evaluated under the minimum and maximum recycle flow rates to assess the relationship between recycle rate and shear. All simulations were run with a time step of 2E-4 s to 0.9 s, except for those at the minimum recycle rate, which were run to 1.46 s. Data was collected every five timesteps, or 0.001 s. Data from the bottom 7.5 mm of the coupon are not presented here, as droplets pooled on the rotor in this region and therefore did not contribute to the bulk of the shear stress and fluid dynamics investigated.

SOP variance assessment

Log density and biovolume results from the three SOP runs were assessed to evaluate the ISBR's repeatability. P. aeruginosa densities under SOP conditions ranged from 7.68 to 7.98 log10(CFU/cm2) with an overall mean of 7.81 log10(CFU/cm2) and SDr equal to 0.11. This value indicates that when the ISBR SOP described herein is implemented by other laboratories, they are predicted to generate a biofilm log density on a single coupon within 0.11 of the true mean log density for that lab. Approximately 77% of the variance could be attributed to between-experiment sources, such as differences in inoculum preparation or lab temperature; the remaining 23% was attributable to within-experiment sources such as differing environmental conditions in the reactor between coupons.

P. aeruginosa biovolumes under ISBR SOP conditions ranged from 4.20 to 5.30 log10(μm3) with an overall mean of 4.78 log10(μm3) and SDr equal to 0.25. Approximately 43% of the variance was attributed to between-experiment sources; the remaining 57% was due to within-experiment variation.

As described in Section 2.1.6, the ISBR's reproducibility was roughly estimated by assuming that results from each of the nine unique ruggedness tests (Table S1) represented those from nine different laboratories. The SDR was equal to 0.46, which indicates that if a new lab were to run the ISBR SOP in a single experiment, the log density of P. aeruginosa on one coupon is predicted to be 0.46 away from the true mean log density. For log biovolumes, the SDR was equal to 0.27.

Although the lowest impact location (6° coupon) is hit by droplets with the longest free fall and thus highest momentum, the starting wall shear stress was not substantially larger for 6° coupons than it was for 9° or 12° coupons (∼25 Pa). Likewise, regardless of coupon position or droplets' position along coupons, wall shear stress was greatest at droplets’ leading edge where the droplet height was tallest and around the contact line between the droplet and coupon. Shear was also elevated at the tail end of droplets and remained between 10 and 20 Pa for the 6° and 9° coupons throughout their 0.06-0.08 s travel times. Interestingly, for the 12° coupon, shear stress at the tail end of the droplet was less than 10 Pa within 0.01 s of droplet travel, perhaps due to the difference in droplet shape for the 12° coupon compared to the 6° and 9° coupons. As shown in Fig. 9, droplets on 6° and 9° coupons quickly become teardrop-shaped, whereas droplets on 12° coupons maintain their roundness for at least 0.04 s. This behavior might also be caused by the speed at which flow transitioned from droplet-dominated to film-dominated for 12° coupons: shear stress was lower in film dominated areas; therefore, fluid film encountered by the tail end of the primary droplet might be expected to decline faster in these well-wetted zones.

Although shear stress at the leading droplet edge was not affected by where droplets landed on coupons, the average wall shear stress under droplets was strongly affected by droplets’ impact location (Fig. 10). Droplets that fell further prior to impact provided higher and more variable shear stress than those that fell a shorter distance. For example, for each droplet that hit the 6° coupon under SOP recycle flow rates, average wall shear stress peaked at 20 Pa and sharply declined to 5 Pa within 0.05 s (Fig. 10). It is important to note that the impact frequencies listed in Table 4 apply only to the simplified CFD model and do not reflect those in the ISBR experiments described herein. Although recycle droplets will fall at the frequencies listed in Table 4, they will not always fall on the same coupon, since coupons are rotating. Instead, approximately one droplet will hit one coupon per rotation under SOP conditions. The lower frequency at which droplets impact the same coupon may decrease the fluid film remaining on coupons and thereby increase wall shear stress. However, measurements taken during preliminary SOP experiments indicated 80-90% humidity in the ISBR, and therefore we anticipate that coupons remain sufficiently wet between droplet impacts to maintain some level of fluid film. For that reason, we expect that the wall shear stresses depicted in Fig. 9, Fig. 10 accurately approximate shear under bulk recycle droplets but overestimate the frequency that droplets impact the same coupon.

Discussion

The ISBR versus ASTM-standardized biofilm reactor systems

As a novel reactor system, the ISBR was not previously validated. Development of a standard operating procedure was thus a prerequisite for its broader application in biofilm research. In this study, we developed the first ISBR SOP that accounts for key operational factors (influent, recycle, and rotation rates) and demonstrated that the ISBR reliably supports biofilm cultivation. Repeatability, reproducibility, and ruggedness for ISBR biofilm densities were comparable to values for ASTM-standardized systems, summarized in Table 5, and indicative of excellent repeatability and reproducibility [28,48,49]. Similarly, repeatability and reproducibility values for biovolume measurements (0.25 and 0.27, respectively) were within the range of SD values listed in Table 5, which may indicate acceptable biovolume repeatability for the developed SOP. The biovolume repeatability SD was also comparable to that for P. aeruginosa biovolumes in the CDC reactor (SDr = 0.19) [30]. However, Parker et al. (2022) [30] is the only study to our knowledge to analyze biovolume repeatability, and therefore an adequate SD threshold for biovolume repeatability is essentially unknown. Log density repeatability and reproducibility values of 1 or less are generally considered adequate for ASTM standardization of biofilm growth methods [28,48,49,[51], [52], [53], [54]]. Since recycle rate changes significantly impacted biovolume, but did not impact log density (Fig. 3, Fig. 4), we suggest that the conventional SD threshold of 1 is insufficient for biovolume repeatability or reproducibility. Findings herein suggest that biovolume repeatability SD values over 0.25 may indicate biofilms with non-repeatable or non-reproducible morphologies.

Although log density results indicated the ISBR is a rugged system for variations in rotation and recycle rate, increased influent feed rate was correlated with more biomass. This trend between increased influent feed rate and log density was also documented in ruggedness testing of the drip flow reactor [8], though it was not observed in CDC reactor ruggedness tests [6]. Flow is also droplet-based in the drip flow reactor, which may explain why this trend was shared between the two systems. Unfortunately, ruggedness test results for the different bioreactors in Table 5 could not be compared directly since media concentrations and growth periods differed. It was also curious that increased influent feed rates did not correspond to increased biovolumes, despite increases in log density. The reasons for this are unclear, likely because biovolume analyses are missing from ruggedness studies. Log density has been observed to poorly correspond to biovolume [30], which may partially explain this discrepancy. Likewise, nutrient limitations have been shown to stimulate EPS production [55], which may explain why biofilms maintained their biovolumes under low influent feed conditions. Altogether, of the three parameters tested, influent feeding rates must be most carefully controlled by ISBR users to obtain P. aeruginosa biofilms with log densities at the levels measured herein.

Additionally, ruggedness testing on biovolumes showed that increased recycle rates reduced biofilm biovolume but in a spatially-dependent manner, with the largest reductions occurring at the top of the coupon and diminishing toward the middle and bottom regions. This spatial differentiation implies that differences in local biofilm thickness and morphology across the coupon surface are affected by shear, since average wall shear stress was found to vary spatially with droplet impact location (Fig. 10). Further discussion of the relationship between shear and biovolume continues in Section 4.2. Importantly, biofilm thickness and morphology influence key physicochemical properties including substrate diffusion [56], extracellular polymeric substance composition and distribution [57], hydraulic resistance [58], and porosity [59]. Thus, biovolume changes with recycle flow rate likely indicate biofilms with significantly different characteristics, and log density measurements alone will not capture this change. We therefore argue that both biomass and morphological assessment are necessary to accurately assess a method's ruggedness, ensuring that standardized procedures capture not only microbial abundance but also structural features that influence function and stability of biofilms.

Fluid dynamics in the ISBR

Hydrodynamic shear stresses are well known to strongly impact biofilm development and persistence, with community diversity, density, thickness, adhesion, and cohesion highly dependent on the magnitude and spatial distribution of fluid forces [[60], [61], [62], [63]]. Given the influence of this parameter, we sought to characterize the shear conditions in the ISBR to improve understanding of its growth environment and compare ISBR growth conditions to those in other common biofilm reactors. Computational fluid dynamic modeling demonstrated that fluid behavior and wall shear stresses across ISBR coupons were highly variable. Average shear stress was strongly dependent on droplets’ impact location (Fig. 10), and since droplets may fall anywhere on the upper 40% of coupons, coupons are likely to experience wide variations in shear throughout multiday experiments. Furthermore, shear varies both spatially and temporally across coupons (Fig. 9), with areas of extremely high shear (25 Pa) concentrated under bulk droplets, areas of moderate shear (∼2 Pa) created by smaller droplets and fluid film movement, and areas completely free of any fluid-driven shear outside of the main fluid film (black regions in Fig. 8).

Wall shear stress distributions like those across ISBR coupons do not appear to occur in any other common biofilm reactor. For example, in a modeling study by Johnson et al. (2021), shear across CDC reactor coupons was shown to range from 0 to 1 Pa [64]. Shear has been estimated at 7.3 Pa in rotating annular reactors [65], 0.32 Pa in tissue culture plate reactors [42], and 0.29 Pa in drip flow reactors [10]. Of course, wall shear stress in all cases depends on multiple factors (e.g., fluid flow rates, rotational speeds, biofilm properties, etc.), and therefore the aforementioned systems may provide higher wall shear stresses than those listed here. Regardless, the ISBR provides a unique, wide-ranging mixture of shear conditions, with some coupon areas experiencing extremely high shear and others near zero. ISBR shear conditions appear most similar to those in a cooling tower, as originally intended by the ISBR designers [20], but may recapitulate conditions in any system wherein flow is predominantly droplet-based. Biofilm growth was mainly concentrated under the droplet pathway (Fig. S1) and therefore the extremely high shear provided by the bulk droplet does not appear to have significantly impeded biofilm growth, though future studies should assess if growth patterns change over longer (>5 day) growth periods.

CFD results also helped interpret ruggedness results, particularly regarding biofilm biovolume. We initially interpreted the recycle rate-dependent changes in biovolume (Fig. 4) as potentially resulting from increased average wall shear stress at higher recycle flow rates. However, results in Fig. 10 contradict this interpretation: the average shear under bulk recycle droplets is consistent, regardless of flow rate. Instead, droplet impact frequency appears to be the major driver of declining biovolume. This suggests that the temporal pattern of shear exposure, rather than shear magnitude alone, is important for decreases in biovolume. Since log densities were unaffected by recycle rate changes, we conclude that a higher frequency in localized shear stress is consistent with thinner, more compact biofilm structures rather than biomass loss. Additionally, the decrease in biofilm biovolume under high recycle was more pronounced at the top of the coupon than at the center (Fig. 4), which contrasts with model predictions indicating that average shear stresses were substantially higher when droplets struck the center of the coupon (6° coupon) than the top (12° coupon; Fig. 10). Therefore, as droplets fall more frequently, the center of coupons will more often experience 20 Pa wall shear stress compared to 10 Pa at the top. However, the CFD model captures peak shear events and impact frequency, not the duration or cumulative shear exposure experienced by the biofilm. This discrepancy may suggest that peak shear stress magnitude alone does not govern biovolume response in the ISBR, particularly given the intermittently wetted, droplet-driven nature of this system. Multiple studies have shown that higher shear stresses cause biofilms to decrease in thickness while increasing in cell density [11,66], however, these studies are largely based on steady or continuous flow systems. Results presented herein indicate that such relationships may not translate directly to intermittently wetted, droplet-driven reactors like the ISBR, and biomass biovolume might be governed by shear timing, intermittency, and exposure patterns rather than average shear stress magnitude alone.

The SOP's ruggedness to changing rotation rates was also verified by the CFD model, as preliminary results showed negligible changes in shear stress with changing rotation rates. Since increasing influent feed rates were correlated with higher log density (Fig. 3), but had no impact on shear stress or biovolume (Table 3), we can conclude that greater nutrient availability leads to overall higher log densities but shear stress remains the driving force for biofilm biovolume characteristics.

Lastly, modeling the presence of biofilm was beyond the scope of our CFD model, despite its likely substantial impact on fluid dynamics. Biofilm may redirect primary recycle droplets along new flow paths, exposing different regions of the coupon to higher shear stresses and previously inaccessible nutrients. In addition, biofilm elasticity and porosity are likely to dampen wall shear stress on the outer biofilm-fluid interface, potentially creating areas of low- and high-density biofilm within this boundary region.

The ISBR as a multidomain biofilm cultivation system

Multidomain biofilms are ubiquitous in the real world but are rarely represented in lab environments, in part because it is difficult to maintain stable populations from multiple domains over long growth periods [[67], [68], [69]]. As a result, most laboratory studies grow MDBs in batch systems, such as multiwell plates, for sub-10-day periods [[69], [70], [71], [72]]. However, biofilm morphology, extracellular polymeric substances (EPS), populations, resistance to antimicrobials, and many other characteristics differ as biofilms increase in age or experience fluid flow [[73], [74], [75], [76], [77], [78]], and all will impact experimental conclusions. For example, Pan et al. (2022) observed that biofilms growing under turbulent fluid flow had higher EPS-associated protein concentrations than biofilms growing in transitional or laminar flow regimes [78]. EPS-associated proteins are linked with antimicrobial protection, resource capture, and enzymatic activity, among other functions [79], and therefore shifts in their concentration may monumentally impact biofilm characteristics. Furthermore, microorganisms in older, established biofilms will express different genes than those in younger, developing biofilms [80]. This is also certain to impact experimental outcomes and may also affect population stability [81]. In short, flow conditions and biofilm age are important factors in MDB studies, and the method detailed herein, as well as the unique growth conditions in the ISBR, provide a valuable springboard for development of longer-term MDB growth methods in flow-through systems.

As an MDB case use, we grew P. aeruginosa and R. mucilaginosa for an approximately month-long period in the ISBR and demonstrated that both yeast and bacterial populations were stable over testing period (Fig. 6). Although this timeframe is longer than that in most MDB studies [[69], [70], [71], [72]], it is still short relative to the lifetimes of most real-world MDBs. Future work should therefore evaluate cross-domain population stability in the ISBR over longer timeframes, as there is evidence that “engineered MDBs”—i.e., MDBs cultivated from pre-defined species, like the cooling tower and yeast-bacterial MDBs described herein—are less stable over time than “natural MDBs”, or those cultivated from naturally-established MDB samples [82,83]. Despite this, engineered MDBs often have more repeatable and reproducible characteristics than natural MDBs [84]. Our results also support this trend: both MDBs had highly repeatable log densities across multiple domains and time points (Fig. 5, Fig. 6). Furthermore, engineered MDBs may still recapitulate valuable characteristics of natural MDBs. For example, L. pneumophila densities on ISBR coupons (Fig. 5) were similar to those of biofilms in real cooling tower systems [85,86]. Altogether, MDBs grown via the methods detailed herein may not exhibit the same nuances in morphology or communities as natural MDBs, but exhibit the robust repeatability expected for engineered biofilms with the added benefit of longer population stability than that of most other engineered MDBs.

Conclusions

In this study, a standard operating procedure for P. aeruginosa biofilm growth in a novel bioreactor was developed and ruggedness tested. The ISBR SOP had excellent repeatability in a single lab and was rugged for rotation and recycle rate changes in terms of log density, which suggested excellent reproducibility across multiple laboratories. However, biofilm morphology analyses indicated that the SOP was not rugged for recycle rate changes over the range tested, as higher recycle rates resulted in thinner biofilms. A wall shear stress CFD model corroborated this finding, as shear stress was elevated more frequently across coupons at higher recycle rates. These findings highlight the importance of evaluating biofilm morphology in addition to log density, as log density analyses alone would have falsely indicated that recycle rate changes did not impact the biofilm. Lastly, two methods for growth of unique multidomain biofilms in the ISBR were developed. The repeatability of both methods was excellent, and both yeast and bacterial populations remained stable over an approximately month-long growth period. Altogether, this study establishes the ISBR as a robust platform for biofilm cultivation.
