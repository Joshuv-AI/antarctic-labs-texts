Evaluating the role of plan complexity metrics in online adaptive radiotherapy for pancreatic cancer patients
Authors: Samuele Cavinato, Matteo Galetto, Alessandro Scaggion, Andrea Bettinelli, Matteo Nardini, Giuditta Chiloiro, Angela Romano, Marta Paiusco, Luca Boldrini, Lorenzo Placidi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo systematically investigate the behavior of plan complexity metrics (PCMs) in an MR‐Linac online adaptive radiotherapy (oART) workflow for pancreatic cancer, and to evaluate their potential as surrogate indicators of delivery accuracy.MethodsThirty‐seven patients with locally advanced pancreatic cancer were retrospectively analyzed, yielding 222 MR‐Linac plans (37 reference and 185 delivered fractions). Fifteen PCMs were extracted from plans generated with three optimizers: Penalty, Objectives and Constraints, and A3i (current clinical practice). Plan specific quality assurance (PSQA) has been performed through an independent dose calculation algorithm. Statistical analyses included: (i) inter‐optimizer comparisons (ANOVA and mixed‐effects models), (ii) variance decomposition of adapted‐plan complexity metrics using linear mixed‐effects models (LMEMs), and (iii) evaluation of PSQA stability using statistical process control (SPC) and leave‐one‐patient‐out (LOPO) cross‐validation.ResultsOptimizer choice strongly influenced plan complexity. The Penalty optimizer generated higher‐complexity plans, whereas Objectives and Constraints and A3i produced more modulation‐efficient configurations with fewer small, low‐MU segments. Variance decomposition identified a subset of metrics that exhibited consistent behavior across all optimizers, serving as robust descriptors independent of the algorithm. Metrics dominated by between‐patient variance (σ2 between) emerged as reliable surrogates for patient‐specific complexity Tongue & Groove Index, Average Leaf Gap and Number of Active Leaves consistently showed high between‐patient contributions (σ2 between > 74%) among others. In contrast, metrics related to low‐MU segments (SegMU < 5 and SegMU < 5 [%]) were dominated by within‐patient variance (σ2 within), with A3i showing the most pronounced fluctuations (85.8% and 84.8%, respectively). These descriptors are therefore more sensitive to plan‐specific or optimizer‐related stochasticity than to stable patient factors. SPC analyses demonstrated that the current adaptive workflow is robustly stable for most patients: 11 of 13 never experienced a fraction below the tolerance level (TL), and 12 of 13 never exceeded the action level (AL), even when thresholds were dynamically recalculated within the LOPO‐CV.ConclusionThis study provides the first systematic assessment of PCMs in MR‐guided oART, demonstrating optimizer‐specific complexity signatures, predominant inter‐patient variability, and the predictive value of selected metrics for delivery accuracy. Although limited to a single tumor site and workflow, the methodology supports the development of institution‐specific, complexity‐aware scorecards to enhance adaptive planning and quality assurance.Keywords: MRI‐guided radiotherapy, MR‐Linac, online adaptive radiotherapy, pancreatic cancer, patient‐specific quality assurance, plan complexity metrics

INTRODUCTION

The integration of magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) with linear accelerators (MR‐Linacs) has enabled the clinical implementation of MRI‐guided radiotherapy (MRgRT). 1 , 2 A major innovation of this technology is online adaptive radiotherapy (oART), which allows treatment plans to be re‐optimized on the patient according to daily anatomy. This capability is particularly relevant for pancreatic cancer, where tumor position and the configuration of surrounding organs at risk (OARs), such as stomach, duodenum, and small bowel, may vary considerably between fractions. 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 The superior soft‐tissue contrast of MRI facilitates more accurate target definition and motion management, while also enabling planning target volume (PTV) margin reduction and thereby improving the therapeutic ratio. 7 , 8 , 9 , 10

Plans generated within oART workflows typically rely on intensity‐modulated radiation therapy (IMRT) and inverse planning algorithms. These techniques inherently produce highly modulated fluence patterns and complex multileaf collimator (MLC) motions. 11 , 12 While such complexity supports conformal dose distributions, it also raises concerns regarding deliverability, robustness, and overall plan quality. 13 , 14 Established professional guidelines, such as those of AAPM TG‐218, recommend pre‐treatment patient‐specific quality assurance (PSQA) through direct measurements or independent dose calculations. 15 , 16 , 17 In the oART setting, measurement‐based QA is not feasible within the adaptive workflow, since the patient remains on the couch during the entire process and strict time constraints apply. 18 , 19 Independent dose calculations, by contrast, can be integrated into the online workflow and are commonly adopted as secondary dose verification in the OART context.

Complementary QA approaches have also been explored. Machine log‐file analysis enables near‐real‐time dose reconstruction during or after delivery, providing valuable information on actual treatment execution. However, being inherently post‐hoc, it cannot serve as pre‐treatment verification and may not fully capture all delivery uncertainties, particularly in the presence of magnetic fields. 20 , 21 Independent Monte Carlo (MC) dose simulations can account for these effects and offer accurate dose calculations, yet their computational cost limits their real‐time applicability and they do not verify the actual mechanical delivery of the plan. 22 These limitations highlight the need for complementary strategies that are rapid, predictive, and tailored to the adaptive workflow.

Plan complexity metrics (PCMs) have emerged as potential surrogate indicators of deliverability. They quantify features such as modulation degree, aperture shape variability, and MLC travel. Although no single metric has proven universally predictive, combining multiple descriptors has been proposed as a way to identify plans that may be less robust or more prone to delivery errors 23 , 24 , 25 Recent contributions include methods to limit excessive complexity during optimization, 23 the development of standardized software for metric extraction, 26 and surveys documenting the increasing clinical interest in complexity monitoring. 14

Despite this growing body of literature, the behavior of PCMs in MR‐Linac–based oART remains largely unexplored. In particular, it is not yet known how complexity evolves across multiple adapted fractions for the same patient, nor to what extent patient‐specific anatomical conditions influence the resulting plan characteristics. Understanding these aspects is essential if PCMs are to be used not only as general indicators of plan deliverability, but also as instruments to support adaptive decision making at the fraction level.

The purpose of this work is to provide a systematic assessment of plan complexity in the context of MR‐guided online adaptive radiotherapy. This study should be interpreted as a complexity‐oriented analysis embedded in the real clinical oART QA workflow, rather than as a comprehensive delivery validation study. As a clinically relevant test case, we retrospectively analyzed pancreatic cancer patients treated with stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT) on a 0.35 T MR‐Linac, a setting where daily adaptation is often indispensable. Using a comprehensive set of metrics extracted with a freely available tool, 26 we evaluate complexity across different optimizers, quantify its variability within and between patients, and test its association with PSQA outcomes using statistical process control and leave‐one‐patient‐out (LOPO) cross‐validation analysis. By doing so, we aim to establish a methodological foundation for integrating plan complexity into quality assurance strategies tailored to the specific challenges of online adaptive radiotherapy.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

MRIdian system

The MRIdian Linac (ViewRay Systems, Inc.) is a hybrid 0.35 T MRI‐guided radiotherapy platform that integrates a 6 MV flattening‐filter‐free (FFF) photon beam with a transverse magnetic field (oriented inferior–superior, orthogonal to both beam and MLC motion). The MLC consists of double‐stacked, double‐focused, and staggered tungsten leaves. Each leaf measures 0.83 cm at the isocenter, yielding an effective leaf width of 0.415 cm with a source‐to‐axis distance of 90 cm. The system delivers at a fixed dose rate of 600 MU/min 1.

Collected dataset and oART workflow

This retrospective study included 37 patients with locally advanced, inoperable pancreatic cancer treated between March 2020 and October 2023 with stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT) on the MRIdian Linac. Two prescription levels were used (50 Gy or 62.5 Gy in five fractions). All patients underwent an online adaptive workflow (Figure 1).

Schematic of the online adaptive radiotherapy (oART) workflow on the MRIdian MR‐Linac.

Each patient received an MRI simulation using a balanced SSFP (TrueFISP) sequence for target and OAR delineation, followed by a CT simulation (Siemens SOMATOM go.Open Pro) with identical immobilization for electron density mapping. For the first treatment fraction, a reference plan (f0) was generated in the treatment planning system (TPS) while the adaptation is performed at the treatment delivery system (TDS). During the accrual period, three successive optimizers were employed: Penalty (P), Objectives & Constraints (O&C), and A3i (A detailed description of the optimization algorithms is provided in the Supplementary Material). Dose distributions were computed with the primary Monte Carlo engine (Kinetic Monte Carlo—KMC). 27 , 28

Before each treatment fraction, a 3D TRUFI MRI was acquired to verify patient setup and daily anatomy. The planning CT was deformably registered to this image to create the daily updated electron density map of the patient. If required, target volumes and OAR contours were adapted. In earlier TDS versions (which included P and O&C optimizers), the adaptive process began with an automatic prediction of the reference plan on the daily anatomy, followed by either a weighted IMRT optimization or manual re‐optimization if constraints were not met. In the current TDS version (adopting A3i optimizer), both a fast re‐optimization (fixed number of segments) and a fully re‐optimized plan (variable number of segments) are automatically generated, with manual re‐optimization available as a fallback. Fast re‐optimization is never selected in clinical setting and all the online adapted plans considered in this study have been fully or manually re‐optimized. Once plan objectives were satisfied, the final dose distribution was recalculated with the KMC engine before treatment. The delivered plan at fraction i then served as the reference for fraction i+1. Patients were stratified into three groups according to the optimizer used (Table 1).

All patients were treated with a VMAT‐like delivery technique as described in Placidi et al. 29 This method approximates rotational delivery on the MRIdian system by distributing multiple static IMRT fields around the target. In this cohort, each plan used ∼25 fields with 10° angular spacing, excluding directions intersecting the patient's arms and the couch edges.

For each A3i plan (group G3), PSQA was performed by comparing the final TPS KMC dose distribution with that recalculated by ZEUS, an independent Monte Carlo secondary dose engine used in our clinical MRIdian workflow, using global gamma analysis (2%G, 2 mm), hereafter referred to as PR%(2%G, 2 mm). 30 , 31 ZEUS is based on a modified implementation of the DPM Monte Carlo code, extended for magnetic‐field electron transport, and was adopted as the clinically feasible MC‐based secondary verification approach for online adaptive treatments, 28 , 32 while periodic 3D‐array measurements on representative pancreas plans were maintained as constancy checks.

Plan complexity metrics (PCMs)

For every plan, 15 plan complexity metrics (PCMs) were extracted using the publicly available UCoMX (Universal Complexity Metrics Extractor) package. 26 General plan characteristics included the average number of active leaf pairs (LeafNum), number of fields (NFields), and segments per field (NSegField). To quantify the plan modulation, the normalized monitor units (MUcGy), the Modulation Complexity Score (MCS), the total number and relative proportion of very low‐MU segments (< 5 MU) were computed (SegMU < 5 and SegMU < 5%, respectively).

MLC aperture and motion characteristics were assessed through Average Leaf Gap (ALG), Equivalent Field Size (EFS), Small Aperture Score 10 mm (SAS10mm), Tongue‐and‐Groove index (TG), Plan Irregularity (PI), Edge Metric (EM), Aperture Area Variability (AAV), and Leaf Sequence Variability (LSV). These metrics are widely used in the literature to characterize plan modulation, aperture geometry, and delivery complexity, and their clinical relevance has been comprehensively reviewed elsewhere. 13 , 24 , 33

Finally, each metric was computed on a segment basis and then averaged across all fields to yield plan‐averaged values. The details of PCM calculation can be found in https://zenodo.org/records/15673571.

Statistical comparison across optimizers

The complexity signatures of the three optimizer groups (Penalty, O&C, A3i) were first compared on the reference plan (f0) and then across adapted fractions (f1–f5). For f0, one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed on each plan complexity metric, followed by Tukey's honestly significant difference (HSD) test for pairwise post‐hoc comparisons. For adapted fractions, a linear mixed‐effects model (LMEM) was fitted with the optimizer as the fixed effect and a random intercept for the patient, in order to account for statistical dependence among repeated fractions within the same individual. A3i, representing the current clinical practice, was selected as the reference level for comparisons. The global significance of the optimizer effect within the LMEM framework was assessed using the Likelihood Ratio Test (LRT). Since multiple complexity metrics were tested, we controlled for multiplicity across the 15 metrics by applying the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) adjustment to the p‐values of the global tests. The correction was applied separately for the f0 analysis (ANOVA) and for the adapted‐fractions analysis (LMEM). Significance was assessed at α = 0.05. All models were implemented in Python (statsmodels v0.14.0).

Variance decomposition through linear mixed‐effects models

To assess inter‐ and intra‐patient variability in plan complexity metrics for the adapted plans created with each TDS optimizer (f1–f5) we applied an approach based on linear mixed‐effects models with a random intercept for each patient. These models account for repeated measures across fractions of the same individual while estimating population‐level effects. In this case, each patient has a random intercept capturing how that patient deviates from the population mean, and the fraction‐level residual error represents fluctuations around the patient's average. We assume both the random intercepts and residuals are normally distributed. Variance components were estimated via restricted maximum likelihood (REML), yielding two components: a between‐patient variance ( representing the systematic differences across patients, and a within‐patient variance (σwithin2) accounting for the day‐to‐day fluctuations across fractions for the same patient. This decomposition allows us to quantify whether variability in plan complexity is driven predominantly by systematic differences between patients or by daily fluctuations within the same patient.

Statistical process control of PSQA

Statistical process control (SPC) was applied to PR% (2%G,2 mm) values for A3i‐optimized plans, following the AAPM TG‐218 framework. 34 The tolerance level (TL) was defined as the mean PR% minus 2.660 times the moving range, as appropriate for individual–moving‐range charts. The action level (AL) was based on the mean and standard deviation across delivered fractions, with a nominal PR% target of 100%. Plans with PR% below AL were flagged as potentially non‐robust. This framework enables both retrospective benchmarking and prospective identification of out‐of‐control fractions.

Process stability evaluation via LOPO‐CV

To evaluate the robustness of the PSQA procedure, a leave‐one‐patient‐out cross‐validation (LOPO‐CV) procedure was implemented. This approach was chosen to simulate the real‐world clinical scenario where a metric is tested on a new, previously unseen patient, thereby preventing data leakage. The LOPO‐CV procedure consisted of the following steps for each of the N iterations (where N is the total number of patients):

Data Split: One patient was designated as the test set, while the remaining N‐1 patients constituted the training set.

The primary endpoint of this analysis was the fraction of “in‐control” patients, serving as a measure of the clinical process's robustness.

Variance decomposition through linear mixed‐effects models

The variance‐component analysis across all three TDS optimizers—Penalty, O&C, and A3I—reveals both shared characteristics and significant optimizer‐dependent differences in plan variability (Table 4). A subset of metrics demonstrated consistent behavior across all optimizers, proving to be robust descriptors regardless of the algorithm. Those dominated by between‐patient variance (σ2 between) are reliable surrogates for patient‐specific complexity. For instance, metrics like TG, ALG, NFields, and LeafNum consistently showed high between‐patient contributions (e.g., σ2 between > 74%). For the A3I optimizer, this was particularly evident for LeafNum (81.5%), NFields (82.2%), and TG (84.8%), suggesting these descriptors are strongly influenced by stable factors like anatomical configuration or planner choices. Conversely, metrics related to low‐MU segments (SegMU < 5 and SegMU < 5 [%]) were consistently dominated by within‐patient variance with A3I showing the most extreme fluctuations (85.8% and 84.8%, respectively). These descriptors are therefore sensitive to plan‐specific or optimizer‐related stochasticity rather than stable patient factors. The most insightful distinctions arose from metrics whose variance profiles shifted significantly between optimizers. A clear trend was observed where variance progressively shifted from between‐patient to within‐patient when moving from the Penalty to the O&C and A3I optimizers. For SAS10mm, the within‐patient variance contribution grew from 27.4% with Penalty to become the dominant component with A3I at 61.7%. A similar pattern was evident for EM, where σ2 within increased from 23.5% to 57.1%. AAV showed a particularly dramatic shift: it was strongly patient‐dominated under Penalty (80.4% σ2 between) but became almost perfectly balanced under O&C and A3I, with within‐patient variance rising to 47.4% and 40.9%, respectively.

Following the AAPM TG‐218 approach TL and AL resulted in 98.50% and 97.76%, respectively. The LOPO‐CV analysis (Figure 2) demonstrated that the adaptive workflow is robustly stable for the large majority of patients: 11 out of 13 patients never experienced a fraction below the tolerance level (TL), and 12 out of 13 patients never crossed the action level (AL), even when thresholds were recalculated dynamically at each iteration. This confirms that out‐of‐control events were confined to a very limited subset of patients.

Nevertheless, inspection of the relationship between PCMs and PSQA outcomes revealed strong heteroscedasticity, at least for some predictors (Figure 3). Considering MU/cGy, TG, and PI, it is evident how patients with lower‐complexity plans consistently achieved high and stable PR% (2%G, 2 mm) values, while higher‐complexity plans showed markedly greater outcome variability. Failures thus occurred almost exclusively in the high‐complexity region, suggesting that PCMs may help to identify the subset of fractions at greater risk of PSQA failure. While overall correlations remain moderate, the stratification into quartiles of metric values shows that higher‐complexity bins are associated with both lower medians and wider variability of PSQA outcomes.

DISCUSSION

This study represents, to our knowledge, the first systematic evaluation of plan complexity metrics (PCMs) within an online adaptive MR‐guided radiotherapy workflow. By applying a comprehensive panel of PCMs to a cohort of pancreatic SBRT treatments delivered on a 0.35 T MR‐Linac, we characterized optimizer‐specific complexity fingerprints, quantified inter‐ and intra‐patient variability, and linked PCMs to patient‐specific QA outcomes. These findings underscore both the potential and the challenges of incorporating complexity‐aware monitoring into clinical oART practice.

Significant differences among optimizers were observed in both reference (f0) and adapted (f1–f5) plans. The Penalty optimizer produced more complex modulation patterns, with a higher number of segments per field and a greater proportion of low‐MU apertures. In contrast, the more recent Objectives and Constraints and A3i optimizers generated convergent complexity profiles with reduced modulation. These findings highlight the need to interpret PCMs within the context of the underlying optimization strategy, as metrics may capture algorithmic design features as much as anatomical or clinical requirements.

Applying linear mixed‐effects modeling to adapted plans created with the three TDS optimizers revealed that between‐patient variance was the dominant source of variability for most metrics for each optimizer, often exceeding 74%. This suggests that baseline anatomy and target–OAR configuration largely dictate a patient's intrinsic “complexity signature,” which then remains relatively stable throughout adaptive fractions. A smaller subset of descriptors (e.g., SegMU < 5, SAS10mm, EM) exhibited larger within‐patient components, indicating sensitivity to fraction‐level planning dynamics or optimizer stochasticity. These results support the notion that PCMs can be stratified into those reflecting anatomy‐driven complexity and those reflecting process‐driven variability, each with different potential roles in adaptive QA.

SPC analysis confirmed that most patients remained consistently within tolerance and action thresholds, with out‐of‐control events confined to a small minority. The LOPO‐CV framework demonstrated that process stability can be evaluated prospectively by recalculating thresholds on a per‐iteration basis, simulating real‐world deployment (Figure 2). Figure 3 illustrates the substantial overlap between complexity metrics and PSQA outcomes, highlighting the limited ability of the investigated descriptors to fully capture delivery performance. Although patient 06 exhibited recurrent PSQA action‐level events, no corresponding anomalies were observed in the evaluated complexity metrics (Figure 3), suggesting that factors not captured by the current complexity descriptors may contribute to delivery performance in individual cases. In fact, collected data revealed clear heteroscedasticity: patients with low‐complexity plans achieved uniformly high QA pass rates, whereas higher‐complexity plans were associated with increased variability and occasional failures. This suggests that complexity‐aware monitoring may complement conventional QA by flagging fractions at higher risk of delivery inaccuracy.

Previous studies have addressed plan complexity in conventional IMRT/VMAT 35– , 42 and in MRI‐guided RT 43 , 44 but have largely focused on average plan features or offline QA workflows. Although other works have reported complexity metrics for MRI‐guided platforms, 22 , 45 , 46 direct cross‐platform comparisons are inherently hindered by differences in delivery hardware, optimization software, and metric calculation methods. To provide a contextual reference while mitigating some of these confounding factors, we compared our findings with those of Khan et al., 22 who utilized the same treatment platform (MRIdian) and included a subset of pancreatic cancer cases, focusing exclusively on the offline phase. Specifically, the complexity of the plans generated during our offline phase (fraction 0) across the three groups (G1‐G3) was comparable to Khan et al.’s results when evaluated in terms of SAS10mm ([0.084; 0.130], SD range [32.3%; 102.4%] vs. 0.09, SD 98.9%), LSV ([0.65; 0.69], SD range [5.8%; 7.6%] vs. 0.72, SD 9.9%), and PI/Aperture irregularity ([3.00; 3.40], SD range [13.5%; 19.9%] vs. 3.86, SD 46.4%). Conversely, our plans demonstrated a higher degree of complexity in terms of ALG/ALPO ([2.18; 2.42], SD range [18.2%; 23.6%] vs. 6.5, SD 34.3%), MCS ([0.36; 0.43], SD range [13.9%; 14.3%] vs. 0.78, SD 29.9%), and AAV ([0.51; 0.59], SD range [9.8%; 13.6%] vs. 1.04, SD 26.4%).

Our study therefore focused on system‐specific characterization within the MRIdian OART pipeline by performing fraction‐level decomposition of inter‐ versus intra‐patient variability, linking PCMs to on‐table QA outcomes in a clinical oART pipeline and implementing a LOPO‐CV scheme to mimic prospective application. In contrast to prior reports emphasizing the average efficiency of new optimizers, our results identify which individual PCMs may serve as actionable indicators of robustness in the adaptive setting.

This study has some limitations. The dataset is modest in size and restricted to a single tumor site and platform. Identified thresholds should therefore be considered as proof‐of‐concept rather than generalizable cutoffs. The study did not include a comprehensive delivery validation analysis on the adapted fraction, since its major intent was to embed the complexity‐oriented analysis in the real clinical oART QA workflow. Therefore, a dedicated follow‐up study could explicitly test whether the complexity metrics identified here, and the associated thresholds, also correlate with adapted fraction measurement‐based QA results, thereby providing an additional external validation of their clinical relevance. Furthermore, our analysis was retrospective and based on plan complexity rather than direct dosimetric failure modes. Prospective, multi‐institutional studies are needed to validate complexity thresholds, evaluate their impact on clinical decision‐making, and explore integration with automated QA pipelines. Despite these limitations, our results demonstrate that PCMs capture meaningful variability at both inter‐ and intra‐patient levels and may support the PSQA framework.

CONCLUSIONS

This study provides the first systematic assessment of PCMs in MR‐guided oART, demonstrating optimizer‐specific complexity signatures, predominant inter‐patient variability, and the predictive value of select metrics for delivery accuracy. Although limited to a single tumor site and workflow, the methodology supports the development of institution‐specific, complexity‐aware scorecards to enhance adaptive planning and quality assurance.
