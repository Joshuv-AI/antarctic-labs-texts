Novel Concept of “Right-at-the-right-place” Transplantation: Rejuvenating Effects of Mouse Fascial Fibroblasts
Authors: Ryoga Wake, Kento Takaya, Rikako Matsuura, Shigeki Sakai, Keisuke Okabe, Noriko Aramaki-Hattori, Kazuo Kishi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Live autologous cultured fibroblasts improve sagging and wrinkles when injected into the dermis; however, their diversity and differences in efficacy depending on the skin layer to which they are transplanted remain unclear. This study investigated the behavior and effects of dermal- and fascial-derived fibroblasts from mouse fetuses on the dermis and fascia.Methods:The collagen-producing capacity of fibroblasts from the shallow (dermis) and deep (fascia) skin layers of fetal and adult C57BL/6-Tg(CAG-EGFP) mice was compared using western blotting and real-time polymerase chain reaction. Contractile capacity was investigated using a collagen contractility assay and an ex vivo mouse skin model. We transplanted these cells into the shallow and deep layers of the back skin of aged C57BL/6 mice and observed the engraftment rate and histological changes in the skin. The anti-inflammatory effects of these cells were also compared.Results:Fascial fibroblasts from embryonic day 17 fetal mice exhibited higher collagen production and contractile capacity than papillary fibroblasts from adult mice. The transplanted fascial and dermal cells showed high engraftment rates in the deep and shallow (injected near the layer from which they were derived) layers, respectively. Collagen fiber density was highest when embryonic day 17 fascial fibroblasts were transplanted into the deep layer, and an anti-inflammatory effect was observed.Conclusions:Dermal fibroblast transplantation improves the phenotype of aging skin via collagen synthesis and contraction, with greater efficacy when transplanted into a layer consistent with the skin layer from which the primary cells are harvested.

Takeaways

Question: What are the differences in the effects of autologous cultured fibroblast transplantation for rejuvenation depending on the diversity of the cells and the skin layer to which they are transplanted?

Findings: Fascial fibroblasts from embryonic day 17 fetal mice showed higher collagen production and contractile capacity. The highest improvement in engraftment rate and collagen fiber density was observed when embryonic day 17 fascial fibroblasts were implanted in the deepest layers.

Meaning: Dermal fibroblast transplantation has greater efficacy when transplanted into a layer consistent with the skin layer from which primary cells are harvested.

INTRODUCTION

Dermal fibroblasts are involved in skin immune response, wound healing, and communication with the nervous and vascular systems.1 As skin ages, fibroblasts become dysfunctional, impairing collagen production.2,3 Senescent fibroblasts undergo irreversible cell proliferation arrest and enhance senescence-associated secretory phenotype release, promoting skin aging.4,5 These cellular changes lead to clinical signs such as loss of firmness, wrinkles, and sagging.6 To ameliorate this problem, cell therapy, involving the injection of young fibroblasts into the skin, has gained attention.7 Studies have evaluated various skin symptoms, including reduced wrinkling, scars, and atrophies.8,9 However, there is no consensus on which skin areas should be harvested for fibroblasts. Fibroblasts divide during growth and synthesize substrates and, unlike epithelial cells, can migrate within the substrate layer.10 Not all transplanted cells settle in the recipient area, affecting therapeutic outcomes. Older donor fibroblasts develop senescence-associated secretory phenotype, showing aging and reduced migration.11 The collection and transplantation of appropriate fibroblasts to suitable skin sites is crucial.

Fibroblasts in the human dermis are classified into reticular and papillary layers.12 In mice, dermal and mesenchymal fibroblasts from the dermis and fascia serve distinct wound-healing roles.13 Papillary dermis fibroblasts repair wounds and reduce scarring, whereas fascial fibroblasts form scar tissue as myofibroblasts. Recent analyses of dermal fibroblast development have shown that these types of fibroblasts differentiate through different driver genes and localize in the upper and lower layers.14 We aimed to investigate dermal- and fascial-derived fibroblasts from mouse fetuses on embryonic day 13 (E13) and embryonic day 17 (E17).

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Ethical Consideration

The research protocol was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at Keio University School of Medicine. All experiments were conducted in accordance with the institutional guidelines for animal experiments at Keio University.

Cell Culture

Cells were collected from C57BL/6-Tg(CAG-EGFP) mice (Sankyo Lab Service, Tokyo, Japan) at 13 and 17 days of gestation and at 8 weeks of age. Pregnant mice were checked for vaginal plugs twice daily, and fetuses were designated as E0 when vaginal plugs were observed. Each recovered skin tissue sample was divided into dermis and fascia under a microscope, and fibroblasts were obtained using the explant method.

Cells were cultured in low-glucose Dulbecco modified Eagle medium (Wako Pure Chemical Industries, Osaka, Japan) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Thermo Fisher Scientific) in a humidified incubator at 5% CO2 and 37°C. The medium was changed every 2–3 days.

Immunocytochemistry

Cells were placed on glass slides, fixed in acetone for 5 minutes at room temperature (15–25°C), and dried completely before staining. Cells were incubated overnight at 4°C with anti-DPP4/CD26 antibody (1:100; Protein Tech, Rosemont, IL) diluted 1:100 in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). After washing 3 times with PBS, the slides were incubated with Alexa Fluor 555–conjugated goat antihuman antibody (Thermo Fisher Scientific) diluted 1:2000 in PBS for 1 hour at room temperature. After incubation, the nuclei were washed thrice with PBS and counterstained for nuclear visualization using ProLong Gold Antifade Mountant (Thermo Fisher Scientific) containing 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI). All slides were viewed under a confocal laser scanning microscope (FLUO-VIEW FV3000; Olympus Life Science, Tokyo, Japan).

Collagen Contraction Assay

A mixture containing dermal papillary fibroblasts from 8-week-old C57BL/6-Tg(CAG-EGFP) mice, E13 dermal (E13D) fibroblasts, and E17 fascial (E17F) fibroblasts (5 × 105 cells/mL each) and a control mixture containing no cells were mixed with Cell Matrix Type I-A (Nitta Gelatin, Tokyo, Japan). The cells were seeded in 12-well plates and incubated at 37°C for 1 hour. After adding 0.5 mL of cell culture medium and incubating for 2 days, 2,3-butanedione monoxime was added. The collagen gel was peeled off the well wall using a sterile spatula, and the change in collagen gel size over time was measured using the change in the bottom area of the culture plate as an indicator. Image analysis was performed using ImageJ software (version 1.53p; National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD).

Ex Vivo Tissue Contraction Assay

Skin from the backs of 8-week-old C57BL/6 mice (Sankyo Lab Service) was collected in 1.5-cm squares from all layers. The specimen was inverted and pinned onto a polyethylene board. A 5-mm square of green acrylic paint was marked on the fascial side of each tissue. Within the marked area, 100 µL of a mixture containing E13D and E17F fibroblasts (5 × 105 cells/mL each) was injected. The same volume of PBS was injected as a control. Tissues were maintained in a humidified incubator in low-glucose Dulbecco modified Eagle medium supplemented with whole skin 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin/streptomycin at 5% CO2 and 37°C. The areas of the markings were photographed, and image analysis was performed using the ImageJ software.

In Vivo Cell Transplantation Assay

C57BL/6-Tg(CAG-EGFP) mouse–derived E13D and E17F fibroblasts (1 × 107) suspended in 100 µL of PBS were injected intradermally and subcutaneously into the back of old (90 weeks old), male C57BL/6 mice. A single cell type was injected into a single layer of each mouse. Cells were transplanted into a 5-mm square area on the back of the mouse, and 5 injection sites were established per mouse. Mice with the same cell type and transplanted layer were grouped, with 5 mice in each group. Skin samples were collected from the injection site after 1, 3, 5, 7, and 28 days. For tissue observation, the skin was fixed overnight by immersion in 4% paraformaldehyde. Paraffin-embedded specimens were sliced into 7-µm-thick sections and mounted on the glass slides. The samples were then dried overnight at room temperature to allow them to adhere to the slides. The slides were then deparaffinized using 2 xylene exchanges at room temperature (5 min each). The slides were rehydrated by transferring them to 100% ethanol in 2 exchanges (3 min each), and then transferred once to 95%, 70%, and 50% ethanol and water (3 min each) at room temperature. Masson trichrome staining and hematoxylin and eosin staining were performed using standard protocols.

Collagen Density Analysis

To quantify collagen density, the background of the Masson trichrome images was subtracted (n = 3–5, with at least 3 microscopic fields of view per group), and the color deconvolution plug-in of ImageJ software was used. Briefly, red, green, blue images were separated into three 8-bit background images for each color, and the blue component was evaluated to quantify the collagen intensity in the region of interest (ROI). Rectangular ROIs (0.01 mm2) were randomly assigned to areas deeper than 200 µm from the epidermis and not containing skin appendages below the basement membrane, and the integration density was measured as the sum of pixel values in the selected ROIs.

Immunohistochemistry

Paraffin-embedded specimens were sliced into 7-µm-thick sections and mounted on the glass slides. The specimens were allowed to dry overnight at room temperature (15–25°C) to adhere to the slides. Xylene was replaced twice at room temperature (5 min each), and the slides were deparaffinized. Slides were transferred twice to 100% ethanol (3 min each) and once to 95%, 70%, and 50% ethanol (3 min each), and rehydrated at room temperature. After antigen activation by heat, the samples were incubated with 2% goat serum in PBS for 30 minutes at room temperature to block nonspecific binding sites. The cells were then incubated overnight at 4°C with anti-green fluorescent protein antibody (1:100, ab290; Abcam) diluted in PBS (1:100). After washing 3 times with PBS, the nuclei were incubated with Alexa Fluor 488 (1:2, 500; Thermo Fisher Scientific) diluted in PBS for 1 hour at room temperature and then with DAPI solution (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 1:500) for 1 h at room temperature. Slides were sealed on glass slides using ProLong Gold (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and observed using an integrated stereomicroscope (BZ-X800; KEYENCE, Osaka, Japan).

Western Blotting

Total protein was extracted from the cells using radio-immunoprecipitation assay buffer (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Dallas, TX). Each sample (30 μg) was electrophoresed on a 10% polyacrylamide gel and transferred to a polyvinylidene difluoride membrane (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA) using a Trans-Blot Turbo Transfer System (Bio-Rad Laboratories).

After blocking with 3% fat-free milk at room temperature for 1 hour, the membrane was incubated overnight at 4°C with the following primary antibodies diluted in blocking solution: Col1a1 (sc-293182, 1:200 dilution; Santa Cruz Biotechnology), MMP9 (ab137332, 1:100 dilution; Abcam, Cambridge, United Kingdom), and ACTB (1:2000 dilution; Santa Cruz Biotechnology). After overnight incubation, the samples were incubated for 1 hour at 37°C with the following secondary antibodies: donkey antigoat immunoglobulin G H&L (horseradish peroxidase) (ab6885; Abcam) and goat antirabbit immunoglobulin G H&L (horseradish peroxidase) (ab205718; Abcam). After washing, the protein bands were visualized using an electrochemiluminescence detection kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The bands were imaged using a chemiluminescence imager (ImageQuant LAS 4000 mini; GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL). Image analysis was performed using the ImageJ software. Each experiment was repeated thrice.

RNA Recovery and Reverse Transcription

Total RNA was extracted from the cells or skin tissues using a monophasic solution of phenol and guanidine isothiocyanate (ISOGEN; Nippon Gene, Tokyo, Japan) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Total RNA was mixed with random primers, reverse transcriptase, and deoxynucleoside triphosphate mixtures (Takara Bio). The mixture was incubated in a T100TM thermal cycler (Bio-Rad Laboratories) at 25°C for 5 minutes for annealing, 55°C for 10 minutes for synthesis, and 80°C for 10 minutes for heat inactivation of reverse transcriptase to make cDNA.

Real-time Quantitative Polymerase Chain Reaction

Real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (PCR) was performed using an Applied Biosystems 7500 Fast Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Forty cycles were performed, and the fluorescence of each sample was measured at the end of every cycle. PCR was performed in 2 stages: holding the reagents at 95°C for 3 seconds (denaturation) and at 60°C for 30 seconds (annealing and extension). In the subsequent melting curve analysis stage, the temperature was increased from 60 to 95°C, and fluorescence was measured continuously. Gene expression was performed using primers and PCR master mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for Col1a1, MMP9, IL1α, and IL6 (all from Thermo Fisher Scientific), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Gene expression levels in dermal fibroblasts or skin tissues of 8-week-old mice were used as the baseline, and fold change values were determined using the 2−ΔΔCt method.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences software (version 22.0; Chicago, IL). One-way analysis of variance and Tukey post hoc tests were used to compare the differences between 3 or more groups. Statistical significance was set at a P value of less than 0.05.

RESULTS

E17F Fibroblasts Have High Collagen Production and Contractile Capacity

E13D and E17F fibroblasts were in the process of differentiation and did not express CD26, unlike adult papillary dermal fibroblasts (APF) of other animals (APF versus E17F, P = 0.0092; APF versus E17F, P = 0.0092). Therefore, in this study, E17F cells, which have higher collagen-producing capacity and contractility than APF cells, were superior in terms of skin rejuvenation.

E17F Fibroblasts Have the Ability to Contract in an Ex Vivo Contraction Model

When cells were transplanted within a specific marked area and the changes in the area were compared, E13D and E17F cells contracted in the area compared with the controls, and the effect was significantly greater in E17F cells (control versus E13D, P = 0.061; control versus E17F, P = 0.021) (Fig. 1). Thus, consistent with the results of the in vivo collagen contraction assay, E17F cells exhibited a strong contractile capacity.

Transplanted Cells Show Higher Fixation Rates in the Same Skin Layer of Origin and Improve Dermal Collagen Density and Subcutaneous Fat Loss

Because the fixation rate is important in cell transplantation, to investigate the appropriate recipients of fibroblasts, fibroblasts derived from the E13 dermis and E17 fascia of embryonic green fluorescent protein mice were transplanted into the dermis and fascia, respectively, of adult mice, and the behavior of the fibroblasts after transplantation was examined. The results showed that the number of cells decreased over time after transplantation, not all cells were established, and the decrease ended approximately 1 week after transplantation (Fig. 2A). Based on these results, E13D and E17F cells were transplanted into the shallow (intradermal) and deep (suprafascial) layers, respectively, 14 days after transplantation, and the engraftment rates were compared. Better fixation rates were observed when E13D cells were transplanted into the dermis and E17F cells were transplanted into the fascia (subcutaneously), that is, into the tissue layer from which the cells were derived (E13 shallow versus deep, P = 0.049; E13D versus E17F, P = 0.034) (Fig. 2B). Furthermore, as a histological change, a comparison of collagen density in the dermis in tissue fragments 1 month after transplantation showed that the density was significantly increased when E17F was transplanted subcutaneously (P = 0.0049) (Fig. 3A). The subcutaneous fat layer increased when E13D and E17F mice were transplanted near the fascia (E13D, P = 0.0095; E17F, P = 0.0017) (Fig. 3B).

Anti-inflammatory Effects of Cell Transplantation Vary Depending on the Transplanted Cells and Site

We investigated whether the anti-inflammatory effects of cell transplantation varied depending on the skin layer from which the cells were derived and the tissue to which they were transplanted. The group that received the combination with a high fixation rate, that is, E13D intradermal transplantation and E17F subcutaneous transplantation, showed a significant decrease in the gene expression of IL6 and IL1α, which are markers of skin inflammation, compared with the control group (IL6: old control versus E13D shallow, P = 0.0011; old control versus E17F deep, P = 0.0019; IL1a: old control versus E13D shallow, P = 0.027; old control versus E17F deep, P = 0.024) (Fig. 4).

DISCUSSION

We investigated the efficacy of “right-at-the-right-place” fibroblast therapy by transplanting E13D and E17F fibroblasts into the dermis and fascia, respectively, of adult BL6 mice. Results showed that fibroblasts from fetal fascia have high collagen-producing and contractile capacities, and transplantation of these cells into deeper skin layers can result in high engraftment rates, subcutaneous fat gain, and improved collagen density.

In aging skin tissue, the number of dermal fibroblasts decreases and ECM protein deposition changes, impairing tissue homeostasis and wound healing.15–17 Cell-based therapies, including autologous or allogeneic cell transplantation, have been used to improve tissue repair.10 Fibroblasts derived from different developmental stages have different ECMs, motility, cytokine secretion, and mediator response patterns; therefore, this study focused on fetal fibroblasts.18 In particular, with regard to ECM secretions, which contribute to sagging or wrinkles, collagen, the most important ECM, is more abundant in the fetus (type 1 collagen) than in the adult, and the activity of prolyl hydroxylase, which is involved in the regulation of collagen synthesis, is significantly increased in fetal fibroblasts compared with that in adult fibroblasts.19 Furthermore, the fetus is richer in ECM glycoproteins such as elastin and tenascin than the adult.20,21 Fetal fibroblasts and their derived dermal substitutes have been used safely in phase I and II clinical studies; they reduce wound-healing time and improve repair quality without immune or inflammatory responses.22 These results suggest that fetal fibroblast transplantation may effectively rejuvenate human skin through signaling pathways by replacing aged fibroblasts.

Reports of ambiguous descriptions of subcutaneous/intradermal transplantation and unknown engraftment rates provide limited information on maximizing treatment efficacy. Our results show that fibroblast transplantation to the same site as the source skin tissue leads to high cellular engraftment, termed “right place, right time” therapy. Fibroblasts in the fetal skin are classified into dermal and fascial groups.23 Lineage-tracking experiments using platelet-derived growth factor receptor A as a pan-fibroblast marker showed that dermal fibroblasts, including papillary, reticular, and dermal papilla, originate from multipotent mesenchymal progenitor cells at E12.5, with lineage determination occurring by E16.5.24 By postnatal day 2, papillary, reticular, and subcutaneous markers can be differentiated according to the expression of cell surface markers such as CD26, SCA1, and DLK1, with CD26 marking the upper dermal lineage. However, in the present study, the undifferentiated fetal fibroblasts did not express upper-layer lineage markers. Papillary fibroblasts in adult animals are required for the formation of new hair follicles after wounding, whereas reticular fibroblasts mediate the early stages of wound repair and express fibroblast activation markers, such as α-smooth muscle actin.25 The present study showed high collagen-producing capacity and contractility in E17 fibroblasts near the fascia, which are thought to differentiate into reticulofibroblasts, consistent with their preliminary stage of differentiation, which mediates the wound-healing process. Although there have been no previous reports demonstrating anti-inflammatory effects, collagen production, or contractile capacity of transplanted embryonic fibroblasts, the combination of fractional laser and embryonic cell transplantation has been shown to reduce scarring, and its potential ability to do so requires further investigation.26

One limitation of our study is that we observed that the transplanted cells died or migrated elsewhere approximately 1 week after transplantation, resulting in a decrease in cell numbers compared with the initial transplantation. Further experiments are required to confirm this hypothesis. In addition, this study focused on histological changes in mice, and its applicability to humans has not yet been confirmed. In addition, the limited number of individual mice would not rule out individual differences, and further analysis with a larger sample size is necessary.

Although fetal fibroblasts show advantages for skin treatment, including the benefits found in this study, obtaining fresh skin specimens for isolating fetal fibroblasts is difficult due to ethical issues. One strategy involves reprogramming adult fibroblasts into pluripotent stem cells or differentiating adult stem cells into fetal fibroblasts. When stem cells differentiate, progenitor cells divide into subpopulations and express different markers.27 For future human applications, isolating human fetal fibroblasts consistent with the identified subtypes is important because of immune rejection in xenotransplantation.

In conclusion, transplanting fascia-derived cells into the subcutaneous myofascial layer in fibroblast transplantation therapy, used as an antiaging treatment, could become a new rejuvenation strategy.
