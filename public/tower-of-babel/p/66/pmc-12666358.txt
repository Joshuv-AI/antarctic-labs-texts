Characteristics of pain symptoms in Parkinson’s disease
Authors: Dung Thi Hoang, Frank Xing, Daniel Truong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPain is among the most common non-motor symptoms in patients with Parkinson’s disease. Pain is generally underestimated and undertreated in this population, with approximately 50% of patients not receiving analgesic treatment. Pain is complex and has multiple etiologies. Improved understanding of the types of pain, their underlying mechanisms, and appropriate treatments may help neurologists to identify pain earlier, classify it appropriately, and determine the best treatment approach to reduce patient burden and outcomes. In this review, we describe the classification and types of pain, relevant pathophysiology, and both pharmacologic and nonpharmacologic therapies.

Introduction

Pain is among the most common and earliest nonmotor symptoms in patients with Parkinson’s disease (PD) [1,2]. Prevalence estimates for pain in PD range from 24 % to 85 %, approximately twice as high as in the general population, and higher than in the general population over 60 years of age [1,3,4]. Pain in PD is heterogeneous in type, localization, relationship to motor disability, and how, when, and in whom it is measured [[4], [5], [6], [7]]. It has been reported that patients with PD have heightened pain sensitivity related to abnormal nociceptive processing [8]. About 50 % of patients with PD report moderate to severe pain at some point during the disease course and it is continuous is at least two-thirds of cases [4,7]. Between 60 % and 85 % of patients experience chronic pain [9,10].

In most patients, pain is secondary to dystonia or musculoskeletal deformities resulting from PD; up to 40 % of patients, however, experience primary pain prior to onset of motor symptoms, sometimes by many years [4,11]. A nationwide prospective cohort study found that patients with pain had a dose-dependent increase in both the incidence and severity of PD [11]. This finding is consistent with data showing altered sensory and pain thresholds in patients with PD compared with controls and suggests dysfunctional nociceptive processing [11].

Despite data indicating that pain is ubiquitous in patients with PD, it is frequently underestimated and undertreated [1]. Approximately 50 % of PD patients with pain do not receive any analgesic treatment [1]. Patients who are treated with levodopa and conventional analgesics do not typically get adequate relief from pain over the long term [9]. Uncontrolled pain in patients with PD can impact motor function, complicate treatment decisions, and limit the patient's quality of life, functional status, and professional and social engagement [2,3,9]. Clearly, there is an unmet need for improved recognition and management of pain in patients with PD.

Pain in PD is complex and has multiple etiologies. It is dynamic in intensity and symptoms [1,2,9,10], and may not relate to motor symptoms or underlying dopaminergic deficits [12]. It is therefore important to understand the varying types and presentations of pain, its assessment, natural history, underlying mechanisms, and management approaches to optimize outcomes for patients [1,9,12]. The aim of this review is to provide an overview of pain in patients with PD and to lend guidance to clinicians on appropriate management.

Clinical risk factors for pain in PD

Given the need to improve recognition of pain in patients with PD, it is instructive to review some common risk factors that may help to identify affected patients earlier. Clinical risk factors for pain in PD include female sex [4], early disease onset [6,13], long disease duration, motor complications, depressive symptoms, and certain comorbidities (e.g., diabetes mellitus, osteoporosis, rheumatologic conditions, and disc herniation) [1]. Increasing age may also be a risk factor, although results are conflicting and a matter of some debate [14].

Pain is closely related to other non-motor symptoms such as depression, fatigue, daytime sleepiness, and sleep disorders [5]. For example, patients with PD that displayed depressive symptoms had significantly higher pain severity and pain interference scores compared with control patients without depression [[15], [16], [17]].

Patients with PD and poor sleep quality reported higher scores on pain severity and severity of depression and anxiety. These patients primarily characterized their pain as radiating, paresthesia, and akathisia-related [16,18]. Patients with PD and pain displayed longer sleep latencies, shorter total sleep times, less time in the rapid eye movement stage of sleep, more wakefulness after sleep onset, and poor sleep efficiency compared with patients with PD but no pain [19]. Sleep architecture also varies in patients with vs without pain, in that patients with pain have more light sleep and less deep sleep than those without [16,18,19].

The relationship between cognitive dysfunction and pain in PD is less clear. Although one study found a relationship between memory impairment, concentration, and pain based on a non-motor symptom scale, another study using the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) found no relationship [19]. A third study using a non-motor symptoms scale showed a correlation between pain and attention/memory but only included patients with MMSE scores ≥ 24 [5].

Classification of pain in PD

The accurate classification of pain is vital to selecting the appropriate therapy, as different types of pain may have distinct underlying mechanisms and therefore may respond differently to diverse treatments [9]. Several rubrics for classifying pain in PD have been published. The Ford classification is commonly used in clinical practice and classifies PD pain into musculoskeletal, radicular/neuropathic, dystonia-related, and akathisia discomfort [20]. Pain is also commonly classified based on the King’s Parkinson’s Disease Pain Scale (KPPS), which has seven categories: musculoskeletal pain, chronic pain, fluctuation-related pain, nocturnal pain, orofacial pain, discoloration, edema/swelling and radicular pain [4].

Overview of Pain in Parkinson’s Disease.

Musculoskeletal pain

Musculoskeletal pain is the most common type of pain in patients with PD, with an estimated prevalence of 40 % to 75 %, higher than that reported for elderly patients without PD [4]. More than 50 % of patients with PD report low back pain and those with low back pain have longer disease duration than those without [4]. Musculoskeletal pain from the musculoskeletal system and joints may be secondary to changes in posture associated with spinal deformities, joint problems, and/or higher than normal muscle tone [14,21]. It can manifest as paraspinal muscle pain, joint pain, lumbar spine pain, and frozen shoulder. Paravertebral, back, and lower limb pain are the most common types [13,22].

Previous researchers have identified risk factors for musculoskeletal pain, the most important one being dopamine deficiency [13,22]. Spinal degeneration is associated with a greater likelihood of painful paresthesias and discomfort from akathisia [23].

PD patients with musculoskeletal pain report greater reduction in quality of life than PD patients without pain [24].

Radicular pain

This is the second most common type of pain after musculoskeletal pain, with prevalence ranging from 5-20 % [[24], [25], [26]]. Radicular pain may be caused by deformities of the body and the accompany lumbar and cervical pathology during disease progression and is often accompanied by symptoms of numbness and muscle weakness in the root distribution [27]. Radicular pain is often included in the group of neuropathic pain in patients with PD and negatively impacts patient quality of life [26]​.

Dystonic pain

Dystonic pain is experienced by 8–50 % of patients with PD generally [21,25], and by approximately two-thirds of patients with longer levodopa treatment [4,28]. Dystonic pain can present in the face, abdomen, back, and extremities including foot and toe dystonia [2] and can include both simple forms (inversion or extension of the big toe) to more complex forms (combination of inversion, flexion of the foot and toes, inward rotation, and extension of the big toe toward the instep0. Foot and toe dystonia is often very painful and causes difficult movement. This type of pain often occurs in the morning before the first dose of levodopa or as a wearing off phenomenon [29,30].

Orofacial pain

This is an anatomic classification of pain characterized by chewing pain, nocturnal bruxism, and burning mouth syndrome [31]. Chewing pain occurs secondary to temporomandibular joint damage [31]. The temporomandibular joint is innervated by the auriculotemporal branch of the mandibular nerve, which is believed to have some role in PD. Bruxism is also related to activity at the temporomandibular joint and is therefore considered by some to reflect musculoskeletal pain. It is noteworthy that patients with PD have a higher incidence of bruxism both at night and during the day [31]. Burning mouth syndrome manifests as a burning pain in the mouth that is not related to oral abnormalities. It is estimated to occur in up to 24 % of patients [32].

Fluctuating pain

Fluctuating pain is a unique type of pain experienced by 10.6 to 67.4 % of patients with PD [15,33,34]. This type of pain is more severe in the OFF stage of the disease, reflecting a relationship to synaptic dopamine concentrations. Patients with PD at higher risk for fluctuating pain are those with a younger disease onset, later Hoehn-Yahr stage, and higher rate of dyskinesia. These patients are more likely to have wearing-off and poor quality of life [34]. This type of pain is evidence that both motor and non-motor symptoms fluctuate in patients with PD.

Abdominal pain

Abdominal pain is relatively rare, with a prevalence of only 3.4 % [35] to 3.7 % [36]. It may appear in the early stages of PD in patients with onset less than 3 years [36] or after long-term use of Elvodopa [37]. The pathophysiology of such abdominal pain has not been confirmed, although constipation and delayed gastric emptying contribute. It is associated with the “End of Dose Wearing Off” (EODWO). It can be associated with EODWO and may be an early indicator of EODWO [35,37]. Abdominal pain may appear hypertonic in the rectus abdominis suggesting dystonia [35].

Pain across the PD disease course

Pain differs between early-and advanced-stage PD in terms of incidence, presentation, and response to dopaminergic therapy.

Early PD

Pain is common in early PD, with one study reporting pain in 79.3 % of patients [38]. The same study showed that among early-stage patients, musculoskeletal pain was the most common (70.1 %), followed by nocturnal, radicular, and chronic pain (42–44 %) and finally fluctuating (34.6 %) and orofacial pain (16.8 %). Nearly 75 % of patients reported having more than one type of pain [38]. Interestingly, severity of pain in early PD does not seem to be associated with severity of motor symptoms or disease duration [39]. Pain severity does appear to be correlated with affective symptoms, autonomic symptoms, motor complications, female gender, and younger age [40].

Studies have shown that patients with early-stage PD (Hoehn & Yahr stages I-III;[41]) have increased sensitivity (lower threshold) to painful stimulation including heat, cold, electrical stimulation, and a fixed pain stimuli compared to controls [11]. These findings suggest that pain in patients with early PD reflects disrupted nociceptive and aberrant processing associated with central and peripheral deafferentation, although it remains unclear if this is the case before motor symptoms are not prominent​ [11]. Thus, the loss of connection between the central and peripheral nervous systems may be the main mechanism of pain in the early-stage PD. Furthermore, the development of Lewy body pathology in non-dopaminergic areas of the brainstem contribute to the clinical features of pain in early-stage PD [4]. Mechanisms underlying pain in PD will be discussed in more detail later in this review.

Advanced PD

Patients with advanced-stage PD (i.e., Hoehn and Yahr stages IV and V) had a 35 % higher incidence of pain than those with early-stage disease in one study [42]. Patients with advanced-stage PD commonly experience secondary disorders, including radicular compression, musculoskeletal deformities and contractures, cramps, dysesthesia, and frozen shoulder, that may play an important role in the source of pain [4]. One study found that patients at advanced stages of PD reported a lower frequency of neuropathic pain but a higher frequency of central pain compared with those in early stages [19], while another reported that the prevalence of polyneuropathy increased with either longer levodopa treatment durations or advanced disease stages [43]. Pain symptoms in advanced stages are unresponsive to dopaminergic treatment in contrast to those in early PD [42].

Assessment of pain in PD

Pain is assessed based on its characteristics, frequency, severity, and impact [18]. The characteristics of pain are often very diverse and described as spreading, aching, dull, tight, sharpening, boring, penetrating, exploding, pulsating, burning, stabbing, cramping, paresthesia and restlessness​ [16]. There are many scales for assessing pain in the general patient population, but to date only a few scales are designed for pain in assessment in patients with PD. These tools include the Visual Analogue Scale: VAS), McGill Pain Questionnaire [44], and King's Parkinson's Disease Pain Scale [28].

Visual analog scale (VAS)

The VAS is used for assessment of pain intensity in many types of pain and in various specialties. Patients rate their pain at that moment by drawing a hatch mark along a line labeled on the left with 0 (labeled “None”) and on the right with 100 (labeled something like “Worst pain imaginable”) [4,45]. This is scale that can be completely quickly and easily, making it highly practical in clinical settings.

The Numerical Rating Scale (NRS) is a similar assessment tool. Patients rate their pain at a moment in time on a scale from 0 (“No pain”) to 10 (“Worst pain imaginable”) [46].

The McGill pain Questionnaire

The McGill Pain Questionnaire [47], is used to assess 3 components of a patient's pain experience (sensory, affective and evaluative). The questionnaire consists of 4 main questions: 1) Where do you feel pain? 2) What do you feel pain like? 3) How do you feel your pain changes over time? 4) How much pain do you feel? Question 2 consists of 20 subgroup questions; for each, patients choose a descriptor from a list of 74 adjectives. Subgroups yield 4 categories including sensory, affective, evaluative, and other pain. The total scale score ranges from 0 to 78, with higher scores indicating more severe pain [47].

King’s Parkinson’s disease pain scale

The King’s Parkinson’s Disease Pain Scale is a well-established, well-validated, and reliable tool for assessing pain severity available in several languages. The KPPS categorizes pain into 7 distinct types, including musculoskeletal, chronic, fluctuation-related, nocturnal, orofacial, discoloration/edema/swelling, and radicular pain, reflecting both nociceptive and neuropathic types of pain. Each category of pain based on responses to 14 items, which include ratings of frequency (scored from 0 to 4) and severity (scored from 0 to 3) [4]. The maximum score for each sub-score is 12 and for the overall scale is 168. In addition to the 7 main types of pain, the KPPS also evaluates pain related to OFF state and dystonia [4].

Mechanism of pain in Parkinson's disease

Pain in PD can be broadly divided into 2 types based on etiology: 1) nociceptive pain, or pain that originates in the peripheral nervous system as a result of actual or threatened damage to non-neural tissue; and 2) neuropathic pain, which arises secondary to identifiable pathology of the somatosensory system [7,48]. Nociceptive pain in PD presents as localized fluctuating musculoskeletal pain, usually affecting the trunk or limbs [7,12,48]. An estimated 55 % of patients with PD experience nociceptive pain [48].

Neuropathic pain can be peripherally or centrally mediated. Peripheral neuropathic pain is localized and results from nerves or spinal roots that are constricted following PD-related changes in motor function or posture. In contrast, central neuropathic pain is diffuse and arises from dysfunction in central pain pathways [7]. Approximately 16 % of patients with PD experience neuropathic pain [48].

The experience of pain results from a complex interaction between external stimuli, receptors, and responses involving both voluntary and involuntary activities with different brain structures in a hierarchy in which high-level brain activity is the last step. The experience of pain consists of three distinct dimensions, each of which have distinct underlying mechanisms: sensory-discriminative (primary), motivational-affective (secondary), and cognitive-evaluative (tertiary) [7].

In general, stimulation at the receptor is transduced as an electrical signal along the afferent pathway into the spinal cord, where it is both locally modulated and inhibited by the release of monoamines and opioid neuropeptides in the dorsal horn. From there, the lateral spinothalamic tract traverses the ventral thalamus and ventromedial nucleus toward sensory regions of the cortex. This lateral system underlies the primary, sensory aspect of pain as well as pain localization and intensity. In addition, the spinothalamic tract projects to the thalamus and structures in the limbic system. This medial system is thought to underlie the secondary and tertiary aspects of pain [7].

Studies have demonstrated structural changes in dopaminergic and non-dopamine systems both centrally and peripherally in PD pain [7,11,49,50]. Genetic studies also show that genetic polymorphisms affecting the metabolism of catechol-O-methyltransferase (COMT) are associated with differences in COMT activity and functional differences in pain sensitivity and intensity in PD [51].

Studies of peripheral mechanisms of pain in PD have used the skin biopsy technique to assess the integrity of cutaneous nerve fibers [52,53]. These studies have shown that the degeneration of small sensory nerves and deposition of phosphorylated α-synuclein in sensory and autonomic nerves in the skin occur in the early stages of PD​ [52,53]. Biopsy results also show that the degeneration of sensory nerves and nerve redistribution are related to the pain perception in the early stages of the disease. This suggests that peripheral pain-sensing nerves are also altered in addition to the central pain management system [54].

Upon study of peripheral inflammatory cytokines, serum interlerkin-1 (IL-1) was higher in patients with PD patients than in healthy people, and IL-1 was higher in late stages of the disease, with higher UPDRS-III scores, H-Y stages and L-Dopa doses [55]. A study on rat models also shows that the regulatory receptors of IL-1a, IL-6 and TNF-α in the brain also have clear changes [55,56].

Treatment of pain in PD

Recommended medical treatments for pain in patients with PD differ based on the stage of PD. In early stages, treatments comprise nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), anxiolytics, and antidepressants. In patients with intermediate- or advanced-stage PD, dopaminergic therapies, and deep brain stimulation (STN-DBS) are more commonly used. It is noteworthy that varied responses to dopaminergic medications have been reported and underscore the potential importance of other neurotransmitter systems in pain in PD [2]. Lastly anecdotal reports of successful pain alleviation exist for alternative treatment modalities such as acupuncture, message therapy and yoga. Though rigorous evidence-based investigations into these alternative treatment modalities do not yet exist, they remain ntriguing possibilities for future research and exploration. The potential effectiveness of these alternative treatment modalities is yet another reminder to the astute clinician and researcher that scientific basis for pain pathways remain yet to be fully understood.

Medications

Dopaminergic medications

The goal of dopaminergic medications in the context of pain in PD is to increase the patient's objective pain threshold [58]. In PD patients, dopamine deficiency has been shown to be one of the causes of reduced pain threshold. The administration of levodopa markedly raised pain threshold in PD patients but not healthy patients [59]. Levodopa also reduced pain symptoms in some patients with PD but exacerbated it in others [59].

Levodopa may be more appropriate for generalized, nonspecific pain as compared to radicular, neuropathic, or akathisia-related pain [60]. When pain is associated with worsening motor disability or with fluctuation-dependent dystonia generally, the first step is to adjust the dose of anti-parkinsonian medication [61,62]. When the pain does not respond to dopaminergic medication adjustments, other causes should be evaluated; for example, pain might be caused by rotator cuff syndrome, frozen shoulder, cervical and lumbar spondylosis, or a cardiorespiratory disorder [61]. Taken together, these findings suggest that patients with PD have abnormal sensory and pain thresholds, and pain could be partially relieved after the administration of levodopa. Whether dopamine has a real antinociceptive effect or only a pain modulation effect requires further investigation [4].

Analgesic medications

Analgesic medication is commonly used for musculoskeletal pain, starting with NSAIDs and moving to opioids and COX-2 selective NSAIDs as needed. Anticonvulsants, triptans, and antidepressants have limited efficacy for this type of pain and are used only occasionally [23]. However, recent studies suggest that anticonvulsants such as gabapentin, pregabalin, lamotrigine, carbamazepine, and tricyclic antidepressants (TCAs) may be appropriate when treating chronic pain in PD patients [57].

Duloxetine is an option for central or primary pain [61,63]. As a serotonin-norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor, duloxetine exerts its analgesic effects by blocking the reuptake of serotonin and norepinephrine in the central nervous system [[64], [65]]. This inhibition leads to increased concentrations of both neurotransmitters in the synaptic cleft, particularly within the descending inhibitory pain pathways [[66], [67]]. Serotonin and norepinephrine play a crucial role in modulating pain signals from the brain to the spinal cord [[67], [68]]. By enhancing the activity of these descending antinociceptive pathways, duloxetine effectively suppresses pain transmission [[66], [67]]. This mechanism contributes significantly to its recognized efficacy in various chronic pain conditions, including neuropathic and musculoskeletal pain [[64], [67]]. An early meta-analysis showed that duloxetine and rotigotine could effectively treat pain in PD [63]. Another meta-analysis found that the greatest reductions in pain were observed with the dual MAOB and glutamate inhibitor, safinamide, followed by cannabinoids, opioids, and COMT inhibitors. Safinamide was shown to reduce pain over the long term [69].

The combination of the opiate agonist, oxycodone and the peripheral opiate antagonist, naloxone (Targinact®), has been shown to be effective in the treatment of pain in PD [[70], [71]]. The new opioid medication, tapentadol, has shown promise in treating pain in PD. Tapentadol has reduced affinity for the μ-opioid receptor, and it inhibited serotonin/noradrenaline reuptake.

Botulinum toxin

It has been suggested that botulinum toxin can reduce pain related to dystonia. Results of trials on this topic have been mixed. In a double-blind trial of patients with foot dystonia, administration of botulinum toxin resulted in significant reductions in pain and dystonia compared to baseline but not compared to a placebo group [30]. A randomized double-blind crossover study tested botulinum toxin type A for limb pain in patients with advanced PD and found no significant reduction in pain. However, a subgroup analysis showed that botulinum toxin did significantly improve dystonic pain [72]. Finally, a recent meta-analysis concluded that although no randomized, controlled trials have supported the use of botulinum toxin for PD-related dystonia pain, open-label trials suggest it may have a role [73].

Interference methods

Deep brain stimulation

Many studies have shown that deep brain stimulation (DBS) might alleviate pain in patients with PD [57]. DBS in both the globus pallidus internus (GPi) and subthalamic nucleus (STN) have been shown to alleviate pain, with follow-up durations ranging from 3 months to 2 years [74]. Other studies have shown that STN-DBS can relieve pain in more than 80 % of PD patients during OFF periods [74,75]. STN-DBS has been shown to significantly increase subjective pain threshold and to reduce pain-induced cortical activity in PD patients with pain, while it had no effect on those without pain [75]. The greatest improvements were noted in dystonic and musculoskeletal pain, followed by central and neuropathic pain [74].

DBS might have a direct anti-nociceptive effect by stimulating or modulating the basal ganglion circuitry [74,75]. Bilateral STN-DBS improved pain, particularly pain related to dystonia, in 50 % of patients with PD after a mean follow-up period of 5 years [44]. One study reported a mean improvement of 31.7 % in the pain score for lower back pain, at both 6 months and 1 year after bilateral STN-DBS [44]. In contrast, another study failed to show an effect of long-term (4.6 ± 1.3 years) use STN-DBS on pain but did show a statistically significant improvement in anxiety and fatigue [76]. These findings suggest that future longitudinal studies on the long-term effects of STN-DBS on pain in PD are warranted.

The effects of DBS in the GPi have received relatively little attention with regard to pain relief in patients with PD. One study found that unilateral GPi-DBS improved contralateral pain by 80 % and limb dystonia by 90 % [77]. Bilateral GPi-DBS has been shown to reduce dystonia by 86 %, pain by 90 %, cramps by 90 %, and dysesthesia by 88 % [74]. These observations suggest GPi-DBS is most beneficial in relieving dystonia and dystonic pain in patients with PD.

Non-invasive cortical stimulation

Non-invasive cortical stimulation, including transcranial direct current stimulation and transcranial alternating current stimulation, are currently under investigation for treating pain in PD. Direct cortical stimulation may improve symptoms of pain in PD [78].

Repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) may have a role in relieving painful dystonia in unmedicated patients with PD [79]. A study on 56 PD patients treated with 5 sessions of TMS at 20-Hz frequency showed reduction of musculoskeletal pain as well as depression, anxiety and dyskinesia [80].

Alternative treatments

Other methods such as Massage therapy, acupuncture, exercise, and yoga have also been shown to be effective in alleviating pain in PD patients [4,81]. Physical therapy and rehabilitation have been shown to be particularly effective for musculoskeletal pain [81].

Massage therapy (MT)

Massage therapy (MT) is one of the most common forms of complementary and alternative medicine [82]. Studies have shown that pressure applied during MT may stimulate vagal activity, reducing anxiety and pain, as well as increasing levels of serotonin [83]. In a systematic review of 12 studies on MT for PD, Angelopoulou et al. found that MT can lessen pain, induce relaxation, and improve overall quality of life in PD patients. Specific techniques that have proven effective are classical deep therapeutic massage, traditional Japanese massage, and Thai massage [82].

Acupuncture

Acupuncture is another technique that has been studied extensively for the treatment of pain conditions. Studies in PD patients have found that patients who received acupuncture had significant improvement in pain scores compared to controls [84]. A single center randomized controlled trial was conducted with sixty patients with Parkinson's disease with skeletal muscle pain were randomly divided into electroacupuncture group and sham acupuncture control group with 30 patients each. The electric acupuncture group was treated with electric acupuncture, while the control group was treated with Park needle pseudo-acupuncture. The results showed, electroacupuncture therapy can effectively improve skeletal muscle pain in patients with Parkinson's disease, reduce the muscle hardness of patients, improve patients' daily life ability, and improve patients' emotional disorders [85].

Exercise and physical therapy

Exercise and physical therapy, though often employed for improvement of motor symptoms in PD patients, have also been evaluated for positive effects on pain. These techniques have shown some benefits especially in improving musculoskeletal pain. In a survey study of PD patients, rehabilitative therapy and physical therapy were rated as the most effective for pain reduction, though with only temporary relief [86]. An uncontrolled study of 20 PD patients found that a 12-week exercise program resulted in a slight, though nonsignificant improvement of pain symptoms [87]. Reuter et al. conducted a randomized controlled trial of 90 PD patients, comparing walking, Nordic walking, and flexibility and relaxation programs. The walking groups had further reduction of pain intensity as compared to a flexibility and relaxation regimen, and also had decreased numbers of patients with neck, hip and sacroiliac joint pain [88].

Yoga

Yoga has been studied for patients with a wide array of neurological disorders. A recent systematic review of 94 studies concluded that yoga can be an effective adjunct to medical treatment [89]. In a study of PD patients, Myers et al. found that yoga reduced the incidence of low back pain and improved balance [90].

Discussion

Although pain is common in patients with PD, is frequently underestimated and undertreated [1]. This is likely due, at least in part, to the dynamic and complex nature of pain and lack of awareness of the types or time courses of pain in this population. Better understanding of pain in PD should improve its recognition and assessment. Even when properly identified, many patients with PD don’t get treated for their pain. Available treatments have limited efficacy in managing pain in PD, and a nuanced and multimodal approach is required to match appropriate therapies to the characteristics of each patient’s pain. Given the significant negative impact of pain in PD on patient quality of life, psychosocial function, and motor symptoms, there is an urgent need for clinician education on optimal use of available treatments as well as the development of novel therapies. More research is needed into the effectiveness of alternative therapies alone and in combination with medication or interference strategies for specific types of pain in specific types of patients.

Conclusion

A critical need to address existing knowledge gaps in the understanding and management of pain in Parkinson's disease is highlighted in this review. The complex pathophysiology of pain remains yet to be elucidated, and reliable biomarkers for pain development, severity, and treatment response need to be identified. The current unmet need for more effective, targeted interventions is underscored by the ongoing exploration of both non-pharmacological and pharmacological treatments. Despite its prevalence and significant impact on the quality of life for individuals with Parkinson's disease, there remains a key opportunity for clinicians to improve their evaluation of pain in these patients. Many patients currently do not receive adequate pharmacological or non-pharmacological treatment, often due to a lack awareness. By fostering greater awareness and providing targeted education, healthcare professionals can ensure that pain is effectively recognized and managed, thereby improving patient outcomes and overall well-being.
