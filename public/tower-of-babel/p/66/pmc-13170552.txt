Knowledge, Attitude, and Practice of Bathing Care Among Patients with a Stoma and Their Correlations With Quality of Life: A Cross-sectional Study
Authors: Haiyan Chen, Ziyi Zhou, Weizhen Li, Feixia Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
OBJECTIVE:To investigate the knowledge, attitude, and practice of bathing care among patients with a stoma and to examine the correlations between bathing care and patients’ quality of life.METHODS:A cross-sectional study was conducted, involving 663 patients with a stoma from 22 tertiary hospitals across 7 provinces in East China. A self-designed Knowledge-Attitude-Practice of Bathing Questionnaire and City of Hope-Quality of Life-Ostomy Questionnaire were used. Data were analyzed following descriptive statistics, t test, analysis of variance, and ordinal logistic regression.RESULTS:Patients with a stoma demonstrate a moderate level of knowledge, attitude, and practice of bathing care. Educational level, marital status, primary caregiver, time after surgery, stoma-related complications, and primary bathing method were identified as independent influencing factors for bathing care in patients with stoma (P<.05). A positive correlation was found between bathing care and quality of life (r=0.434; P<.01).CONCLUSIONS:Bathing practices of patients with a stoma were affected by the presence of a stoma, and the level of bathing care was closely linked to the quality of life. In clinical practice, nurses should pay greater attention to bathing issues for patients during education and follow-up. Future studies should focus on developing interventions to improve the bathing-care abilities and quality of life among these patients.

INTRODUCTION

Stoma surgery is widely used in clinical practice and is applicable for the treatment of various conditions, including colorectal cancer, bladder cancer, inflammatory bowel disease, intestinal obstruction, and abdominal trauma. The number of patients with a stoma in China has exceeded 1 million, and this number is gradually increasing.1 Although stoma surgery saves patients’ lives, the presence of a stoma inevitably causes problems in daily living, which negatively affect patients’ quality of life (QoL). Many studies have investigated the physical, psychological, social, and daily life challenges faced by patients with a stoma.2–4 However, specific aspects of their daily activities receive less attention. Bathing, as one of the daily challenges for patients with a stoma, is an important but often overlooked issue.

Bathing is one of the essential activities performed regularly by individuals to maintain skin hygiene and promote comfort. Clinically, bathing care is one of the components of basic nursing. Moreover, bathing serves as a crucial indicator in evaluating patients’ ability to perform activities of daily living.5 Patients with a stoma have unique bathing requirements compared with other patients. It is not only a necessary part of maintaining cleanliness but also an important element in sustaining mental health and QoL. In addition, patients with a stoma often face some unique challenges during bathing due to the presence of a stoma, such as leakage, inconvenience, psychological discomfort, stigma, concerns about waterproofing of stoma appliances, and stoma damage. Bathing-related issues have been gradually recognized as one of the problems that significantly affect the daily lives of patients with a stoma. Nevertheless, limited research has specifically addressed bathing-related issues, and bathing care education is frequently neglected in clinical nursing practice. Studies have reported a decline in the frequency of bathing and an increased demand for bathing-related care among patients with a stoma during the early postoperative period, with many individuals experiencing challenges due to the presence of the stoma.6,7 In addition, the leakage of stoma bags often leads to difficulties in cleaning and bathing.8 These studies highlighted the limited education on bathing provided by hospitals and the need for more education programs to improve patients’ self-care abilities.

In the guidelines published by the Registered Nurses’ Association of Ontario (RNAO), bathing care has been included in health education for patients with a stoma.9 Educational interventions on bathing care require a comprehensive understanding of the knowledge, attitude, and practice (KAP) of bathing in patients with a stoma. The KAP model points out that knowledge is the foundation for the establishment of a positive attitude, and attitude is the driving force for behavior change.10 Understanding the KAP relating to bathing care is critical for establishing proper bathing behaviors and improving patients’ QoL. However, the current state of bathing care among patients with a stoma remains unknown. Therefore, this study aims to investigate the KAP of bathing care among patients with a stoma, while also evaluating their QoL and exploring the relationship between them.

METHODS

Design

This study employed a descriptive, cross-sectional design.

Setting and Participants

The study was conducted at stoma outpatient clinics of 22 tertiary hospitals across 7 provinces in East China from July to October 2023. The 7 provinces in East China share similarities in terms of climate, cultural background, and economic development. The sample size is estimated to be 10 to 20 times the number of variables.11 Accounting for a 20% nonresponse rate, the minimum sample size required in this study was 252 patients. Convenient sampling was used to conduct this survey. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age between 18 and 80 years; (2) patients with abdominal stomas, including enterostomies and urostomies, whose surgical incisions had healed and who were recovering at home; (3) preoperative bathing method was showering or bathing in a tub; and (4) voluntary participation. The exclusion criteria were patients who were unable to complete the survey due to language barriers or other conditions.

Instruments

Sociodemographic Information Questionnaire

The questionnaire for collecting demographic data was independently designed by researchers. The content includes sex, age, educational level, employment, marital status, primary caregiver, household income per capita/month, primary disease, type of stoma, status of stoma, time since stoma creation, stoma-related complications, type of stoma bag, and primary bathing method.

Knowledge-Attitude-Practice of Bathing Questionnaire for Patients with Ostomy

This questionnaire was independently developed by researchers to evaluate the KAP of bathing care among patients with a stoma, with the KAP model as the theoretical framework.12 The development of the questionnaire was based on the review of relevant literature and ostomy guidelines published by the Wound, Ostomy and Continence Nurses Society, Vancouver Chapter of the United Ostomy Association of Canada, and Registered Nurses’ Association of Ontario.9,13,14 The content validity of the questionnaire was evaluated by 29 experts from 20 provinces across China. The item-level content validity index ranged from 0.800 to 1.000, and the scale-level content validity index was 0.933, indicating good content validity. A sample of 386 patients from a stoma outpatient clinic at a tertiary hospital in Zhejiang Province was selected to validate the questionnaire. The Cronbach α of the questionnaire was 0.922, with the Cronbach α of KAP dimensions being 0.920, 0.862, and 0.897, respectively. The 2-week test-retest reliability for each dimension was 0.856, 0.942, and 0.926, respectively, suggesting good reliability. The final questionnaire contains 3 dimensions with 24 items: 9 items in the knowledge dimension (K1-K9), 7 items in the attitude dimension (A10-A16), and 8 items in the practice dimension (P17-P24). The knowledge dimension covers practical information on stoma care during bathing, cleaning methods, safe product use, handling of ostomy bags, and skin care considerations. The attitude dimension focuses on patients’ perceptions about the impact of their stoma on bathing, concerns about difficulties, potential leaks, and changes in bathing habits. The practice dimension examines actual practices, such as frequency and methods of bathing, the use of protective measures, timing adjustments, and the seeking behavior of additional support. Each item is rated using a 5-point Likert-type scale. In the knowledge dimension, 1 to 5 points represent “very well understood” to “completely not understood;” in the attitude dimension, 1 to 5 points represent “strongly agree” to “strongly disagree;” and in the practice dimension, 1 to 5 points represent “always” to “never.” The total score of the questionnaire ranges from 24 to 120, with higher scores indicating better knowledge mastery, more positive attitudes, and less impact of the stoma on bathing practice.

City of Hope-Quality of Life-Ostomy Questionnaire—Chinese Version

The City of Hope-Quality of Life-Ostomy Questionnaire was developed by Grant et al.,15 which evaluates the QoL from 4 dimensions: physical, psychological, social, and spiritual. It contains 43 items measured on a 10-point Likert-type scale, with higher total scores reflecting better QoL. A total QoL score is obtained by adding all items and dividing by 43. The Cronbach α of the questionnaire is 0.940, and 0.77 to 0.90 for each dimension. In this study, the authors used the Chinese version translated and adapted by Gao et al,16 which includes 32 items and 4 dimensions: physical (11 items), psychological (9 items), social (7 items), and spiritual (5 items). The scale has been validated in Chinese patients with a stoma. The Cronbach α of the scale was 0.930, with a content validity greater than 0.7 and a 2-week test-retest reliability above 0.8.

Data Collection

All researchers received unified training in advance. Eligible patients were informed about the content and purpose of the study. After obtaining informed consent, questionnaires were distributed on-site. The survey was conducted anonymously, and patients were promised that the information collected would be kept confidential. All questions were independently completed by the patients, with researchers available to clarify any doubts. The time for completing the questionnaire was limited to 15 to 30 minutes. The data from the questionnaires were entered by 2 individuals independently to ensure accuracy.

Data Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 27 (IBM Corp.). Measurement data were reported as the mean and SD, whereas those non-normally distributed were expressed as median, upper, and lower quartiles (P25, P75). Count data were expressed as the frequency (n) and percentage (%). The t test, analysis of variance (ANOVA), and ordinal logistic regression were used to determine the factors influencing the KAP of bathing. Pearson r was used to measure the correlation between the bathing KAP and QoL. Missing values were excluded from the analysis. All analyses were considered statistically significant at P<.05.

Ethical Considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Research Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine (Acceptance No. IIT2022354B, Approval No. 2022-800). All respondents provided informed consent.

RESULTS

A total of 695 questionnaires were distributed, of which 663 (95%) were included in the final analysis. Four hundred twelve males (62.1%) and 251 females (37.9%) participated in the study, with an average age of 63.17±11.18 years. Most were married (91.7%) and retired (49.0%), with an education level of middle school or below (69.3%). The majority (97.4%) formed a stoma because of cancer, with 45.1% being ileostomies, 40.6% being colostomies, and 13.9% being urostomies. Slightly more than half (51.7%) had permanent stomas, and 37.9% took sponge baths after surgery. More details are shown in Table 1.

Influencing Factors of KAP of Bathing

Univariate analysis of KAP relating to bathing indicated that educational level, employment, marital status, primary caregiver, household income per capita/month, type of stoma, status of stoma, time since stoma creation, stoma-related complications, and primary bathing method were factors influencing the KAP of bathing (P<.05). The results are shown in Table 3. Statistically significant variables were included in the multivariate model. Based on the lower and upper quartiles of KAP scores, respondents were categorized into 3 tiers: low (below quartile 1), medium (between quartile 1 and 3), and high (above quartile 3). Ordinal logistic regression was conducted to analyze determinants of KAP relating to bathing. The proportional odds assumption was checked by the test of parallel lines. The parallel line test showed that χ2=37.1 (P=.056), indicating that the slope of the model had no significant difference in different categories of the explained variables. In the model-fitting data, the −2 log-likelihood test Pearson χ2 was 259.6 (P<.001), indicating that the regression analysis had statistical significance. As shown in Table 3, 6 variables were associated with the KAP relating to bathing (P<.05). The KAP of bathing in patients with a college education or higher was 2.54 times higher than that of patients with primary education or lower (OR=0.39; 95% CI: 0.20-0.79). Unmarried (OR=8.89; 95% CI: 2.39-33.06) and married patients (OR=5.69; 95% CI: 2.75-11.75) had 8.89 and 5.69 times higher KAP of bathing, respectively, than divorced or widowed patients. Self-care patients had 2.33 times higher KAP of bathing than those who rely on others (OR=2.33; 95% CI: 1.22-4.46). A longer postoperative period was associated with higher bathing care levels. Patients without complications had 1.58 times higher KAP of bathing than those with complications (OR=0.63; 95% CI: 0.43-0.92). Patients who took a tub bath had 6.04 times higher KAP of bathing than those who took sponge baths (OR=0.17; 95% CI: 0.04-0.67). The variance inflation factors (VIF) for the variables ranged from 1.018 to 1.754, all of which were below 5, indicating that there was no collinearity problem among the variables.

DISCUSSION

This study represents the first cross-sectional investigation in China to focus on bathing issues among patients with a stoma and to explore their linkage with QoL. The study found that patients with a stoma demonstrated a moderate level of bathing knowledge. However, their attitude and bathing practices were somewhat affected by the stoma, which may be related to changes in bathing methods and difficulties encountered during bathing.7 Furthermore, there is a positive correlation between bathing KAP and QoL, with issues such as bathing timing, products, frequency, methods, duration, and concerns about bathing having a greater impact on their QoL.

Influencing Factors of KAP of Bathing

In this study, KAP of bathing was higher among patients with higher educational levels. Higher educational attainment is often linked to greater health literacy. Educated individuals may have better access to health care resources.17 In addition, compared with divorced or widowed patients, unmarried and married patients exhibited higher bathing KAP, which may be attributed to the levels of social support. Married patients often have a partner who provides emotional and physical support, which can positively impact their attitude and bathing practices. This finding is consistent with the results of the study by Baykara et al,18 which indicated that social support is an important factor in adapting to living with a stoma. Furthermore, self-care patients had higher bathing KAP scores. It was reported that patients with a stoma with high levels of independence were better adjusted to daily life activities.19 Self-care patients are directly involved in bathing practices and experience a sense of independence and control over their health, which likely improves their knowledge, skills, and attitudes toward bathing care. The study also found that the KAP of bathing improves as the postoperative time increases. This may be associated with the accumulation of bathing-related knowledge over time.18 This finding is consistent with previous findings, which reported that patients adjusted to stoma-related changes and gradually gained greater competence in stoma care as they had stomas for a longer time.20,21 Thorpe et al22 also indicated that most patients with a stoma constructed a routine after 9 months following surgery. In this study, complications negatively impact the KAP of bathing as they made it more difficult to bathe and clean the skin, which has also been mentioned by Villa et al.23 Finally, sponge bathing was linked to lower levels of KAP. Patients who choose sponge bathing may lack sufficient bathing knowledge and, consequently, may be unsure how to bathe properly or fear that water exposure could harm the stoma, leading them to prefer sponge bathing as a safer option. Showering and tub bathing are generally the preferred bathing methods and are typically viewed as the normal form of bathing. Thus, patients who choose to shower or tub bathe may be more proactive in acquiring relevant knowledge and integrating it into daily routines.

It was reported in other studies that income is directly related to the frequency of use of stoma products.24,25 However, the multivariate model did not identify an effect of income on bathing care. In this study, some patients reported opting for sponge baths to extend the lifespan of stoma appliances and reduce financial costs. Income is crucial to maintain patients’ livelihood and treatment.26 Patients with low income may be unable to afford treatment and stoma supplies, leading to a negative attitude toward their care.27

Correlations Between KAP of Bathing and QoL

The study identified a significant correlation between the bathing KAP and QoL. Patients with a high level of knowledge and positive attitude can not only manage their stoma effectively during bathing but also enhance their independence in daily life, which has a direct impact on QoL.28 Enhanced knowledge and positive attitudes facilitate behavior changes, reducing the adverse effects of the stoma on bathing practice, thereby improving the QoL. Previous studies have similarly identified a positive relationship between patients’ ability to perform daily activities, such as bathing and dressing, and their QoL.6,29 The creation of stomas damages patients’ QoL, with the most prominent impact being on activities of daily living, where they feel restricted in continuing with routine activities, including clothing, exercising, bathing, working, traveling, and socializing.30–32 The ability to resume normal activities is one of the key criteria for assessing QoL.8 It was reported that 55% of patients with a stoma considered education on resuming activities of daily living to be the second most useful category.33 Bathing practices also represent one of the concrete manifestations of patients’ adaptation to a stoma.34 A study on cancer patients also showed that bathing can alleviate anxiety.35 Therefore, bathing should be regarded as an important health concern of patients with a stoma and should receive appropriate attention.36

The study found that the bathing items most strongly associated with QoL were focused on the timing of bathing, bathing products, duration, leakage, and concerns about bathing. Similar results were found in Cengiz and Bahar’s7 study, which showed that the stoma affected patients’ daily lives in terms of bathing frequency, products, and bathing experience. Inappropriate timing and bathing products may negatively affect the bathing experience and skin condition, reducing patient satisfaction with life and QoL. In this study, some patients indicated that they usually bathe in the morning, when stoma output is relatively low, and that their bathing schedule often depends on the availability of their caregivers, as they require assistance during bathing. Moreover, the presence of a stoma often leads to a decreased frequency and a longer bathing duration. Patients also often experience leakage during bathing.37 In this study, uncontrolled stoma output and leakage pose a significant challenge for patients with ileostomies. Patients reported that bathing reduces the adhesion of stoma products, leading to leakage and higher consumption of stoma supplies. When bathing without the pouch, the irregular output of ileostomies often causes uncontrolled discharge, resulting in a greater cleaning burden. The most critical concern of patients with urostomies during bathing is maintaining a sterile urinary environment and preventing water from entering the stoma.38 Many patients expressed concerns about the risk of backflow and infection. For patients with ileostomy with irregular bowel movements and patients with urostomy, researchers generally recommend wearing an ostomy pouch while bathing. Therefore, waterproof measures and leakage prevention are important for them. Effective management of these issues is crucial for improving their QoL,39 such as stoma shields or waterproof tapes. For patients with colostomies who have regular bowel movements, it is generally recommended to remove the ostomy pouch and bathe according to their bowel movements. The involvement of wound, ostomy, and continence (WOC) nurses is critical for these patients, as their expertise can help mitigate these issues and improve their QoL. In clinical practice, WOC nurses provide more detailed advice on daily living guidance, compared with physicians.40 At the researchers’ hospital, WOC nurses have developed a comprehensive bathing care plan, covering perioperative education and extended care, which includes guidance on prebath preparations, selection of bathing products, water temperature control, and so on. Bathing skills, waterproofing measures, and prevention for leakage are taught through demonstrations and simulated practice. Caregivers also receive education to clarify their roles as assistants, with instructions on adjusting their level of involvement based on the patient’s recovery stage to accommodate changes in the patient’s abilities and bathing care levels. This is consistent with the principles of therapeutic patient education.41 In addition, patients often perceive their stoma as a wound and are scared to bathe. Therefore, nurses also focus on correcting misconceptions about stomas and provide online consultations to promptly answer patients’ questions.

Nurse education and support play an important role in helping patients with a stoma build confidence, transition from sponge bathing to a full-body shower, return to normal life, and improve their QoL. Bathing is just one of the daily activities affected by a stoma. Nurses should encourage patients to gradually address other aspects of daily living impacted by the stoma, ultimately achieving a higher level of QoL.

Limitations

The study has some limitations. The data were collected from 7 provinces in East China, which limits the generalization of the findings, especially considering regional differences. Also, respondents were not selected randomly, which may be a selection bias. Future research could expand the sample size, investigate patients from different regions, and conduct longitudinal studies to explore the changes in bath practice among patients with a stoma.

CONCLUSIONS

Patients with a stoma demonstrated a moderate level of bathing knowledge, but their attitude and bathing practices were affected by the stoma to some extent. Furthermore, a positive correlation between KAP of bathing care and QoL was observed. These findings underscore the importance of bathing care for their QoL. In clinical practice, bathing issues should be included in health education and follow-up. Stoma care specialists should offer targeted professional guidance and make optimal recommendations based on patients’ levels of bathing KAP to improve their QoL. Future research could focus on interventions to improve bathing care levels and further conduct longitudinal studies.
