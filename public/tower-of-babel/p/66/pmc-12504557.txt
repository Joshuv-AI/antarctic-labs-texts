Family satisfaction and healthcare providers’ moral distress in intensive care units: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Atena Shojaie, Sirous Pourkhajoei, Neda Asadi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractHealthcare providers in intensive care units face numerous moral distresses due to conflicts between professional principles, personal values, and the demands of patients’ families. In contrast, family satisfaction is considered one of the important indicators for evaluating the quality of medical services. Therefore, the present study aims to investigate the relationship between healthcare providers’ moral distress and family satisfaction in intensive care units. A cross-sectional study was conducted in four intensive care units with the collaboration of 128 family members and 70 healthcare providers (16 physicians and 54 nurses). The samples were selected using an available sampling method according to the inclusion criteria. Data were collected using the moral distress scale and the family satisfaction-ICU questionnaire. The results showed that mean scores for the frequency and intensity of moral distress were 49.88 ± 3.25 and 62.27 ± 2.51, respectively, indicating a moderate level of moral distress among ICU healthcare providers. The mean FS-ICU score was 59.83 ± 3.19, suggesting a generally good level of family satisfaction. The results revealed a significant inverse relationship between the frequency of moral distress and family satisfaction. This indicates that as the frequency of moral distress among ICU healthcare providers increases, family satisfaction tends to decrease (P = 0.03, r = − 0.7). These results emphasize the importance of reducing moral distress in healthcare settings through organizational support, the development of specific ethical strategies, and strengthening the communication skills of the healthcare team. Introduction

Moral distress is a psychological state experienced by healthcare professionals when they recognize ethically appropriate action but are constrained from acting due to institutional, legal, or contextual barriers1. This phenomenon, first conceptualized by Andrew Jameton in 1984, has garnered increasing attention, especially in high-stakes environments like intensive care units (ICUs), where ethical dilemmas are frequent and complex2. Research shows that the occurrence of a moral event, combined with psychological tension, is an essential condition for the creation of moral distress3.

Numerous studies have demonstrated that the level of moral distress among healthcare professionals, especially nurses and physicians, is higher than that observed in many other professions4. This moral tension, especially in nurses, can negatively impact patient care, lead to avoidance of certain clinical situations, and, ultimately, contribute to nurses leaving the profession5. Nurses are especially vulnerable due to their continuous interactions with patients and families, as well as the conflicting demands from colleagues, institutional policies, and resource limitations6. The relationship between moral distress and emotional exhaustion has been increasingly emphasized in recent studies, underlining its toll on clinicians’ mental well-being7.

Several studies have shown that various situations can cause moral tension among nurses. For instance, nurses experience greater moral distress when they are placed in care environments with a negative moral atmosphere8,9. Additionally, nurses may feel more moral distress when they are in situations where the care they provide is not in the best interests of the patient, such as when providing futile care10. Furthermore, moral tension is intensified when patients or their family members behave inappropriately or show disrespect toward the nurse11.

Several studies have been conducted in the field of moral distress. Patient and family satisfaction are important factors that influence the level of moral distress experienced by healthcare providers. A study by Anagnostopoulos et al.12 investigated the impact of doctors’ burnout on patient satisfaction and quality of care, showing that doctors’ burnout and emotional exhaustion are significantly related to patient satisfaction. Similarly, a study by Lamiani et al.11 explored the relationship between healthcare providers’ moral distress and family satisfaction in five special care units. The results indicated that doctors’ moral distress is related to the level of family involvement in the decision-making process.

A study by Dodek et al.13 investigated the intensity of psychological stress among Canadian ICU physicians. Among the 225 usable and completed surveys, moderate levels of moral distress were reported. One-third of the respondents indicated that they were considering leaving their position or had already left due to the stress, and about one-third met the criteria for burnout syndrome13.

Recent studies on COVID-19 have shown that the pandemic significantly exacerbates moral distress among medical staff. Factors contributing to this include the lack of complete knowledge and certainty regarding treatment methods, the severity and rapid spread of the virus, limited resources, and policies that intentionally separate patients from their families. These conditions have not only affected the psychological health of healthcare workers but have also compromised the quality of patient care14,15.

The results of Epstein and Hamric’s studies show that, as a result of prolonged exposure to moral distress, a phenomenon known as the “Crescendo effect” occurs. This means that when a doctor or nurse is placed in a new, morally distressing situation, it often triggers stronger reactions by recalling previous uncomfortable experiences16.

Family satisfaction with care in ICUs is another critical aspect influenced by moral distress. Studies have shown that inadequate family involvement in decision-making processes can heighten moral distress among healthcare providers, while high levels of staff burnout can negatively impact family satisfaction. This bidirectional relationship emphasizes the need for strategies that address both provider well-being and family engagement17,18.

While these studies have provided important insights, they were conducted in developed countries healthcare systems, which differ significantly from developing countries settings in terms of context, culture and organizational structures. In countries like Iran, cultural expectations about care delivery, communication, and authority vary considerably. These contextual differences can influence both the experience of moral distress among clinicians and the satisfaction levels of patients’ families19.

Moral distress is a widespread issue among medical staff, particularly nurses and doctors. Given that providing holistic nursing care is a fundamental professional value in nursing, the inability to achieve this goal due to moral distress can negatively impact the quality of care. Therefore, it is crucial to explore the factors that influence the intensity and frequency of moral distress, especially those related to the patient and their family20.

To date, few studies have examined these dynamics, where clinicians face unique cultural and systemic stressors that may exacerbate moral distress. Additionally, localized evidence on how family satisfaction interacts with provider well-being in this context remains scarce. Therefore, this study aims to extend prior research by exploring the relationship between clinicians’ moral distress and family satisfaction in Iranian ICUs. The replication of previous models in a culturally distinct healthcare system provides a necessary step toward validating and expanding our understanding of moral distress and its correlations. Our findings may inform locally relevant interventions and broader cross-national comparisons in the future. Given the critical importance of holistic, patient- and family-centered care in nursing, the presence of moral distress threatens not only the psychological health of caregivers but also the quality and humanity of care delivery. Exploring the contributing factors to moral distress, particularly those related to patient-family interactions, is thus essential. In this context, the present study investigates the relationship between healthcare providers’ moral distress and family satisfaction in the ICU setting.

Materials and methods

Study design

A cross-sectional study was conducted in collaboration with four ICUs in southeastern Iran. Questionnaires were administered to ICU physicians and nurses, as well as to the family members of patients admitted to the ICUs, during the same time as the healthcare providers’ survey. Data collection took place over two months in each ICU, from March to May 2024.

Ethics

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Kerman University of Medical Sciences (Approval No. IR.KMU.REC.1401.348), and all procedures were conducted under relevant national and international ethical guidelines, including the Declaration of Helsinki. Participation in the study was entirely voluntary for both healthcare providers and family members. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before data collection.

For healthcare providers, consent was obtained directly by the researchers at their workplace, after explaining the study’s purpose, procedures, and their right to withdraw at any time without any consequence. For family members of ICU patients, recruitment was conducted with sensitivity to their emotional and psychological state. They were approached only after receiving an initial assessment by the head nurse, ensuring that they were emotionally stable and willing to engage in communication. A trained research assistant who was not involved in direct patient care introduced the study in a quiet and private area outside the ICU, using simple and non-technical language to avoid additional stress.

Participants were explicitly informed that their decision to participate or decline would not affect the care their loved ones received. They were given ample time to consider participation and could ask questions freely. Voluntariness was emphasized, and no form of pressure or persuasion was applied. Only those who provided written informed consent were included in the study. Confidentiality and anonymity were strictly maintained throughout the study process, and all collected data were stored securely and used solely for research purposes.

Procedure

A meeting was held with each ICU team to introduce and explain the study objectives. Following the meeting, and based on a census sampling method, questionnaires were distributed to all healthcare providers. These questionnaires assessed healthcare providers’ sociodemographic characteristics and levels of moral distress. The self-administered questionnaires were completed voluntarily over 3 days. To ensure confidentiality, each questionnaire was assigned a random identification number.

The records of patients admitted during the period of healthcare providers’ survey completion were reviewed to assess their eligibility for participation in the study. Patients were considered eligible if they had an ICU stay of at least 24 h and had a Persian-speaking family member available to provide care. Sampling was conducted using a convenience sampling method, and the sample size was calculated based on previous similar studies11 using the following formula. The minimum required sample size was determined to be 85 participants. However, considering a design effect coefficient of 1.5 and the potential for dropout, the final estimated sample size was set at 128 patients. (α = 0.05, β = 0.2, r = 0.3).

Measures

Sociodemographic and clinical data

At the beginning of the questionnaire, healthcare providers were asked to provide information on their age, gender, marital status, years of experience in the ICU, and hours worked per week. Family members were asked to report their age, gender, level of education, relationship with the patient, previous experience with ICU care, place of residence, whether they currently live with the patient, the reason for the patient’s ICU admission, and the length of the ICU stay.

Moral distress scale (MDS)

This questionnaire has been used in numerous domestic and international studies. In Iran, Joolaee et al. (2012) modified the original questionnaire to better reflect the conditions of service provision in Iranian hospitals and to address existing challenges21. The adapted version was validated for both content and reliability, achieving a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.86. To assess test-retest reliability, the questionnaire was completed by 15 participants twice, with a two-week interval. Internal consistency was also confirmed, with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.87. The MDS consists of 24 items, each describing a specific situation encountered in hospital care. It evaluates two dimensions of nurses’ experiences: the intensity of moral distress and the frequency of stressful situations encountered. Nurses indicate how often they face situations that may lead to moral distress and rate the intensity of distress caused by each. The scale uses two 5-point Likert scales: one for the frequency of moral distress (ranging from 0 = never to 4 = very often) and one for the severity of distress (ranging from 0 = no distress to 4 = severe distress), with a total score ranging from 0 to 96. Higher scores indicate greater intensity and frequency of moral distress. Scoring interpretation is as follows: 0–31 indicates low intensity or frequency, 32–64 indicates moderate, and 65–96 reflects high levels of moral distress22.

Family satisfaction-ICU (FS-ICU)

The FS-ICU questionnaire was used to assess family satisfaction with care in ICU. This tool was validated by Wall et al. (2007b) and consists of 24 items that measure two dimensions: family satisfaction with care (14 items) and satisfaction with decision-making (10 items). Each item is rated by family members on a 5-point Likert-type scale, ranging from 1 = excellent to 5 = very poor. These responses are then converted to a 0–100 scale as follows: excellent = 80–100, very good = 60–79, good = 40–59, poor = 20–39, and very poor = 0–19. Higher scores indicate greater satisfaction with care23.

In Iran, the reliability and validity of the FS-ICU (including face, content, and construct validity) have been confirmed. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for both the individual dimensions and the total questionnaire was reported to be above 0.7, indicating acceptable internal consistency24.

Data analysis

Data was analyzed using SPSS version 22. Descriptive statistics, including frequency, percentage, mean, and standard deviation, were used to summarize the data. As the main variables in the study were normally distributed, the Pearson correlation coefficient was employed to assess the relationship between scores on the MDS and the FS-ICU questionnaire. A significant level of 0.05 was considered for all statistical tests.

Discussion

This study investigated the relationship between healthcare providers’ moral distress and family satisfaction in the ICU. The results revealed a moderate level of moral distress among ICU staff and an overall good level of family satisfaction with ICU care. Importantly, the main finding demonstrated an inverse relationship between healthcare providers’ moral distress and family satisfaction: as moral distress increased, family satisfaction significantly decreased.

This finding is consistent with the study by Lamiani et al. which explored the relationship between moral distress and family satisfaction in five ICUs and found that increased moral distress among clinicians was associated with a reduced sense of family inclusion in decision-making and decreased clarity of communication11. Similarly, in our study, specific FS-ICU items demonstrated statistically significant correlations with moral distress. For example, a higher frequency of moral distress was associated with lower satisfaction regarding the consistency of information and perceived support during decision-making. Likewise, increased intensity of moral distress was linked to reduced satisfaction with nurse communication and the clarity of explanations provided. These findings underscore the critical importance of compassionate and effective communication in mitigating both moral distress and dissatisfaction among families.

The study by Laurent et al. emphasized that meaningful involvement of families in care decisions can reduce moral distress among providers, highlighting the importance of relational and ethical environments in ICU settings25. This aligns with the systematic review by McAndrew et al. which confirmed that moral distress in the ICU is often related to poor communication, ethical ambiguity, and a lack of provider autonomy5.

In addition, Wong et al. reported that insufficient transparency and collaboration with families contribute to increased emotional tension, poor satisfaction, and reduced family control over patient care26. This supports our finding that FS-ICU items related to physician communication, such as “doctors discussing the patient’s condition” and “doctors caring for the family”, received the lowest satisfaction scores.

Notably, items related to emotional support, empathy, and clarity of explanations received the highest satisfaction ratings in our study. This contrast between high ratings for emotional support and low ratings for physician-family communication highlights the need for increased physician involvement in family-centered care. These findings support the conclusions of Epstein and Hamric, who described the protective role of empathetic communication in handling morally distressing situations16.

In another study, the feeling of respect, compassion, and empathy from physicians towards patients’ families was identified as a crucial factor in family satisfaction. The study also noted that a lack of effective communication negatively impacts this factor, which aligns with the findings of the present study27. In our study, the items related to doctors caring for family members and doctors discussing the patient’s condition with the family received the lowest scores in the FS-ICU.

Other studies have similarly identified compassion, respect, and empathy from physicians as key contributors to family satisfaction27. Our findings are in line with this evidence, as items reflecting physician attention to the family were rated lowest, signaling an area in need of intervention. While overall satisfaction levels were moderate to high, consistent with prior literature11,28, our findings highlight a persistent gap in physician communication that may undermine satisfaction and contribute to staff moral distress. This points to an opportunity for targeted improvements, especially considering that moral distress was significantly associated with reduced satisfaction in areas such as understanding the patient’s condition and support during decision-making.

The impact of the COVID-19 pandemic should also be considered. Previous research has shown that the pandemic intensified moral distress among healthcare professionals due to increased clinical uncertainty, limited family visitation, rapid patient decline, and heavier emotional burden29. These stressors may partially explain the heightened moral distress and reduced satisfaction observed in our study. Restrictions on family presence likely deepened emotional distance and strained communication, affecting both perceptions of care and provider well-being30.

Moreover, organizational culture plays a crucial role in shaping moral experiences in healthcare settings. Studies have shown that supportive leadership, ethical clarity, interprofessional collaboration, and access to ethics consultation can buffer against moral distress31. Our findings emphasize the importance of fostering an ethical climate that promotes open dialogue, supports healthcare professionals, and prioritizes family-centered practices.

Policy implications and conclusion

Despite the study’s strengths, several areas for improvement were identified. The generally good satisfaction levels were offset by communication challenges and limited family involvement in care planning. Targeted interventions, such as communication skills training, interdisciplinary ethics rounds, and structured family engagement protocols, may help alleviate moral distress and enhance satisfaction. Finally, our findings support the “Crescendo effect” described by Epstein and Hamric, where repeated experiences of moral distress accumulate over time and contribute to burnout and reduced quality of care. This reinforces the urgent need for institutional strategies to build moral resilience among ICU staff, particularly during ethically complex situations like end-of-life decisions or resource scarcity.

Considering the findings, several important implications for ICU policy and practice can be drawn. First, the significant association between healthcare providers’ moral distress and family satisfaction underscores the need for system-level interventions. Hospitals should prioritize the development of structured communication protocols in ICUs, including regular family meetings, transparent information-sharing systems, and clear roles for staff in family communication30,32. Second, there is a clear necessity for targeted staff training programs aimed at improving ethical decision-making, emotional resilience, and effective communication with families, especially during critical and end-of-life care. Third, institutional efforts should focus on establishing organizational support mechanisms, such as clinical ethics consultation services, peer support groups, and workload management strategies, to reduce the burden of moral distress among ICU staff29. Finally, given the cultural specificity of patient–family dynamics in Iran, ICU protocols should be adapted to align with local norms while promoting shared decision-making and family-centered care. Implementing these changes may not only improve the working environment for ICU staff but also enhance the overall experience and satisfaction of patients’ families.

Study limitation

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the study relied on self-reported questionnaires from both healthcare providers and family members, which may introduce recall bias or social desirability bias, potentially affecting the accuracy of responses. Second, the research was conducted in four ICUs located in a single geographic region. This limited geographic scope may reduce the generalizability of the findings to other healthcare settings or regions with different organizational cultures, staffing models, or patient demographics. Third, the sample included a disproportionate number of nurses (77%) compared to physicians (23%), which may have influenced the overall assessment of moral distress and its correlates. Nurses and physicians may experience and report moral distress differently due to the nature of their roles, responsibilities, and interactions with patients and families. Finally, due to the cross-sectional nature of the study, causality cannot be inferred. Future research should consider using multi-center designs with more diverse and balanced samples across professional roles, as well as longitudinal or mixed-method approaches to better understand causal pathways and contextual factors influencing both moral distress and family satisfaction in ICUs.
