A comparative study of ProGRP and CEA as serological markers in small cell lung cancer treatment
Authors: Mingjie Yu, Ping Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSmall Cell Lung Cancer (SCLC) presents a significant clinical challenge due to its aggressive nature and the need for effective biomarkers for treatment monitoring. This study aimed to compare ProGRP and CEA as serological markers in the monitoring of SCLC treatment.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed data from 80 SCLC patients and 80 matched controls. ProGRP and CEA levels were measured, and their associations with clinical parameters, including tumor stage and treatment response, were assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient. Multivariate logistic regression models were employed to identify independent predictors of treatment response and disease progression.ResultsProGRP and CEA levels were considerably higher in cases than controls, with median ProGRP levels at 198.5 pg/mL versus 48.7 pg/mL and median CEA levels at 5.2 ng/mL versus 2.9 ng/mL (both p < 0.001). ProGRP levels correlated positively with tumor stage (ρ = 0.58, p < 0.001) and negatively with treatment response (ρ = − 0.45, p = 0.001). CEA levels also showed positive correlation with tumor stage (ρ = 0.48, p = 0.002) and negative correlation with treatment response (ρ = − 0.35, p = 0.005). Multivariate analysis revealed that ProGRP was an independent predictor for treatment response (OR 1.25 per 100 pg/mL increase, p = 0.001) and disease progression (OR 1.25 per 50 pg/mL increase, p = 0.012), while CEA was a marginal predictor for treatment response (OR 0.95 per 1 ng/mL increase, p = 0.045).ConclusionBoth ProGRP and CEA are significant serological markers in SCLC patients, with ProGRP showing a stronger correlation with tumor stage and treatment response. ProGRP may serve as a superior independent predictor of treatment response and disease progression compared to CEA. These findings support the incorporation of ProGRP in SCLC treatment monitoring protocols.

Introduction

Lung cancer remains one of the most prevalent and deadly malignancies worldwide, with small cell lung cancer (SCLC) accounting for approximately 15% of all lung cancer cases [1]. Characterized by rapid growth and early metastasis, SCLC poses significant challenges in both diagnosis and treatment. Despite advancements in therapy, the prognosis for SCLC patients is often poor, with a five-year survival rate that remains discouraging [2]. In this context, effective monitoring of treatment response is crucial for optimizing therapeutic strategies and improving patient outcomes [3].

Serological biomarkers have emerged as valuable tools in the management of lung cancer, providing non-invasive methods for diagnosis, prognosis, and monitoring of treatment efficacy. Among the various biomarkers investigated for SCLC, Pro-gastrin-releasing peptide (ProGRP) and carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) have gained attention due to their clinical relevance and potential utility [4]. ProGRP, a precursor of gastrin-releasing peptide, is a relatively specific marker for SCLC, while CEA is a well-established marker used in a variety of cancers, including non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) [5].

The differential expression of these markers in SCLC patients provides a rationale for comparative studies to evaluate their effectiveness. Previous research has suggested that ProGRP might possess superior specificity for SCLC due to its limited expression in other tissues, while CEA is known for its broader expression in both malignant and benign conditions, potentially leading to less specificity [6, 7]. However, the sensitivity of CEA, particularly in conjunction with other markers, could offer a complementary advantage in the detection and monitoring of SCLC [8].

Treatment monitoring in SCLC is particularly challenging due to the aggressive nature of the disease and the rapid development of resistance to chemotherapy and radiotherapy. The early identification of treatment response is critical, as it can guide decisions regarding the continuation or alteration of therapeutic regimens [9]. Moreover, the detection of relapse through biomarker surveillance can be pivotal in the timely initiation of second-line treatments [10]. Consequently, the comparative efficacy of ProGRP and CEA in reflecting the tumor burden and treatment response in SCLC must be thoroughly investigated.

This study aims to address the gap in knowledge regarding the performance of ProGRP and CEA as serological markers in SCLC treatment monitoring. By conducting a comprehensive comparative analysis, we seek to determine the sensitivity, specificity, and overall utility of these biomarkers in reflecting treatment response and disease progression in patients undergoing therapy for SCLC. The findings of this study could significantly impact clinical practice by informing the selection of biomarkers for routine monitoring and potentially improving the clinical outcomes for patients with this aggressive disease.

Material and methods

Study design and participants

This study was designed as a retrospective case–control investigation. From November 2021 to December 2023, we carefully compiled data from 80 patients with a confirmed diagnosis of small cell lung cancer (SCLC), alongside a control group comprising 80 age and sex-matched individuals without a history of any cancer. Inclusion criteria for the SCLC group included a histological or cytological confirmation of SCLC, documented serological measurements of ProGRP and CEA at baseline and during treatment, and adherence to a treatment regimen consistent with the current standards of care for SCLC. The control group was selected from a pool of patients who presented to the hospital for routine health check-ups and had serum samples available for analysis. Exclusion criteria for all participants were any prior cancer diagnosis, concurrent serious systemic diseases, or incomplete serological data. The protocol for this study received approval from the The Ethics Review Committee of Bozhou City People’s Hospital, ensuring that all procedures adhered to ethical guidelines. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, thereby authorizing the use of their medical data and serum samples for research purposes.

With these parameters, initial calculations indicated that a substantial number of participants would be required. However, due to logistical constraints and participant availability, we adjusted the total sample size to 80 cases and 80 controls, acknowledging that this reduces the study’s power to detect smaller differences.

Participant recruitment

With the calculated sample size in mind, we proceeded to recruit 80 SCLC patients from our institution’s cancer registry who met the inclusion criteria. Concurrently, 80 control participants without a history of any cancer were selected from the general population who had visited our outpatient department for routine health check-ups during the same period. Controls were matched to cases based on age, sex, and smoking status to minimize confounding variables.

Sample collection and processing

Serum samples from both cases and controls were systematically collected following a standardized protocol, with slight modifications [11]. For the cases, samples were taken at initial presentation (prior to the initiation of any treatment) and subsequently after every two cycles of chemotherapy, totaling four to six samples per patient depending on the number of treatment cycles completed. For controls, one sample was obtained during their routine health examination. Blood was drawn under aseptic conditions into serum separator tubes and allowed to clot for 30 min at room temperature. Centrifugation was performed at 1500 × g for 15 min to separate the serum, which was subsequently aliquoted into cryovials and stored at − 80 °C until analysis to prevent degradation of the biomarkers. All SCLC patients were grouped together for the purpose of measuring biomarker concentrations. However, the monitoring of these concentrations over time, especially after every two cycles of chemotherapy, allows for individualized assessment of disease response within the group.

Biomarker assays

Quantitative measurements of ProGRP and CEA levels in the serum were carried out using commercially available enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kitskits (Thermo Fisher Scientific), following the manufacturers’ instructions to ensure consistency and reliability of the results [12]. The assays were conducted in a blinded fashion, with laboratory personnel unaware of the case or control status of the samples. Each sample was run in duplicate to assure precision, and calibration curves were established for every plate to calculate the concentrations of the biomarkers. The limit of detection and the range of quantification for both ProGRP and CEA assays were established prior to the commencement of the study, and all assays were validated for accuracy, reproducibility, and specificity.

Disease progression monitoring and time to diagnosis

To monitor disease progression in patients with SCLC, biomarker levels were measured at key intervals during the treatment regimen. The first sample was collected at initial presentation, providing a baseline measurement of ProGRP and CEA levels prior to any therapeutic intervention. Subsequent samples were collected after every two cycles of chemotherapy, aligning with the clinical assessment points to detect changes in biomarker levels that might indicate disease progression or response to treatment.

The biomarkers were measured at 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, and 12 months, corresponding approximately to the completion of chemotherapy cycles. Changes in biomarker levels were closely monitored to assess their correlation with the clinical course of the disease. An increase in biomarker levels could suggest disease progression, while a decrease would likely indicate a response to treatment.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS statistical software, with a significance level set at p < 0.05. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the baseline characteristics of the study population, including means and standard deviations for continuous variables and frequencies and percentages for categorical variables [13]. The Mann–Whitney U test was employed to compare the levels of ProGRP and CEA between cases and controls due to the non-normal distribution of the biomarkers. To assess the utility of ProGRP and CEA in monitoring treatment response, changes in biomarker levels from baseline were analyzed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Correlations between biomarker levels and clinical parameters, such as tumor stage and treatment response, were determined using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient. Cox proportional hazard model was constructed to identify independent predictors of treatment response and disease progression [14]. All statistical tests were two-sided.

Discussion

In this study, we identified ProGRP and CEA levels among a Chinese population afflicted with SCLC. There was a significant difference in median ProGRP and CEA levels between SCLC cases and controls (p < 0.001 for both), underlining the potential of these markers in differentiating between diseased and healthy states. Furthermore, the positive correlation of both ProGRP and CEA levels with tumor stage, and the inverse correlation with treatment response, as detailed in Table 3, suggested that these biomarkers could reflect disease burden and response to therapy. The statistical significance of these correlations (p-values ranging from < 0.001 to 0.005) lent weight to the reliability of these markers.

The findings from the multivariate logistic regression analyses indicated that ProGRP levels were an independent predictor of both treatment response and disease progression (OR 1.25 per 100 pg/mL increase for treatment response, p = 0.001; OR 1.25 per 50 pg/mL increase for disease progression, p = 0.012). CEA levels also emerged as a marginal predictor for treatment response (OR 0.95 per 1 ng/mL increase, p = 0.045). Notably, smoking status was inversely associated with treatment response, indicating a potential confounding factor in the management of SCLC. The study’s findings suggest that ProGRP, in particular, could be a more reliable marker for monitoring SCLC treatment compared to CEA. The robust statistical associations observed for ProGRP with clinical parameters highlight its importance in the clinical setting.

Cavalieri et al. [15] have previously reported that ProGRP levels were significantly higher in patients with SCLC compared to those with NSCLC, which is consistent with the findings of our study indicating elevated ProGRP levels in cases compared to controls. The specificity and sensitivity of ProGRP in Cavalieri et al. study were high, reinforcing the utility of ProGRP as a diagnostic marker for SCLC. However, unlike our study which found significant differences in CEA levels between cases and controls, they did not find significant differences in CEA levels between SCLC and NSCLC patients. This could be due to different patient selection criteria, disease stages, or the statistical power of the respective studies.

Huang et al. [16] also investigated ProGRP, NSE, and CEA in various lung conditions. They found ProGRP to have high sensitivity and specificity for SCLC diagnosis, similar to our study’s findings. Additionally, they suggested the combination of ProGRP and CEA for the diagnosis of adenocarcinoma, which is in line with our findings where CEA levels also had predictive value. However, our study did not compare the diagnostic performance of combined biomarkers, which is a notable difference.

Huang and Ren [17] further demonstrated the diagnostic value of combining serum biomarkers with imaging techniques. They found that the combined approach had higher sensitivity and specificity than single tests, which is in agreement with our study’s implication of ProGRP and CEA as significant independent predictors of treatment response. The similarity here suggests that a multi-modal diagnostic approach, incorporating biomarkers and imaging, is superior for lung cancer diagnosis and treatment monitoring.

Our findings revealed that ProGRP and CEA levels were significantly higher in lung cancer cases compared to controls, and these biomarkers were correlated with tumor stage and treatment response. This finding is consistent with the study of Li et al. [18], which found that ProGRP levels in SCLC patients decreased significantly following chemotherapy in responsive patients, but not in non-responsive patients. The correlation between ProGRP levels and treatment response supports our observation of an inverse correlation between treatment response and ProGRP levels, although our study encompassed lung cancer cases more broadly and did not specify SCLC exclusively. Furthermore, Li et al. [18] noted that changes in ProGRP levels were more accurate than NSE levels for monitoring chemotherapy effects in SCLC patients. This suggests a potential similarity with our findings where ProGRP served as a reliable biomarker for assessing treatment response. However, Li group also highlighted that ProGRP levels at diagnosis could predict the objective response to chemotherapy, especially in patients with initially higher ProGRP levels. Our study did not specifically measure the predictive value of initial ProGRP levels on treatment response, which represents a gap when compared to Li group findings.

The study by Benjamin et al. [19] also supports our findings regarding the diagnostic utility of ProGRP, showing it had better performance than NSE in differentiating between SCLC and benign pulmonary disease (BPD) or NSCLC. This aligns with our conclusion that ProGRP is an important biomarker in lung cancer, although they focused on the diagnostic rather than prognostic utility. Furthermore, they reported a significant association of low ProGRP levels with high-grade NSCLC tumors, which is somewhat converse to our finding where higher ProGRP was associated with advanced disease stages. It is possible that this discrepancy could be due to differences in study design, patient populations, or the definition of “high-grade” tumors. In addition, Benjamin et al. [19] found that ProGRP was associated with survival in both NSCLC and SCLC in univariate analysis but did not maintain significance in multivariate analysis when other factors like performance status and CYFRA 21–1 were considered. Our study similarly analyzed the multivariate context but focused on treatment response and disease progression rather than survival as an endpoint.

The potential prognostic significance of ProGRP in SCLC has been a subject of ongoing research, with initial skepticism giving way to more nuanced understanding. Subsequent research focused on the prognostic implications of ProGRP levels, adopting a threshold of 50 ng/L to stratify patients based on survival outcomes. It was found that an overwhelming majority of SCLC patients (over 80%) exhibited elevated ProGRP levels prior to treatment initiation [20, 21]. A pivotal study delineated the survival impact of ProGRP, revealing that patients with limited-stage cancers and pretreatment ProGRP levels below 410 ng/L experienced a median overall survival of 27 months. In contrast, those with levels exceeding 410 ng/L had a median survival of just 18 months [22]. In a cohort including a substantial proportion (66%) of advanced-stage cancer patients, a higher ProGRP threshold of 800 ng/L was linked to markedly poorer prognoses [19, 23]. The initial ProGRP level has been corroborated as a critical factor in survival prognosis, as evidenced by the determination of an optimal ProGRP cutoff at 140 ng/L, which was derived in the context of gauging the therapeutic response to chemotherapy in SCLC patients [20]. The study composition has been identified as a determinant of the prognostic value attributed to ProGRP.

Further studies by Ono et al. [24] and Sunaga et al. [25] have substantiated the prognostic value of ProGRP, with the former reporting an association between the percentage change in ProGRP levels before the third chemotherapy cycle and one-year survival, and the latter noting that a 50% reduction in ProGRP post-treatment indicated improved two-year survival prospects. The duration of follow-up is also a critical factor, with survival curve divergences based on pre-treatment marker levels becoming more pronounced with longer observation periods [19, 25, 26]. Research conducted at the Oncology Center in Cracow, which included predominantly limited-disease (LD) SCLC patients, confirmed the link between initial ProGRP levels and survival [27, 28]. However, when multivariate analyses factored in disease stage, performance status (PS), and NSE concentrations, ProGRP did not emerge as an independent prognostic indicator.

Conversely, Nisman et al. [19] provided evidence of ProGRP’s independence as a prognostic marker, demonstrating that patients with initial ProGRP levels over 140 ng/L faced a death risk more than four times higher than those with lower levels. This risk was comparatively higher than that associated with poor PS, non-response to treatment, and advanced disease stage [29]. Notably, these studies did not evaluate NSE as a prognostic factor. Consensus amongst researchers posits disease stage as the primary prognostic indicator in cancer patient evaluations. While NSE levels are closely correlated with disease stage in SCLC patients, ProGRP’s association is less pronounced. Consequently, NSE is retained as an independent survival predictor in multivariate analyses that include clinical parameters and both biomarkers’ concentrations.

Future implications of our study include the potential integration of ProGRP level monitoring into routine clinical practice for managing SCLC. Further prospective studies could validate the utility of ProGRP as a prognostic tool and help refine treatment strategies based on biomarker levels. Additionally, these findings may encourage the exploration of other novel biomarkers with similar or greater efficacy in predicting treatment response and disease progression in SCLC patients.

This study, while providing valuable insights into the potential utility of ProGRP and CEA as biomarkers for the monitoring of SCLC treatment, had several limitations that must be considered when interpreting the findings. Firstly, the retrospective nature of the analysis introduced inherent biases associated with the selection of patient records and the historical collection of data. Such biases potentially affect the generalizability of the results to broader and more diverse patient populations. Secondly, the sample size, though adequate for initial observations, was relatively small, limiting the power of the study to detect smaller effects that might be clinically relevant. This sample size constraint also restricted the capacity for subgroup analyses, which could have provided more nuanced insights into the applicability of the biomarkers across different stages of the disease and demographic categories. Thirdly, the study relied solely on serological markers without integrating other potential diagnostic and prognostic factors, such as imaging studies or genetic profiles, which may have provided a more comprehensive understanding of the disease state and treatment outcomes. The exclusion of these factors limited the scope of the analysis and the depth of the conclusions that could be drawn.

Conclusion

As per our findings, the evaluation of ProGRP and CEA as serological markers revealed their elevated levels in patients with SCLC compared to a control group, indicating their potential role in disease identification. The observed correlations of these biomarkers with tumor stage and treatment response provided evidence of their relevance in reflecting disease burden and the effectiveness of therapeutic interventions. ProGRP in particular demonstrated a stronger association with both the extent of the disease and the outcomes of treatment, suggesting its superiority over CEA in monitoring disease progression and response to therapy. Despite the limitations inherent to the study design, such as its retrospective nature and the relatively small patient cohort, the results consistently pointed towards ProGRP robustness as a predictive marker for treatment outcomes. Furthermore, the statistical analyses supported the independent predictive value of ProGRP levels for both treatment response and disease progression, even when adjusted for confounding variables such as age and smoking status. The assessments further underscored the potential utility of integrating ProGRP level monitoring into the clinical management of SCLC, proposing a shift in current biomarker-based protocols. Future research directions were implied, advocating for prospective studies with larger and more diverse populations, standardized treatments, and a multidimensional approach to prognostic evaluations. The conclusions of this study, while preliminary, lay a foundational basis for enhancing the precision of SCLC monitoring and tailoring individualized treatment strategies.
