Disease Burden Attributable to Hip Fractures in Asian Women
Authors: Weijun Sun, Zhangsheng Dai, Xiaocong Lin, Kaibin Fang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study is the first to comprehensively evaluate the disease burden of hip fractures in Asian women, presenting epidemiological trends from 1990 to 2023.MethodsData on hip fractures among women in Asian countries and regions were sourced from the 2023 Global Burden of Disease(GBD) Study, covering incidence, prevalence, and years lived with disability (YLDs) from 1990 to 2023. Secondary analysis of these data yielded the overall disease burden of hip fractures and attributable risk factors among Asian women. Building on this information, we further reported the epidemiological trends of hip fractures among women across Asian countries and regions. We used an autoregressive integrated moving average (ARIMA) model to project the disease burden of hip fractures among Asian women over the next 15 years.ResultsIn 2023, the total incidence of hip fracture among Asian women was 6,307,956 (4,029,635–9,519,173), with an ASIR of 278.43 (178–419.48). Based on Joinpoint regression, the AAPC of the age-standardized incidence rate (ASIR) of hip fractures in Asian women from 1990 to 2023 was 1.79 (95% CI: 1.74–1.84), P < 0.05. Falls are the leading cause of hip fractures among Asian women. Based on the ARIMA model, by 2040 the age-standardized incidence rate (ASIR) of hip fracture in Asian women is projected to be 346.62 (95% CI: 299.40–393.84) per 100,000, with 10,699,484 (6,416,769–14,982,198) new cases.ConclusionIn Asia, hip fracture in women constitutes a substantial and growing disease burden; promoting fall-prevention initiatives across the continent is essential to curb further increases.Keywords: female, hip fracture, disease burden, incidence, Asia

Female hip fracture is a common and devastating injury, posing the greatest threat to elderly women.1 It usually results from a decline in bone strength caused by osteoporosis and can occur during a minor fall—or even routine daily activities.2 Because of its high disability and mortality rates and slow recovery, it is often called “the last fracture of life”.3 The risk is markedly higher in women than in men, especially among Asian populations.4 After menopause, estrogen levels drop sharply; since estrogen is essential for maintaining bone mineral density, this decline accelerates bone loss, leaving the skeleton porous and fragile.5 In some cases, no obvious trauma is required—simply twisting or stumbling slightly is enough to break the hip.6 Falls are recognized as the dominant precipitating factor, and women are believed to face a greater fall risk than men.7

Across Asia, hip-fracture incidence varies enormously between countries.8 Earlier work reported age-standardized rates of 180.72 per 100,000 in China,9 141.7 per 100,000 in Qatar,10 and 207 per 100,000 in South Korea.11 Yet no study has focused specifically on the epidemiology of hip fracture in Asian women—precisely the subgroup at highest risk.

Understanding the trends unique to Asian women is indispensable for designing targeted prevention and control strategies.12 The Global Burden of Disease (GBD) study is the world’s most comprehensive health resource, pooling sex-specific data for 204 countries and territories from 1990 to 2023 and quantifying the burden of 459 conditions, including hip fracture.13 Using the newly released GBD 2023 dataset, we conducted a secondary analysis to derive burden metrics for hip fracture in women across Asian countries and territories.A thorough analysis of the epidemiological trends of hip fractures in women is essential for directing public health resources towards targeted screening, prevention, and rehabilitation support for high-risk groups, thereby significantly improving patient outcomes and quality of life.14 While previous research on the burden of hip fractures has largely concentrated on global estimates and all-age populations, our study offers a focused examination of women, who are at high risk for these fractures,15 and presents the first comprehensive report on the disease burden of hip fractures specifically in the female population across Asia.

Methods

Data Sources

GBD 2023 is a comprehensive epidemiological assessment that integrates multiple data streams—from national health surveys, censuses, vital registration systems, and health-information databases—into a single analytical framework.16 Standardized protocols are applied to ensure cross-national comparability of health metrics, enabling the estimation of key indicators such as incidence and prevalence. The GBD initiative adopts the International Classification of Diseases (ICD) framework for systematic disease coding and classification, allowing standardized, population-wide analyses of health trends.17

Due to the lack of direct data for “Asia” as a single entity in the GBD database, information for Asian countries and territories was collected individually. According to the GBD regional order in the database, the disease burden of female hip fractures for Afghanistan, Armenia, Azerbaijan, Bahrain, Bangladesh, Bhutan, Brunei Darussalam, Cambodia, China, Cyprus, Democratic People’s Republic of Korea, Georgia, India, Indonesia, Iraq, Israel, Japan, Jordan, Kazakhstan, Kuwait, Kyrgyzstan, Lao People’s Democratic Republic, Lebanon, Malaysia, Maldives, Mongolia, Myanmar, Nepal, Oman, Pakistan, Palestine, Philippines, Qatar, Republic of Korea, Saudi Arabia, Singapore, Sri Lanka, Syrian Arab Republic, Taiwan (Province of China), Tajikistan, Thailand, Timor-Leste, Turkey, Turkmenistan, United Arab Emirates, Uzbekistan, Vietnam, and Yemen were extracted.

Based on the “Injuries by nature” category in the GBD 2023 database, we extracted disease burden of female hip fractures for 20 age groups: “<5”, “5 to 9”, “10 to 14”, “15 to 19”, “20–24”, “25 to 29”, “30 to 34”, “35 to 39”, “40–44”, “45 to 49”, “50 to 54”, “55 to 59”, “60–64”, “65 to 69”, “70 to 74”, “75 to 79”, 80–84”, “85 to 89”, “90 to 94”, “95 plus”.

Statistical Analysis

Data on the incidence, prevalence, and years lived with disability (YLDs) for female hip fractures across Asian countries and territories were obtained from the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2023 study. Subsequently, we calculated the age-standardized rates for incidence (ASIR), prevalence (ASPR), and YLDs, reporting each estimate along with its corresponding 95% uncertainty interval (UI).

Age-Standardized Rate

To estimate disease burden, we used age-standardized rates per 100,000 population. These rates were calculated to enable valid comparisons across regions and over time by adjusting for differences in population age structure. Absolute case numbers were also reported to reflect the tangible public-health burden and to guide resource-allocation decisions. Our age-standardized indicators included ASIR, ASPR, and the age-standardized YLD rate. The direct method was used for standardization, thereby removing the confounding effect of different age distributions and ensuring comparability of disease-burden estimates. Calculations were performed with the ageadjust.direct function in the epitools package. This function requires three inputs: the observed number of cases, the population size in each age stratum of the study population, and the proportional age distribution of the standard population. Through this process, age-standardized estimates were generated, allowing comparable assessment of disease burden across different time periods and regions and providing a more accurate description of epidemiological distribution patterns. Age-standardized calculations were performed with population data from the GBD database(https://ghdx.healthdata.org/record/ihme-data/gbd-2023-demographics-1950-2023). Age-specific population counts for Asia were obtained by summing the corresponding counts from 48 selected Asian countries and territories, and standardization was carried out using the global age-specific population proportions.

Average Annual Percent Change

Temporal patterns in ASIR, ASPR and age-standardized YLD rates were evaluated with Joinpoint (v.5.1.0.0) segmented regression, yielding average annual percentage change (AAPC) estimates and 95% confidence intervals.18 This approach identifies the most parsimonious set of statistically significant inflection points while permitting user-defined limits on joinpoint number to preserve model simplicity.

Risk Factors

To further explore risk factors for hip fracture in women, we used the GBD 2023 database to quantify the proportion of disease burden attributable to 18 specific risk factors.

Autoregressive Integrated Moving Average

An autoregressive integrated moving-average (ARIMA) model was used to forecast the disease burden of hip fracture among Asian women over the next 15 years. Widely applied in medical research, ARIMA integrates three components—autoregression (AR), integration (I), and moving average (MA)—to capture trends, seasonality, and random fluctuations in time-series data.19

Results

Disease Burden in 2023

In 2023, the total incidence of hip fracture among Asian women was 6,307,956 (4,029,635–9,519,173), with an ASIR of 278.43 (178–419.48). The overall prevalence was 12,151,995 (9,795,434–15,277,655), yielding an ASPR of 536.94 (432.57–674.67). Total YLDs amounted to 1,377,208 (919,885–1,979,202), and the age-standardized YLDs rate was 60.46 (40.41–86.76). Joinpoint Regression

Based on Joinpoint regression, the AAPC of the age-standardized incidence rate (ASIR) of hip fractures in Asian women from 1990 to 2023 was 1.79 (95% CI: 1.74–1.84), P < 0.05. The AAPC of the age-standardized prevalence rate (ASPR) of hip fractures in Asian women was 2.23 (95% CI: 2.20–2.27), P < 0.05. The AAPC of the age-standardized years-lived-with-disability (YLDs) rate for hip fractures in Asian women was 0.65 (95% CI: 0.61–0.69), P < 0.05. Autoregressive Integrated Moving Average

Based on the ARIMA model, by 2040 the age-standardized incidence rate (ASIR) of hip fracture in Asian women is projected to be 346.62 (95% CI: 299.40–393.84) per 100,000, with 10,699,484 (6,416,769–14,982,198) new cases; the age-standardized prevalence rate (ASPR) is forecast at 656.59 (586.53–726.65) per 100,000, corresponding to 19,163,671 (5,828,938–32,498,403) prevalent cases; and the age-standardized years-lived-with-disability (YLDs) rate is expected to reach 62.56 (54.44–70.68) per 100,000, totaling 1,678,883 (1,509,324–1,848,442) YLDs. Discussion

Based on the newly released GBD 2023 database, Asian women experienced 6.31 million incident hip fractures in 2023, with an ASIR of 278.43 per 100,000. These figures underscore the enormous disease burden that hip fracture imposes on Asian women. In addition, about 12.15 million Asian women are currently living with the consequences of a previous hip fracture—a number far higher than the annual incidence because the injury often leads to long-term disability.20 Hip fracture typically causes prolonged pain, limited mobility, loss of independence and even permanent bed-rest.21 Consequently, Asian women accrued 1,377,208 YLDs from hip fracture in 2023. Many patients never regain their pre-fracture mobility, require continuous care and suffer markedly reduced quality of life; thus the disability burden greatly exceeds the mortality directly attributable to the fracture.22 Our analyses show that hip fracture is an extremely common and serious public-health problem for Asian women. Age-specific rates reveal a steep rise in ASIR with advancing age, especially after 70 years. Post-menopausal women are at highest risk for osteoporosis, and skeletal fragility is the principal underlying factor.23 Rapid population ageing across Asia further amplifies this burden, because age is the strongest risk factor for hip fracture: as bone mass declines, even minor trauma can precipitate a fracture.24

Based on Joinpoint regression analysis, the AAPC for the ASIR of hip fractures among Asian women was 1.79. This indicates a significant increase in the ASIR of hip fractures in Asian women, presenting a major public health challenge. The AAPC for the ASPR was 2.23, which is higher than the growth rate of the ASIR. This finding suggests improvements in the healthcare system, allowing more women to survive after experiencing a hip fracture. However, it also implies that the population of disabled patients requiring care after hip fractures is expanding rapidly.25 This places enormous and continuously growing economic and caregiving pressures on the healthcare system, long-term care services, families, and society as a whole.26 The AAPC for the Age-Standardized YLDs rate was 0.65, significantly lower than the AAPC values for ASIR and ASPR. This result reflects advancements in the treatment of hip fractures among Asian women, enabling many patients to avoid long-term disability.27 Surgical methods for treating hip fractures, such as hemiarthroplasty and PFNA, have become widely available in many Asian countries.28 Many hip fracture patients are also able to undergo surgery promptly.29 Furthermore, postoperative patients are encouraged to mobilize early, which shortens the duration of severe disability.30 Nevertheless, the persistently rising ASIR and ASPR still indicate that hip fractures are evolving into an increasingly severe crisis for Asian women. There is an urgent need to establish sustainable, high-quality long-term care and rehabilitation service systems for the growing population of Asian women who are survivors of hip fractures, in order to address the challenges posed by the increasing disease burden.31

Based on our findings, the overwhelming majority of incident hip fractures, prevalent cases and fracture-related YLDs among Asian women are attributable to falls. This underscores the critical importance of fall prevention in curbing the hip-fracture epidemic.32 It should be noted, however, that falls are not the sole causative factor; rather, falls and osteoporosis act synergistically to generate the enormous disease burden currently observed.33 ARIMA projections indicate that this burden will continue to rise, highlighting the urgent need for targeted, evidence-based interventions. Practical measures include installing non-slip flooring and grab bars to improve home safety, and promoting balance-enhancing activities such as tai-chi or yoga for older women to prevent falls in the first place.34 Concurrently, calcium and vitamin D supplementation, resistance exercise and, when appropriate, clinician-guided anti-osteoporosis pharmacotherapy can increase bone mineral density and strength, thereby reducing the risk of fracture if a fall does occur.35 For older or postmenopausal women, supplementation with calcium and vitamin D can be considered to reduce the risk of hip fracture.36 After menopause, the inhibitory effect of estrogen on osteoclasts diminishes, leading to accelerated bone resorption and rapid bone loss.37 Supplementing with calcium and vitamin D serves as fundamental nutritional support to help compensate for this rapid bone loss.38 Any public-health programme aimed at hip fracture must therefore place fall prevention at its core.39

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, GBD data rely on model-based estimates, and variations in the quality of raw data across regions and countries may introduce bias. This could lead to inaccurate estimates of the true disease burden of hip fracture in women; for example, under-reporting in low- and middle-income countries due to inadequate regional infrastructure and policy frameworks, or overestimation in countries where data are predominantly derived from urban settings. In addition, the results of the projection model may be influenced by future interventions. The absence of out-of-sample validation, diagnostic tests, or sensitivity analysis to assess the reliability of the predictions constitutes one of the limitations of this study. The fact that femoral neck fractures and intertrochanteric fractures were not discussed separately is also a limitation of this study.

Conclusion

In Asia, hip fracture in women constitutes a substantial and growing disease burden; promoting fall-prevention initiatives across the continent is essential to curb further increases.
