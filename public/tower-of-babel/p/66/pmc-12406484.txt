Investigating serum NRF2 as a screening biomarker and potential therapeutic target in diabetic foot ulcers: a prospective observational study
Authors: Wentao Yang, Hui Cao, Song Lin, Fangtao Tian
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThe study aimed to investigate the relationship between serum nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (NRF2) levels and the severity of diabetic foot ulcers (DFU) and to identify potential risk factors in severe DFU patients. Understanding this relationship could offer insights into mechanisms underlying DFU progression and inform diagnostic and therapeutic strategies.MethodsThis prospective observational study included 195 DFU patients from January 2023 to May 2024, categorized into mild and severe groups using the Wagner classification system. Serum NRF2 and C-reactive protein (CRP), interleukin-6 (IL-6), interleukin-17 (IL-17), and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) levels were measured at multiple timepoints during treatment. Statistical analyses, including logistic regression and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, were performed to evaluate NRF2's diagnostic value and its association with DFU severity.ResultsWe found that severe DFU patients had significantly lower serum NRF2 levels and higher serum IL-6, IL-17, and CRP levels compared to those in the mild group. NRF2 levels were negatively correlated with IL-6 levels. ROC curve analysis demonstrated that NRF2 was a reliable screening biomarker for severe DFU, with notable sensitivity and specificity. In addition, logistic regression identified reduced NRF2 levels as an independent risk factor for severe DFU.ConclusionsSerum NRF2 levels could serve as a valuable screening biomarker for identifying severe DFU cases. Integrating NRF2 measurements with other biomarkers could improve patient stratification and clinical outcomes. Further research is needed to validate its role and therapeutic potential in DFU management.Keywords: Diabetic foot ulcer, NRF2, Inflammatory markers, Biomarkers, Oxidative stress

Introduction

The International Diabetes Federation (IDF) reported in 2017 that the global prevalence of diabetes was 8.8%, and it is projected to increase to 9.9% by 2045, making it a global health issue [1]. Diabetic foot ulcers (DFU) are a common and severe complication of diabetes mellitus, affecting a significant proportion of diabetic patients worldwide [2]. It is estimated that up to 15–25% of individuals with diabetes will develop a DFU during their lifetime [3]. DFUs not only contribute to a substantial economic burden but also severely impact the quality of life of affected individuals, often leading to disability or amputation in severe cases [4]. The severity of DFUs is influenced by various factors, including the depth and extent of the ulcer, the presence of comorbidities, such as peripheral arterial disease and neuropathy, and the patient’s overall glycemic control [5]. Despite advancements in wound care and treatment strategies, the management of DFUs remains challenging, and severe cases continue to be a major concern for both patients and clinicians [6, 7].

Serum biomarkers have emerged as valuable tools for assessing the pathophysiology and severity of DFU [8]. Inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin-6 (IL-6), interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β), and C-reactive protein (CRP) have been widely studied in the context of DFUs, as they are implicated in the inflammatory processes that contribute to impaired wound healing [9, 10]. Recently, nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (NRF2), a key regulator of cellular oxidative stress response, has gained attention as a potential biomarker in various diseases, including diabetes and its complications [11]. NRF2 plays a crucial role in modulating the inflammatory response and oxidative stress, both of which are central to the pathogenesis of DFU [12, 13]. Elevated NRF2 expression has been associated with improved wound healing [14], suggesting its potential as a diagnostic tool for identifying severe DFUs and guiding therapeutic strategies. A recent animal study also showed that activating the NRF2 signaling pathway affected oxidative stress, inflammation, and autophagy in wound tissue, thereby promoting wound healing in DFU rats [15].

Given the significant burden of delayed diagnosis and poor prognosis in severe DFUs, there is a pressing clinical need for reliable biomarkers that can assist in early identification and risk stratification. Although NRF2 has been shown to modulate oxidative stress and inflammation—two key pathways in DFU pathogenesis—its value as a clinical biomarker remains unclear. We hypothesize that lower serum NRF2 levels are associated with increased DFU severity and that NRF2 may serve as a useful screening biomarker in clinical practice. Early detection of severe DFU patients through NRF2 assessment may guide timely intervention and improve outcomes. Therefore, the aim of this prospective observational study was to investigate the relationship between serum NRF2 levels and the severity of DFU, and to identify potential risk factors in patients with severe DFU. Understanding the role of NRF2 in severe DFU may provide valuable insights into ulcer progression mechanisms and support the development of new clinical strategies for managing patients at higher risk.

Methods

Participants

This prospective observational study included 195 DFU patients who were treated at our hospital from January 2023 to May 2024. The diagnosis of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) was in line with the guidelines for prevention and treatment of type 2 diabetes in China (2017 edition) [16], including (1) typical diabetes symptoms (polydipsia, polyuria, hyperphagia, unexplained weight loss); (2) fasting plasma glucose concentration higher than 7.0 mmol/L, or plasma glucose concentration higher than 11.1 mmol/L, or the oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) 2 h plasma glucose concentration higher than 11.1 mmol/L. The diagnosis of DFU was based on the diagnostic criteria established by the International Working Group on the Diabetic Foot (IWGDF) [17]. The diagnosis was confirmed when a diabetic patient presented with an ulcer on the lower extremity, accompanied by clinical signs of infection or ischemia, and was confirmed through imaging and laboratory tests. The inclusion criteria were: (1) patients aged 18 years or older; (2) diagnosed with type 2 diabetes; and (3) presence of DFUs as defined by the Wagner classification system. The exclusion criteria included: (1) active cancer; (2) severe heart, liver, or kidney dysfunction, or significant organ involvement; (3) other major systemic diseases affecting wound healing (e.g., autoimmune diseases); (4) use of immunosuppressive drugs; (5) non-compliance with treatment protocols; and (6) pregnancy. This study was approved by our ethics committee (No.XYYYE20240213) and all patients signed an informed consent form.

Patient treatment

This was an observational study, and no intervention was made regarding the patients’ treatment regimes. All patients received standard care for DFU management according to hospital protocols. Treatment typically included blood glucose control, infection management with antibiotics, debridement, and wound dressing. For the anti-inflammatory therapy, all patients were treated with a standardized anti-inflammatory regimen, which included the administration of nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs such as ibuprofen or diclofenac for pain management and to reduce local inflammation. In cases with severe inflammation or infection, corticosteroids such as methylprednisolone were administered under clinical supervision to help manage acute inflammatory responses. The patients’ treatment was closely monitored throughout their hospital stay, with adjustments made based on clinical indications, such as the patient's response to the anti-inflammatory therapy and changes in the inflammatory markers. However, no modifications were made to the standard care or the underlying treatment protocols for managing DFU. Data regarding the management of these patients, including the specifics of anti-inflammatory treatment, were collected at different timepoints as part of routine clinical practice.

Blood sampling and analysis

Blood samples were collected from each patient at the time of admission and at designated follow-up timepoints after anti-inflammatory treatment. The timepoints for blood sampling were at admission (before initiating therapy), at 7 days, 14 days, and 28 days. Serum was isolated from blood samples through centrifugation at 3000 rpm for 10 min. The levels of NRF2, CRP, IL-6, interleukin-17 (IL-17), and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) were measured using commercially available ELISA kits (NRF2-MBS024775 detection range 62.5–2000 pg/ml, IL-6-MBS175877 detection range 4.69–300 pg/mL, TNF-α-MBS2886257 detection range 15.6–1,000 pg/mL, IL-17-MBS2127177 detection range 7.8–500 pg/ml, CRP-MBS2021863 detection range 62.5–4000 pg/ml, MyBioSource, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, serum samples were added to pre-coated wells, followed by the addition of specific detection antibodies. After incubation and washing steps, a colorimetric substrate was added, and the absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. All samples were measured in duplicate, and the average values were used for statistical analysis.

Data collection and scoring

Demographic and clinical data were collected for all enrolled patients. Demographic data included age, sex, body mass index (BMI), duration of diabetes, smoking status, and comorbidities (hypertension, coronary artery disease). In addition, fasting plasma glucose (FPG), total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) levels, and serum creatinine (SCr) levels were measured using the Hitachi 7600 automated biochemistry analyzer.

DFUs classification

All DFU patients were graded according to the Wagner classification system. Grade 0 refers to the presence of risk factors for foot ulcers without any actual ulceration. Grade 1 indicates a superficial skin ulcer without infection. Grade 2 describes a deeper ulcer, often accompanied by soft tissue inflammation, but without abscess or bone infection. Grade 3 is characterized by deep infection with bone involvement or abscess formation. Grade 4 represents localized gangrene affecting the toes, heel, or forefoot, while Grade 5 is complete foot gangrene. Patients with Wagner grades 1–2 were classified into the mild group, and those with grade ≥ 3 were classified into the severe group.

Statistical analysis

Kolmogorov–Smirnov analysis confirmed the normal distribution of the data. Normally distributed data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, while non-normally distributed data were presented as median (range). Mann–Whitney U test or Student’s t test was used to compare the two groups of patients. Chi-square test was used for categorical variables. Spearman’s correlation analysis was performed to assess the correlations among cytokines. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was used to evaluate the value of serum biomarkers in severe DFU. To investigate the risk factors for severe DFU, multivariate regression analysis was performed. A p value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. A post hoc power analysis was conducted based on the comparison of serum NRF2 levels between the mild and severe DFU groups. Using the observed means, standard deviations, and group sizes, the effect size (Cohen’s d) was calculated to be approximately 1.00. The achieved statistical power for a two-tailed independent t test at α = 0.05 was 99%, indicating that the sample size was sufficient to detect a significant difference. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). All figures were generated using GraphPad Prism 9.5.1 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA).

The changes in NRF2 and inflammatory cytokines during the treatment of patients

We further measured the expression levels of NRF2 and inflammatory cytokines in all DFU patients at 24 h after admission, and at 7, 15, and 30 days of treatment. As shown in Fig. 2, compared to the mild group, the severe group had significantly lower serum NRF2 levels at admission, while serum CRP, IL-6, and IL-17 levels were significantly higher. During the treatment process, serum NRF2 levels gradually increased in all DFU patients, while inflammatory markers IL-17, CRP, IL-6, and TNF-α gradually decreased. In addition, compared to the severe group, the mild group showed significantly lower serum CRP and IL-6 levels at all timepoints, while NRF2 levels were significantly higher (Fig. 3). Spearman correlation analysis indicated that serum NRF2 levels in DFU patients were negatively correlated with IL-6 levels (Table 2).

Serum NRF2 as a screening biomarker for severe DFU patients

We constructed a ROC curve using serum NRF2 levels measured at admission to evaluate its diagnostic value for severe DFU patients. As shown in Fig. 4, the results indicated that serum NRF2 levels might serve as a screening biomarker to help identify patients with severe DFU, with an AUC of 0.746, a cutoff value of 185.65 pg/ml, sensitivity of 71.3%, and specificity of 63.0% (Fig. 3).

Risk factors for severe DFU patients: logistic regression analysis

Based on variables that showed significant differences between the mild and severe groups in Table 1 and Fig. 1, we performed a multivariate binary logistic regression analysis to identify risk factors for severe DFU. The results of Hosmer and Lemeshow test (P = 0.270) and Nagelkerke R2 (0.592) showed well goodness of fit. The analysis revealed that decreased serum NRF2 levels and increased levels of CRP, IL-17, IL-6, and 2-h postprandial glucose were independently associated with a higher risk of severe DFU (Table 3).

Discussion

DFUs are one of the most serious complications of diabetes mellitus, often leading to severe outcomes, such as infection, amputation, and even death. The increasing prevalence of diabetes worldwide has led to a rise in DFU cases, which remain a significant cause of morbidity, disability, and healthcare costs [18]. This study underscores the critical importance of early identification of severe DFU cases to enable better management. We found that serum NRF2 levels were significantly lower in severe DFU patients compared to those with mild DFU. Moreover, the serum NRF2 levels of DFU patients were correlated with the serum IL-6 levels.

The treatment of DFUs continues to present significant challenges, with current strategies primarily targeting infection control, blood glucose regulation, and wound debridement [19]. However, despite these efforts, many patients, particularly those with comorbidities, such as peripheral arterial disease, neuropathy, and poor glycemic control, experience slow or non-healing ulcers. As the complexity of DFU management increases, there is a growing interest in the role of serum biomarkers to aid in the diagnosis, monitoring, and treatment of these ulcers. Serum biomarkers have emerged as valuable tools for assessing the underlying mechanisms of DFU pathogenesis, including inflammation, oxidative stress, and tissue remodeling [20]. Chen et al. found that the serum levels of lipoprotein-associated phospholipase A2 (Lp-PLA2) and IL-18 were increased significantly with the progression of diabetes and were positively associated with the severity of DFU, suggesting their potential role as biomarkers for assessing DFU severity [21]. Another study identified differentially expressed circular RNAs (circRNAs) between DFU and normal human wounds and found that serum hsa_circ_0000907 and hsa_circ_0057362 showed strong diagnostic capabilities for early DFU diagnosis, with high AUC values, making them promising biomarkers for early detection of DFU [22]. Furthermore, Xu et al. focused on the role of IL-6 in promoting angiogenesis in DFU patients after undergoing transverse tibial transfer, showing that IL-6 levels increased postoperatively and were correlated with improved wound healing, suggesting its potential as a therapeutic target to enhance healing in severe DFUs [23]. In this study, we similarly found that serum levels of the inflammatory factors CRP, IL-6, and IL-17 were significantly elevated in patients with severe DFU, whereas serum NRF2 levels were significantly lower. Although the correlation between serum NRF2 and IL-6 was statistically significant (r = −0.176, p = 0.014), the effect size was relatively modest. This likely reflects the multifactorial nature of inflammation, which involves numerous signaling pathways and mediators. While NRF2 plays a critical role in modulating oxidative stress and inflammatory responses, it represents only one component of a broader regulatory network in diabetic wound pathology. Nevertheless, the observed inverse correlation supports a mechanistic link between reduced NRF2 levels and heightened inflammatory states in DFU, particularly through pathways involving IL-6, and is consistent with prior experimental findings. This reinforces the biological plausibility of NRF2 as a clinically relevant marker in the context of diabetic wound inflammation.

NRF2 has been extensively studied for its role in managing oxidative stress and inflammation in a variety of diseases, including cardiovascular diseases, neurodegenerative disorders, and cancer [13, 24]. In the context of DFUs, NRF2 activation has been shown to counteract oxidative stress, which impedes wound healing while promoting tissue repair and reducing inflammation. For example, activation of the SIRT1/Nrf2/p62 pathway in DFU models has been found to affect cell proliferation, ferritin deposition, and oxidative stress, ultimately promoting wound healing in DFU rats through autophagy-dependent ferritin deposition [15]. Similarly, miR-27b is considered a key regulator of Nrf2-mediated angiogenesis in DFUs, with reduced expression of Nrf2 and its targets, such as vascular endothelial growth factor, in chronic DFU tissue. This suggests that regulating miR-27b could enhance angiogenesis by activating NRF2 signaling, thus improving wound healing in a hyperglycemic environment [25]. In addition, activating the Nrf2 pathway has shown potential in enhancing M2 macrophage polarization and promoting tissue repair, alleviating chronic inflammation and oxidative stress in diabetic wounds. This further underscores the potential of NRF2 in improving DFU healing [26]. Moreover, the activation of NRF2 under hyperglycemic conditions was shown to restore endothelial progenitor cell function, reduce oxidative stress, and promote wound healing in diabetic models [27]. These experimental findings provide mechanistic support for our clinical observation that lower serum NRF2 levels were associated with greater DFU severity. In this study, serum NRF2 not only differed significantly between mild and severe DFU groups but also emerged as an independent risk factor for severe DFU. Taken together, these data suggested that NRF2 might serve as a clinically relevant screening biomarker and potentially played a regulatory role in DFU progression.

Combining NRF2 with other established biomarkers could provide a more comprehensive screening approach. For example, using NRF2 alongside inflammatory markers, such as CRP, IL-6, and IL-1β, could improve the accuracy of diagnosing severe DFUs and allow for more personalized treatment strategies. Identifying high-risk patients earlier through biomarker screening could enable clinicians to intervene promptly, potentially preventing the progression to more severe stages and improving patient outcomes. Furthermore, the integration of biomarkers like NRF2 into routine clinical practice could help in stratifying patients based on ulcer severity, allowing clinicians to tailor interventions based on the individual patient's condition. However, while the screening potential of NRF2 is promising, further studies are required to validate its role in clinical settings and to explore the possible synergistic effects when combined with other biomarkers in predicting DFU severity.

From a clinical perspective, integrating serum NRF2 measurement into routine practice for DFU risk assessment requires addressing several important considerations. Currently, the lack of standardized and widely validated assays for serum NRF2 remains a barrier to clinical application. Future work should focus on assay optimization, inter-laboratory reproducibility, and establishing reference ranges in diabetic populations. Cost-effectiveness analyses comparing NRF2 with existing inflammatory biomarkers such as CRP and IL-6 are also essential to determine its practical value in clinical workflows. Furthermore, NRF2’s established role in modulating oxidative stress and inflammation suggests it may serve not only as a biomarker but also as a therapeutic target. Preclinical studies have shown that pharmacological activation of NRF2 promotes wound healing, reduces oxidative stress, and improves immune regulation in diabetic models. Building on these findings, future clinical trials are warranted to evaluate NRF2-targeted therapies, either alone or in combination with existing treatments, in DFU and other inflammation-driven complications of diabetes. These investigations may clarify NRF2’s full clinical utility in both diagnosis and management.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, although the sample size was adequate for preliminary analysis, it remains relatively modest and may limit the generalizability of the findings. Second, as a prospective observational study, the data can establish association but not causality, and unmeasured confounders may still influence the observed relationships. Third, while our analysis included adjustments for common clinical variables, the possibility of residual confounding cannot be excluded. Fourth, participants were stratified based on DFU severity; however, we did not include a diabetic control group without foot ulcers. This limits our ability to determine whether the observed alterations in NRF2 and inflammatory cytokines were attributable to DFU itself or simply reflected severity differences. Including such a control group in future studies would help clarify the baseline NRF2 profile in diabetic individuals and provide more nuanced insight into the biomarker’s diagnostic significance.

Conclusion

This study suggested that serum NRF2 levels could serve as a useful screening tool for identifying severe DFUs. The ability to measure serum NRF2 levels could help clinicians in screening and identifying patients at risk of severe DFU, allowing for more timely and effective interventions. The integration of NRF2 into routine clinical practice, alongside other biomarkers, might enable better stratification of patients based on the severity of their condition and personalize treatment strategies.
