Latent Profiles of Mental Health in Spanish Youth: Analysing Internalizing and Externalizing Problems
Authors: Jose P Espada, Àngela Belzunegui‐Pastor, Samuel Tomczyk, José A Piqueras, Alexandra Morales, Mireia Orgilés
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPsychological problems are a common concern among children and adolescents. Identifying distinct profiles of internalizing and externalizing problems offers a comprehensive understanding of the psychological challenges faced by this population. This study aimed to explore mental health profiles in a sample of 5652 Spanish children and adolescents aged 8–16 years, examining differences between these profiles and reference groups. Using the Child and Adolescent Assessment System (SENA) and latent profile analysis (LPA), six profiles were identified among children and seven among adolescents, based on internalizing, externalizing and contextual problems. Among children, the most frequent profiles were ‘moderate problems, low conflict’ and ‘low problems’, while adolescents most commonly exhibited ‘moderate internalizing, low externalizing’ and ‘low problems’ profiles. Significant differences across profiles were linked to emotional regulation, self‐esteem and social competence. These findings provide a nuanced perspective on the mental health landscape of Spanish youth, emphasizing the critical role of early detection of emotional difficulties in educational settings. Furthermore, the study offers valuable insights for developing tailored interventions to meet the specific emotional needs of children and adolescents.Keywords: externalizing, internalizing, latent profile analysis, mental health for youths

Psychological problems are a common problem among children and adolescents worldwide, accounting for 16% of the global burden of illness and injury among 10–19 years old (World Health Organization [WHO] 2021). In a systematic review carried out with children and adolescents of Europe, it was concluded that nearly one in five young people experienced a mental health disorder (Sacco et al. 2024). The most prevalent psychological problem found was anxiety disorders, followed by attention deficit hyperactivity disorder, oppositional defiant disorder, depressive disorder, conduct disorder and autism spectrum disorder.

Specifically, anxiety disorders are a relevant public health problem for Spanish early adolescents, with specific phobia and generalized anxiety as the most prevalent subtypes of anxiety disorders in this population (Canals et al. 2019). In a study conducted with Spanish children and adolescents between 4 and 14 years old, it was demonstrated that 5% of the sample presented risk of poor mental health and 8.9% obtained limited punctuations (Zaldívar et al. 2018). The current EmoChild study (Emotional Disorders Among Childhood; Orgilés et al. 2024) carried out with Spanish children and adolescents aged 9–16 years, concludes that between 9% and 12% of children and between 11% and 16% of adolescents, depending on the problem examined, have scores that are considered to be at risk of developing clinically significant problems. Specifically, this study concludes that 8.8% of children and 11.0% of adolescents are at risk of depression, 11.3% of children and 16.2% of adolescents are predisposed to experience generalized anxiety and 11.2% of children and 11.3% of adolescents are at risk of social anxiety.

Different research on psychological problems in children and adolescents has used latent class analysis (LCA) to examine mental health profiles in this population. Previous research has identified distinct profiles of co‐occurring internalizing and externalizing problems (Göbel et al. 2022), depression patterns in adolescents (Wojcieszak et al. 2021) and varying levels of suicidal behaviour risk (Díez‐Gómez et al. 2020; Lee et al. 2023). LCA is recognized for its robust capacity to uncover latent class structures (Ondé and Alvarado 2019). This methodological approach is particularly valuable as it identifies heterogeneous subgroups of individuals sharing similar risk factors, thereby facilitating the development of targeted interventions and treatment plans tailored to these specific risk profiles (Göbel and Cohrdes 2021). Latent profile analysis (LPA) is a variant of LCA, both of which are statistical methods used to identify subgroups within a population based on latent variables. While LCA is designed for categorical data, LPA extends this framework to continuous variables, making it suitable for analysing more nuanced patterns in data. Unlike LCA, which assigns individuals to classes based on discrete response patterns, LPA uses the means and variances of continuous variables to determine probabilistic membership in latent profiles.

Among Spanish children and adolescents, Morales, Rodríguez‐Menchón, et al. (2021) identified four distinct profiles based on internalizing and externalizing problems in children aged 6–8: the high difficulties profile, externalizing profile, internalizing profile and well‐adjusted profile. Similarly, Morales, Melero, et al. (2021) found five latent classes in children aged 7–12, categorized as high difficulties profile, internalizing profile, externalizing profile, hyperactivity profile and well‐adjusted profile. Additionally, Fonseca‐Pedrero et al. (2020) identified three profiles among adolescents based on emotional and behavioural difficulties and prosocial behaviour: low risk, externalizing and internalizing profiles.

Identifying latent mental health profiles among Spanish children and adolescents is crucial for developing a comprehensive understanding of mental health within this population. This approach allows for the detection of psychological needs and supports the creation of effective intervention plans. Although previous research involving Spanish children and adolescents has been significant, these studies have predominantly relied on small and specific samples drawn from limited regions of the country.

Consequently, the present study focuses on the entire population of Spanish children and adolescents aged 8–16 years, with the aim of identifying different mental health profiles between children and adolescents. A second objective was to analyse the differences between these profiles, comparing them with the reference group in each sample. It is hypothesized that different profiles will be found between children and adolescents, depending on the presence and intensity of the scores obtained in internalized, externalized and contextual problems. Moreover, it is expected to find differences between the latent profiles of being at risk for distinct mental health problems, in variables such as age and sex, according to previous scientific literature (Göbel and Cohrdes 2021; Morales, Rodríguez‐Menchón, et al. 2021).

Methods

Participants

The sample consisted of 5652 Spanish children and adolescents aged 8–16 years. Of the total, 50.6% were female, with an average age of 12.22 years. Participants were drawn from all Spanish autonomous communities. Regarding academic level, 12.7% of participants were in fourth grade of primary school, 11.5% in fifth grade, 13.9% in sixth grade, 14.3% in first of secondary school, 15.1% in second year, 11.7% in third year, 9.2% in fourth year and 11.7% did not report their academic year. In terms of academic performance, 5.3% reported having failed grades, 12.2% received a sufficient grade, 27.9% reported usually good grades, 31.6% indicated usually excellent grades, 10.5% reported usually outstanding grades and 11.8% did not specify their grades.

Among the participants in compulsory secondary education, 4.4% reported attending psychological therapy and/or receiving medication related to mental health. The reasons for seeking psychological therapy were: anxiety (0.8%), family problems (0.7%), attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (0.7%), peer‐related issues (0.6%), depression (0.4%), eating disorders (0.3%), suicidal ideation (0.2%), study‐related difficulties (0.2%), autism spectrum disorder (0.2%), learning difficulties (0.1%), behavioural problems (0.1%), post‐traumatic stress disorder (0.1%), tics (0.1%), obsessive–compulsive disorder (0.0%) and other unspecified reasons (1.1%).

Prior to the participation of children and adolescents in the study, informed consent was obtained from their families. The consent form revealed that 57.3% of the participants lived with their mother and father. Most of the sample indicated that the level of education of the mother (40.1%) and the father (30.4%) was tertiary education.

From the initial sample of 5652 participants, a final sample of 5317 (94% of the original sample) was used for LPA and multinomial logistic regression. The remaining 6% of cases were excluded due to missing data essential for inclusion in the analysis. Of the 5317 cases included, 43.75% were primary school children (N = 2326) while 56.25% were adolescents in compulsory secondary education (N = 2990).

Instruments

Sociodemographic Variables

Sociodemographic variables were collected for children and adolescents, as well as for their families. For the children, the following sociodemographic variables were recorded: grade, school, age, sex, year and country of birth and grades obtained in the previous academic year. For the adolescents, additional variables were collected, including weight, height and whether they attended psychological therapy, alongside the same variables as for the children (grade, school, age, sex, year and country of birth and academic performance).

The sociodemographic variables included in the parents' consent were cohabitation status, the educational level and occupation of the parents or guardians and the family's gross annual income.

Child and Adolescent Evaluation System (Sistema de Evaluación de Niños y Adolescentes, SENA; Fernández‐Pinto et al. 2015)

The self‐reported version was administered to youth in primary (8–12 years) and secondary (12 to 18 years) schools. The primary school version consists of 134 items, while the secondary school version contains 188 items. Both versions utilize a 5‐point Likert scale (1 = never or almost never; 2 = rarely; 3 = sometimes; 4 = many times; 5 = always or almost always). The SENA is comprised of six global indices (GLO: global problem index, EMO: emotional problems index, CON: behavioural problem index, AXIS: index of problems in executive functions, CTX: contextual problems and REC: personal resources index), three control scales (INC: inconsistency, NEG: negative impression and POS: positive impression), four scales of problems (internalized problems, externalized problems, contextual problems and other problems and the latter only for adolescents), two scales of vulnerabilities (REG: problems of emotional regulation and BUS: sensation seeking, the latter only for adolescents) and personal resource scales (AUT: self‐esteem, SOC: integration and social competence and CNC: awareness of problems, the latter only for adolescents).

Procedure

A stratified cluster sampling method combined with multistage sampling was employed to select the educational centres. A comprehensive list of all schools and high schools in each autonomous community was compiled by consulting the Search Engine for Non‐University Teaching Centres of the Ministry of Education, Vocational Training and Sports (https://www.educacion.gob.es/centros/home.do). The selection criteria included the autonomous community, the type of studies (primary education or compulsory secondary education) and the tenure of the institutions. Separate Excel files were generated for the schools and high schools, and a random number was assigned to each institution. After assigning numbers, the institutions were randomly sorted, and the first 50 from each community were selected. These initial centres were contacted to request their collaboration with the project. Due to refusals from some schools and high schools, additional centres were approached.

Assessments were conducted in a total of 108 educational centres. Informed consent was obtained from the families of students prior to their participation. The students completed an online survey, which was created using the Lime Survey platform. The administration of the questionnaires took approximately 45 min, with each student completing the survey individually. Data collection was carried out between October 2023 and March 2024. This study received approval from the Ethics Committee of Miguel Hernández University (CIPROM/2021/031).

Statistical Analysis

LPA

To identify latent profiles of psychopathology in children and adolescents, we conducted LPA via Mplus (Version 8) (Muthén and Muthén 1998–2017). For the analysis, we used SENA subscales for internalizing (depression, anxiety, social anxiety, somatic complaints, post‐traumatic symptomatology and obsessive–compulsive symptoms [adolescents only]), externalizing (attention problems, hyperactivity–impulsivity, anger control, aggression and defiant behaviour; adolescents only: antisocial behaviour, substance use, schizotypy and eating behaviour) and contextual problems (family, school and peers).

In the analysis, we compared model fit criteria and estimated solutions for two to eight latent profiles. We utilized robust maximum likelihood estimation (MLR in Mplus) with 500 initial random starts. For model fit examination, we looked at statistical indicators of goodness of fit, sparseness and classification quality (Nylund et al. 2007; Tomczyk et al. 2020; Tomczyk et al. 2018) and checked the interpretability and theoretical tenability of the results.

Goodness of Fit

To assess overall fit, we used the bootstrapped likelihood ratio test (BLRT) to compare each estimated model with k profiles to a model with k − 1 profiles using 100 random starts with 50 bootstrap draws for each comparison. A significant BLRT value suggests a preference for the model with k profiles over the model with k − 1 profiles.

Sparseness

As indicators of sparseness, we reported the Akaike information criterion (AIC) and the sample size–adjusted Bayes information criterion (SSABIC). For both criteria, lower values indicate greater sparseness of the model.

Classification Quality

We summarized average latent profile probabilities (ALPP) and entropy of each model. ALPP and entropy range between 0 and 1; the closer to 1, the better the fit (Nylund et al. 2007), meaning the identified latent profiles are substantially different from each other.

Interpretability

Next to statistical criteria, the theoretical tenability and interpretability of latent profiles are also important in the model selection process. We examined each model (two to eight latent profiles) and checked their tenability with regard to the literature.

In addition to LPA, a multinomial regression model (r3step in Mplus) tested age (in years), gender (male vs. female), level of parental education (children only), emotion regulation, self‐esteem, integration and social competence (all continuous) as predictors of latent profiles. In the adolescent sample, additional predictors comprised sensation seeking and awareness of problems (all continuous). For these models, we report relative prevalence ratios (RPRs) and 95% confidence intervals for each predictor. All analyses were based on α = 0.05.

Transparency and Openness

We report how we determined our sample size and all measures in the study, and we follow JARS (Appelbaum et al. 2018). This study's design and its analysis were not pre‐registered. Materials and analysis code for this study are not available because the data are part of an ongoing project.

Discussion

The present study used LPA to identify profiles of emotional symptomatology in Spanish children and adolescents aged 8–16 years. A secondary objective was to analyse the differences between the profiles identified, comparing them with the reference group within each sample of children and adolescents.

Latent profiles analysis conducted with the sample of children (8–12 years old) identified six profiles: low problems, high problems, moderate problems with low conflict, high internalizing, high externalizing and moderate problems with high conflict. The results of this study align with previous research findings. For example, Mezulis et al. (2011) found six profiles of depressive and externalizing problems among American children: very low depressive and externalizing class, moderate depressive and low externalizing class, moderate externalizing and low depressive class, moderate externalizing and high depressive class, high depressive and externalizing class and very high depressive and externalizing class.

In the present study, the group with internalizing problems was larger than the group with externalizing problems (15.8% vs. 10.9%, respectively). These findings are consistent with previous research, which has consistently shown that internalizing problems are more common than externalizing ones (Husky et al. 2018; Morales, Rodríguez‐Menchón, et al. 2021). Our results indicated that the largest profile was ‘moderate problems, low conflict’, characterized by moderate levels of both internalizing and externalizing problems and low contextual problems, and comprising 35.8% of the children. The second largest profile was the ‘low problems’ group (27.4%). These results align with prior studies, which have found that the most common profiles tend to be those characterized by fewer problems (Bianchi et al. 2017; Mezulis et al. 2011; Morales, Rodríguez‐Menchón, et al. 2021).

In the adolescent sample (12–16 years old), seven latent profiles were identified: moderate internalizing, low externalizing; low problems; moderate internalizing, moderate conflict; high internalizing, high conflict; moderate externalizing, moderate conflict; moderate problems; and high externalizing, high conflict. These profiles align with findings from previous research. Kiye and Boysan (2021) identified six similar profiles with Turkish children: externalizing and internalizing problems, internalizing problems, externalizing problems, at risk for externalizing problems, at risk for internalizing problems and healthy subjects. Similar to the results for the children's profiles, the two largest adolescent profiles in our study were moderate internalizing, low externalizing (31.3% of the sample) and low problems (31.1% of the sample). In line with these findings, Ling et al. (2016) reported that 31.1% of their sample belonged to the middle‐risk group and 49.1% were classified in the low‐risk group.

Multinomial logistic regression analyses in the sample of children revealed an association between more severe problems and higher emotional regulation scores, as well as lower self‐esteem and social competence scores. Additionally, relationships were identified between lower levels of parental education and higher levels of externalizing problems. Sex differences were also observed, with girls being less likely to exhibit externalizing and contextual problems. These findings are consistent with previous research that has identified sex differences in internalizing and externalizing problems. Specifically, girls are more likely to experience emotional symptoms but show fewer conduct problems, while boys tend to exhibit more conduct problems with fewer emotional symptoms (Petersen et al. 2022).

In the adolescent sample, more severe problems were associated with higher emotional regulation and age scores, as well as low self‐esteem and social competence scores. Additionally, adolescent girls were more likely to experience internalizing problems. Previous research has also found that older adolescents (Ling et al. 2016; Norris et al. 2020) and adolescent girls (Ling et al. 2016) are more likely to exhibit internalizing problems. Prior studies comparing adolescents in low‐risk groups with those in moderate co‐occurring, chronic co‐occurring and pure‐internalizing groups have found that the latter three groups tend to have lower levels of resilience, higher levels of insecure attachment and increased family conflicts and academic pressure (Wang et al. 2023).

The results obtained indicate a six‐factor solution for children and a seven‐factor solution for adolescents. Although statistical criteria (such as the AIC and aSSABIC) suggested that the eight‐profile model presented the best overall fit, we decided to proceed with the six‐ (children) and seven‐ (adolescents) profile solutions based on the principles of parsimony, theoretical interpretability and analytical robustness. The additional subgroups introduced by the more complex models did not provide significantly new theoretical or practical information. These supplementary profiles consistently constituted very small subgroups, which could compromise their analytical stability in subsequent analyses. Furthermore, these profiles tended to be redundant splits of an already existing profile, differing from the main profiles only by subtle variations in a single indicator, without offering a distinctive behavioural or theoretical pattern. Therefore, despite acknowledging the superior statistical fit, we prioritized a more robust, interpretable and theoretically relevant profile solution for the subsequent inferences.

Limitations and Practical Applications

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged, particularly regarding the sample recruitment process. While the sample size was large, it was limited by the low acceptance of participation rate of schools. Additionally, the analyses were based solely on self‐report measures. Including information from other informants, such as parents and teachers, would have been valuable to provide a more comprehensive understanding and to compare responses.

Despite these limitations, the results offer important practical applications. Schools and high schools play a critical role in the early identification of psychological problems.

Understanding the existence of different profiles of internalizing and externalizing symptoms in Spanish children and adolescents benefits the development of programmes for the prevention and intervention of mental health problems in this population. Moreover, detecting different profiles of this symptomatology at an early stage allows for the development of targeted action plans to enhance mental health, highlighting the relevance of implementing preventive and health‐promoting approaches in educational contexts (López‐Villegas and Sánchez‐Sandoval 2024).

Furthermore, identifying distinct profiles of psychological problems among Spanish children and adolescents provides valuable insights into the current state of this population's mental well‐being, informing future interventions and policies.

Conclusions

The results of this study provide a comprehensive perspective on the mental health of Spanish children and adolescents. Six latent profiles were identified in the sample of children: low problems; high problems; moderate problems, low conflict; high internalizing; high externalizing; and moderate problems, high conflict. In the adolescent sample, seven latent profiles were identified: moderate internalizing, low externalizing; low problems; moderate internalizing, moderate conflict; high internalizing, high conflict; moderate externalizing, moderate conflict; moderate problems; and high externalizing, high conflict. Understanding the heterogeneity within these emotional difficulties is crucial for developing targeted interventions that address the diverse needs of children and adolescents.
