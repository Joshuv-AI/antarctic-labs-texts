Recruitment, retention and recouperation: lessons learned from a study aimed at assessing burnout mitigation in physician learners
Authors: Jacqueline Pflaum-Carlson, Ashley Redding, Dana Murphy, Lisa MacLean, Julie Hamilton, Sara Santarossa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aimed to assess the effectiveness of acceptance and commitment therapy (ACT) compared to virtual body mapping (VBM) on reducing burnout symptoms. The recruitment and data collection for this study took place between May 2023 – May 2024. PLs were randomized into either ACT or VBM. Baseline and post-intervention measures included the Maslach Burnout Inventory (MBI), the Perceived Stress Scale (PSS-10), the State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI tx-1), Centers of Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CES-D), the Brief Resilient Coping Scale (BRCS), and the Acceptance and Action Questionnaire (AAQ-II). Descriptive statistics were calculated.ResultsOf 58 enrolled participants, 13 completed the study (23% completion). Descriptive results suggest that PLs in ACT saw improvement in the average scores for STAI tx-1, CES-D, and AAQ-II. PLs in VBM saw improvement in the average score for the MBI, PSS-10, STAI, CES-D, BRCS, and AAQ-II. Barriers to recruitment and retention affected the study’s success, resulting in modifications to the recruitment strategy, study protocol, and data analysis. Insights from this study offer researchers an opportunity to improve the design of future work involving PLs. Studies involving PLs should consider integrating participation into clinical schedules and ensuring funding to support recruitment and retention.Keywords: Burnout, Coping skills, Acceptance and commitment therapy, Art therapy

Introduction

Healthcare workers face rising levels of distress, anxiety, and emotional exhaustion, with burnout and workplace attrition escalating [1, 2]. Studies during and since the COVID-19 pandemic show an increased physician burnout rate with a nearly threefold increase in the self-reported median level of stress as compared to pre-pandemic [3], and physician learners (PLs; i.e., residents and fellows) have been left particularly vulnerable. Strategies for mitigating physician burnout have ranged from traditional therapy to storytelling gatherings [4, 5]. Few studies have compared strategies to assess which methods are most effective in reducing burnout symptoms [6]. While much has been published on the ethics of using PLs as research subjects [7–9], little has been written about pitfalls and strategies to optimize their recruitment and retention in research [10].

Described here is a study entitled “Burnout Mitigation for Physician Learners (BuMP)”. BuMP was designed to compare acceptance and commitment therapy (ACT) [11] and virtual body mapping (VBM) [12] as interventions to reduce the symptoms of PL burnout. ACT, a modern cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT), has increasingly been adapted for use in group settings with health care professionals to address mental health and burnout [13, 14]. Although limited, emerging evidence suggests that creative art therapies can also reduce burnout among healthcare providers [15, 16]. Body mapping, an art-based method, has been used as a therapeutic tool, an educational and advocacy strategy, and a means of coping in diverse populations [17–22]. VBM, a digital adaptation of this method, was developed by members of this study team (SS, AR, DM) as a research and reflective tool [23]. To our knowledge, neither ACT nor Body Mapping/VBM has been evaluated specifically for reducing burnout in PLs, and Body Mapping/VBM has not previously been applied in this population. BuMP introduces VBM as both a novel methodology and intervention experience for PLs. Highlighted are descriptive data from PLs who completed the study as well as lessons learned during recruitment, retention, and conclusion of the interventions.

Main text

Ethical approval was obtained from Henry Ford Health (HFH) Institutional Review Board (#16267-01).

Recruitment and retention

Based on our previous VBM experience [23, 24], the recruitment goal included 40 PLs (20/intervention). Interested participants submitted a digital intake form (i.e., name, email, PL status). Due to study attrition, recruitment occurred in two phases. The recruitment and retention efforts from both phases have been consolidated into Fig. 1. Signed informed consent was obtained from all participants via REDCap (Vanderbilt University, Nashville, TN) [25, 26].

Phase 1 recruitment (May-July 2023)

Eligibility included PLs who were ≥ 18 years old and employed at HFH. There were 705 potential HFH PLs (56% non-White; 39% female). Recruitment occurred digitally via institution- and department-wide emails and program director announcements. After 3 months, 35 eligible PLs expressed interest, of which 28 consented. Poor study engagement led the research team to increase the monetary incentive (from $25 to $50) and personally call all consented participants to solicit intervention scheduling. Ultimately, the attrition rate of consented participants was 75%.

Phase 2 recruitment (January-March 2024)

Due to unsuccessful recruitment and retention in Phase 1 and anecdotal participant feedback (e.g., inability to attend sessions due to competing demands), the research team modified (and amended) study eligibility and protocol. PLs outside of HFH were now eligible to participate and VBM was reduced from three 2-hour bi-weekly sessions to three 1-hour weekly sessions (see Data Collection). In addition to the recruitment strategies used in Phase 1, recruitment assets were shared on personal and institutional social media platforms and via Graduate Medical Education forums and listservs. After 2 months, 73 eligible PLs expressed interest, of which 30 consented. Ultimately, the attrition rate of consented participants was 80%.

Data collection (May 2023 – May 2024)

Consented PLs completed a baseline survey (i.e., demographics) and were randomized [27] into one of two interventions (ACT or VBM). A pre-intervention survey was deployed and measures included the: Maslach Burnout Inventory (MBI) [28], Perceived Stress Scale (PSS-10) [29], State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI tx-1) [30], Centers of Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CES-D) [31], the Brief Resilient Coping Scale (BRCS) [32], and Acceptance and Action Questionnaire (AAQ-II) [33]. Interventions occurred, and an identical post-intervention survey was sent via email immediately following the last sessions and completed within 14 days.

ACT

ACT is designed to promote increased psychological flexibility and improve overall general psychological health by combining values and mindfulness with self-acceptance [11]. Each virtual session (via Webex) was 1-hour and occurred weekly over 3 weeks. Sessions focused on stress management, self-care, improving focus and performance and enhancing psychological flexibility. Details of ACT session protocol are published elsewhere [11].

VBM

VBM is used to visually represent aspects of people’s lives and environments [34]. Body maps are life-size human body images created using various art-based mediums. PLs participated in three 2-hour (bi-weekly) VBM sessions (via Webex) that included guided exercises, similar to the protocol previously published by members of the research team [12]. However, in Phase 2, the intervention time commitment was halved and moved to a weekly schedule. Sessions were recorded to allow absent participants opportunities to complete on their own time.

Data analysis

The planned analysis included a series of paired t-tests to compare the pre- and post-intervention results of each intervention for each of the measures to determine if there was a change due to treatment effect. An independent samples t-test would be used to assess differences by group within each time point.

Although 58 participants were enrolled, only 13 completed the study (Fig. 1). The PLs mostly fell within the 25–34 year age range (n = 12; 92%), the majority identified as women (n = 8; 62%), and White (n = 9; 69%). Participants were in different years of training ranging from post-graduate year-1 to year-7. The majority were in post-graduate year-2 (n = 3) and year-4 (n = 4).

Due to the small sample size, planned analysis could not be completed. For illustrative purposes, descriptive statistics were calculated for the PLs that did complete the study. Average scores for each test were compared pre- and post-intervention and reported in Table 1. Results suggest that those in the ACT condition did not see improvement in average scores on the MBI subscales, PSS-10 or BRCS. There was improvement in the average scores for STAI tx-1, CES-D, and AAQ-II. For the VBM condition, there was improvement in average scores for all tests: each of the MBI subscales, PSS-10, STAI, CES-D, BRCS, and AAQ-II.

Burnout is a multifacted problem known to affect healthcare workers, with a projected larger effect on PLs due to longer work hours and their position as early career medical professionals [35]. Presented here is a study aimed to mitigate PL burnout where, coupled with recruitment challenges, only 23% of consented participants completed the study. Ultimately, the sample size was too small to complete the planned analysis; however, descriptive findings suggest that VBM should be explored as a potential method to prevent burnout in PLs. Insights from this study offer researchers an opportunity to improve the design of future work involving PLs.

Limitations

Most burnout studies are plagued by low enrollment [6], which persisted in BuMP. Though PLs value research, they are limited by conflicts between work and life commitments [9]. Improvements in PL research engagement occur when study leaders are in direct contact with participants [10]; however, reminders to participate must be balanced to avoid PLs feeling coerced to participate [7]. Embedding study participation into departmental didactics was originally proposed to optimize PL scheduling. Unfortunately, support for dedicated time became unavailable and as study lead (JP-C) is a residency program director, there was concern that repeated contact to PLs may result in perceived pressure to participate. As a result, recruitment strategies were limited and PLs were required to participate during off hours, impacting study outcomes.

Funding was another limitation to successful execution. Initially, BuMP’s projected budget was based on a similar project that used VBM ($160,000) [24], where funds were largely for salary time. Ultimately, the research team received $16,500 to execute the study. Lack of available funding required a redirection of monies and reduced available incentives and staff time to work on the study, impacting recruitment and retention. Additionally, given that ACT interventions are designed to integrate gradually into daily life, a longer follow-up period of 2–3 months or more may be necessary to fully assess outcomes, which was not feasible within the current study timeline.

Although the research team made efforts to mediate study design flaws specific to conducting research on PLs and adapted throughout the study, we were ultimately unsuccessful. To address the challenges we have outlined, future research could benefit from following established frameworks for feasibility and pilot studies [36, 37]. Applying these frameworks to research with PLs would involve systematically assessing recruitment barriers and facilitators early on, for example by piloting different engagement strategies and timing (demand), ensuring sufficient funding and staffing to support recruitment and retention (practicality), and adapting study procedures to minimize participant burden and perceived coercion (adaptation) [37]. Embedding participation into protected educational time could improve implementation and integration within the PLs’ workflow. Regular monitoring and iterative adjustment of these factors during the study (e.g., using polling early on as a systematic feasibility assessment to understand attrition) would allow research teams to identify and address challenges promptly, increasing the likelihood of successful recruitment, retention, and data collection in larger-scale studies.
