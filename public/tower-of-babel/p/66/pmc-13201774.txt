Lattice strain-mediated MoSe2 enable superior piezocatalysis activity for upcycling of organic pollutants
Authors: Qiang Zhong, Yue Sun, Shao-Gui Yang, Yin-Hao Dai, Huan He, Zhao-Qing Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Piezocatalytic upcycling of organic pollutants in wastewater into value-added chemicals rather than CO2 is a dual solution to the environmental and energy crisis, but due to the rapid recombination of piezoelectric electron-hole pairs and the significant reaction barriers for intermediate adsorption and dissociation, which result in a sluggish reaction kinetics. Here, we present a MoSe2 piezocatalyst with lattice strain that collaboratively regulates d-band center and the orbitals hybridization between metal sites and *CO2 as well as *COOH intermediates to achieve rapid conversion of carbon intermediate to critical *COOH intermediates, accelerating the overall carbon reduction with a lower reaction energy barrier. Mechanism study reveals that lattice strain can regulate the local electronic structures of Mo sites, not only improve the adsorption and activation of carbon intermediate, but also inhibit the H* formation from the H2O dissociation, thereby shifting the carbon reduction mechanism from H*-mediated reduction to proton-coupled electron transfer. Simultaneously, peroxymonosulfate acts as an important bridge to promote oxidation reaction and then provide a sufficient carbon source for CO formation. The lattice strained MoSe2 exhibits CO production performance of 155.60 μmol·g−1 and phenol degradation of 100%, 20 mg·L−1. This work deepens the comprehension of how lattice strain influences carbon reduction, and simultaneously offers a feasible approach for recycling real wastewater through fuel production and synchronous advanced treatment.

Introduction

Persulfate-based advanced oxidation processes have garnered significant interest as promising remediation technologies for refractory organic pollutants, while their treatment processes are usually accompanied by a substantial amount of uncontrolled carbon dioxide (CO2) emissions or a large amount of sludge due to the polymerization of organic pollutants, which seriously impede the progress toward carbon neutrality and pollution control1–5. Considering that organic wastewater is a carrier of resources and energy, conversion of organic pollutants into value-added chemical feedstocks, carbon monoxide (CO), supplies a promising strategy for simultaneous pollutant removal and resource recovery, along with the reduction of carbon footprint and energy consumption, and the process can be seamlessly integrated with the established Fischer-Tropsch synthesis to produce olefins6,7. Indeed, research has rarely documented the production of value-added chemicals from real wastewater, as most wastewater treatment only focuses on the efficient removal and deep mineralization of organic pollutants8–10. Currently, potentially promising technologies for the sustainable conversion of pollutants include photocatalysis11, electrocatalysis12, and piezocatalysis13, especially piezocatalysis with simplicity, cleanliness, and high efficiency characteristics14–16. Notably, the transition metal co-catalyst-modified piezocatalysts coupled with persulfate can achieve efficient pollutant degradation synchronizes carbon reduction13,17. However, the current research seems to pay little attention to the piezocatalyst itself, as loading metal cocatalysts undoubtedly will significantly increase the cost, and it may also cause secondary pollution due to leakage, which severely limits their wide applications. In addition, the reaction mechanism for converting organic pollutants into value-added chemicals, especially for the carbon reduction stage, remains largely unexplored.

Ensuring efficient piezoelectric catalytic performance in practical applications remains a challenge, primarily due to the rapid recombination of piezoelectric electron-hole pairs18. In addition, most of the developed catalysts for carbon reduction reaction through a H*-mediated reduction mechanism, which encompasses the production of reactive H* to serve as the reductant and the subsequent hydrogenation of carbon intermediate11,19,20. To be specific, the H2O molecule initially reorients from a parallel arrangement to a thermodynamically advantageous H-end configuration, resulting in the formation of H*. Subsequently, the generated H* progresses along the catalyst surface to interact with the adsorbed carbon intermediate21,22. Clearly, the energy-consuming and kinetically unfavorable nature of these dynamic deformations of reactants is evident23. Meanwhile, the simultaneous dimerization of H* to H2 would significantly reduce the efficiency of carbon reduction. Therefore, a significant challenge for carbon reduction is the considerable reaction barrier for the carbon intermediate adsorption and dissociation, which makes the carbon reduction kinetics sluggish and complex. To realize efficient H*-independent carbon reduction reaction, catalysts should possess strong carbon intermediate but poor H2O adsorption ability, which can be realized with electropositive transition metal (Mn+) catalytic sites, because the unoccupied orbitals of Mn+ can accept the electrons from carbon intermediate, while the occupied d orbitals of Mn+ can back donate electrons to the antibonding π* orbitals of carbon intermediate, enabling an adequate hybridization of metal-oxygen orbitals through the interactions of “acceptance-backdonation“24,25. In this case, the carbon intermediate is capable of being directly reduced by electrons originating from the Mn+ catalytic sites, along with protons provided by H2O in the solution through a proton-coupled electron transfer (PCET) pathway, similar to the process catalyzed by CO2 reductase26,27. This PCET mechanism is anticipated to reduce the competition between the solution and the carbon reduction reaction. Meanwhile, the hydrogenation of the carbon intermediate may be initiated by directly capturing protons from H2O, in which the spontaneously formed hydrogen bond network around the carbon intermediate can significantly enhance the proton mobility among the interlaced H2O to build a kinetically feasible path28,29. Obviously, enhancing the catalytic activity of piezocatalysts and improving the reaction path of PCET are the goals being pursued, but it remains a great challenge.

Strain engineering has been a promising approach to modulate the band structure of two-dimensional transition metal dichalcogenides and tailor the electronic properties at the nanoscale30,31. Notably, the essential origin of piezoelectricity lies in the noncentrosymmetric lattice structure and renders piezoelectric materials highly sensitive to strain states32,33. It is well accepted that when the symmetry of the semiconductor crystal structure is broken, the polarization electric field generated by the asymmetric center can overcome the coulomb field, which will supply a significant driving force for the separation and movement of piezoelectric electron-hole34,35. In addition, stretching the surface of catalysts can create vacancies and improve the electronic field, ultimately benefiting catalytic performance36. Notably, the tensile strain affords an increased level of electron accumulation near the metal atoms, thus facilitating the interactions among d and s/p orbitals24,37. The adsorption thermodynamic energies of polyatomic molecules (e.g., H2O, H, OH, CO, CO2, N2, NH3, etc.) can be adjusted on a strained solid surface38–41, indicating the significant potential of strain engineering across various catalytic applications. Accordingly, the development of piezoelectric catalysts via lattice strain engineering presents a promising approach for simultaneously enhancing the activity and selectivity of carbon reduction. However, there have been relatively few studies on piezoelectric catalysts with strain engineering in piezocatalysis, and the relationship between lattice strain and carbon reduction remains unclear.

Here, we demonstrate a lattice-strained (LS) MoSe2 with excellent piezoelectric performance to efficiently promote organic pollutants degradation, simultaneously carbon reduction performance, which exhibits 100% selective conversion of phenol into CO with a production rate of nearly 155.60 μmol·g−1 in the LS-MoSe2-II/PMS/ultrasound (US) system, significantly outperforming the comparable catalyst of MoSe2. Experimental and theoretical analyses indicate that the introduction of lattice strain can lead to a stronger internal electric field in the crystal materials, thus accelerating the separation and migration of piezoinduced charges. Moreover, LS-MoSe2-II features higher oxidation states and d-band centers closer to the Fermi level as compared to MoSe2, which effectively raises the quantity of electronic states situated close to the Fermi level and lowers energy barriers for *COOH generation as well as *CO desorption and enhances the transfer of piezo-induced charges. More importantly, LS-MoSe2-II with unsaturated Mo sites in favor of stronger CO32-/CO2 adsorption and inhibits the H* formation from the H2O dissociation, thereby altering the carbon reduction mechanism from H*-mediated reduction to PCET. Besides, the developed LS-MoSe2-II piezocatalyst is also extended to other substrate wastewaters with excellent stability and adaptability. This work not only delves deep into strain-induced effects on kinetics optimization and profiling of catalytic mechanisms in wastewater treatment systems, but also provides instructive guidelines for achieving carbon neutralization by low-carbon emissions in wastewater remediation.

Results

Surface structural characterization of lattice strain

The MoSe2 nanoflowers were produced by a hydrothermal method, then as-obtained MoSe2 was treated with hydrazine hydrate to introduce lattice strain into the catalyst (Supplementary Fig. 1). Due to the simple preparation process, the cost of LS-MoSe2-II has been significantly reduced and it can be easily produced at the gram level (Supplementary Fig. 2). The precise control of the amount of hydrazine hydrate is crucial for regulating the strain degree, and among all the samples, LS-MoSe2-II shows the best activity and therefore selected as the model catalyst. The MoSe2 and LS-MoSe2-II exhibit similar specific surface areas (Supplementary Fig. 3). X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns (Supplementary Fig. 4) illustrate that the as-prepared MoSe2 and LS-MoSe2-(I, II, III) exhibit pure MoSe2 (JCPDS card number: 87–2419) without any other crystalline impurities42. Besides, the peak position in the XRD pattern of LS-MoSe2-II moves towards a lower angle compared to the MoSe2, LS-MoSe2-I, and LS-MoSe2-III patterns, suggesting a decreased diffraction angle that confirms the increased average lattice spacing43. Under the action of strain, the cell volume is increased, and the bond length is changed. The lattice-related parameter information of the LS-MoSe2-II and MoSe2 catalysts was further determined via Rietveld refinement. LS-MoSe2-II exhibits evident cell expansion, indicating that the lattice stretching occurred (Fig. 1a). The Raman spectrum verifies the 1 T phase of MoSe2 and LS-MoSe2-II, suggesting that the crystal phase remains unaffected by lattice strain (Fig. 1b). It is well-established that the E1g vibration modes are responsive to the structural characteristics of MoSe2. Contrasted to MoSe2, the E1g mode of LS-MoSe2-II exhibits a noticeable red shift due to the introduction of lattice strain, which is typically associated with the rise in vibrational energy39. Furthermore, the energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) elemental mappings (Supplementary Fig. 5-7) verified the presence of Mo and Se elements and the uniform distribution on individual nanoflowers of MoSe2 and LS-MoSe2-II. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images (Supplementary Fig. 8) further emphasize that MoSe2 and LS-MoSe2-II consist of randomly layered ultrathin nanosheets, forming a nanoflower-like structure, which is more beneficial to the deformation under pressure, thereby promoting more efficient harvesting and conversion of mechanical vibration energy44. The selected area electron diffraction (SAED) pattern for LS-MoSe2-II exhibits continuous bright rings that matched with the (100) and (110) planes of 1T-MoSe2, respectively42 (Supplementary Fig. 9), indicating that LS-MoSe2-II is mainly 1 T phase, which is consistent with Raman results. In addition, LS-MoSe2-II exhibits a (002) interplanar spacing of 0.69 nm, which is higher than that of MoSe2 due to lattice tension strain (Supplementary Fig. 10). To examine the distinct atomic arrangement, geometric phase analysis (GPA) was utilized to identify variations in lattice strain (Fig. 1c–f and Supplementary Figs. 11–12). In these GPA maps, LS-MoSe2-II displays different levels of lattice strain in shear strain (Ɛxy), in-plane strain (Ɛxx) and out-of-plane strain (Ɛyy), and produces more intense dislocation dipoles, especially the Ɛyy maximum values of ~8.1% are reached on the LS-MoSe2-II, which all are significantly better than MoSe2 (0%), LS-MoSe2-I ( ~ 2.5%), and LS-MoSe2-III ( ~ 5.2%), and supplying the visual evidence of the lattice strain existence in catalyst surface. Notably, the average normal strains are either positive or tensile, which aligns with the average lattice strain derived from measuring lattice constants. The enhanced strain field in LS-MoSe2-II is likely to lead to a more altered surface atomic configuration in LS-MoSe2-II, thereby potentially influencing the reactivity of the catalyst surface. The electronic structure and chemical environment of MoSe2 and LS-MoSe2-II were first investigated via X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). The high-resolution XPS Mo 3 d signals indicate the double peaks centered at ~227.9 and ~231.1 eV can be attributed to 3 d5/2 (Mo4+) and 3 d3/2 (Mo4+) (Supplementary Fig. 13a), respectively39. By introducing lattice strain for LS-MoSe2-II, the binding energy of Mo 3d gradually blue shifts, indicating a more delocalized local electric structure. Obviously, the electron acceptor properties of Mo centers are enhanced, creating favorable conditions for the electron transfer of the reaction intermediate during the carbon reduction process. Furthermore, the Se 3d peaks of LS-MoSe2-II shift to lower binding energy in comparison to MoSe2 (Supplementary Fig. 13b), meaning Se sites are enriched with an abundance of electrons due to the effect of strain, which is in favor of proton-coupled electron transfer steps and thus accelerating carbon reduction performance45. X-ray absorption near-edge spectroscopy (XANES) was performed on the MoSe2 and LS-MoSe2-II composites at the Mo K-edge to investigate inherent electronic structures. The absorption edges observed for the catalysts fall within the range established by the Mo foil and MoO3 references, suggesting that the average valence of Mo species is between 0 and +6, which aligns with the XPS findings (Fig. 1g and Supplementary Fig. 14). The peak near ~20010 eV primarily originates from electron transitions occurring between the 1 s and 4 d orbitals in the Mo-anion configuration ([MoSe6] within MoSe2)46,47. By introducing lattice strain, the adsorption edge of LS-MoSe2-II towards higher energy is contrasted to that of MoSe2, which is attributed to the significant electronic delocalization by the strain effect. In addition, the extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) (Fig. 1h and Supplementary Fig. 15) shows a gradual decrease in intensity compared to MoSe2. Notably, the Mo-Se coordination numbers (CN) of LS-MoSe2-II are 4.7, and lower than that of MoSe2 (5.2), which is mainly due to the introducing lattice strain into LS-MoSe2-II, leading to the disruption of Mo-Se bonds and the formation of unsaturated Mo sites (Fig. 1i). Moreover, compared with MoSe2, a notable shift of Mo-Mo bonds to a longer position in R-space is observed for LS-MoSe2-II, which can be ascribed to the tensile strain present on the surface and the increased length of the Mo-Mo bonds. Due to confinement by Mo, the adjacent Se atoms leave the lattice, resulting in electron imbalance and lattice distortions, thus triggering the creation of self-regulating Se vacancies (Vse) as a response to Jahn-Teller distortions. Wavelet transform (WT) analysis of EXAFS spectra, which supplies both R- and k-space information and differentiates the back-scattering atoms, was performed to visualize and validate the Mo-Se bond and Mo-Mo bond (Supplementary Fig. 16). Furthermore, electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) was conducted to explore the state of Vse (Supplementary Fig. 17a). The EPR signal intensity at g = 2.003 for LS-MoSe2-II is significantly stronger than that of MoSe2, indicating that it is mainly due to the lattice strain introduced in LS-MoSe2-II. Besides, the d-band center of LS-MoSe2-II and MoSe2 is calculated to assess the impact on electronic structure over strain engineering (Supplementary Fig. 17b). The d-band projected density of states (PDOS) reveals a shift in the d-band center towards the Fermi energy level and has more unoccupied active metal 3 d orbitals with the introduction of tensile strain. The movement of the d-band center is highly advantageous for enhancing catalyst performance due to its potential effect on the adsorption/desorption characteristics of specific intermediates48. Moreover, LS-MoSe2-II exhibits a smaller band gap (Supplementary Fig. 18a). In other words, the density of states (DOS) integral of LS-MoSe2-II is higher than that of MoSe2, indicating that more charge carriers are activated (Supplementary Fig. 17b). Proper lattice strain can change the electron concentration in the semiconductor and may have a better catalytic performance. To enhance comprehension of the interfacial charge transfer and electronic structure of MoSe2 and LS-MoSe2-II, we calculated the work functions (Wf) for the samples utilizing the electron cutoff (Ecutoff) obtained from ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy (UPS) spectra. The Ecutoff of MoSe2 and LS-MoSe2-II are determined to be 18.79 eV and 19.08 eV, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 18b). The work function (ø) is calculated by the equation ø = 21.22 eV − Ecutoff. Specifically, LS-MoSe2-II affords a lower work function compared to MoSe2, suggesting that the application of tensile strain results in a decrease in Fermi energy. When calculating the d-band center and eg orbital occupancy, the Fermi energy serves as the reference zero point. Obviously, the lower Wf of LS-MoSe2-II implies its lower energy requirement to supply electrons to reaction intermediates, thus facilitating electron transfer.

Piezoelectricity and electrochemical analysis of the catalysts

The piezoelectric characteristics of MoSe2 and LS-MoSe2-II were investigated using piezoresponse force microscopy (PFM). Figure 2a presents an atomic force microscope (AFM) image obtained during probe scanning on the surface of LS-MoSe2-II. As shown in Fig. 2b, c, the corresponding amplitude and phase of LS-MoSe2-II under alternating current also exhibit obvious piezoelectric response signals. The amplitude curve with a butterfly shape (Fig. 2d) shows significant changes under alternating current, while the phase angle varies by about 140° within the hysteresis loop. A similar result on the MoSe2 is also observed (Fig. 2e and Supplementary Fig. 19). Besides, the Kelvin probe force microscopy (KPFM) test (Supplementary Fig. 20) exhibits notable piezo-potential on the LS-MoSe2-II surface due to piezoelectric polarization. These findings verify the significant piezoelectric properties of LS-MoSe2-II. Notably, the maximum effective piezoelectric coefficient (d33) value derived from the amplitude loops for MoSe2 and LS-MoSe2-II is 5.82 and 11.70 pm V−1 (Fig. 2f), respectively, which confirms the stronger piezoelectric properties of LS-MoSe2-II due to the presence of lattice strain. The excellent piezoelectricity of LS-MoSe2-II can produce more polarization charges with the applied external stress, facilitating the surface redox reactions efficiently. Moreover, the surface potentials of LS-MoSe2-II (96 mV) inside the region are higher than that of MoSe2 (71 mV), the larger surface potential difference of LS-MoSe2-II suggests the more efficient charge separation (Supplementary Fig. 20-21)14. The Zeta potential can further illustrate this point (Supplementary Fig. 22), as LS-MoSe2-II isoelectric point of −13, much smaller than that of MoSe2 (−8). The piezoelectric potential for MoSe2 and LS-MoSe2-II induced by strain was analyzed by COMSOL simulations. Obviously, the LS-MoSe2-II displays much higher piezo-potentials than MoSe2 under the same stress (Fig. 2g, h), thereby providing a stronger reaction driving force for organic pollutants degradation, which synchronously realizes carbon reduction. Interestingly, the piezoelectric potential of MoSe2 is mainly distributed on the edge of the nanoflower, while the piezoelectric potential of LS-MoSe2-II is evenly distributed on the surface of the nanoflower, and the potential is larger; thus, the introduction of lattice strain can effectively affect the distribution of piezopotential and regulate the redox activity of LS-MoSe2-II. In theory, the mechanical vibrations in piezoelectric materials cause a cyclic change in the direction of the electric field, which results in a swift recombination of piezoelectric charges. This phenomenon negatively affects the efficiency of piezocatalysis. Therefore, it is essential to improve the lifespan of piezoelectric charges and facilitate charge separation to enhance the performance of redox reactions driven by piezoelectricity. And the LS-MoSe2-II exhibits a smaller electrochemical impedance spectra (EIS) arc radius (Supplementary Fig. 23a), illustrating a faster charge separation efficiency of LS-MoSe2-II13. MoSe2 and LS-MoSe2-II exhibit a significant current response with the US vibration (Supplementary Fig. 23b), particularly the magnitude of piezocurrent response in the LS-MoSe2-II is higher than MoSe2, indicating the more charge can migrate to the surface of material to adsorb and activate reaction intermediate, which consists with the linear sweep voltammetry (LSV), cyclic voltammetry curve (CV) and the results observed from PL attenuation results (Supplementary Fig. 24-25)13,34. Moreover, as illustrated in Fig. 2i, the Mott-Schottky curves for MoSe2 exhibit a steeper slope compared to that of LS-MoSe2-II, corresponding to a smaller surface charge of MoSe2 than that of LS-MoSe2-II49. Generally, the piezo-reactive site of MoSe2 is located at the edge of the nanoflower50 (Supplementary Fig. 26). Piezocatalytic CO production and synchronous wastewater treatment

Encouraged by the above results, the activity of the catalysts was evaluated based on the conversion of pollutants into CO under various conditions without any employment of sacrificial agents and loading of the cocatalyst in a sealed gas system, which verified that the lattice strain can improve the catalysis reactivity (Supplementary Fig. 28). The LS-MoSe2-II catalyst demonstrates the highest catalytic efficiency compared to the other samples featuring various lattice-strained MoSe2. As displayed, the LS-MoSe2-II/PMS/US system exhibits great activity with near 100% degradation of phenol and 69.52 μmol·g−1 CO production rate with a remarkable selectivity of 100% without other gas and liquid production (Fig. 3a, b and Supplementary Figs. 29–31). Comparably, MoSe2/PMS/US system also shows 100% phenol degradation, but the CO yield is only 7.98 μmol·g−1 with a small amount of unwanted H2 production (Fig. 3a, b and Supplementary Fig. 32). The PMS, US, and LS-MoSe2-II alone do not produce CO, besides the production of CO significantly decreases and 48% of phenol could be degraded in the PMS/US system with 4 h, indicating the limited oxidation capability of PMS without activation, as well as its activation mainly via the catalyst (Supplementary Fig. 33). Only 41% of phenol is decomposed in the LS-MoSe2-II/US system, with nearly no CO gas observed, suggesting that the LS-MoSe2-II indeed exhibits a piezoelectric effect under US vibration. However, this effect is insufficient for the direct oxidation of phenol, and deep oxidation of phenol via activated PMS is the premise for CO production. Notably, although the achievement of 100% phenol oxidation is fulfilled in the LS-MoSe2-II/PMS system, almost no CO production is detected (Supplementary Fig. 33), suggesting that US plays a critical role in triggering the CO evolution reaction. In the Co2+/PMS, Fe2+/H2O2, and Fe2+/PDS systems (Fig. 3c), phenol is also fully decomposed, with TOC removal achieving rates of 66.8%, 39.3%, and 36.9% (Supplementary Fig. 34), respectively. However, the lack of CO gas production further underscores an indispensable role of piezocatalysis in generating CO. In addition, it can be observed that when hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) or peroxydisulfate (PDS) is employed rather than PMS as the oxidant, which can still achieve organic pollutants degradation synchronous piezocatalytic CO production (Supplementary Fig. 35a). Based on the above results, we can infer that the remarkable conversion performance of phenol to CO in the LS-MoSe2-II/PMS/US system can be ascribed to the effective synergy between PMS activation and piezocatalysis. Moreover, when AgNO3 is introduced as an electron trapping agent into the LS-MoSe2-II/PMS/US system, the degradation rate of phenol remains at 100%, while the yield of CO remarkably decreases from 69.52 to 8.76 μmol·g−1 (Supplementary Fig. 33). Clearly, phenol oxidation is primarily triggered via activated PMS, and the piezoelectric electrons play a significant role in the reduction of products derived from phenol oxidation to CO evolution. The negligible CO production observed in the LS-MoSe2-II/PMS/US system in the absence of phenol demonstrates that the source of CO certainly originates from phenol rather than the presence of impurity carbon. In-situ Raman analysis shows that MoSe2/US and LS-MoSe2-II/US systems with similar PMS activation properties, indicating that two systems afford similar oxidative degradation capacity of phenol (Supplementary Fig. 35b). Notably, the degradation rates of phenol in MoSe2/PMS/US and LS-MoSe2-II/PMS/US systems all reach 100%, but the CO yields of the two systems are significantly different (Fig. 3a, b), indicating that the selective production of CO is closely related to the carbon reduction half-reaction. Remarkably, as displayed in Fig. 3d, the CO evolution rate of LS-MoSe2-II is significantly superior to that of MoSe2, LS-MoSe2-I, and LS-MoSe2-III, indicating the inherently high catalytic activity of LS-MoSe2-II and the great contribution of lattice strain in promoting carbon reduction into CO. Given the importance of PMS in the reaction system, the effects of different amounts of PMS are examined. As displayed, CO production is increased and then slowed with increasing PMS concentration (Supplementary Fig. 36). The LS-MoSe2-II catalyst exhibits the highest catalytic efficiency with 1.8 mg·mL−1 of PMS, resulting in 69.52 μmol·g−1 CO production, 100% phenol degradation and 65.78% TOC removal. Furthermore, the CO production rate increases from 69.52 to 155.60 μmol·g−1 when the concentration of phenol is elevated from 10 to 20 mg·L−1 in the LS-MoSe2-II/PMS/US system with 4 h (Supplementary Fig. 37). With an increase in phenol concentration to 30 mg·L−1, the production of CO reaches 149.69 μmol·g−1, while the increase is not substantial contrasted to the yield observed at 20 mg·L−1 phenol, which might be ascribed to excessive intermediates resulting from the degradation of phenol obstructing the reaction site. In comparison with reported organic pollutant degradation and CO generation via photocatalysis and piezocatalysis, this LS-MoSe2-II/PMS/US system converted organic pollutant into CO, exhibiting an advantage in the CO production, even superior to some of the co-catalyst-modified piezocatalyst systems (Supplementary Table 1). LS-MoSe2-II, subject to strain induced by the ultrasonic vibration with different frequencies, exhibits noticeable variations in CO yield (Supplementary Fig. 38a), with the frequency of 40 kHz resulting in the highest CO yield. The influence of pH on the CO production in the LS-MoSe2-II/PMS/US system was investigated. A notable reduction in CO production is observed as the pH value increased, which is linked to a considerable decline in the efficiency of phenol removal (Supplementary Fig. 38b). Notably, the system exhibits remarkable resistance to interference from a range of inorganic anions (Supplementary Fig. 39a). Besides, even with the addition of gentle stirring (900 rpm for 30 h) rather than US, the CO evolution of LS-MoSe2-II/PMS system still reaches 9.72 μmol·g-1 (Supplementary Fig. 39b). Moreover, the phenol degradation rate and CO yield rate of LS-MoSe2-II remain consistently stable over 6 successive reaction cycles, demonstrating its long-term durability and significant potential for real-world applications (Fig. 3e). The XRD, Raman, and TEM analyses further reveal the excellent stability of LS-MoSe2-II during reaction (Supplementary Figs. 40 and 41), as verified by the negligible Mo dissolution of LS-MoSe2-II within 6 successive reaction cycles.

Active free radicals and reaction intermediates

Typically, the process of activating the PMS resulted in the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which was studied using the EPR technique alongside 5,5-dimethyl-1-pyrroline (DMPO) and 2,2,6,6-tetramethyl-4-piperidone (TEMP) as spin trapping agents1,9. As illustrated in Fig. 3f, the EPR signals of DMPO-·OH (aN = aH = 14.9 G) and DMPO-SO4•− (aN = 13.2 G, aH = 9.6 G, aHγ1 = 1.48 G, aHγ2 = 0.78 G) adducts are detected when LS-MoSe2-II and PMS are co-present under US vibration, suggesting the generation of SO4•− and ·OH, and the DMPO-SO4•− signals are significantly greater than those for DMPO-·OH. In addition, the signals for DMPO-O2•− and TEMP-1O2 are not observed, suggesting that the reaction system does not generate 1O2 and O2•−. In order to investigate the contribution of various radical types on the phenol removal, free radical quenching experiments were conducted (Supplementary Fig. 42). The introduction of MeOH (quencher for ⋅OH and SO4⋅−) along with TBA (a ⋅OH quencher) leads to a reduction in phenol degradation and CO yield13, demonstrating the generation of SO4⋅− and ⋅OH, with SO4⋅− are identified as the primary active species. Moreover, the presence of L-histidine (a 1O2 quencher) and 4-hydroxy-2,2,6,6-tetramethylpiperidine-N-oxyl (TEMPOL) (a ⋅O2− quencher) did not reveal any significant inhibition of phenol degradation or CO production, indicating that neither 1O2 nor ⋅O2− is generated, aligning with the findings from the EPR analysis. A similar conclusion on the MoSe2/PMS/US was also observed (Supplementary Fig. 43). These findings demonstrate that MoSe2 and LS-MoSe2-II can effectively activate PMS to produce ·OH and SO4•− for phenol degradation and provide sufficient carbon source for the subsequent efficient carbon reduction produces CO. To reveal the potential reaction mechanism of phenol conversion into CO, a number of characterization and comparison experiments were conducted. As illustrated, TOC measurement displays the decrease alongside a rise in inorganic carbon (IC), demonstrating the effective mineralization of phenol into CO2 present as carbonate (Supplementary Fig. 44a). Afterward, the analysis of inorganic ion presence throughout the reaction demonstrates that the concentration of CO32– rises initially and subsequently decreases during the 4 h US vibration (Fig. 3g), which signifies the mineralization of organic compounds and the ongoing cycle of accumulation and consumption as time advances. A comparable alteration in the pH value throughout the one-pot transformation on LS-MoSe2-II also suggests the process of dynamic accumulation and consumption (Supplementary Fig. 44b). In addition to this, clear peaks of carbonate (C=O) signals at 288.68 eV can be observed following the degradation of phenol within the LS-MoSe2-II/PMS/US system (Supplementary Fig. 45a), further proving the above conclusion13. Therefore, we reasonably speculate that the generation of CO is connected to carbonate and CO2, as phenol can be oxidized and degraded to carbonate or CO2 and provide a sufficient carbon source for the subsequent efficient carbon reduction, which produces CO. To illustrate this point, carbonate and CO2 were chosen as the carbon source, while other reaction parameters stayed constant. Figure 3h shows that the yield of CO progressively rises from 265.89 to 331.64 μmol·g−1 as the amount of carbonate (Na2CO3) is increased from 5.6 to 9.8 mg in the LS-MoSe2-II/PMS/US system, and CO generation is still observable even when low concentrations of CO2 replace the carbonate. Besides, when KSCN is present, the yield of CO2 or carbonate converted into CO in the LS-MoSe2-II/PMS/US system is significantly suppressed (Supplementary Fig. 45b). As SCN- is readily adsorbed onto metal sites, the pivotal metal-poisoning experiment confirmed that CO2 or carbonate reduction is closely related to metal sites. It is accepted that the carbon reduction process is controlled by either H*-mediated reduction or PCET mechanism51, hinging on the production and utilization of H*, which has been then investigated through the EPR technology employing 5,5-dimethyl-1-pyrrolidine-N-oxide (DMPO) as a trapping agent to form a spin adduct DMPO-H*52,53. In the pure water, the distinct nine signals of DMPO-H* are detected but disappeared after the introduction of CO32-/CO2, suggesting the possible involvement of H* in carbon reduction within the LS-MoSe2-II/US and MoSe2/US systems (Fig. 4c and Supplementary Fig. 46). Simultaneously, the inclusion of Tert-butyl alcohol (TBA) or DMPO significantly diminish the efficiency of CO32-/CO2 conversion, confirming the essential role of H* in the carbon reduction process on the MoSe2/US system (Supplementary Figs. 46 and 47). Nevertheless, TBA quenching could not hinder CO32-/CO2 reduction on LS-MoSe2-II/US system, excluding the involvement of H* in carbon reduction on LS-MoSe2-II/US system (Supplementary Figs. 48 and 49), while the introducing of DMPO to quench H* produces a comparable experimental outcome for LS-MoSe2-II/US system (Supplementary Fig. 50). These experimental findings provide robust evidence for the distinct carbon reduction mechanisms present in the LS-MoSe2-II/US and MoSe2/US systems, as the introduction of lattice strain changes the electronic structure of LS-MoSe2-II and the exposure of the active site, the adsorption and activation energy of the active site to carbon intermediates are changed. Notably, the surface-adsorbed H2O on LS-MoSe2-II is considerably weaker than that on MoSe2, suggesting that LS-MoSe2-II could significantly suppress the H2O adsorption (Fig. 4d), as further confirmed by the larger H2O contact angle on the LS-MoSe2-II than that on MoSe2 (Fig. 4e). Obviously, as the preferential CO32-/CO2 adsorption on the Mo in LS-MoSe2-II can hinder the H2O adsorption, thereby transitioning the H*-mediated reduction mechanism on MoSe2/US system to a more viable PCET mechanism on LS-MoSe2-II/US. In addition, the i-t curve was employed to assess the efficiency of electron transfer between the catalyst and CO32- (Supplementary Fig. 51). After adding CO32-, the current of LS-MoSe2-II increases sharply, suggesting that substantial electron transfer takes place between LS-MoSe2-II and CO32-, and the electron transfer of LS-MoSe2-II is significantly stronger than that of MoSe2, emphasizing that LS-MoSe2-II has a better PCET path for carbon reduction reactions. Furthermore, CO2-temperature-programmed desorption (CO2-TPD) characterization was conducted up to 800 °C to examine the interaction between the CO2 reactant and the prepared samples for comparative analysis. Notably, two distinctive peaks at 156.3 and 306.2 °C in the CO2-TPD curves of LS-MoSe2-II, indexing as the adsorbed CO2 molecule and HCO3-/*COOH, which are ascribed to the weak and medium basic sites of LS-MoSe2-II6, respectively, show greater intensities and the peak at 306.2 °C signally shifts to a higher temperature contrasted to MoSe2 (Fig. 4f). This further suggests that the emergence of lattice strain boosts the formation of medium basic sites, which aid in the production of *COOH. Furthermore, the pathway of phenol converts into CO over LS-MoSe2-II was thoroughly examined through using in situ diffuse reflectance infrared Fourier transform spectroscopy (DRIFTS) to shed more insights into the origin of the enhanced for CO conversion. Under US vibration, the intensity of peaks between 1000 and 2200 cm−1 progressively rises for the LS-MoSe2-II due to the generation of carbonate species. As illustrated in Fig. 4g, the emergence of new peaks at 1303, 1371, 1427 and 1338, 1461 cm−1 corresponds to the C-O stretching associated with the bidentate carbonate (b-CO32-) and monodentate carbonate (m-CO32-) groups, respectively25,54, as the oxidation of phenol to carbonate, which is in accordance with XPS and IC results. Besides, the vibration peaks at 1230 cm−1 can be ascribed to the stretching vibration of adsorbed CO2 (*CO2) molecules on the LS-MoSe2-II and MoSe2, respectively25. The varying locations of identical intermediates may result from the distinct adsorption reaction sites found in various materials. Obviously, due to the electronic difference between LS-MoSe2-II and MoSe2, the Mo bearing different electron densities interact with CO2, not in the same way, and the peak intensity for *CO2 on the LS-MoSe2-II is much stronger than that of MoSe2. Interestingly, with increasing ultrasonic time, the characteristic peaks of *COOH appeared at 1137, 1556 and 1791 cm−1 illustrate the effective hydrogenation route from *CO2 to *COOH11,25,48, which is the most crucial intermediate in the carbon piezoelectric reduction into CO process. Moreover, the *COOH peaks on the LS-MoSe2-II is significantly strengthened relative to that on MoSe2 (Supplementary Fig. 52a), indicating more facile conversion from *CO2 into *COOH on LS-MoSe2-II. The gradually enhanced peaks at 1189 cm−1 and 2075 cm−1 are linked to the produced CO54, as the *COOH species receive an additional proton and undergo dehydration to generate *CO species. Notably, the peak associated with *CO intermediates on LS-MoSe2-II is more readily desorbed to CO than MoSe2, and this point can be firmly demonstrated by the CO-TPD results (Supplementary Fig. 52b), as the CO molecule in LS-MoSe2-II is more easily released and desorbed from the surface due to lower desorption temperature. In addition, the weak absorption signal of *CO ( ~ 1902 cm−1) suggests that the formed *CO intermediate is easily desorbed at the catalytic sites to generate CO as the final product rather than being further hydrogenated to other products25. The above results demonstrate that phenol converts into CO follows a COOH* pathway. Therefore, the potential process for recycling the carbon source derived from phenol in the LS-MoSe2-II/PMS/US system may proceed as follows (Fig. 4h): (1) PMS is activated by LS-MoSe2-II and some piezoelectric electrons to produce SO4•− and ·OH and attack phenol to produce carbonate/CO2 intermediates; (2) the adsorption of the produced CO2/carbonate from phenol; (3) the activation of attached *CO2 to form a carboxyl *COOH; (4) the reduction and later breakdown of *COOH to yield *CO through a proton-electron transfer reduction mechanism; and (5) the release of CO.

Mechanism of lattice strain regulation of catalytic performance

Density functional theory (DFT) calculations were performed to explore the mechanism of lattice strain regulation of catalytic performance in the CO2/carbonate conversion into CO mechanism in the MoSe2 and LS-MoSe2-II system. Theoretical models were developed for two strain dimensionalities of MoSe2, one without strain and the other with ~8.1% strain applied. And we firstly explore the adsorption behavior and mechanism of CO2/carbonate on the MoSe2 and LS-MoSe2-II. The partial density of states (PDOS) revealed the d-band center of LS-MoSe2-II (−2.38 eV) is situated nearer to the Fermi energy level than that of MoSe2 (−2.58 eV) (Supplementary Fig. 17b). Therefore, Mo sites in LS-MoSe2-II feature more anti-bonding states and higher valency, which reinforces the binding of Mo centers to reactive intermediates (Fig. 5a). According to Sabatier principle, the modulation of adsorption strength of the key intermediates on the catalytic substrate is the key factor for the catalytic efficiency24. This result can be further confirmed by Hamilton population (COHP), as the absolute value of the integrated pCOHP (ICOHP) increases for CO2 and CO32- in LS-MoSe2-II compared to MoSe2 (Supplementary Fig. 53). In addition, LS-MoSe2-II with perfect and Se vacancy was studied (Supplementary Fig. 54). It is found that the adsorption of CO32-/CO2 by LS-MoSe2-II with Se vacancy is significantly stronger than the perfect LS-MoSe2-II, which further emphasizes that the influence of Se vacancy induced by tensile strain on the electronic structure of LS-MoSe2-II and improves the adsorption of CO32-/CO2 by Mo. Furthermore, the CO32-/CO2 adsorption Gibbs free energy of LS-MoSe2-II is much stronger than that of MoSe2 (Fig. 5b and Supplementary Fig. 55). In the case of H2O adsorption, the calculated Gibbs free energy on MoSe2 is higher than that on LS-MoSe2-II. Particularly, the adsorption of H2O is stronger than that of CO32-/CO2 on MoSe2, thereby MoSe2 promotes H2O dissociation for H* generation, enabling the subsequent carbon reduction through the H*-mediated reduction mechanism. In contrast, the enhanced adsorption of CO32-/CO2 on LS-MoSe2-II inhibits the adsorption and dissociation of H2O, shifting to the mechanism of PCET for carbon reduction. Notably, the calculated Gibbs free energy profiles for the HER indicate that MoSe2 is more likely to generate *H than LS-MoSe2-II (Fig. 5c), further showing that LS-MoSe2-II is more consistent with the PCET mechanism for carbon reduction. In addition, as displayed in Fig. 5d, e, the band gap of MoSe2 is calculated to be about 1.52 eV. After introducing lattice strain, the band gap of LS-MoSe2-II is decreased to about 1.43 eV, indicating that lattice strain can change the electron concentration in the semiconductor. Similar conclusions can be further demonstrated from the electron contour maps (Supplementary Fig. 56). Therefore, Bader charge analysis reveals electrostatic attraction between the Mo and CO32-/CO2 contribute to a more electron deliver for the CO32- (1.24 |e | ) and CO2 (0.65 |e | ) adsorption on LS-MoSe2-II than the case of MoSe2 for CO32- (1.17 |e | ) and CO2 (0.30 |e | ) (Fig. 5f), indicating that the Mo sites in LS-MoSe2-II possess stronger adsorption and activation ability for CO2/carbonate. To understand the distinctive function of lattice strain for highly efficient carbon reduction, the carbon reduction reaction processes on LS-MoSe2-II and MoSe2 were investigated. As discussed above, the phenol converts into CO follows a *COOH pathway, and we calculate the hydrogenation steps for CO2 and CO32- on MoSe2 and LS-MoSe2-II. As illustrated in Fig. 5g, h, the initial bonding of substrate to CO32-/CO2 can proceed smoothly for both non-strain and strain models, within which a lower energy barrier for the strained model is required, suggesting a more dynamically advantageous adsorption process for CO32-/CO2. Notably, both CO32- and CO2 reduction processes to produce CO go through the process of producing *CO2 intermediates, and the free energy of H* in MoSe2 is lower the *CO2, but the opposite results in LS-MoSe2-II (Fig. 5c, g, h), further emphasizing that the formation of H* in MoSe2 is easier than the formation of *CO2, while the formation of H* is more difficult than the formation of *CO2 in LS-MoSe2-II, and the hydrogenation pathway from *CO2 to COOH* becomes more accessible for the strained model. However, this also complicates the subsequent hydrogenation route from *COOH* to *CO*, due to an increased uphill energy barrier from *CO2 to COOH*. This significant uphill energy barrier suggests that it may serve as the potential rate-limiting factor, ultimately hindering the overall efficiency of the reaction. For the CO2 reduction reaction on MoSe2, the potential-determining step is *CO2 to *COOH with a thermodynamic barrier of 1.74 eV, while the potential-determining step is also determined to be *CO2 to *COOH with a thermodynamic barrier of 0.51 eV on LS-MoSe2-II, thus leading to a significantly enhanced carbon reduction activity compared to MoSe2. Supplementary Fig. 57 shows that there is a correlation between lattice strain and the thermodynamic energy barrier for *CO2 to *COOH, further emphasizing the promoting effect of lattice strain on catalytic activity. Notably, in the PDOS of *COOH and metal sites (Fig. 5i and Supplementary Fig. 58), the d-band center of LS-MoSe2-II ⋅ ⋅ ⋅ *COOH (−2.42 eV) is significantly higher than that of MoSe2 ⋅ ⋅ ⋅ *COOH (−2.61 eV), indicating a stronger adsorption affinity between *COOH and LS-MoSe2-II. This potential-determining step change stems from the influence of lattice strain on LS-MoSe2-II, leading to the significantly decreased free energy of *COOH to *CO from 0.69 eV (MoSe2) to 0.48 eV (LS-MoSe2-II). The necessary barrier energy has dropped by 0.21 eV, exhibiting a greater intensity compared to the adsorption process, suggesting that the dissociation of the *CO intermediate is significantly more favorable. This can also be observed from the weaker C-O bond strength (more negative crystal orbital Hamilton population (COHP) in the COHP plot), as shown in Fig. 5k–l. To assess the environmental and health impacts of LS-MoSe2-II/PMS/US system for water treatment, an initial assessment of the toxicity of phenol and its byproducts was performed utilizing analytical software (Supplementary Figs. 60–61 and Supplementary Table 2-3). The findings indicate that some intermediates may still represent toxicity, the ecotoxicity of the majority of degradation products is significantly reduced, and forms CO due to the efficient carbon reduction process. To accurately evaluate the genuine toxicity of both systems, their toxicity was critically assessed utilizing microbial and animal models. Life cycle analysis (LCA) was subsequently conducted to quantify the sustainability and environmental impacts. As a contrast, catalytic systems based on MoSe2/PMS/US and MoS2/PMS/US were employed. The analysis encompasses the entire water treatment process, including catalyst preparation and pollutant degradation, synchronous CO production. This is assessed using 18 pertinent descriptors, including potential for global warming and ecotoxicity in freshwater/marine environments (Fig. 6c and Supplementary Figs. 63–64 and Supplementary Table 4-6). The analysis reveals that MoSe2/PMS/US and MoS2/PMS/US systems treated phenol wastewater generate significant environmental burdens across multiple categories. In contrast, the LS-MoSe2-II/PMS/US system demonstrates considerable benefits over both the MoSe2/PMS/US and the MoS2/PMS/US systems, as evidenced by its reduced impact scores in the majority of descriptors. This transformative outcome stems directly from avoiding resource-destructive mineralization while enabling high-value-added chemical CO recovery reclamation. The quantifiable ecological advantages position the LS-MoSe2-II/PMS/US system as a paradigm-shifting solution where wastewater remediation actively enhances environmental health rather than merely reducing damage, pointing out the significant role of piezocatalysis coupled with persulfate technology that efficiently reduces pollution and carbon emissions, while enabling simultaneous and effective resource conversion during wastewater treatment. To assess the potential application of the LS-MoSe2-II/PMS/US system, the performance of LS-MoSe2-II is further investigated through the conversion of different organic pollutants, including phenols, antibiotics, dyes, microplastics, per-and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS), and practical industrial wastewater. As expected, phenols, antibiotics, dye, and PFAS could be almost completely degraded in LS-MoSe2-II/PMS/US system, with the associated CO production observed following 4 h of US vibration (Fig. 6d). Besides, microplastics can also partially degrade with the production of CO, further highlighting the potential of LS-MoSe2-II/PMS/US system to treat microplastics and reduce carbon emissions. Moreover, the COD of practical wastewater decreases from an initial measurement of 1478.2 to 1095.6 mg·L−1 after 4 h of US vibration, resulting in a corresponding CO yield of 335.65 μmol·g−1 (Fig. 6e). Therefore, the above results vigorously prove that the LS-MoSe2-II/PMS/US system possesses significant practical application potential for effectively removing pollutants while achieving low-carbon emissions in wastewater treatment.

Discussion

In summary, we demonstrate a simple strain engineering strategy to obtain LS-MoSe2-II, which achieves remarkable catalytic performance with 100% of phenol degradation and an impressive TOC removal and CO yield rate (155.60 μmol·g−1), inhibiting the counterpart of unstrained MoSe2 catalyst. Notably, the conversion of phenol into CO involves an initial oxidation of phenol to carbonate/CO2 through PMS activation, followed by its reduction to CO via piezoelectric electrons with the benefit of thermodynamics. Lattice strain engineering modifies the electronic properties of LS-MoSe2, which regulates the d band center and the unsaturated state of Mo sites, improving the separation of piezogenerated charges. Importantly, for the carbon reduction process, experimental and theoretical calculations reveal that the optimized Mo sites enhance orbital hybridization with carbon reduction intermediate, construct a fast transfer channel of site-to-surface adsorbed molecules, resulting in rapid conversion of *CO2 and stabilization *COOH. Further examination revealed the intrinsic factors of superior carbon reduction performance in LS-MoSe2-II, which means that LS-MoSe2-II can drive the carbon reduction process via a kinetics-favorable PCTE mechanism rather than an H*-mediated one. Moreover, the LS-MoSe2-II/PMS/US system demonstrated significant potential in treating various refractory pollutants, including phenols, antibiotics, dyes, microplastics, PFAS, and practical industrial wastewater with excellent stability and adaptability. This work demonstrates the potential of lattice strain engineering of MoSe2 for the degradation of organic pollutants, providing an effective approach for creating an advanced piezoelectric catalyst.

Methods

Preparation of catalysts

The MoSe2 nanoflower was prepared by a modified one-step hydrothermal method: 0.1 mmol Na2MoO4·2H2O, 0.2 mmol Se, and 0.2 mmol NaBH4 were dissolved in 30 mL deionized water by ultrasound and mixed by stirring for 20 min. The mixture was then transferred into a 50 mL Teflon-lined autoclave and heated at 210 °C for 20 h. After cooling, the resulting mixture was washed several times with ultrapure water and ethanol to obtain MoSe2 samples. The second step was to synthesize lattice strain MoSe2 (LS-MoSe2). Specifically, 0.20 g of the as-prepared MoSe2 powder is stirred at a low temperature for 1 h in a solution (30 mL) containing hydrazine hydrate. Subsequently, the solution was filtered, washed, and dried at 60 °C in a vacuum oven. In particular, the products were prepared by adding 0, 0.5, 1, and 1.5 mL of hydrazine hydrate, which were denoted as LS-MoSe2, LS-MoSe2-I, LS-MoSe2-II, and LS-MoSe2-III, respectively.

Experimental procedures and analyses

The piezocatalytic reaction was carried out in a gas-closed system with ultrasound generated by an ultrasonic cleaner (120 W, 40 kHz). Typically, 30 mg LS-MoSe2 was dispersed in a 50 mL aqueous solution containing 10 mg·L−1 organic pollutants. After transfer to a 250 mL volume class reactor, the airtight reactor was degassed completely and filled with argon to remove any residual air in the solution. Following this procedure, the reactor was pressurized with argon to 1 bar. A certain amount of PMS was injected into the reactor before US irradiation during piezocatalytic CO production and synchronous pollutant degradation. The piezocatalytic reaction proceeded under ultrasonic irradiation for 4 h. The gaseous mixture was collected by a gas chromatograph (GC-2014, Shimadzu, Japan) every 30 min, with the reaction system maintained at 25 °C controlled by a cryogenic circulating pump. During the process, CH4, CO, C2H4, and C2H6 were detected through a hydrogen flame ionization detector (FID), and a methane reformer is installed in front of the FID. H2 can be collected by a thermal conductivity detector (TCD). The liquid products were ascertained by a 1H nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy. The concentration of contaminants following the reaction was subsequently measured using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC, 1290, Agilent Inc.) or a UV–Vis spectrophotometer (Shimadzu UV-2450). All performance evaluation experiments in this study have been carried out in triplicate, and the averaged values with standard deviations as error bars are reported.

High-performance liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (HPLC-MS) experimental procedure

To identify the intermediate products, the oxidation intermediates in the system were analyzed using a high-performance liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (HPLC-MS) instrument (LTQ Orbitrap Discovery, Thermo Fisher Scientific) equipped with an electrospray ionization (ESI) interface. Phenol and its degradation intermediates were separated on a reversed-phase column (Phenomenex C18, 5 μm, 4.6 × 250 mm). The mobile phases consisted of ultrapure water (A) and methanol (B) at a constant flow rate of 200 μL·min-1. The linear gradient elution program was set as follows: 0–1 min, 95% A and 5% B; 1–8 min, 95–5% A and 5–95% B; 8–10 min, 5% A and 95% B; 10–11 min, 5–95% A and 95–5% B; and 11–15 min, 95% A and 5% B. The column temperature was maintained at 30 °C, and the injection volume was 5 μL. The acquired data were further processed using MS software to analyze the mass-to-charge ratios (m/z) and deduce the molecular structures of potential intermediates.

Toxicity assessment by culture tests in zebrafish embryos and Escherichia coli (E. coli)

After cleaning the fish embryos and microscopically selecting uniformly staged specimens, the experimental group was exposed to solutions derived from raw phenol and post-reaction from MoSe2/PMS/US/phenol, and LS-MoSe2-II/PMS/US/phenol systems. For the MoSe2 and LS-MoSe2-II groups, the exposure solution consisted of water that had soaked the respective materials for five days. The control group was exposed directly to a nutrient solution. Embryos (five per experimental group) were individually cultured in 24-well plates, with each well containing 1 mL of nutrient solution and 1 mL of the corresponding exposure solution. Development was microscopically monitored every 24 h until hatching, allowing for a systematic assessment of teratogenic effects on both zebrafish embryos and post-hatch larvae. The toxicity evaluation experiment of Escherichia coli (E. coli) is similar to the above steps. The experiments were performed following the Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals in China. This study was approved by the Nanjing Normal University Animal Ethics Committee (approval no.: SYXK [Jiangsu] 2015-0028).

Calculation details

All calculations in our work were based on the periodic slab model and preceded by using the projected augmented wave method (PAW) implemented in the Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP 6.4.3)55–57. The generalized gradient approximation method (GGA) and the Perdew−Burke−Ernzerhof (PBE) functional58,59 were employed to describe the nonlocal exchange−correlation energies with a kinetic energy cutoff of 400 eV. The vacuum layer between two adjacent slabs along the z-axis was set as 15 Å. Dispersion correction was considered by using the DFT-D3 method60,61. The Brillouin zones were sampled by the Monkhorst–Pack 2 × 2 × 2 k-point grids for optimization and electronic structure calculations. For the theoretical model, 1T-MoSe2 was first constructed with a c2/m space group and a a×b×c of 16.28 × 17.64 × 18.40 Å. For LS-MoSe2-II, a tensile strain was applied along x and y axes with a a×b×c of 17.58 × 19.05 × 18.40 Å. The Gibbs free energy is defined as ΔG = ΔE + ΔZPE – TΔS62, where ΔE is the adsorption or reaction energy based on DFT calculations, ΔZPE is the zero-point energy (ZPE) correction, T is the temperature, and ΔS is the entropy change. In addition, the ZPE and TS values are presented in Supplementary Table 7. The atomic coordinates of different structural models are shown in Supplementary Tables 8–23.

Piezo-response simulation

Finite element analysis was performed using COMSOL Multiphysics® software to simulate the induced piezoelectric potential and polarized electric field distribution on MoSe2 and LS-MoSe2-II, following these steps: (1) Selection of the Model Wizard for constructing the MoSe2 and LS-MoSe2-II model; (2) Choosing the physics option, specifically piezoelectric devices in structural mechanics, and defining the sample structures; (3) Incorporating material parameters and configuring other relevant parameters; (4) Initiating the simulation process. Subsequently, the simulation yielded graphs depicting the displacement field, piezo-potential field and piezo-polarization field.

LCA analyze

In this study, we used Simapro 9.5 and data Ecoinvtent 3.9.1 for modeling, and quantified the model through ReCiPe 2016 Midpoint (H) V1.08/World (2010) H. The life cycle environmental effects of treating 10 mg·L−1 phenol of wastewater synchronous CO production by three processes (LS-MoSe2-II/PMS/US system, MoSe2/PMS/US system, and MoS2/PMS/US system) were obtained. Electrochemical measurements

Electrochemical tests were performed on a three-electrode system using a CH660E electrochemical workstation with a saturated calomel electrode (SCE) as a reference electrode, a platinum wire act as counter electrode and a working electrode comprised a fluorine-doped tin oxide (FTO) surface (with an active area of 1 cm2) coated with the catalysts. The preparation of the working electrode involved dispersing 6 mg of the catalysts in a solution of ethanol (0.48 mL), water (0.48 mL), and Nafion (40 μL). The experimentation encompassed examining the transient piezo-current, conducting linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) measurements, cyclic voltammograms (CV), electrochemical impedance spectra (EIS), and generating Mott-Schottky plots using a 0.5 M Na2SO4 electrolyte solution.

GPA strain mapping

Strain mapping of catalysts was conducted by using the open-source program Strain + + developed by J.J.P. Peters, which is based on the geometric phase analysis (GPA) algorithm as detailed in Martin Hÿtch’s paper. GPA can measure and map the strain fields of an AC-TEM image by comparing the phase of crystalline planes measured from the image with the phase of a set of perfect planes, where the phase of the perfect planes is defined by a g-vector. Specifically, the AC-TEM image is first Fourier transformed, and two non-collinear strong reflection spots are selected for further analysis. Then, performing the inverse Fourier transform of selected spots, the two-dimensional displacement field is deduced from the phase components of the resulting complex images compared to the phase of the perfect lattice. Finally, the strain field is derived by analyzing derivatives of the displacement field.
