From executive control to social emotions: mediation analysis of theory of mind linking executive functions to envy but not Schadenfreude
Authors: Daniel Franco-O´Byrne, Tamara Andrea Cavalieri Ortiz, David Huepe Artigas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundComplex social emotions such as envy and Schadenfreude require the integration of high-level cognitive processes, particularly Executive Functions (EFs) and Theory of Mind (ToM). However, little is known about how these factors differentially influence the intensity of these emotions.MethodsOne hundred and three individuals were assessed using the Ineco Frontal Screening (IFS), the Reading the Mind in the Eyes Test (RMET), and the Social Emotion Task for envy and Schadenfreude. Correlation and mediation analyses were employed to examine the relationships among EFs, ToM, and the intensity of these emotions.ResultsEFs were significantly associated with both envy and Schadenfreude, as well as with ToM. In contrast, ToM was associated only with envy. Mediation analyses indicated that ToM mediated the relationship between EFs and envy, but not Schadenfreude.ConclusionsThese findings suggest that envy and Schadenfreude might be supported by partially distinct cognitive mechanisms: envy seems to rely on cognitive and sociocognitive processes, whereas Schadenfreude is primarily linked to evaluative and reward-related processes. This highlights the presence of differential cognitive profiles underlying complex social emotions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-026-04315-w.Keywords: Executive Functions, Theory of Mind, Schadenfreude, envy

Introduction

Social emotions enable individuals to regulate interpersonal relationships and maintain the cohesion of social systems [1–3]. Among these, envy—displeasure at the good fortune of others—and Schadenfreude—pleasure at the misfortune of others—stand out as complex social emotions. Their complexity stems from the integration of basic executive processes and higher-order sociocognitive abilities, particularly Theory of Mind (ToM), defined as the capacity to infer the mental states of others [4–7]. Envy and Schadenfreude are often considered closely related, with some theories even conceptualizing Schadenfreude as a manifestation of envy [8, 9]. While these emotions indeed share certain features, evidence suggests that their overlap is partial rather than absolute [10–14], with each exhibiting distinguishable cognitive and neurophysiological profiles. Despite these distinctions, relatively few studies have explored the differential mechanisms that may underlie envy and Schadenfreude.

Building on their complexity, both envy and Schadenfreude appear to share a critical cognitive substrate in EFs. This convergence may emerge from the canonical regulatory role of EFs, which includes modulation of affective responses, cognitive control, and appraisal processes [15–18]. Previous studies report correlations between both emotions and EFs, suggesting that individual differences in executive functioning may underlie variability in the intensity of both emotions [19, 20]. Beyond regulation, EFs also support effortful cognitive operations necessary for moral decision making [21–23], a key facet of envy and Schadenfreude [24–26]. In this sense, executive processes can contribute to the evaluation of an individuals’ deservingness or responsibility of an outcome (i.e. deserving to go to jail after a crime/not deserving recognition after plagiarism). However, the implementation of EFs can vary across complex social emotions. For example, in the case of envy, moral and deservingness judgements seems to be biased towards “tall poppies”, or high achieving individuals [27–30]. For Schadenfreude, decision making appears to be mainly driven by targets’ responsibility over outcomes and objective fairness [13, 31]. Altogether, the evidence suggests that EFs can differentially modulate both emotions through the articulation of social comparison processes.

Beyond their direct regulatory and evaluative roles, EFs may facilitate envy and Schadenfreude by serving as a cognitive scaffold for higher-order sociocognitive processes [32–34], such as Theory of Mind (ToM). EFs—including working memory, inhibitory control, and cognitive flexibility—support the manipulation and maintenance of mental representations, enabling the tracking of others’ mental states [32, 35]. This, in turn, allows individuals to map others’ achievements or misfortunes and to engage in social comparison [36], key aspects of complex social emotions [3, 10, 12, 37]. Longitudinal studies indicate that early executive skills predict subsequent ToM development, whereas early ToM does not reliably predict improvements in EFs [38–41]. Furthermore, executive deficits are associated with lower ToM performance in vulnerable populations, including individuals with mild cognitive impairment [42] and autism [43]. These findings highlight the foundational role of EFs not only in general emotion regulation but also in supporting effective ToM processes, which may shape complex social emotions.

ToM may influence envy and Schadenfreude in distinct ways depending on the nature of the emotion. For envy, which is closely tied to social comparison [12, 37, 44], ToM could modulate the interpretation of others’ advantages or achievements relative to oneself, amplifying the intensity of the emotional experience [3, 45, 46]. In contrast, Schadenfreude may not rely on ToM to the same extent as envy, since it tends to be more self-centered and closely associated to rapid evaluative or reward-based processes [9, 13, 14, 47] which can occur independently of explicit perspective-taking. In other words, while sociocognitive processes may still be components of Schadenfreude, evidence indicates that there are other facets to this emotion. This framework allows for the possibility that ToM contributes differently across emotions, preparing the conceptual ground for exploring emotion-specific mediating pathways. However, to our knowledge such framework has not been empirically tested.

The present study therefore aimed to examine the relationships between EFs, ToM, and the intensity of envy and Schadenfreude. Specifically, we evaluated the potential mediating role of ToM as a mechanism articulating the contribution of EFs to the emotional experience, considering both its direct influence on mental state inference and its interaction with executive processes. We hypothesize that: [1] EFs will predict the intensity of both envy and Schadenfreude, and [2] ToM will mediate this relationship, potentially in a differential manner across emotions, with a stronger mediating role for envy than for Schadenfreude. This approach allows us to test the theoretical framework that links executive and sociocognitive processes while acknowledging the partial dissociation between these two complex social emotions.

Method and Materials

Participants

A total of 103 participants (52% men), were recruited, aged between 30 and 50 years (SD = 2.0), with an average level of education of 15 years. The participants came from diverse socioeconomic backgrounds and were representative of the general Chilean population. Exclusion criteria included visual and/or hearing impairments, as a well as psychiatric or neurological history.

All participants provided written informed consent before beginning their participation, and the study was approved by the Adolfo Ibañez university ethics committee, complying with the guidelines of the declaration of Helsinki for research involving human subjects.

Procedure

Participants were evaluated in a controlled environment, where they completed the self-reported instruments included in this study. Before the experimental session, they were asked to register via an online link or by telephone, ensuring that the inclusion and exclusion criteria were met. During the evaluation, participants sat in front of a trained psychologist, who was responsible for supervising the correct application of the questionnaires and paradigms, as well as resolving any questions and ensuring compliance with the protocol.

Instruments

Robust, previously validated self-report instruments were used to assess the variables of interest in this project.

To assess theory of Mind (ToM), the Reading the Mind in the Eyes Test (RMET) [48] was used, a psychological test designed to measure the ability to infer mental and emotional states from eye expressions. The RMET consists of 36 photographs of the eye region of different individuals, and participants must select, from four options, the emotion or mental state that best describes what the person in the image might be feeling or thinking [49]. This instrument is widely used for the individual measurement of ToM [50]. Due to the dichotomous nature of the responses, reliability analysis is typically performed using a test-retest approach [51], which showed a good index in the present sample, with a Spearman-Brown reliability coefficient of 0.8.

The executive function (EF) variable was assessed using the Ineco Frontal Screening (IFS), a screening tool designed to comprehensively measure executive functioning, including response inhibition, abstraction ability, and working memory, providing an integral measure of executive abilities [52, 53]. The IFS has previously been administered in both clinical and non-clinical populations [54, 55]. In the present sample, the instrument showed an adequate reliability index, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0,70.

To assess the intensity of envy and Schadenfreude, we used a social emotions task previously reported in both in clinical [6, 47, 56] and healthy [7, 57, 58] populations. Following previous procedures [7, 57, 58], the task consisted of two blocks, one assessing envy and the other Schadenfreude. Each block included 15 situations designed to elicit the target emotion, as well as 5 neutral situations embedded within the same block. In the envy block, participants read sentences describing fortunate events involving a target character (e.g., She went on vacation with the money she saved by evading taxes) and rated the degree of displeasure elicited by each scenario. In the Schadenfreude block, participants read sentences describing unfortunate events involving the same character (e.g., While mocking a woman for being overweight, she spilled wine on her dress) and rated the degree of pleasure elicited. Neutral situations within each block described events not intended to evoke the target emotion; however, they were evaluated using the same affective dimension as the corresponding block. Accordingly, neutral trials functioned as within-block contextual controls rather than as affectively independent baseline conditions. Following previous procedures, participants were asked to rate, on a Likert scale from 1 to 9, the intensity of displeasure (in the case of envy) or pleasure (in the case of Schadenfreude) they experienced in each situation (for the complete social emotion task stimuli refer to supplementary instrument section). In the present sample, the task showed high reliability indices, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.931 for envy and 0,947.

It is important to note that participants were asked to report their Schadenfreude and envy in terms of pleasure/displeasure, as these dimensions capture the core affective states associated with each emotion [6]. Envy has been defined as an unpleasant and often painful emotion arising from awareness of another’s desirable attributes, typically involving feelings of inferiority, hostility, and resentment [4, 59–61]. In contrast, Schadenfreude refers to the pleasure or self-satisfaction experienced in response to others’ misfortune or failure [4, 31, 59].

Because explicit expressions of envy and Schadenfreude are often socially discouraged, directly asking about these emotions may be particularly susceptible to social desirability biases [6, 7, 62, 63]. Assessing pleasure and displeasure circumvents this limitation and aligns with methodological recommendations for studying social and affective cognitive processes [64]. Accordingly, asking about levels of pleasure/displeasure is the standard procedure in prior studies of envy and Schadenfreude [6, 9, 56, 65]. Finally, the terms “pleasure” and “displeasure” are easier to understand and thus more reliable than “envy” and “Schadenfreude” as verbally cued measures.

Measures of envy and Schadenfreude intensity were computed at the block level by subtracting the mean rating of neutral situations from the mean rating of emotion-eliciting situations within each respective block. This procedure yielded an index of relative emotion intensity, adjusted for nonspecific affective and response-related variance within the same contextual frame. Accordingly, the resulting scores reflect context-adjusted intensity of the target emotion for each participant.

Data Analysis

The relationships between variables were evaluated using Pearson’s bivariate correlations. Subsequently, to examine whether the relationship between executive functions (EF) and the intensity of envy was mediated by theory of mind (ToM), a mediation analysis was performed using a linear regression approach [66, 67]. This technique allows the role of a variable as an explanatory factor in the relationship between two other variables evaluated, as well as quantifying the proportion of the effect that is explained by the mediating variable. The mediation analyses were performed using the mediation analysis function of the Pingouin library in Python [68], using 10,000 bootstrap iterations to estimate the confidence intervals of the indirect effect. Direct, indirect and total coefficients were reported, along with their standard errors, p-values, and 95% confidence limits.

The rest of the data management and correlation analyses were performed using the pandas [69], numpy and scipy.stats [70] libraries.

Mediation analysis

Results indicated that the indirect effect of executive functions (EFs; IFS) on envy intensity, mediated by Theory of Mind (ToM), was statistically significant, β = 0.08, SE = 0.042, p = .043, 95% CI [0.006, 0.172] (see Figure 1A, supplementary table 2). The direct effect of EFs on envy, controlling for ToM, did not reach significance, β = 0.14, SE = 0.104, p = .179, 95% CI [−0.066, 0.347], indicating significant mediation of the EFs effect on envy by ToM. The total effect of EFs on envy was significant, β = 0.22, SE = 0.10, p = .024, 95% CI [0.03, 0.41].

Executive functions were positively associated with ToM, β = 0.39, SE = 0.09, p < .001, 95% CI [0.21, 0.57], and ToM was positively associated with envy intensity, β = 0.26, SE = 0.10, p = .009, 95% CI [0.07, 0.45]. The proportion mediated was 36.6%, supporting partial mediation and suggesting that additional mechanisms may also contribute to the observed effect.

Consistent with the correlation patterns reported above, the mediation analysis with Schadenfreude intensity as the outcome was not significant (See figure 1B, supplementary table 3). Although EFs were positively associated with ToM, β = 0.392, SE = 0.092, p < .001, 95% CI [0.210, 0.573], ToM was not related to Schadenfreude intensity, β = −0.028, SE = 0.099, p = .777, 95% CI [−0.226, 0.169]. Consequently, the indirect effect of EFs via ToM was non-significant, β = −0.052, SE = 0.041, p = .186, 95% CI [−0.143, 0.021] (see Figure 1B, Supplementary Table 3). The direct and total effects of EFs on Schadenfreude were significant, β = 0.270, SE = 0.105, p = .012, 95% CI [0.061, 0.479], and β = 0.217, SE = 0.097, p = .027, 95% CI [0.026, 0.410], respectively.

Discussion

This study evaluated the relationship between Theory of mind (ToM), executive functions (EFs) and the intensity of complex social emotions such as envy and Schadenfreude. The results show that EFs correlate significantly with both ToM and the intensity of envy and Schadenfreude. ToM showed significant associations with the intensity of envy, but not with Schadenfreude. Accordingly, mediation analyses indicated that ToM only significantly mediated the relationship between EFs and envy intensity, whereas no significant mediation was found when Schadenfreude was the outcome. These findings highlight a potential pathway through which the intensity of envy may relate to the interplay of executive and sociocognitive components. At the same time, the observed dissociation between envy and Schadenfreude suggests that complex social emotions are not uniformly driven by mentalization mechanisms, highlighting the need to consider partially distinct pathways underlying their experiential intensity.

The results of this study suggest that EFs are positively and significantly related to the intensity of both envy and Schadenfreude. This finding is consistent with previous evidence linking inhibitory control, cognitive flexibility, and emotional regulation to emotional experiences of a social nature (33,35–37). In the case of envy, executive deficits can hinder the regulation of emotional responses to social comparisons in the face of other’s fortunes (4,7,20), thus flattening their intensity. By contrast, in Schadenfreude, EFs may be more closely involved in decision making or evaluative processes in the context of the perceived targets’ responsibility/deservingness of their misfortunes (13,31), contributing to individual differences the emotion’s intensity. Thus, more robust executive functioning could modulate the way envy and Schadenfreude are processed, potentially supporting more adaptive regulation. However, these executive processes do not act in isolation; rather, they interact with high-order sociocognitive processes, such as ToM (35,36,39–42), which may differentially shape how executive control is engaged across these complex social emotions.

Building on this interaction between executive control and sociocognitive processes, as reported, the intensity of envy but not Schadenfreude appears to predominantly relate to ToM. ToM has been described as a key requirement for understanding social emotions, as it enables individuals to represent and infer others’ mental states, intentions, and desires (10,72). This capacity supports social comparison —a defining feature of envy— by allowing individuals to interpret others’ achievements or advantages in relation to the self (38,45,73,74), thereby amplifying the intensity of the emotion. In contrast, the observed lack of a significant association between ToM and Schadenfreude in the present study suggests that this emotion may place lower demands on mental state inference, relying instead on more immediate evaluative, and reward driven responses to others’ outcomes (14,48,75,76). This differential involvement of ToM across emotions is particularly relevant given that mentalization is not an isolated capacity, but strongly interlinked with general executive resources (34,39,43,77).

Consistent with this framework, our results indicate that executive functions relate to the intensity of complex social emotions through partially overlapping but dissociable pathways. Specifically, EFs appear to be linked to envy in the context of mentalization processes which are thought to support the interpretation of social outcomes in terms of others’ intentions, desires, and self-relevance. This is consistent with previous evidence suggesting that in envy, perceived deservingness of a target’s outcome is biased for high-achieving targets (28–30), highlighting the role of the social comparison component (27,38,60). In this sense, ToM may operate in a dual manner: first, as a socio-cognitive capacity, it enables individuals to infer and represent others’ mental and emotional states, making social outcomes psychologically salient and potentially amplifying the intensity of envy (3,46,47); second, it interacts with executive processes—such as maintaining goal-relevant information in working memory, inhibiting one’s own perspective, and flexibly integrating contextual cues—allowing complex social comparisons to be structured and regulated adaptively (34,46,47).

In contrast, the non-significant mediation of ToM in the association between EFs and Schadenfreude suggests that executive control may relate to this emotion primarily through evaluative mechanisms regarding targets responsibility over experienced outcomes with less involvement of explicit mental state inference. This is consistent with previous studies showing that Schadenfreude is modulated by varying degrees of targets deservingness of an outcome in the absence of social comparison (13,31). Taken together, these findings suggest that although executive functions provide a shared regulatory substrate for complex social emotions, the extent to which their influence is channeled through ToM depends on the specific cognitive and social demands of the emotion, highlighting partially distinct pathways underlying envy and Schadenfreude. In this context, envy might be associated with cognitive-sociocognitive processes while Schadenfreude could mainly reflect evaluative-reward processes.

When interpreting these results, it is important to consider certain methodological limitations. First, the cross-sectional design restricts causal inference, particularly with respect to the mediation analyses, and precludes assessment of the temporal stability or directional ordering of the observed associations, although prior work suggests some consistency in these relationships (6,25,31,48,66,77). In addition, while our sample size (N = 103) is comparable to previous mediation studies (78,79), replication in larger and more diverse samples—including clinical and cross-cultural contexts—will be necessary to further assess the robustness and generalizability of the findings.

A second set of limitations relates to the experimental task used to elicit social emotions. The block-wise presentation of emotion-specific scenarios may have introduced contextual or expectancy effects influencing emotion ratings. Although this structure was identical across participants and conditions and is therefore unlikely to systematically bias the observed associations, future studies employing alternative designs or trial-wise emotion sampling could help disentangle these potential influences. Moreover, the observed asymmetry between envy and Schadenfreude —particularly the absence of a mediating role of ToM for Schadenfreude—suggests that the present model may not capture all processes relevant to this emotion. Finally, although emotional intensity was operationalized through pleasure and displeasure ratings to capture the core affective experience elicited by envy and Schadenfreude, this approach does not exhaust all appraisal-based and relational components of these complex social emotions. Future work incorporating additional affective, motivational, or normative measures may help further clarify the mechanisms underlying Schadenfreude intensity.

Conclusion

This study provides evidence that EFs relate to the intensity of envy and Schadenfreude, through partially overlapping but dissociable pathways. ToM mediates the relationship between EFs and envy, whereas Schadenfreude appears to rely more directly on evaluative and reward processes. These findings highlight that complex social emotions may exhibit differential cognitive profiles, reflecting the distinct ways in which executive and sociocognitive processes are engaged across these emotions.
