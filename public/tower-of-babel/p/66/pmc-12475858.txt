Epigenetic memory: The role of the crosstalk between histone modifications and DNA methylation
Authors: Domitilla Del Vecchio
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractEpigenetic memory allows different cells to maintain distinct gene expression patterns despite a common genetic code and plays a role in several biological processes. Chemical modifications to DNA and histones have appeared as critical mediators of epigenetic memory and much attention has gone into characterizing their dynamics. The network of positive feedback loops that these modifications form generates a rich set of dynamics that both recapitulate the traditional binary memory paradigm and also predict a new form of memory that we call analog memory. In this paper, we review models of chromatin modifications and describe how binary or analog memory hinge on the presence or lack of positive feedback loops between repressive histone modifications and DNA methylation. Future research using advanced genetic engineering tools will be able to validate the molecular interactions that dictate different forms of memory, and will thus deepen our understanding of how epigenetic memories form in different biological contexts.

Introduction

Epigenetic memory allows to maintain different gene expression states despite a common genetic sequence and is at the basis of many biological processes, including the maintenance of cell identity [1], [2], the immune system response [3], [4], [5], and brain function [6], [7]. It could also serve as a tool for cell engineering [8]. As an example, cellular identity is associated with specific gene expression patterns wherein one cell type keeps fate specific genes “on” and those of alternative fates “off”. Distinct gene expression patterns are maintained in the different cell types of our body and mistakes compromising maintenance often lead to illness such as cancer.

Due to its importance, the molecular mechanisms enabling epigenetic memory have been extensively researched [9]. It was proposed that gene regulatory networks (GRN) with multiple stable steady states, i.e., multi-stable GRNs, would enable memory of gene expression patters corresponding to stable equilibria [10], [11]. The PU.1/GATA-1 GRN is a well studied example, which is constituted of two genes (the PU.1 gene and the GATA-1 gene) that repress each other and also self-activate [12]. This network controls the decision between the erythroid and myelomonocytic cell fates, each represented by one of two mutually exclusive patterns of PU.1 and GATA-1 expression. Each of these stable equilibria can be regarded as a valley in a potential landscape. Nevertheless, it is a question how can the state remain in any of these valleys under the continuous influence of noise. This question is also relevant when considering that repression and activation of a gene requires transcription factors (TFs) to be bound to the gene. Upon DNA replication and cell division, some TFs stay bound to the DNA [13] while others may unbind to let the replication machinery go through [14]. Chromatin modifications, in the form of histone modifications and DNA methylation, have thus been proposed as a molecular mechanism that could convey additional robustness to the stable gene expression states established by the GRN [15].

In fact, chromatin modifications have mechanisms that enable inheritance [15], [16], [17], [18]. DNA methylation and histone modifications have the ability to affect gene expression and to propagate through DNA replication. Specifically, these chemical modifications affect the extent by which chromatin is compacted and hence accessible to transcriptional machinery [19], [20]. Highly compacted chromatin, or heterochromatin, silences gene expression while a loose chromatin structure, or euchromatin, allows transcription. Both DNA methylation and histone modifications have processes that copy the modification on the new DNA strand and histones, thereby allowing the modification to persist despite DNA replication [21], [22]. Because of these properties, chromatin modifications have been the subject of intense investigation and some mathematical models aiming at capturing their dynamics have appeared in the literature (see [23], [24], and [25] and the references therein).

In this paper, we describe how epigenetic memory can emerge from models of chromatin modifications, focusing on how binary and analog memory result from the architecture of the chromatin modification circuit. We focus on models that assume species are well-mixed, to keep the treatment simple, and thus disregard the spatial dynamics that pertains to histone modifications, as well as the spatial chromosomal structures that they mediate, for which we refer the reader to other works [24], [26], [27], [28], [29].

The histone modification circuit supports binary memory

A well studied chromatin modification model is one that includes histone modifications only, specifically Histone H3 lysine-9 tri-methylation (H3K9me3) and Histone H3 lysine-4 tri-methylation (H3K4me3). The former leads to chromatin compaction and gene repression while the second one promotes an open chromatin state and hence gene expression [22]. These histone modifications can be modeled as enzymatic reactions, wherein a writer enzyme establishes the modification in the first place and an eraser enzyme removes the modification. Modifications are also passively removed via dilution. A distinctive feature of these modifications is that modified histones bind “reader” proteins that recruit writers of the same modification [22]. This can be captured by an autocatalytic enzymatic reaction, in which a modification “catalyzes” more of itself [23], [25]. Additionally, activating histone modification H3K4me3 binds to proteins that recruit erasers of the repressive H3K9me3 modification and viceversa [22]. This creates the mutual inhibition circuit with autocatalysis shown in Fig. 1(A). Gene expression results from H3K4me3 but not from H3K9me3 [22].

We will say that the system has memory if it remembers what occurred in the past. More precisely, if the system's trajectories approach a different attractor depending of where the state started in the (x,y) plane, then we will say that the system has memory. Therefore, if the system admits a unique stable equilibrium such that all initial states converge to it, then by our definition the system has no memory. By contrast, if the system admits more than one stable steady state, then different initial conditions will result in different asymptotic values of the state, implying that the system remembers where it started from. It is possible to algebraically compute the steady states of the system, which shows bistability or monostability, depending on the value of ε [25]. Specifically, bistability is achieved when ε is sufficiently small such that the system admits two stable steady states, one in correspondence of x≈1 and the other with y≈1, that is, the gene is fully modified either with H3K4me3 or with H3K9me3 (Fig. 1(B)). When ε is large, the system is monostable and hence is memory-less (Fig. 1(C)).

Qualitatively, the role of ε as determinant of memory can be explained as follows. When this parameter is large, both modifications are diluted much faster than they can be re-established by the autocatalysis. As a consequence, they will reach an intermediate value where both modifications co-exist at a level dependent on the inputs uA and uR. By contrast, when ε is small, the autocatalysis is much faster than the dilution rate. Therefore, an initial state biased toward more x (more y) will quickly build up through the autocatalysis while also enabling the conversion of y (x) back to the unmodified state, from which more of x (y) can be formed. Therefore, bistability emerges. It is also possible to show that both autocatalysis and mutual antagonism are necessary to achieve bistability.

Once we have bistability, then the question becomes: Under the influence of noise, what is the probability that the system will leave the stable state, thereby loosing memory? This is critical since all biological reactions are subject to noise, which could in principle push the state out of its stable configuration. To address this question, it is necessary to consider a stochastic model of the circuit. In such a model, every reaction has a probability of firing, which increases with the value of the reaction rate [33]. Therefore, once the system's state is in one of the stable configurations, one can investigate the probability of leaving those configurations. Mathematical analysis of these probabilities, reveals that the smaller the value of ε, the smaller is the probability of leaving these stable states [25]. Accordingly, stochastic simulations of the chemical reaction system show that the probability of finding the state at either of the two (deterministically) stable configurations increases as ε decreases (Fig. 1D). This fact is reflected by peaks of the probability distribution that become narrower about the two stable equilibria as ε decreases. Consistent with this, the probability of finding the state at any intermediate level between the two peaks becomes practically zero as ε approaches zero. As a result, gene expression is either locked in a silenced state (by H3K9me3) or in a maximally active state (by H3K4me3). In this case, we will say that the system has binary memory since in the long run any initial state will permanently achieve one of these two configurations in which the gene is either “on” or “off”.

Therefore, the histone modification circuit supports binary memory by virtue of a fast autocatalysis re-establishing H3K9me3 and H3K4me3 faster than they decay. This circuit architecture thus enables stable maintenance of two states only, active and silenced, recapitulating the binary memory paradigm that has been observed in experimental systems [34], [35].

The crosstalk between histone modifications and DNA methylation enables long-term memory states

Several studies have reported that H3K9me3 decays over time instead of persisting, suggesting that the decay rate of H3K9me3 is not overpowered by its autocatalysis [36], [37]. In this case, binary memory cannot be achieved by the histone modification circuit alone. Consistent with this, studies have shown that heterochromatin, a highly stable state where gene expression is shut down, carries both H3K9me3 and DNA methylation [38]. Thus, we investigate how H3K9me3, and more generally histone modifications, interact with DNA methylation and how this interaction can shape memory. Indeed, although histone modifications and DNA methylation have been traditionally studied as separate processes, they do not operate in isolation, but rather interact with each other [39], [40], [41], [42]. In order to understand the consequences of these interactions, we first review briefly a simple lumped model of DNA methylation, and then compose it with the histone modification circuit of Fig. 1.

In our lumped model, we represent DNA methylation as an enzymatic reaction. Specifically, methylation is established by writer enzymes DNA Methyl Transferases (DNMTs), decays passively through cell division and DNA replication, and is copied back onto the nascent DNA strand upon replication by a different class of DNMT maintenance enzymes [21]. This copy process is very fast compared to the speed of DNA replication and cell division, such that the effective dilution rate constant changes from γ to γ′=γ⋅(1−η), in which η is the efficiency of the DNA methylation maintenance mechanism and can often be well approximated by ≈1 [21]. Therefore, we can regard the effective dilution rate as being almost absent, consistent with what has been observed on the timescales of several months [37]. For a more detailed model that precisely recapitulates the inheritance dynamics of DNA methylation, accounting for maintenance errors at the cell population level, we refer the reader to [43]. Nevertheless, DNA methylation can be reversed to non-methylated forms by enzymes of the TET family, which modify methylated cytosines into forms not recognized by maintenance methylation enzymes [42]. This effectively reduces the efficiency η, which can be pushed to almost 0 by large levels of TET enzymes, so that γ′≈γ. This relatively simple “birth-death” model should be combined with the histone modification circuit of Fig. 1A according to the documented interactions between histone modifications and DNA methylation. Specifically, it is known that DNA methylation recruits proteins that “read” (bind) the methylated sites and that these, in turn, recruit writers of H3K9me3 [39]. Similarly, it is possible for H3K9me3 to recruit proteins that “read” (bind) the methylated domains and these, in turn, recruit DNMTs for de novo DNA methylation [40], [45]. This creates a positive feedback loop between DNA methylation and H3K9me3 (Fig. 2A), which allows H3K9me3 to be stably maintained even when its autocatalysis is not sufficiently fast, if DNA methylation is maintained. Finally, DNA methylation and H3K4me3 are antagonist to each other, wherein DNA methylation recruits readers that bind to erasers of H3K4me3 and, in turn, the latter recruits readers that bind TET1 enzymes [41], [42]. As a consequence, these two marks each enhance the removal of the other one (Fig. 2B). By combining these interactions with the histone modification circuit of Fig. 1A, we arrive at the chromatin modification circuit depicted in Fig. 2C. When the effective decay rate of DNA methylation is small, the system is bistable for a large range of activator inputs uA. On the other hand, when the decay rate of DNA methylation increases, the system with an activator will always be in the active state (Fig. 2D). This suggests that the chromatin modification circuit leads to a constitutively expressed gene that is either on or off depending on whether the chromatin state is driven to the H3K4me3 state or to the silenced state equipped with H3K9me3 and DNA methylation. Therefore, a constitutive promoter to which we transiently recruit de novo writers for either H3K9me3 or DNA methylation, will endup in either of two configurations: active or silenced, which is again consistent with binary memory. This is shown in the top panel of Fig. 2E, which recapitulates experimental observations [34].

The next question that we address is whether “on” and “off” are the only gene expression states that the chromatin modification circuit can maintain.

Breaking the positive feedback between H3K9me3 and DNA methylation unshields analog memory

Although the chromatin modification circuit of Fig. 2C includes interactions that have been documented in the literature, these may not all be simultaneously present. In fact, their strength (and presence) depends likely on the cellular and genetic context since chromatin modifications vary greatly depending on the cell type, promoter, and genomic location [44], [46]. In particular, while it is known that H3K9me3 recruits writers of DNA methylation, this has been shown not to be the case in some cell types [37], [34]. We therefore investigate the impact on epigenetic memory of removing the recruitment of DNA methylation by H3K9me3, which is encoded by the arrow with strength α′ in Fig. 2C.

In the limit in which such a recruitment is missing, and there is no TET enzyme that removes DNA methylation (so γ′=0), the yellow and light blue arrows in the chromatin modification circuit disappear (Fig. 3AB). Therefore, the total DNA methylation level will be conserved because there is no reaction that changes it. This also implies that any initially set DNA methylation level is conserved in time, leading to an analog form of information storage, in which the DNA methylation grade can be dialed to a specific level by de novo DNMT writer enzymes and then maintained after the enzymes have been removed. In particular, in the absence of TET enzymes, that is, no light blue arrows in the diagram of Fig. 3A, one can study the behavior of the chromatin state and gene expression as α′ is decreased, making the positive feedback loop between DNA methylation and H3K9me3 weaker. A population of cells whose DNA methylation grade was initialized at an intermediate level, leading to intermediate gene expression, approaches over time a state of no methylation (active gene) or full methylation (silenced gene) when α′ is non zero (Fig. 3C, right). The speed at which this convergence occurs increases as α′ is increased [37]. In the opposite limit where α′ is very small, this speed of convergence approaches zero and, as a result, a cell population that was initialized at any intermediate DNA methylation grade will keep this grade and the corresponding gene expression level over time (Fig. 3C, left).

When α′=0, the positive feedback loop between DNA methylation and H3K9me3 is broken and the chromatin modification circuit reduces to the histone modification circuit with the DNA methylation grade as an added “input” (Fig. 3B). Here, for simplicity, we have assumed that all the recruited erasure rate constants are scaled by ε′ to study the role of recruited erasure in the behavior of the system. When z is low, corresponding to a low grade of DNA methylation, the histone modification circuit state is biased towards more H3K4me3 (x) than H3K9me3 (y), resulting for very low z in a stable configuration where the gene is all modified with H3K4me3 and gene expression is high (Fig. 3D, left). On the other hand, when z is high, corresponding to a high grade of DNA methylation, the gene is all modified with H3K9me3, resulting in gene silencing (Fig. 3D, right). An interesting scenario occurs for intermediate grades of DNA methylation. In this case, if ε is small, that is, the autocatalysis of histone modifications is sufficiently faster than basal decay, the histone modification circuit can become “weakly” bistable. Here, weakly refers to the fact that the two stable steady states are not at the extreme configurations characterized by either all H3K9me3 or all H3K4me3, but contain a mix of H3K9me3 and H3K4me3. In this situation, with the effect of noise, it is possible that the chromatin state switches back and forth between these two mixed stable steady states. The result is a distribution of gene expression across a cell population that is very broad since the chromatin state keeps repressing and de-repressing gene expression under the influence of noise (Fig. 3D, middle plots). The resulting cell population will thus not be homogeneous in terms of gene expression [37].

One question that is especially relevant for engineering purposes is how well can one modulate the DNA methylation grade, along with the corresponding gene expression level, and hence the analog memory state of the system. Indeed, a temporal pulse of DNA methylation writer enzyme (DNMT3, Fig. 4A) should in principle result into a DNA methylation grade that persists after the writer enzyme has been removed. Conclusions and discussion

In summary, we have reviewed models describing how chromatin modifications enable epigenetic memory. We have shown that the histone modification circuit supports binary memory of gene expression in conditions where the autocatalysis of histone modifications is sufficiently fast compared to their dilution. In cell types where cell division is less frequent, dilution will be slower and hence it is possible that the histone modification circuit, alone, may enable long-term maintenance of the “on” and “off” gene states. On the other hand, in cells where the division rate is fast, the autocatalysis may be insufficient to overpower dilution and, as a consequence, memory may not be possible with histone modifications alone. We then introduced DNA methylation and explained how it interacts with histone modifications. These interactions give rise to a positive feedback loop between DNA methylation and repressive histone modification H3K9me3. Such a positive feedback, combined with the temporal stability of DNA methylation, confers long-term stability to H3K9me3. As a result, the chromatin modification circuit model that includes both histone modifications and DNA methylation can enable long-term memory of binary states (on and off) even in situations where the decay rate of histone modifications overpowers autocatalysis.

In situations where this positive feedback loop is absent, either because H3K9me3 does not recruit DNA methylation writers or DNA methylation does not recruit H3K9me3 writers, analog memory emerges. With analog memory, any level of DNA methylation in a gene is maintained in time and, with it, the corresponding gene expression level. Analog epigenetic memory challenges the traditional view that chromatin modifications enable maintenance of binary gene states only [34], [35]. Here, we propose that gene expression can be dialed to any level by editing DNA methylation and that it can then be memorized at that level by virtue of the long-term stability of DNA methylation. Although analog epigenetic memory has only recently been experimentally observed [37], we expect that it plays a role in a variety of biological processes. These include, the development and architecture of the hippocampus, where our memories form, in which gradients of cell types and DNA methylation have been observed [47], [48]. We also expect that analog memory may be at play in more plastic cell types, where graded DNA methylation and gene expression has also been reported, including in lymphocytic leukemia cells [49], in embryonic stem cells [50], in human placenta [51], and in human stem/progenitor cells [52].

The crosstalk between histone modifications and DNA methylation is context-dependent and varies with the cellular and genetic context [44]. We therefore expect that the strength of the positive feedback loop between DNA methylation and histone modifications, encoded by α′ in our model, will also vary with the context of which the gene is part. It is therefore plausible that some combinations of genes and cell types will show analog memory while others will not. Additional experimental work will be required to validate the hypothesis that the strength of the positive feedback loop dictates the emergence of analog versus binary memory. This can be accomplished, for example, by performing large screens of combinations of cell types, promoters, and genomic locations for analog memory and for α′. This parameter, in turn, can be inferred by characterizing the chromatin state post recruitment of writers of DNA methylation or H3K9me3 to the genetic locus of interest, as performed in earlier work [37], [34]. In this paper, we have focused on how the crosstalk between DNA methylation and histone modifications can control the transition between analog and binary epigenetic memory. Other literature has analyzed how analog or binary modes of gene regulation can arise, suggesting a transient analog form of regulation before commitment to binary states [53], [54]. Different work further found that silencing establishment proceeds through gradual downregulation of transcription over several cell cycles [55].

For engineering purposes, it would be highly desirable if we could encode the DNA methylation grade in a gene with precision. This could allow to generate permanent gradients of gene expression across cells without requiring any permanent morphogen gradient [56]. When achieved in pluripotent stem cells, this ability could enable directed differentiation to novel tissues and organs, as well as to hippocampus-like structures, where gradients of cell types have been reported. For computing applications, analog information storage in the epigenetic state of a gene would dramatically expand our current biocomputing abilities, enable future biotechnology applications and next generation therapeutics [57], [58], [59]. Additional research will be required to determine ways to encode the DNA methylation grade with precision through user-defined inputs. In fact, experimental evidence suggests that the value of ε′ may be large enough to provide an ultrasensitive response of the steady DNA methylation grade to the amplitude of the DNMT3 pulse (Fig. 4, [37]). In this case, a broad bimodal distribution of gene expression is expected for different values of the pulse amplitude. Research in this direction, will need to find ways to make this response graded so that by controlling the DNMT3 level one can achieve desired DNA methylation grades. It is possible that because the gene expression distribution is bimodal in response to DNMT3 editing even if memory is analog (Fig. 4, large ε′), that analog memory has gone unnoticed so far due to the lower frequency of cells expressing intermediate gene expression levels. If true, this could lead to the discovery of new cell states in both healthy and disease conditions and hence to new areas of research.
