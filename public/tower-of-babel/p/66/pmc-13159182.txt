Clinical significance of mouth breathing as a marker of severity in pediatric obstructive sleep apnea
Authors: Jingyi Chen, Yao Wang, Wenke Du, Mingjiang Xia, Yan Chen, Yinghong Zhang, Yingchao Yang, Kaiming Su
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTo investigate whether clinically assessed mouth-breathing severity is independently associated with polysomnography–defined obstructive sleep apnea severity in children with adenoidal hypertrophy and/or tonsillar hypertrophy, and to evaluate its potential as a non-invasive predictor of moderate-to-severe obstructive sleep apnea.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed pediatric inpatients admitted for planned adenotonsillectomy at Shanghai Sixth People’s Hospital. Based on a mouth-breathing score (range 1–7), patients were categorized into mild (scores 1–3) or severe (scores 4–7) mouth-breathing groups. Obstructive sleep apnea severity was classified as non-to-mild [obstructive apnea–hypopnea index ≤ 5 events/h] or moderate-to-severe (obstructive apnea–hypopnea index > 5 events/h). Demographic, clinical, and polysomnography parameters were compared. Correlation and logistic regression analyses were performed to identify independent predictors of moderate-to-severe condition, and the diagnostic performance of the predictive model was evaluated.ResultsCompared with mild mouth-breathing group, the severe group demonstrated significantly higher apnea–hypopnea index, obstructive apnea–hypopnea index, percentage of total sleep time with oxygen saturation < 90%, and lower mean oxygen saturation (all P < 0.05). Mouth-breathing scores positively correlated with apnea–hypopnea index and obstructive apnea–hypopnea index (rho = 0.2173, 0.2102, P < 0.0001). Multivariate analysis identified severe mouth-breathing scores (OR = 2.357; 95% CI, 1.354–4.101; P = 0.002), female sex (OR = 0.602; 95% CI, 0.377–0.961; P = 0.034), and tonsillar grade > II (OR = 1.820; 95% CI, 1.147–2.886; P = 0.011) as independent predictors of moderate-to-severe diseases. The multivariable model yielded an AUC of 0.658. At the optimal cutoff defined by the Youden index (0.228), it demonstrated a sensitivity of 73.1%, a specificity of 49.6%, a positive predictive value (PPV) of 35.7%, and a negative predictive value (NPV) of 82.8%. In comparison, using a mouth-breathing score ≥ 4 as a standalone diagnostic criterion produced an AUC of 0.636, with corresponding sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV of 82.4%, 35.5%, 32.8%, and 84.0%, respectively.ConclusionsMouth-breathing score was independently associated with the severity of obstructive sleep apnea in children with adenoidal hypertrophy and/or tonsillar hypertrophy. These findings indicate that structured mouth-breathing assessment may serve as a simple, non-invasive adjunct to clinical risk stratification for pediatric obstructive sleep apnea.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-026-08238-1.

Background

Pediatric obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) is a prevalent yet frequently underdiagnosed condition, primarily caused by adenoidal hypertrophy (AH) and/or tonsillar hypertrophy (TH). It is clinically characterized by habitual snoring, mouth breathing (MB), witnessed apneas and restless sleep. Although polysomnography (PSG) remains the diagnostic gold standard, its routine application in children is constrained by the high cost, the technical complexity, the limited accessibility and the poor tolerance among young patients. These limitations have driven growing interest in identifying accessible, reliable and symptom-based markers to facilitate early detection and risk stratification of pediatric OSA.

Several symptom-based instruments, including the Pediatric Sleep Questionnaire–Sleep-Related Breathing Disorder scale (PSQ-SRBD), the OSA-18 quality-of-life questionnaire, and the Sleep Clinical Record (SCR), have been introduced to developed to assess clinical severity in pediatric OSA [1, 2]. For instance, studies have shown that PSQ and OSA-18 demonstrate high sensitivity but limited specificity in children with comorbidities like allergic rhinitis or asthma, potentially leading to overdiagnosis [3, 4]. Tools such as the Obstructive Sleep Apnea Five-Item Questionnaire (OSA-5) and the Childhood Adenotonsillar Sleep Evaluation–OSA Score (CHASE-OSA) offer improved simplicity and moderate predictive capacity, but their application remains largely confined to selected populations, including obese children or those scheduled for adenotonsillectomy (AT) [5, 6].

MB, a common manifestation of pediatric OSA, plays a paradoxical role in clinical practice. While it can serve as a compensatory mechanism to bypass upper airway obstruction, particularly in cases of AH, it may reflect more chronic or severe upper airway compromise [7]. Although included in tools such as the PSQ and OSA-18, MB is generally recorded as a binary symptom, with little attention to its severity. Existing evidence is conflicting: some studies suggest it may alleviate hypoventilation during obstructive events, whereas others associate it with worsened nocturnal hypoxemia and hypercapnia [8]. For examples, a secondary analysis of the Childhood Adenotonsillectomy Trial (CHAT) dataset found no significant correlation between mouth-breathing scores (MBS) and AHI, although that cohort comprised children aged 5–9 years with relatively mild disease [9, 10]. Despite its frequency and inclusion in screening tools, the severity of MB has seldom been quantitatively assessed or examined for its independent association with OSA severity. Clarifying whether MB reflects a compensatory symptom or an indicator of more severe disease could improve the clinical risk stratification, especially in settings with limited PSG availability.

To address this gap, we conducted a study in children aged 2–12 years undergoing preoperative evaluation for adenotonsillar hypertrophy (ATH). Our primary objective was to determine whether MBS was associated with PSG-defined OSA severity and to assess its potential role as a practical clinical indicator of moderate-to-severe disease. This investigation may enhance symptom-based risk stratification, particularly in resource-limited settings where PSG access is restricted.

Methods

Ethics

This was a single-centre, hospital-based cross-sectional study conducted at Shanghai Sixth People’s Hospital (Shanghai, China) in accordance with the 1964 Helsinki declaration and its subsequent amendments or comparable ethical standards. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of the Shanghai Sixth People’s Hospital, with the application number 2024 − 198. Informed written consent was obtained from the parents and/or legal guardians of all participating children before data collection.

Study design and participants

Pediatric patients aged 2–12 years who were hospitalized for preoperative evaluation of ATH between February 2024 and July 2025 were consecutively enrolled. Inclusion criteria were: (1) parental-reported snoring, MB, and/or witnessed apnea episodes persisting for more than three months; (2) presence of AH or ATH confirmed by endoscopic or physical examination; (3) completion of overnight PSG and symptom questionnaires during hospitalization. Exclusion criteria included: (1) onset of mouth-breathing symptoms preceding that of other upper airway obstruction–related symptoms; (2) craniofacial syndromes, neuromuscular diseases, or genetic disorders; (3) history of upper airway surgery; (4) acute respiratory infection within two weeks before PSG; (5) incomplete clinical or polysomnographic data.

Symptom and anatomical assessment

Weight status was classified using age- and sex-specific body mass index (BMI) percentiles established in Chinese guidelines: underweight (at or below the 5th percentile), normal weight (above the 5th percentile but below the 85th percentile), overweight (at or above the 85th percentile but below the 95th percentile), or obese (at or above the 95th percentile) [11].

MBS was assessed using the mouth-breathing subdomain of the Obstructive Sleep Apnea–18 (OSA-18) questionnaire. This instrument has been previously published and validated for the evaluation of children with OSA [12]. Caregivers were instructed to rate the frequency of specific symptoms their child experienced over the past four weeks using a 7-point Likert scale: 1 = none of the time, 2 = hardly any of the time, 3 = a little of the time, 4 = some of the time, 5 = a good bit of the time, 6 = most of the time, and 7 = all of the time. For group comparison, patients were stratified into mild (score 1–3) and severe (score 4–7) MB groups, consistent with prior literature [10].

TH was graded according to the Brodsky scale: grade 1, tonsils located entirely within the tonsillar pillars; grade 2, tonsils extend slightly beyond the pillars; grade 3, tonsils reach midway between the pillars and the uvula; grade 4, tonsils meet at the midline [13]. Adenoidal size was assessed by endoscopic nasopharyngoscopy and graded according to the Percentage 4°- grading method as follows: approximately 25% obstruction was defined as grade 1, 26% – 50% as grade 2, 51% – 75% as grade 3, and 76% – 100% as grade 4 [14]. For both tonsils and adenoids, Grade III and above were considered as pathological.

PSG

All participants underwent overnight polysomnography (PSG) using the ApneaLink Air system (ResMed, Germany) at the institutional sleep medicine center. Sleep stages and respiratory events were scored by a qualified sleep technologist who was blinded to the study objectives, in accordance with the 2012 guidelines of the American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM). The PSG parameters recorded included the apnea–hypopnea index (AHI), obstructive apnea–hypopnea index (OAHI), oxygen desaturation index (ODI), time with oxygen saturation below 90% (T90) and time with oxygen saturation between 80% − 90% (T90-80), mean oxygen saturation (MSaO₂), and lowest oxygen saturation (LSaO₂). Obstructive sleep apnea syndrome (OSAS) severity was classified as mild (OAHI 1–5 events/hour) or moderate-to-severe (OAHI > 5 events/hour), which served as the primary outcome measure.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS (version 27.0) for general statistical procedures, R for post-hoc power and diagnostic performance evaluation, and GraphPad Prism (version 10.1.2) for graphical presentation. As this was a cross-sectional study with consecutive enrollment, a formal prospective sample-size calculation was not performed. Normally distributed variables were expressed as means ± standard deviations, and compared using independent samples t-tests; non-normally distributed variables were presented as the medians (interquartile range, IQR) and were compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables were expressed as frequency (percentage) and analyzed using the chi-square test. Associations between MBSs and PSG parameters were assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation. Independent predictors of moderate-to-severe OSA were identified through binary logistic regression, with variables showing P < 0.10 in univariate analysis entered into the multivariable model. A post-hoc power analysis for the primary multivariable logistic regression model was performed using a two-sided Wald test (α = 0.05), with coefficients and standard errors derived from the adjusted odds ratios and their 95% confidence intervals. This analysis estimated the achieved statistical power and the sample sizes required for 80% power for each predictor retained in the final model. Model stability was also assessed by calculating the events-per-variable (EPV) ratio. Diagnostic performance was evaluated by calculating sensitivity, specificity, and the area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve. A two-tailed P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 390 pediatric patients with AH or ATH [mean age, 6.18 (2.0) years; 54.3% boys] who underwent preoperative evaluation were included and classified into a mild MB group (score 1–3) or a severe MB group (score 4–7). The baseline demographics and PSG parameters for both groups were summarized in Table 1. There were no significant differences in age, sex distribution, or weight status between the two groups (all P > 0.05), indicating comparable baseline demographics. Severe MB was associated with a higher prevalence of grade III–IV TH (P = 0.036). On PSG, the severe MB group exhibited significantly worse sleep-disordered breathing, characterized by higher AHI, OAHI, T90, T90–80, and lower MSaO2 (P < 0.05 for all comparisons).

Logistic regression analysis for predicting moderate-to-severe OSA

Among the 390 children included in the analysis, 108 (27.7%) were diagnosed with moderate-to-severe OSA. In univariate logistic regression (Table 3), some variables regarding to gender, obesity, tonsillar grade and MBS were identified as significant factors. According to multivariate logistic regression, female gender (OR = 0.602; 95% CI, 0.377–0.961; P = 0.034), the TH grade > II (OR = 1.820; 95% CI, 1.147–2.886; P = 0.011) and MBS ≥ 4 (OR = 2.357; 95% CI, 1.354–4.101; P = 0.002) were independently associated with moderate-to-severe OSA. As shown in Fig. 2, the optimal cut-off identified by the Youden index (0.228) produced the following performance metrics for the multivariable model: sensitivity 73.1%, specificity 49.6%, positive predictive value (PPV) 35.7%, negative predictive value (NPV) 82.8%, and an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.658. By comparison, using only an MBS ≥ 4 as the diagnostic criterion resulted in an AUC of 0.636, with a sensitivity of 82.4%, specificity of 35.5%, PPV of 32.8%, and NPV of 84.0%.

Discussion

In this study, a higher MBS was independently associated with moderate-to-severe OSA in children with ATH. Elevated MBS correlated positively with AHI and OAHI, and with impaired oxygenation parameters. These findings suggest that frequent MB may serve as a readily observable clinical indicator of OSA severity.

The role of MB in this context likely extends beyond a passive compensatory response. Although often initiated by nasal obstruction, habitual MB may actively worsen upper-airway collapsibility through posterior mandibular rotation, tongue retrusion, and decreased soft-palate tone, thereby creating a potential vicious cycle. Our observation of a graded relationship between MBS and OSA severity supports this pathophysiological model. Consistent with that, a prospective study reported an OSA prevalence of up to 42% among mouth-breathing children,, further underscoring the clinical relevance of this symptom [15].

Beyond direct airway effects, chronic MB is also closely linked to the development of the “adenoid facies” phenotype, including maxillary constriction, a high-arched palate, mandibular retrognathism, and increased lower facial height, which may further narrow the upper airway and sustain obstruction. This interplay is supported by cephalometric evidence of reduced posterior airway space in MB children and by epidemiologic data linking greater ATH severity to a higher prevalence of maxillary constriction [16, 17]. A recent multidisciplinary study in MB children found that sleep-related OSA symptoms clustered with clinical signs of upper-airway obstruction such as TH and an obstructive Mallampati score, whereas chewing-related complaints were linked to lip incompetence and Class II malocclusion, underscoring the clinical overlap between MB, craniofacial/dental development, and airway risk [18]. Furthermore, early orthodontic interventions like rapid maxillary expansion and mandibular advancement appliances have been reported to improve PSG parameters in selected pediatric OSA cases, supporting their potential role as adjunctive or alternative treatment options [19, 20].

When interpreting the relationship between symptom scores and PSG outcomes, the characteristics of the study population are crucial. Our results, which demonstrate an association between MBS and OSA, differ from a secondary analysis of the Childhood Adenotonsillectomy Trial (CHAT). That analysis found no significant correlation among children aged 5–10 years with moderate-to-severe OSA [19]. This discrepancy may arise from methodological differences: the CHAT analysis focused on a narrow, severe-OSA cohort within a clinical trial, which may leave the relationship unexplored in younger children and across the full disease spectrum, including mild cases. This suggests that the predictive utility of MB assessment may be more evident in general clinical populations, highlighting the impact of spectrum bias. From a clinical perspective, our results support the integration of structured MB evaluation into routine screening. Although traditionally viewed as a compensatory sign, MB is increasingly recognized for its prognostic and pathophysiological significance in craniofacial development, orofacial function, and sleep architecture [21–23]. As one of the earliest symptoms noted by caregivers, it often prompts clinical evaluation before overt apneic events are reported, making its structured assessment a practical tool for initial risk stratification and family counselling [24]. While PSG remains the gold standard for OSA diagnosis, its variable availability and differing guideline recommendations regarding preoperative use highlight the need for practical triage tools. The American Academy of Pediatrics recommends PSG whenever feasible, whereas the American Academy of Otolaryngology–Head and Neck Surgery acknowledges that surgery may be appropriate based solely on clinical evaluation in certain scenarios [25, 26]. In this context, symptom-based markers such as MB become particularly valuable. Moreover, during PSG, mouth closure is not routinely monitored or required, and the dynamic interplay between nasal obstruction and oral airflow remains under-characterized. Thus, incorporating MB severity into clinical triage algorithms could enhance decision-making for AT candidacy, especially in borderline or uncertain cases [27]. Our analysis further supports this utility, showing that an MBS ≥ 4 has high sensitivity and negative predictive value for ruling out moderate-to-severe OSA, validating its role as a pragmatic screening instrument in resource-conscious settings. However, its low specificity and positive predictive value affirm that it cannot replace PSG for definitive diagnosis, emphasizing its utility primarily for triage and for prioritizing further testing.

Nonetheless, the study has several limitations. First, its cross-sectional design precludes casual inference. Second, as a single-center study involving only children scheduled for AT, the sample represents a referred, higher-risk surgical population, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to community settings. Accordingly, the diagnostic accuracy of MB reported herein should be interpreted as exploratory, as sensitivity and specificity may be influenced by disease prevalence and population characteristics. Third, the reliance on caregiver-reported MBS introduces subjectivity and the potential reporting bias. Fourth, caregivers were instructed by a single clinician to rate symptoms using a standardised questionnaire; inter-rater reliability was therefore not formally assessed, and variability in how different clinicians deliver instructions or interpret questionnaire items may affect the reproducibility of results across settings. Finally, although the consecutive-enrollment design did not include an a priori sample-size calculation, a post-hoc power analysis for the primary multivariate model indicated adequate power for severe MBS (0.891) and tonsillar grade > II (0.715), but lower power for female sex (0.590), as shown in Table S1. The model satisfied the recommended events-per-variable criterion (EPV = 36), yet the post-hoc nature of this analysis should be noted.

Future research should focus on externally validating these findings through prospective, multi-centre studies with larger and more demographically diverse pediatric populations to establish the diagnostic performance of MB assessment, determine optimal classification thresholds, and evaluate its incremental value over existing screening questionnaires. Equally important, multi-observer studies are also needed to assess inter-rater reliability of questionnaire administration and the test–retest reliability of caregiver responses, which will be essential for broader clinical implementation across diverse settings. In addition, developing objective measures of nocturnal MB (e.g., using sensor-based or wearable technology), and establishing multicenter longitudinal cohorts would be valuable. Such cohorts would help evaluate whether early identification of high-risk children through symptom-based screening can optimize the timing and outcomes of surgical intervention.

Conclusions

Frequent MB is not only a visible and easily identifiable symptom but also correlates significantly with the severity of pediatric OSA in this single-centre inpatient cohort undergoing AT. Assessment of MB frequency may serve as a useful adjunct in early triage and risk assessment, particularly when access to PSG is limited. These findings support the incorporation of structured MB evaluation into routine clinical screening for pediatric sleep-disordered breathing.
