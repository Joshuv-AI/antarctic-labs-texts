The impact of Shanghai’s community nursing center policy on chronic wound care practice: a qualitative study
Authors: Shiyu Tang, Jun Li, Tao Wang, Ruiqi Pang, Huahua Dong, Teng Xu, Hong Xu, Ying Ma, Dan Xiao, Qiao Wang, Joanna Nian Chang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectThis qualitative study explores how the community nursing center policy reshapes chronic wound care. It captures frontline nurses’ experiences to identify strengths and barriers, providing evidence for optimizing and scaling community nursing center models.BackgroundGlobal aging and lifestyle changes have intensified the demand for chronic wound care, a service predominantly delivered in primary care settings. To strengthen community capacity, Shanghai has been establishing community nursing centers since 2023. While this policy represents a novel care model, its impact on nurses and real-world effectiveness remains underexplored. This study examines how the policy reshapes wound care practices, evaluates its outcomes, and proposes directions for improvement.MethodsThis qualitative study combined field visits and in-depth interviews conducted in 10 communities across 6 districts of Shanghai, selected based on healthcare resource data. The sites included central urban (N = 4), suburban (N = 3), and outer suburban (N = 3) areas. Over a month period, semi-structured in-depth interviews were conducted face-to-face with 30 nurses. The study followed the COREQ guidelines for data collection and employed Colaizzi’s 7-step phenomenological analysis to examine patterns across settings.ResultsCommunity-based chronic wound care primarily operates through three practice models: home-based care, nursing home care, and community healthcare center care. The standardized development of nursing centers has significantly improved foundational infrastructure and enhanced nurses’ professional motivation. However, three core bottlenecks in the implementation: (1) policy and training gaps: suburban nurses rely on informal networks due to systemic gaps, while urban nurses face service homogenization under functional constraints; (2) digital care lacks institutional support: particularly in home-based care models, nurses’ after-hours social-media guidance though vital for continuity, remains voluntary, unregulated, and unpaid, and (3) financial sustainability pressures: all interviewed centers mainly depend on health insurance reimbursements and government subsidies.ConclusionsTo enhance the quality of community-based chronic wound care, a regionally differentiated strategy is essential. Suburban areas should establish institutionalized expert consultation systems, university-based generalist and comprehensive training and appropriately broadening the scope of nursing practice, while urban settings ought to prioritize sub-specialized training focused on locally prevalent chronic conditions. Financial sustainability can be improved through public-private partnerships with nursing homes, supported by policy and technological measures that formally recognize nursing labor, including after-hours contributions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12912-026-04516-z.Keywords: Community nursing centers, Nursing-led care, Chronic wounds care, After-hours digital care, Professional development of nurses, Localization

Background

In the 21st century, the significant rise in the prevalence of chronic diseases associated with population aging and lifestyle factors, such as cardiovascular diseases, diabetes, and obesity, has driven chronic wounds to emerge as a “silent epidemic” [1]. Globally, chronic wounds are estimated to affect approximately 1.67 individuals per 1,000 population [2]. Chronic wounds are clinically charactered as wounds that fail to heal within a predictable period [3]. The most prevalent types of chronic wounds include pressure ulcers (PU), venous leg ulcers (VLU), arterial ulcers (AU), diabetic foot ulcers (DFU), as well as traumatic or surgical wounds and chronic infected wounds [4]. Among these, chronic leg ulcers predominate, with a reported prevalence of 1.51 per 1,000 population [5]. This substantial patient base, coupled with the protracted treatment course, collectively presents a major challenge to individual patient health and public health systems. For patients, wounds that fail to close over an extended period exert a profoundly negative impact on physical health, quality of life, mental and emotional well-being, social life, and self-perception. Concurrently, the high and ongoing cost of treatment imposes a severe economic burden [6, 7]. In China, the average hospitalization expense per episode in 2024 was 11,707 RMB ($1,673, averaging 8.7 days). For patients with chronic wounds, the average per-episode cost was significantly higher at 22,073 RMB ($3,243, averaging 18.17 days), which is approximately twice the former amount [8, 9]. In the United States, the average adjusted cost per inpatient stay at community hospitals in 2019 was $14,101, treating a single pressure injury can add an additional $20,000 to $151,000 to the cost of care per episode [10]. From the perspective of national health systems, the expenditure on chronic wound care is substantial. Statistics indicate that in 2019, the average spending on chronic wound treatment exceeded $1 billion across more than 30 national healthcare systems. The three countries with the highest expenditures were the United States ($126.8646 billion), China ($26.9452 billion), and Japan ($18.8967 billion) [11]. Therefore, wound care has transcended its status as a specialty concern to become an urgent global public health imperative.

Chronic wound management requires multidisciplinary collaboration across extended treatment timelines. While physicians lead medical and surgical interventions, continuous nursing care is essential throughout the prolonged recovery process [12]. According to the Wound, Ostomy and Continence Nurses Society (WOCN), the core responsibilities of specialist nurse in wound care encompass conducting a comprehensive assessment of both the wound and the patient, followed by the development and execution of an evidence-based, individualized care plan. Key activities range from selecting appropriate dressings and monitoring for complications to providing essential patient education. Furthermore, specialist nurse involves providing leadership in interdisciplinary collaboration to ensure optimal patient outcomes [13].

Primary medical care (community healthcare centers in China) serves as the principal setting for long-term management of chronic wounds [14]. For example, community nurses are responsible for treating up to one-third of all chronic lower leg ulcer cases [15]. This reality underscores the pivotal role of community nursing in chronic wound care. Consequently, the practical experience accumulated by these nurses constitutes a highly valuable source of real-world data.

The chronic wound care system within international primary care is relatively mature and is primarily characterized by four distinct models: (1) nurse-led care, (2) home-based telemedicine care, (3) community-based social model of care, and (4) multidisciplinary wound care team [16]. Evidence from practice consistently demonstrates that these models effectively improve healing outcomes, reduce healthcare burdens, and lower overall costs; however, they also impose higher demands on specialist nurse training, procedural coordination, and quality control. Guided by international experience, China has developed three models for chronic wound care: (1) a “hospital-community-home” system, (2) specialty alliance support, and (3) incorporates “Internet+” enabled hybrid home care [17]. Overall, these initiatives demonstrate strong policy-driven implementation, rapid digital penetration, and improved community access. However, significant challenges remain, including disparities in community-level capacity, uneven availability of advanced dressings and resources, underdeveloped reimbursement mechanisms, and fragmented health information exchange across institutions.

In response to this context, Shanghai has innovatively introduced a new policy to establish community-based nursing centers. Under this initiative, designated communities meeting standardized criteria now provide specialized nursing services, including diabetes management, wound care, PICC maintenance, and palliative care, alongside routine primary care. In December 2023, the first 36 community nursing centers were accredited through this selection process. These approved centers are authorized to operate specialized nursing clinics and are equipped with a minimum of 30 nursing beds. Moreover, a pivotal component of this policy is granting qualified specialist nurses the authority to independently conduct outpatient consultations, perform assessments, and prescribe within the community setting [18]. Whether the nursing center has tangibly enhanced nurses’ professional practice and improved the quality of community care, however, requires empirical examination from within the community setting itself.

Centered on this key question, the study utilized a qualitative approach. It aims to systematically capture, within authentic community work settings, frontline nurses’ detailed perceptions of practice improvements and personal experiences following the policy’s implementation, as well as the opportunities and challenges they encounter. The ultimate objective is to distill actionable insights that can inform policy design and organizational implementation in other, similar regions.

Method

Study design

Guided by Husserlian descriptive phenomenology, this study employed an exploratory qualitative design set in community health centers. Data were collected through semi-structured in-depth interviews and non-participant observation. The interviews explored three key themes: institutional profiles, characteristics of chronic wound patients, and nurses’ practical experience, focusing on the implementation of the nursing center policy and its impact on care models.

Non-participant observations were conducted across wards, outpatient clinics, dressing rooms, and health centers without disrupting routine clinical operations. This approach provided triangulation and contextual supplementation to the interview data by documenting actual care delivery practices.

Sampling

This study employed a purposive sampling strategy to enhance sample representativeness and maximize group diversity, thereby mitigating the homogeneity bias common in urban research. Using publicly available government data on administrative districts and healthcare facilities, researchers quantified medical resource metrics across regions, including distribution density and per capita availability. These quantitative findings, combined with the official list of first-batch community nursing centers, informed the selection of study sites in distinct suburban and urban areas with varying development levels. The quantitative data underpinning the sampling process are presented in Supplementary Material 1.

Potential communities meeting the sampling criteria were contacted through public channels, with those consenting to participate comprising the final sample. In accordance with qualitative research principles, sampling termination was determined dynamically based on theoretical saturation reached during the data collection process.

Participants

Within the communities selected through purposive sampling, wound care nurses were invited for in-depth interviews based on maximum variation sampling across five criteria: age, administrative position, professional title, specialization, and clinical experience.

Inclusion criteria: licensed nurses from community nursing centers who are engaged in wound care and have provided voluntary consent to participate.

Exclusion Criteria: with less than 3 years of licensed practice and no experience in chronic wound care.

Data collection

This study convened a multidisciplinary research team consisting of three wound care physicians, three certified wound and ostomy nurses, four doctoral candidates, and one full-time researcher. During field investigations, doctoral candidates served as primary interviewers in community settings, while specialist physicians and certified nurses provided backend academic supervision. Each field visit involved at least two doctoral candidates conducting interviews, with one specialist nurse accompanying for clinical guidance.

Prior to formal data collection, a preliminary interview guide was developed through literature review and team discussions. Research team conducted pilot interviews in one urban and one rural community to test the initial guide, while also using these sessions as training exercises to prepare for field work. Based on feedback from the pilot phase, the guide was revised and subsequently reviewed by an external panel of wound care experts, including two chief nurses, two full-time researchers, and three specialist physicians. The final version was incorporated into a structured paper-based interview protocol (detailed in Supplementary Material 2), with the complete interview outline presented in Table 1. Based on the interview guide, a structured checklist (Supplementary Material 3) was also developed to guide non-participant observations. This tool served to systematically check for potential oversights against the interview themes and to supplement field notes accordingly.

Prior to each interview, researchers introduced their credentials and explained the purpose and rationale. Respondents provided informed consent after fully understanding the research protocol. Each face-to-face session lasted 30–90 minutes and was conducted in a quiet, private, and comfortable setting. During the interviews, researchers adapted the question sequence as needed, incorporated open-ended probes, and documented significant responses. All sessions were audio-recorded via mobile devices in addition to written notes taken by the researchers. Team members cross-checked in real-time to ensure all protocol questions were addressed, while seeking timely clarification and confirmation of respondents’ statements.

Interviews and field observations were interwoven without a fixed sequence, with observational data collected systematically using the checklist. The fieldwork strictly prioritized non-disruption to clinical services. The process involved nurses first introducing and explaining the center’s layout and functions, followed by spontaneous questions from researchers based on direct observations, extending beyond the standard interview protocol. Prior to each tour, all other staff members present were fully briefed on the study’s purpose, each lasting between 10 and 30 minutes. No participants declined to participate during the observations, and no dropouts occurred in the follow-up process. In addition to audio recording, photographic documentation of specific facilities was obtained using mobile devices. All images were anonymized prior to analysis by blurring identifiable facial features and name tags of participants and overlaying the blurred area with a unique coded label for subsequent data linkage.

Data from non-participant observation (structured notes, audio, photos) served to triangulate interview findings. This method provided contextual detail absent from self-reports and allowed for cross-verification. For instance, under Theme 4 (How chronic wound patients manage care at home), interviewees stated they educated caregivers during dressing changes but did not elaborate on methods. Observation in the treatment room revealed concrete educational tools: wound care pamphlets, wall posters, and QR codes for health apps. These tangible materials then informed targeted follow-up questions to probe educational strategies and home care guidance.

Sample size was determined by data saturation. Saturation was considered achieved when subsequent interviews no longer yielded new information, themes, or codes, and the data consistently reflected concepts already captured in earlier interviews [19]. To further validate saturation, one to two additional community samples were interviewed. Confirmation that no new concepts emerged from these final interviews marked the definitive endpoint of data collection, with the final sample size set as the total accrued prior to this validation step.

Data analysis

All interview data were anonymized to protect respondent confidentiality. Within 24 hours post-interview, audio recordings were transcribed using the software. One researcher meticulously reviewed the transcripts against the original recordings for accuracy, while another cross-referenced the text with observational notes and visual documentation to enhance contextual completeness. During data analysis, NVivo 15 software was utilized for managing and coding the interview transcripts.

The analytical procedure strictly followed Colaizzi’s seven-step phenomenological method: (1) Familiarization: both researchers immersed themselves in the transcripts to gain holistic understanding; (2) Identifying significant statements: extracting phrases directly relevant to the research phenomenon; (3) Formulating meanings: articulating condensed meaning units from each statement; (4) Clustering themes: grouping meaning units into coherent themes across cases; (5) Developing an exhaustive description: synthesizing themes into comprehensive narrative; (6) Producing the fundamental structure: distilling the narrative into essential phenomenological elements; (7) Seeking verification: returning findings to respondents for confirmation and refinement [20]. Data analysis proceeded iteratively alongside data collection. Within three days of each field visit, the two researchers completed transcription, verification, and initial coding for that batch. After each coding round, the research team held in-person consensus meetings to cross-check their work, compare results, and discuss discrepancies to refine the codebook. Disagreements requiring discussion were more frequent in the first one to three rounds. By the final round, their outputs were virtually identical with no substantive disagreements remaining.

Researchers’ backgrounds and potential biases

The principal investigators in this project are doctoral candidates without formal medical training. This creates a knowledge asymmetry when engaging with clinical nurse respondents, potentially leading to biases in question formulation and data interpretation. Furthermore, interviewees may consciously emphasize the positive impacts of the policy while underreporting negative experiences, due to concerns that critical remarks might adversely affect their professional standing or future community development. These considerations necessitate rigorous quality control measures throughout the research process.

Quality control

To mitigate potential respondent bias, respondents were informed of their right to withdraw at any time during the interview. They were assured that all data would be used solely for research purposes, with all personal and institutional identifiers replaced by codes.

To enhance the reliability of reporting policy effectiveness, this study specifically designed the interview questioning strategy. For policy-validity-related questions, the team employed repeated, context-adapted questioning from both positive and negative angles, for instance, posing positive questions during formal interviews and introducing negative probes through observational scenarios. Only responses consistent across both rounds were considered valid.

To minimize potential bias arising from the non-clinical background of field investigators, the research team incorporated physicians and specialist nurses as scientific validators. Within one week following each field visit, transcribed interviews were distributed to this expert panel for ongoing monitoring of research progress and timely verification of data interpretation.

To ensure methodological rigor, data analysis was conducted collaboratively by two researchers. An independent doctoral candidate trained in qualitative methods, who was not involved in the study, reviewed selected transcripts, and refined thematic interpretations to enhance objectivity. Following the analysis, a chief physician and a head nurse were consulted to review and discuss the emergent themes and resolve any contested interpretations. During manuscript preparation, the study adhered strictly to the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) guidelines [21]. Prior to finalization, a subset of participants validated the thematic findings, all of whom confirmed the accuracy and relevance of the results.

Following the completion of the overall data analysis, the team drafted a preliminary research report based on the findings. On August 26, 2024, the head nurses from all ten participating communities (totaling 10 respondents) were invited to attend a feedback session. During the seminar, the team presented the research report to the attendees, inquiring whether the descriptions and findings accurately reflected their experiences and perspectives. All respondents affirmed that the results were generally consistent with their perceptions and practice. The team then facilitated an open discussion, inviting them to provide feedback on the drafted discussion section and to professionally review the arguments and interpretations presented.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

The research received approval from the Ethics Committee of Shanghai Jiao Tong University (Approval No. H20240334I). Moreover, each participant completed and signed an informed consent form.

Results

Selected community healthcare centers profile

Guided by the principle of maximum variation derived from statistical analysis, this study selected 10 community health centers across 6 administrative districts of Shanghai (see Fig. 1 for the geographic distribution). Fieldwork was conducted over seven rounds between July 12 and August 16, 2024, with one to two centers visited per round. The six surveyed districts comprise three distinct types: central urban districts (Changning, Xuhui, Jing’an), characterized by compact size (38km2, 54.93km2, 37.18km2) and high density (693 K, 1.1 M, 975 K); transitional districts (Minhang, Pudong), featuring expansive area (372.56 km2, 1210 km2), moderate density (265 M, 568 M), and an urban-suburban span; and suburban districts, Jinshan, distinguished by vast area (586.05 km2) and dispersed, low-density (822 K) settlement patterns.

The ten community healthcare centers were categorized into three types according to their geographical locations: centers A, B, C, and D representing central urban communities; E, F, and G representing transitional zone communities; and H, I, and J representing suburban communities (Fig. 1). On August 6, 2024, the research team completed the sixth round of research across ten communities. After coding the data by August 9, preliminary analysis suggested thematic saturation. Notably, with the exception of community B, all other nine communities had passed the selection process for designated community nursing centers. Community B was intentionally retained as a special comparative case to explore the constraints and challenges in the implementation of the community nursing center system.

Participants’ profiles

The study employed community-based sampling, meaning community characteristics naturally influenced respondent recruitment and data saturation. As previously described, ten community health centers across six administrative districts were selected.

Within each center, researchers interviewed at least one certified wound care nurse and one head nurse to understand both clinical practice and management perspectives under the updated policy. The actual number of interviewees per site varied based on local conditions. In smaller centers like Site B, where one individual held both roles, only one interview was conducted. In contrast, centers with well-developed chronic wound services (e.g., Sites G and J), employing multiple certified nurses, provided up to four participants each.

Data collection continued until theoretical saturation was achieved, resulting in a total of 30 nurses participating in the study. As shown in Table 3, the participants ranged in age from 21 to 49 years (M = 35.43, SD = 7.65) and had 3 to 32 years of clinical experience (M = 17.57, SD = 8.54). The nursing sample was composed of one associate chief nurse, 15 head nurses, and 14 charged nurses. No participants withdrew from the study. The interviews lasted between 30 and 90 min (M = 56, SD = 23.7).

Chronic wound patient profiles in the community healthcare center

Based on community-reported chronic wound data and aggregated statistics, researchers estimated that chronic wound patients account for approximately 0.22% to 2.5% of the total patient visits in community health centers. In specific communities recognized for specialized chronic wound care (such as Community G), this proportion rises significantly, reaching 8.75% of the total community population. Patients are predominantly aged 60–70 and retain some degree of mobility. Those aged 80–90 generally require home-visit nursing services. Research shows that the average age of chronic wound patients in Chinese tertiary hospitals is 56.00 (44.00, 69.00), with the highest incidence among those aged 50–59 (21.44%), followed by 60–69 (19.71%) [22]. This indicates that community-based patients are notably older than those treated in tertiary hospitals.

Many elderly individuals prefer not to inconvenience their children, so they value the proximity and accessibility of community clinics. With convenient services such as wound dressing and gradually familiarizing themselves with the environment and staff, they develop a habit of relying on our facility for ongoing care. (H2)

According to respondents, diabetic foot ulcers are the most common type of chronic wound among community patients, with over 50% of cases classified as wagner 3 or 4. Pressure ulcers (bedsores) and venous/arterial ulcers follow, accounting for approximately 25% and 10% of cases, respectively. The remaining 5% consist of non-healing surgical wounds and other chronic infections. This distribution aligns closely with epidemiological patterns observed in tertiary hospitals, where patients also frequently present with comorbidities such as diabetes (31.62%), hypertension (24.22%), and paralysis (9.87%) [22]. Chronic wound care typically spans 4–24 weeks, with dressing changes three times weekly initially, tapering to twice weekly during stabilization, and once weekly in the healing phase. Each session lasts over 10 min. For home-visit dressing changes, community specialist nurses charge a flat fee of RMB 80 ($12) per visit.

Since our community clinic focuses on rehabilitation and basic care, the cases we handle are usually less severe. Most patients recover well, with high healing rates after treatment—unlike the more complex cases seen in larger hospitals. We also offer home visits for more serious cases, typically involving older patients. Overall, outcomes remain positive and treatment success rates are high. (J1)

Evolving models of community-based chronic wound care

Home-based care model

“Early involvement, later guidance” is the most prevalent model in current practice. Community healthcare centers maintain a moderate level of involvement, while daily care is primarily provided by family members (spouses or children) or home caregivers. When patients are mobile, they can visit community healthcare centers for treatment. Those with limited mobility or complete loss of function receive regular home visits from community nurses for wound dressing.

Although chronic wound care is a highly specialized field, once standardized procedures are mastered, the implementation becomes relatively straightforward and easy to execute. (G4)

As a result, the early stage requires nurses to conduct home visits and provide hands-on care. In later stages, they transition to guiding family members in performing care until the wound is fully healed. Guidance and communication mostly take place online, typically by having family members photograph the wound and send images via WeChat, a private social media platform, allowing nurses to assess healing progress and provide instructions accordingly.

Nursing home care model

Nursing home care model represents the second most common approach in practice, characterized by a relatively lower level of direct involvement from community health centers. Day-to-day care is primarily delivered by institutional caregivers. Although some nursing homes are equipped with in-house medical staff, their physicians usually coordinate with community nurses to support home visits conducted by family physicians.

In addition, community hospitals regularly conduct health education sessions at nursing homes to provide foundational care knowledge to caregivers. However, the current caregiver workforce in these facilities is predominantly composed of older adults, most of whom originate from rural areas with consequently limited educational backgrounds. This demographic profile leads to a reduced capacity for assimilating new knowledge, while high staff turnover further undermines the long-term effectiveness of training initiatives. As a result, the sustained improvement of care quality continues to rely on regular on-site visits and repeated health education.

Often, I would train them (the caregivers), but after two months they were gone. Later, we found it more effective to just visit regularly ourselves. (H3)

Community healthcare center care model

Community healthcare center care model is the least prevalent in practice yet involves the highest degree of engagement from community health centers. Medical staff from the community provide daily wound care and nursing services directly to patients. Compared to the previous two models, this approach demonstrates significantly enhanced professional standards, leading to superior chronic wound healing rates and shorter treatment durations. In response to the progressing aging population, communities in suburban areas with particularly high proportions of elderly residents, such as Sites H and I, have begun collaborating with nursing homes to expand palliative care capacity. This initiative aims to enhance ward availability and accommodate more patients with chronic wounds.

The interviewed nurses highlighted that effective communication with patients and caregivers as essential. Such communication builds trust, allowing nurses to gradually transfer care tasks to caregivers. This delegation enables limited nursing staff to serve more community patients. However, the current communication largely relies on private social media platforms between healthcare providers and patients. While this reduces nurses’ direct care workload during official hours, it not only raises potential privacy and ethical concerns but also increases their digital labor beyond working hours.

While infrequent, patients have filed complaints regarding delayed responses to messages during off-duty hours. (J2)

Indeed, there have been instances of patients directly contacting nurses via WeChat voice calls as late as 9 pm for consultation. But I can understand why patients get anxious. (A4)

Empowerment through policy: the impact of community nursing centers

In the standardized development of community nursing centers outlined in the policy background, the Shanghai Municipal Health Commission has established four specific selection criteria for implementation: (1) standardized infrastructure, (2) standardized service offerings, (3) standardized staffing, and (4) standardized equipment allocation. The policy requires community healthcare centers to establish independent and standardized dressing rooms and employ at least three specialized nurses, one with senior professional certification and two with intermediate-level certification, all of whom are granted independent patient scheduling authority [18]. The core objective of the nursing center initiative is to integrate previously fragmented specialized care into a centralized unit. Through tangible infrastructure standardization and professional management, the centers aim to deliver consistent, high-quality nursing services to residents within their jurisdiction, thereby enhancing both foundational and continuing care capacities at the community level.

Of the ten community health centers included in this study, nine, all except Center B in Jing’an district, had been selected as part of Shanghai’s first batch of officially accredited Community Nursing Centers. Nurses interviewed consistently reported that the establishment of independent nursing centers had tangibly strengthened their sites’ capacity to meet community nursing needs. In terms of infrastructure, each accredited center had been allocated a dedicated integrated service zone, including consultation rooms, standardized wound dressing rooms, PICC maintenance rooms, and procedure rooms. Regarding service delivery, the introduction of specialized nursing clinics has reconfigured traditional workflows: patients returning for follow-up now access nursing care directly, without requiring prior physician consultation and referral. This nurse-led outpatient model has not only improved the efficiency of community-based care but also elevated the structural position of nursing within the primary health system. By granting nurses greater clinical autonomy and recognized outpatient roles, the reform has effectively empowered the nursing workforce and enhanced their professional motivation.

Previously I just assisted doctors with dressings, following instructions and earning fixed fees. Now we run our own clinics and prescribe care independently, which is highly motivating. Even my junior nurses are more driven to study and get certified so they can eventually treat patients on their own. (F1)

Beyond enhancing community nursing capacity, the nursing centers have also uncovered previously unmet healthcare needs within the communities, particularly in suburban areas. For example, Center H in Jinshan, a remote suburb of Shanghai, reported its first-ever PICC care cases after the center’s establishment, along with increased demand for chronic wound care. Similarly, Center J in Pudong New Area serves as the only community nursing center across nearly 700 square kilometers of suburban Pudong. It routinely handles 30 to 50 chronic wound cases per day, meeting the needs of patients not only in its own district but also attracting those from neighboring Fengxian District due to its reputation for high-quality care. Almost all respondents emphasized that the local demand for chronic wound care remains high. Even with the establishment of nursing centers, existing capacity still falls short of fully addressing community needs.

Now that I have my own appointment slots, patients are even from distant areas like Situan in Fengxian District specifically to see me. (J1)

Our ward consistently maintains a bed occupancy rate exceeding 90%. (J3)

Working amidst limited resource

While the implementation of the community nursing center system has significantly optimized resource allocation within community healthcare centers, these institutions remain the most vulnerable component of the public healthcare system. The nursing centers consistently operate at a financial loss. Although national health insurance covers most costs associated with chronic wound care, including consultation fees, wound dressing procedures, and materials, thus largely ensuring patient access to necessary services, the delayed reimbursement from national insurance schemes frequently creates cash flow disruptions that strain hospital operations. Many districts have turned to value-added services such as internet-based healthcare to boost revenue, yet these initiatives have yielded limited success. At present, the sustained operation of nursing centers across districts relies heavily on the dual support of national health insurance payments and government financial safeguards.

The nursing center continues to operate at a financial loss and relies on health insurance reimbursements to maintain its functions. Although it’s challenging, our director strongly supports our work, because the community residents still need us. (C1)

During the fieldwork, the interviewed nurses openly acknowledged facing numerous resource-related challenges in chronic wound care practice, which stem from both institutional and structural constraints.

Institutional limitations primarily arise from the hierarchical medical system, which restricts community health centers to providing basic medical services. These centers lack both the qualifications to perform surgical procedures and the necessary supporting hardware. When encountering chronic wound patients requiring surgical debridement, community nurses must refer them to higher-level hospitals, with patients only returning to the community for rehabilitation post-surgery. This significantly disrupts the continuity of chronic wound care and adds unnecessary referral procedures for patients.

Severe chronic wounds require thorough surgical debridement to heal. Although our community hospital physicians are clinically capable of performing such procedures, we currently face two fundamental constraints: first, we lack both a functional operating room and the necessary equipment; second, regulatory credentials do not permit us to perform surgeries here. We have no choice but to refer these patients to higher-level hospitals for debridement. (A1)

Due to the scope of practice for community hospitals, many procedures we are capable of performing are not permitted, forcing us to refer patients to higher-level hospitals… If such cases could be handled at our level, it would be much more convenient for patients and also provide us with reasonable additional income, not to mention that performing more clinical procedures would enhance the professional skills of our doctors and nurses. (I1)

Structural constraints, closely tied to local socioeconomic development, present even greater complexity than institutional barriers. Patients with chronic wounds often present with multiple comorbidities, necessitating collaboration from specialists in tertiary hospitals. However, as the statistical survey clearly demonstrates, remote suburban areas suffer from severe shortages of medical resources. Hence, nurses frequently leverage their personal networks to invite specialists for patient consultations. Nurses from remote communities (Sites H, I, and J) consistently reported relying on their interpersonal connections to bridge local resource gaps. They emphasized that timely involvement of senior physicians and specialists plays a decisive role in the recovery of patients with complex chronic wounds.

Recently, we had a complex case, a patient with what we call a volcano wound: a small surface opening that tunneled deep underneath. I had been changing her dressings for some time with no improvement. Eventually, I brought in a specialist. He identified the underlying issue immediately, and after switching to a different type of dressing, the wound healed quickly. (J1)

The constraints in central urban areas are more related to physical infrastructure and patient demographics. First, community healthcare centers in these zones are typically housed in older, spatially limited buildings, where shortages of beds, treatment rooms, and supporting facilities restrict the scope of services that can be offered. Second, urban centers face the dual challenge of concentrated medical resources and patient outflow. For instance, because a geriatric hospital is located directly above Community B, many older adults with chronic wounds to seek care there first, rather than at the community level.

In our urban location, healthcare resources are overwhelmingly abundant, we have Huashan Hospital, Huadong Hospital, United Family Healthcare, and various specialty hospitals all nearby. Most residents in this area simply go directly to one of the nine tertiary hospitals here in Jing’an District when they need care. (B1)

Consequently, relatively few residents choose to seek care at the community level. The head nurse of Community B candidly acknowledged that despite their concerted efforts to integrate compassionate care into services and strengthen engagement with the local population, patient outflow persists, a trend that profoundly undermines the sustainable development of community hospitals.

Right now, our community center focuses heavily on humanistic care. We regularly organize free community health outreach events and invite young volunteers to interact with elderly patients in our wards. But despite these efforts, patient visits just aren’t increasing. (B1)

Indeed, limited by its infrastructure, staffing, and patient numbers, Community B was the only site in the study without an independent nursing center. Although located in a central urban area with significant aging population, it continues to provide nursing care within general wards. This situation lends support to the head nurse’s concerns regarding the future development of Community B. Notably, nurses from other centrally located communities (A, C, and D) echoed her concerns during the fieldwork.

Discussion

This study provides an in-depth exploration of the innovative practices of China’s Community Nursing Center system in chronic wound management through field investigation. The findings align with internationally recognized chronic care frameworks, including the Chronic Care Model (CCM) developed by the U.S. MacColl Institute for Healthcare Innovation in the 1990s, and the World Health Organization’s Innovative Care for Chronic Conditions (ICCC) Framework, both of which emphasize empowering frontline providers and integrating health and social services [23].

From support to leadership: autonomy & regional training

The nursing center policy enhances nurses’ professional autonomy within the community healthcare system, by establishing dedicated clinical spaces and an independent outpatient service framework. This shift aligns with international efforts to implement nurse-led wound care models. As early as 1994, the United Kingdom pioneered nurse-led venous leg ulcer clinics [24]. Subsequently, nurse-led community wound clinics emerged in the United Kingdom and other countries, where specialized community nurses took full responsibility for wound follow-up and recurrence prevention [25]. Nursing-led care model persists in the UK today, effectively addressing inefficiencies in primary care through integrated nursing and novel care pathways [26]. Similarly, Canada and Australia have adopted and advanced their own versions of nurse-led wound care, with studies demonstrating improved healing rates, reduced healing times, and enhanced patient satisfaction [27, 28]. During the follow-up research seminar, head nurses, after reviewing our findings, specifically highlighted the policy’s impact on nurses from a management perspective. They noted that the greater autonomy allows nurses to engage directly with patients, manage full treatment cycles, and receive timely feedback, enhancing their professional fulfillment.

Moreover, the establishment of nursing centers has increased the demand for specialized nurses, creating broader career pathways. Since implementation, this policy has indeed enhanced nurses’ motivation for learning, as noted from head nurses. However, significant gaps remain in the current training system for community nurses. Interviews revealed that nurses, particularly those in suburban area, frequently mentioned relying on personal networks to compensate for the lack of systematic training and expert support. This underscores the urgent need for standardized and sustainable professional development mechanisms.

Internationally, several established models offer valuable reference points. In 2008, the National Council of State Boards of Nursing (NCSBN) and the Advanced Practice Registered Nurse (APRN) Consensus Work Group in the United States jointly established a regulatory model for APRNs, mandating ongoing training as a requirement for license renewal and recertification [29]. Notably, the UK government places strong emphasis on community nurse training, facilitating interdepartmental coordination to secure stable funding for continuing education. Health Education England (HEE), the National Health Service (NHS), local health authorities, and community health institutions share responsibility for financing ongoing professional development for community nurses [30]. In addition to government and professional organizations, universities also serve as key providers of educational resources for community nurses. For instance, universities such as the University of Sunderland [31], the University of Edinburgh [32], the University of Manchester [33], and the University of Southampton [34] all offer flexible courses specifically designed to meet the professional development and academic needs of community nurses. These structured educational opportunities effectively enhance learning motivation and strengthen professional identity among community nursing practitioners.

In Shanghai, however, comparable institutionalized training requirements currently apply only to community physicians, who are expected to complete 3–6 months of advanced training at higher-level hospitals every five years [35]. Drawing on international models and local practical needs, this study proposes extending a sustainably funded, ongoing training mechanism to community nurses. In suburban areas with relatively scarce medical resources, community hospitals often serve as the only accessible healthcare facilities for local residents. Nurses in these settings face more diverse clinical situations. Hence, there is a need to establish institutionalized and routine mechanisms for expert outreach and remote guidance. Additionally, field investigations revealed that all nursing centers are equipped with information technology infrastructure to support telemedicine and online consultations. Notably, many Chinese universities are located in or near such resource-constrained regions, offering geographical advantages for leveraging academic resources to support the professional development of community nurses. Fostering university-community health center partnerships enables the co-development of tailored, practice-based training modules that address grassroots needs and translate knowledge into strengthened generalist and comprehensive competencies. Nurses in central urban areas also face professional development needs, yet their challenges differ structurally from those in suburban settings. The key challenge they face lies in establishing a complementary collaborative relationship, rather than a purely subordinate one, with secondary and tertiary hospitals. This requires building specialized skills in locally common chronic conditions, like diabetic foot care, helps move community wound nursing beyond just compassionate support.

Furthermore, numerous practical details remain to be clarified, such as defining the scope of practice for nurse-led clinics. During follow-up seminars, head nurses reiterated these concerns. However, the focus differs markedly between urban and suburban settings: urban nurses are more concerned that without expanding their range of services, their professional relevance and career development will be constrained; whereas suburban nursing staff face the fundamental challenge of how to systematically enhance their service capacity and professional recognition amid regional resource scarcity. Regarding this issue, this study contends that, similar to the career development pathways for nurses, the design of nursing scope of practice should be differentiated based on local needs. Urban areas should focus on enhancing specialized care, while suburban settings could benefit from appropriately relaxed policies, such as permitting select community-based surgical procedures or establishing regular on-site consultations by tertiary hospitals. These steps would improve healthcare access in underserved areas while offering local nurses opportunities for clinical observation and skill development.

In summary, policy restrictions on the scope of practice and the lack of systematic training mechanisms jointly constitute dual constraints on the transformation of nursing roles. Establishing a tiered and regionally adapted professional training system can effectively address the barriers, thereby systematically enhancing the overall effectiveness of nursing services and facilitating nurses’ transition to the role of leading caregivers on the basis of adequate empowerment.

After-hour digital care: toward an institutionalized pathway

This study identified the unique contribution of community nurses’ voluntary labor beyond their formal duties: spontaneous after-hours digital care, which is particularly common in home-based care models, fosters emotional bonds with patients and underpins the successful implementation of the nursing center system.

The “after-hours digital care” demonstrated by nurses, providing continuous support to patients via social media beyond their formal working hours. Although nurses expressed understanding of patients’ emotions during the interviews, healthcare administrators and policymakers should not take these contributions for granted. In systems such as the United Kingdom, the United States, and Germany, well-established primary care physicians serve as the main providers of continuous care. In China, however, where the primary care physician system remains underdeveloped, nurses have spontaneously filled this structural gap, forming a distinctive Chinese model of transitional care.

In the modernization of China’s nursing work system, there is a clear need to systematically learn from mature international experience. This includes efforts to institutionalize its nursing system by establishing mechanisms for time compensation, professional recognition, and legal protection, thereby formalizing nurses’ digital care practices. Technologically, AI-enhanced official platforms (e.g., chatbots) should be developed to transform individual care into systematic, sustainable nursing capacity, shaping a digital care governance model suited to China’s healthcare context. This dual-driver strategy, combining technology and institutional support, not only acknowledges the value of nurses’ efforts but is also essential for building a sustainable, high-quality chronic wound care system. This approach is particularly relevant for developing countries that have yet to establish robust primary care systems, have limited medical resources, but benefit from high mobile phone penetration.

Precision supply: the localized configuration of nursing centers

This study has also documented China’s targeted efforts to optimize the allocation of healthcare resources. While community healthcare centers are typically regarded as supplementary healthcare solutions for underserved or rural areas [36, 37], previous studies have also proposed introducing advanced resources, such as medical imaging and surgical support, from tertiary hospitals into community settings, forming a hub-and-spoke collaborative model to enhance community nursing care [38]. Within such a framework, Australia has promoted the establishment of Cooperative Wound Clinics in urban general practices, shifting part of chronic wound management to community settings [39]. The United States, meanwhile, has integrated nurse-led clinics into tertiary hospitals to alleviate specialty care burdens [40]. Shanghai, however, has adopted a more context-sensitive implementation strategy. Rather than applying a one-size-fits-all model, the city has treated nursing centers as a genuine mechanism for optimizing resource allocation. Among the first batch of designated centers, only eight, accounting for just 21%, were located in central urban areas. Taking surveyed communities, A and B as examples: both are located in Jing’an District, a medically well-resourced area, yet only Community A established a nursing center. Community A covers 3.72 km² with a population of 121,799, while Community B spans 1.57 km² with 29,173 residents [41]. Originally developed as resettlement housing during early urbanization, Community A is significantly denser. Medical resources also differ markedly: within a 2 km radius, Community B has five tertiary hospitals, compared to only one near Community A. Given the functional overlap between nursing centers and existing medical facilities, the policy was not extended to Community B to avoid redundant resource allocation and potential inefficiency.

The “targeted supply” model adopted in Shanghai demonstrates that successful policy implementation requires differentiated positioning based on local resource endowments rather than applying a uniform template. This resource allocation framework provides important insights for countries and regions grappling with uneven distribution of healthcare resources.

The fiscal paradox: community-based apparent financial strain and optimization of systemic healthcare funds

The implementation challenges, however, are evident, primarily stemming from the gap between policy design and on-the-ground practice. While the community nursing centers have tangibly enhanced both the volume and quality of local nursing services, the model remains heavily reliant on government funding to sustain operations. The financial challenges in chronic wound care show both parallels and distinctions when compared with international experiences. In Australia, government funding is directed primarily at promoting evidence-based care within communities, with patients bearing the cost of chronic wound treatments themselves [42]. By contrast, the Shanghai model incorporates medical insurance coverage for the majority of treatment expenses, yet, as the current study indicates, revenue performance remains weak, raising questions about the model’s scalability.

Nevertheless, earlier adopters of nurse-led center models in other health systems have adopted a broader perspective, evaluating economic benefits from a system-wide standpoint, rather than purely community-level. Studies have shown that shifting patient care from higher-level hospitals to community settings can significantly reduce overall medical expenditures for the healthcare system [43] and lower out-of-pocket costs for patients [44]. These findings provide a more macro-level framework for assessing the effectiveness of such models, helping to counter concerns over fiscal dependency and scalability. This underscores the need for ongoing longitudinal research.

To address the structural tension between the financial sustainability of community health centers and the overall efficiency of the healthcare system, this study proposes institutionalizing a nursing home care model, drawing on international public-private collaboration mechanisms. In response to reported challenges such as high caregiver turnover and insufficient training effectiveness, a systematic professional support system should be developed. This includes optimizing compensation and career pathways, implementing tiered skill certification, delivering ongoing clinical-scenario-based training, and enhancing training outcomes and staff retention through measures such as a dedicated training fund (providing subsidies for nurses and caregivers) and hospital-nursing home mentoring partnerships. Such an integrated approach can help achieve an institutional balance between improving care quality and ensuring financial sustainability.

Limitation

Currently, there are 36 community-based nursing centers in Shanghai. This study included only nine nursing centers from ten selected community health centers, representing 25% of the total. Future research should expand to include additional nursing centers to enhance the representativeness of the findings. Second, key data came from nurse self-reports (e.g., interviews), offering rich subjective insight but potentially influenced by social desirability and recall bias. Future work could strengthen validity through triangulation, such as adding direct observation or record review. Furthermore, as nursing centers continue to evolve, follow-up studies will be necessary to ensure that the resulting recommendations can be accurately and reliably generalized to other regions across the country.

Conclusion

This phenomenological qualitative study, based on in-depth interviews with 30 nurses from community healthcare centers, identifies four key challenges in nursing center implementation. First, policy and training gaps: policy-limited practice scope and insufficient professional development. A tiered, region-specific system is needed to transition nurses from supportive to leading roles. Second, unstructured digital engagement: nurses’ voluntary after-hours digital care, while maintaining service continuity, lacks systematic support. Perhaps future integration could be achieved through dedicated technology platforms and institutional recognition. Third, the financial sustainability gap: operational deficits mask broader systemic savings. To close this gap, it may be valuable to draw on international public-private collaboration models by institutionalizing a nursing home-based chronic wound care system. In summary, a coordinated strategy should address policy- and training-related professional barriers, formalize currently informal digital nursing work, and restructure financing through cross-sector partnerships, enabling community nursing to evolve into a sustainable, leading component of integrated chronic care. For China and similar contexts, success requires adapting, not simply adopting, international models to local conditions. Future policies should integrate these emerging practices into sustainable care pathways, supported by further systematic research.
