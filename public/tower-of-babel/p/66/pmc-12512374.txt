Parental expectation and psychological distress of Chinese youth: the chain mediating effects of core self-worth and perceived stress
Authors: Jing Zhou, Wanjie Tang, Zeyuan Sun, Shuang Xue, Qiaolan Liu, Siqi Wu, Qian Dai, Jiuping Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHigh parental expectations are increasingly recognized as a risk factor for youth mental health, yet the mechanisms underlying this association remain understudied, particularly in contexts like China where academic and familial pressures are pronounced. Existing literature highlights links between parental expectations and psychological distress but lacks clarity on mediating pathways.MethodsThis cross-sectional study analyzed data from 1,445 Chinese college students, using structural equation modeling (SEM). The model examined direct and indirect effects of parental expectations on psychological distress, with perceived stress and core self-worth as mediators, while controlling for co-variables.ResultsParental expectations exhibited a substantial total effect on psychological distress (β = 0.216, p < 0.001). Direct effects accounted for 43% of this association (β = 0.093), while 57% operated indirectly through three pathways: (1) perceived stress alone (27.3% mediation, β = 0.059), (2) a sequential chain of heightened stress, reduced core self-worth, and exacerbated distress (22.7% mediation, β = 0.049), and (3) reduced core self-worth alone (7.0% mediation, β = 0.015). The dual-mediator model revealed how parental expectations trigger stress-driven erosion of self-worth and, independently, a direct erosion of self-worth, disproportionately affecting young adults.ConclusionThese findings underscore the dual nature of parental expectations as both direct and cascading stressors. The study advances stress-appraisal theories by elucidating mechanisms through which external pressures from parental expectation internalize as psychological harm. Culturally tailored interventions should target stress resilience and core self-worth reinforcement. By addressing conditional acceptance and fostering autonomy, strategies can mitigate mental health risks tied to excessive parental demands. This research calls for policy and educational reforms to balance parental aspirations with children’s well-being, emphasizing systemic support for marginalized youth.

Introduction

Psychological distress, encompassing symptoms of depression, anxiety, and stress, has emerged as a global priority [1], particularly in low- and middle-income countries [2]. In China, this issue is particularly pressing among college students (roughly 30–35% report clinically significant symptoms) [3], given cultural and structural factors that Magnify academic pressure. Recent studies report depression prevalence rates of 19.6–32.8% among Chinese college students [4], while another study reported pooled prevalence rates of 33.6% for depression and 39.0% for anxiety [5]. These challenges demand urgent public health attention, given their profound ripple effects on psychosocial outcomes, including educational attainment, employment stability, income trajectories, and social relationships [6, 7]. Despite abundant studies investigating the potential mechanisms of this public health concern, research to date has failed to identify socio-cultural antecedents and mediating processes that contribute to distress in this population.

Parental expectations significantly influence the mental health of Chinese college students, often serving as a major source of psychological stress [8]. Rooted in Confucian values of filial piety and academic excellence, these expectations push students toward elite academic and career achievements, which are seen as both a personal and familial obligation [9]. This cultural framework profoundly shapes students’ academic and personal lives, creating a complex interplay between motivation and pressure [10, 11]. Research highlights a dual impact of parental expectations [12]. High expectations can drive academic success, motivating students to excel [13, 14]. However, they also frequently lead to increased stress and anxiety, undermining well-being [15–18]. Some studies suggest that moderate expectations may foster resilience and self-efficacy, helping students balance challenges while maintaining family ties [19]. Despite these insights, existing research is limited by small sample sizes and inadequate control for confounding factors, necessitating further studies with larger, more diverse samples to better understand these dynamics.

To thoroughly examine the link between parental expectations and psychological distress among Chinese college students, it is critical to account for key sociodemographic factors. Gender differences are significant, as research consistently shows that female adolescents report higher rates of mental health challenges, such as anxiety and depression, compared to their male counterparts [20, 21]. This disparity may influence how parental expectations are perceived and internalized, potentially amplifying distress in female students. The left-behind child (LBC) experience, prevalent among students from rural China [22], also warrants careful consideration. Many parents migrated to urban areas for work, leaving their children in the care of relatives in rural communities [23]. This prolonged parental absence often disrupted emotional bonding and support, contributing to psychological challenges for these children [24]. Approximately one in four Chinese college students has an LBC background, which studies suggest can heighten vulnerability to mental health issues [25]. Therefore, controlling for both gender and LBC status is essential to isolate the specific effects of parental expectations on psychological distress, ensuring a clearer understanding of their impact across diverse student populations.

To explore the impact of parental expectations on the mental health of Chinese college students, we adopted an integrated theoretical framework combining the Conservation of Resources (COR) theory [26] and the Sociocultural Self Model [27]. The COR theory suggests that stress arises when individuals lose or perceive a threat to their psychological resources, such as self-worth or emotional stability, due to external pressures like high parental expectations [28, 29]. In Chinese culture, where academic success is closely tied to family honor, these expectations can intensify stress, depleting students’ psychological resources and contributing to distress [30]. The Sociocultural Self Model complements this by emphasizing the interdependent nature of self-concept in Chinese society, where individuals define themselves through family expectations and social roles [31]. When students internalize high parental expectations, poor academic performance or setbacks can feel like personal failures, undermining their core self-worth [26]. This erosion of self-worth may mediate the pathway from stress to psychological distress [27].

By integrating these frameworks, we can better understand how parental expectations create a dual burden, depleting psychological resources while challenging self-worth, ultimately affecting students’ mental health. Integrating these threads, we propose a sequential mediation model (Fig. 1). In this chain: (1) high parental expectations amplify perceived pressure; (2) sustained perceived stress undermines core self-worth; (3) compromised self-worth precipitates psychological distress; (4) parental expectations had a direct and indirect effect on psychological distress via perceived stress and core self-evaluation as the chain mediating variables.

Methods

Participants

This study involved a total of 1,445 students from two distinct universities in Chengdu, China, who were recruited to participate. Participants were asked to complete a comprehensive 46-item questionnaire. To ensure a representative sample of the college student population in China, participants were selected from diverse academic disciplines, with 17.2% from medical subjects, 42.4% from science and technology subjects, and 40.4% from arts and humanities subjects. The mean age of the participants was 18.30 years (SD = 0.74).

Procedure

The research followed a structured procedure, beginning with a collaborative consultation with local education authorities and the hosting university to secure their endorsement for the study. Ethical approval for the research was obtained from the ethics committee of each respective university before commencing the study.

Participants, situated in a classroom setting during their regular classes, were invited to complete a comprehensive questionnaire. The questionnaire covered demographic information, a parenting expectation assessment, a psychological distress questionnaire, a perceived stress assessment, and a core self-worth questionnaire. Before completing the questionnaire, participants were informed about the study’s objectives, assured of confidentiality, and committed to maintaining their anonymity. Written consent was obtained from each participant, upholding the highest ethical standards in the research process.

Measures

Parental expectation questionnaire

This study employed the Parental Expectations(PE)subscale from the Chinese version of the Frost Multidimensional Perfectionism Scale (FMPS) [32, 33] to assess parental imposition of high goals and excessive demands. This 5-item subscale measures individual perceptions of parental pressure through statements rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = Strongly Disagree to 5 = Strongly Agree) [34]. The validated instrument shows strong psychometric properties in Chinese youth samples [35] and includes items such as: “My parents set very high standards for me,” “My parents expected me to excel in all domains,” “My parents demanded exceptional achievements from me,” “My parents had higher expectations for my future than I did,” and “I never felt capable of meeting my parents’ standards.” The internal consistency reliability for PE in this study was represented by a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.86. For the Parental Expectations Scale, the fit indices indicated a good model fit (χ²/df = 2.8; CFI = 0.96; TLI = 0.94; RMSEA = 0.07; SRMR = 0.04).

Core Self-worth scale

To assess core self-worth, the study employed the Core self-evaluations scale, a 10-item psychometric scale crafted to evaluate an individual’s core belief in their capacity to navigate challenging situations and accomplish their goals [36]. This scale has been proven to satisfy validation in Chinese college students [37]. The questionnaire items focused on gauging an individual’s perception of their self-efficacy across various life domains. In the present study, Cronbach’s alpha of the scale was 0.89. For the Core Self-Evaluations Scale, the fit indices also supported a good model fit (χ²/df = 2.5; CFI = 0.94; TLI = 0.92; RMSEA = 0.06; SRMR = 0.05), confirming the adequacy of the single-factor structure.

Perceived stress questionnaire (PSS)

The study employed the PSS-10, a self-reported scale designed to assess the extent of perceived stress among individuals over the remainder of the month [38]. This scale has been validated for use among young people in China [39]. Participants were instructed to indicate how frequently they experienced specific emotions over the past month using a five-point Likert scale (1 = never, 2 = rarely, 3 = sometimes, 4 = often, and 5 = always). In this study, the Cronbach’s alpha of the scale was 0.71. For the Perceived Stress Scale (PSS), the model fit was acceptable to good (χ²/df = 3.1; CFI = 0.92; TLI = 0.90; RMSEA = 0.08; SRMR = 0.06).

Psychological distress

The 10-item Kessler Scale (K10) was employed to assess the severity of psychological distress [40]. This scale primarily evaluates an individual’s symptoms of depression and anxiety, providing insight into general mental health status [41] and validated in Chinese population [42]. It consists of 10 self-reported items that measure anxiety and depression symptoms experienced over the past four weeks. The scale utilizes a five-point Likert scale, with scores ranging from 10 to 50; higher scores indicate a greater level of psychological distress. In this study, the K10 demonstrated a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.95. For the K-10 scale, the model demonstrated an excellent fit to the data (χ²/df = 1.9; CFI = 0.98; TLI = 0.97; RMSEA = 0.05; SRMR = 0.03).

Covariables

Gender, age, left-behind status, only-child status, and major were also assessed and included as control variables.

Statistical analysis

Correlations among parental expectations, core self-worth, perceived stress, and psychological distress were calculated using Pearson correlation analysis. The means and standard deviations of the continuous variables were also computed. To examine the mediating roles of perceived stress and core self-worth in the relationship between parental expectations and psychological distress, structural equation modeling was conducted using Mplus 7.0 [43]. Gender, left-behind status, only-child status, and other sociodemographic variables were included as control variables. The binary variables among them were converted into dummy variables in the regression models for analysis. In addition, 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported, and all results were based on standardized estimates.

Results

Descriptive statistics

The sample comprised 1,445 participants, including 605 males (41.9%) and 840 females (58.1%). Among them, 353 (24.4%) had left-behind experience, and 1,092 (75.6%) were non-left-behind. The age distribution was as follows: 112 (7.8%) participants were aged 16–17 years, 879 (60.8%) were 18 years old, 383 (26.5%) were 19 years old, and 71 (4.9%) were aged 20–25 years. Regarding family structure, 925 (64.0%) participants were only children, while 520 (36.0%) were non-only children.

LBC reported significantly higher psychological distress (23.08 ± 7.1 vs. 20.86 ± 7.6, t = 4.71, p < 0.001) than non-LBC. No differences were found between other variables.

Correlation analysis

Parental expectation showed positive correlations with psychological distress (r = 0.22) and perceived stress (r = 0.17), and a negative correlation with core self-worth (r = − 0.17; all p < 0.01). Perceived stress was strongly associated with higher psychological distress (r = 0.64) and reduced core self-worth (r = − 0.74), while core self-worth itself inversely predicted psychological distress (r = − 0.66, p < 0.01). Gender and left-behind status have some effects on the outcome variable, while the influence of other variables is negligible, indicating a strong and statistically significant association. This total effect includes both direct and indirect pathways. A notable 43% of the total effect is attributed to the direct effect of parental expectations on psychological distress (β = 0.093).

The remaining 57% of the total effect operates through three mediating pathways. The Main indirect pathway, mediated by perceived stress, accounts for 27.3% of the total effect (β = 0.059, 95% CI = [0.039–0.082]). In this pathway, parental expectations elevate perceived stress, which subsequently exacerbates psychological distress. In addition, core self-worth alone serves as an independent mediator, explaining 7.0% of the total effect (β = 0.015, 95% CI = [0.001–0.031]). Finally, a sequential process where parental expectations increase perceived stress, diminish core self-worth, and ultimately intensify distress explains an additional 22.7% of the total effect (β = 0.049, 95% CI = [0.031–0.066]). This chain illustrates the cascading impact of perceived stress on core self-worth and subsequent mental health outcomes.

Discussion

This study reveals that parental expectations significantly contribute to psychological distress among Chinese college students, both directly and indirectly through increased stress and diminished self-worth. Our findings confirm a dual-mediator model, showing that high parental expectations can trigger stress, which erodes students’ sense of self-worth, ultimately worsening mental health outcomes. This model, which consists COR theory and the sociocultural self-model, clarifies how external pressures translate into internal struggles, offering valuable insights for developing interventions that enhance stress resilience and self-worth, particularly for vulnerable groups such as left-behind children. These findings strengthen stress-appraisal theories and support the creation of culturally sensitive mental health strategies tailored to high-expectation environments.

Our findings indicate that college students with a left-behind childhood experience significantly higher psychological distress, while gender does not appear to play a notable role. Since these students grew up separated from their parents and were often raised by relatives in rural areas while their parents worked in cities, they consequently experiencing heightened emotional challenges. This aligns with prior research suggesting that such separation can disrupt emotional bonds, weaken support systems, and introduce socioeconomic stressors, all of which contribute to lasting mental health vulnerabilities [44]. Compared to their non-left-behind peers, left-behind students often face a higher risk of mental health issues [24]. These findings highlight the need for targeted interventions, such as mental health screenings, counseling, and enhanced social support, to address the unique challenges faced by this group.

We found that parental expectations directly increase psychological distress in youth. This finding aligns with previous studies conducted on different samples in China, which identified parental expectations as a significant factor contributing to poorer well-being [17, 45]. According to COR theory, high parental expectations function as chronic demands on the students’ psychological resources (such as time and emotional energy), elevating perceived stress and, ultimately, psychological distress [46]. The sociocultural self-framework adds to this view: in Chinese (Confucian) culture, young people’s self-concepts are deeply tied to family and academic success. As Zhang and Yang (2025) noted, cultural norms make parental expectations part of the student’s identity and goals [16]. This mutual constitution of self and social context means that family expectations become internalized standards guiding self-evaluation. Thus, when expectations become excessively high, they not only threaten students’ resources (via COR) but also conflict with their personal needs, creating stress and undermining core self-worth.

The finding that core self-worth independently mediates the relationship between parental expectations and psychological distress is noteworthy. Specifically, core self-worth accounts for 7.0% of the total effect, suggesting that individuals with higher parental expectations tend to report lower levels of core self-worth, which in turn is linked to increased psychological distress. This result aligns with self-determination theory, which posits that excessive external pressure and conditional regard from parents may undermine individuals’ intrinsic sense of worth [47]. When young people perceive that their value is contingent on meeting high parental standards, they may internalize self-doubt and experience a weakened core self-worth. Such diminished self-worth can make them more vulnerable to stress, anxiety, and other forms of psychological distress.

Furthermore, these findings underscore the importance of nurturing unconditional acceptance and support within the family environment. Interventions aimed at bolstering core self-worth, such as self-compassion training or programs fostering unconditional parental support, may be effective in buffering the negative impact of high parental expectations. Future research could further explore the causal mechanisms underlying this mediation effect, as well as identify protective factors that can shield youth from the adverse effects of conditional parental expectations.

Our study also shows that perceived stress from high parental expectations undermines Chinese college students’ sense of self-worth, which in turn intensifies psychological distress. When students internalize these expectations as ongoing pressure, it erodes their self-worth, making them more vulnerable to mental health challenges such as anxiety and depression [48]. This finding aligns with prior research indicating that stress negatively affects self-efficacy, further impacting mental well-being [31]. By applying this to the Chinese cultural context, our results clarify how stress translates into mental health issues through diminished self-worth. This pathway emphasizes the importance of stress management interventions to support students facing intense parental expectations, helping to protect their self-worth and mitigate distress.

Our findings offer important implications for both research and practice in addressing the mental health of Chinese college students. From the perspective of the COR theory, interventions should prioritize protecting and restoring students’ psychological well-being by reducing stress and enhancing resilience. The Sociocultural Self Model highlights the need to consider Chinese cultural values, where self-worth is often tied to meeting parental expectations. This suggests that interventions must address not only individual coping strategies but also the cultural narrative linking personal value to academic success.

University mental health services could deliver evidence-based interventions including mental health literacy, cognitive-behavioral therapy (CBT), and mindfulness training. Empirical research supports their efficacy in alleviating distress and strengthening coping mechanisms among Chinese university students [49]. Engaging both students and parents in these initiatives helps cultivate a healthier balance between parental expectations and academic performance, thereby enhancing student mental health and well-being [50, 51].

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. Its cross-sectional design prevents us from confirming causal relationships between parental expectations, stress, and psychological distress. Longitudinal or experimental studies are needed to better understand how these factors interact over time. Additionally, reliance on self-reported data may introduce biases, such as social desirability or inflated correlations, which could affect the findings. Finally, our sample was limited to Chinese college students, where cultural values like Confucianism may uniquely influence outcomes, limiting generalizability to other cultures or age groups. Future research should explore these dynamics in diverse populations and incorporate multiple data sources, such as behavioral or third-party observations, to validate self-reported findings.

Conclusions

This study enhances our understanding of how high parental expectations contribute to psychological distress among Chinese college students by integrating the COR theory and the Sociocultural Self Model. It shows that intense expectations create chronic stress, which erodes students’ self-worth, particularly in a cultural context where family goals are deeply internalized. These findings highlight the need to reduce stress and strengthen self-worth to improve mental health outcomes. Practically, universities, mental health professionals, and parents should work together to balance expectations with supportive practices. Implementing psychoeducation, resilience training, and balanced parenting approaches can help mitigate stress and protect students’ well-being, fostering healthier academic and personal development.
