Molecular insights into the anti-COVID-19 activity of reduced (quinol) and oxidized (quinone) forms of coenzyme Q10
Authors: Mangala Hegde, Vipul Kumar, Dharmender Gupta, Yoshiyuki Ishida, Keiji Terao, Sunil C Kaul, Shudhanshu Vrati, Durai Sundar, Renu Wadhwa, Ajaikumar B Kunnumakkara
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCoronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19), caused by SARS-CoV-2, was declared a global pandemic by the World Health Organization in March 2020 and rapidly emerged as a systemic mitochondrial disorder rather than a pure respiratory illness. The disease disrupts mitochondrial bioenergetics, redox balance, and electron transport, with significant impairment of the quinone-quinol pool. Coenzyme Q10 (CoQ10), a central component of the mitochondrial respiratory chain, shuttles electrons between complexes I/II and III through dynamic interconversion between its oxidized quinone (CoQ; QN) and reduced quinol (CoQH2; QL) forms. COVID-19 patients exhibit reduced endogenous CoQ10 levels, defective oxidative phosphorylation, and excessive oxidative stress, linking QN-QL imbalance to inflammation, thrombosis, fatigue, and multi-organ dysfunction. Restoration of mitochondrial redox cycling through CoQ10 supplementation or QN-based bioactive compounds has therefore gained attention as a therapeutic strategy. In this study, we investigated the effects of mitochondrial CoQ10 redox forms on key factors involved in COVID-19 infection using computational analyses and human oral squamous cell carcinoma (HSC3)-based assays. Molecular docking analyses demonstrated that QN binds more strongly than QL to host cell receptors (AR, ACE2, and TMPRSS2), which are key regulators of viral infection. However, cell-based experiments revealed similar effects of both compounds on cell viability, clonogenicity, and the expression of host cell receptors ACE2 and TMPRSS2. Furthermore, similar effects of QN and QL were observed in cells exposed to oxidative, metal, and hypoxic stress. Higher, but not lower, nontoxic concentrations of both compounds led to comparable downregulation of host cell receptors. In contrast, lower doses of QN and QL similarly protected cells against oxidative, metal, and hypoxia stress. These findings suggest that both forms exert similar modulatory effects on host cell receptors and stress-responsive pathways implicated in SARS-CoV-2 pathogenesis, supporting their potential as candidates for further investigation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00210-026-05621-1.Keywords: COVID-19, Oxidative stress, CoQ10, Quinone, Quinol, Antioxidant, Anti-COVID, Recovery

Introduction

Coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19), caused by Severe Acute Respiratory Syndrome Coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2, a novel single-stranded RNA virus), emerged in December 2019 and was declared a pandemic by the World Health Organization in March 2020. The rapid global spread of SARS-CoV-2 highlighted the profound risk that zoonotic viruses pose to human health (Holmes 2022). Like the other coronaviruses, including HCoV-229E, HCoVOC43, HCoV-NL63, HCoV-HKU1, SARS-CoV, and MERS-CoV, SARS-CoV-2 binds its host cell receptor ACE2 (Angiotensin-converting enzyme 2) through the viral spike protein (S). The latter is cleaved by the host-cell transmembrane protease serine 2 (TMPRSS2), facilitating fusion of the viral particle with the host cell membrane. Due to their pivotal role in viral binding and membrane fusion, aiding entry of the virus into host cells, ACE2 and TMPRSS2 proteins have served as promising targets for new drug development as well as repurposing of existing drugs and natural compounds for COVID-19 treatment (Duan et al. 2022; Kalra et al. 2022; Kumar et al. 2022; Baby et al. 2025; Mothae et al. 2025; Tang et al. 2025). The genome of SARS-CoV-2 encodes 16 non-structural, 4 structural, and 9 accessory proteins, which mediate the viral life cycle, including entry into host cells, replication, transcription, virion assembly, and release (Duan et al. 2022). After entry into the host cell, the virus genome is replicated and transcribed into polyproteins involving imperative activities of three viral proteins, i.e., (i) Mpro, 3C-like protease; (ii) PLpro, papain-like protease; and (iii) RdRp, RNA-dependent RNA polymerase, that have been considered the main drug targets for antiviral drug development (Kumar et al. 2021, 2024; Duan et al. 2022; Alagarsamy et al. 2023; Rabie et al. 2025).

COVID-19 symptoms are marked by excessive inflammation, oxidation, and an exaggerated immune response that causes fatal damage to organs, especially the lungs and cardiovascular system (Saha et al. 2025). Patients with preconditions of metabolic syndrome, type 2 diabetes, obesity, lung and cardiovascular diseases show severe symptoms (Jasirwan et al. 2025). Numerous experimental and clinical studies have identified synthetic and natural compounds (including monoclonal antibodies, convalescent plasma, anti-inflammatory drugs, and immunomodulators) as candidate drugs for COVID-19 (Aboul-Fotouh et al. 2023). Amongst these, sotrovimab, vilobelimab, tocilizumab, sarilumab, baricitinib, tofacitinib, convalescent plasma, remdesivir, Paxlovid (ritonavir/nirmatrelvir), molnupiravir, corticosteroids, and JAK and interleukin-1/6 inhibitors were used globally during the COVID-19 pandemic. However, with high mutation rates and the emergence of new SARS-CoV-2 strains, many of these therapeutics have been reported to be ineffective, underscoring the need for continued efforts in drug development (Aboul-Fotouh et al. 2023; Santamaria-Castro et al. 2025; Zheng et al. 2025). For example, Paxlovid (which targets the viral Mpro) was approved by the FDA during the COVID-19 pandemic. It combines nirmatrelvir (an Mpro inhibitor) with ritonavir, which protects nirmatrelvir from CYP3A4-mediated degradation (https://www.fda.gov/media/155194/download), and has been shown to be effective during the early stages of acute infection. Paxlovid-treated patients, however, experienced recurrent infections and its low efficacy against post-acute sequelae of COVID-19 (PASC/Long COVID) (Bader et al. 2025). Ritonavir was seen to cause liver toxicity and adverse interactions with other medications, restricting its wider use (Rock et al. 2014; Owen et al. 2021; Bader et al. 2025). Such toxicity of anti-virus drugs, increasing incidence of long COVID, and systemic dysfunction have raised significant clinical and societal concern, warranting the development of new nontoxic anti-COVID agents/drugs (Davis et al. 2023; Bader et al. 2025). A new drug targeting viral PLpro was recently reported to be highly efficacious in a preclinical model of long COVID (Bader et al. 2025). Extracts of Vitex negundo and Macaranga tanarius were shown to inhibit SARS-CoV-2 RdRp and offer antiviral efficacy against SARS-CoV-2 variants, including alpha, beta, delta, and omicron. A combination of Vitex or Macaranga extract with remdesivir showed a synergistic effect against SARS-CoV-2, underscoring the potential of drug combinations in SARS-CoV-2 treatment and inhibiting the emergence of mutant variants (Raekiansyah et al. 2025). The small-molecule SHEN26 has recently been reported as an oral anti-COVID-19 drug effective against a range of RNA viruses (Zheng et al. 2025). In addition to their specific antiviral activity, the anti-inflammatory effects of drugs such as Doramapimod (BIRB-796, a kinase inhibitor) and vitamins have been reported to mitigate cytokine storm symptoms, thereby offering effective treatment (Al-Khrasani et al. 2025; Rabie et al. 2025).

COVID-19-infected cells exhibit pathological alterations in mitochondrial dynamics and ATP synthesis via oxidative phosphorylation, including a deficiency of CoQ10 (an essential non-protein component of the mitochondrial respiratory chain), which is associated with chronic inflammation, severe pneumonia, and multi-organ failure (Hargreaves and Mantle 2021). Mitochondrial dysfunction, one of the hallmarks of COVID-19 risk factors, is a focus of preventive treatments aimed at improving mitochondrial turnover, dynamics, and function (Lee et al. 2025). In particular, post-viral fatigue syndrome (PVFS) and its associated wide range of complex neuroimmune disorders (cognitive impairments, sleep disorders, and neuropsychiatric symptoms) have attracted interest in mitochondrial therapies (Gvozdjakova et al. 2020; Moreno Fernandez-Ayala et al. 2020; Hargreaves and Mantle 2021; Mantle et al. 2024). Mitochondrial-targeted ubiquinone (MitoQ), a mitochondrial-targeted antioxidant, and several other natural compounds, including alkaloids, terpenoids, flavonoids, polyphenols, cannabinoids, steroids, glucosinolates, diarylheptanoids, and quinones, have shown beneficial effects in various diseases by improving mitochondrial dysfunction (Ouyang and Gong 2020; Aggarwal et al. 2023). Interestingly, many of these (including flavonoids, quinones, alkaloids, chromones, stilbenes, tannins, lignans, terpenes, and other polyphenolic compounds) were also determined as Mpro inhibitors (Khanfar and Saleh 2023). Piroxicam (PM), commonly used for the treatment of inflammatory disorders, often causes gastric, hepatic, and renal toxicity that is attributed to oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction. On the other hand, PM-mediated toxicity was attenuated by CoQ10 supplementation in a rat model (Abdeen et al. 2020). Many viruses impair mitochondrial function, although the exact mechanisms underlying SARS-CoV-2’s effects remain undefined. In a study, platelet mitochondrial respiratory chain function, oxidative phosphorylation, and endogenous CoQ10 levels were examined in 10 post-COVID patients and 15 healthy individuals. Reduced levels of these parameters in patients supported disruption of mitochondrial bioenergetics in COVID-19. Targeted mitochondrial therapy with CoQ10 supplementation was suggested to improve mitochondrial function and accelerate recovery in patients with COVID-19 (Sumbalova et al. 2022).

Coenzyme Q10 (CoQ10) plays an essential electron carrier role in the mitochondrial electron transfer chain. Along with the mitochondrial cofactors alpha-lipoic acid (ALA) and carnitine (CARN), CoQ10 protects against several chronic disorders and acute pathologic conditions, such as sepsis and pneumonia, and is termed a mitochondrial nutrient (MN) (Pagano et al. 2021). Nigella sativa has been used for medicinal purposes in folk medicine systems, including Unani, Tibb, Ayurveda, and Siddha, for centuries. Its main active ingredient, thymoquinone (TQ), is known for anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, antiviral, antimicrobial, and immunomodulatory activities. TQ was shown to bind (i) to the spike and envelope proteins of SARS-CoV-2 and potentially inhibit COVID-19 by hindering entry of the virus into the host cell (ii) to the SARS CoV2 proteases resulting in inhibition of viral replication, and (iii) to angiotensin-converting enzyme 2 and transmembrane serine protease 2 host cell receptors interfering with virus uptake into the host cell (Badary et al. 2021; Islam et al. 2021; Kadil et al. 2021; Khan and Younus 2021; Khazdair et al. 2021; Kulyar et al. 2021; Khan et al. 2022; Taysi et al. 2022). Emodin (1,3,8-trihydroxy-6-methyl-anthraquinone) is a natural anthraquinone derivative recognized as a protein tyrosine kinase inhibitor with anticancer activity. It is often used in combination chemotherapy to enhance therapeutic efficacy and reduce the toxicity of conventional drugs. Beyond its antineoplastic activity, emodin exhibits a broad spectrum of pharmacological properties, including antimicrobial, antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, hepatoprotective, neuroprotective, muscle-relaxant, and immunomodulatory effects (Semwal et al. 2021). Lapachol (1,4-naphthoquinone) has been shown to interact with Nsp9 and play a key role in the transcription of the RNA genome of SARS-CoV-2 (Junior et al. 2022). A nanocurcumin-based formulation using pyrroloquinoline quinone (PQQ) was shown to offer higher bioavailability, maintain metabolic and mitochondrial homeostasis under hypoxic conditions, and several other therapeutic effects attributed to anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties (Kushwaha et al. 2022). Platelet hyperreactivity and oxidative stress are important contributors to thrombotic disorders in patients with COVID-19. CoQ10 exhibits strong antioxidative activity and antiplatelet effect (Wang et al. 2022). It transitions between three redox states, viz. the oxidized form (quinone-QN; CoQ) that undergoes a two-step reduction involving the uptake of two electrons and two protons, resulting in a semiquinone intermediate, a free radical (CoQH•), and the fully reduced form (quinol-QL; CoQH₂) (Pallotti et al. 2022). Recombinant SARS-CoV-2 main protease exposed to tryptamine-4,5-dione (TD) or QN from 5-hydroxyindoleacetic acid (Q5HIAA) showed a dose-dependent decrease in protease activity, suggesting their potential as inhibitors of viral replication (Kato et al. 2023). In this study, we investigated the effects of CoQ10 (QN and QL) on key determinants of SARS-CoV-2 infection, including host cell receptors and the viral protease Mpro.

Materials and methods

Experimental techniques

Cell viability assay

The human oral cancer cell lines HSC3 and SAS were obtained from the Japanese Collection of Research Bioresources (JCRB) Cell Bank in Japan. The cytotoxicity of QL and QN was determined by quantitative cell viability assay using the MTT [3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)−2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide] assay (Sigma-Aldrich, Tokyo, Japan). Cells were seeded in 96-well plates (TPP, Trasadingen, Switzerland) at a density of 2 × 103 cells/well and incubated for 24 h at 37 °C in a CO2 incubator, followed by treatment with QN and QL for 24–72 h, followed by the addition of MTT solution (5 mg/mL; 10 μL/well) and incubation at 37 °C for another 3–4 h, and then the addition of 100 μL DMSO. The plates were incubated for 1 h to ensure complete dissolution of the formazan crystals formed. Absorbance was measured at 570 nm using a microplate reader (Varioskan LUX, Thermo Scientific, USA). Statistical data from three experiments are expressed as mean ± standard deviation. An unpaired Student’s t-test (GraphPad Prism version 10.5.0, GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA) was performed to assess statistical significance between control and experimental samples.

Colony formation assay

For the clonogenic assay, HSC3 cells (~ 5 × 102) were seeded into each well of a 6-well plate and allowed to adhere overnight. The following day, cells were treated with QL or QN at concentrations of 0.1, 0.25, 0.5, 1, and 5 µM, with the treatment medium replaced every 48 h. After 12–15 days of incubation, once colonies were visibly formed, cells were fixed with ethanol and stained with crystal violet. Colonies were counted using ImageJ software, and the number of colonies in each treatment group was expressed as a percentage of the untreated control (set to 100%).

Western blotting

Western blot analysis was performed as described earlier (Wadhwa et al. 2024). For protein extraction, control and treated cells were lysed in whole cell lysis buffer (20 mM N-[2-hydroxyethyl]piperazine-N′-[2-ethanesulfonic acid], HEPES), 2 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), 250 mM sodium chloride (NaCl), 2 μg/mL aprotinin, 0.1% (v/v) Triton X-100, 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF), 2 μg/mL leupeptin hemisulfate, and 1 mM dithiothreitol (DTT)). Total protein content was quantified using the Bradford assay. Proteins (15–20 µg per sample) were separated by SDS-PAGE using the Mini-Protean Tetra cell system (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) and subsequently transferred to a 0.2 μM nitrocellulose membrane (Bio-Rad, USA) via a semi-dry blotting system (Bio-Rad, USA). Membranes were blocked with 3% BSA in TBS containing 0.1% Tween-20 (TBS-T) to prevent nonspecific binding and then incubated with primary antibodies: ACE2 (1:2500 dilution), TMPRSS2 (1:2500 dilution), and β-actin (1:5000 dilution). After washing, membranes were probed with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (anti-mouse or anti-rabbit; CST, USA). Protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL Plus, GE Healthcare, UK) and captured using a Gel Doc imaging system (Bio-Rad, CA, USA). Signal intensity was measured using ImageJ software, and protein levels were normalized to β-actin as internal controls. Data were obtained from three or four independent experiments. Statistical analyses were performed using a two-tailed t-test or two-way ANOVA (GraphPad QuickCalcs), and results were considered significant at *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, and ***p < 0.001.

Immunostaining

HSC3 cells were seeded at a density of 8 × 104 cells per well onto sterile glass coverslips placed in 12-well plates to evaluate the impact of QL and QN on ACE and TMPRSS2. After a course of treatment with QL and QN, cells were washed with 1 × PBS thrice and fixed with 4% formaldehyde for 20 min at room temperature. After fixation, cells were permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS for 10 min, then incubated in 2% BSA for 10 min to block nonspecific binding sites. The samples were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies: anti-ACE2 (1:100 dilution) and anti-TMPRSS2 (1:100 dilution). Following primary antibody incubation, samples were incubated with Alexa Fluor 594-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (Invitrogen, 1:1000 dilution) for 45 min at room temperature. All cells were then counterstained with DAPI (HiMedia, India, 0.01 µg/ml) to visualize nuclei. Images were captured using an Olympus BX43 upright fluorescence microscope and processed with CellSens imaging software. The intensity was quantified using ImageJ software, and statistical significance was denoted as *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, and ***p < 0.001.

Antiviral activity assay

The assay was performed using Vero E6 cells (kidney epithelial cells from Cercopithecus aethiops, ATCC), which have been shown to be highly permissive for SARS-CoV-2 replication (Matsuyama et al. 2020), and SARS-CoV-2 (USA-WA1/2020 strain; obtained from the World Reference Center for Emerging Viruses and Arboviruses, The University of Texas Medical Branch, USA). Cells (1 × 104 per well) were plated in triplicate in 96-well plates and incubated overnight at 37 °C to allow monolayer formation. Cells were incubated in culture medium containing QL/QN or their combination at a nontoxic concentration (as determined by an independent cytotoxicity assay), followed by the addition of virus at a multiplicity of infection of 0.01. Control cells were incubated with culture medium containing 0.5% DMSO (solvent control). Remdesivir (1 μM) was used as a positive control. Plates were incubated at 37 °C for 30 h, and cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde. The fixed cells were permeabilized with 0.3% Tween-20 and stained with primary (anti-SARS-CoV-2 nucleocapsid mouse monoclonal antibody; Sino Biological US Inc. (USA.); Catalog # 40,143-MM05) and secondary (anti-mouse Alexa Fluor 568) antibodies. Hoechst 33,342 was used for nuclear staining. Immunofluorescence in control and treated cells was compared, and any reduction in treated cells was expressed as inhibition of viral replication.

Statistical analysis

The mean and standard deviation of data from three or more experiments were calculated. The significance between control and experimental samples was determined using an unpaired t-test (GraphPad Prism, GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Statistical significance was defined as not significant (ns, p-value > 0.05), significant (*p-value ≤ 0.05), very significant (**p-value ≤ 0.01), highly significant (***p-value ≤ 0.001), and extremely significant (****p-value ≤ 0.0001).

Computational assays

Retrieval and preparation of protein targets and ligands for molecular docking

The detailed methods for protein target retrieval, molecular docking, and molecular dynamics simulations have already been reported (Kumar et al. 2024). A brief description is provided here. The molecular targets, namely AR, TMPRSS2, hACE2, and Mpro, were obtained from the Protein Data Bank under PDB IDs 1Z95, 7MEQ, 1R4L, and 6LU7, respectively. The structures of QL and QN were retrieved from PubChem with PubChem IDs CID 161220 and 14,185,354, respectively. The protein structures and ligands were prepared for molecular docking as described earlier (Kumar et al. 2024).

Grid generation and molecular docking

After the protein and ligand structures were prepared, grid boxes were generated at the catalytic sites of the protein targets. For AR, the grid was generated using the coordinates of the bound antagonist, R-bicalutamide (PDB ID: 1Z95). The grid coordinates were X, − 5.90; Y, − 4.24; Z, 2.47, and the grid dimensions were 25 Å3. In the case of TMPRSS2, the grid was generated by selecting the catalytic triad, His296, Asp345, and Ser441, with coordinates X, − 1.29; Y, − 7.56; Z, − 8.39, and dimensions of 25 Å3. Finally, for hACE2, the already-bound inhibitor MLN4760 (PDB ID: IR4L) was selected, with coordinates X, − 0.16; Y, − 0.04; Z, − 0.008, and a box volume of 25 Å3. For Mpro, the grid was generated at the active site with coordinates X, − 19.50; Y, 19.24; Z, 64.35. Grid generation was followed by molecular docking using the Vina wizard integrated into the PyRx software (Dallakyan and Olson 2015).

Molecular dynamics simulations

The best-docked poses of the protein–ligand systems were then used in MD simulations to investigate their stability and key interactions. The detailed MD protocol is described elsewhere (Malik et al., 2021). Briefly, the protein–ligand systems were solvated with the TIP3P water model and neutralized with appropriate Na+ and Cl− ions. Further, the solvated systems were minimized using a 100-ps small-step, low-temperature Brownian MD simulation to remove any steric clashes. The minimized systems were then equilibrated and relaxed in 7 steps using the “Relax model system before simulation” option in the Desmond MD GUI of the Schrödinger suite. Finally, the relaxed systems were subjected to 100 ns of classical MD simulation in an NPT ensemble at 300 K and 1 atm, with 2 fs time steps and a 100 ps recording interval. The simulated protein–ligand systems were then analyzed for Root Mean Square Deviation (RMSD), Root Mean Square Fluctuation (RMSF), number of hydrogen bonds, types of interactions, and their occupancy between protein and ligand throughout the simulation using the academic version of the Desmond suite integrated with Maestro (Schrödinger 2020). Finally, the Molecular Mechanics/Generalized Born Surface Area (MM/GBSA) of the 10 frames extracted from the last 50 ns of the simulations is calculated to estimate the binding free energy using the farPPI web server (cadd.zju.edu.cn/farppi/) (Wang et al. 2019).

Results

Effect of quinol (QL) and quinone (QN) on the viability of HSC3 cells

In order to determine the effects of QL and QN on key factors of virus infection, we first determined the expression of ACE2 and TMPRSS2 receptors in human oral squamous cell carcinoma (HSC3 and SAS) and selected HSC3 based on the 2–3 folds high level of expression of both ACE2 and TMPRSS2 as compared to SAS cells (SupplementaryFig. 1). Cells cultured in medium supplemented with 0.1, 0.25, 0.5, 1, and 5 µM of either QL or QN were subjected to MTT assay after 24, 48, and 72 h. As shown in Fig. 1A, cells treated with 0.1 to 0.5 µM of either QL or QN for 24 h did not show any effect on viability. On the other hand, cells subjected to 48–72 h of treatment showed a 20–40% reduction in viability at 0.5–5 µM, respectively. Based on these data, the lower concentrations (0.1 and 0.25 µM) were considered nontoxic (Fig. 1A). Further, morphological assessment of cells also showed no change in cells treated with these concentrations for 24 h (Fig. 1B). Cells treated for 48–72 h with higher concentrations (≥ 0.5 µM) resulted in noticeable alterations in cell morphology, indicative of growth arrest (Fig. 1B). Long-term effects of QL and QN were examined by clonogenic assay (Fig. 1C). Whereas QL/QN concentrations < 0.25 µM showed no effect, cells treated with 0.5–5.0 µM showed ~ 10% reduction in clonogenicity. Based on these data, QL/QN were considered nontoxic at doses of 0.1–0.5 µM.

Expression analysis of host cell receptors in cells treated with quinol (QL) or quinone (QN)

The effects of QL and QN on the host cell receptors ACE2 and TMPRSS2 in control and treated cells were determined by Western blot analysis. As shown in Fig. 2, cells treated with 0.1 to 5.0 µM QL/QN showed dose-dependent decreases (10–80%) in ACE2 and TMPRSS2 expression. Of note, QL-treated cells showed a moderately sharper decrease in ACE2 and TMPRSS2 expression as compared to the QN-treated cells. Immunofluorescence analysis showed a dose-dependent decrease in ACE2 and TMPRSS2 expression levels in cells treated with QL/QN (Fig. 3). Both proteins showed a sharp decrease in treated cells compared with control cells. Quantitation of immunofluorescence revealed a sharp reduction in ACE2 expression in cells treated with ≥ 0.25 µM of either QL or QN. TMPRSS2, on the other hand, showed a strong decrease in cells treated with ≥ 0.1 µM. Taken together, these data suggest that the QL and QN possess equal capability to attenuate the expression of host cell receptors involved in viral infection.

Molecular docking of quinol (QL) and quinone (QN) with host cell receptors and virus protease

We first investigated the binding ability of QL and QN with the Androgen receptor (AR). R-Bicalutamide (R-Bic, a known AR inhibitor) was used as a positive control. As reported earlier, R-Bic bound to Leu704, Asn705, Gln711, and Arg752 at the catalytic site (Kumar et al. 2024). As shown in Fig. 4, similar to R-Bic, both QL and QN hydrogen-bonded with Leu704 and Gln711 in the most energetically favorable docking conformation predicted by molecular docking analysis. However, the docking score of QN was much higher (− 7.30 kcal/mol) as compared to QL (− 4.64 kcal/mol) (Fig. 4). The docked complexes, when further subjected to 100 ns of classical MD simulation, showed that both QL and QN could interact stably at the binding site. The root mean square deviation (RMSD) analysis showed almost equal average deviation of AR-QL (2.93 ± 0.22 Å) and AR-QN (2.92 ± 0.18 Å) complexes (Fig. 4). Furthermore, the root mean square fluctuation (RMSF, average fluctuation of residues throughout the simulation) did not show any abrupt fluctuation (more than 2 Å), except for the region between residues 840 and 856 harboring loop region of the protein accounting for slightly higher fluctuation (Fig. 4). We also analyzed interaction of the two ligands throughout the simulation to identify important residues within the binding pocket of AR providing stability to these ligands. The calculation showed that QN had more stable and better interactions than QL. Whereas in the case of QL, two residues Leu705 and Thr857 were involved in critical interactions (more than 40% of the simulation time), QN showed involvement of four residues, i.e., Gln711, Met745, Phe764, and Thr877 (Fig. 4). In line with these analyses of ligand interactions, MM/GBSA binding free energy calculation showed that QN (− 46.01 kcal/mol) had higher binding affinity towards AR than QL (− 32.44 kcal/mol).

The interactions among QL, QN, and hACE2 were investigated next. We found that QL possessed a weak affinity toward hACE2 (docking energy − 3.17 kcal/mol). QN, on the other hand, showed a docking score of − 4.6 kcal/mol. In the best binding pose, QL was making hydrogen bonds with Glu402 and Tyr515, while QN was making interactions with Tyr127 and Cys344 (Fig. 5). Further, the RMSD analysis showed that the binding of molecules was primarily stable, with some fluctuation towards the end of the simulation due to the movement of molecules within the binding pocket (Fig. 5). The RMSF plot did not show any abrupt fluctuation due to the binding of ligands near the binding pocket (Fig. 5). However, due to weak binding, these ligands were found to be moving around the catalytic site and making numerous interactions surrounding the binding pocket (Fig. 5). The MM/GBSA binding free energy showed a weak affinity of QL (− 12.2 kcal/mol) and a relatively strong affinity of QN (− 38.02 kcal/mol) against hACE2.

We then examined the affinity of QL and QN for hTMPRSS2, a cell-surface receptor critical for SARS-CoV-2 entry into host cells. Ligands, when docked at the triad (His296, Asp345, and Ser441) catalytic site of the receptor, revealed similar docking scores: QL (− 4.99 kcal/mol) and QN (− 5.1 kcal/mol) (Fig. 6). In the best binding pose, QL showed hydrogen bonding with Ser460 and Gly464 in MD simulation analyses, they were found stable throughout the 100 ns simulation. QN showed direct interactions with one of the catalytic triad residues, His296, and other residues, such as Gln438, Ser460, Gln462, and Gly464, also showed binding. The RMSD calculations showed a stable and similar trajectory throughout, with an average RMSD of 3.90 ± 0.24 Å for TMPRSS2-QL and 3.95 ± 0.27 Å for TMPRSS2-QN (Fig. 6). No significant difference was found in the fluctuation of the protein residues due to the binding of any of the ligands, except for some high fluctuation (more than 2 Å) in the flexible loop region of the protein (Fig. 6). Further, the interaction fraction showed that both compounds had a similar number of crucial interactions throughout the simulation time. Of note, Ser441, one of the catalytic triad residues, showed hydrogen bonding with both ligands for a significant fraction of time (Fig. 6). The MM/GBSA binding affinity analysis showed higher binding energy for QL (− 21.54 kcal/mol) as compared to QN (− 35.29 kcal/mol).

We also determined the binding of QL and QN to the SARS-CoV-2 main protease (Mpro), which is essential for viral replication in host cells. While the docking score of QL was low (- 3.59 kcal/mol), QN showed a good docking score (− 5.51 kcal/mol). In the best binding pose, QL was making hydrogen bonds with Asn141 and His163, while QN was making interactions with Ser144, His163, and Asn189 (Fig. 7). The simulation showed a stable trajectory for the complexes; RMSD calculation showed an average deviation of 3.15 ± 0.33 Å for QL and 2.83 ± 0.18 Å for QN (Fig. 4). The RMSF calculation also showed a stable simulation without any abrupt fluctuation in any of the residues throughout the simulation (Fig. 7). Cys145 and His166 have been shown to be crucial binding site residues for various Mpro inhibitors (Aboul-Fotouh et al. 2023; Caruso et al. 2020). We found that these two residues were making crucial interactions with QL involving Gln189 and Gln192 of Mpro. However, the MM/GBSA binding free energy calculation, which directly correlates with binding affinity, showed very weak binding energies for QL (− 17.9 kcal/mol) and QN (− 15.20 kcal/mol) against Mpro.

Based on the experimental and computational data above, we predicted that QL/QN would exhibit good antiviral activity. However, a Vero E6 cell (kidney epithelial cells from C. aethiops)-based virus replication assay on control and QL/QN treated cultures at a nontoxic concentration showed no difference in reference to the positive control Remdesivir (1 μM) that caused > 95% inhibition of virus replication. In light of these data, we hypothesized that QL/QN may protect cells against stress-related pathology during COVID-19 and therefore recruited three stress models (oxidative, metal, and hypoxia) to determine their effects.

Quinol (QL) or quinone (QN) protects cells against a variety of stresses

We first determined the nontoxic stress concentrations (IC20) of hydrogen peroxide (IC20 = 25 μM), cadmium nitrate (IC20 = 1.5 μM), and cobalt chloride (IC20 = 23 μM) using several independent experiments (SupplementaryFig. 2). The cells under these stress conditions were then treated with either QL or QN. As shown in Fig. 8, cells treated with H2O2 and subsequently recovered in either QL- or QN-supplemented medium (0.005 or 0.010 M) showed better recovery. Similarly, cells treated with 0.005 μM of QL/QN showed better recovery subsequent to cadmium nitrate-induced stress (Fig. 8). Cells subjected to hypoxia stress also showed better recovery in the culture medium supplemented with QL concentrations of 0.005 and 0.010 M and QN concentrations of 0.005, 0.01, 0.05, and 0.1 μM (Fig. 8). These data demonstrated the multi-stress reducing ability of QL and QN; both forms showed equal effect.

Discussion

Coenzyme Q10 (CoQ10) is an amphipathic molecule predominantly localized in the inner mitochondrial membrane, where it serves as a key component of the electron transport chain and cellular energy production (Crane 2001). Beyond its function in the mitochondrial electron transport chain, CoQ10 acts as a potent antioxidant and exhibits anti-inflammatory, anti-apoptotic, and immunomodulatory effects (Hernandez-Camacho et al. 2018; Sifuentes-Franco et al. 2022; Fakhrolmobasheri et al. 2023; Mantle and Golomb 2025). Its therapeutic potential has been explored in conditions marked by inflammation, including heart failure, viral myocarditis, neurodegenerative disorders, rheumatoid arthritis, fatty liver disease, and diabetes (Fakhrolmobasheri et al. 2023). CoQ10 exhibits potent anti-inflammatory properties by downregulating pro-inflammatory mediators, including tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interleukin-6 (IL-6), C-reactive protein (CRP), and other related cytokines (Fakhrolmobasheri et al. 2023). Clinical studies also report low CoQ10 levels in critically ill patients. Supplementation, alone or with nutrients such as selenium or carnitine, supports immune regulation and mitigates cytokine-driven inflammation during viral infections (Fan et al. 2017; Fakhrolmobasheri et al. 2023).

Notably, Fernandez-Ayala and colleagues provided evidence that mitochondrial dysfunction is a key factor in the progression and severity of SARS-CoV-2 infection, with a particular vulnerability observed in individuals aged 65 and older. This increased susceptibility was associated with a significant decline in endogenous CoQ10 levels (Moreno Fernandez-Ayala et al. 2020). Similarly, another study reported that oxidative stress triggered by viral infection could lead to genetic mutations affecting enzymes involved in CoQ10 biosynthesis, further impairing mitochondrial function (Gvozdjakova et al. 2020). Clinical trials investigating the therapeutic potential of CoQ10 supplementation in patients with COVID-19 have reported encouraging results. In one such study, Israel and colleagues conducted a clinical trial demonstrating a notable reduction in the risk of hospitalization among COVID-19 patients who received CoQ10 supplementation compared with those who did not (Israel et al. 2021). Additionally, a prospective observational study evaluated the effects of CoQ10 combined with alpha-lipoic acid in patients with long COVID symptoms (Barletta et al. 2023). Participants received 200 mg of CoQ10 and 200 mg of alpha-lipoic acid daily for 2 months. The intervention group showed a significant reduction in fatigue severity and an improvement in overall functional status compared with the placebo group, suggesting that mitochondrial-targeted therapy may help alleviate persistent post-viral symptoms (Barletta et al. 2023). CoQ10, also known as ubiquinone because of its widespread presence in tissues, exists in two interconvertible forms: the oxidized form (ubiquinone) and the reduced form (ubiquinol), with ubiquinol being the dominant form in the human body (Pallotti et al. 2022).

In the current study, we investigated the binding interactions and stability of QL and QN with key viral and host targets involved in SARS-CoV-2 infection. Molecular docking and dynamic simulations revealed that both compounds, particularly quinone, exhibited strong and stable interactions with the AR. QN exhibited a higher docking score and MM/GBSA binding energy than QL, as evidenced by interactions with critical residues, including Gln711, Met745, Phe764, and Thr877. These findings suggest that quinone has a higher affinity and stability within the AR binding pocket. Further evaluation of TMPRSS2, an essential protease for viral entry, revealed that both ligands bind near the catalytic triad with similar docking scores. However, quinone exhibited slightly higher binding energy and more stable interactions, including direct contacts with His296 and Ser441. In contrast, ACE2, another key host receptor, showed weaker overall interactions, with quinone again exhibiting a relatively higher binding affinity than QL. RMSD and RMSF analyses confirmed the stability of the complexes, although both ligands exhibited some mobility within the ACE2 binding pocket, likely due to their weak interaction profiles. With respect to SARS-CoV-2 main protease (Mpro), docking and simulation studies showed modest interactions for both compounds. Although quinone demonstrated slightly better docking and RMSD values, MM/GBSA energies indicated poor binding affinities for both ligands, suggesting limited inhibitory potential against Mpro. Collectively, the data supported preferential binding of QN over QL, particularly for AR, ACE2, and TMPRSS2, highlighting QN’s potential as a lead molecule to modulate viral entry and host-virus interactions in SARS-CoV-2 pathogenesis. Coronaviruses use specific host receptors and proteases to facilitate their entry to host cells, with ACE2 and TMPRSS2 being central to SARS-CoV and SARS-CoV-2 infections. The viral spike protein binds ACE2, and TMPRSS2 subsequently primes the spike, enabling membrane fusion and viral entry (Lukassen et al. 2020; Zhou et al. 2020). We further explored whether the compounds QL and QN affect these critical host-entry factors in vitro. Cytotoxicity assessment using the MTT assay showed that QL and QN were nontoxic to HSC3 cells at concentrations of 0.1 and 0.25 µM, as evidenced by no change in cell viability or morphology. Western blotting demonstrated that low concentrations of QL and QN did not affect ACE2 or TMPRSS2 expression, whereas higher concentrations significantly downregulated both proteins. These results were corroborated by immunofluorescence analysis, which showed a similar trend: no change at lower doses, but reduced fluorescence intensity at ≥ 0.5 µM, indicating suppression of both receptor expressions. Together, these findings suggest that QL and QN modulate ACE2 and TMPRSS2 expression in a concentration-dependent manner. While nontoxic at lower doses, higher concentrations may exert antiviral effects by downregulating key viral entry receptors, offering potential therapeutic relevance against SARS-CoV-2.

This study provides novel insights into the potential of QL and QN as modulators of host-virus interactions in SARS-CoV-2 infection; however, several aspects warrant further exploration. Future research should extend beyond molecular docking, simulation analyses, and in vitro experiments to models that more closely mimic the complex physiological environment of human infection. Expanding cytotoxicity and receptor-modulating assessments across multiple cell lines and tissue systems will be important for establishing broader relevance. In addition, systematic evaluation of potential off-target effects, pharmacokinetic properties, and long-term safety is necessary to determine their suitability as therapeutic agents. Most importantly, in vivo studies and rigorously designed clinical trials will be critical for validating the efficacy and translational potential of QL and QN in modulating viral entry and disease progression.

Conclusion

In conclusion, both QL and QN exhibited similar effects on HSC3 cells, with no significant differences observed between the two compounds. At lower, nontoxic concentrations, neither compound altered ACE2 or TMPRSS2 expression, while higher concentrations led to a comparable downregulation of these viral entry receptors. These results suggest that both compounds may exert similar modulatory effects on host-cell factors involved in SARS-CoV-2 entry, supporting their potential as antiviral strategies warranting further investigation.
