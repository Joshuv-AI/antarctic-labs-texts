Behavioral Postprandial Distress in Autism Spectrum Disorder: Behavioral Clues Beyond Physiology
Authors: Lin Long, Haiyan Zhang, Jing Liu, Jun Zhou, Peiling Bao
Published: 2025

TO THE EDITOR: We read with great interest the article by Sanchez et al1 entitled “Delayed gastric emptying correlates with decreased post-prandial motility in children: a single-center retrospective review”. Their retrospective study provides valuable insight into the interplay between delayed gastric emptying (DGE) and antroduodenal manometry (ADM) findings in pediatric patients. The authors clearly demonstrated that decreased postprandial motility index and fewer antral contractions are associated with DGE.

We commend the authors for their rigorous methodology, particularly their careful control of patient variables such as body mass index and age, and for their comparative analysis of ADM and gastric emptying scintigraphy outcomes. Of particular relevance to the field of neurogastroenterology is their finding that postprandial antral hypomotility is significantly associated with DGE (P = 0.008), and that patients lacking an antral component in the migrating motor complex exhibited reduced gastric emptying percentages.

Our intent is not to reinterpret these findings, but to highlight an important opportunity for hypothesis generation and future research directions. Specifically, we believe this work provides a compelling physiologic benchmark for exploring behavioral manifestations of postprandial distress in neurodevelopmentally diverse populations, such as children with autism spectrum disorder (ASD), who often struggle with interoceptive awareness and verbal expression.

While Sanchez et al1 did not explicitly examine neurodevelopmental subgroups, their rigorous correlation between ADM findings and subjective symptoms (nausea, vomiting, and fullness) supports our call to extend such investigations to behaviorally complex populations.

Children with ASD frequently suffer from gastrointestinal (GI) discomfort, including symptoms consistent with postprandial distress syndrome (PDS), such as early satiety, bloating, and post-meal agitation. However, due to limited verbal expression and atypical sensory responses, such children may not report discomfort traditionally. Instead, they manifest it through nonverbal behavioral cues—agitation, meal refusal, abdominal guarding, and withdrawal. It is important to acknowledge, however, that such behavioral cues may arise from multiple non-GI-related factors—such as sensory sensitivities, anxiety, or environmental stressors—and should therefore be interpreted cautiously within a comprehensive clinical context.

These clinical patterns suggest that, beyond gastric motor abnormalities, additional neurodevelopmental factors—such as altered interoception and sensory dysregulation—may mediate postprandial responses in this group.

In practice, future pediatric GI motility studies could incorporate caregiver-reported tools such as the Brief Autism Mealtime Behavior Inventory, alongside systematic behavioral coding during motility assessments (eg, video observation during standardized meals). This multimodal approach—linking ADM and gastric emptying scintigraphy data with behavioral and autonomic markers such as heart rate variability and salivary cortisol—could enrich our understanding of the gut-brain interface in neurodevelopmentally diverse children.

Our perspective is supported by prior studies demonstrating a high prevalence of GI symptoms in ASD,2,3 the utility of behavioral indicators linked to GI discomfort,4 and the potential value of caregiver reports and structured observational instruments.5,6

In conclusion, the study by Sanchez et al1 provides a critical physiologic foundation from which researchers can begin investigating behavioral manifestations of postprandial distress in children with neurodevelopmental differences. Their findings emphasize the role of gastric motility—particularly migrating motor complex activity—in postprandial symptomatology and open the door to a broader, more inclusive model of clinical evaluation that incorporates both objective physiologic metrics and neurobehavioral patterns.
