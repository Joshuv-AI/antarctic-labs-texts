Experimental Investigation of the Impact of Particle Size on Its Distribution within a Horizontal Wellbore
Authors: Shabeeb Alajmei, Ose Budiman, Murtada S Aljawad
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractFracture conductivity is directly affected by an uneven proppant distribution, which affects the productivity of a well. The distributions of 40/70 mesh white sand compared to 100 mesh brown sand in a horizontal wellbore were investigated in this study. The apparatus used to conduct these tests was a 30 ft-long transparent horizontal wellbore with three perforation clusters. The proppant distribution was examined experimentally by injecting slurries at three different injection rates ranging from 20 to 75 gpm. The proppant concentration of both sands increased for each test from 0.5 to 2 ppg. The proppant concentrations and injection rates were used with multiple perforation configurations (orientations) including extreme limited entry perforations. The results confirmed that the injection rates significantly affected the proppant distributions across the different perforation clusters. Even distribution of the 40/70 mesh sand can be obtained using a 1 shot per foot (SPF) top perforation design. The 100 mesh sand was evenly distributed using a 4 SPF perforation configuration. The settling percentage was calculated for each experiment to determine the quantity of 40/70 mesh and 100 mesh sand settled at the bottom of the horizontal wellbore. A carrier fluid can suspend the sand more effectively with a higher injection rate, reducing the average sand settling to 2% in the case of the 100 mesh sand compared with approximately 8% for the 40/70 mesh sand at similar injection rates. This research confirms the need to use a 100 mesh at high injection rates to reduce sand settling during the hydraulic fracturing process.

Introduction

The extraction of hydrocarbons from tight formations is significantly affected by the effectiveness of the hydraulic fracturing treatment. The hydraulic fracturing process aims to create high-conductivity pathways extending from the wellbore to the formation.1

The success of the hydraulic fracturing treatment depends on the effectiveness of proppant placement. It is essential to place sufficient proppants within the newly created fractures to keep them propped open.2 The allocation of proppants among various perforation clusters significantly impacts the treatment efficiency. This ultimately influences the wellbore productivity. Uniform distribution of the proppant across the different perforation clusters ensures that the created hydraulic fractures are propped. Therefore, it enhanced the permeability of the formation. A potential approach to promote uniform proppant distribution is to complete the wellbore with limited entry perforations. McDaniel et al.3 defined limited entry as a completion technique that aims to reduce the number and size of perforations. Consequently, a large differential pressure between the wellbore and the formation is achieved. The extreme limited entry technique is characterized by achieving a perforation pressure exceeding 1500 psi. Therefore, the implementation of this method allows bypassing the pressure variations in the vicinity of the wellbore.4 However, this perforation pressure is expected to decrease after the proppant is injected with the fracturing fluid owing to perforation erosion. This is particularly noticeable near the heel of the horizontal wellbore.5

One of the challenges in hydraulic fracture treatment design is the ability of the fracturing fluid to distribute the proppant evenly across multiple clusters. The volumetric rate, proppant size, proppant concentration, proppant specific gravity, and rheology of the carrier fluid are some of the variables that affect the proppant transport.6 According to a study conducted by Crespo et al.7 on a large-scale experimental apparatus, different distribution behaviors of 20/40 mesh sand, 200 mesh silica flour, and 16/30 mesh ceramic were observed. The injection rate was varied from 8 to 14 bbl/min. Additionally, it was observed that the 20/40 mesh sand was evenly distributed when water was used as the carrier fluid. Furthermore, increasing the fluid viscosity enhanced the proppant distribution at a low injection rate of 8 bbl/min.

The productivity of unconventional wells is adversely affected by the uneven distribution of the injected proppant. Miller et al.8 found that only 30% of the perforated intervals in unconventional shale reservoirs contribute to 70% of production. Yi et al.9 stated that the challenge of refracturing a horizontal well is to avoid heel-dominated proppant placements. If achieved, the productivity of the refractured well can be increased, and the entire well can be protected from offset-well fracture hits. An optimal proppant distribution increases the likelihood of maximizing production from unconventional shale gas and oil resources. Therefore, several investigations were conducted to understand the proppant distribution behavior numerically using computational fluid dynamics (CFD).10−15

Recently, a field-scale simulation model using CFD was used to predict proppant distribution using slickwater.16 They used erosion data of perforations obtained from downhole cameras to calibrate their model. The proppant distributions of both the 100 mesh and 40/70 mesh sands were investigated. They concluded that the proppant distribution can be improved significantly by designing vertical perforations and pumping out smaller particles. In addition, another simulation study using CFD confirms the capability of slickwater to transport and optimize the proppant allocation in a horizontal wellbore.17

Few studies have recently used experimental data and proposed empirical models to predict proppant distributions in a horizontal wellbore.18,19 Different approaches have been used to quantify the proppant distribution, including the calculation of the percentage change in the proppant concentration at each perforation cluster and the use of standard deviation between the proppant concentrations received at each perforation cluster. One of the major challenges in modeling proppant distribution behavior is predicting the ability of proppant particles to turn from the wellbore to exit the perforation. Dontsov20 developed a mathematical model to predict proppant behavior in a horizontal wellbore by quantifying the turning efficiency of the proppant particles. He found that particle turning was more predominant in the heel cluster, whereas perforation phasing was more sensitive in the middle and toe clusters.

An equal distribution of proppants can be achieved by selecting a suitable proppant size and perforation design for stimulation treatments. Different perforation configurations were employed to investigate the perforation effect on proppant transport and distribution using comparable injection rates and proppant concentrations for the same proppant size. This study also compares the 100 mesh distribution published previously by Alajmei and Miskimins21 to the 40/70 mesh sand distribution conducted in this study.22 In addition, an in-depth comparison analysis was performed to optimize sand selection for hydraulic fracturing applications.

Methodology

The apparatus, shown in Figure 1, was used to evaluate the distribution of 40/70 mesh white sand and 100 mesh brown sand.19 First, the proppant was blended with fresh water in a 200 gallon tank for at least 10 min to ensure consistency in the slurry mixture. Because of the constraints imposed by the size of the tank, the time of the injection process for each experiment ranged from one to one and a half minutes. The apparatus consists mainly of a 1.5 in. internal diameter, translucent, horizontal pipe that is 30 feet long. The wellbore was made of visible PVC with an absolute roughness value of 0.0015 mm and a minimum wall thickness of 0.20 in. Additionally, it can withstand a maximum pressure of 470 psi at a temperature of 73 °F. Multiple flanges were utilized to interconnect various parts of the PVC pipe, creating a 30 foot wellbore. The utilization of these flanges facilitated the breakdown of the horizontal pipe during wellbore cleaning. To evaluate the various perforation configurations, valves were fitted to each of the three perforation clusters. These perforation clusters were evenly spaced apart by 6 feet. These perforations were designed to resemble the limited entrance technique and had a 0.25 in. diameter. Prior to the first two clusters, two pressure gauges were placed at the top of the horizontal wellbore. The main purpose of these gauges was to measure the pressure during slurry injection.

Schematic of the laboratory experimental apparatus.19

A slurry pump was used to inject the mixture into the see-through horizontal wellbore. A 1.5 in. valve was fitted at the end of the horizontal wellbore and was slightly opened (with 10% opening) to prevent high back pressure on the pump. A high back pressure could harm the pump during the injection and disrupt the proppant transport experiments.

The pump used was a self-priming centrifugal pump with a 3 in. size. It also had a semiopen impeller made of stainless steel. The pump is capable of handling semisolids with a diameter of up to 3/8 in. and is capable of producing a maximum flow rate of 375 gallons per minute at a maximum working pressure of 125 pounds per square inch. A variable-frequency drive was used to manually control the pump. A flow meter was also included in the laboratory setup. The flowmeter was used to quantify the injection rate of the slurry with a precision of 0.25%. It consists of a transmitter and detectors that gauge the voltage induced by the movement of the slurry as it crosses the magnetic field coils. The induced voltage was subsequently transformed into the flow rate based on the internal pipe diameter. Before the heel and middle clusters, two pressure transducers were installed to measure the pressure drop of the proppant-laden slurry before the horizontal wellbore.

Alajmei and Miskimins21,23 used two video cameras positioned before the first cluster and after the second cluster to observe sand transport and deposition inside a translucent horizontal wellbore. The proppant distribution was evaluated at three different injection rates and four different proppant concentrations.

This study compares the distribution of 40/70 mesh sand, which has a median particle diameter of 0.312 mm, to that of 100 mesh sand, which has a median particle diameter of 0.1854 mm. The specific gravity of the sands was 2.65 g/cm3. All data for the 100 mesh sand were obtained by Alajmei and Miskimins.21 All 40/70 mesh sand experimental tests, except for the 4 shot per foot (SPF) perforation, were performed by Alajmei.22 Experiments conducted on 40/70 mesh sand using 4 SPF perforation with a 90 degree phasing were performed by Ahmad and Miskimins.25,26 Larger sand particles settled in higher proportions at the bottom of the mixing tank owing to their high specific gravity and size.24

Results and Discussion

The findings of this study cover the proppant settling at the bottom of the horizontal wellbore as well as the proppant (sand) distributions across different perforation clusters for all the examined perforation configurations.

Proppant Settling Analysis

The settling of the proppant was quantified as a percentage of the total sand injected into the horizontal wellbore using eq 1.

For each experiment, the amounts of 40/70 mesh and 100 mesh sand that settled at the bottom of the horizontal wellbore were measured, and a settling percentage analysis was performed. With an increase in the injection rate, the settling percentage at the bottom of the horizontal wellbore decreased. The carrier fluid, fresh water, became more capable of suspending sand at higher injection rates. The turbulence associated with the high injection rate of the carrier fluid facilitates the transportation of the sand particles to the toe cluster. In contrast, when used as the carrier fluid, insufficient injection rates of freshwater do not effectively transport the proppant to the toe cluster of the horizontal wellbore. This results in inadequate propping of the hydraulic fractures that have been initiated, and these clusters ultimately will not contribute to production. Furthermore, the sand that has accumulated at the bottom of the horizontal wellbore may cause further problems during well flowback, which requires the installation of extra surface separation and treatment equipment. Compared with the 100 mesh sand described by Alajmei and Miskimins,21 the settling percentage of the 40/70 mesh sand was generally higher. This can be explained by the larger particles of the 40/70 mesh sand.

For the 40/70 mesh sand at an injection rate of 20 gpm and a proppant concentration of 0.5 and 1 ppg, the 1 SPF top perforation configuration produced the highest settling, which was observed to be 99%. However, the settling percentage was drastically reduced to 11 and 12% of the total injected proppant concentration by increasing the injection rate to 40 gpm at proppant concentrations of 0.5 and 1 ppg, respectively. As demonstrated in Figure 2, at an injection rate of 20 gpm, the settling percentage reduced from 99 to 67% when the proppant concentration was increased from 0.5 to 2 ppg. In general, increasing the injection rates can result in an increase in the ability of the carrier fluid to suspend more 40/70 mesh and 100 mesh sand using the 1 SPF top perforation configuration. Nevertheless, as the proppant size increased from 100 mesh to 40/70 mesh sand, the settling percentage also increased owing to the large void space between the sand particles. This results in a decrease in the local velocity of sand particles supported by higher gravitational forces, making it easier for sand to settle at the bottom of the wellbore. Conversely, increasing the proppant concentration led to a decrease in the void spaces between sand particles. This results in an increase in the local velocity of the sand particles and a decrease in sand settling at the bottom of the horizontal wellbore.

Settling percentage using the 1 SPF top perforation.

At increasing injection rates, the average settling percentages for the 100 mesh and 40/70 mesh were 54.8 and 87.5% at 20 gpm, 12 and 32.3% at 30 gpm, and 6 and 13.8% at 40 gpm, respectively. This can be explained by the gravitational forces acting more strongly on larger sand particles than on smaller particles.

At an injection rate of 20 gpm and a proppant concentration of 0.5 ppg, the settling percentage using the 1 SPF bottom perforation configuration was 26% for 40/70 mesh sand. Increasing the injection rate to 40 gpm improved the capability of the carrier fluid to sustain additional sand particles, resulting in a settling percentage of only 1.4%. As shown in Figure 3, an increase in the proppant concentration from 0.5 to 2 ppg at a constant injection rate of 20 gpm resulted in a decrease in the settling percentage for the 40/70 mesh sand to 11%.

Settling percentage using the 1 SPF bottom perforation.

However, using this 1 SPF bottom perforation arrangement reduced the tendency of the larger sand particles (i.e., 40/70 mesh) to settle at the bottom of the wellbore. At increasing rates, the average settling percentages for the 100 mesh and 40/70 mesh were 21 and 13% at 20 gpm, 7.5 and 3.5% at 30 gpm, and 4.5 and 2.8% at 40 gpm, respectively. The findings suggest that employing 40/70 mesh sand rather than 100 mesh sand results in more uniform void spaces between the sand particles, which enhances the velocity and helps to transfer more sand particles to the middle and toe clusters.

The 40/70 mesh sand settled differently when the perforation arrangement was changed to a 2 SPF top and bottom perforation configuration, as shown in Figure 4. A maximum settling percentage of 32.5% was obtained with a proppant concentration of 2 ppg and an injection rate of 20 gpm. The settling percentage at the bottom of the wellbore decreased to 10.6% when the proppant concentration was reduced from 2 to 1 ppg at a constant injection rate. The highest settling percentage of the 100 mesh sand reached 28% using a 1.5 ppg proppant concentration and a 20 gpm injection rate.

Settling percentage using the 2 SPF top bottom perforation.

At all proppant concentrations, the amount of sand that settled at the bottom of the horizontal wellbore decreased significantly as the injection rate increased. At increasing rates, the average settling percentages for the 100 mesh and 40/70 mesh were 24.8 and 18.8% at 20 gpm, 5.5 and 6% at 30 gpm, and 3.3 and 2.9% at 40 gpm, respectively. This could be explained by the high turbulence linked to the high injection rate, which assisted in suspending more sand particles for transportation to the toe cluster. The 2 SPF perforation configuration provided lower proppant settling compared with both 1 SPF top perforation and 1 SPF bottom perforation configurations.

Figure 5 illustrates the settling behavior of both sands using a 3 SPF perforation configuration. For the 40/70 mesh sand, the settling percentage varied from 11% at 1.5 and 50 gpm to 3% at 2 and 70 gpm.

Settling percentage using the 3 SPF perforation.

The amount of 40/70 mesh sand that settled at the bottom of the horizontal wellbore was slightly higher than that of the 100 mesh sand. At increasing rates, the average settling percentages for the 100 mesh and 40/70 mesh were 3.2 and 10.4% at 50 gpm, 5 and 8.9% at 60 gpm, and 3.8 and 4.4% at 70 gpm, respectively. According to the interpretation of the results, the gravitational effect of the larger particles of the 40/70 mesh sand was greater than that of the 100 mesh sand.

Ahmad and Miskimins26 investigated the settling behavior of the 40/70 mesh sand using the 4 SPF perforation configuration at injection rates of 35, 60, and 75 gpm. These tests were compared with those conducted on 100 mesh sand at injection rates of 50, 60, and 70 gpm by Alajmei.22Figure 6 shows the low sand settling behavior of the 100 mesh sand compared to that of the 40/70 mesh sand. For the 100 mesh sand, the highest settling percentage of 8% was observed at a proppant concentration of 0.5 ppg and an injection rate of 50 gpm, whereas the lowest settling percentage of 0.15% was observed at a proppant concentration of 1 ppg and an injection rate of 70 gpm. Meanwhile, the 40/70 mesh sand settled more in the wellbore with a maximum settling of 34.3% at a 2 ppg concentration and 35 gpm injection rate.

Settling percentage using 4 SPF perforations of 100 mesh and 40/70 mesh.

The settling percentage increased in the case of the 40/70 mesh size owing to the particle size and gravitational forces, which were more pronounced at higher proppant concentrations. At increasing rates, the average settling for the 100 mesh and 40/70 mesh were 6.3% at (35 gpm) and 24.7% at (50 gpm), 3.8% and 11.9% at 60 gpm, and 2% at (70 gpm) and 7.6% at (75 gpm), respectively.

In general, the larger-sized particles of the 40/70 mesh sand exhibit more settling at the bottom of the horizontal wellbore than the 100 mesh sand. For the same sand experiments, the design and orientation of the perforations were crucial. Designing the perforation with 1 SPF located at the top of the perforation leads to higher settling percentages compared to 1 SPF at the bottom of the horizontal wellbore. This is due to the fact that gravity drains most of the sand particles from the bottom perforations, especially when the sand particles are injected at low injection rates.

Proppant Distribution

The proppant distribution behavior of both the 100 mesh and 40/70 mesh sands was investigated by using various perforation designs.

1 SPF Top Perforation Configuration

The results in this section examine the proppant distribution among the three perforation clusters using the 1 SPF top perforation configuration. Top perforations from the three clusters were used to allow the slurry to leave the horizontal wellbore.

In general, the distribution of both sands at the three distinct injection rates (20, 30, and 40 gpm) was balanced. Figure 7a shows a comparable uniform distribution of proppant observed among the three perforation clusters, which used an injection rate of 20 gpm at a proppant concentration of 0.5 ppg. However, for all injection rates, when the proppant concentration increased, the proppant distribution was biased toward the toe cluster, as shown in Figures 7–9. Most of the proppant moved to the toe side owing to the large momentum acting on the particles. This made it difficult for proppants to divert into the first perforation cluster. The distance between the injection streamlines was decreased by increasing the injected proppant concentration at lower injection rates. This caused the injection velocity to increase, which helped move the additional proppant particles to the toe cluster.

The proppant distribution between the 40/70 mesh and 100 mesh sand follows a similar pattern when the results were compared with those of Alajmei and Miskimins.21 The lower sand concentration experiments showed a uniform distribution, and the higher concentration changed the behavior to a toe-biased distribution.

SPF Bottom Perforation Configuration

An uneven heel-biased distribution was observed when the bottom perforations were opened in each cluster. The momentum force produced by the injection rate was offset by the gravitational force acting on both sand particle sizes. A more imbalanced distribution was shown using the 1 SPF bottom perforation configuration compared with those observed using the 1 SPF top perforation configuration. The proppant left the horizontal wellbore at the heel rather than the toe at each injection rate (20, 30, and 40 gpm). Overall, as the injected proppant concentration increased, the proppant concentration that exited the heel cluster increased.

Owing to the strong gravitational effect, uniform proppant distributions could not be attained using this bottom perforation configuration at the evaluated injection rates and proppant concentrations, as shown in Figures 10–12. However, this completion design can be helpful if more proppants were to be allocated to the heel cluster.

SPF Perforation Configuration

The other set featured a configuration that used 3 SPF perforations. The experiments using this perforation arrangement used a higher range of injection rates. Each perforation cluster included an additional open perforation, which reduced the impact of the high back pressure on the pump and allowed it to achieve higher injection rates.

At the three different injection rates, the distributions of both the 100 mesh and 40/70 mesh sands were typically biased toward the toe cluster, as shown in Figures 16–18. The amount of proppant exiting the toe cluster increased as the injection rate increased. The majority of sand particles did not turn around the corners and exited the first two clusters (i.e., heel and middle) owing to the high inertia associated with the high injection rates.21

Applications

In hydraulic fracturing completions, proppant transport and distribution are crucial for enhancing the conductivity of the generated fractures. Therefore, it is essential to conduct field tests in addition to the laboratory experiments used in this study. This study showed that the perforation configuration and alignment along a horizontal wellbore have a substantial effect on the success of hydraulic fracturing treatments. Consequently, the associated fracture conductivity and well productivity may be affected.

The choice of proppant in hydraulic fracturing is a key factor that affects the economics of stimulation and field development. The decision is frequently influenced by estimations of the dimensionless flow capacity of the fracture and the stimulation cost. For unconventional reservoirs, the completion method of choice is a combination of a horizontal well and multistage propped fracture treatments. Since the hydraulic fracture surface area significantly affects productivity, hydraulic fracturing design for low-permeability reservoirs places greater emphasis on building fracture networks than on conductivity.27

Ahmad and Miskimins’s26 experimental results using 4 SPF configurations on 20/40 and 40/70 ultralight weight ceramic indicated very little settling inside the wellbore. Furthermore, a reasonably uniform distribution of the 20/40 mesh was observed across all perforation clusters. Slickwater fluids often have lower viscosities, which prevent the proppant from being properly suspended. This indicates that low-density proppants can be effective when used with these fluids, making them a potential choice for unconventional stimulation treatments. Recently, Dontsov20 developed a mathematical model to predict particle behavior in a horizontal wellbore. The model investigated the effects of inertia and drag forces associated with high injection rates in the field. The model predicts a high turning efficiency of the particles when the calculated efficiency is equal to 1. Consequently, the particles leave the horizontal wellbore and enter the perforated tunnel.

Ensuring that the proppant type can maximize the results of the fracturing process is one of the most crucial parameters to consider when optimizing the proppant distribution across all fracture clusters. The perforation configuration and injection rate are important aspects to consider for optimal proppant distribution results. Sand settling at the bottom of a horizontal wellbore is another critical component of the hydraulic fracturing design. Increased sand settling involves a comprehensive flowback cleaning of the wellbore and may cause a number of completion problems including corrosion and plugging. The factors that primarily affect how the sand settles are the injection rates, interactions between the particles, and interactions between the particles and the wellbore. According to the experimental findings, higher injection rates can lower the sand settling percentage at the bottom of the horizontal wellbore. The orientation of the perforation also affects the amount of sand that accumulates at the bottom of the wellbore. This needs to be considered when executing a hydraulic fracturing treatment.

Conclusions

Varying clusters and perforations within each cluster were found to have different distributions of the transported proppant. This information is useful for understanding transport in horizontal, multistage completions and how it affects the total treatment efficiency, particularly when limited-entry perforation techniques. In addition, this finding supports the idea that injection rates influence the proppant distribution. Furthermore, the outcomes of this study contradict the existing conclusion that low-viscosity carrier fluids are ineffective in achieving an equal distribution of proppants.
