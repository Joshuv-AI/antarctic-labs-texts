A targeted metabolic intervention via exercise applied after vaccination shapes kinetics and durability of serum antibody accompanied by a distinct immunometabolic signature: pilot study
Authors: Siddhant Kothadiya, Kaitlyn G. Holden, Gabriel P. Cutshaw, Tyanez C. Jones, Catarina Bittencourt de Almeida, Zoe Sirotiak, Jacob D. Meyer, Rizia Bardhan, Marian L. Kohut
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Vaccination under deliberately varied metabolic conditions provides a framework to identify metabolic determinants of immune response to vaccine and to identify metabolic pathways that may serve as immunomodulators or future adjuvant targets. Suboptimal vaccine immunogenicity and waning antibody durability remain significant public health challenges requiring effective interventions. To examine the association between vaccine immunogenicity and altered metabolic microenvironments, we applied a short-term metabolic intervention coinciding with SARS-CoV-2 immunization. In this randomized trial, half of the participants completed a metabolically targeted 90-min exercise bout immediately post-immunization, a stimulus known to induce sustained shifts in systemic metabolic changes for up to 24 h. Participants in the control group did not exercise after vaccination. Serum anti-RBD IgG antibody and a putative biomarker of germinal center activity (CXCL13) were assessed as measures of immunogenicity in longitudinally collected samples, and metabolic profile was analyzed by Raman spectroscopy. Associations between age, BMI, psychosocial stress and immune response and metabolic profiles were determined. The exercise-metabolic treatment enhanced antibody durability and shifted the kinetics of CXCL13 and serum antibody response in participants without pre-existing immunity. Specific immuno-metabolic signatures distinguished exercise-metabolic treatment from control, identified participants with pre-existing immunity, and revealed effects of age. This study demonstrates the feasibility of a readily accessible short-term metabolic intervention applied at vaccination in shaping in vivo adaptive immunity and provides critical insights into metabolic predictors of immunogenicity. These findings can be used to interrogate further host factor-related metabolic predictors of vaccine response (i.e., age, BMI, stress) and develop innovative vaccine platforms that influence the metabolic microenvironment.

Introduction

Vaccine-induced immunogenicity and durability remain ongoing challenges, as antibody responses vary by host factors and often wane over time. Vaccination also provides a powerful and clinically relevant human model to examine how metabolism modulates immune responses. The availability and allocation of metabolic resources are fundamental determinants of organismal survival and shape immune function. Under conditions of metabolic stress, resources available for host defense may be constrained, while immune activation itself induces substantial shifts in metabolic profiles to meet the energetic and biosynthetic demands of immunometabolic reprogramming (Chi, 2022; Lercher et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2019; Wensveen et al., 2024). An individual's metabolic state therefore strongly influences the development of lasting humoral and cellular immunity, making the characterization of immunometabolic signatures directly relevant for assessing vaccine efficacy. Although observational studies have reported associations between specific metabolites and vaccine immunogenicity (Dagla et al., 2022; de Souza Nogueira et al., 2023; He et al., 2022), interventional approaches that can more directly link metabolic variation to vaccine efficacy remain scarce. Whether a targeted exercise intervention that induces defined metabolic stress can enhance the magnitude or persistence of antibody responses is not well understood. Exercise represents a physiologically relevant metabolic stimulus that may modulate immune function, providing a framework to investigate the relationship between metabolic state and vaccine-induced immunogenicity.

In these experiments, we employed long-duration (90 min) moderate-intensity exercise as a defined metabolic stressor that alters metabolic homeostasis and nutrient availability for ≥24 h (Li et al., 2022; Magkos et al., 2009; Schranner et al., 2020). Exercise was performed immediately following SARS-CoV-2 mRNA vaccine to determine whether metabolic perturbations during the first 24 h of immune activation influence vaccine immunogenicity. These proof-of-concept studies provide direct evidence that physiological manipulation of metabolism at the time of vaccination alters antibody kinetics and durability, and results in distinct immunometabolic signatures consistent with altered immunometabolic reprogramming. This post-immunization timing was chosen based on evidence that differentially expressed genes associated with metabolism and innate immunity occur 1-3 days post-vaccination and predict longer-term humoral and T cell immunity across vaccine platforms (Arunachalam et al., 2021; Blengio et al., 2023; Bucasas et al., 2011). Therefore, we hypothesized that metabolic modulation at the onset of immune activation may shape immune memory, a premise supported by studies demonstrating that pharmacologic immunometabolic interventions (e.g., mTOR inhibitors or metformin) modify vaccine responses (Diaz et al., 2017; Mannick et al., 2014).

A critical factor for the experiments herein was the selection of a metabolic stressor capable of inducing substantial alterations to fuel availability persisting 24 h or longer. We utilized a long-duration exercise treatment because, unlike short-duration exercise (≤60 min), 90-120 min of moderate-intensity activity induces shifts toward lipid-based fuels, increases free fatty acids, ketones, and gluconeogenic substrates, and produces persistent alterations in circulating metabolites for at least 24 h (Hearris et al., 2018; Lundsgaard et al., 2020; Magkos et al., 2009; Schranner et al., 2020; Watt et al., 2002). Therefore, early immune activation occurs in a metabolic environment characterized by modest energy deficit, increased AMPK signaling, decreased mTOR activity, and elevated lipid-derived metabolites. Dendritic cell (DC) activation and trafficking to vaccine draining lymph nodes occurs within this 24hr period, and changes in extracellular lactate, fatty acids, or TCA intermediates can alter DC function (Wculek et al., 2020). Importantly, exercise-induced metabolic changes are detectable in secondary lymphoid organs (Rundqvist et al., 2020), coinciding with dendritic cell (DC) activation, trafficking to draining lymph nodes, and early T cell and TFH cell priming (Linterman and Hill, 2016). Because metabolites can serve as signaling molecules that influence immune cell functions and fate (Baker and Rutter, 2023; Kedia-Mehta and Finlay, 2019) and recent evidence supports the concept that nutrients serve as a new “Signal 4” in activation of adaptive immunity (Raynor and Chi, 2024), we propose that this altered metabolic milieu modulates interactions between DCs, TFH cells, and B cells, thereby influencing germinal center (GC) formation, antibody quality and durability (Linterman and Hill, 2016). Prior work shows that antibody responses are enhanced following long-duration – but not short-duration – exercise following vaccination (Hallam et al., 2022; Long et al., 2012). Another approach involving eccentric exercise of the muscle near the time of vaccine injection also results in increased antibody but likely operates via a different mechanism potentially involving danger signals released by minor muscle damage (Edwards et al., 2007, 2010, 2012). Prior work evaluated inactivated vaccines, whereas the present study examines an mRNA vaccine platform, allowing assessment of whether similar exercise-associated effects are observed across different vaccine types. As a means of non-invasively assessing immunogenicity beyond serum antibody levels, we measured CXCL13 as putative biomarker of germinal center activation that may reflect T follicular helper cell (TFH) activity (Havenar-Daughton et al., 2016).

Although metabolite associations with antibody responses have been reported (Dagla et al., 2022; de Souza Nogueira et al., 2023; He et al., 2022) how host factors such as age, BMI, and psychosocial variables influence these metabolic responses remains poorly defined. Here we leverage Raman spectroscopy (RS), a highly sensitive optical technique that captures integrated metabolic signatures and has been widely applied by our group and others to characterize metabolic shifts across physiological and disease contexts (Huefner et al., 2020; Kothadiya et al., 2024; Mathew et al., 2024; Vanna et al., 2024; Cutshaw et al., 2023). The aim of this study was to determine whether a metabolically targeted exercise bout administered immediately post-immunization enhances the magnitude and durability of vaccine-induced antibody responses. The predefined primary outcome was serum antibody response measured longitudinally for up to 6 months post-immunization. A predefined secondary outcome was serum metabolic profiling, included to evaluate associations between exercise-induced metabolic responses and antibody outcomes. We hypothesized that the metabolically targeted exercise intervention would enhance antibody response over time, and that associations between metabolites and antibody response would differ by treatment group.

Experimental methods

Research participants

Research participants were recruited via advertisements posted in the local community as well as through email sent directly to university staff and students, or to community organizations that schedule group immunization clinics. A total of 50 individuals (aged 18-62 years) who received the initial SARS-CoV-2 vaccine (Pfizer BNT162b2 n = 37; Moderna mRNA-1273 n = 13) were enrolled in the study. Primary outcome of antibody titer dictated minimum sample size as based on power calculations from previous work (Hallam et al., 2022). For the analysis, antibody response to influenza vaccine (A/California/7/09) single antigen vaccine was compared between the 90min exercise group to no exercise. The effect size using this data set was considered large by Cohen's criteria. With an alpha = 0.05 and the sample size used in this study of 8-9 per group, statistical power is > 0.90. In terms of a conservative approach, we estimate that the effect size of exercise for the mRNA-based may be smaller and there may be twice the variability in response across ages. With these conservative estimates and a smaller effect size of 1.2 fold difference in antibody which may be considered biologically relevant, an n = 24 per group still yields statistical power >0.80, A CONSORT diagram shows participant flow throughout the study (SI Fig. 1). Inclusion criteria for participants included regular participation in moderate to vigorous-intensity exercise two or more times per week, with at least one session lasting a minimum of 50 min for the previous six months, and age <65. Although restricting enrollment to individuals with prior exercise experience and under age 65 limits generalizability, this criterion was implemented to ensure participant safety in accordance with guidelines from the American College of Sports Medicine, given that the exercise intervention was performed outside of a medically supervised setting. Participation guidelines also followed the American College of Sports Medicine recommendations for pre-participation health screening and recommendations for activity and initiating an exercise program (Riebe et al., 2015). The International Physical Activity (IPAQ) Short Form was completed by participants at the first visit and used to quantify overall physical activity (Craig et al., 2003). Participants were excluded from the study if they were pregnant, had an immune condition that would be expected to impact the variables of interest in the study, were taking a medication that significantly alters immune response, or were unwilling to donate blood. All procedures involving human subjects were approved by the Institutional Review Board at Iowa State University (IRB 21-073-00).

Randomization and exercise conditions

Prior to immunization, participants were randomly assigned to either an exercise metabolic intervention (n = 25) or no exercise control group (n = 25). Participants were assigned using a restricted block randomization approach with a maximum block size of 20 to ensure balanced group allocation. Within each block, assignments were generated using a simple random mechanism (coin toss) until one group reached 10 participants; subsequent allocations within that block were assigned to the alternate group to maintain equal group sizes. Participants that were assigned to the exercise metabolic intervention group performed 90 min of light to moderate-intensity exercise within a controlled heart rate range on the day of their first SARS-CoV-2 mRNA vaccine dose, while participants in the control group were instructed to go about their daily routine but to avoid exercise. All participants, regardless of group, were asked to avoid exercise on the day of their second vaccine dose. The objective of this study was to evaluate the effect of the exercise intervention on the primary immune response to vaccination, rather than on recall responses following booster immunization, and therefore, exercise was applied only after the initial immunization. Compliance with the no-exercise requirement on the day of the first immunization (control group) and on the day of the second immunization (all participants) was assessed by participant self-report; objective activity monitoring devices (e.g., accelerometers) were not used. Exercise consisted of walking/jogging a designated route outdoors near the location where participants were vaccinated in the local community. Participants were not fasted prior to exercise or immunization. Exercise participants were encouraged to hydrate prior to exercise, and water was freely available as needed during the exercise session. Exercise sessions were conducted outdoors under typical environmental conditions (spring and early summer), with access to an indoor walking track that could be used if necessary, during periods of inclement weather to maintain consistency and participant safety. Exercise commenced within 30 min after receiving the vaccine. Participants were monitored throughout the active period with heart rate and Rating of Perceived Exertion (RPE) recorded approximately every 10 min. Manual pulse checks throughout exercise conducted by research personnel were used to monitor heart rate and watches with heart rate measures were worn by participants. Participants were asked to target a heart rate zone of approximately 120 - 140 bpm which is consistent with the average heart rate targeted in influenza vaccine studies (Hallam et al., 2022) and equates to 60 - 70% of heart rate max (as based on the formula: Target Heart Rate = (Maximum Heart Rate - Resting Heart Rate) x % Intensity + Resting Heart Rate). Exercise intensity was monitored with the target RPE being from “light” to “somewhat hard”. The RPE was used to adjust exercise intensity for any participants treated with beta-blocker medications, rather than heart rate.

Blood/serum collection, immunization, surveys, and timeline

Whole blood (approximately 30 mL) was collected from the antecubital vein of participants at five timepoints during the study. A pre-immunization sample was collected within the week preceding immunization (timepoint A). Immunizations were administered in the local community (schools, clinics, pharmacies, or worksite). Following their first vaccine dose, participants returned at 2-week post immunization (timepoint B). Additional samples were collected at 1-week post second vaccine dose (i.e., 4 weeks post first dose) (timepoint C), and three- (timepoint D) and six-months (timepoint E) following the first vaccine dose. A laboratory-based respiratory viral screening panel was not performed prior to study visits, but participants completed a symptom checklist and were asked to reschedule visits if ill. One participant assigned to the control group did not complete immunization and return for post-immunization blood collection (total n = 49 for post-immunization blood samples). Following blood collection, blood was allowed to clot for 45 min at room temperature, and then centrifuged at 900 g for 15 min. The serum was stored at −80°C until subsequent analysis. The primary outcome of the clinical trial was antibody titer over time.

All participants received side effect report forms listing the potential side effects described on the emergency use authorization for their respective vaccine (i.e., Pfizer BNT162b2 or Moderna mRNA-1273). The side effect checklist included injection site pain, tiredness, headache, muscle pain, chills, joint pain, fever, injection site swelling, injection site redness, nausea, and swollen lymph nodes. The presence of side effects was recorded every 24 h for the first 72 h following immunization, for both dose 1 and dose 2, by checking each side effect experienced. A score of “1” was assigned each day a given side effect was recorded such that scores ranged from “0” (side effect does not present on any day) to “3” (side effect present on each of the first three days following immunization) for each potential side effect. At each study visit, health updates were recorded including recent COVID-19 infection. Participants were instructed to complete the 14-item Perceived Stress Scale (Cohen et al., 1983) and the 25-item Resilience Scale (Wagnild and Young, 1993) on the day before or the day of vaccination.

Serum antibody detection

Serum antibody levels against SARS-CoV-2 Spike RBD proteins were measured via Enzyme-linked Immunosorbent Assay. Briefly, high-binding 96-well flat-bottom plates (Immulon 2 HB) were coated with SARS-CoV-2 (2019-nCoV) Spike RBD(Y453F)-His recombinant protein (Sino Biological cat#40592-V08H80) and incubated overnight at 4°C. Plates were blocked with 3% milk in PBS-T (PBS with 0.05% TWEEN 20) for 3 h at room temperature and washed with PBS-T. Serum samples were plated in duplicate in a 2.5-fold dilution series for 12 dilutions to determine endpoint antibody titer, starting with a 1:2500 dilution of sample. Plates were incubated for 90 min at room temperature, washed with PBS-T, and incubated 90 min with HRP-conjugated goat anti-human IgG (Invitrogen cat#A18811) at a concentration of 0.5 μg/mL Plates were washed with PBS-T and developed with tetramethylbenzidine (TMB + One-Step Substrate; Agilent Dako cat#S160185) for 15 min at room temperature in the dark then stopped with 1N H2SO4. Optical density was assessed at 450 nm on a SpectraMax iD3 microplate reader (Molecular Devices). Antibody data was summarized by geometric mean titers (GMTs) based on logarithmically transformed data. A positive control (sera from recent infection) and negative control (pooled pre-pandemic sera) were included with all runs. Inter-day coefficient of variation was calculated from controls (%CV < 15). Serum antibody dilution endpoint from Timepoints A, B, C, D, and E were used to calculate area under the curve (AUC) and summed to determine total AUC for each participant. In some analyses of serum antibody, the samples from individuals classified as pre-immune prior to study onset, or from participants that were infected with SARS-CoV-2 prior to the final blood collection were excluded. Infection was assessed by self-report. In addition to self-report, all participant samples were analyzed for presence of antibodies to SARS-CoV-2 Nucleocapsid protein (NP) IgG antibody in serum using the LEGEND MAX™ SARS-CoV-2 Nucleocapsid Protein ELISA Kit (Biolegend). Individuals classified as pre-immune had OD values > 1.0 in the Nucleocapsid Protein ELISA assay, and showed a recall-type response upon immunization with the first vaccine dose. The antibody response for recall response was assessed with both the assay described above and a commercial ELISA kit (GenScript SARS-CoV-2 Spike S1-RBD IgG&IgM ELISA Detection Kit, Cat. No. L00845) targeting the receptor-binding domain (RBD) of the ancestral SARS-CoV-2 spike protein at a 1:100 serum dilution. Results were consistent across assays to identify participants with likely pre-existing immunity. Differences in serum antibody responses between participants with and without pre-existing immunity, as determined by the GenScript assay, are shown in Supplementary Fig. S1. Study personnel that carried out the assays were blinded to treatment group.

CXCL13 and cytokines in serum

Serum samples at timepoints A, B and C were used to assess serum CXCL13 concentration. CXCL13 in blood serum was measured using an Enzyme-linked Immunosorbent Assay (Human CXCL13/BLC/BCA-1 Quantikine ELISA kit DCX130; Biotechne R&D Systems Inc. Minneapolis, MN, 55413 USA). All samples were analyzed in duplicate on four plates and values were accepted if CV<10% (per the manufacturer's recommendation). Study personnel that assessed CXCL13 were blinded to treatment group. Serum cytokines at timepoint A were measured using the MILLIPLEX® Human High Sensitivity T Cell Panel Premixed 17-plex - Immunology Multiplex Assay (Millipore-Sigma, Burlington MA). Manufacturers instructions were followed. Results were analyzed using the Bio-Plex Multiplex Instrument System (Bio-Rad).

Raman sample preparation and analysis

Post-thawing, the serum was diluted in an equal volume of milliQ water, with 3 μL of the resulting solution aliquoted onto a 2 mm diameter calcium fluoride substrate. This was then dried for 30 min at 37°C. A Renishaw inVia Raman confocal microscope was used with a 785 nm laser to take scans of the dried serum film. The spectrophotometer was calibrated daily using a standard silicon wafer at 520.5 nm. The samples were measured under a 50× objective with a power of 40 mW at sample, a grating of 1200 lines per mm, a single accumulation, and an exposure time of 10 s. 100 static scans for each sample were conducted with by line maps in the WIRE 5.5 software. Cosmic ray artifacts were removed using Renishaw's WiRE 5.5 cosmic ray removal algorithm. A custom MATLAB code (R2021b) was then employed to apply baseline correction, smoothing, and standard normal variate (SNV) normalization to the raw spectra. The spectra were smoothed using a Savitzky-Golay filter with a 10th order polynomial and a frame width of 45 points. Background fluorescence was removed using a modified polyfit method with a 9th order polynomial and a threshold value of 0.0001 (Zhao et al., 2007). All spectra were min-maxed normalized to the maximum peak at 1447 cm−1, resulting in normalized intensities ranging between 0 and 1. This peak was selected for normalization due to its minimal variation across samples.

Given the high dimensionality of Raman data, a robust dimensionality reduction method was required. t-distributed Stochastic Neighbor Embedding (tSNE), a non-linear and unsupervised multivariate dimensionality reduction and clustering machine learning method, was used for this purpose. Prior to tSNE, principal component analysis (PCA) was used to reduce noise in the data by selecting for the top principal components, forming a PCA-tSNE technique. The number of PCA components and perplexity used in tSNE varied as noted in the figure captions. tSNE enables visualization of data clustering and separation based on similarity and dissimilarity, respectively, in the original higher-dimensional space (Hinton and Roweis, 2002). In the tSNE plots, each point represents a single participant's pooled and normalized Raman + antibody titer data.

MetaboAnalyst 6.0

The web-based platform MetaboAnalyst 6.0 was employed for the analysis of metabolic data and facilitated the generation of figures for correlation analysis, enrichment analysis, and correlation heatmaps. The “Statistical Analysis (metadata)” module was utilized to conduct correlation analysis using the Pearson r moment, correlating RS peaks with BMI and age. Correlation heatmaps were produced using the “Statistical Analysis (one factor)” module, employing the default parameters of MetaboAnalyst 6.0, such as the Pearson r distance measure. The “Enrichment Analysis” module was used in conjunction with the KEGG pathway, the ID type as compound names, and the feature type as metabolites to perform quantitative enrichment analysis. For the enrichment analysis, individual Raman peak assignments (corresponding to different metabolites) and the peak intensities for all participants in this study were uploaded into MetaboAnalyst.

Statistical analysis

The endpoint dilution titer was determined for serum antibody samples and converted to geometric mean titer (GMT) over time measures for all participants. A general linear model with repeated measures compared antibody titer over the blood collection time points with vaccine type, pre-immune status, and treatment condition as between-subjects' factors, time as within-subjects factor, and age as covariate as the initial analysis (IBM SPSS software). Because pre-existing immunity and vaccine type significantly influenced antibody responses, and significant interactions among time, vaccine type, pre-existing immunity, and treatment were identified in the repeated-measures analysis, treatment effects were subsequently evaluated separately within each vaccine type among participants without pre-existing immunity. This stratified approach was used to isolate the effect of exercise on the primary immune response, independent of recall responses associated with prior immunity. Area under the curve (AUC) analysis was then used in follow-up analyses to generate a single interpretable summary value per subject that reflects both the magnitude and duration of antibody response over time. Age was included as a covariate in the AUC models. Because treatment effects on total AUC were evaluated separately within each vaccine type among participants without pre-existing immunity, two related comparisons were performed. To control for the increased risk of type I error associated with these multiple comparisons, false discovery rate (FDR) correction using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure was applied to p-values obtained from treatment versus control comparisons within each vaccine type. Adjusted p-values were considered significant at an FDR threshold of q < 0.05. CXCL13 concentration was determined as the difference from pre-immunization to adjust for age-related baseline differences in CXCL13. A general linear model with vaccine type, treatment group and age as a covariate was analyzed in all participants and separately in those without pre-existing immunity for CXCL13 assessment at each time point. Data presented in the manuscript are shown as the standard error of the mean, standard deviation, and/or individual data points as indicated in each figure legend. Figure legends report F values, degrees of freedom and p values. For the Raman analysis we measured a total of 49 samples each for three timepoints – Pre-immunization (timepoint A), 2-week post vaccine dose 1 (timepoint B), and 1-week post vaccine dose 2 (timepoint C) to have biological replicates. We measured 100 points per sample as technical replicates. An unpaired, two-sided, heteroscedastic student's t-test was used to calculate p-values, where ∗: p < 0.05, ∗∗: p < 0.01, and ∗∗∗: p < 0.001.

Results

Participants

Individuals aged 18-64 years old and planning to receive the SARS-CoV-2 mRNA vaccination, either Pfizer BNT162b2 or Moderna mRNA-1273, were recruited for participation in this pilot study. All participants (n = 50) were initially screened, enrolled, and serum blood samples were collected for baseline measures (anti-RBD IgG, CXCL13, Metabolites). At the baseline measure visit, participants were given two psychosocial surveys, the 14-item Perceived Stress Scale(Cohen et al., 1983) and the Resilience Scale(Wagnild and Young, 1993). Participants were instructed to complete the surveys on the day before or the day of immunization. As indicated in Fig. 1, blood samples were collected at four additional timepoints. As indicated in Fig. 1, the 90-min metabolic intervention (exercise) was administered within 30 min after receiving vaccine dose 1 for participants in the exercise group (n = 25), whereas no-exercise control (n = 25) did not exercise. Neither group exercised after dose 2. Serum was collected at five timepoints relative to immunization (Fig. 1). The subject characteristics for each treatment group (Table 1) show no significant differences between exercise and non-exercise control participants at baseline with respect to age, BMI, or overall physical activity level (IPAQ) (Craig et al., 2003). A subset of participants (78%) was considered to have no pre-existing immunity to SARS-CoV2 based on serum anti-RBD IgG titer at pre-immunization (timepoint A), the relative change from pre-immunization to two weeks after the initial SARS-CoV-2 vaccine (timepoint B; reflective of primary versus recall response), and the presence of antibodies against SARS-CoV-2 nucleocapsid protein, along with self-report of infection. The subject characteristics for the select group of participants without pre-existing immunity also showed no significant difference between exercise and non-exercise (SI Table 1). The distance covered in the 90-min exercise metabolic intervention ranged from 3.75 to 10.7 miles illustrating the wide range of fitness levels in participants. Although exercise distance varied, the relative workload per person as based on heart rate was held constant such that all exercise participants completed the same intensity of exercise relative to maximal intensity for 90 min.

Serum antibody response to immunization and effect of the physiological metabolic intervention (exercise)

The serum anti-RBD IgG antibody geometric mean titer in response to the initial mRNA SARS-CoV-2 vaccine series was assessed at timepoints A-E as indicated in Fig. 1. A general linear model fit the data from all subjects, and the primary analysis of antibody titer across the serum collection time points included effects for treatment (exercise or no exercise), vaccine type, pre-immune status, time relative to vaccination, and age as covariate (Fig. 2 legend describes statistical results of overall model). Individuals identified as having pre-existing immunity (likely due to prior SARS-CoV-2 infection) exhibited higher antibody titers pre-immunization and post-immunization (Fig. 2a). Antibody response varied by type of mRNA vaccine. Participants that received the Moderna vaccine demonstrated higher antibody titers at all post-immunization timepoints compared to Pfizer vaccine recipients (Fig. 2b). These findings align well with a recent study (Srivastava et al., 2024) that showed (i) participants with pre-existing immunity (i.e. previously infected with SARS-CoV-2) had higher titer responses compared with those that did not have pre-existing immunity; (ii) higher antibody titers in the Moderna vaccine recipients compared with the Pfizer recipients, which may be due to higher mRNA content in Moderna vaccine. To analyze the effects of the exercise metabolic intervention on antibody response without the confounding factors of prior infection and vaccine type, and to isolate the effect of exercise on the primary immune response, independent of recall responses associated with prior immunity, follow up analyses compared the effect of treatment for each vaccine. Area under the curve (AUC) analysis was used in these follow-up analyses to yield a single interpretable summary value per subject. Treatment effects on AUC were evaluated separately within vaccine conditions. To account for multiple comparisons across these analyses, false discovery rate correction was applied. For recipients of the Pfizer vaccine, exercise treatment significantly increased AUC calculated up to six months’ post-immunization (Fig. 2c) (FDR adjusted p = 0.032). For recipients of Moderna vaccine, analyses were considered exploratory due to the small sample size; however, the direction of the treatment effect was similar to Pfizer vaccine response (FDR-adjusted p = 0.123) (Fig. 2d). AUC plots for each group and each vaccine are shown in Fig. 2e and f. Because age was included as a covariate in the primary analyses and was significant, treatment effects within middle-aged (44–62 years) and young (18–43 years) participants are presented descriptively in separate plots for those without pre-existing immunity and immunized with Pfizer vaccine (SI Figure S3), to facilitate visualization of the data. However, formal statistical comparisons were not performed for these age-stratified subgroups due to limited sample sizes, including only one middle-aged participant in the Moderna vaccine group. Across both vaccines, the relative magnitude of increased antibody durability due to exercise at 6 months post-immunization, approximately ∼5 AUC units, was comparable to the difference between the higher dose vaccine, Moderna mRNA-1273 containing 100μgmRNA, and the lower dose vaccine Pfizer BNT162b2 with 30 μg mRNA (Fig. 2e and f); and this vaccine antigen dose difference has previously been shown to be clinically relevant (Ioannou et al., 2022). A separate analysis of Timepoints A, B, C, and D was carried out with the GenScript commercial ELISA and results were highly correlated with the in-house assay, with correlations reported in Table S2. There was no difference in pre-immunization antibody concentration between exercise and control participants without pre-existing immunity for either Pfizer or Moderna vaccine recipients, as expected (SI Figure S3). Raman metabolic profile distinguishes between high antibody responders and poor responders and reveals separate profiles for participants with pre-existing immunity

Next, metabolic profiling with Raman spectroscopy (RS) was performed to understand (i) the extent to which metabolite profiles reflect immune activation and vaccine response, (ii) how these profiles are impacted by a metabolic intervention i.e. exercise delivered immediately after immunization, and (iii) how these profiles correlate to other factors including participant age and BMI. RS spectra were collected for Timepoints A, B, and C in sera, and the metabolic peaks imaged by RS were correlated to age, BMI, and normalized antibody titer. The Raman peak assignments and their associated vibrational modes are shown in SI Table S3; each peak was tentatively assigned to metabolites based on well-established Raman references on biological samples such as blood, and blood components noted in the reference list of SI Table S3. Our prior studies have confirmed the identify of these peaks to relevant metabolites through serum and cell media spiking studies and validated with mass spectrometry metabolomics (Cutshaw et al., 2024; Ghazvini et al., 2024; Mathew et al., 2024). Metabolic studies in the literature focused on exercise and immunity were also reviewed to confirm the relevance of the identified metabolites (Movasaghi et al., 2007; Zhu et al., 2011). To compare immunogenicity and metabolite profile, we selected three participants from the non-exercise control group that showed the highest antibody response (best responders) at Timepoint A and B, and three others that showed the lowest response (poor responders) all from the non-exercise control group. The participants were restricted to the non-exercise control group so that the differences between best and poor responder could be examined without the confounding influence of the exercise intervention. This subset comparison analysis was studied to understand how distinct antibody-response phenotypes were accompanied by differences in Raman spectral patterns. Age was represented across best and poor responders such that each group included an older adult (age 60-64), a middle-aged adult (age 40-50), and a young adult (age 18-35). The Raman spectra averaged for the best and poor responders to immunization (Fig. 4a) at timepoint B showed significant differences in peak intensities. The changes observed in the Raman spectra were also reflected in the difference spectra (Fig. 4b), obtained by subtracting the spectra corresponding to timepoint A from that of timepoint B. The difference spectra show greater metabolic differences in the best responders over time compared to the poor responders. Despite heterogeneity in participants age and BMI, the Raman difference spectra showed distinct patterns between the selected best and poor responders. Key Raman metabolites were identified for poor responders (Fig. 4c) and best responders (Fig. 4d) that changed between timepoint A and B including amino acids (glutamic acid: 878 cm−1, histidine: 1316 cm−1), lipids and FAs (1080 cm−1), saturated long-chain FAs (1174 cm−1), unsaturated FAs (1264 cm−1), and proteins (amide III: 1248 cm−1). These metabolites changed significantly in the best responders but changed minimally in the poor responders, consistent with a relationship between metabolic shifts and antibody-response phenotype. We also queried whether RS would distinguish between participants with and without pre-existing immunity after immunization. RS measurements at timepoint B showed significant differences between participants with pre-existing immunity to SARS-CoV-2 compared to those classified as naïve (SI Figure S7a). The corresponding difference spectra (SI Figure S7b) shows several metabolites are altered among those with pre-existing immunity. This is not unexpected as timepoint B likely reflects a recall response in these individuals rather than a primary response. Based on these findings, we conjectured that participants’ collective immunometabolism profile could show differences in participants with pre-existing immunity as further observed in the Raman spectra, comparing not previously infected naïve to participants with pre-existing immunity (Fig. 4e), and difference spectra (Fig. 4f). To visualize differences in the groups, a t-distributed Stochastic Neighbor Embedding (tSNE) and principal component analysis (PCA), a PCA-tSNE technique was used. This approach included both the normalized Raman data and antibody titer data together for the participants with pre-existing immunity vs. those without pre-existing immunity. The PCA-tSNE visualization, when comparing between timepoint A (pre-immunization) (Fig. 4g), timepoint B (2-week post dose 1) (Fig. 4h) and timepoint C (1-week post dose 2) (Fig. 4i), shows a group-level separation at timepoint B. To complement the tSNE visualization, PCA was also performed on the combined immunometabolic dataset (SI Figure S6). The corresponding PERMANOVA results are included in the caption for each PCA plot. This trend in timepoint B suggests a distinct metabolic profile in a primary immune response (naïve participants) compared to those with pre-existing immunity. Studies have shown that following immunization, individuals with prior SARS-CoV-2 infection have increased memory B cells and exhibit greater serum antibody responses compared to those without prior infection (Krammer et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2021). In contrast, at timepoint C, all participants have some pre-existing immunity, either vaccine-induced or a hybrid immunity of vaccine and prior infection, and thus metabolite profiles are not as easy to differentiate. Immunization following recovery from a SARS-CoV-2 infection, or “hybrid immunity,” has been reported to enhance both the potency and breadth of the humoral response to SARS-CoV-2 (Bates et al., 2022). From the spectral and antibody trends in our study and those observed in the literature we conclude that the immunometabolic profile of participants with prior immunity to SARS-CoV-2 significantly differs from naive. Therefore, those participants were excluded from further analysis such that we can determine the immune and metabolic underpinnings that result from exercise without confounding influence from prior infection.

A physiological metabolic intervention (exercise) immediately after immunization changes the immunometabolic signature two weeks’ post-immunization

We measured the serum Raman peak intensities associated with metabolites and biochemical species in participants that completed the 90-min exercise metabolic intervention relative to those who did not (Fig. 5a). Raman spectra showed notable changes in multiple metabolites at timepoint B (Fig. S8–S10) that is also reflected in the corresponding difference spectra between timepoint A and B (Fig. 5b). PCA-tSNE clustering (Fig. 5c–e) for the exercise treatment and non-exercise control subjects show a group-level separation at timepoint B (Fig. 5d), demarcated by dotted lines. Each point in the PCA-tSNE plots represents a single participant's pooled and normalized Raman + antibody titer data. Note that some overlap between the two groups is expected since considerable heterogeneity exists across both groups with a wide range in age, BMI, and activity level, which are all factors that may influence metabolic response. To complement the tSNE visualization, PCA was also performed on the combined immunometabolic dataset (SI Figure S11). The PCA score plot for PC1 versus PC2 corresponding to Fig. 5d, showed group-level structure between exercise and non-exercise participants at timepoint B, and PERMANOVA on the PCA scores supported a significant multivariate difference between groups (F = 5.588, R2 = 0.134, p = 0.008; 999 permutations). To elucidate the impact of exercise on vaccine-induced activation of immune cells and resulting metabolite shifts, the key metabolites for which a significant difference between exercise and no exercise control was found are shown in Fig. 5f, across timepoints A, B, and C. The trends for other groups of metabolites are provided in the SI (SI Figures S12 – S15). The profile for these individual metabolites aligns well with the PCA-tSNE analyses which indicate that significant differences are only observed at timepoint B (Fig. 5d). Note that the exercise metabolic intervention took place two weeks prior to timepoint B; therefore, it is likely that differences in metabolites at timepoint B do not capture the metabolic changes that occurred immediately post exercise but instead represent a differential immune activation profile and immunometabolic signature that was induced by exercise. This also explains the lack of a difference in metabolites at timepoint C as the exercise metabolic intervention was only applied after the first vaccine dose. We observe lower Raman peak intensities assigned to lipids including cholesterol, (700 cm−1) and phospholipids (PC/PE) (722 cm−1) and higher intensity for the glucose/mannose associated peak (1125 cm−1) in the exercise group at timepoint B. A decline in serum concentration for cholesterol and phospholipids may reflect increased uptake or turnover. A role for cholesterol involving sterol regulatory element-binding protein (SREBP) in vaccine-induced antibody and TFH cell response has been demonstrated using a systems vaccinology approach, with further evidence showing that SREBP signaling is required for germinal center formation and antibody (Li et al., 2017; Luo et al., 2023). Phosphatidylcholine is required for optimal GC B cell response and the number of antibody-secreting cells (Brewer et al., 2016). The significant difference in cholesterol and phosphatidylcholine may reflect a greater magnitude of TFH cell expansion and germinal center response represented by the increase in CXCL13, and/or a shift in the kinetics of these responses in the exercise group. Germinal center formation typically peaks ∼2 weeks' post-immunization (Elsner and Shlomchik, 2020) corresponding to our timepoint B. The bioenergetic requirements for GC B cells vary over time, with a requirement for adequate glucose for glycolysis at 7 days' post-immunization (Jellusova et al., 2017) but heavy reliance on fatty acid oxidation with minimal glycolysis at 2 weeks' post-immunization (Weisel et al., 2020). CD4+ TFH cells are also essential in GC formation and regulating clonal selection, and the differentiation of memory and antibody-secreting B cells (Nettelfield et al., 2023); with early activation potentially supported by glycolysis (Chen et al., 2022), but rely more heavily on mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation in the lymph node environment with increased metabolic flexibility under conditions of limited nutrient availability (Mayberry et al., 2022). By two-weeks post-immunization (timepoint B), the substantial requirements for glucose uptake and glycolysis may have lessened with shifts towards memory over effector function, which may be reflected in the greater glucose/mannose Raman peak intensities in the exercise group. Metabolite associations with antibody response defined by Raman spectroscopy

Our findings show that RS metabolic data aligns well with trends in serum antibody titer which suggests that there is likely a direct correlation of serum metabolites to immunity, and the observed differences in response to immunization results from a culmination of “immunometabolic” shifts. To support this hypothesis, the change in intensity of metabolites from timepoint A to B was correlated to the antibody titer at timepoint B (Titer B) and change in CXCL13 from timepoint A to B in both the exercise and non-exercise groups (Fig. 6a). The heatmaps for the non-exercise and exercise group for other timepoints and their corresponding correlation ratios are provided in the SI (SI Figure S16 – S19; SI Table S6 – S13). In the heatmaps, blue cells denote an inverse correlation, and red cells indicate a direct correlation; values between ± (0–0.29) suggest a weak correlation, ± (0.30–0.50) indicate a moderate correlation, and ± (0.51–1) denote a strong correlation of variates to each other. Our findings show that the correlations between metabolite shifts and antibody titer at timepoint B (Titer B) or change in CXCL13 differ for the non-exercise and exercise groups. In the non-exercise group, we find an inverse correlation of antibody titer to monosaccharides (1102 cm−1), glucose/mannose (1125 cm−1), saturated FAs (1064 cm−1), lipids & FAs (1080 cm−1), and amino acids (tryptophan 1555 cm−1). Each of these trends is either completely reversed or dampened in the exercise group. A potential explanation for the observed metabolite profiles is a difference in the kinetics of B cell expansion and plasma cell antibody production between groups at two weeks post-immunization. Peak uptake of metabolites from serum may have already taken place if the kinetics of cell expansion were accelerated in the exercise group, as suggested by increased antibody (Fig. 2), and the inverse correlation in the control group may represent ongoing active utilization of metabolites. A similar explanation with respect to a slight shift in kinetics of immune response may hold true for CXCL13. We find that serum CXCL13 change from timepoint A to B has moderate joint correlations to many of the metabolites (phenylalanine, proline, thymine, tyrosine, arginine, etc.) in the non-exercise control group, which are significantly dampened in the exercise group. The correlation of metabolite changes to participant age, BMI, stress, and resilience scores was also assessed, with all these factors being normalized through a min-max method between 0 and 1. Age is inversely correlated with antibody titer at timepoint B (Titer B), as expected, consistent with the findings in Fig. S2. Participant age also has a joint correlation to citric acid and threonine, both required for immune cell activation (discussed earlier) in the non-exercise group, which is dampened in the exercise group. These trends suggest that these metabolites may be effectively consumed for immune function in participants who exercised immediately post-immunization. Our findings show that metabolites impact antibody response to immunization (or vice versa) as recently documented by others (Dagla et al., 2022; de Souza Nogueira et al., 2023); therefore, such “immunometabolic signatures” should be collectively considered as a measure of immune activation. Participant BMI also shows a joint correlation to many of the metabolites in the non-exercise group, which are either dampened or reversed in the exercise group. This finding further suggests the benefits of exercise in modulating metabolites that participate in immune activation as well as humoral response.

Baseline stress levels did not significantly influence the metabolic response to immunization, but it had a moderate direct correlation with BMI in the non-exercise group, and moderate inverse correlation to carotenoids specifically in the exercise group. However, participant resilience scores demonstrated clearer associations. In the non-exercise group, resilience pre-immunization was associated with moderate direct correlation to multiple amino acids (phenylalanine, tyrosine and arginine), but moderate inverse correlation to glutamic acid and phospholipids. Each of these trends is either completely reversed or dampened in the exercise group. Resilience also exhibited a moderate positive correlation with carotenoids in the exercise group. Although direct evidence linking resilience, carotenoids, and immunization outcomes is limited, the pattern observed here aligns with established roles of the antioxidant properties of carotenoids in immune regulation and psychological resilience (Medina-García et al., 2025; Rasmus and Kozłowska, 2023; Stringham et al., 2018). Collectively, these findings suggest that serum amino acid profiles could serve as metabolic indicators of resilience and targets for interventions aimed at stress buffering (Cervenka et al., 2017; Nagatsu, 2006; Popoli et al., 2012).

Further, the change in intensity of metabolites post-immunization was correlated to the chemokines/cytokines at pre-immunization levels (Timepoint A) (Fig. 6b; SI Table S14 and S15). In the non-exercise group, several baseline cytokines (CXCL11, CX3CL1, IFNγ, IL-6, TNFα, IL-1β, CCL3, and CCL4) showed moderate inverse correlations with the change in multiple metabolites. This may suggest that individuals with higher baseline higher baseline cytokines correlate with reduced change in metabolites in response to immunization. Notably, many of these cytokines have moderate to strong joint associated with body mass index (BMI), consistent with prior evidence that adiposity drives chronic low-grade inflammation characterized by elevated IL-6, TNFα, and related chemokines (Ellulu et al., 2017). Therefore, a higher BMI at baseline may diminish the degree of metabolite change in response to immunization. We also find this trend is reversed in the exercise group suggesting exercise may overcome the unfavorable effect of higher BMI. This is also supported in the associations of metabolites to cytokines in the exercise group that were either entirely reversed or dampened relative to the non-exercise group further supporting the beneficial impact of exercise on immunization (Hagan et al., 2022).

Metabolite pathway analysis reveals broader pathway distribution in exercise intervention group

The metabolites identified from RS measurements enabled pathway analysis (Fig. 6c) that are enriched from immune activation post-immunization and impacted by exercise at timepoint B relative to A. The intensity of the Raman peaks served as a measure of metabolite-associated signal, allowing us to identify approximate metabolic pathways.

It should be noted that RS peaks such as amino acids (1208 cm−1), amide III (1248 cm−1), amide I (1683 cm−1), and DNA/RNA (669 cm−1, 805 cm−1) were excluded from the enrichment analyses as they represent broad families rather than specific metabolites. MetaboAnalyst 6.0, a web-based interface, was employed for the quantitative enrichment analysis using KEGG database (Pang et al., 2021) that identified several pathways that change with exercise with an enrichment ratio above 1. Note that the enrichment ratio is an absolute number that represents a net change in the assigned metabolites that contribute to that pathway regardless of a decrease or increase in each metabolite. Further, these enrichment ratios represent a culmination of change from many metabolites instead of a direct linear correlation to a single metabolite. The overall trend in these pathways shows broader metabolite shifts in participants who exercised immediately post-immunization. For example, pathways associated with histidine metabolism, and those associated with glycolysis, TCA cycle, are significantly enriched in the participants who exercised. We also find that pathways associated with glutamine/glutamic acid, including glutathione metabolism, have greater representation in the exercise treatment group. Although the requirements for glutamine are not firmly established for TFH cells or GC B cells, evidence suggests glutamine may be required for optimal TFH function (Bcl6, or CD40L expression), GC B cell number, and serum Ab (Choi and Morel, 2020; Choi et al., 2018). Other amino acid-related pathways, including those associated with phenylalanine and tryptophan, are also relevant to T cell function and immunity (Stone and Williams, 2023) and showed greater representation in the exercise group. Notably, the exercise-induced change across multiple pathways mirrors the findings shown in Fig. 4, highlighting that higher antibody responders showed a more remarkable metabolic shift in response to immunization. One possible explanation for the effects of exercise is that the metabolic state during and after exercise may have led to limited or altered fuel availability, prompting greater metabolic flexibility during immune activation.

Discussion

Our findings indicate that an intervention aimed at manipulating metabolism and the allocation of fuel resources when administered at the time of vaccine-induced immune activation, influences immune memory. Based on this work, we propose that 90 min of moderate-intensity exercise is sufficient to induce energy-conservation metabolic programs during the initial hours after immunization. Our interpretation is supported by other studies demonstrating that the metabolic state of dendritic cells, along with the local metabolic environment, play a key role in T cell priming. For example, a low glucose microenvironment can enhance T-cell priming (Lawless et al., 2017). With nutrients being considered as “Signal 4” in T cell activation (Raynor and Chi, 2024), it is not unexpected that altering nutrient availability in the microenvironment during antigen presentation would impact adaptive immunity. The experiments herein did not directly test the influence of the local metabolic environment on dendritic-cell antigen presentation to T cells, cues for TFH development, and TFH-B cell GC interaction, due to limitations in lymph node sampling from human participants. Our results are consistent with the possibility of enhanced TFH – GC interaction, as indicated by putative biomarker CXCL13, which was increased in the metabolic intervention group two weeks post-immunization. The plausibility of this scenario is further supported by the durability of serum antibody six months' post-immunization. The relative increase in antibody durability associated with exercise at 6 months post-immunization (total AUC) was similar in magnitude to the difference observed between the higher-dose vaccine (Moderna) and the lower-dose vaccine (Pfizer) (Fig. 2e and f). Given that dose-related differences between these vaccines have been reported to be clinically relevant (Ioannou et al., 2022), this comparison provides context for the potential magnitude of the exercise effect. To our knowledge, our results are the first to demonstrate that a metabolic intervention impacts CXCL13 in response to vaccination. Additionally, serum metabolite data at two weeks' post-immunization bolster our proposed concept. At this timepoint, T cell and B cell expansion are occurring, along with a transition from effector-like to memory precursor populations (Kim et al., 2017; Sallusto et al., 2010). Given that the metabolic exercise intervention was administered two weeks’ prior, the greater changes observed across multiple metabolites and metabolic pathways in exercise participants (Fig. 6) likely reflect more robust immunometabolic reprogramming rather than direct effects of exercise, although differences in broader systemic metabolic adaptation may also contribute. Further support for this possibility stems from our data showing that “best responders” in terms of antibody titer experienced more significant overall metabolic changes from pre-immunization to timepoint B. Two of the six metabolites significantly elevated in exercise compared to control (Fig. 5), histidine and threonine, have also been reported to correlate with higher antibody response to SARS-CoV-2 vaccination. Overall, these findings serve as initial “proof of concept” studies indicating that non-pharmacological metabolic interventions influence immune memory when delivered at the time of immunization supported by evidence showing distinct metabolic profiles post-immunization. However, additional experiments are required to accurately define the role of specific metabolites and nutrient pathways in the local environment of immune activation following vaccination.

The findings presented here also have relevance for aging and immunity to vaccines. We observed that age impacts the metabolite changes in response to immunization, and increasing age has negative consequences for antibody response (Fig. 7). The metabolic signature that correlates with age differs between exercised and non-exercise at timepoint B, and these findings may be helpful in defining pathways to be targeted in improving vaccines for middle-aged to older adults. Metabolite changes that correlated positively with age, i.e. metabolites increased with increasing age in the exercise participants at timepoint B but showed a negative correlation with age in non-exercise control participants include citric acid, sugars, phospholipids, and amino acids including glycine, tyrosine, and phenylalanine. These findings may imply that a greater availability of these metabolites could be more advantageous during immune response to vaccination. These results support suggested strategies to improve vaccines immunogenicity by targeting certain metabolic pathways in aging.

Psychosocial variables including stress and resilience appear to influence early immunometabolic responses following vaccination, consistent with psychoneuroimmunology frameworks. The observed negative association between stress and CXCL13 align with prior work in animal studies suggesting that psychological stress may impair germinal center formation, perhaps related to the effects of corticosterone (Sun et al., 2019), although to our knowledge these are the first findings to show this association in humans using a putative biomarker for germinal center activation. High stress may also divert metabolic resources toward stress-response, resulting in impairments in glucose and lipid homeostasis (Kivimäki et al., 2023). Conversely, psychological resilience is associated with favorable metabolic states, potentially reflecting lower chronic inflammation and improved energy allocation (Ghulam et al., 2022; Picard and McEwen, 2018). Interestingly, exercise appeared to moderate these associations as resilience was positively associated with CXCL13 only among individuals who did not perform the exercise task following vaccination, but not those who did exercise. This suggests that exercise may mitigate stress-related immune dysregulation by performing a protective role among individuals with higher levels of psychosocial stressors. Although immunomodulatory mechanism involving acute exercise may differ from regular physical activity, the results presented here are consistent with other research showing benefits of physical activity on immune response to vaccination (Chastin et al., 2021; Palotino-Ferreira et al., 2025). These findings extend prior work by integrating psychosocial and behavioral factors with immunometabolic markers, yet this work is exploratory, and future work will be important to validate these psychoneuroimmunological findings in future mechanistic and clinical validation studies.

Our findings indicate that immune cell activation results in a significant shift in metabolic flux that controls response to SARS-CoV-2 mRNA immunization enabling an integrated immunometabolic signature that distinguishes best and poor responders to immunization, and those with pre-existing immunity. RS metabolic analysis highlights distinct metabolic signatures between best and poor responders at 2-week post dose 1 (timepoint B) that indicate changes in metabolites are closely linked to the magnitude of the immune response, with stronger antibody responses correlating with greater shifts in lipids and FAs, and amino acids. Further, those with pre-existing immunity had a distinct antibody titer response and metabolic profile at timepoint B likely due to a “recall” response than naïve participants who elicited a primary immune response. This finding aligns well with previous studies (Krammer et al., 2021) that have shown that individuals with prior SARS-CoV-2 infection exhibited vaccine-induced antibody responses after a single immunization dose comparable to the responses observed in non-infected individuals following a complete two-dose immunization regimen (Ebinger et al., 2021; Manisty et al., 2021). Studies have also shown pre-existing immunity altered gene expression profiles after immunization, with reduced antiviral Type I interferon and plasma cell signatures (Hagan et al., 2022). While our findings that antibody response differs among naïve individuals compared to those with pre-existing immunity are expected, our results add to the literature by showing that metabolite profiles are also distinctly different.

Conclusions

In summary, our study demonstrates that physiological metabolic intervention in human participants induced by 90-min moderate intensity exercise after SARS-CoV-2 immunization impacts the antibody response, serum CXCL13, and specific immunometabolic signatures at two weeks post dose 1 of immunization (timepoint B). The results provide compelling evidence that a metabolic intervention at the time of immunization influences the long-term immune response through a unique immunometabolic signature. Additionally, our findings indicate that host factors such as age and BMI affect metabolite profiles in response to immunization, with a more significant impact observed in the exercise group. These results suggest that middle-aged and older adults may benefit from engaging in acute exercise following immunization to enhance vaccine-mediated protection. Key metabolites that contribute to energy metabolism, amino acid metabolism (phenylalanine, threonine, histidine, tyrosine) and lipid metabolism (cholesterol, phospholipids) are altered in the exercise group and these metabolites also show an association to participant age. This study also indicates that a potential biomarker of germinal center activation (CXCL13) shows moderate to strong association with multiple metabolites. GC formation is facilitated by TFH cell differentiation which also drives antibody-secreting B cells. Exercise appeared to attenuate or reverse the psychosocial-CXCL13 relationship, highlighting its moderating effect on stress and resilience in the context of vaccine response. The findings of this study underscore the significance of immunometabolic health, which is intricately governed by the interplay between metabolic and immunologic pathways, in determining the efficacy of SARS-CoV-2 mRNA vaccinations. Furthermore, the results suggest that the metabolic environment, modulated by specific durations and intensities of exercise conducted immediately following immunization, exerts a substantial influence on this relationship. Our future controlled study with metabolic assessments at the time of immunization and exercise metabolic intervention in a larger group of participants will elucidate how single or multiple sessions of exercise post immunization impacts longitudinal changes in immunometabolic signatures.
