Diagnostic performance of “pons chocolate chip sign” in heterozygous HTRA1-related cerebral small vessel disease
Authors: Yu Guo, Qing Peng, Yishuang Yang, Zhe Wang, Yong Shan, HaoTian Yan, Haiqiang Jin, Li Bai, Wei Sun, Zhaoxia Wang, Zihao Zhang, Yun Yuan, Chen Ling
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background and purposeHeterozygous HTRA1-related cerebral small vessel disease (hHTRA1-CSVD) presents diagnostic challenges due to its clinical and imaging similarities with cerebral autosomal dominant arteriopathy with subcortical infarcts and leukoencephalopathy (CADASIL) and sporadic cerebral small vessel disease (CSVD). Recently, the “chocolate chip sign” around the midbrain has been proposed as a potential imaging marker for hHTRA1-CSVD. However, the diagnostic value of similar findings around the pons remains unclear. This study aims to assess the diagnostic performance of the “pons chocolate chip sign” in distinguishing hHTRA1-CSVD from CADASIL and sporadic CSVD.Materials and methodsThis cross-sectional study included seven patients with hHTRA1-CSVD, twenty-seven patients with CADASIL and twelve patients with sporadic CSVD. All participants underwent 7.0T magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). The “pons chocolate chip sign” was defined as round or ovoid hypointense dots (≥ 2 mm in diameter) surrounding the pons on T2*-weighted gradient echo images. The number of chocolate chips was independently assessed by two blinded neurologists. Diagnostic performance was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, with genetic diagnosis as the gold standard.ResultsThe “pons chocolate chip sign” was found in 5/7 patients with hHTRA1-CSVD, compared to 2/27 in CADASIL and 0/12 in sporadic CSVD. ROC analysis revealed that it exhibited good discriminatory capability for hHTRA1-CSVD (area under the curve [AUC] = 0.84, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.63-1.00, P = 0.004). At an optimal cutoff of chocolate chips ≥ 1, the sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), and Youden index were 71.42%, 94.87%, 71.42%, 94.87%, and 0.66, respectively. When the cutoff was increased to ≥ 3 chocolate chips, the specificity improved further, reaching 100%.ConclusionsThe “pons chocolate chip sign” demonstrates high specificity for hHTRA1-CSVD and good discriminatory performance in differentiating hHTRA1-CSVD from CADASIL and sporadic CSVD.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12883-025-04374-3.

Introduction

Pathogenic HTRA1 mutations cause two genetic patterns of monogenic cerebral small vessel disease (CSVD). Homozygous mutations lead to cerebral autosomal recessive arteriopathy with subcortical infarcts and leukoencephalopathy (CARASIL) [1], while heterozygous mutations result in a dominantly inherited form known as heterozygous HTRA1-related CSVD (hHTRA1-CSVD) [2]. Compared with CARASIL, hHTRA1-CSVD usually presents with later onset and lacks extra-neurological features [3]. As a result, it can resemble sporadic CSVD and is often underdiagnosed [4]. In addition, hHTRA1-CSVD shares clinical and imaging features with CADASIL, making differentiation difficult without genetic testing [5].

The currently identified magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) features of hHTRA1-CSVD are in line with sporadic CSVD, including lacunes, white matter hyperintensities (WMHs) and cerebral microbleeds (CMBs), and lack disease-specific characteristics [2, 6]. Recently, Ando et al. [7] reported that hypointense dots (chocolate chips) around the midbrain on susceptibility-weighted imaging (SWI)—referred to as the midbrain “chocolate chip sign”—may serve as a promising imaging marker for hHTRA1-CSVD. The midbrain “chocolate chip sign” exhibited strong discriminative ability in distinguishing hHTRA1-CSVD from CADASIL and sporadic CSVD, with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.817 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.624–1.00) [7]. Notably, the chocolate chip sign is not confined to the midbrain. It has also been reported on the pons surface but remain unreported in the medulla [8]. Its visibility on the pons surface appears comparable to, or even greater than, that of the midbrain [8]. However, the diagnostic performance of “pons chocolate chip sign” remains unexplored.

In this study, we compared the brain MRI of patients with hHTRA1-CSVD, CADASIL and sporadic CSVD, and found that “pons chocolate chip sign” also exhibit a high specificity for hHTRA1-CSVD.

Materials and methods

Participants

This cross-sectional study was approved by the Institutional Review Board and Ethics Committee at Peking University First Hospital. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. From April 2021 to July 2024, we consecutively enrolled patients with hHTRA1-CSVD, CADASIL and sporadic CSVD from an ongoing 7.0T MRI cohort in our department. The inclusion criteria for patients with hHTRA1-CSVD/CADASIL were as follows: (1) confirmed genetic diagnosis of hHTRA1-CSVD/CADASIL, (2) presence of WMHs with Fazekas score ≥ 1 in either the periventricular or deep white matter regions, along with the number of lacunes ≥ 1, (3) age > 18 years old. The inclusion criteria for patients with sporadic CSVD were as follows: (1) no disease-causing mutations detected by second-generation sequencing, (2) presence of WMHs with Fazekas score ≥ 1 in either the periventricular or deep white matter regions, along with the number of lacunes ≥ 1, (4) no other identifiable cause of stroke (e.g., > 50% stenosis of large arteries or myocardial embolism) [9], (5) age > 18 years old. Exclusion criteria for all groups were: (1) contradictions to MRI, and (2) poor image quality or incomplete images.

Initially, eight patients with hHTRA1-CSVD, thirty-three patients with CADASIL, and eighteen patients with sporadic CSVD were consecutively enrolled and underwent 7.0T MRI scans. After image quality assessment, one patient with hHTRA1-CSVD, four patients with CADASIL, and three patients with sporadic CSVD were excluded due to poor image quality resulting from head motion. Additionally, two patients with CADASIL and three patients with sporadic CSVD were excluded due to incomplete images.

Brain MRI

Participants underwent an MRI examination on a MAGNETOM 7 T plus MR scanner (Siemens Healthcare) using an 8-channel transmit and 32-channel receive head coil (NOVA medical). The imaging parameters of MRI sequences were summarized in Supplemental Data, Table SI.

MRI lesion burden

The lesion burden of participants was defined in accordance with the STandards for ReportIng Vascular changes on nEuroimaging (STRIVE) [10]. Two neurologists (Li Bai and Chen Ling) independently assessed the MRI lesions, and the average of their measurements was utilized. WMHs was measured using the Fazekas scale [11] and age-related white matter change (ARWMC) scale [12]. The Fazekas scale was used for participant selection due to its simplicity and widespread use in CSVD research, while the ARWMC scale was applied to assess the regional burden of WMHs in subsequent analysis. The number of lacunes in the white matter, basal ganglia, thalamus, brainstem, and cerebellum were manually counted. The number of CMBs was assessed using the microbleed anatomic rating scale (MARS) [13].

Clinical Measures

At the time of study enrollment, we collected a comprehensive set of data from patients with hHTRA1-CSVD. These information includes the HTRA1 variants, age, sex, age at onset (determined by the first occurrence of neurological symptoms), initial symptoms, history of hypertension, history of diabetes mellitus, history of hyperlipidemia, and history of smoking/alcohol consumption [14]. Furthermore, we recorded clinical features including transient ischemic attack/stroke, cognitive impairments, mood disorder, gait disturbance, alopecia and lumbago. We used the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) guidelines to determine the pathogenicity of HTRA1 gene variants [15]. Only patients carrying HTRA1 gene variants with a rating of “likely pathogenic” or “pathogenic” were included in this study. For patients with CADASIL and sporadic CSVD, we collected similar information including age, sex, as well as history of hypertension, diabetes mellitus, hyperlipidemia, and smoking/alcohol consumption.

Analysis of “pons chocolate chip sign”

The “pons chocolate chip sign” was defined as round or ovoid hypointense dots (≥ 2 mm in diameter) surrounding the pons on T2*w gradient echo (T2*w-GRE), adapted from the definition of “chocolate chip sign” proposed by Ando et al. [7]. Hereafter, we use the term “chocolate chips” to refer to these hypointense dots for consistency. The number of chocolate chips around the pons was counted by two neurologists (Haiqiang Jin and Qing Peng) independently and blindly, and the average count was used for analysis.

We further evaluated the proportion of chocolate chips with venous connection in patients with hHTRA1-CSVD. Chocolate chips with venous connection were defined as lesions connected to the veins on at least one T2*w-GRE slice, which is an adaptation of the definition of “microbleeds with venous connection” by Johanna Rotta et al [16].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 25.0 (SPSS, Inc, Chicago). Continuous variables are presented as the mean ± SD or median with interquartile range (IQR), and categorical variables are presented as proportions. Kruskal-Wallis one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Dunn’s posthoc test was used in comparison of continuous variables among the three groups. In instances where statistical differences were identified in univariate analysis, multiple linear regression was then applied to adjust for age and sex. Chi-square test was employed for analyzing categorical data. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was used to evaluate the discriminative ability of “pons chocolate chip sign” in differentiating hHTRA1-CSVD from CADASIL and sporadic CSVD. Genetic tests were used as the gold standard. Optimal cutoff value was calculated as described by Youden [17]. The sensitivity, specificity, Positive Predictive Value (PPV), Negative Predictive Value (NPV), and Youden index were calculated. Inter-rater agreement was assessed using Cohen’s kappa coefficient. All hypothesis testing was 2-tailed. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05. Bonferroni correction was used to adjust multiple comparison (statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05/number of individual tests).

Comparison of baseline MRI lesion distribution in hHTRA1-CSVD, CADASIL and sporadic CSVD

We analyzed the distribution of WMHs and lacunes among three groups (Supplemental Data, Table SIII). The ARWMC scores in the parietal-occipital lobe of patients with hHTRA1-CSVD were significantly higher than those of patients with sporadic CSVD (P = 0.001), after adjusting for age and sex. In addition, patients with hHTRA1-CSVD had significantly fewer lacunes in the thalamus compared to patients with CADASIL (P = 0.013), but more lacunes in the white matter than patients with sporadic CSVD (P = 0.019), after adjusting for age and sex.

Using MARS, we found that patients with hHTRA1-CSVD had a higher number of CMBs in the brainstem compared to patients with CADASIL (P = 0.011), after adjusting for age and sex. Additionally, patients with hHTRA1-CSVD had a significantly higher number of CMBs in the temporal lobe (P = 0.004) and brainstem (P = 0.011) compared to patients with CADASIL, after adjusting for age and sex. (Supplemental Data, Table SIV)

“Pons chocolate chip sign” are of high specificity for hHTRA1-CSVD

At the pons level, in addition to the conventional CMBs located within the brain parenchyma, there are also some hypointense dots (chocolate chips) situated outside the parenchyma, surrounding the surface of the pons on T2*w-GRE. Due to their distinctive distribution and appearance, resembling scattered chocolate chips, we refer to this pattern as the “pons chocolate chip sign”. (Fig. 1)

Then we evaluated the ability of “pons chocolate chip sign” to predict hHTRA1-CSVD using ROC curve analysis. Genetic test was used as the gold standard. The results demonstrated that “pons chocolate chip sign” exhibited strong discriminatory power for predicting hHTRA1-CSVD (AUC = 0.84, 95%CI: 0.63-1.00, P = 0.004) (Fig. 3). The optimal cutoff value was determined to be chocolate chips ≥ 1. Using this cutoff, the sensitivity, specificity, PPV, NPV, and Youden index of “pons chocolate chip sign” in identifying patients with hHTRA1-CSVD were 5/7 (71.42%), 37/39 (94.87%), 5/7 (71.42%), 37/39 (94.87%), and 0.66, respectively (Table 3). When the cutoff value was set at chocolate chips ≥ 3, the sensitivity, specificity, PPV, NPV, and Youden index were 3/7 (42.86%), 39/39 (100.00%), 3/3 (100.00%), 39/43 (90.70%), and 0.43, respectively (Supplemental Data, Table SVI).

Chocolate chips are connected with the veins on the pons surface

Using minimum intensity projection (Min-IP) to reconstruct T2*w-GRE images, we found that some chocolate chips are connected with veins on the pons surface. Among the thirty-seven chocolate chips identified in patients with hHTRA1-CSVD, eighteen (49%) exhibited a direct connection to veins (Table 2; Fig. 2).

“Pons chocolate chip sign” can also be observed in 3.0T MRI

Among the five hHTRA1-CSVD patients exhibiting the “pons chocolate chip sign,” three underwent 3.0T MRI scans that included SWI sequences, and the same sign were clearly identifiable (Supplemental Data, Figure SI). The remaining two patients did not undergo susceptibility-sensitive MRI sequences, and thus the presence of such a sign could not be evaluated.

Discussion

Our primary finding highlights the high specificity of “pons chocolate chip sign” for hHTRA1-CSVD and its good discriminatory ability in differentiating hHTRA1-CSVD from CADASIL and sporadic CSVD.

The ROC curve analysis showed that the “pons chocolate chip sign” had diagnostic performance comparable to the previously reported “midbrain chocolate chip sign” [7], with an AUC of 0.84 compared to 0.817. Interestingly, this level of performance was achieved at a substantially lower optimal cutoff of ≥ 1 chocolate chips, in contrast to a cutoff of ≥ 5 chocolate chips in the “midbrain chocolate chip sign”. However, both studies were conducted in relatively small cohorts, so the statistical power supporting this comparison is limited.

Another point worth noting is that only a very small proportion of CADASIL patients exhibit the chocolate chip sign, and the number of chocolate chips is usually very limited. As a result, increasing the cutoff value substantially improves specificity.

In the comparison of baseline MRI lesions, we found that patients with hHTRA1-CSVD had significantly higher ARWMC scores in the parieto-occipital lobe compared to those with sporadic CSVD, suggesting a regional predominance in distribution. However, this distribution could not differentiate hHTRA1-CSVD from CADASIL. Regarding CMBs, MARS scores revealed that patients with hHTRA1-CSVD had more CMBs in the brainstem and temporal lobe compared to CADASIL, but such distribution could not differentiate hHTRA1-CSVD from sporadic CSVD. As for lacunes, hHTRA1-CSVD had fewer lacunes in the thalamus compared to CADASIL, but more lacunes in the white matter compared to sporadic CSVD. However, such regional distribution is also not specific to hHTRA1-CSVD.

It was widely accepted that CMBs are perivascular focal deposition of hemosiderin in adjacent brain parenchyma [18]. Although these chocolate chips highly resemble CMBs in terms of morphology, diameter and signal intensity, their anatomical location does not conform to the typical definition of CMBs, which requires that the lesion to be located within the brain parenchyma. However, a consensus has not yet been reached regarding the histological correlate of these chocolate chips. Kobayashi et al. [8] considered these dots to be genuine CMBs, possibly microhemorrhagic scars on or close to the surface of the brainstem, after excluding other potential causes such as aneurysms and venous dilation. However, in our 7.0T MRI analysis, 49% of these chocolate chips in patients with hHTRA1-CSVD appeared to have a direct venous connection, suggesting that they might be part of the venous structures. Previous study also showed that vessel structural abnormalities, including vessel wall dissections and microaneurysms, are known contributors to CMBs mimics like chocolate chips [19]. In 2025, Ando et al. [7] provided evidence suggesting that some of these chocolate chips may represent dilated extraparenchymal vessels, based on three-dimensional reconstruction of SWI. Evidence from a 2024 study also suggested that sulcal hypointense dots in hHTRA1-CSVD, similar in appearance to chocolate chips, might represent sulcal vessels [20]. Although definitive histological evidence is still lacking, these findings raise the possibility that venous walls abnormalities may correspond to the histological correlates of these chocolate chips. Notably, several studies have demonstrated that HTRA1 mutations lead to abnormalities in the transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β) signaling pathway, resulting in irregularities in the extracellular matrix, which may be underlying the pathogenesis of venous wall abnormalities [1, 21, 22]. Nonetheless, the aforementioned hypotheses require further validation through high-resolution magnetic resonance vessel wall imaging and histopathological studies.

Given that different centers utilize various MRI models, the sensitivity for detecting chocolate chips may differ. Therefore, we enrolled participants from an ongoing 7.0T MRI cohort for CSVD in our department to maintain baseline consistency. However, this does not mean that “pons chocolate chip sign” can be detected solely by 7 T MRI. It can also be observed in all of the three patients who underwent 3.0T SWI in our cohort, and in previously reported cases who underwent low-field MRI [23–26].

This study has several limitations. First, our sample size was relatively small. In particular, although the AUC for the “pons chocolate chip sign” was comparable to that of the “midbrain chocolate chip sign”, the confidence intervals for both methods were wide, indicating considerable uncertainty of the diagnostic performance. Thus, these two features should be applied with caution in clinical diagnosis currently. And a larger sample size would help improve the precision of the intervals. Second, the study’s cross-sectional design inevitably introduces selection bias. Third, this study lacks pathological evidence, and the underlying mechanisms of the “pons chocolate chip sign” require further investigation. Given the difficulty of obtaining postmortem confirmation, high-resolution vessel wall imaging in future studies could provide valuable insights.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study demonstrates that “pons chocolate chip sign” exhibit high specificity for hHTRA1-CSVD and good discriminatory performance in differentiating hHTRA1-CSVD from CADASIL and sporadic CSVD.
