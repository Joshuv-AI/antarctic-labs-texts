Distinct functions in fertility and patterns of paternal incorporation of the histone H2A variants HTAS-1 and HTZ-1 in C. elegans
Authors: Swadha Singh, Tomoko Tabuchi, Keiko Hokeness, Noelle Anderson, Jesus Zuniga Paiz, Allen Ramsey, Arnie Maravillas, Emma Harms, Elida Hernandez, Eric Tang, Aiza C Go, Tammy F Wu, Andreas Rechtsteiner, Scott W Roy, Susan Strome, Diana S Chu, C Phillips
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAs germ cells transform into sperm, the packaging of DNA is critical for transcriptional reprogramming and transmission of paternal genetic and epigenetic information vital for male fitness. Here we investigated the roles in Caenorhabditis elegans spermatogenesis of two histone H2A variants: HTAS-1, a rapidly-evolving sperm-specific paralog, and HTZ-1, an H2A.Z homolog. We show that, in contrast to the ubiquitiously expressed HTZ-1 that is required for germ cell function, HTAS-1 is incorporated late in sperm formation to improve sperm production and development after fertilization. ChIP-seq analysis of sperm chromatin reveals that HTZ-1 and HTAS-1 are incorporated genome-wide but under-represented on the transcriptionally-repressed X chromosome. HTAS-1 incorporates within gene bodies; in contrast, HTZ-1 incorporates at most targets at the promoter, except at those shared with HTAS-1, where it also incorporates in gene bodies. During spermatogenesis, loss of HTAS-1 has modest effects on expression of germline and nongermline genes, which may cumulatively influence sperm production; loss of HTZ-1 results in both increased and decreased expression of mostly germline-expressed genes. In striking contrast, during oogenesis, HTZ-1 loss causes dramatic upregulation of sperm genes, revealing tissue-specific modes of HTZ-1 regulation. Post-fertilization, HTZ-1 is removed in embryos; however, sperm-supplied HTAS-1 is retained and lack of HTAS-1 contributes to developmental delay of htas-1 mutants. Thus, H2A variants have unique incorporation profiles during spermatogenesis, distinct fates postfertilization, and tissue-specific functions in gene expression. Our studies provide evidence for how evolution of sperm-specific chromatin organizing proteins like H2A variants can optimize male fertility, development, and reproductive fitness.Keywords: histone variant, spermatogenesis, epigenetics, transcription, chromatin, fertility

Introduction

Germ cells are continually optimized to maximize reproductive fitness. For example, in many organisms sperm repackage chromatin by replacing canonical histones in part with rapidly-evolving histone variants. These histone variants can regulate gene expression during sperm formation, facilitate the tight DNA packaging that contributes to efficient sperm motility, and serve as paternal epigenetic markers (Torres-Flores and Hernandez-Hernandez 2020; Moritz and Hammoud 2022; Okada 2022; Patankar et al. 2022). After fertilization, these marks can be removed or retained (Hammoud et al. 2009; Samson et al. 2014; Yang et al. 2015; Jung et al. 2017). However, how histone variants have adapted to influence gene expression before and after fertilization to improve reproductive processes remains unclear.

The histone H2A family exhibits the largest number of variants, likely due to the critical interactions H2A has within nucleosomes and with other chromatin proteins (Molaro et al. 2018; Herchenrother et al. 2022; Oberdoerffer and Miller 2023). The most well-studied variant is the highly-conserved H2A.Z, which is deposited by the SWR chromatin remodeler at promoters to regulate transcription of genes that play roles in cell-specific functions and development from yeast to mammals (Jin et al. 2009; Sun and Luk 2017; Colino-Sanguino et al. 2022; Dijkwel and Tremethick 2022). During spermatogenesis, H2A.Z also replaces macroH2A to assemble facultative heterochromatin on the silenced sex chromosomes in mice (Greaves et al. 2006). Interestingly, in zebrafish embryos, gene loci and repetitive elements compete for H2A.Z (Meng et al. 2023), suggesting that the balance and levels of H2A variants that incorporate within the same tissue to form different chromatin structures are an important, though poorly understood, aspect of tissue-specific gene regulatory functions.

One set of tissue-specific H2A variants are those expressed in testis or sperm, which evolve rapidly (Talbert and Henikoff 2010; Molaro et al. 2018; Osakabe and Molaro 2022). Some of these variants enable distinct gene regulatory mechanisms during sperm formation. For example, H2A.B3 occupies the transcription start site (TSS) of active genes after mouse meiosis (Soboleva et al. 2012). It also functions in splicing through localizing in the 5′ region of gene bodies, binding RNA, and replacing H2A.Z at intron-exon boundaries to increase expression and exon inclusion (Soboleva et al. 2017; Anuar et al. 2019). However, incorporation profiles and roles of other tissue-specific H2A variants in comparison to ubiquitously expressed variants like H2A.Z in the same tissue are not clear.

Sperm-specific H2A variants can also influence sperm function and reproductive fitness through sperm-specific chromatin packaging. The bulk repackaging of paternal chromatin in mammals starts with histone acetylation followed by histone variant and transition protein incorporation, then replacement with sperm nuclear basic proteins (SNBPs) called protamines (Baarends et al. 1999; Rathke et al. 2007; Bao and Bedford 2016; Moritz and Hammoud 2022). Some organisms partially or fully retain histones in sperm (Mann et al. 1982; Gatewood et al. 1987; Hammoud et al. 2009; Wu et al. 2011; Samson et al. 2014; Yamaguchi et al. 2018; Yoshida et al. 2018). For instance, the testes-specific H2A.L.2 in humans is retained and enriched in pericentric regions (Barral et al. 2017; Hoghoughi et al. 2020). Testis- or sperm-specific histones or SNBPs evolve rapidly to increase male fitness (Kasinsky et al. 2011; Chang et al. 2023; Chai et al. 2024; Singh et al. 2024). For example, knock-out of H2A.B causes decreased male fitness by affecting male fertility and mating success (Molaro et al. 2020). There is currently little understanding of how broadly divergent sperm- or testes-specific H2A variants influence transcriptional regulation or compaction of sperm DNA to affect fitness.

The fate and functions of epigenetic information carried by paternal H2A variants after fertilization are known for only a few cases. In mouse, H2AL1 and H2AL2, which are paternally incorporated at pericentric heterochromatin, are rapidly removed after fertilization and serve no known roles during embryogenesis (Wu et al. 2008). Paternally contributed mouse H2A.Z is also removed upon fertilization (Liu et al. 2022). Paternal H2A variants can also have roles post-fertilization. Parentally supplied H2A.3 is important for embryo viability and growth (Molaro et al. 2020). In zebrafish, nucleosomes with the paternal histone H2A variant H2A.Z (FV) and H3K4me1 deter DNA methylation of housekeeping gene and early embryonic transcription factors promoters before and after fertilization (Murphy et al. 2018). It is important to explore more examples of how paternal H2A variant marks may serve important roles in early embryos.

We have previously shown that paternal chromatin in Caenorhabditis elegans (C. elegans) is marked by a sperm-specific H2A variant, HTAS-1. HTAS-1 is 48% identical to the C. elegans S-phase H2A (referred to henceforth as H2A.S) and HIS-35, which differs from H2A.S by only one amino acid and is ubiquitously expressed (Supplementary Fig. 1) (Chu et al. 2006; Samson et al. 2014). HTAS-1 is also distinct (39% identity) from the highly evolutionarily conserved H2A.Z variant called HTZ-1, with particularly distinct N and C-termini. Interestingly, the high degree of divergence of HTAS-1 occurred recently, similar to the rapid evolution of other proteins important for male reproduction (Kasimatis and Phillips 2018; Singh et al. 2024). This suggests that HTAS-1 has continually adapted to optimize male fitness, though the effects and extent of its influence on sperm formation and function before and after fertilization are unknown.

Studying sperm chromatin in C. elegans provides a unique opportunity to determine ubiquitous and sperm-specific H2A variant function and incorporation before and after fertilization. Histone variants may play important roles in establishing epigenetic information because classic DNA methylation on 5-methyl cytosine is rare in C. elegans, which lacks a conventional DNMT DNA methyltransferase (Simpson et al. 1986; Wenzel et al. 2011). HTAS-1 incorporates as cells progress through the pachytene stage of sperm meiosis, when global transcription levels decrease (Chu and Shakes 2012). HTAS-1 stays associated after meiotic divisions and the tight compaction of the sperm genome. After fertilization, HTAS-1 is retained on the paternal DNA but is sequentially diluted with each embryonic division (Samson et al. 2014). In contrast, HTZ-1 is present in all germ cells during sperm and oocyte formation and is contributed by both parental gametes to the newly fertilized embryo. However, parental HTZ-1 is rapidly removed before the first mitotic division and replaced by maternal HTZ-1 (Samson et al. 2014). Depletion of HTZ-1 causes sterility and embryonic lethality: progeny from heterozygous htz-1 hermaphrodites develop via maternally loaded HTZ-1 into sterile adults that produce dead embryos (Whittle et al. 2008). HTZ-1 has other roles in embryo and larval development, including dosage compensation, foregut development, and cell fate specification (Updike and Mango 2006; Whittle et al. 2008; Petty et al. 2009; Shibata et al. 2014; Negishi et al. 2022). Probing the unique features of HTAS-1 and HTZ-1 incorporation that influence gene expression of developing sperm or after fertilization is a unique opportunity to determine differences that specify histone variant function.

Our goal is to determine tissue-specific roles of H2A variants in germ cell formation and fertility. We hypothesize that, in contrast to the evolutionarily conserved H2A.Z homolog HTZ-1, the recently-evolved H2A variant HTAS-1 has distinct incorporation patterns and functions during spermatogenesis and after fertilization that improve male fitness. The tractability of C. elegans and available tools, including deletion mutants of the htas-1 and htz-1 genes, allow us to (1) compare differences in the functions of two distinct H2A variants in a single tissue because both the sperm-specific HTAS-1 and ubiquitous HTZ-1 are expressed during spermatogenesis; (2) investigate sex-specific regulatory roles of the H2A.Z homolog, HTZ-1, by comparing its roles in sperm- vs oocyte-producing germlines; and (3) determine potential epigenetic roles of the paternally-supplied HTAS-1 after fertilization because HTAS-1 is delivered and retained in early embryos. Our studies thus reveal unique features of the incorporation patterns of highly-conserved and rapidly-evolved H2A variants that influences chromatin structure, gene expression, and fertility before and after fertilization.

Materials and methods

C. elegans strains and culture conditions

C. elegans strains were maintained using standard conditions on NGM (Nematode Growth Medium) agar plates (Brenner 1974). Strains used in this study are N2, CB1489 him-8(e1489), CB4088 him-5(e1490), XC18 htas-1(tm1849), XC24 htas-1(tm1849); him-5(e1490), EKM11 htz-1(tm2469) V/nT1 [qIs51] (IV;V), AV125 spe-8(hc40) I; dpy-4(e1166) IV. The htas-1(tm1849) deletion is a null as it removes 495 of 506 base pairs plus contains 9 bp insertion and immunostaining does not detect protein presence (Fig. 1a) (Consortium 2012). The htz-1(tm2469) deletion removes 345 of 885 base pairs and results in a null phenotype (Petty et al. 2009; Consortium 2012). XC18 animals maintained as homozygous mutants were used for RNA-seq and ChIP-seq analyses and backcrossed at least three times for phenotypic analyses.

HTAS-1 is required for optimal fertility and developmental timing. a) Immunolocalization of HTAS-1 in isolated and fixed C. elegans male gonads from wild-type (N2) or htas-1(tm1849) mutant with DNA (left panels), HTAS-1 (middle panels), and DNA/HTAS-1 merged (right panels). Scale bar represents 10 µm. b) Self-progeny counts from N2 (wild-type, gray) and htas-1 mutant (gold) hermaphrodites (n = 99). c) Progeny counts from N2 and htas-1 mutant males mated to spe-8 mutant hermaphrodites, which do not produce sperm (n = 39, see Materials and methods). d) Progeny counts from N2 and htas-1 mutant hermaphrodites mated to N2 males (n = 19). e) Numbers of developing germ cells (hatched bars) and mature sperm (solid bars) in N2 and htas-1 mutant males (n = 27). The significance P-values for overlap using a two-tailed t-test are indicated in panels b–e) is in italics. f) Development of N2 and htas-1 mutant hermaphrodites (n = 100) through larval stages to egg-laying was tracked over 100 h (see Materials and methods).

Immunostaining

Immunostaining was conducted using a methanol/acetone fixation on dissected gonads of wild-type males [him-5(e1490)] or htas-1 mutant (htas-1(tm1849); him-5(e1490)) to detect HTAS-1 in tightly compacted sperm chromatin using anti-HTAS-1 antibodies as previously described (Chu et al. 2006; Samson et al. 2014). DNA was stained with DAPI. Confocal images were obtained using a Zeiss LSM710 confocal microscope.

Hermaphrodite progeny production assay

Individual wild-type (N2) and htas-1(tm1849) mutant (XC18) L4 hermaphrodites (n = 99) were plated on NGM agar plates containing fresh spots of the E. coli strain OP50. Plates were checked every 24 h where embryos and viable progeny were counted. Total viable progeny and lethal embryos were counted at 48 h. Hermaphrodites were transferred to freshly spotted plates every 24 h to allow for daily progeny production tracking. Counting continued until initial hermaphrodites were laying unfertilized oocytes. Statistical comparisons were conducted using a paired t-test.

Mated hermaphrodite progeny production assay

Individual wild-type (N2) and htas-1(tm1849) mutant (XC18) L4 hermaphrodites (n = 19) were mated with three N2 males on NGM agar plates containing fresh spots of OP50. Hermaphrodites were changed to fresh plates every 24 h with fresh N2 males then assessed for production of males, embryos, and viable progeny. Counting continued until hermaphrodites laid unfertilized oocytes. Statistical comparisons were conducted using a paired t-test.

Male progeny production assay

Virgin wild-type (N2) and htas-1(tm1849) mutant (XC18) L4 males were each mated with three virgin L4 AV125 spe-8(hc40) I; dpy-4(e1166) hermaphrodites on NGM agar plates with fresh OP50. AV125 spe-8(hc40) I; dpy-4(e1166) hermaphrodites have defective sperm and produce nondpy cross-progeny when mated with a fertile male (L’Hernault et al. 1988). Males were transferred every 24 h to fresh L4 AV125 hermaphrodites until progeny displayed the phenotype indicating self-fertilization rather than male sperm fertilization. Progeny were quantified by counting the number of objects laid, which were unfertilized oocytes, embryos and larvae. Eggs that had not hatched by 48 h were scored as dead embryos. Statistical comparisons were conducted using a paired t-test.

Male sperm production quantitation

Because males tend to wander off plates, seeded NGM plates were first incubated with 2 hermaphrodites for 1 h, and that were then removed along with any laid embryos so that residual hermaphrodite ascarosides could help attract males to stay on the plate (Srinivasan et al. 2008). L4 males (n = 27) were incubated on these plates for 24 h, picked into microcentrifuge tubes, washed in 0.5 mL PBST (PBS + 0.01% Triton X-100) twice, then fixed using 40% isopropanol, stained with DAPI, and mounted onto a microscope slide with the addition of Vectashield. Images were acquired using a Leica Stellaris 5 confocal microscope with a 63X oil immersion objective. LAS-X tiling software was used to acquire images with a 10% overlap for alignment and reconstruction of whole germlines. Because of differences in size and shapes of germline nuclei in different stages, counting was performed using the FIJI cell counter for mitotic through division zone nuclei and Imaris spot tool for the region from the division zone forward through mature spermatids. A paired t-test was used for statistical comparisons.

Development assay

Wild-type (N2) and htas-1(tm1849) mutant strains were backcrossed three times then grown two days to ensure hermaphrodites were gravid. Hermaphrodites were collected and bleached to release embryos. Embryos were incubated in M9 overnight to synchronize L1 larvae. Plates of L1 C. elegans (n = 100 each) were monitored every 12 h at 19 °C for developmental stage (L1-gravid) for 100 h.

C. elegans male and sperm isolation

Isolation of C. elegans males and sperm were conducted as previously described (Samson et al. 2014; Tabuchi et al. 2018).

ChIP-seq analysis

We focused ChIP-analysis on spermatids because this allows enrichment for single cells that are in the final stage of sperm formation and also carry over epigenetic information upon fertilization. Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) of spermatid chromatin, library preparation, and sequencing were performed as in (Tabuchi et al. 2018). 1ug of polyclonal antibodies to HTAS-1 (N-terminal and C-terminal) and HTZ-1 proteins were used with the extract from ∼2.6 million sperm cells (Chu et al. 2006; Petty et al. 2009; Samson et al. 2014). As a control, input samples were generated in parallel that were not subjected to antibody incubation. Binding of HTZ-1 and HTAS-1 relative to background bulk nucleosomes are referred to as variant enrichment, which is a measure of specific variant incorporation. Raw sequence reads for datasets (3 replicates [2 with C-terminal and 1 with N-terminal specific antibodies] for HTAS-1 and 2 replicates for HTZ-1) from the Illumina HiSeq (Illumina HiSeq4000, PE150) were subjected to FastQC quality control. The technical replicates of both datasets were merged, and the sequences were then mapped to the C. elegans genome (Wormbase version 235) by using Bowtie with default settings (Langmead et al. 2009; Davis et al. 2022). MACS2 was used to call peaks and to create Bedgraph files with the following settings: -g ce –bdg –keep-dup = auto –broad –broad-cutoff = 0.01 –nomodel –extsize = 250 (Langmead 2010). The Bedgraph files were scaled to 10 million total autosomal reads (the X chromosome reads were not included for scaling due to the different X:A ratios between sperm) and converted to bigwig by using the bedGraphToBigWig UCSC Genome Browser tool (Zhang et al. 2008). Peak annotations were conducted with the R-Bioconductor package ChIPseeker v1.33.1 (Kent et al. 2010). ChIP-seq data of histone post-translational modifications from sperm were downloaded from the GEO database (Series GSE115709) (Tabuchi et al. 2018) and analyzed with MACS2 and ChIPseeker with the same settings as described above.

RNA-extraction and sequencing

During the final stages of sperm formation, a large portion of RNAs are jettisoned from the cell (Ward et al. 1981; Stoeckius et al. 2014). Thus, to purify enough RNA we focused on whole male gonads expressing RNA during sperm formation. Male gonads were dissected and isolated from N2, XC18 htas-1(tm1849), or EKM11 htz-1(tm2469) V/nT1 [qIs51] (IV;V) (M+Z−) homozygous adult males. Hermaphrodite gonads were collected from N2 or EKM11 htz-1(tm2469) V/nT1 [qIs51] (IV;V) (M+Z−) homozygous hermaphrodites. Embryos were collected from N2 or XC18 htas-1(tm1849) adult hermaphrodites. Tissues were placed into trizol and flash-frozen. Three replicates were analyzed for each condition. RNA was prepared and sequenced by GENEWIZ (NEBExt Ultra II RNA library Prep Kit with poly-A selection, Illumina HiSeq4000, PE150).

RNA-seq processing, alignment, and differential expression analysis

Adapter sequences were removed from paired-end RNA-seq reads (∼150bps) using the “-m 1” function of Cutadapt v2.10. FastQCv0.11.9 was used to filter high-quality reads (Andrews 2010). Trimmed and filtered reads were aligned to the C. elegans reference genome (WormBase version WS235) using STAR v2.7.3a (Dobin et al. 2013). Read counts per gene were obtained using HTSeq v0.12.4 (Putri et al. 2022). Genes with fewer than 10 total counts across all samples were excluded prior to downstream analysis to remove low-abundance transcripts with unreliable estimates.

Differential expression analysis was performed in R using DESeq2 v1.30.0 (Love et al. 2014) on raw HTSeq count data. The design formula was “∼ condition”, comparing htz-1 and htas-1 mutants against wild-type controls. Log2-fold changes were estimated and shrunk using the apeglm method. Genes were considered significantly differentially expressed (DE) if the adjusted P-value (FDR) was <0.05 and log2 absolute fold-change >1, resulting in only genes having over 2-fold-change represented. Visualization of expression-dependent trends was performed in R using ggplot2, ggrepel, and RColorBrewer to generate volcano plots.

To ensure DE genes do not reflect stochastic low-count behavior, normalized counts across biological replicates in each dataset were examined. Replicate support was defined as a normalized count ≥5 in all replicates of at least one condition to determine whether observed changes were consistently detected across samples rather than driven by random variation. Under this conservative, replicate-consistency criterion, 76% to 100% of DEGs across datasets were replicate-supported, with more than 90% supported in three datasets and 76% supported in the HTAS-1 embryo dataset. Further, DE genes were not driven by near-zero normalized counts in at least half of biological replicates within each condition (≥3/6 replicates in the embryo dataset; ≥2/3 in the others).

Comparative gene sets

ChIP-seq and RNA-seq datasets were compared with the following previously published datasets. Germline expressed genes found either in sperm-producing (sperm-2,498 genes), oocyte-producing (oocyte-1,671 genes) or those in both (sex-independent-5,843 genes, originally called gender-neutral) were identified and classified by (Ortiz et al. 2014). Genes with expression in embryos (8,152 genes) are from (Baugh et al. 2003). Gene sets associated with histone post-translational modifications are from (Tabuchi et al. 2018). Genes associated with HTZ-1 in embryos are from (Whittle et al. 2008). Statistical significance of the overlap for differentially regulated genes, ChIP-bound genes, and post-translationally modified genes was evaluated using a one or two-tailed Fisher's exact test implemented via the fisher.test() function in R. Testing was applied to 2 × 2 contingency tables, which contained counts of: (1) all 20,665 protein-coding genes as the background when comparing to germline or embryo-expressed genes, (2) protein-coding genes unbound/unmodified by either histones or PTM marks, (3) coding genes bound/modified by one histone or PTM mark, (4) coding genes bound/modified solely by the other histone or PTM mark, and (5) coding genes bound or modified by both. P-values shown as =0 or <2.2e−16 result from extremely small P-values calculated by Fisher's exact test, which are represented this way due to the limits of double-precision floating-point representation in R.

Heatmap analysis was conducted using Heatmapper using average clustering and the Pearson Distance Measurement Method (Babicki et al. 2016). GO term and tissue enrichment was conducted using the Enrichment tool found at WormBase (https://wormbase.org/tools/enrichment/tea/tea.cgi) (Angeles-Albores et al. 2016, 2018).

Results

The sperm-specific H2A variant HTAS-1 functions in fertility by optimizing sperm production in males and hermaphrodites

We first sought to determine differences in H2A variant function during spermatogenesis. HTZ-1 is required for germ cell development and fertility; it is present at high levels in all developing sperm and oocyte cell nuclei and loss of HTZ-1 results in sterility and embryonic lethality (Whittle et al. 2008; Samson et al. 2014; Negishi et al. 2022). However, the role of HTAS-1 is unknown. One hypothesis is that the sperm-specific H2A variant HTAS-1 is required for the tight compaction of sperm chromatin. We thus examined the consequence of loss of HTAS-1 on germ cell progression during spermatogenesis. We isolated, fixed, and immunostained nuclei in male gonads from wild-type N2 males and htas-1 null mutants (Fig. 1a). In wild-type N2 males, HTAS-1 becomes detectable in late stages of sperm formation as chromosomes condense into compact spermatid nuclei. The gross morphology of late-stage and spermatid DNA in htas-1 null mutants was not measurably different than in wild-type N2 males (Fig. 1a, data not shown). Thus, although HTAS-1 is incorporated in later stages of spermatogenesis, it is not required for the tight packaging of spermatid chromatin.

Another potential role for HTAS-1 in sperm is to optimize fertility. To test this, we compared progeny production of htas-1 mutants to wild-type N2 animals (see Materials and methods). For hermaphrodites, which express HTAS-1 during sperm formation, loss of HTAS-1 resulted in a 15% reduction in self-progeny production (Fig. 1b). While some individual hermaphrodites produced progeny at levels comparable to wild-type, others produced significantly fewer progeny. Next, we assessed male fertility by mating htas-1 mutant males to spe-8 mutant hermaphrodites, which do not self-fertilize (L’Hernault et al. 1988; Stanfield and Villeneuve 2006; Muhlrad et al. 2014). Loss of HTAS-1 resulted in a 29% reduction in cross-progeny number compared with wild-type (Fig. 1c). Thus, HTAS-1 is important for sperm function. Finally, to rule out HTAS-1 function in hermaphrodite oocyte function, we assessed cross-progeny production of htas-1 mutant hermaphrodites mated with wild-type males. Mated htas-1 mutant hermaphrodites produced similar numbers of cross-progeny as mated wild-type N2 hermaphrodites (Fig. 1d). Taken together, these results support that HTAS-1 is important for sperm function in males and hermaphrodites.

To assess how HTAS-1 affects sperm development, we next compared wild-type N2 and htas-1 mutant germ cell progression by counting developing germ cells and mature sperm in synchronized unmated adult males (see Materials and methods). We found the same number and proportions of developing germ cells between htas-1 mutant and wild-type N2 males (Fig. 1e). However, htas-1 mutant males produced 17% fewer mature spermatids than wild-type. This represents an average rate of production of ∼1.19 sperm/min for the wild-type compared with 0.98 sperm/min for htas-1 mutants. Our findings show that the loss of function htas-1 impacts sperm production and fertility, revealing an important role of HTAS-1 to optimize reproductive fitness.

Lack of HTAS-1 causes developmental delay

Because HTAS-1 is delivered paternally and retained in the embryo, we posited that HTAS-1 could affect development. We thus tracked the development of htas-1 mutant and wild-type N2 animals by synchronizing 100 L1 larvae, then assessing larval stages every 12 h for 100 h (Fig. 1f). By 36 h we detected a delay in progression through the L2-L3 stages. This delay was more pronounced by 48 h; whereas wild-type animals were in L3/L4 stages, 10% to 15% of htas-1 mutants were still L2 s. At 60 h, wild-type N2 animals had progressed to L4/Young Adults while htas-1 mutants were still in L3/L4 stages. Wild-type N2 animals were gravid by 72 h, while htas-1 mutants were not gravid until 84 h. This delay suggests another facet of how HTAS-1 can optimize reproductive fitness by influencing the development of progeny through its paternal delivery to the embryo.

HTAS-1 and HTZ-1 have distinct incorporation profiles in spermatid chromatin

The function of HTAS-1 in fertility suggests that, as a histone variant, it may regulate gene expression during sperm formation. Both HTAS-1 and HTZ-1 are present in sperm and are under-represented on the transcriptionally repressed X chromosome (Chu et al. 2006; Samson et al. 2014); however, HTAS-1 and HTZ-1 are incorporated into chromatin at different times during spermatogenesis, which we hypothesize may lead to distinct patterns of incorporation. To determine the incorporation patterns of HTAS-1 and HTZ-1 in spermatids and therefore on the paternal chromatin delivered to oocytes, we performed chromatin immunoprecipitation followed by sequencing (ChIP-seq) on solubilized formaldehyde-fixed mononucleosomes from purified C. elegans spermatids (Tabuchi et al. 2018) (Fig. 2a, Materials and methods). Using strict criteria to define HTAS-1 and HTZ-1 bound genes (bound in 3/3 replicates for HTAS-1 and 2/2 replicates for HTZ-1), we found 901 genes associated with HTAS-1 and 2,694 genes associated with HTZ-1 in C. elegans sperm (Supplementary Tables 1 and 2 in File S1). We identified 673 genes associated with both HTAS-1 in 3/3 replicates and HTZ-1 in 2/2 replicates (Fig. 2a). Thus, a large proportion of HTZ-1 incorporation sites (75%) are distinct from those bound by HTAS-1.

HTAS-1 and HTZ-1 incorporate at genes that promote germ cell or embryo development

One hypothesis is that HTAS-1 or HTZ-1 incorporates during spermatogenesis at genes that function in sperm formation or early development. We thus determined whether HTAS-1- and HTZ-1-bound genes had been previously identified as expressed in germlines either during sperm formation (sperm genes), oocyte formation (oocyte genes), or during both sperm and oocyte formation (sex-independent) (Ortiz et al. 2014). We found that 89% of HTAS-1-bound genes and 90% of HTZ-1-bound targets are germline expressed genes (Fig. 2b). However, HTAS-1 and HTZ-1 associated with a small number of sperm genes compared with germline sex-independent genes: HTAS-1 associated with 2% sperm, 14% oocyte, and 73% sex-independent genes, while HTZ-1 associated with 4% sperm, 14% oocyte, and 72% sex-independent genes. In terms of embryo function, 45% of HTAS-1 and 44% of HTZ-1 bound genes exhibit embryonic expression (Baugh et al. 2003). Thus, though HTAS-1 is a sperm-specific H2A variant, it does not incorporate exclusively at genes important for spermatogenesis. Rather, like HTZ-1, it is incorporated at genes important for general germ cell function and development regardless of sex.

HTAS-1 and HTZ-1 display different patterns of chromatin association in spermatids

H2A.Z incorporates at promoters in many organisms and tissues, including during C. elegans embryogenesis and larval stages (Fan et al. 2002; Raisner et al. 2005; Whittle et al. 2008 #11; Latorre et al. 2015; Giaimo et al. 2019), but H2A variant incorporation during spermatogenesis is unknown. To determine differences in gene-level patterns of HTZ-1 and HTAS-1 during sperm formation, we examined their distributions in sperm chromatin relative to the TSS of bound genes. For the profiles of the 2021 genes bound by HTZ-1 only, we found a pattern similar to that observed in embryos (Whittle et al. 2008): HTZ-1 incorporates largely around the TSS in sperm, with peaks before and after the TSS (Fig. 2c, solid olive line). In contrast, the pattern of all 901 genes bound by HTAS-1 (Fig. 2c, HTAS-1 only [solid gold line] or the subset of HTAS-1 genes that overlap with HTZ-1 [dotted gold line]) display a dip before the TSS then gene body enrichment after the TSS, indicating that HTAS-1 is incorporated into gene bodies but not promoters. Notably, the incorporation pattern of HTZ-1 at targets also occupied by HTAS-1 displays diminished promoter enrichment upstream of the TSS (Fig. 2c, dotted olive line). Our finding that a subset of HTZ-1 sites exhibit depletion in the promoter indicates that HTZ-1 is likely not incorporated by promoter-situated chromatin remodelers at sites shared by HTAS-1. These results suggest that HTAS-1 and HTZ-1 are incorporated at distinct subsets of genes by different mechanisms.

Incorporation of HTAS-1 and HTZ-1 correlate with different active histone marks

Genes expressed during spermatogenesis carry a unique multivalent set of histone post-translational modifications (Tabuchi et al. 2018). This consists of three marks: trimethylation of histone H3 at lysine 4 (H3K4me3) and trimethylation of histone H3 at lysine 36 (H3K36me3), which correlate with active transcription, and the repressive mark of trimethylation of histone H3 at lysine-27 (H3K27me3) (Kolasinska-Zwierz et al. 2009; Rechtsteiner et al. 2010; Tabuchi et al. 2018; Kaneshiro et al. 2019). One hypothesis is that H2A variant occupancy in sperm correlates with these marks. We therefore determined the correlation of HTAS-1 and HTZ-1 occupancy with key post-translationally modified (PTM) histones that indicate active and repressive chromatin in sperm (Samson et al. 2014; Zhang et al. 2015; Tabuchi et al. 2018; Laugesen et al. 2019; Chan et al. 2022; Sharda and Humphrey 2022).

We find that HTAS-1 and HTZ-1 are not incorporated at genes with the sperm multivalent marking of H3K36me3, H3K4me3, and H3K27me3 (Tabuchi et al. 2018). Instead, ∼54% of HTAS-1 and HTZ-1-bound genes carry the active mark H3K4me3, and ∼47% to 48% of HTAS-1 and HTZ-1-bound genes carry H3K36me3 (Fig. 2d). In contrast, few HTAS-1 (5%) and HTZ-1 (4%) bound genes overlap with the repressive mark H3K27me3 (Fig. 2e). Thus, epigenetic information conveyed by H2A variants is likely distinct from that carried by sperm multivalent marking (Tabuchi et al. 2018).

Because different histone methylation marks are laid at distinct stages of transcription, we compared HTAS-1 and HTZ-1 profiles with that of H3K4me3, which is enriched at promoters during transcription initiation, and H3K36me3, which is enriched in gene bodies during elongation (Fig. 2c). HTZ-1 bound only (solid olive line) and H3K4me3 (dark teal line) display similar distributions at the promoter, suggesting a correlation between HTZ-1 presence and transcriptional activation. HTZ-1 occupancy falls sharply across gene bodies and is low at the transcription termination site (TTS), while H3K4me3 also decreases across gene bodies but peaks slightly at the TTS. In comparison, HTAS-1 (gold lines) and HTZ-1 overlap with HTAS-1 (dotted olive line) are depleted at the promoter and instead occupies gene bodies and decreases at the TTS. H3K36me3 (light teal line) also occupies gene bodies but increases at the TTS. These patterns support that HTAS-1 incorporation is distinct from HTZ-1 and may be due to mechanisms that occur in the gene body, like transcriptional elongation, histone replacement, and splicing.

H2A variants have tissue-specific incorporation profiles

We wondered if a single H2A variant incorporates distinctly in different tissues. In fact, via immunostaining we find dramatic changes in HTZ-1 levels, where paternal and maternal chromatin-associated levels drop substantially in one-cell embryos, then are high on chromatin in 4-cell embryos (Samson et al. 2014). To determine incorporation differences between sperm and embryos, we compared the 2694 HTZ-1-bound genes in sperm to the 3,650 genes previously found associated to HTZ-1 in embryos (Whittle et al. 2008). 1,361 genes are shared, which is half of the sperm-bound and 37% of the embryo-bound genes (Fig. 3a), indicating that HTZ-1 is incorporated at a substantial number of distinct sites during embryogenesis. This also supports that HTZ-1 is distinctly incorporated in sperm and embryo chromatin.

The significance P-value for overlap using a one-tailed Fisher's exact test is in italics. b) Chromosomal distribution of HTAS-1 and HTZ-1 in sperm and embryos shows they are substantially under-represented on the transcriptionally repressed X chromosome in sperm in contrast to HTZ-1 occupancy in embryos.

We further found differences in H2A variant distribution across C. elegans chromosomes in different tissues. The most notable is that HTAS-1 and HTZ-1 bound genes in sperm are under-represented on the X chromosome compared with the genome-wide distribution of genes and to that of HTZ-1 bound genes in embryos (Fig. 3b, Supplementary Tables 1 and 2 in File S1). We also note differences for distributions of HTAS-1 and HTZ-1-bound genes compared with genome-wide distributions on chromosomes 1, 3, and 5. These results support tissue-specific differences in incorporation for H2A variants, with those on X potentially tied to X chromosome repression during spermatogenesis (Kelly et al. 2002).

HTAS-1 and HTZ-1 influence activation and repression of different sets of genes in the male sperm-producing germline

To further investigate how HTAS-1 and HTZ-1 may regulate genes differently during the highly dynamic sperm formation process, we determined the mRNA profiles of full gonads isolated from wild-type males compared with htas-1 or htz-1 null mutant males (Fig. 4a, see Materials and methods) (Petty et al. 2009; Consortium 2012). A total of 232 genes for HTAS-1 and 1,803 genes for HTZ-1 are significantly DE by 2-fold or more (Fig. 4b). HTAS-1 likely affects fewer genes because its incorporation begins in late pachytene, whereas HTZ-1 is present in all stages (Chu et al. 2006; Samson et al. 2014). Interestingly, only 50 genes overlap, representing 3% of HTZ-1 and 22% of HTAS-1-affected genes. Thus, HTAS-1 and HTZ-1 influence regulation of largely different genes in the male germline.

We next addressed how HTAS-1 regulation of gene expression influences germline function. One hypothesis is that HTAS-1 incorporation could implement the global gene silencing that occurs during late sperm formation (Wu et al. 2010). However, we found HTAS-1 influences gene activation more than repression: 88% (205/232) genes are downregulated (htas-1 DE down) compared with 12% (27/232) genes upregulated (htas-1 DE up) in htas-1 mutant male gonads (Fig. 4c, Supplementary Table 3 in File S1). Thus, HTAS-1 incorporation in later sperm formation is likely not primarily for global gene silencing. An alternative hypothesis would be that HTAS-1 could be activating genes needed for sperm formation (Ortiz et al. 2014). However, 31/205 (15%) of downregulated genes and 6/27 (22%) of upregulated genes in htas-1 mutants were sperm genes, suggesting HTAS-1 is not a primary driver of sperm gene activation. Finally, HTAS-1 could also be repressing genes important for oocyte formation. However, only a small proportion of HTAS-1-regulated genes are oocyte genes, which show both up and downregulation (16/205 [8%] downregulated in htas-1 mutant and 1/27 [4%] upregulated in htas-1 mutants). HTAS-1 also affects sex-independent germline genes (34/205 [17%] of genes downregulated and 10/27 [37%] of upregulated in htas-1 mutants) during spermatogenesis. Overall, 42% of HTAS-1-regulated genes ([81/205] downregulated and [17/27] upregulated in htas-1 mutants) are those with previously identified germline expression (Ortiz et al. 2014). Thus, HTAS-1 may serve different roles in sperm-producing germlines by modulating the dynamics or timing of expression of germline genes during later stages of sperm formation.

Because HTAS-1 is packaged on sperm chromatin and paternally delivered to the embryo (Samson et al. 2014), we hypothesized that, during spermatogenesis, HTAS-1 could influence expression levels of genes that are expressed in embryos (Baugh et al. 2003). For example, HTAS-1 could repress embryo genes not necessary during spermatogenesis. HTAS-1 presence affected 83 genes, 76 of which are downregulated and 7 are upregulated in htas-1 mutants during spermatogenesis that were found previously as embryo-expressed (the embryonic gene class) (Fig. 4c) (Baugh et al. 2003). This indicates HTAS-1 does not solely repress embryo genes during spermatogenesis.

The nongermline genes (135) DE in htas-1 mutants span a variety of potential cellular and development functions (Supplementary Table 3 in File S1). A large proportion 44/135 (33%) are uncharacterized proteins, 42 of which are downregulated in htas-1 mutants. The most highly altered in terms of fold-change are upregulated in htas-1 mutants, suggesting that HTAS-1 may function to ensure tissue-specific repression of a subset of genes. One example is tbx-2, which encodes a T-box transcription factor that functions in neural fate specification and muscle development (Roy Chowdhuri et al. 2006; Smith and Mango 2007; Singhvi et al. 2008). The altered expression of sets of genes with varied functions not normally expressed in sperm or germ cells could contribute to defects in sperm production or function in mutants.

HTZ-1 loss caused downregulation of 652 (35%) and upregulation of 1,151 (65%) of the 1,780 htz-1-affected genes (Supplementary Table 4 in File S1). HTZ-1 regulates more germline genes than HTAS-1 in male germlines. 61% (1083/1780) of genes regulated by HTZ-1 are germline genes (Fig. 4d) (Ortiz et al. 2014). HTZ-1 loss causes both downregulation and upregulation of germline genes as well as those expressed in embryos (Baugh et al. 2003), indicating roles in both gene activation and repression. Overall, the small degree of overlap and differences in the numbers of genes regulated, the expression level differences, and the downregulated and upregulated proportions between HTAS-1 and HTZ-1 mutants indicate that these two H2A variants have distinctive modes of regulation in the male germline.

HTZ-1 plays tissue-specific roles to regulate germline function in hermaphrodites

Because HTZ-1 is ubiquitously expressed, we took the opportunity to test if HTZ-1 has similar or different roles in regulating genes during spermatogenesis vs oogenesis. To do this, we examined hermaphrodite oocyte-producing (oogenic) gonads (Fig. 5a). We purified RNAs from dissected gonads of wild-type and htz-1(tm2489) homozygous mutant hermaphrodites undergoing oogenesis, which are able to reach adulthood because of residual maternally-deposited HTZ-1 from heterozygous mothers (Whittle et al. 2008; Petty et al. 2009).

We found that HTZ-1 has a strong repressive role in the hermaphrodite oocyte-producing gonad. Of the large number of genes (4,546 genes total) affected by loss of htz-1, 3998 (88%) are upregulated compared with 548 (12%) downregulated in the hermaphrodite oogenic germline (Fig. 5b, Supplementary Table 5 in File S1). 62% of these genes were previously identified as germline-expressed genes but only 10% are downregulated by HTZ-1 loss, indicating HTZ-1 has a strong repressive role in the hermaphrodite germline. In particular, HTZ-1 has a very strong repressive influence for sperm genes (1,603 upregulated), which also had large log2-fold-change expression differences. HTZ-1 also represses many sex-independent germline (667) and embryonic (1618) genes (Baugh et al. 2003). In contrast, relatively few oocyte genes were affected by loss of HTZ-1. Again, because HTZ-1 is incorporated in all germ nuclei in the hermaphrodite germline, we surmise that genes upregulated in htz-1 mutants reflect that HTZ-1 may turn genes off or on to drive progression through oocyte developmental stages.

Overall, HTZ-1 affects more genes in the hermaphrodite oocyte-producing germline (4,546 genes) compared with the male sperm-producing germline (1,803 genes). Further, in oocyte-producing germlines, HTZ-1 has a strong repressive role for a large number of germline genes, particularly genes that are expressed during spermatogenesis. Therefore, HTZ-1 has both activating and repressive modes of germline- and tissue-specific transcriptional regulation.

Sperm-supplied HTAS-1 can modulate gene expression in embryos

One potential role for paternally incorporated HTAS-1 that is delivered to oocytes could be to influence gene expression in the embryo. Paternal HTAS-1 is detectable during early embryonic cell divisions but is progressively diluted and becomes undetectable by immunostaining by the 16-cell stage (Samson et al. 2014). To determine the extent to which sperm-delivered HTAS-1 influences embryo gene expression, we conducted RNA-seq analysis on mixed-staged embryos isolated from wild-type and htas-1 mutant hermaphrodites (Fig. 5c).

We detected effects on gene expression in mixed-staged embryos that differed from those regulated during spermatogenesis. 177 genes had expression changes of 2 to 32-fold. HTAS-1 influenced the repression of more genes (144, 81% upregulated) compared with activation (32, 19% regulated) (Fig. 5d, Supplementary Table 6 in File S1). We find 34% genes were germline expressed, while 38% are expressed in embryos, suggesting a proportion of affected genes regulate fertility or developmental processes. Genes with altered expression exhibit a range of potential functions, including 66/177 genes with uncharacterized function (Supplementary Table 6 in File S1). The most highly differentially experssed genes by 16 to 32-fold (including CUB and WD repeat domain encoding proteins) have mutant alleles with no associated phenotypes (Davis et al. 2022). Though some of the expression differences observed in htas-1 mutants may be indirect, we also consider that because HTAS-1 is a general chromatin packaging protein, it may have more distributed and modest effects on gene expression. These distributed changes could cumulatively impact cellular function and development. In addition, 1,114 genes showed expression differences <2-fold, with 494 upregulated and 621 downregulated in htas-1 mutant embryos. Overall, modest changes across a collective of genes could contribute to developmental delay (Fig. 1f).

HTAS-1 marks a subset of genes for regulation during embryogenesis

Thus far, our results show that HTAS-1 is incorporated in sperm chromatin and that loss of HTAS-1 results in fertility and developmental defects, with moderate degrees of changes in gene expression. However, when we compare HTAS-1-bound genes in sperm to genes downregulated or upregulated in htas-1 mutant male germlines, we find little overlap. Only 6 htas-1-downregulated genes and 1 htas-1-upregulated gene expressed 2-fold or higher overlapped with the 901 HTAS-1-bound sites (Supplementary Fig. 2, Supplementary Tables 1 and 3 in File S1). Though we were initially surprised, we reasoned that sperm formation is a highly dynamic process. In the male germline, genes are temporally activated and/or repressed throughout different stages to implement cell fate changes (Kulkarni et al. 2012; Ragle et al. 2020, 2022; Choi et al. 2021; Li et al. 2025). Then, in late spermatogenesis, transcription is largely globally silenced as chromosomes are tightly compacted for efficient delivery to oocytes (Chu and Shakes 2012). Thus, we hypothesize that HTAS-1-bound genes identified in spermatids may represent a small subset of sites that are ultimately packaged by HTAS-1 at the end of spermatogenesis. As such, HTAS-1-regulated genes may be those that are dynamically turned on or off during all stages of sperm formation found in the full germline, while HTAS-1 bound genes in spermatids are those that are paternally marked by HTAS-1 when delivered to the new embryo. Another possibility is that genes affected in embryos may be a result of indirect effects caused by loss of HTAS-1, which as a general chromatin packaging factor, could affect chromatin organization that influences a wider range of genes important for development.

To further examine the potential influence of paternally delivered HTAS-1 on embryonic expression, we used existing time-resolved transcriptomics of embryo development (Boeck et al. 2016). We found that 876 of the 901 HTAS-1-bound genes exhibited expression during embryogenesis in the Boeck et al. study (Fig. 6a). Further, 87% of these genes are expressed in early embryos, before the 200-cell stage, while 13% exhibit expression at later stages of development. These results suggest that paternal HTAS-1 is delivered on genes that can impact embryo development, particularly in early stages.

We also conducted enrichment analysis of Gene Ontology (GO) and Tissue Enrichment (TE) terms to assess the potential roles of HTAS-1 association with genes in spermatids and how those genes may influence sperm or embryo development (Angeles-Albores et al. 2016, 2018). For all 901 HTAS-1-bound genes, GO terms were enriched for those related to reproduction, meiosis, metabolism, and translation, and TE terms that include germline, reproduction, and embryonic cell types (Fig. 6b). This is consistent with our findings that HTAS-1 bound genes are enriched for germline-expressed genes for HTAS-1 bound genes (Fig. 2b).

Discussion

Our study comparing the association of two H2A variants with chromatin in different tissues before and after fertilization in C. elegans defines their distinct incorporation patterns and roles in regulating genes important for fertility and reproduction. We show that the ubiquitously-expressed HTZ-1 incorporates both at promoter regions and gene bodies to affect the activation and repression of genes required for fertility and viability. Further, HTZ-1 has tissue-specific modes of transcriptional regulation to help specify germ cell fate. Our study is also the first to define roles of the H2A variant HTAS-1 in fertility and development. We determined the incorporation profile of this sperm-specific H2A variant, which occupies gene bodies and results in modulation of the expression of a smaller subset of genes that may cumulatively optimize sperm production and impact development after fertilization (summarized in Fig. 7).

A model for histone H2A variant incorporation and function during and after spermatogenesis. Relative H2A variant levels (based on [Samson et al. 2014]) increase during sperm formation to replace S-phase H2A.S. A proportion of HTZ-1 is incorporated at promoters in early meiosis through pachytene for strong transcriptional activation and repression to implement tissue-specific transcriptional programs. At late pachytene, HTAS-1 is incorporated in gene bodies to modulate gene expression at low levels, which contributes to improving male reproductive fitness. HTZ-1 is also incorporated at genes shared with HTAS-1 also within gene bodies, both of which may act to facilitate histone replacement. During fertilization, HTZ-1 is removed from paternal and maternal pronuclei upon fertilization and appears again in early embryos with functions in transcription that are important for development. Paternal HTAS-1 is retained and could act as an epigenetic marker to influence larval development.

While H2A.Z incorporation at promoters is well documented in C. elegans and other organisms, HTAS-1 incorporation mechanisms are likely distinct (Updike and Mango 2006; Whittle et al. 2008; Hardy et al. 2009; Jin et al. 2009; Liu et al. 2011; Giaimo et al. 2019; Iyer 2020). H2A.Z can be incorporated at promoters by the SWR1 chromatin remodeler (Mizuguchi et al. 2004), which recognizes a region known as “M6” that contains the C-terminal alpha helix that is 54% identical to canonical H2A (Supplementary Fig. 1) (Wu et al. 2005; Brewis et al. 2021). HTAS-1 is more divergent in this region than either H2A (34% identical) or HTZ-1 (39% identical), reducing the likelihood that it is recognized by SWR1. Consistent with this, HTAS-1 is depleted in the promoter 5′ to the TSS.

Interestingly, the occupancy pattern of HTZ-1 at targets shared with HTAS-1 is different than at targets with HTZ-1 only. Instead of HTZ-1 levels peaking in the promoter within ∼750 bp 5′ of the TSS, both HTZ-1 and HTAS-1 are incorporated 3′ of the TSS in the gene body of shared targets (Figs. 2c and 7). This distinct profile suggests that H2A variants may not be acting in transcription initiation at these sites (Iyer 2020). It also suggests that HTAS-1 and HTZ-1 may not use the same chromatin remodelers that position HTZ-1 at promoters but instead are incorporated 3′ of the TSS through different mechanisms. In C. elegans L3 larval animals, HTZ-1 is enriched in gene bodies of genes that are largely repressed by the DREAM complex (Latorre et al. 2015; Goetsch et al. 2017); however, the mechanisms of DREAM target gene repression are not yet well characterized (Hoareau et al. 2024).

Another role for gene body incorporation of HTAS-1 or HTZ-1 could be related to histone replacement (Huang et al. 2020; Scacchetti and Becker 2021). For example, incorporation into the gene body may be through transcription-coupled histone exchange (Hirsh et al. 1976; Hall et al. 1999; Robert and Jeronimo 2023). In the syncytial C. elegans germline, nuclei in the distal end are in mitosis, where canonical histones, like H2A.S, are expressed in bulk to package newly synthesized DNA (Fig. 7). Nuclei progress through meiotic prophase during spermatogenesis (which lasts 20–24 h) by transcribing genes needed for each stage, during which H2A-H2B dimers or histone octamers are displaced by passage of RNA-pol II (Kireeva et al. 2002; Belotserkovskaya et al. 2003; Jaramillo-Lambert et al. 2007; Kulaeva et al. 2013). Later-stage nuclei may then incorporate the highly expressed HTAS-1 to replace H2A.S and HTZ-1, resulting in an overall change in H2A composition (Figs. 1a and 7). In fact, our previous studies found a ∼70% reduction of HTZ-1 levels in sperm compared with embryos (Samson et al. 2014). Transcription-coupled histone exchange could be facilitated by a chromatin remodeler like FACT, which assists with chromatin reassembly during transcription elongation in the gene body. Previous studies in C. elegans have shown that the hmg-3 gene, which encodes a component of the FACT complex, exhibits germline-restricted expression in hermaphrodites and males and is required to maintain germline cell fate (Kolundzic et al. 2018; Suggs et al. 2018). The exact mechanisms for how HTAS-1 or HTZ-1 are incorporated in gene bodies during sperm formation can now be further investigated.

The differential fates of HTAS-1 and HTZ-1 post-fertilization further accentuate their distinct functionalities in C. elegans (Fig. 7). HTAS-1 is retained on the paternal pronuclei in the one-cell embryo, even as HTZ-1 is removed from both paternal and maternal chromatin (Samson et al. 2014). In mouse, H2A.Z is also removed after oocyte meiosis and reappears in later embryo stages (Nashun et al. 2010; Boskovic et al. 2012). HTAS-1 retention is distinct from other H2A variants in different organisms. For example, the sperm-specific H2A variants in mouse, H2AL1/2, are incorporated at heterochromatic pericentric regions during spermatogenesis, delivered to the embryo, and subsequently removed when sperm chromatin decondenses (Govin et al. 2007; Wu et al. 2008). Further, we detected defects on gene expression in embryos in htas-1 mutants. While some of these changes may results from indirect effects, we propose that the cumulative effects of relatively moderate gene misregulation could contribute to developmental delay and reduced fertility. This would be one way for how a widely distributed chromatin packaging protein, like HTAS-1, could act as a paternal epigenetic factor that influences gene regulation in the embryo to impact reproductive fitness.

The adaptation of HTAS-1 to contribute to fertility optimization is consistent with the rapid evolution of genes during spermatogenesis (Swanson and Vacquier 2002; Kasimatis and Phillips 2018; Murat et al. 2023). In particular, because C. elegans does not rely on classic 5-methyl cytosine DNA methylation as an epigenetic regulator (Simpson et al. 1986), adaptations or modifications to histone variants can have critical roles in gene expression. This is because single changes in an H2A variant could influence expression of many genes in the vicinity of incorporation of that H2A variant into chromatin or even those effected downstream of HTAS-1 targets indirectly. We previously found that the amino acid sequence of HTAS-1 is highly diverged from core H2A, despite its relatively recent evolution within Caenorhabditis species, supporting a rapid evolution rate (Singh et al. 2024). While rapid evolution can improve sperm function by affecting gamete recognition or sperm head shape, fine-tuning gene expression of cadres of genes could provide important selective advantages (Wang et al. 2024). The combined downstream effects of amino acid changes in an H2A variant, as a general chromatin packaging protein that is incorporated across the genome, may combine to offer reproductive advantages that changing a single gene could not. Thus, testing mutants of individual genes with expression differences in htas-1 mutants will likely not recapitulate defects in sperm production or developmental defects. Instead, our study is one example of how genetic analysis of a single chromatin factor can show how cumulative, distributed, and/or indirect changes influenced by that factor can lead to phenotypic differences that contribute to reproductive fitness advantages.

As a rapidly-evolving protein, C. elegans HTAS-1 is distinct from other sperm- or testes-specific H2A variants, which, in other organisms, are shorter than canonical H2A (Molaro et al. 2018). Nucleosomes composed of short H2As form more open chromatin through wrapping shorter stretches of DNA (120 to 130 bp) compared with those with canonical H2A (∼150 bp) (Bao et al. 2004; Gautier et al. 2004; Syed et al. 2009; Arimura et al. 2013; Wong and Tremethick 2025). Some short H2A variants localize in promoter regions of occupied genes (Soboleva et al. 2012). Others, like mouse H2A.L.2 and Th2a, facilitate histone replacement (Shinagawa et al. 2015; Barral et al. 2017). This contrasts with HTAS-1, which is longer than canonical H2A, resides in gene bodies, and is retained in spermatid chromatin. While macroH2A is also a longer H2A variant, it is unlike HTAS-1 in that macroH2A has a large C-terminal ADP-ribose binding domain and is expressed in many tissues and organisms (Rivera-Casas et al. 2016; Sun and Bernstein 2019; Grau-Bove et al. 2022; Osakabe and Molaro 2022). Thus, C. elegans HTAS-1 has evolved distinctly from other H2A variants, in terms of structure, incorporation, and regulation of expression of germline genes both before and after fertilization.

Our work in C. elegans provides an example of how multiple, diverged H2A variants provide avenues to improve reproductive fitness. While HTAS-1 evolved recently and rapidly in a subset of Caenorhabditis species, HIS-35, which differs from H2A.S by only one amino acid, is ancestral to Caenorhabditis and its sister group (Singh et al. 2024). We have posited that HIS-35 enables H2A expression across the cell cycle, providing partially redundant functions with H2A.S or other variants. The expression of multiple H2A-like proteins would maintain relative H2A levels (Fig. 7) and allow for partial compensation of any loss of H2A function, which could manifest as mild defects in gene expression or fertility when lost. For example, htas-1 and his-35 mutants are each partially infertile but htas-1, his-35 double mutants are not viable (unpublished data). Redundancy can also provide a safeguard for maintaining H2A levels that would enable the rapid evolution of sperm-specific variants, like HTAS-1, to adopt new functions that provide reproductive advantages over time. Our finding that misregulation of genes in the absence of HTAS-1 suggests that cumulative gene expression changes can impact reproductive success. Overall, our studies show that histone H2A variants have differential roles in impacting fertility and reproduction.
