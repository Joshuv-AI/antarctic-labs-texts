Study on Failure Mechanism and Numerical Simulation of Argillaceous Slate in Southeastern Guizhou
Authors: Jinfeng Zhang, Wuzhou Zhang, Kunpeng Lu, Genlan Yang
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe content of quartz and clay minerals has a crucial influence on the mechanical properties of slate, which is manifested by the differences in microscopic displacement and energy conversion during uniaxial compression. With Qiandongnan argillaceous slate in Guizhou Province as the research object, the parameters for numerical simulation were determined through indoor uniaxial tests, and argillaceous slate models with different mineral contents were established to analyze crack formation and energy conversion during uniaxial compression. The damage rate of the specimen was qualitatively analyzed by the fractal dimension of the crack. The results show that ① the number of fractures increases with the increase of quartz content, and the number of cracks in Group 4 is 33.4% higher than that in Group 1, and macroscopic fractures are dominated by cracks while microscopic fractures by tension fractures, accompanied by a small amount of shear; ② the energy change curves of the four groups of specimens are consistent with the stress–strain curves, which are divided into three stages, namely elastic deformation, yield, and strength loss. The strain energy in the strength loss stage is negatively correlated with the dissipated energy, and the dissipated energy of the four groups of specimens is positively correlated with the content of quartz; ③ the damage rates for the specimens in Groups 1 to 4 quantitatively analyzed using fracture fractal dimension are 56.8, 59.1, 65.8, and 70.7%, respectively. The research results obtained in this article have further improved the failure mechanism of argillaceous slate under uniaxial compression.

Introduction

The internal energy of rocks will be converted under the action of external load. According to the laws of thermodynamics, energy dissipation is the essential attribute of rock deformation and failure. In the uniaxial compression process, with the increase of stress, microscopic displacement occurs inside the rock, and cracks begin to expand and develop into microcracks, which reduces the strength of rocks, aggravates deformation, and accelerates the increase and development of cracks. As a consequence, more rock energy dissipates, which promotes the positive feedback loop of increased cracks. Therefore, research on rock failure characteristics under external load is of particular importance to geotechnical engineering stability analysis.

At present, scholars at home and abroad have made numerous attempts to analyze energy dissipation and failure mechanisms, with many remarkable results achieved. Yang et al.1 carried out cyclic loading and unloading tests on sandstone under different confining pressures and established the characteristic curve of energy consumption ratio change in the process of stress and strain, which showed that the energy consumption ratio tended to decrease with the increase in confining pressure. Zhang and Gao2 recorded the evolution and distribution of elastic energy and dissipated energy of red sandstone during uniaxial compression and found that the smaller the loading rate, the higher the dissipated energy. Yang et al.3 pointed out that rock failure strain energy, fracture energy, and confining pressure were linearly related. Cheng and Chuanqi4 conducted true triaxial cyclic loading and unloading tests on fractured sandstone and discovered that energy consumption was related to the number of cycles and that the fracture angle of rock specimens had an inhibitory effect on energy dissipation capacity. Li et al.5 studied the change law of sandstone energy index under the action of cyclic loading and unloading. Their findings indicated that the proportion of damping energy was negatively correlated with the stress ratio. The energy dissipation process of rock under external load is summarized at the macro level, but it generally originates from the expansion and development of internal microcracks. Therefore, it is necessary to simulate and analyze the energy dissipation process from the microscopic structure.

The discrete element method is often used to simulate discontinuous media problems. Regarding jointed rock mass as a combination of discrete rock mass and joint surfaces, this method can simulate the development of microcracks and the degree of energy diffusion in the case of a broken medium at the mesoscopic level. Feng et al.6,7 established a rock particle model and summarized the relationship between particle breakage energy and its fractal characteristics. Zhou et al.8 simulated the uniaxial compression of red sandstone using PFC2D, and their research results suggested that dissipation energy promoted the continuous propagation of microcracks. Tang and Li9 simulated the fracture development characteristics of pre-set fractured rock samples in biaxial tests, indicating that the increase in confining pressure inhibited the development of tension fractures but promoted the development of shear fractures. Cong et al.10 explored the influence of material microscopic parameters on macroscopic strength. Their research results demonstrated that the normal stiffness and tangential stiffness between particles were the main reasons for different fracture shapes. As can be seen from the aforementioned research, the expansion of microcracks and the energy conversion process of rocks should be simultaneously analyzed. Furthermore, the mechanical parameters of cracks will be affected by the difference not only in external load but also in the mineral composition of the rock mass. Many scholars have already confirmed the inseparable relationship between rock strength, mineral content, and mineral strength.11−14 There are few related studies on argillaceous slate at present. Argillaceous slate is widely distributed in Qiandongnan, Guizhou, but there is still a lack of analysis on its failure mechanism and energy conversion under external load. Therefore, taking argillaceous slate in southeastern Guizhou as the research object, this article identified the mechanical parameters of specimens by means of an indoor uniaxial compression test. A total of four control groups with different mineral contents were established based on the PFC2D particle flow simulation software, and the uniaxial compression process was simulated by using the fish language embedded in the software. Subsequently, the relationship between the failure mode and energy of specimens was analyzed, and finally, their damage rates were quantitatively analyzed through the crack fractal dimension. The research results are of great significance to the design and construction of geotechnical engineering in related areas.

Results

Indoor Uniaxial Compression Test

The rock specimens used for testing in the laboratory were taken from Rongjiang County, Guizhou Province. The slate was light gray, approximately cuboid in shape, and had a crisp sound when knocked. The internal microcracks fell off in smaller cuboid or layered shapes, with obvious plate-like characteristics. Since the argillaceous slate has high water content and is easy to weather, the specimens were wrapped with plastic immediately after being processed, and their mass and size were recorded before the test. The retrieved raw rock specimens were transported back to the laboratory for cutting and processed into cylindrical specimens with a size of 50 mm × 100 mm according to the specification. When stress was applied in the direction parallel to the layered structure of the slate, friction was generated between thin layers, thereby increasing the overall strength of the slate.

In addition, a displacement-controlled loading method was adopted to keep the displacement rate and strain rate constant in the loading process.

Numerical Model and Test Results

Commonly used rock numerical simulation software includes PFC, FLAC3D, etc. The PFC particle flow software contains contact models with various attributes15−17 such as linear, contact bonded, parallel bonded, or flat joint models, among which the parallel bonded model (ParallelBondModel (PBM)) can well reflect the mechanical behavior of rock materials by using the bonding force between particles and thus mirror the shaping characteristics of rock materials. Therefore, the parallel bonding model was used in this article to provide a certain cross-section shape and thickness for particles, and the bonded components parallel to linear elements were established to give elasticity between the contacts in order to transfer forces and moments between different entities.

The particles were established based on the “ball group” demand according to the identification results of rock ore. The particles of different components were distributed by controlling their areas. And then the macroscopic mechanical parameters of the indoor uniaxial test (see Table 2) were used as the target for parameter calibration. The results showed that the simulation results were very similar to the actual test results. After setting the contact parameters between particles and groups, the velocity was applied to the upper and lower walls through the servo control program and the embedded fish language to load rocks and simulate the uniaxial compression process. As stress balance was reached in the particle loading process and the stress–strain curve of the simulation test did not show crack compaction, the “trial and error method”18,19 was used to adjust the parameters and establish the test model (see Figure 3). The calibration results of the determined particle parameters are shown in Table 3. The simulation results and actual indoor test results were compared, as shown in Figure 4, which were basically consistent.

Discussion

Analysis of Fracture Shape

The difference in mineral content leads to the difference in control factors in the formation of fractures and subsequent development of rocks. The fracture morphology of rocks with high clay mineral content is usually fine and curved, with many branches and discontinuities, while that of rocks with high quartz content is usually straight with sharp edges. The number and size of cracks reflect the physical and mechanical properties and have an impact on compressive strength and elastic modulus. At present, there are few studies on the cracks in argillaceous slate models with different mineral contents. As a result of mine identification, four sets of argillaceous slate models with different mineral contents (Figure 5) were established, and fracture morphology was analyzed after uniaxial compression. A group of test pieces was taken to analyze the fracture development in the stress–strain process, as displayed in Figure 6, where Point B corresponds to the time step when cracks first appeared. According to the slope of the crack growth curve, Point C with a large slope change was added, dividing the failure process of the specimen into four stages.

Histogram of mineral content in numerical simulation group.

Numerical model stress–strain curve and crack growth number.

It can be seen from Figure 6 that crack growth was positively correlated with strain at the initial stage of loading. The bond strength was not achieved in Sections A and B due to the bonding effect between particles. According to Figure 7a, cracks had not yet occurred inside the specimen; after entering Sections B and C, tiny cracks appeared inside the rock. As can be seen from Figure 7b, microcracks expanded, and tensile cracks emerged; after entering Sections C and D, stress continued to increase and cracks expanded more rapidly, presenting a complex distribution and shape. As shown in Figure 7c, there were multiple interlaced and parallel microcracks on the left side of the specimen. Intertwined with each other in the form of fracture aggregates, macroscopic tensile cracks appeared in the specimen and reached peak stress after entering Sections D and E, before cracks increased sharply. At this stage, as the internal force of the rock was reduced to the lowest state, the crack group further increased and a complex network was formed inside the rock; when large cracks in the rock were connected into sections, the strength of the rock was rapidly weakened, and cracks continued to grow until the rock lost its original structure.

Fracture evolution diagram under different monitoring points.

In order to further study the crack morphology characteristics of the numerical model, crack diagrams of the test results on the four groups were drawn using image processing software and then compared with actual test results (Figure 8).

Comparison of crack shapes in uniaxial compression test. Note: The red cracks in (b) are shear cracks, and the blue cracks are tension cracks.

Comparing the crack shapes of the numerical model and indoor test model (see Figure 8a), the macroscopic crack shape of the specimen was dominated by obviously crack-like shear crack, manifested as branches, nets, and multiple branches developed along discontinuous surfaces such as fissures, gaps, and weak surfaces inside the test piece. In addition, the avalanche at the end of the test piece was damaged because of the uniform stress on both sides of the test piece. Furthermore, a weak deformation zone was formed inside the rock. The crack development further caused the specimen to fall off. It can be seen from Figure 8b that the number of shear cracks was far less than that of tension cracks. The reason was that in the uniaxial compression test, the specimen was mainly subjected to compressive stress perpendicular to the loading direction, which was not conducive to formation, so there was a relatively small number of shear cracks.

Analysis of Energy Conversion Mechanism

The deformation of rock mass in the uniaxial compression process was macroscopically manifested as an external load that results in the compacted internal structural surface of rock mass, the deformation, and break of cracks, which was essentially the energy conversion inside it.20,21 Analysis of rock energy evolution in this process can help to identify the energy conversion and storage process of rock under load as well as reveal the intrinsic nature of rock mechanical properties and the response characteristics in complex mechanical environments. In PFC2D, the total energy input during the loading of the specimen was the boundary energy from the boundary of the upper and lower walls, so the boundary energy was defined as the total energy. According to the law of energy conservation, the dissipation energy Ud was defined as the total energy-strain energy. In order to determine the crack formation mechanism and damage characteristics of argillaceous slate specimens under different mineral contents, energy dissipation was taken as the starting point, and that of the four groups in the simulation was recorded. Combined with the analysis of Figure 9 in Section 2.1, the energy change of the specimen in the uniaxial compression process could be divided into three stages:

①Elastic stage: The linear elastic stage experienced by the specimen under the action of stress. This stage corresponds to the crack compaction stage and elastic deformation stage in the stress–strain curve. The rock mass originally had a closed structural surface and the specimen had low hardness, so this stage resumed for a short time. The energy curve tended to be approximately flat, and the internal particles of the specimen experienced microdeformation under the action of external load, resulting in a small amount of energy. There was a small number of cracks, most of which were scattered.

This stage corresponds to the crack compaction stage and elastic deformation stage in the stress–strain curve. The rock mass originally had a closed structural surface and the specimen had low hardness, so this stage resumed for a short time. The energy curve tended to be approximately flat, and the internal particles of the specimen experienced microdeformation under the action of external load, resulting in a small amount of energy. There was a small number of cracks, most of which were scattered.

②Yield stage: When stress continued to increase, a nonlinear yield stage followed, corresponding to the stable fracture stage in the stress–strain curve. The compression deformation of particles gradually increased, and the increase in the absorbed energy of the specimen accelerated the growth of the strain energy, and microcracks began to be damaged, so the dissipated energy slowly increased while the strain energy significantly increased. The energy curve showed an upward concave shape. Driven by the strain energy, cracks gradually expanded and became connected, and then microcracks emerged.

The energy curve showed an upward concave shape. Driven by the strain energy, cracks gradually expanded and became connected, and then microcracks emerged.

③Strength loss stage: The continuous increase in stress made the lower-strength part of the specimen exceed the elastic limit, and thus the specimen entered the plastic deformation stage, which corresponded to the progressive failure stage in the stress–strain curve. Cracks increased sharply, microcracks expanded, and the stress field around cracks resulted in a stress concentration effect. With the progressive development of the fracture, the weaker part was damaged until the specimen was completely destroyed. The expansion of cracks led to a decrease in the strength of the rock and aggravated deformation, which further promoted the increase and development of cracks. The formation of cracks accelerated the energy dissipation of rock, and the energy dissipation promoted the positive feedback cycle of increased cracks, which was released in a large amount, showing an approximate exponential growth trend after peak strength. On the contrary, after stress reached peak strength, the cementation between particles decreased and particles became loose, causing the strain energy curve to drop downward.

After uniaxial compression, the dissipated energy of the four groups of specimens was counted, and the ratios of the dissipated energy of Groups 1–4 to the total energy were 45.46, 48.76, 50.19, and 51.44%, respectively, which was consistent with the occurrence of four groups of cracks. Additionally, increased dissipated energy was accompanied by the generation of cracks, which was consistent with the experimental conditions.

Analysis of Fractal Dimension

The mathematical calculation method was used to study the evolution process of internal complex structure in argillaceous slate specimens.

The change of external stress state was the main reason for the damage to the rock. The fractal dimension of the fracture could reflect the damage degree and stress state of the rock to a certain extent. The clay mineral contents of Groups 1–4 were 66.3, 60.9, 55.4, and 49.9%, respectively, the quartz content was 32.6, 38, 43.5, and 49% respectively, and the compressive strength was 34.251, 34.837, 36.067, and 37.448 MPa, respectively. The analysis of the test results combined with the mineral composition showed that the higher the clay mineral content of the specimens, the lower the elastic modulus and compressive strength, and the higher quartz content of the specimens, the higher the elastic modulus and compressive strength. There were many cohesive structures between particles in clay minerals, and the cohesive force resulted in the increase of the chemical potential energy on the particle surface, thereby loosening the rock and reducing its elastic modulus and compressive strength. In simulation Groups 1–4, with the decrease of clay mineral content and quartz content, the number of cracks tended to increase, and the corresponding rock damage rate also gradually increased, because with the increase of quartz content, compressive strength and hardness increased. When subjected to sufficiently large pressure, quartz particles began to crack and be damaged before other mineral particles, and crack expansion and connection rates increased, resulting in the overall instability and destruction of the specimen, with a higher damage rate.

Conclusions

Taking the indoor uniaxial compression test results of argillaceous slate as the target for parameter calibration, the PFC2D particle flow was modeled. Four sets of argillaceous slate models were established by controlling their mineral composition contents through particle content, and the uniaxial compression was tested. In the test, the PFC2D-embedded fish language was used to monitor the energy change of the specimen in the uniaxial compression process. After the change curves of total energy, strain energy, and dissipated energy were determined based on the first law of thermodynamics, we obtained the following conclusions:

(1) The compressive strength and elastic modulus of the four groups of specimens increase with the increase of the quartz content in the mineral composition, and the number of cracks increases with the decrease of the clay mineral.

(2) When stress reaches its peak in the uniaxial compression process, the number of cracks increases sharply and expands and intersects to form a crack cluster, macroscopic cracks are mostly cracked, and microscopic cracks are mostly tension cracks, accompanied by a small amount of shear cracks.

(3) The energy change curve can be divided into elastic stage, yield stage, and strength loss stage. The energy curve in the elastic stage increases gently. With the change of quartz content, the proportion of dissipated energy in Groups 1–4 increases successively.

(4) The rock damage degree was analyzed by the fracture fractal dimension of the four specimens. The results show that the damage rate of the specimens in Groups 1–4 exhibits an upward trend, and the higher the quartz content of the specimens, the faster the fracture expands, the greater the connection rate, and finally the higher the damage rate.
