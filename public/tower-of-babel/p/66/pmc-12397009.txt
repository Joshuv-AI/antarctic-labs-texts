Treat‐To‐Target: Emergence of Second‐Generation CD40L Inhibitors for Treatment of SLE—Identifying Beneficial Patient Candidates for CD40L Inhibitors in a Cross‐Sectional SLE Cohort
Authors: Kathrine Pedersen, Annette Gudmann Hansen, Yaseelan Palarasah, Anne Troldborg, Steffen Thiel
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSystemic Lupus Erythematosus (SLE) is a chronic autoimmune disease characterised by heterogeneous clinical manifestations and varying degrees of organ involvement. The CD40‐CD40 Ligand (CD40L) pathway is implicated in autoimmune responses, with elevated levels of soluble CD40L (sCD40L) observed in SLE patients. This study investigates sCD40L as a biomarker for disease activity and its utility in stratifying patients for CD40L‐targeted therapies. Plasma levels of sCD40L were quantified in SLE patients (n = 169) and healthy controls (n = 100). Correlations between sCD40L levels and disease activity measures were analysed using Spearman's correlation and logistic regression. K‐means clustering grouped patients based on sCD40L concentrations, and principal component analysis (PCA) was performed to assess relationships among disease activity variables. SLE patients exhibited significantly higher sCD40L levels (median 2.2 ng/mL) than healthy controls (median 0.81 ng/mL; p = 0.0079). Elevated sCD40L levels correlated weakly with higher SLE Disease Activity Index (SLEDAI) scores (p = 0.0418), positive Anti‐Nuclear Antibody status (p = 0.0133), increased IgG levels (p = 0.0148) and decreased lymphocyte counts (p = 0.0327). Clustering analysis and PCA revealed that patients with higher sCD40L levels tended to have increased disease activity, elevated anti‐dsDNA antibody concentrations, and higher C‐reactive protein (CRP) levels. Elevated sCD40L levels in SLE patients correlate with disease activity markers, suggesting its potential as a biomarker for monitoring disease progression and severity. Additionally, sCD40L may aid in stratifying patients who could benefit from CD40L‐targeted therapies within a treat‐to‐target framework. Further longitudinal studies with larger cohorts are warranted to validate and explore the role of sCD40L in personalised SLE treatment strategies.Keywords: biomarker, CD40L inhibitors, sCD40L

Introduction

Systemic Lupus Erythematosus (SLE) is a chronic, systemic autoimmune disease. SLE affects primarily women (9:1 female to male ratio) and is characterised by being a heterogeneous disease [1], presenting with varying disease severity and clinical manifestations, as well as varying degrees of organ involvement. Heterogeneity complicates treatment efforts and requires lifelong management. Current treatment approaches for SLE involve corticosteroids and general immunosuppressants that provide symptomatic relief but often lead to side effects and comorbidities that compromise patient quality of life and long‐term outcomes. Administration of corticosteroids frequently persists even long after an initial diagnosis, unfortunately contributing to complications and increased mortality among SLE patients [2].

Although advances in biologics have significantly improved the treatment of autoimmune diseases, especially rheumatoid arthritis (RA), the options for SLE remain limited to only two approved monoclonal antibodies [3]: Belimumab, targeting the B‐cell activating factor (BAFF) [4] and Anifrolumab, an inhibitor of the type I interferon receptor [5]. This limitation highlights the pressing need for targeted treatments in SLE.

CD40L (CD154 or gp39) is a trimeric type II transmembrane protein of the tumour necrosis factor (TNF) superfamily [6, 7, 8]. It is primarily expressed on activated T cells, with additional expression on activated B cells and platelets. It can also be present in a soluble form, soluble CD40L (sCD40L), due to proteolytic cleavage of membrane‐bound CD40L facilitated by different proteases and metalloproteinases [6, 9]. Cleavage of CD40L from activated platelets is the primary source of soluble CD40L in healthy individuals. However, CD40L can also be cleaved from activated T cells and, while platelets remain the major source of sCD40L, activated T cells can significantly contribute to sCD40L levels in an inflammatory and autoimmune context [10]. The binding of CD40L to its receptor, CD40, especially present on B cells, provides an important co‐stimulatory signal for T‐dependent B‐cell activation and differentiation [7, 8, 11]. In an autoimmune setting, the CD40‐CD40L pathway is often dysregulated [12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17]. Several studies have identified overexpression of CD40 and CD40L on RNA and protein level, as well as overactivation of the CD40‐CD40L pathway in SLE patients [13, 14, 18, 19]. Furthermore, demethylation of the CD40L gene has been identified to contribute to CD40L gene overexpression in female lupus patients [20, 21]. The CD40‐CD40L pathway is an emerging therapeutic target due to its significant role in promoting T‐dependent B‐cell activation and subsequent autoantibody production. Elevated levels of sCD40L in SLE patients suggest its potential as a biomarker for disease activity and as an indication for implementing novel targeted therapies [13, 14, 15, 22].

With the emergence of promising CD40L inhibitors and the outlook of implementing them in SLE treatment, it is important to identify the patients that would benefit from anti‐CD40L treatment.

Second‐generation CD40L inhibitors, designed to mitigate the thromboembolic risks of earlier tested CD40L inhibitors, show promise in treating autoimmune diseases. The second‐generation inhibitors work by blocking CD40L while avoiding Fc‐mediated side effects observed in the first trials (first trials: Ruplizumab (BG9588) and Toralizumab (IDEC‐131)) [23, 24, 25, 26, 27, 28]. Letolizumab (BMS‐986004) and Tegoprubart (At‐1501) were some of the first second‐generation anti‐CD40L antibodies with modified versions of the Fc domain unable to bind the FcγRIIA on platelets [29, 30, 31, 32, 33]. Further, three therapeutic agents aiming to inhibit the CD40‐CD40L interaction are currently in phase III clinical trials: Frexalimab (SAR441344) [34, 35], Dapirolizumab (CDP7657) [36, 37, 38] and Dazodalibep (VIB4920) [39, 40, 41, 42, 43].

Previous studies have demonstrated how sCD40L is elevated and dysregulated in SLE [13, 14, 15]. These studies underline the importance of the CD40‐CD40L pathway in SLE and the potential of utilising CD40L as a biomarker for disease activity and severity.

In the updated version of the EULAR recommendation for treatment of SLE, there is an increased focus on a treat‐to‐target (T2T) strategy, which aims to achieve optimal disease control and reduce corticosteroid use [44, 45]. Given the therapeutic advances and the increasing emphasis on T2T strategies, it becomes crucial to identify SLE patients who would benefit the most from specific targeted treatments. Furthermore, the recent ACR and EULAR guidelines recommending earlier and more aggressive use of biologics for managing lupus nephritis underlines the need to identify the most appropriate biologic treatment for each individual patient [46, 47].

In this study, we investigate sCD40L as a biomarker of disease activity and evaluate its utility in stratifying patients for CD40L‐targeted therapy.

Plasma samples from 100 healthy controls were collected from blood donors at the blood bank of Aarhus University Hospital, Denmark.

Plasma samples were obtained by collecting blood in EDTA plasma tubes (Alere #367525) and centrifuging samples at 2000 × g for 10 min. Plasma was collected and aliquoted into Eppendorf tubes, diluted ¼ in Tris Buffered Saline (TBS) and stored at −80°C until analysis.

Quantifying Soluble CD40L—Time‐Resolved Immunofluorometric Assay

SCD40L was quantified in plasma samples from SLE (n = 169) and healthy controls (n = 100). A Time‐Resolved Immunofluorometric assay (TRIFMA) developed in‐house [49] was used to quantify sCD40L in plasma samples. A detailed description of the assay is provided in Pedersen et al. (2024) [49]. In brief, monoclonal anti‐CD40L antibodies are coated in microtiter wells, the diluted samples are added, and bound sCD40L is detected with another monoclonal biotinylated anti‐CD40L antibody. This is followed by adding europium‐labelled streptavidin and monitoring of europium in the wells by time‐resolved fluorometry.

Statistics

Statistical analysis of data was performed using GraphPad Prism 10.3.1 (GraphPad Software).

Normality was assessed, and non‐parametric tests were applied where data deviated from Gaussian distribution. Data not following Gaussian distribution were expressed as median (Interquartile range (IQR), Q1–Q3). p values ≤ 0.05 were considered statistically significant (α = 0.05). Statistical analysis and considerations were conducted under the supervision of a biostatistician from the Biostatistical Advisory Service (BIAS) core facility at Aarhus University.

The diagnostic potential of sCD40L was assessed using receiver operating characteristics (ROC) analysis. Correlations with disease activity were analysed using Spearman's correlation, logistic regression and Principal Component Regression. For the Spearman's correlation, a mix of continuous and categorical variables was included. The categorical variables were only included if they were interpreted as ordinal variables. Positive likelihood ratios (LR+) were obtained from ROC and logistic regressions. To avoid the issue with multiple testing, a Principal Component Regression was conducted to identify if disease measures have a causal relationship with sCD40L.

The Elbow method was used for the determination of clustering [50]. For clustering analysis, the unsupervised learning algorithm K‐means clustering was implemented, clustering data based on sCD40L levels (Figure S1). Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was conducted to assess the variance explained by different disease measures across clusters. Principal Component 1 (PC1) represents the disease measures that induced the most variation in data, while PC2 explains the second dimension of variance to the data perpendicular to PC1. In the low sCD40L group (cluster 1), PC1 (30.95%) and PC2 (21.88%) accounted for 52.83% of the variance of data for disease measures. In the medium‐high sCD40L group (cluster 2), PC1 (32.19%) and PC2 (23.79%) accounted for 55.98% of the variance in data. In the high sCD40L group (cluster 3), PC1 (43.96%) and PC2 (33.63%) cumulatively accounted for 77.59% of the variance of the data.

Ethics Statement

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The study was approved by the Danish Data Protection Agency and the Regional Committee of Healthy Research Ethics (case #1‐10‐72‐214‐13).

Results

Soluble CD40L Is Significantly Increased in Plasma Samples From SLE Patients but Cannot Be Utilised as a Diagnostic Biomarker

Quantification of sCD40L in SLE patients (n = 169) and healthy controls (n = 100) revealed significantly higher sCD40L levels in SLE patients (2.2 ng/mL, 0.81–5.95 ng/mL) compared to healthy controls (0.81 ng/mL, 0.81–5.158 ng/mL) (Mann–Whitney U‐test, p = 0.0079, Figure 1A). Thus, sCD40L could be a valuable marker in SLE, as implied in previous studies [13, 14, 16, 17, 22, 51].

To investigate whether sCD40L could serve as a biomarker in SLE, we first analysed the diagnostic potential of sCD40L. A ROC analysis demonstrated that sCD40L could serve as a diagnostic biomarker (AUC = 0.59, p = 0.0123, Figure 1B); however, testing for sCD40L would have to sacrifice both sensitivity and specificity (e.g., sCD40L cut‐off of 0.905 ng/mL equaled sensitivity = 59.76% and specificity = 61%, LR+ = 1.532). Thus, sCD40L does not offer any value regarding the diagnosis of SLE based on this cross‐sectional cohort, even though sCD40L levels are significantly higher in SLE patients.

Evaluation of sCD40L Concentrations and Correlations to SLE Disease Activity and Manifestations

By dividing SLE patients into patients with low disease activity (SLEDAI < 4) and patients with active disease (SLEDAI ≥ 4), we find that patients considered to have active disease presented with significantly higher levels of sCD40L (4.0 ng/mL, 0.81–8.1 ng/mL) compared to patients with low disease activity (0.81 ng/mL, 0.81–5.075 ng/mL) (Mann–Whitney U‐test, p = 0.0111, Figure 1C). Thus, sCD40L seems to be associated with disease activity (SLEDAI score).

Next, we investigated if the levels of sCD40L correlated with other measures of disease activity and SLE manifestations, that is, a correlation analysis of sCD40L with 37 different variables (Figure 2A). The levels of sCD40L positively correlated with SLEDAI, meaning that patients with a higher SLEDAI score also presented with increased sCD40L levels (p = 0.0418, Spearman r = 0.157). However, only a weak correlation was observed without subdividing patients into active and inactive diseases (Figure S2A).

The levels of sCD40L also showed a positive correlation with the current Anti‐Nuclear Antibody (ANA) status of the patients (p = 0.0133, Spearman r = 0.190), and a division of patients into ANA positive and ANA negative demonstrated significantly higher levels of sCD40L in ANA positive patients (2.4 ng/mL, 0.81–6.3 ng/mL) compared to ANA negative patients (0.81 ng/mL, 0.81–1.283 ng/mL) (Mann–Whitney U‐test, p = 0.0119, Figure 2B). Again, only a weak correlation was identified. The number of ANA‐negative patients was relatively low (n = 10). Therefore, we conducted a logistic regression analysis of all SLE patients, which supported the correlation between sCD40L and current ANA status (Figure S3A,B, p = 0.0095, LR+ = 6.733, AUC = 0.725).

From the initial correlation analysis, we identified that IgG levels correlated with sCD40L in plasma samples from SLE patients. A weak positive correlation between levels of IgG and sCD40L was demonstrated (p = 0.0148, Spearman r = 0.187, Figure S2B). Stratification of patients into normal (< 14.9 g/L) and abnormally high (> 14.9 g/L) IgG levels revealed that patients with higher IgG levels in the blood presented with significantly higher concentrations of sCD40L (4.1 ng/mL, 0.905–10.65 ng/mL) compared to patients with normal IgG levels (1.8 ng/mL, 0.81–5.85 ng/mL) (Mann–Whitney U‐test, p = 0.0273, Figure 2C). Finally, a weak negative correlation was identified between sCD40L concentration and lymphocyte counts (p = 0.0327, Spearman r = −0.166, Figure S2C).

No other measures of SLE disease activity or manifestations correlated with levels of sCD40L. This included platelets counts, a potential confounding variable, due to the possible contribution to sCD40L levels from activated platelets. No significant association between sCD40L levels and platelet counts were identified (Spearman r = 0.0083, p = 0.914). However, a logistic regression analysis of SLE manifestations revealed a tendency towards a connection between sCD40L and CNS involvement (Figure S3C,D, p = 0.0521, LR+ = 3.77, AUC = 0.568). Thus, the analysis of data from this cohort of SLE patients suggests that ANA‐positive patients with SLEDAI ≥ 4, elevated IgG levels and CNS involvement correlates with levels of sCD40L.

A Principal Component Regression with sCD40L as the dependent variable and five measures of disease activity, inflammation and organ damage/involvement (SLEDAI, CRP, total number of ACR criteria, anti‐dsDNA concentrations and SLICC, respectively) demonstrated an association between sCD40L and the number of ACR criteria (p = 0.0379), SLEDAI (p = 0.0323), as well as with concentrations of anti‐dsDNA antibodies (p = 0.0430). Thus, these three variables significantly contribute to explaining the variance seen for levels of sCD40L.

Evaluation of SLE Patient Characteristics by Stratifying Patients Based on sCD40L Clusters

Since not all SLE patients present with elevated sCD40L levels, we decided to group them into clusters based on their sCD40L plasma concentrations. The Elbow method was used to identify the optimal number of clusters (k). The Elbow method identified an optimal k of 3 (Figure S1). We thus clustered the data into three groups using the unsupervised learning algorithm K‐means clustering. The algorithm created three groups of SLE patients, one cluster with the lowest levels of sCD40L (n = 143, 1.2 ng/mL, 0.81–4.4 ng/mL), a cluster with medium‐high sCD40L levels (n = 20, 14.55 ng/mL, 12.23–20.43 ng/mL) and a cluster with the highest levels of sCD40L (n = 6, 46.9 ng/mL, 38.28–56.35 ng/mL) (Figure S4).

PCA was performed on 5 disease measures including: SLEDAI, CRP, the total number of ACR criteria at inclusion, anti‐dsDNA antibody concentration and SLICC. By analysing these variables simultaneously, PCA can help identify patterns and groupings that may not be apparent when considering one variable at a time. PCA was applied across the three patient clusters and a loading plot was generated with Principal Component 1 (PC1) on the x‐axis and Principal Component 2 (PC2) on the y‐axis. This plot visualises how the five disease activity measures contribute to PC1 and PC2 (explained variance) and highlights differences in their effects across the three clusters (Figure 3, Table 3).

Discussion

This study highlights the potential of sCD40L as a biomarker for monitoring disease activity in SLE and its limitations as a diagnostic marker. Our findings demonstrate that sCD40L levels are significantly elevated in SLE patients compared to healthy controls, supporting the notion of CD40‐CD40L pathway dysregulation in autoimmune settings. However, while sCD40L showed some correlation with disease activity markers, such as SLEDAI, high IgG, low lymphocyte counts and ANA status, it was not sufficient as a standalone diagnostic tool due to limited specificity and sensitivity. While this study's findings reinforce the potential of sCD40L in monitoring disease activity, more extensive studies are needed to clarify its role.

The Potential of sCD40L as an Indicator of Disease Activity

The positive correlation between sCD40L levels and SLE disease activity indicators, particularly in patients with high SLEDAI scores, supports its role as a potential marker for assessing disease severity. Elevated sCD40L levels in patients with active disease suggest that CD40‐CD40L signalling may contribute to inflammatory processes and autoimmune responses in SLE. This aligns with previous research indicating that CD40L expression is heightened in autoimmune diseases, including rheumatoid arthritis and multiple sclerosis, further establishing sCD40L as a standard feature of systemic autoimmunity [13, 14, 16, 17, 22].

We observed a correlation between elevated sCD40L levels and ANA positivity, IgG concentrations and decreased lymphocyte counts, although these correlations were weak. Based on our data, we speculate that sCD40L enhances B‐cell activation by engaging with CD40 on the cells, a critical interaction in autoimmune conditions like SLE. This engagement amplifies the production of autoantibodies including IgG and ANAs, which are hallmark features of SLE pathology [52, 53]. Additionally, CD40L‐CD40 signalling may influence other immune cell subsets, such as dendritic cells and macrophages, leading to heightened inflammatory cytokine production and perpetuating the autoimmune cascade leading to increased disease activity (SLEDAI). Although the association between sCD40L levels and the three disease measures, SLEDAI, anti‐dsDNA antibody concentrations and total number of ACR criteria, strengthens the arguments for sCD40L as a biomarker in SLE, the weak correlations underscore the need for more robust and comprehensive studies to validate these findings and explore the temporal dynamics of sCD40L in disease flares and remission. Conceivable reasons for the weak correlations observed could reflect the heterogeneity of SLE pathogenesis, as well as the limitations of the SLEDAI score for explaining immunological activity. Additional reasons include variability in treatment modalities, and cross‐sectional study design and may have limited the ability to detect stronger correlations in our study. Although cardiovascular disease did not appear to be prevalent in this cohort based in the SLICC score (mean score = 0.94), we acknowledge that, in the absence of systematic cardiovascular history, some degree of residual confounding cannot be ruled out.

Clustering patients into 3 distinct groups based on plasma concentrations of sCD40L revealed the same tendencies as described above, with higher sCD40L levels supporting tendencies towards increased disease activity (SLEDAI, anti‐dsDNA antibody concentrations and CRP). No statistically significant differences were identified, partly due to the low number of patients in cluster 3 (n = 6), as well as the exploratory approach and scope of this article, which limits the possibility for statistical analysis. A potential increase in CNS involvement and cases of nephritis was observed in patients with increased sCD40L levels (Table 2). This aligns with previous studies that have also identified a potential influence of CD40L on CNS inflammation and nephritis [17, 23]. CD40L has been shown to play a role in disruption of the blood–brain barrier while also being implicated in promoting neuroinflammation by interacting with resident glial cells, contributing to neuronal damage and cognitive impairment in SLE [17]. Similarly, in the kidneys, heightened CD40L activity could exacerbate glomerular inflammation and promote the development of lupus nephritis. These findings highlight the potential of sCD40L as a marker of severe organ involvement, particularly in CNS and renal manifestations, and warrant exploration in longitudinal studies to assess whether elevated sCD40L precedes or predicts organ‐specific flares.

Implications for Patient Stratification and Personalised Therapy

Given the emergence of second‐generation CD40L inhibitors designed to avoid Fc‐mediated thromboembolic events [32, 34, 36, 38, 39], our findings support the rationale for further investigation into whether sCD40L might serve as a biomarker for identifying SLE patients who may benefit from targeted CD40L inhibition therapies. Patients with elevated sCD40L levels, particularly those with higher SLEDAI scores, elevated anti‐dsDNA antibodies, and more severe organ involvement (e.g., CNS and renal manifestations), represent a subgroup that could benefit from emerging CD40L‐targeted therapies, even though further studies are needed. This future potential for patient stratification aligns with the T2T approach recently emphasised in the EULAR recommendations [44, 45], which aims to optimise therapeutic strategies based on individual disease profiles and minimise corticosteroid use.

Since SLEDAI cannot identify improving or deteriorating disease and does not involve specific measures of the immunological status or severity of organ system involvement [54], additional biomarkers of disease activity would be valuable. Especially if sCD40L could serve as a marker of CNS involvement, since CNS involvement can be particularly difficult to define [55]. Furthermore, SLEDAI does not consider central manifestations such as hemolytic anaemia, lymphopenia and gastrointestinal symptoms [54, 56, 57], further underlining the need for specific biomarkers to assess disease activity and severity to evaluate the true efficacy of targeted treatments in a T2T approach. Hence, SLEDAI, as a suboptimal disease activity score combined with the lack of suitable biomarkers, the inclusion of quantification of sCD40L could help evolve the landscape of biomarkers in SLE. Given the heterogeneity of SLE, different patients may require distinct biomarkers, with specific markers proving more relevant for certain subgroups. The inclusion of sCD40L in a panel of established disease activity markers for example, SLEDAI score, anti‐dsDNA antibody levels and complement activation markers, may enhance disease monitoring in selected patient populations and support more effective patient stratification for different therapeutic strategies. Returning to the emerging CD40L inhibitors, adding sCD40L quantification in the clinical assessment of patients could be the most optimal way to identify patients who would benefit from treatment with these inhibitors. On the other hand, if more studies can demonstrate a stronger link between sCD40L levels and SLEDAI or anti‐dsDNA concentrations (positive ANA status), these measures could also be used to stratify patients into groups of patients who would benefit and those who would not benefit from CD40L inhibitors.

A limitation of this study is the cross‐sectional design and the relatively small sample size, particularly in the highest sCD40L concentration cluster, which restricted our ability to perform statistical analyses within this subgroup. Additionally, while sCD40L shows promise as a biomarker for disease activity, its limited diagnostic utility suggests that it should be used in conjunction with other clinical and laboratory markers. Future studies with larger cohorts are needed to confirm the observed associations and to evaluate whether sCD40L levels fluctuate with treatment responses, particularly with CD40L inhibitors.

Expanding the research to longitudinal studies would help elucidate the dynamics of sCD40L levels throughout disease flares and remissions, providing valuable insights into its role as a monitoring tool. Furthermore, mechanistic studies investigating how sCD40L contributes to the pathophysiology of SLE, especially in relation to organ‐specific manifestations like CNS involvement and nephritis, could uncover novel therapeutic insights.

Conclusion

This study underscores sCD40L's potential as a biomarker in SLE. Our findings suggest that looking further into sCD40L as a biomarker in SLE could prove to be a valuable tool in patient stratification and personalised treatment approaches, especially when aiming to identify the most appropriate biologic therapy for each patient as early as possible. Although sCD40L lacks the diagnostic specificity required for standalone use, its correlation with disease activity parameters underscores its potential to enhance clinical assessments of SLE. As part of a T2T framework, sCD40L could facilitate personalised treatment strategies by identifying patients who are more likely to benefit from targeted therapies, such as CD40L inhibitors. Moreover, its potential as a marker of organ‐specific involvement, like CNS disease and nephritis, highlights its broader relevance in SLE management, ultimately contributing to improved quality of life for patients with SLE.
