Three-dimensional analysis of condylar displacement after orthognathic surgery in patients with facial asymmetry
Authors: Teresa Bae, Kavya Shankar Muttanahally, Cheng-Shiuan Kuang, Tzu-Ying Wu, Cheng-Hsien Wu, Po-Jung Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThis study aimed to evaluate condylar positional changes in patients with facial asymmetry undergoing orthodontic treatment and orthognathic surgery by comparing the affected side (the side toward which the chin deviates) and the non-affected side (the contralateral side) across different treatment stages.Materials and MethodsA retrospective cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) analysis was conducted on 25 patients with facial asymmetry (menton deviation >4 mm) treated at Taipei Veterans General Hospital. CBCT scans were obtained at 4 timepoints: pre-treatment (T1), post-orthodontic dental decompensation (T2), post-surgery (T3), and post-treatment (T4). Linear measurements of anterior joint space (AJS), superior joint space (SJS), posterior joint space (PJS), and the condylar angle were assessed.ResultsAt baseline (T1), no significant differences were observed in linear measurements between the affected and non-affected sides; however, the condylar angle was significantly smaller on the affected side (69.6° vs. 76.5°, P < 0.05). Following surgery (T2–T3), AJS increased significantly on both sides (P < 0.05), whereas SJS and PJS changes varied by side. From T3 to T4, significant differences between the affected and non-affected sides emerged in SJS (P < 0.05), PJS (P < 0.05), and condylar angle (P < 0.05), suggesting differential remodeling during post-surgical orthodontic finishing. Overall, orthodontic decompensation alone (T1–T2) did not significantly influence condylar position.ConclusionPre-surgical orthodontic treatment did not significantly affect condylar position, whereas orthognathic surgery induced measurable positional and angular changes. Post-surgical orthodontic finishing revealed divergent remodeling patterns between affected and non-affected condyles. Further studies with larger samples and long-term follow-up are necessary to clarify the clinical implications of these changes.Keywords: Facial Asymmetry, Temporomandibular Joint, Orthognathic Surgery, Mandibular Condyle

Introduction

Facial asymmetry is a multifactorial condition that can significantly affect both aesthetics and function. Management often requires a combination of orthodontic and surgical interventions to achieve optimal outcomes. Although mild facial asymmetry may be managed with orthodontic treatment alone, severe asymmetry typically necessitates orthognathic surgery to correct underlying skeletal discrepancies.

Chin deviation accompanies facial asymmetry in approximately 74% of affected individuals.1 The etiology of facial asymmetry varies and includes congenital or genetic malformations (such as unilateral cleft lip or palate and hemifacial microsomia), environmental factors (such as trauma or parafunctional habits), and functional deviations (such as mandibular shifts due to occlusal interferences).2,3,4

Treatment modalities for facial asymmetry depend on patient age, severity of asymmetry, and underlying etiology. Mild cases may be addressed with growth modification or orthodontic treatment alone, whereas severe cases often require a combination of orthodontic therapy and orthognathic surgery.5 Orthognathic surgery aims not only to correct facial asymmetry but also to address jaw discrepancies to establish functional occlusion, improve masticatory efficiency, and enhance facial aesthetics.6

Before undergoing orthognathic surgery, patients with facial asymmetry typically receive pre-surgical orthodontic treatment to prepare the dentition for accurate surgical repositioning and post-surgical stability. This preparation may involve 3-dimensional dental decompensation to eliminate occlusal interferences and achieve at least 3-point occlusal contact when the jaws are repositioned symmetrically with appropriate roll and yaw correction.7 Surgical simulation can help verify potential interferences and establish an optimal tooth movement plan.

Studies investigating condylar displacement after orthognathic surgery have reported that, in patients without facial asymmetry, condyles tend to move inferoposteriorly immediately after surgery and gradually relocate anterosuperiorly over time.8 However, in patients with facial asymmetry, condylar displacement following surgical correction is less predictable because of complex spatial repositioning of distal bone segments.8,9

Although substantial research has examined condylar positional changes after orthognathic surgery, comparatively fewer studies have focused specifically on patients with facial asymmetry. Patients with facial asymmetry may more frequently exhibit transverse functional shifts, and the true condylar position may not be evident until occlusal interferences are eliminated. If discrepancies exist between the deviated and non-deviated sides before surgery, the true condylar position may influence surgical simulation planning and final outcomes. To date, no studies have compared condylar changes across the timepoints of pre-orthodontic treatment, pre-surgery, and post-orthognathic surgery.

One aim of this study was to characterize baseline morphologic differences between affected and non-affected condyles. It also sought to measure positional changes of the condyle using linear and angular measurements at each timepoint to determine whether 3-dimensional positional changes occurred. If positional changes were identified, comparisons were made between affected and non-affected sides to assess potential differences.

Materials and Methods

This study conducted a retrospective analysis using cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) images obtained from Taipei Veterans General Hospital (VGHTPE). The dataset comprised images from patients who underwent orthodontic treatment and orthognathic surgery within the past 5 years in the Department of Orthodontics at VGHTPE. A total of 25 patients were evaluated. Inclusion criteria required facial asymmetry, defined as menton deviation >4 mm from the facial midline. The facial midline was defined as a vertical line passing through the midpoint of the glabella.10 Exclusion criteria included a history of trauma, congenital facial deformity (such as cleft palate), or temporomandibular joint (TMJ) disorder. Four timepoints were evaluated: pre-treatment (T1), after orthodontic dental decompensation before surgery (T2), post-orthognathic surgery (T3), and post-treatment (T4). CBCT images were acquired using a NewTom 5G scanner (NewTom Cone Beam 3D Imaging, Verona, Italy). Scans were obtained under the following standard settings: tube current, 10 mA; kilovoltage, 110 kVp; field of view, 18 × 16 cm; exposure time, 10 seconds; and slice thickness, 0.3 mm. All datasets were saved in Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine (DICOM) format. The DICOM data were imported into Dolphin software (ver. 11.95.08.73 Premium; Dolphin Imaging and Management Solutions, Chatsworth, CA, USA).

Once imported into Dolphin software, the CBCT images were oriented according to 2 reference planes: the Frankfort horizontal (FH) plane and the nasion-frontozygomatic (NFZ) plane (a frontal plane passing through the frontozygomatic suture between the inner orbital rim and the nasion) (Fig. 1). Linear and angular measurements were obtained at timepoints T1 through T4 on both the affected and non-affected sides. The affected side was defined as the side toward which the chin deviated, and the non-affected side was defined as the contralateral side.

Orientation in Dolphin. A. Sagittal view oriented according to the Frankfort horizontal plane. B. Frontal view oriented according to the nasion-frontozygomatic plane.

To assess changes in condylar position across timepoints, the following linear measurements were compared (Fig. 2A): posterior joint space (PJS), superior joint space (SJS), and anterior joint space (AJS). After orientation of the CBCT images according to the FH and NFZ planes, SJS, AJS, and PJS were measured in the sagittal view. SJS was measured from the superior aspect of the condyle to the glenoid fossa. AJS was measured by drawing a tangent line from the most superior point of the condyle to its most anterior point and extending it to the glenoid fossa. PJS was measured by drawing a tangent line from the most superior point of the condyle to its most posterior point and extending it to the glenoid fossa.11

Linear and angular measurements. A. Superior, anterior, and posterior joint spaces measured in the sagittal plane. B. Condylar angle measurement.

Changes in the mandibular condylar angle were also analyzed (Fig. 2B). The condylar angle was measured in the axial view. The slice demonstrating the greatest mesiodistal condylar width was selected. The angle was measured between a line through the midsagittal plane and a line through the long axis of the condyle.

Statistical analyses were performed using SAS software (ver. 9.4; SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA), and a P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Intra-examiner reliability was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). Reliability testing was performed by repeating measurements of the right condylar angle at least 2 weeks after the initial measurement in 15 patients. The paired t-test was used to compare affected versus non-affected sides at T1. A linear mixed model (LMM), with a random effect for patient (to account for within-patient correlation) and a fixed effect for deviation (i.e., affected vs. non-affected side), was used to compare changes in measurements between sides. Model-adjusted means and standard errors were used to describe measurements at each timepoint.

Results

The mean patient age was 24.9 ± 6.55 years. The study included 9 males and 16 females. Menton deviation was to the right in 8 patients and to the left in 17 patients. The magnitude of menton deviation ranged from 4.0 mm to 14.6 mm. Regarding skeletal classification, 5 patients were skeletal Class I, 5 were skeletal Class II, and 15 were skeletal Class III. For the mandibular plane angle, 9 patients were high-angle, 13 were normal-angle, and 3 were low-angle. Six patients underwent mandibular surgery only, and 19 underwent 2-jaw surgery.

No statistically significant differences were observed in AJS, SJS, or PJS between affected and non-affected sides at T1 (Table 1). However, the mean condylar angle at T1 differed significantly between sides (P < 0.05). The mean T1 condylar angle was 69.6° on the affected side and 76.5° on the non-affected side; the non-affected side angle was significantly larger than the affected side angle.

From T3 to T4, the mean change in SJS was −0.33 mm on the non-affected side compared with 0.25 mm on the affected side, and the mean change in PJS was −0.12 mm on the non-affected side compared with 0.27 mm on the affected side. Discussion

One aim of this study was to determine whether baseline morphologic differences exist between affected and non-affected condyles in patients with facial asymmetry. Prior studies have reported that, in individuals without asymmetry, no differences are observed in 3-dimensional condylar position. Farinazzo et al. evaluated the condyles of 30 individuals without asymmetry and reported no differences in AJS, SJS, PJS, or condylar angle.12 Given the critical role of the mandibular condyle in mastication, it is important to assess whether patients with facial asymmetry exhibit discrepancies in articular space or condylar morphology. In the present study, no significant between-side differences were observed in AJS, SJS, or PJS, consistent with findings reported by Chou et al.13 and Kim et al.14 In a study by Chou et al., condylar position, shape, and volume were analyzed in patients with and without facial asymmetry.13 They reported no significant differences in AJS, SJS, or PJS, but did observe a significant between-side difference in condylar angle in the asymmetry group, with a larger angle on the non-affected side. This finding is consistent with the present results (Table 1) and has also been reported in other studies.13,14 In the current study, the mean condylar angle was 76.54° on the non-affected side compared with 69.59° on the affected side. The larger angle on the non-affected side may reflect asymmetric growth patterns, in which the non-deviated side exhibits greater growth than the deviated side.13 Consequently, the non-affected condyle may demonstrate a larger angle than the affected condyle.

Another aim of this study was to determine whether condylar position differs across treatment timepoints. Patients underwent either mandibular surgery alone or combined maxillary and mandibular surgery. These procedures involve mandibular and/or maxillary osteotomies, with bony segments fixed in a new position planned preoperatively. Fixation methods include bicortical screws (rigid fixation) and monocortical screws with miniplates (semi-rigid fixation). Rigid fixation has been associated with a higher likelihood of skeletal relapse and condylar displacement. Bicortical screw fixation may increase the risk of condylar sag because forceful fixation can impose tension on the condyle, potentially resulting in medial, lateral, or inferior displacement.15 Additional factors that may affect condylar position include the magnitude of segment movement, patient anatomy, and surgeon experience. Condylar positional changes may be associated with postoperative complications such as relapse, condylar resorption, TMJ functional disorders, and malocclusion, and therefore should be minimized. Condylar sag, or inferior condylar positioning, can be categorized as central or peripheral. Condylar sag may contribute to early relapse and malocclusion after removal of maxillomandibular fixation or to late relapse and malocclusion. Verhelst et al. reported that bilateral sagittal split osteotomy (BSSO) can induce biomechanical stresses on the TMJ that may lead to physiological joint remodeling.16 If stresses exceed physiological limits, pathological remodeling may occur even in patients without preexisting TMJ problems.16 They identified 3 biomechanical factors that may affect the TMJ: prolonged changes in condylar position, tension in muscles attached to the mandible, and orthodontic forces from postoperative elastics. Although condylar positional changes should be minimized to reduce adverse effects, mild condylar remodeling of <1 mm may be considered within the range of normal after orthognathic surgery.17 Park et al. conducted a long-term study of condylar remodeling in Class III patients after orthognathic surgery and reported a decrease in condylar volume from immediately after surgery to 6 months postoperatively, followed by a significant increase at 6.1 years.18 These findings suggest that substantial remodeling and positional changes can occur long after orthognathic surgery, highlighting the need for long-term follow-up studies.

In this study, AJS and SJS increased, whereas PJS and condylar angle decreased, following orthognathic surgery. These findings suggest that the condyles moved posteriorly and inferiorly and rotated inward on both the affected and non-affected sides after surgery. Several studies have reported similar patterns.19 In contrast, Huang et al. reported different post-surgical movements between sides: on the affected side, the condyle moved anteriorly and inferiorly and rotated inward, whereas on the non-affected side, it moved posteriorly and rotated outward.9 Differences in post-surgical condylar movement may reflect multiple factors, including surgeon experience, fixation method, local anatomy, extent of mandibular rotation, and magnitude of segment movement. Several studies have also reported that postoperative positional changes tend to return toward the preoperative position over time.8,19 For example, condyles that move inferoposteriorly immediately after surgery may gradually relocate anterosuperiorly by 3 months postoperatively.19

Although statistically significant changes were observed in AJS, the magnitude of change was <1 mm and may not be clinically meaningful. The joint may remain within the normal range, and such changes may not be associated with temporomandibular disorders. A significant decrease in condylar angle after orthognathic surgery was also observed. Whether the magnitude of angular change contributes to adverse TMJ outcomes requires further study. Increased use of a ramus guide or other condylar repositioning devices may help minimize condylar positional changes during orthognathic surgery.

No significant changes in TMJ position were observed during pre-surgical dental decompensation (T1–T2) or during post-surgical orthodontic finishing (T3–T4). These findings suggest that orthodontic treatment alone does not substantially influence condylar position. However, further investigation is warranted to determine whether TMJ position remains stable after appliance removal.

Finally, differences in changes in condylar position were assessed across T1–T2, T2–T3, T3–T4, and T1–T4 by comparing affected and non-affected sides. No significant between-side differences were observed in mean AJS change across any interval. However, during the post-surgical orthodontic finishing stage (T3–T4), significant between-side differences were observed in mean changes in SJS, PJS, and condylar angle. Specifically, SJS and PJS increased on the affected side but decreased on the non-affected side, and the condylar angle decreased on the affected side while increasing on the non-affected side. These differences may reflect physiological remodeling after surgery, minor relapse, or post-surgical orthodontic forces from inter-arch elastics.16

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size of 25 patients limits generalizability to broader populations. Second, because all CBCT images were obtained from a single institution, the demographic characteristics may not represent other populations. Third, the measurements used may not fully capture rotational condylar movements (yaw, pitch, and roll). Finally, because no follow-up CBCT scans were obtained beyond the initial post-treatment imaging, long-term data are lacking, limiting assessment of stability over time.

Future studies could incorporate 3-dimensional superimposition to quantify pitch rotation of the condyle and to evaluate the long-term effects of inward TMJ rotation after surgery, including remodeling in the new position. Studies assessing the effectiveness of a ramus guide in maintaining TMJ position during surgery would also be valuable.

In summary, pre-surgical orthodontic treatment did not significantly influence condylar position. AJS increased significantly, and TMJ condyles rotated inward significantly after orthognathic surgery. During post-surgical orthodontic finishing, SJS and PJS increased on the affected side but decreased on the non-affected side, and the condylar angle decreased on the affected side while increasing on the non-affected side. Whether these positional changes throughout treatment are associated with adverse TMJ outcomes requires further investigation.
