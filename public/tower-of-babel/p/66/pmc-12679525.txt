Comparative Analysis of Intravascular Lithotripsy With and Without Atherectomy in Severely Calcified Coronary Lesions Using OCT and OFDI
Authors: Marohito Nakata, Masami Abe, Toshiya Chinen, Kentaro Nakamura
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundIntravascular lithotripsy (IVL) fractures calcified plaques and facilitates lesion expansion; however, unlike atherectomy devices, it does not reduce calcium volume. The therapeutic efficacy and safety of combining IVL with atherectomy remain unclear. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate, through optical coherence tomography (OCT) or optical frequency domain imaging (OFDI), whether IVL combined with atherectomy provides greater effectiveness compared with IVL alone.AimsThis study aimed to evaluate, using optical coherence tomography (OCT) and optical frequency domain imaging (OFDI), whether intravascular lithotripsy (IVL) combined with atherectomy provides greater procedural efficacy and safety compared with IVL alone in the treatment of severely calcified coronary lesions.MethodsA retrospective analysis was performed on 33 lesions in 31 patients who underwent IVL‐based percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) with OCT or OFDI. The primary endpoints were post‐PCI minimal lumen diameter (MLD) and minimal lumen area (MLA). The secondary endpoints included calcium fracture thickness, lesion expansion rate, and vessel expansion index.ResultsThe mean patient age was 72.3 ± 8.5 years, and 80.6% were men. No significant differences were identified in post‐PCI MLD or MLA between the IVL alone and IVL + atherectomy groups (MLD: 2.46 ± 0.38 vs. 2.23 ± 0.56 mm; MLA: 4.9 ± 1.55 vs. 4.19 ± 1.90 mm2). The secondary endpoints were also comparable. However, the combination group demonstrated a longer procedural time (82.7 ± 19.6 vs. 109.8 ± 34.0 min) and a higher incidence of periprocedural myocardial infarction (0 vs. 3 patients).ConclusionsCombination therapy resulted in a longer procedural time and an increased incidence of myocardial injury. IVL alone may be sufficient in lesions where device delivery is feasible, whereas atherectomy should be performed when IVL delivery is challenging.

Introduction

Percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) for heavily calcified coronary lesions remains a major challenge in interventional cardiology. Severe calcification may impede the passage of balloons and imaging devices, leading to suboptimal stent expansion, which has been associated with adverse clinical outcomes [1, 2, 3]. To overcome these challenges, various lesion modification techniques have been developed, including rotational and orbital atherectomy [4]. More recently, intravascular lithotripsy (IVL) has emerged as a novel approach for calcium modification. IVL employs acoustic pressure waves to fracture calcified plaques, thereby improving lesion compliance and facilitating stent expansion. Unlike atherectomy devices, IVL does not reduce calcium volume but offers procedural advantages, such as reduced wire bias, preservation of side branches, and a lower risk of distal embolization [5, 6]. These benefits have supported the increasing adoption of IVL, either alone or in combination with atherectomy [7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12]. Previous studies evaluating combined IVL and atherectomy have relied primarily on angiographic parameters, such as residual stenosis [13, 14, 15]. However, quantitative assessments using high‐resolution intravascular imaging modalities, including optical coherence tomography (OCT) and optical frequency‐domain imaging (OFDI), remain limited. In particular, data on lesion morphology, luminal gain, and the extent of calcium modification achieved by IVL alone or in combination with atherectomy remain scarce. It was hypothesized that atherectomy performed before IVL could facilitate deeper calcium fractures by reducing calcium volume, thereby improving lesion expandability. To test this hypothesis, OCT or OFDI was employed for morphological assessments of calcified lesions, and the procedural efficacy and safety of IVL alone were compared with those of IVL + atherectomy.

Methods

Study Design and Population

This single‐center, retrospective observational study included patients who underwent IVL‐based PCI between May 2023 and December 2024. The patients were allocated to either the IVL alone or IVL + atherectomy groups based on the devices used. OCT or OFDI was performed before and after device application in all patients. Patients without analyzable imaging data or those with in‐stent restenosis were excluded (Figure S1). The study was approved by the institutional ethics committee, and the requirement for informed consent was waived owing to the retrospective nature of the study. The study was conducted in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines. Further methodological details are provided in the Supporting Information Appendix.

Patient Characteristics and Clinical Data

Baseline demographic, clinical, and laboratory data were collected, including age, sex, major comorbidities, cardiac history, left ventricular ejection fraction, and routine blood tests. Baseline and procedural characteristics were compared between the IVL alone and IVL + atherectomy groups. Detailed variable definitions and the complete list of collected parameters are provided in the Supporting Information Appendix.

PCI Procedure

PCI was performed using standard techniques at the operator's discretion. IVL was delivered with a Shockwave catheter, and atherectomy devices included rotational and orbital systems. Device specifications, stent or DCB selection, and adjunctive balloon dilatations were determined by the operator. Procedural time, fluoroscopy time, radiation dose, and contrast volume were recorded (Figure 1).

OCT and OFDI Imaging Analysis

Imaging was performed before IVL or atherectomy and after stenting or DCB inflation, irrespective of the timing. OCT was conducted using a Dragonfly imaging catheter and the ILUMIEN OPTIS system (Abbott Vascular, Santa Clara, CA, USA), whereas OFDI was conducted using a FastView catheter with the LUNAWAVE imaging system (Terumo Corporation, Tokyo, Japan). The lumen diameter and area were measured. The mean vessel area was calculated as the average lumen area at the distal and proximal reference points. Minimal lumen diameter (MLD) and minimal lumen area (MLA) were assessed at the site of the smallest lumen before and after treatment. Posttreatment reference segments were aligned with pretreatment segments using anatomical landmarks, such as side branches or calcification patterns. Lesion length was defined as the distance between the distal and proximal reference points. The preprocedural MLD and MLA were measured at the site of the most severe narrowing before PCI. The postprocedural MLD and MLA were assessed at the same anatomical site to enable paired comparisons. Furthermore, the minimum lumen diameter and area after PCI were independently recorded, representing the narrowest posttreatment segment, which did not necessarily correspond to the preprocedural site. Representative OCT images from the IVL alone and IVL + atherectomy groups are presented in Figure 2. Calcification was defined as a region with clear borders and signal attenuation. The evaluated calcification parameters included the maximum calcium arc, maximum calcium thickness, calcium length, calcium volume index, calcium score, and calcium fracture thickness. The maximum calcium arc, maximum calcium thickness, calcium length, and calcium volume index were independently assessed by two experienced interventional cardiologists. Fractures were defined as newly formed discontinuities within the calcium structure that were absent before treatment and were confirmed on a frame‐by‐frame basis using longitudinal and cross‐sectional imaging. The presence of medial dissection or intramural hematoma was also assessed. Detailed imaging acquisition parameters, definitions, and analytical procedures are provided in the Supporting Information Appendix.

Interobserver Agreement

Quantitative imaging analyses were performed independently by two experienced cardiologists. interobserver agreement was assessed, and representative results are presented.

Endpoints

The primary endpoints were postprocedural minimal lumen diameter and area. The secondary endpoints included imaging‐based measures of calcium modification and vessel expansion. The safety endpoints comprised in‐hospital adverse events, periprocedural MI, postprocedural CK levels, and 30‐day readmission. The complete endpoint definitions are provided in the Supporting Information Appendix.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were compared using standard parametric or nonparametric tests, whereas categorical variables were analyzed using chi‐square or Fisher's exact tests. Statistical significance was defined as a p value of < 0.05. The effect sizes for the primary endpoints and interobserver agreement analyses were also calculated. Additional methodological details are provided in the Supporting Information Appendix.

The quantitative imaging results are summarized in Table 4. The preprocedural mean reference diameters and areas were comparable between the groups, indicating similar vessel dimensions. No significant differences were observed in pre‐PCI MLD (1.50 ± 0.24 vs. 1.50 ± 0.35 mm, p = 0.83) and MLA (1.84 ± 0.57 vs. 1.91 ± 0.93 mm2, p = 0.82). The morphological indicators of calcification—including the maximum and mean calcium arc, calcium length, calcium volume index, and calcium score—were numerically greater in the IVL + atherectomy group; however, none were deemed significant.

Primary Endpoints

Post‐PCI MLD and MLA, the primary endpoints, were 2.46 ± 0.38 versus 2.23 ± 0.56 mm (p = 0.33) and 4.90 ± 1.55 versus 4.19 ± 1.90 mm2 (p = 0.37), respectively. Although no significant differences were observed, the effect sizes indicated small‐to‐moderate differences (Hedges' g for MLD: 0.49, 95% CI –0.27 to 1.26; for MLA: 0.42, 95% CI –0.34 to 1.18).

Secondary Endpoints

With regard to secondary endpoints, no significant differences were observed in calcium fracture thickness, lesion expansion rate, vessel expansion index, or the incidence of medial dissection between the groups. Although the vessel expansion index tended to be lower in the IVL + atherectomy group, this difference was not significant (Table 4). Similarly, both the asymmetric and eccentricity indices were comparable between the groups (Table 4).

Safety Outcomes

Periprocedural MI occurred in three patients in the IVL + atherectomy group but not in the IVL alone group (p = 0.03). Postprocedural CK levels measured on the day after PCI were significantly higher in the IVL + atherectomy group (82.4 ± 28.4 vs. 187.1 ± 33.2 IU/L, p = 0.01). No in‐hospital deaths or 30‐day readmissions were reported in either group.

Discussion

This retrospective imaging‐based study evaluated the efficacy and safety of IVL alone and IVL + atherectomy in the treatment of calcified coronary lesions using high‐resolution intracoronary imaging modalities (OCT and OFDI). No significant differences were identified between the two groups in terms of postprocedural MLD or MLA, suggesting that IVL alone may provide luminal expansion comparable to that achieved with combination therapy. However, the addition of atherectomy was associated with significantly longer procedural times, a higher incidence of periprocedural MI, and elevated postprocedural CK levels. These findings highlight the potential for increased procedural complexity and greater risk of complications with combination therapy.

Previous studies have suggested the potential benefits of combining IVL with atherectomy, particularly with rotational atherectomy (“rotashock” or “rotatripsy”) [7, 8, 9] and orbital atherectomy (“orbitalshock” or “orbitatripsy”) [10, 11, 12]. Although these studies have demonstrated high procedural success rates, the assessments were primarily based on qualitative and subjective angiographic criteria, such as residual stenosis. For example, Sardella et al. [13], Martin et al. [14], and Wong et al. [15] reported favorable outcomes with RotaShock or orbital shock, with procedural success primarily defined by angiographic endpoints, such as residual stenosis or TIMI 3 flow, rather than by intravascular imaging. By contrast, the present study used high‐resolution OCT and OFDI to quantify calcium fracture thickness, lesion expansion rate, and vessel expansion index—parameters that are difficult to assess with intravascular ultrasound. These advanced imaging modalities allowed for a more detailed evaluation of the structural characteristics of calcified lesions and their response to IVL and atherectomy [16].

Building on this distinction, the mechanistic effects of IVL were further investigated. IVL delivers localized acoustic pressure waves that induce calcium fractures, thereby allowing vessel expansion without debulking the calcium volume [17]. It was hypothesized that calcium debulking with atherectomy before IVL could enhance wave penetration, thereby enhancing the MLD and MLA by promoting deeper crack formation. However, the findings of this study did not support this hypothesis. In the OCT sub‐analysis of the DISRUPT CAD study, IVL was shown to be more effective in heavily calcified lesions, contrary to the assumption that larger calcium volumes require prior debulking [18]. This observation was consistent with the present population, in which patients in the highest tertile of calcium burden demonstrated favorable IVL responses (Figure 3).

These findings may be explained by the distinct mechanisms of action of IVL and atherectomy. Atherectomy devices predominantly affect superficial calcium, reducing its volume but exerting limited influence on deeper layers [18]. Conversely, IVL transmits acoustic shock waves to deeper calcified structures, thereby inducing cracks via high‐energy compression. Consistent with these mechanistic differences, Gallinoro et al. demonstrated that IVL achieved lower in‐stent pressure gradients than RA, suggesting more effective lesion expansion with IVL [19]. This effect exploits the brittleness of calcified tissue, which is characterized by low elasticity and a high susceptibility to fracture under mechanical stress. Notably, deeper and thicker calcium layers tend to accumulate more shockwave energy, thereby facilitating deeper fracture formation [18]. These observations are consistent with the findings of Kereiakes et al. who demonstrated that thicker plaster rings (Ultracal‐30 models) fractured more easily under IVL conditions when adequate balloon apposition was achieved [17]. Consequently, the efficacy of IVL may depend more on calcium structure (specifically, thickness and stiffness) than on the overall calcium volume.

In this study, calcium fracture thickness did not significantly differ between the IVL alone and IVL + atherectomy groups, suggesting that volume reduction through atherectomy does not necessarily facilitate deeper calcium cracking by IVL. These findings imply that when IVL delivery across a lesion is feasible, adjunctive atherectomy may not be required. Conversely, in cases where IVL delivery is hindered by severe calcification, atherectomy may serve as a useful preparatory strategy to facilitate device passage, consistent with the “RotaShock” concept [20]. Furthermore, rotational atherectomy may be beneficial for removing spiculated calcific nodules, reducing the risk of IVL balloon rupture, and improving procedural success [21, 22].

These findings are consistent with the recent EAPCI consensus, which recommends IVL for the treatment of deep calcium and atherectomy for superficial lesions, while reserving combination therapy for uncrossable cases [23]. From a safety standpoint, the IVL + atherectomy group demonstrated significantly longer procedural times, a higher incidence of periprocedural MIs, and elevated CK levels. However, no coronary perforation, in‐hospital death, or 30‐day readmission occurred in either group.

Notably, no previous studies have specifically reported CK levels in patients who underwent IVL + atherectomy. Among larger cohort studies, Sardella et al. [13] defined safety as the absence of serious angiographic complications (e.g., type B dissection or worse, perforation, abrupt closure, slow/no flow, a final TIMI flow of < 3, or acute thrombosis), which was achieved in 90.6% of patients. Similarly, Takahashi et al. [24] reported safety outcomes—including procedural complications and MACEs (death, MI, and TVR)—with a higher incidence observed in the IVL + atherectomy group (9.1%) compared with the IVL alone group (1.3%).

By contrast, the present study demonstrated excellent procedural safety, with no serious complications, in‐hospital adverse events, or 30‐day readmissions observed in either group. The elevation in CK levels may reflect distal embolization caused by microparticles released during atherectomy [19]. Periprocedural MI was defined based on post‐PCI CK levels exceeding the upper normal limit (248 IU/L) rather than by the universal definition [25], warranting cautious interpretation. Although the absolute difference in CK levels (82.4 ± 28.4 vs. 187.1 ± 33.2 IU/L) was significant, its impact on clinical outcomes remains uncertain. Nevertheless, from a safety perspective, caution is warranted when using IVL + atherectomy, as this approach is associated with higher CK levels and prolonged procedural times.

Clinical decision‐making should align the device strategy with lesion complexity. IVL alone is generally effective and safe, whereas atherectomy should be reserved for cases in which IVL delivery is not feasible. The potential benefits of combination therapy must be carefully weighed against its greater procedural risks.

Limitations

Despite the significant findings uncovered, this study has some limitations. First, it was a single‐center retrospective study with a relatively small sample size. Only 33 lesions in 31 patients were studied, of which 11 underwent combination therapy with atherectomy. This limited statistical power suggests that the findings should be interpreted with caution when generalizing them to a broader population. In addition, although no significant differences in post‐PCI MLD and MLA were observed, the calculated effect sizes (Hedges' g for MLD: 0.49; for MLA: 0.42) indicated small‐to‐moderate differences with wide confidence intervals, highlighting substantial uncertainty. Therefore, these results should be regarded as exploratory, and confirmation in larger, multicenter studies is warranted.

Second, although calcified lesions were quantitatively assessed using OCT or OFDI, some interobserver variability was observed. Notably, the interobserver correlation coefficient for maximum calcium thickness was low (r = 0.12, p = 0.523), suggesting limited reproducibility. This limitation may be attributed to the inherent difficulty in delineating the outer border of calcium on OCT/OFDI scans [19]. However, strong correlations were observed for other parameters, including the maximum calcium arc (r = 0.85), calcium length (r = 0.90), and calcium volume index (r = 0.91), supporting overall consistency. Although the variability in calcium thickness measurements is noteworthy, its impact on study outcomes is likely minimal. Nevertheless, the mean maximum calcium thickness measured by observer A was 0.99 ± 0.21 mm, whereas observer B reported 1.25 ± 0.24 mm. Both values—approximately 1 mm—indicate substantial calcification, and both observers consistently classified the lesions as thickly calcified. Consequently, this discrepancy is unlikely to have altered the study's overall conclusions. Future investigations should consider incorporating multiple analysts, standardized measurement protocols, and enhanced training to enhance reproducibility.

Third, the selection of atherectomy device (rotational or orbital) and ablation technique was determined at the operator's discretion, and inter‐operator variability was not evaluated. Additionally, as all patients in this study permitted preprocedural OCT/OFDI imaging, lesions in which the imaging catheter could not be advanced were excluded. As the decision to perform an atherectomy was operator dependent, lesions with more severe calcification may have been preferentially assigned to the atherectomy group. These factors introduce potential selection bias and may limit the generalizability of the findings.

In Japan, the combined use of IVL and atherectomy has recently been permitted in clinical practice. However, current reimbursement policies generally require stent implantation following IVL. In this study, several patients underwent IVL (with or without atherectomy) followed by DCB angioplasty without stent implantation. Although this approach deviates from the standard reimbursement framework in Japan, it reflects real‐world clinical practice and aligns with strategies increasingly adopted in other countries where stentless approaches are more widely accepted.

Finally, safety outcomes were evaluated only during hospitalization and within 30 days of PCI. Long‐term clinical outcomes, including MACEs, were not assessed. Moreover, periprocedural MI was defined based on CK elevation rather than the Fourth Universal Definition of MI, limiting the interpretation of its clinical relevance. Future investigations are warranted to comprehensively assess the long‐term efficacy and safety of these strategies.

Despite these limitations, this study provides valuable clinical data comparing IVL alone and IVL + atherectomy using high‐resolution OCT and OFDI. These findings yield practical insights that may guide the development of treatment strategies for severely calcified coronary lesions.

In conclusion, although no significant differences were observed in MLD or MLA between IVL alone and IVL + atherectomy, combination therapy was associated with longer procedural times and higher postprocedural CK levels. IVL alone may provide comparable luminal expansion when device delivery is feasible, whereas atherectomy may be best reserved as a preparatory strategy in cases where IVL delivery is challenging. Given the limited sample size, these findings should be considered exploratory and require confirmation in larger, multicenter studies (Central Illustration 1).
