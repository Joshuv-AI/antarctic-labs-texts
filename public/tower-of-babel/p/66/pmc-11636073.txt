PAD4 Inhibitor‐Loaded Layered Double Hydroxide Nanosheets as a Multifunctional Nanoplatform for Photodynamic Therapy‐Mediated Tumor Metastasis Treatment
Authors: Rong Chen, Tingting Hu, Yu Lu, Shuqing Yang, Min Zhang, Chaoliang Tan, Ruizheng Liang, Yuji Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPhotodynamic therapy (PDT) is demonstrated to be effective in inducing antitumor immune responses for tumor metastasis treatment. However, tumor hypoxia, inferior tissue penetration of light, and low singlet oxygen (1O2) quantum yield significantly hamper the efficacy of PDT, thus weakening its immune function. Moreover, PDT‐mediated neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) formation can further reduce the therapeutic effectiveness. Herein, the use of defect‐rich CoMo‐layered double hydroxide (DR‐CoMo‐LDH) nanosheets as a carrier to load a typical peptidyl arginine deiminase 4 inhibitor, i.e., YW4‐03, to construct a multifunctional nanoagent (403@DR‐LDH) for PDT/immunotherapy, is reported. Specifically, 403@DR‐LDH inherits excellent 1O2 generation activity under 1550 nm laser irradiation and improves the half‐life of YW4‐03. Meanwhile, 403@DR‐LDH plus 1550 nm laser irradiation can stimulate immunogenic cell death to promote the maturation of dendric cells and activation/infiltration of T cells and significantly downregulate H3cit protein expression to inhibit NETs formation, synergistically promoting the antitumor metastasis effect. Taken together, 403@DR‐LDH can kill cancer cells and inhibit tumor growth/metastasis under 1550 nm laser irradiation. Single‐cell analysis indicates that 403@DR‐LDH can regulate the ratio of immune cells and immune‐related proteins to improve the tumor immune microenvironment, showing strong efficacy to inhibit the tumor growth, metastasis, and recurrence.Keywords: immunogenic cell death, immunotherapy, layered double hydroxides, photodynamic therapy, tumor metastasis

Introduction

Malignant tumors are characterized with local and distant metastases, where tumor cells can be transported through the blood to the distal site for colonization and form secondary or metastatic tumors, making its clinical treatment particularly challenging.[ 1 − 4 ] Tumor metastasis and recurrence contribute to the majority of cancer‐related deaths due to its small size, wide distribution, and strong heterogeneity, which makes it difficult to be surgically resected or undergo chemotherapy.[ 5 − 8 ] Peptidyl arginine deiminase 4 (PAD4) has been recognized to be highly associated with tumor growth and metastasis, which can drive the release of neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs), thereby promoting tumor growth/invasion or induce cytotoxicity.[ 9 , 10 ] In view of the critical pro‐metastasis effect of PAD4, a variety of PAD4 inhibitors (e.g., GSK199, GSK484, streptomycin, minocycline, YW4‐03, YW3‐56, F‐amidine, Cl‐amidine and its derivatives, etc.) have been developed to inhibit the activity of PAD4 and block NETs formation, demonstrating promising antimetastasis responses.[ 11 , 12 ] Moreover, PAD4 inhibitors can interfere with the immune response of natural killer cells and cytotoxic T cells, thereby exerting their immunotherapeutic functions.[ 13 , 14 ] Nevertheless, the aforementioned PAD4 inhibitors usually suffer from poor solubility, short half‐life, poor targeting, and insufficient therapeutic activity, greatly reducing their effectiveness in inhibiting tumor metastasis and recurrence. To overcome this dilemma, various nanomaterials have been proposed as carriers to deliver PAD4 inhibitors to enhance their permeability in solid tumors. For example, peptide TAT‐modified Au nanoparticles were adopted to load YW3‐56 to induce autophagy and inhibit histone H3 (H3cit) citrullination for enhanced tumor therapy.[ 15 ] RGD peptide and YW3‐56 co‐loaded gold nanorods were also constructed to inhibit tumor growth and prevent lung metastasis for chemo‐photothermal combined therapy.[ 16 ] However, these nanocarriers either lack therapeutic properties, or their therapeutic efficiency is seriously insufficient. Therefore, the combination of PAD4 inhibitors with nanomaterials equipped with highly effective therapeutic performance is expected to improve their solubility, half‐life, and targeting, as well as enhance the antitumor activity through the intervention of other therapies.

Photodynamic therapy (PDT), as a photochemistry‐based cancer treatment modality, has been well performed and proved to be effective in the treatment of clinical superficial cancer and localized infections, due to its superior advantages in negligible drug resistance, high spatiotemporal selectivity, minimal invasiveness, high efficacy, and low systemic toxicity.[ 17 − 21 ] PDT that utilizes visible or near‐infrared (NIR) light to irradiate photosensitizers (PSs) accumulated at the lesion site can achieve spatiotemporal control of reactive oxygen species (ROS, e.g., superoxide anion radical (·O2 −), hydroxyl radical (·OH), and singlet oxygen (1O2)) generation for effective tumor ablation.[ 22 − 32 ] Moreover, PDT can induce antitumor immune responses by upregulating transcription factor and protein expression, promoting cytokines secretion and antigen‐presenting cells (APCs) mutations, recruiting neutrophils to cause macrophage infiltration, especially initiating immunogenic cell death (ICD) that serves as a bridge to connect the immunotherapy with PDT.[ 33 − 36 ] However, tumor hypoxia, inferior tissue penetration of light, and low 1O2 quantum yield significantly limit the efficacy of PDT, further reducing its ability to induce an immune response. Combining PDT with agents that stimulate immune responses gives new possibilities to circumvent the aforementioned challenges, which are expected to refine the performance of PDT and achieve synergistic PDT/immunotherapy for the treatment of tumor metastasis and recurrence.

Layered double hydroxide (LDH) nanosheets have been widely explored in biomedical applications (e.g., cancer therapy, drug delivery, and tissue engineering) thanks to their acid‐sensitive biodegradability, excellent biocompatibility, adjustable chemical composition, and structure.[ 37 − 44 ] As a typical example, we recently proposed a defect engineering strategy to engineer CoMo‐LDH and NiMo‐LDH nanosheets as high‐efficiency PSs for NIR‐II PDT.[ 45 ] In this work, we employ the defect‐rich CoMo‐LDH (DR‐CoMo‐LDH) nanosheets as a carrier to load YW4‐03 to construct a simple but powerful multifunctional nanoagent (403@DR‐LDH) for synergistic PDT/immunotherapy (Scheme 1 ). Specifically, the DR‐CoMo‐LDH nanosheets improve the stability, dispersibility, and half‐life of YW4‐03, enhancing its effectiveness in inhibiting tumor metastasis and recurrence. Upon 1550 nm laser irradiation, 403@DR‐LDH nanosheets not only significantly downregulate the expression of H3cit protein in neutrophils to inhibit the formation of NETs, but also promote the ROS generation and the release of signaling molecules (e.g., HMGB1, CRT, and ATP) to stimulate ICD, thereby generating long‐term immune memory. More importantly, in vivo experiments demonstrate that 403@DR‐LDH nanosheets can efficiently kill cancer cells and inhibit tumor growth and metastasis under 1550 nm laser irradiation, of which the tumor inhibition rate (58.47%) and antitumor metastasis rate (53.41%) are 2.49‐fold and 1.75‐fold that of free YW‐403, and 1.61‐fold, and 7.82‐fold that of DR‐LDH, respectively. Mechanistic studies reveal that 403@DR‐LDH‐mediated PDT and PAD4 inhibitor‐augmented immunotherapy can induce significant tumor infiltration of cytotoxic T cells and improve the tumor immune microenvironment by regulating the ratio of immunosuppressive cells to immunoreactive cells and immune‐related proteins.

Results and Discussion

Synthesis and Characterization

2D CoMo‐LDH nanosheets were prepared using the low‐temperature hydrothermal method according to previous reports.[ 45 ] High‐resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) images displayed the nanosheet morphology of the CoMo‐LDH sample with a size of 60–120 nm, and the measured lattice distance (≈0.38 nm) is attributed to (006) plane of LDH crystal (Figure S1a, Supporting Information). The powder X‐ray diffraction (XRD) pattern displayed the typical (003) and (006) diffraction peaks of CoMo‐LDH nanosheets (Figure 1a), demonstrating its crystal structure. Subsequently, DR‐CoMo‐LDH nanosheets with a size of 50–100 nm were obtained by etching CoMo‐LDH at pH 4.0 for 4 h (Figure S1b, Supporting Information). No obvious lattice fringes were found in the DR‐CoMo‐LDH sample (Figure S1b, Supporting Information, inset), and no diffraction peaks were observed in its XRD pattern (Figure 1a), indicating its amorphous structure. After loading YW‐403, the 403@DR‐LDH nanosheets maintained the morphology and size of DR‐CoMo‐LDH nanosheets (Figure 1b) without regular lattice fringes (Figure 1b insert). The thickness of 403@DR‐LDH nanosheets measured by atomic force microscope (AFM) was 5.0–6.0 nm (Figure 1c; Figure S2, Supporting Information).

The arrow indicates the time point of light irradiation.

Drug Loading and Release

To verify the successful loading of YW‐403, UV–vis spectroscopy, Fourier transform infrared (FT‐IR) spectroscopy, and zeta potential analysis were performed. As presented in Figure S3 (Supporting Information), a characteristic absorption peak of YW‐403 at 235 nm was observed in the 403@DR‐LDH sample, suggesting the successful combination of YW‐403 and DR‐CoMo‐LDH. Such a result was confirmed by FT‐IR spectroscopy (Figure 1d), as the characteristic band at 696 cm−1 (stretching vibration of C‐Cl) of YW‐403 was found in the 403@DR‐LDH, accompanied with the absorption bands of DR‐CoMo‐LDH at 3433 cm−1 (stretching vibration of O−H). The zeta potential of DR‐CoMo‐LDH nanosheets was −18.4 ± 1.6 mV (Figure 1e), which became 3.5 ± 0.6 mV after loading YW‐403 (25.5 ± 2.3 mV). It can be seen that YW‐403 is loaded onto the surface of DR‐CoMo‐LDH nanosheets via electrostatic interaction. Since the M─OH group is present in LDH, YW‐403 also binds to LDH through hydrogen bonding. The loading performance toward YW‐403 was further studied by recording the UV absorbance of the supernatant at 235 nm. In Figure 1f and Figure S4 (Supporting Information), the loading content (LC) and encapsulation efficiency (EE) of YW‐403 at a mass ratio of DR‐LDH: YW‐403 = 1:2 were 55.2% and 27.6%, respectively, while that of DR‐LDH: YW‐403 = 1:1 were 25.2% and 25.2%, respectively. Due to the higher LC and EE of DR‐LDH: YW‐403 = 1:2, this sample was selected for subsequent testing. The weight percentage of YW‐403 in 403@DR‐LDH was also measured to be ≈47.7% according to the thermogravimetric (TG) analysis (Figure 1g), which was basically consistent with the above result. Additionally, the hydrodynamic sizes of DR‐CoMo‐LDH and 403@DR‐LDH determined by dynamic light scattering (DLS) technique were 89.6 ± 3.2 and 97.0 ± 3.6 nm (Figure 1h), respectively.

Since NIR‐IIB light irradiation can cause a photothermal effect because of the absorption of 1550 nm laser in water, the temperature of 403@DR‐LDH suspension under 1550 nm laser irradiation was monitored. As presented in Figure S5 (Supporting Information), the 403@DR‐LDH suspension (100 µg mL−1) in different pH environments gave a temperature increment of less than 5 °C under 1550 nm laser irradiation (0.5 W cm−2) for 2 min. Such a temperature increase will not result in photothermal therapy of tumors. It has been recognized that temperature rise is beneficial for drug release. Thus, the release behavior of YW‐403 from 403@DR‐LDH was studied with external stimuli (e.g., NIR laser irradiation and pH). In Figure 1i, the release amount of YW‐403 at pH 7.4, 6.5, and 5.4 is 10.23%, 13.55%, and 17.87% after 8 h incubation, respectively. The release performance triggered by laser irradiation was then investigated by applying 1550 nm laser irradiation for 2 min (0.5 W cm−2) at time points 10, 30, and 60 min. In Figure 1i, a significantly increased drug release was found: 31.54% at pH 7.4, 39.92% at pH 6.5, and 51.21% at pH 5.4. The release amount at pH 7.4 is less than that at pH 6.5 and 5.4 due to the partial dissolution of DR‐LDH in acid environments. The above results verified that 1550 nm laser irradiation could promote the release of YW‐403 due to the photothermal effect.

ROS Generation of 403@DR‐LDH

Subsequently, the activities of YW‐403, DR‐LDH, and 403@DR‐LDH nanosheets for ROS generation under 1550 nm laser irradiation were investigated by singlet oxygen sensor green (SOSG) assay.[ 46 ] As observed in Figure 2a–d, the pure YW‐403 showed no activity toward ROS generation under 1550 nm laser irradiation (0.5 W cm−2, 6 min). Promisingly, DR‐LDH and 403@DR‐LDH nanosheets exhibited excellent ROS generation performance under laser irradiation, indicating that the loading of YW‐403 had no effect on the PDT performance of DR‐LDH. Electron spin resonance (ESR) spectroscopy was also performed using 2,2,6,6‐tetramethyl‐4‐piperidone (TEMP) as a 1O2 probe.[ 47 ] In Figure 2e, the characteristic 1O2 (1:1:1) signal was clearly observed in the DR‐LDH and 403@DR‐LDH samples, confirming their conspicuous 1O2 generation activity. This conclusion was further verified by 9,10‐anthracenediyl‐bis(methylene) dimalonic acid (ABDA), 1,3‐diphenylisobenzonfuran (DPBF), and 2′,7′‐dichlorofluorescein diacetate (DCFH‐DA) assays,[ 48 − 50 ] as the absorbance of ABDA (Figure 2f,g; Figure S6, Supporting Information) and DPBF (Figure 2h; Figure S7, Supporting Information) decreased significantly, and the fluorescence intensity of 2′,7′‐dichlorofluorescein (DCF) was obviously increased (Figure 2i; Figure S8, Supporting Information) in the DR‐LDH and 403@DR‐LDH groups. The 1O2 quantum yield of 403@DR‐LDH was measured to be 87.5% using Rose Bengal (RB) as a standard PS (Figure S9, Supporting Information), showing great potential for PDT. The ROS generation mechanism of 403@DR‐LDH can be explained as follows: when 403@DR‐LDH nanosheets are exposed to 1550 nm laser irradiation, the photoinduced electrons and holes produced in the 403@DR‐LDH nanosheets occupy the conduction band and valence band, respectively. The photoelectrons are ejected to surroundings and react with O2 to generate intermediate ·O2 −, which further combines with holes to form the final 1O2.[ 45 ] The formation of ·O2 − was verified using the dihydrorhodamine 123 (DHR 123) probe, which can be oxidized by ·O2 − to emit a fluorescence signal at 526 nm. In Figure S10 (Supporting Information), compared with the negligible fluorescence signal in the YW‐403 group, a strong fluorescence intensity of DHR 123 was found in the DR‐LDH and 403@DR‐LDH groups under 1550 nm laser irradiation, confirming the generation of intermediate ·O2 − mediated by DR‐LDH.

Surface Modification of 403@DR‐LDH

To improve biocompatibility and biosafety, polyethylene glycol (PEG) was adopted to modify 403@DR‐LDH. FT‐IR spectroscopy confirmed the combination of PEG and 403@DR‐LDH (Figure S11, Supporting Information), as the characteristic absorption band at 843 cm−1 (stretching vibration of C─O─C) of the PEG was found in the 403@DR‐LDH‐PEG sample. The zeta potential of 403@DR‐LDH‐PEG nanosheets was close to that of 403@DR‐LDH nanosheets with a potential of 2.5 ± 0.5 mV (Figure 1e). According to the TG analysis, the weight percentage of PEG in 403@DR‐LDH‐PEG was ≈24.2% (Figure 1g). DLS technique showed that the hydrodynamic size of 403@DR‐LDH‐PEG was 105.1 ± 3.9 nm (Figure 1h), with no significant change in water, phosphate buffer solution (PBS), and high sugar medium (DMEM) during two weeks (Figure S12, Supporting Information), indicating its excellent dispersion stability.

In Vitro Study with 4T1 cells

To verify whether 403@DR‐LDH‐PEG could be taken up by 4T1 cells, the intracellular drug distribution was observed by TEM. As shown in Figure 3a, both DR‐LDH‐PEG and 403@DR‐LDH‐PEG could be taken up by the cells into the cytoplasm. The Co content in 4T1 cells was also determined by inductively coupled plasma‐mass spectrometry (ICP‐MS) Calcein acetoxymethyl ester. It was found that the Co content in the control group was 0.068 ppb, which was significantly increased to 202.4 ppb in the 403@DR‐LDH‐PEG group (Figure S13a, Supporting Information), illustrating the high‐efficiency cellular uptake of 403@DR‐LDH‐PEG by 4T1 cells. To determine the intracellular endocytosis pathway, we also evaluated the cellular uptake of 403@DR‐LDH‐PEG in the presence of clathrin‐mediated endocytosis inhibitor chlorpromazine (CPZ), macropinocytosis inhibitor amiloride (AM), and caveolae‐mediated endocytosis inhibitor methyl‐β‐cyclodextrin (β‐MCD), respectively. The results showed that the addition of β‐MCD resulted in a significant decrease in the intracellular Co content, whereas the other two inhibitors did not affect the Co content of the cells (Figure S13b, Supporting Information), suggesting that the cellular uptake of 403@DR‐LDH‐PEG by 4T1 cells was achieved through caveolae‐mediated endocytosis. The biocompatibility of DR‐LDH‐PEG and 403@DR‐LDH‐PEG was evaluated on human normal hepatocytes (L02), renal epithelial cells (HEK‐293T), and lung epithelial cells (MLE‐12) using thiazolyl blue (MTT) assay. It was found that DR‐LDH‐PEG and 403@DR‐LDH‐PEG had negligible killing effects on L02, HEK‐293T, and MLE‐12 within 0−100 µg mL−1 (Figure S14, Supporting Information), demonstrating their excellent biocompatibility. To further verify the biocompatibility of the DR‐LDH‐PEG and 403@DR‐LDH‐PEG nanosheets, the hemolysis assay was performed on mouse red blood cells (RBCs). H2O was used as a positive control and PBS as a negative control. After incubation with RBCs for 2 h, the hemolysis rates of DR‐LDH‐PEG and 403@DR‐LDH‐PEG nanosheets at different concentrations (6.25−100 µg mL−1) were less than 5% (Figure S15, Supporting Information), indicating that there was no significant hemolytic activity.

Subsequently, the killing effects of different concentrations of 403@DR‐LDH‐PEG on 4T1 cells were evaluated using MTT assay. In Figure S16a (Supporting Information), DR‐LDH‐PEG exhibited no killing effect on the cells within 0−50 µg mL−1, whereas the cell viability of YW4‐03 and 403@DR‐LDH‐PEG group decreased obviously (Figure 3b), indicating a certain killing effect of YW4‐03 on tumor cells. Moreover, the cell viability of the 403@DR‐LDH‐PEG + 1550 nm laser group further decreased, demonstrating the synergistic effect of PDT‐mediated ROS generation and YW4‐03‐induced apoptosis. Live‐dead cell staining assay was conducted using Calcein acetoxymethyl ester (Calcein‐AM) and propidium iodide (PI) to visualize the therapeutic effects. In Figure S16b (Supporting Information), 4T1 cells treated with 403@DR‐LDH‐PEG showed the strongest red fluorescence intensity after 1550 nm laser irradiation compared with other groups, verifying that 403@DR‐LDH‐PEG + laser induced an excellent cell‐killing effect. The DNA‐damaging effect of 403@DR‐LDH‐PEG on 4T1 cells was also investigated by immunofluorescence staining assay. Figure S16c,d (Supporting Information) shows that the green fluorescence of cells in the control group without any treatment was weak, while the green fluorescence in the nucleus (blue fluorescence) of cells treated with 403@DR‐LDH‐PEG + laser was significantly increased, demonstrating that 403@DR‐LDH‐PEG might exert a killing effect by breaking the DNA of 4T1 cells. The cell apoptosis after different treatments was analyzed using flow cytometry. In Figure 3c,d, the proportions of cells in the apoptotic stage of control and 403@DR‐LDH groups were 5.55% and 6.35%, respectively, whereas the proportion of apoptotic cells reached 19.55% after treatment with YW4‐03, suggesting that YW‐403 could promote the apoptosis of 4T1 cells. More importantly, the proportion of apoptotic cells reached 63.37% after treatment with 403@DR‐LDH‐PEG + laser (Figure 3c,d), indicating the best effect of 403@DR‐LDH‐PEG in inducing cell apoptosis under 1550 nm laser irradiation, which is consistent with the results of MTT and live‐dead cell staining assays. To confirm the synergistic effect of PDT and immunotherapy, we also calculated the combination index (CI) value for the 50% growth inhibition. The calculated CI value was 0.61 (<1), suggesting that our established nano‐system possessed a synergistic antitumor effect of PDT/immunotherapy. Subsequently, to investigate the ROS generation activity in vitro, DCFH‐DA was used to label intracellular ROS, followed by detection using flow cytometry. Figure 3e,f shows that the percentage of ROS in YW4‐03 and 403@DR‐LDH‐PEG treated 4T1 cells was less than 1% (0.016%, 0.01%), while the percentage of cells producing ROS after treatment with 403@DR‐LDH‐PEG + laser was 78.5%, suggesting massive ROS generation mediated by PDT. In addition, considering that DR‐CoMo‐LDH has been reported to possess O2 generation activity by catalyzing H2O2 decomposition,[ 45 ] the ability of DR‐LDH to relieve tumor hypoxia was evaluated using red hypoxia detection reagent. In Figure S17 (Supporting Information), strong fluorescence signals were observed in the blank, DR‐LDH‐PEG, and YW‐403 + H2O2 groups. In contrast, the fluorescence intensity in the DR‐LDH‐PEG + H2O2 and 403@DR‐LDH‐PEG groups significantly decreased, revealing DR‐LDH‐PEG‐mediated hypoxia relief.

It has been reported that PDT may cause ICD, a cell death pattern that stimulates innate and adaptive immune responses and generates long‐term immune memory,[ 51 − 53 ] characterized by upregulation of signaling molecules such as HMGB1, CRT, and ATP, which act as danger signals to stimulate the maturation of antigen‐presenting cells such as dendric (DC) cells and promote antigen presentation, thereby recruiting effector T cells for immune response.[ 54 − 57 ] In view of this, the expression of CRT and HMGB1 was investigated using immunofluorescent staining. In Figure S18 (Supporting Information), compared with the control group, an increase in green fluorescence and a decrease in red fluorescence was found in 4T1 cells treated with DR‐LDH‐PEG + laser and 403@DR‐LDH‐PEG + laser, indicating that PDT‐mediated ROS generation could promote the expression of CRT and the release of HMGB1, and reduce the retention of HMGB1 in the nucleus. The ATP and HMGB1 contents in cell supernatants were also quantified by Elisa kit, and the results showed that the extracellular ATP and HMGB1 contents were increased after treatment with 403@DR‐LDH‐PEG + 1550 nm laser compared with the control group (Figure S19, Supporting Information). The above results demonstrated that 403@DR‐LDH‐PEG could induce ICD under laser irradiation by upregulating the expression of CRT, HMGB1, and ATP signaling molecules in 4T1 cells.

Subsequently, transwell experiments were carried out to explore the anti‐metastasis ability of 403@DR‐LDH‐PEG. As presented in Figure 3g,h, the number of membrane‐penetrating 4T1 cells in the 403@DR‐LDH‐PEG + laser group decreased markedly and was not significantly different from that of the positive group with RGDS (20 µM) (p = 0.9988), manifesting the excellent in vitro anti‐metastatic activity of 403@DR‐LDH‐PEG‐mediated PDT. Furthermore, the effect of 403@DR‐LDH‐PEG on NETs release was evaluated. In Figure 3i, obvious filaments (red fluorescence), as well as leakage of intracellular DNA (blue fluorescence), could be seen in the control, laser, YW4‐03, and 403@DR‐LDH‐PEG groups after induction by the inducer, while weakened red fluorescence was observed in the 403@DR‐LDH‐PEG + laser group, proving that 403@DR‐LDH‐PEG could effectively inhibit H3cit expression and NETs formation under laser irradiation.

In Vivo Antitumor Effects in 4T1 Tumor‐Bearing Mice

Motivated by the aforementioned in vitro results, the in vivo antitumor and anti‐metastatic effects of 403@DR‐LDH‐PEG were conducted on 4T1 in situ hormonal mice. First, the blood circulation half‐life of YW4‐03 and 403@DR‐LDH‐PEG was calculated to be 0.618 h and 9.201 h (Figure S20, Supporting Information). The long blood circulation time was beneficial for the accumulation of 403@DR‐LDH‐PEG at the tumor site. The biodistribution of 403@DR‐LDH‐PEG was also investigated by collecting major organs and tumors at indicated time points (1‐48 h) after intravenous injection for ICP‐MS analysis. The results revealed that 403@DR‐LDH‐PEG tended to accumulate in the liver and spleen, and was also significantly enriched in tumor tissues, where the maximum accumulation occurred at 8 h post‐injection (Figure S21, Supporting Information). Based on this, laser irradiation of the tumor site was performed after 8 h of drug administration. 4T1‐Luc cells were inoculated at the mouse breast pads, and drug treatment (iv) was started 8 days after inoculation and administered every other day for 14 days (Figure 4a). Figure 4b shows that compared with the saline group (NS), the tumor volume of mice in YW4‐03 and DR‐LDH‐PEG + laser groups grew more slowly, indicating that both of them had certain inhibitory effects on tumor growth. It is worth noting that the tumor volume of mice in the 403@DR‐LDH‐PEG + laser group was the smallest with a significant difference (p < 0.01), indicating that 403@DR‐LDH‐PEG had the best inhibitory effect on tumor growth under laser irradiation. The tumor weight analysis also confirmed the above results (Figure 4c), as the tumors in the 403@DR‐LDH‐PEG + laser group were the lightest in weight compared with other groups with a tumor inhibition rate of 58.47%. Hematoxylin eosin (H&E) staining was further performed and the results showed that dense tumor tissue with significant inflammatory infiltration was observed in the NS (saline), laser alone and DR‐LDH‐PEG groups, and a slight improvement of inflammatory infiltration was found in the YW4‐03 and DR‐LDH‐PEG + laser groups, while no dense tumor tissue with less inflammation was seen in the 403@DR‐LDH‐PEG + laser group (Figure 4d), indicating the excellent activity of 403@DR‐LDH‐PEG to induce cellular damage under laser irradiation. Such a result was further confirmed by Ki67 (one of the indicators for detecting cell proliferation) and Terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase‐mediated dUTP nick‐end‐labeling (TUNEL, one of the methods for detecting apoptosis) staining assays, as less cell proliferation and more cell apoptosis were presented in 403@DR‐LDH‐PEG + laser group compared with other groups (Figure 4d). Furthermore, small animal fluorescence imaging was carried out to verify the antitumor effect. As shown in Figure S22 (Supporting Information), the fluorescence signal intensity at the tumor site in 403@DR‐LDH‐PEG + laser group was the weakest compared with the control, YW4‐03, and DR‐LDH‐PEG groups, and the signal intensity increased slightly after 14 days of tumor growth. The above results demonstrated the enhanced synergistic antitumor effect of 403@DR‐LDH‐PEG under laser irradiation.

Subsequently, the effect of 403@DR‐LDH‐PEG on H3cit expression and NETs formation was assessed by labelling neutrophils with Ly6G. In Figure 4e,f, compared with the strong red fluorescence from H3cit and green fluorescence from NETs in the control group, the 403@DR‐LDH‐PEG + laser group exhibited significantly weakened red and green fluorescence, indicating the inhibited H3cit expression and NETs formation. Such a result was further verified by western blotting (WB) assay, as the expression of H3cit proteins in the tumor tissue significantly decreased after treatment with 403@DR‐LDH‐PEG + laser compared with other groups (Figure S23, Supporting Information), confirming that 403@DR‐LDH‐PEG suppressed the formation of NETs under laser irradiation, which is conducive to inhibiting the growth and metastasis of tumors. Since 403@DR‐LDH‐PEG showed excellent antitumor metastatic activity in vitro, its anti‐metastatic activity in vivo was further validated. As presented in Figure 4g,h, pathologic sections of the lungs showed significantly thickened alveolar septa and severe inflammatory infiltration in the control group, while the 403@DR‐LDH‐PEG + laser group showed normal alveolar structure and the least number of metastatic tumor nodes, demonstrating the excellent antitumor metastatic ability of 403@DR‐LDH‐PEG under laser irradiation with anti‐tumor metastasis rate of 53.41%, which could be attributed to the synergistic effect of YW4‐03 and PDT‐mediated ROS generation.

As evidenced by in vitro assays, 403@DR‐LDH‐PEG could generate a large amount of ROS under laser irradiation (Figure 3e,f), thus causing apoptosis of tumor cells (Figure 3b–d). Apoptotic tumor cells could release damage‐associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) molecules such as HMGB1, ATP, and CRT to cause ICD (Figures S18 and S19, Supporting Information). On the other hand, these DAMPs molecules could also cause neutrophils to release NETs,[ 58 ] which could block immune cell infiltration through MMP‐2 and MMP‐9,[ 59 ] thus affecting the effect of immunotherapy. In view of this, the MMP‐2 and MMP‐9 levels in tumor tissues were evaluated after treatment with 403@DR‐LDH‐PEG + laser. As shown in Figure S24 (Supporting Information), compared with the NS group, the expression levels of MMP‐2 and MMP‐9 in tumor tissues significantly decreased after administration with 403@DR‐LDH‐PEG under laser irradiation, revealing that 403@DR‐LDH‐PEG could inhibit the production of NETs by down‐regulating the expression of MMP‐2 and MMP‐9, thus promoting immune cell infiltration.

In addition, single‐cell analysis was performed on tumor tissues using mass spectrometry flow (CyTOF) to evaluate the effect of 403@DR‐LDH‐PEG on tumor immune microenvironment regulation. As shown in Figure 5a–e, the proportions of neutrophils, dendritic cells (DCs, antigen‐presenting cells), CD4+T, and CD8+T cells in the control group were 16.9%, 5.58%, 4.41%, and 1.99%, respectively, while the proportions of these cells in the 403@DR‐LDH‐PEG + laser group were 9.63%, 8.27%, 14.13%, and 6.71%, respectively, demonstrating that 403@DR‐LDH‐PEG could activate adaptive immune response by reducing neutrophils and increasing effector T cells under 1550 nm laser irradiation. Immunosuppressive signaling molecules in the tumor immune microenvironment were also monitored. Figure 5f shows that the expression of PD‐1 by immune‐associated cells (DCs, CD4+T, and CD8+T) in the 403@DR‐LDH‐PEG + laser group was decreased compared with the control group. In Figure 5g–i, the Forkhead box protein P3 (FOXP3) and CXCR4 content in the 403@DR‐LDH‐PEG + laser group was decreased, while the expression of chemokine receptor 4 (CCR4) in the CD8+T cell was obviously increased in comparison with the control group, indicating that 403@DR‐LDH‐PEG could down‐regulate the immunosuppressive signaling molecules of PD‐1, FOXP3, and CXCR4 to inhibit the immune escape of tumor cells, and up‐regulate the immune‐boosting signaling molecules, such as CCR4, to promote the activation and infiltration of T cells, thereby improving the immune microenvironment of tumors (Figure 5j).[ 60 − 62 ] In addition, since TNF‐α could directly kill tumor cells and induce apoptosis, while IFN‐γ would activate immune cells such as DCs to promote immune response and kill tumor cells, the levels of TNF‐α and IFN‐γ in mouse serum were detected using Elisa kit. The results showed that the serum levels of two factors were increased in mice treated with 403@DR‐LDH‐PEG + laser compared with the other groups (Figure S25, Supporting Information), suggesting that 403@DR‐LDH‐PEG‐mediated synergetic therapy is conducive to creating an antitumor immune microenvironment.

Data are presented as mean values ± s.d. (n = 3). Statistical analysis through unpaired t‐test analysis: * p < 0.05 and ** p < 0.01.

Finally, to evaluate the biosafety of 403@DR‐LDH‐PEG, the body weight and organ weight of mice were recorded, and the organ sections were stained with H&E. The results showed that after 14 days of treatment, there was little change in body weight and little difference in organ weight among experimental groups. The H&E staining images of pathological sections also indicated that the 403@DR‐LDH‐PEG did not cause any damage to the organs of the mice (Figure S26, Supporting Information), demonstrating its excellent biocompatibility. In addition, we determined liver and kidney function indices in the serum of mice. The results, as shown in Figure S27 (Supporting Information), indicated that the serum levels of aspartate alanine aminotransferase (ALT), UREA, creatinine (CREA), and aminotransferase (AST) in 403@DR‐LDH‐PEG+L‐treated mice were not different from those in the normal group of mice without tumors (Sham), which further illustrated the good biosafety of 403@DR‐LDH‐PEG.

Conclusion

In summary, a simple but powerful multifunctional nanoplatform (403@DR‐LDH) was constructed by loading YW4‐03 onto DR‐CoMo‐LDH nanosheets for synergistic PDT/immunotherapy, which significantly improved the solubility and half‐life of YW4‐03. Upon irradiation with a 1550 nm laser, 403@DR‐LDH not only induced robust ROS generation, but also stimulated ICD by triggering the release of signaling molecules (e.g., HMGB1, CRT, and ATP), thereby promoting DCs maturation and T cell infiltration. A series of in vitro studies demonstrated that 403@DR‐LDH nanosheets significantly inhibited the formation of NETs by downregulating the expression of H3cit protein in neutrophils under 1550 nm laser irradiation. Using a 4T1 tumor model, in vivo experiments proved that 403@DR‐LDH nanosheets plus 1550 nm laser irradiation efficiently killed cancer cells and inhibited tumor growth and metastasis, of which the tumor inhibition rate was 58.47% and antitumor metastasis rate was 53.41%, outperforming that of the free YW‐403 and DR‐LDH. This work provided a novel strategy to maximize the effectiveness of combined PDT and immunotherapy.

Materials and Reagents

Dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) was purchased from Sigma‐Aldrich (USA). Fetal bovine serum (FBS) was purchased from Biological Industries (Israel). A reactive oxygen species assay kit, apoptosis assay kit, D‐fluorescein potassium salt (high purity), DAPI, and antifluorescence quenching blocking agent were purchased from Beyotime (Shanghai, China). Characterizations

XRD patterns were performed on a Shimadzu XRD‐6000 diffractometer equipped with a Cu Kα radiation source (λ = 0.15406 nm). TEM images were taken using a JEM‐2010 transmission electron microscope (JEOL, Tokyo, Japan) with an accelerating voltage of 200 kV. The thickness of the CoMo‐LDH nanosheets was characterized by an atomic force microscope (AFM, MultiMode 8, Bruker) in tapping mode. ESR spectra were acquired on the Bruker EMX1598 spectrometer. Zetasizer UV spectrometer (Malvern Instruments, U.K.) was used to investigate the hydrodynamic sizes and zeta potentials of the samples.

Synthesis of CoMo‐LDH and DR‐CoMo‐LDH Nanosheets

CoMo‐LDH nanosheets were prepared by a hydrothermal method according to previous reports.[ 45 ] First, NaOH (2 g) was dissolved in 50 mL distilled water to obtain solution A. (NH4)6Mo7O24·4H2O (5 mmol) and Co(NO3)2·6H2O (1 mmol) were dissolved in 70 mL distilled water (obtained by boiling deionized water with N2 for at least 30 min) and vigorously stirred to obtain solution B. Then, solution A was slowly dropped into solution B at room temperature in the N2 atmosphere, maintaining a pH of 9.7. Third, the obtained precipitation was transferred into a Teflon autoclave reactor (100 mL) and heated at 60 °C for 24 h. Finally, the resulting CoMo‐LDH nanosheets were washed with distilled water by centrifugation. The DR‐CoMo‐LDH nanosheets were prepared by etching CoMo‐LDH nanosheets in a pH 4.0 buffer solution for 4 h.

Synthesis of 403@DR‐LDH and 403@DR‐LDH‐PEG Nanosheets

YW‐403 (20 mg) was dissolved in 10 mL of PBS and then added to DR‐CoMo‐LDH suspension (10 mg dissolved in 10 mL of PBS). The mixture was vigorously stirred at room temperature for 24 h and centrifuged at 6000 rpm to obtain 403@DR‐LDH. To prepare the 403@DR‐LDH‐PEG sample, PEG (100 mg) was added to 403@DR‐LDH suspension (10 mg dissolved in 10 mL of PBS) under ultrasonic treatment for 30 min and then stirred at room temperature for 8 h. The resulting 403@DR‐LDH‐PEG was washed with distilled water four times and then re‐dispersed in distilled water for use.

Evaluation of ROS Generation

The generation of 1O2 was evaluated by SOSG, ABDA, and DPBF assays. For SOSG assay, YW‐403 (0.2 mL, 0.55 mg mL−1), DR‐LDH (0.2 mL, 1 mg mL−1), and 403@DR‐LDH (0.2 mL, 1 mg mL−1 of DR‐LDH) were added to an aqueous solution (1.8 mL) containing SOSG (2 µM), respectively. Then, these mixtures were exposed to 1550 laser irradiation (0.5 W cm−2) for 6 min. The generation of 1O2 was detected by recording the fluorescence intensity of SOSG at 526 nm every 1 min. For ABDA assay, YW‐403 (80 µL, 1.375 mg mL−1), DR‐LDH (80 µL, 2.5 mg mL−1) and 403@DR‐LDH (80 µL, 2.5 mg mL−1 of DR‐LDH) were added to 1920 µL H2O containing ABDA (0.2 mM), respectively. The obtained mixtures were irradiated by a 1550 nm laser (0.5 W cm−2) for 6 min. The generated 1O2 was detected by recording the absorbance of ABDA at 410 nm every 1 min. For the DPBF assay, the 1O2 generation was detected by similar procedures except that ABDA (0.2 mm) was changed to DPBF (25 µg mL−1).

ESR spectroscopy was adopted to verify the 1O2 generation using TEMP as a probe. Simply put, TEMP (0.1 mL, 90 mm) was added to the YW‐403 (0.1 mL, 0.11 mg mL−1), DR‐LDH (0.1 mL, 0.2 mg mL−1) and 403@DR‐LDH solution (0.1 mL, 0.2 mg mL−1), respectively. Then, the generated 1O2 was detected by ESR spectrometer after 1550 nm laser irradiation (0.5 W cm−2) for 6 min.

The ROS generation was further confirmed by DCFH‐DA assay. Briefly, YW‐403 (0.2 mL, 0.55 mg mL−1), DR‐LDH (0.2 mL, 1 mg mL−1), and 403@DR‐LDH (0.2 mL, 1 mg mL−1 of DR‐LDH) were added to aqueous solution (1.8 mL) containing DCFH‐DA (6 mM), respectively. After 6 min of 1550 nm laser irradiation (0.5 W cm−2), the fluorescence intensity of these mixtures at 525 nm was recorded every 1 min.

Cell Uptake Experiment

4T1 cells were inoculated in Petri dishes (1 × 105 cells) and divided into: 1) control, 2) laser (1550 nm, 0.5 W cm−2, 6 min), 3) YW4‐03 (2.5 µg mL−1), 4) DR‐LDH‐PEG (5 µg mL−1), 5) DR‐LDH‐PEG (5 µg mL−1) + laser, 6) 403@DR‐LDH (5 µg mL−1), 7) 403@LDH (5 µg mL−1) + laser. After incubation for 4 h, the cells were irradiated by a 1550 nm laser for 6 min and then incubated for another 4 h. Subsequently, all treated cells were rinsed with PBS three times and then fixed with electron microscope fixative at room temperature for 5 min. All treated cells were collected in 1.5 mL centrifuge tubes using a cell spatula, centrifuged to discard the fixative, and then fixed with a new electron microscope fixative at room temperature for 0.5 h away from light. Finally, the embedded sections were performed for observation using a transmission electron microscope (HITACHI, HT7800/HT7700, Japan).

In Vitro Cytotoxicity

MTT assay was utilized to determine the killing effect of the samples on tumor cells. 4T1 cells were inoculated in 96‐well plates (5000 cells per well), incubated with high sugar DMEM medium added with 10% FBS overnight and then treated with various concentrations of the following samples for a total of 24 h (37 °C, 5% CO2): 1) YW4‐03, 2) DR‐LDH‐PEG, 3) DR‐LDH‐PEG + laser, 4) 403@DR‐LDH, 5) 403@DR‐LDH + laser. 4T1 cells were exposed to 1550 nm laser irradiation for 6 min (0.5 W cm−2) at 12 h post‐incubation. After every 1 min of laser irradiation, alcohol cotton was used to wipe the culture plates (15 s) to avoid potential temperature rise. Subsequently, the cells were added with MTT (5 mg mL−1) for another 4 h. The absorbance at 490 and 570 nm was measured by a multifunctional enzyme marker (SpectraMax ABS, Molecular Devices, America), and the data were analyzed by one‐way ANOVA method using GraphPad Prism 9.4.0.

Calcein‐AM/PI Staining

4T1 cells were inoculated in 6‐well plates (1 × 105 cells per well) and divided into: 1) control, 2) laser (1550 nm, 0.5 W cm−2, 6 min), 3) YW4‐03 (2.5 µg mL−1), 4) DR‐LDH‐PEG (5 µg mL−1), 5) DR‐LDH‐PEG (5 µg mL−1) + laser, 6) 403@DR‐LDH (5 µg mL−1), 7) 403@DR‐LDH (5 µg mL−1) + laser. 4T1 cells were exposed to a 1550 nm laser for 6 min (0.5 W cm−2) at 12 h post‐incubation. After incubation for another 12 h, all treated cells were stained with Calcein‐AM/PI staining reagent, and fluorescence signals were observed using a fluorescence microscope (Zeiss, Germany).

Apoptosis Experiment

4T1 cells were inoculated in 6‐well plates (1 × 105 cells per well) and divided into: 1) control, 2) laser (1550 nm, 0.5 W cm−2, 6 min), 3) YW4‐03 (2.5 µg mL−1), 4) DR‐LDH‐PEG (5 µg mL−1), 5) DR‐LDH‐PEG (5 µg mL−1) + laser, 6) 403@DR‐LDH (5 µg mL−1), 7) 403@DR‐LDH (5 µg mL−1) + laser. 4T1 cells were exposed to a 1550 nm laser for 6 min (0.5 W cm−2) at 12 h post‐incubation. After incubation for a further 12 h, the cells were collected and stained with Annexin‐FITC/PI solution, followed by detection using a flow cytometer (LSRFortessa SORP, Becton, Dickinson and Company, USA).

Detection of ROS In Vitro

4T1 cells were inoculated in 6‐well plates (1 × 105 cells per well) and divided into: 1) control, 2) laser (1550 nm, 0.5 W cm−2, 6 min), 3) YW4‐03 (2.5 µg mL−1), 4) DR‐LDH‐PEG (5 µg mL−1), 5) DR‐LDH‐PEG (5 µg mL−1) + laser, 6) 403@DR‐LDH (5 µg mL−1), 7) 403@DR‐LDH (5 µg mL−1) + laser. 4T1 cells were exposed to a 1550 nm laser for 6 min (0.5 W cm−2) at 12 h post‐incubation and then continued to be incubated for 12 h. Afterward, the ROS produced by the cells were labeled using DCFH‐DA and then detected using flow cytometry (LSRFortessa SORP, Becton, Dickinson and Company, USA).

Cell Migration Assay

4T1 cells were inoculated in the upper chamber of the transwell (5 × 104 cells per well). The lower chamber was added with 600 µL of medium containing 10% serum. Then, 4T1 cells were divided into: 1) control, 2) laser (1550 nm, 0.5 W cm−2, 6 min), 3) YW4‐03 (2.5 µg mL−1), 4) DR‐LDH‐PEG (5 µg mL−1), 5) DR‐LDH‐PEG (5 µg mL−1) + laser, 6) 403@DR‐LDH (5 µg mL−1),7) 403@DR‐LDH (5 µg mL−1) + laser, 8) RGDS (20 µM). After incubation for 12 h, the cells were irradiated by a 1550 nm laser for 6 min and then incubated for another 12 h. The medium of the upper chamber was removed, the cells were rinsed twice with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde at 4 °C for 30 min, and stained with crystal violet at room temperature for 15 min. The cells were washed off the crystal violet to dry and then photographed with a light microscope (Zeiss, Germany). The number of migrated cells was counted by ImageJ and expressed as mean ± SD.

NETs Formation Inhibition Experiment

The hind legs were removed after the decapitation and execution of ICR mice, and the bone ends were cut off to expose the cavity. The bone marrow suspension was rinsed out with PBS and then filtered with a 70 µm nylon filter membrane to obtain the bone marrow single‐cell suspension. Neutrophils were isolated using a mouse neutrophil isolation kit (TBDSceicge, LZS1100) and inoculated into confocal petri dishes divided into: 1) control, 2) laser (1550 nm, 0.5 W cm−2, 6 min), 3) YW4‐03 (2.5 µg mL−1), 4) DR‐LDH‐PEG (5 µg mL−1), 5) DR‐LDH‐PEG (5 µg mL−1) + laser, 6) 403@DR‐LDH (5 µg mL−1), 7) 403@DR‐LDH (5 µg mL−1) + laser. After incubation for 1 h, the cells were exposed to a 1550 nm laser for 6 min and then incubated for another 1 h. Subsequently, 5 µmol L−1 calcium ionophore A23187 was added for 2 h of incubation and then centrifuged to remove the medium. All treated cells were rinsed with PBST, and PBS respectively and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, then rinsed with PBST, and PBS respectively and closed with 5% BSA for 30 min. The cells were further washed with PBST and PBS, respectively, and the H3cit antibody (ABCAM:ab5103) was added overnight at 4 °C. The primary antibody was recovered by centrifugation. The cells were rinsed with PBST and PBS, respectively, and then stained with DAPI for 3 min. After washing with PBST and PBS, an antifluorescence quenching sealer was added and observed using a laser confocal microscope (TCS SP8 STED, Leica, Germany).

Cellular Immunofluorescence

4T1 cells were inoculated in confocal Petri dishes (1 × 104 cells per well) and divided into: 1) control, 2) laser (1550 nm, 0.5 W cm−2, 6 min), 3) YW4‐03 (2.5 µg mL−1), 4) DR‐LDH‐PEG (5 µg mL−1), 5) DR‐LDH‐ PEG (5 µg mL−1) + laser, 6) 403@DR‐LDH (5 µg mL−1), 7) 403@DR‐LDH (5 µg mL−1) + laser. After incubation for 12 h, the cells were exposed to a 1550 nm laser for 6 min and then incubated for another 12 h. The medium was discarded and the cells were rinsed with PBS and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, then washed with PBST and PBS respectively and blocked with 5% BSA for 30 min, and the cells were incubated with the corresponding antibodies at 4 °C overnight. Afterward, the cells were incubated with PBST and PBS overnight and then incubated with a secondary antibody with a fluorescent marker for 2 h. All treated cells were rinsed with PBS and stained with DAPI for 3 min. After washing away DAPI with PBS, the antifluorescent quenching sealer was added to the confocal petri dish for observation with a confocal microscope (Leica, TCS SP8 STED, Germany).

4T1 Breast Cancer Mouse Model

Female BALB/c mice (6−8 weeks old) were acquired from Beijing Vital Liver Laboratory Animal Technology Co. All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with protocols approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Capital Medical University (No. AEEI‐2018‐174). To establish a xenograft tumor model, luciferase‐labeled 4T1 cells were cultured using a high‐sugar DMEM medium containing 10% FBS, washed with PBS three times, and then resuspended in PBS to 1 × 107 cells mL−1.[ 13 ] Subsequently, 0.1 mL of cell suspension was injected into the third pair of breast pads on the left side of each mouse. When the tumor volume of mice reached ≈50 mm3, mice were randomized into: 1) NS (saline), 2) 1550 nm laser (0.5 W cm−2, 6 min), 3) YW4‐03 (250 µg mL−1), 4) DR‐LDH‐PEG (500 µg mL−1), 5) DR‐LDH‐PEG (500 µg mL−1) + laser, 6) 403@DR‐LDH (500 µg mL−1), 7) 403@DR‐LDH (500 µg mL−1) + laser (n = 12). The above drugs were injected intravenously. Laser irradiation of the tumor site was performed for 6 min after 8 h of drug administration, and the tumors were wiped with alcohol cotton (15 s) after every 1 min of irradiation. The above drugs were administered every other day, and the tumor volume and body weight of mice were measured for 15 days.

Small Animal Fluorescence Imaging

Mice bearing 4T1 tumors were injected intraperitoneally with 0.1 mL of D‐fluorescein (10 mg mL−1) at day 7 and day 14 and then anesthetized with isoflurane for 10 min. Fluorescence signals of mice were acquired using the IVIS Spectrum system, and data were processed and analyzed using Living Image 4.4.

Immunofluorescence

The mice were executed after 15 days of treatment. The tumors were collected, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, and then embedded in paraffin. Tumor sections were dewaxed, rehydrated, and then closed with 5% BSA. Subsequently, the sections were treated with the corresponding antibody at 4 °C overnight. Finally, the sections were treated with secondary antibodies with fluorescent labeling for 1 h, stained with DAPI for 5 min away from light, and observed by a section scanner (Pannoramic MIDI, 3DHISTECH, Hungary).

Immunohistochemistry

After the removal of the tumors and major organs, paraffin sections were prepared by fixing them in a 4% paraformaldehyde solution. The obtained sections were deparaffinized and rehydrated to repair the antigen and then treated with 5% BSA occlusion for 1 h. Subsequently, these sections were treated with antibodies at 4 °C overnight, followed by treatment with HRP‐labeled secondary antibody for 1 h and exposure to hematoxylin in the dark. The paraffin sections were finally observed with a positive light microscope (NIKON ECLIPSE E100, Nikon, Japan).

Western Blotting

The tumors were frozen at −80 °C immediately after removal from mice, and the proteins of tumor tissues were extracted according to the instructions of the Protein Extraction Kit (medium) (GenePu/GPP1815). The protein concentration was adjusted with water and SDS‐PAGE Loading Buffer (5×) (GenePu/GPP1820) according to the number of electrophoresis samples required, and the protein was denatured by boiling at 100 °C for 10 min. According to the molecular weight of the target protein, 12% (PAD4), 15% (H3cit) separator gel, and 5% concentrate gel were prepared, and 20 µg of samples were loaded into each well and then transferred to the PVDF membrane. The membrane was closed with 5% milk containment buffer for 1 h and then incubated with the antibody at 4 °C overnight. Subsequently, the membranes were treated with HRP‐labeled secondary antibody for 1 h, immersed in ECL color development solution (GenePu, GPP1824) for 1 min, and then exposed to a dark room. Finally, the grayscale values of the bands were read using Quantity One v.4.6.2 software.

Single‐Cell Mass Cytometry Analysis

The tumor tissues were removed and preserved in a fresh medium and subsequently prepared into single‐cell suspensions. Live cells were isolated and fixed for 15 min and washed with CSB (Cell Staining Buffer, Fluidigm, CA, USA) three times. Subsequently, the cells were stained with antibodies targeting cell surface markers with metal markers for 30 min, washed three times for cell permeabilization, and then stained with antibodies targeting intracellular markers, and washed three times after completion of staining. The cells were stained with iridium‐containing DNA intermediates (191 Ir/193 Ir, final concentration 125 nM) in FIX and Perm (Fluidigm, CA, USA) solutions at room temperature for 1 h. After three washes, all treated cells were detected by a mass spectrometer (Fluidigm, CA, USA).

Statistical Analysis

Data statistics were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.4.0. Fluorescence images were analyzed using Image J. All data were expressed as mean ± s.d. The statistical significance of a portion of the data was analyzed using ANOVA's univariate analysis and expressed as: *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***,p < 0.001, and ****p < 0.0001, while the statistical significance of another portion of the data was analyzed using unpaired t‐tests and expressed as: *p < 0.05 and **p < 0.01.
