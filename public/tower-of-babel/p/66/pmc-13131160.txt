Validation of current mortality risk prediction models in patients with cirrhosis undergoing cardiac surgery
Authors: Haley Jenkins, Ahmed K. Awad, Salvatore Poddi, Bradley Rosinski, Robert O'Shea, Bo Xu, Faisal Bakaeen, Haytham Elgharably
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLiver cirrhosis is a known risk factor for mortality after cardiac surgery. In this study, we investigated the correlation between existing prediction models and actual mortality rates after cardiac surgery.MethodsBetween July 2014 and December 2022, 1257 patients with a history of chronic liver disease underwent cardiac surgery, of whom 220 had a diagnosis of cirrhosis. Preoperative comorbidities, intraoperative details, and postoperative outcomes were analyzed. The Mayo Clinic and VOCAL-Penn scores were applied to the 220 patients and compared to observed mortality.ResultsAmong the 220 patients with cirrhosis, the preoperative median MELD score was 10. According to the Mayo Clinic score, the mean predicted mortality rate was 20.9% at 30 days and 29.6% at 90 days. In contrast, according to the VOCAL-Penn score, the mean predicted mortality rate was 9.0% at 30 days and 14.0% at 90 days, whereas the observed postoperative mortality of the 220 patients was 4.1% at 30 days and 12.6% at 90 days. VOCAL-Penn showed good calibration but still overestimated mortality by 3% at 30 days and by 1.3% at 90 days. The Mayo Clinic score demonstrated weak calibration, overestimating mortality by 15% at 30 days and by 17% at 90 days.ConclusionsIn this single-center analysis, the Mayo Clinic and VOCAL-Penn scores overestimated the postoperative mortality in patients with cirrhosis undergoing cardiac surgery, although VOCAL-Penn provided a more accurate risk prediction. Ultimately, this heterogeneous group of patients often presents with a wide range of comorbidities, which complicate risk stratification; however, patients should not be denied surgery solely on the basis of current risk prediction models.

Central Message

The currently available prediction models for mortality after cardiac surgery in patients with chronic liver disease can overestimate the operative risk and should be interpreted with caution.

Perspective

Patients with chronic liver disease are at increased risk of postoperative complications and mortality after cardiac surgery. Operative risk stratification for these patients remains a clinical challenge, particularly in the absence of a specific prediction model that accurately reflects postoperative outcomes. Thus, an updated mortality risk calculator is needed for patients with cirrhosis.

The incidence of chronic liver diseases is on the rise in the Western world, and as medical treatment improves, more patients with liver cirrhosis are presenting for cardiac surgery.1 This patient group represents a clinical challenge, particularly for procedures requiring cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB).2 Evidence supports that those patients with chronic liver disease, including cirrhosis, are at increased risk of complications and mortality after cardiac surgery, often proportional to their Child-Pugh (CP) and Model for End-Stage Liver Disease (MELD) scores.2,3 While multiple preoperative risk assessment tools are available to risk-stratify noncirrhotic patients before cardiac surgery, surgical risk assessment is not adjusted for liver dysfunction.4 This may be related in part to limited evidence and the lack of validated cut-off data for increased mortality in this complex patient population.

Few studies have demonstrated acceptable mortality in cirrhotic patients undergoing cardiac surgery, particularly in compensated or earlier stages.1,2,5 However, evidence is limited, with discrepancies in safety margins for undergoing surgery. Routine hepatology evaluations are performed to estimate the risk of surgery; nevertheless, adequate risk stratification for these patients is not clearly defined in the literature.4 Several scoring systems, such as the Mayo Clinic and Veterans Outcomes and Cost Associated with Liver Disease (VOCAL)-Penn Scores, are available to risk-stratify these patients prior to surgical procedures.6,7 Both of these risk calculators were recently developed to estimate postoperative mortality after major surgeries.7 Notably, the Mayo Clinic score estimates risk after any abdominal, orthopedic, or cardiac procedure, whereas the VOCAL-Penn score allows for selection of the type of procedure, including chest and cardiac procedures.8 Although there is more evidence supporting the use of risk calculators for general surgery procedures, there is limited information on their accuracy in predicting postoperative complications following cardiac surgery.

In this study, we hypothesized that current preoperative risk assessment for patients with liver disease might not accurately predict postoperative outcomes. To test this hypothesis, we examined the correlation between existing prediction models and observed postoperative mortality in a single-center cohort of patients with cirrhosis undergoing cardiac surgery. Additionally, we analyzed the outcomes of cirrhotic patients stratified by CP and MELD scores and then compared them to the outcomes of a matched cohort of noncirrhotic patients.

Methods

Study Cohort

All adult patients undergoing cardiac surgery with a preoperative diagnosis of chronic liver disease at the Cleveland Clinic between July 2014 and December 2022 were queried. A chart review of these patients was performed to determine which had a preoperative diagnosis of cirrhosis, defined based on imaging evidence of cirrhotic features (by liver ultrasound, computed tomography, or magnetic resonance imaging) or by positive liver biopsy. Exclusion criteria included age <18 years and prior or concomitant liver transplantation, off-pump cardiac surgery, and transcatheter procedures. Data on baseline characteristics, procedural details, and postoperative courses were collected from the Cleveland Clinic database and from chart review. Preoperative variables were used to calculate predicted mortality by the Mayo Clinic and VOCAL-Penn scores manually, as well as preoperative MELD and CP scores. All data were approved for use in research by the Cleveland Clinic’s Institutional Review Board (IRB 23-062; approved January 17, 2023) as Exempt Human Subject Research, with a waiver of informed consent.

Study Endpoints

The primary endpoint was postoperative outcomes in patients with cirrhosis undergoing cardiac surgery. Secondary endpoints included comparing observed versus predicted 30-day and 90-day mortality using the Mayo Clinic and VOCAL-Penn scores, as well as survival after cardiac surgery.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were assessed for normality and are reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or as median (15th to 85th percentile) whenever appropriate. The Student t test was used for normally distributed continuous variables, while the Wilcoxon rank-sum test was applied for skewed continuous variables. Categorical variables are presented as frequency and percentage. The χ2 test was used for standard categorical variables, and the Fisher exact test was used for outcomes with ≤5 events. Baseline characteristics, procedural details, and in-hospital complications were collected.

Preoperative MELD, CP, Mayo Clinic, and VOCAL-Penn scores (Table E1) were calculated manually by inputting preoperative variables into each calculator to ensure accuracy. Observed 30-day and 90-day mortality rates were compared to predicted Mayo Clinic and VOCAL-Penn mortality. The Brier Score evaluation and the Hosmer-Lemeshow test were used to assess the accuracy and calibration of each prediction model against the observed mortality. The discriminative ability of VOCAL Penn and Mayo-Clinic 30-day and 90-day mortality prediction scores was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (C-statistic). Society of Thoracic Surgeons (STS) operative mortality scores were compared to mortality predicted by the VOCAL Penn and Mayo-Clinic scores, and the correlation between STS predicted and observed mortality was evaluated using the Spearman rank correlation coefficient.

The early outcomes of the cirrhosis cohort were stratified by MELD score and by CP class. To reduce selection bias and balance baseline characteristics between patients with cirrhosis and those without cirrhosis, we performed propensity score matching using a 1:3 nearest-neighbor matching algorithm (Figure E1). The propensity scores were estimated using a logistic regression model that included the variables listed in Table E2. Matching was performed using the MatchIt package in R. Statistical significance was defined as a 2-tailed P value <.05. All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.4.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

The Kaplan-Meier nonparametric method was used to analyze the survival of patients after cardiac surgery. Cox proportional hazard and multivariable logistic regression were performed to determine independent risk factors associated with worse survival. Patients were censored at the time of the last known follow-up. Data are presented as hazard ratio (HR) or odds ratio (OR) along with 95% confidence interval (CI).

Results

Of the 1257 patients with history of chronic liver disease undergoing cardiac surgery at our institution between July 2014 and December 2022, we identified 220 patients with a diagnosis of cirrhosis. In the cirrhosis cohort (n = 220), the median age was 64 (15th-85th percentile, 52.0-72.5), and 61% (n = 135) were male. The median MELD score was 10 (15th-85th percentile, 7.0-20.5). Early Outcomes Stratified by MELD Score and CP Class

When stratified by MELD score (<10, 10-15, >15), 92 patients (41.8%) had a score <10, 76 (34.5%) had a score of ≥10 to 15, and 52 (23.6%) had a score >15. Compared to patients with MELD scores of <10 and ≥10 to 15, those with a MELD score ≥15 had higher rates of reoperation for bleeding (11.5% vs 4.3% for <10 and 5.3% for 10-15; P = .003), renal dysfunction requiring dialysis (9.6% vs 3.3% and 7.9%, respectively; P = .3), prolonged ventilation >24 hours (38.5% vs 16.3% and 21.1%, respectively; P = .009), and 30-day mortality (5.7% vs 3.3% and 3.9%, respectively; P = .8) (Table E3).

When stratified by CP class, rates of reoperation for bleeding (class A, 3.5% [n = 5]; class B, 9.6% [n = 7]; class C, 50% [n = 2]; P < .01) and prolonged ventilation (class A, 17.5% [n = 25]; class B, 31.5% [n = 23]; class C, 75.0% [n = 3]; P < .01) were significantly higher in class B and class C patients. However, 30-day mortality (class A, 2.8% [n = 4]; class B, 6.8% [n = 5]; class C, 0% [n = 0]; P = .3) and postoperative dialysis rates (class A, 4.9% [n = 7]; class B, 8.2% [n = 6]; class C, 25.0% [n = 1]; P = .13) were not significantly different across CP classes (Table E4).

Propensity Score Matching 1:3 (Cirrhosis vs No Cirrhosis)

The median MELD score for the noncirrhotic matched cohort was 5.78 (15th-85th percentile, 1.83-11.22). Propensity score matching based on baseline characteristics, comorbidities, and surgical procedures resulted in matching of 660 patients without a history of chronic liver dysfunction to the 220 patients in the cirrhosis cohort. There was no significant difference between the cirrhosis cohort and the no cirrhosis cohort in terms of reoperation rates for bleeding (6.4% vs 5.3%; P = .6), postoperative dialysis (6.4% vs 5.8%; P = .7), or prolonged ventilation >24 hours (23.0% vs 26.0%; P = .4). The hospital LOS was longer in the no cirrhosis cohort (mean, 20.5 ± 23.8 days vs 20.8 ± 15.6 days; P < .01); however, 30-day mortality was significantly higher in the cirrhosis cohort (4.1% vs 1.5%; P = .02) (Table E5).

Validation of Current Prediction Models

Mean predicted 30-day, 90-day, and 180-day mortality rates were 9.0 ± 8.9%, 14.0 ± 11.5%, and 17.5 ± 12.7, respectively, by the VOCAL-Penn score, and mean predicted 30-, 90-, and 1-year mortality rates were 20.9 ± 17.1%, 29.6 ± 20.4%, and 43.1 ± 17.6%, respectively, by the Mayo Clinic score. The observed postoperative mortality of the cirrhosis cohort was 4.1% at 30 days and 12.6% at 90 days.

When comparing observed 30-day mortality to predicted mortality by Mayo Clinic and VOCAL-Penn scores, the Mayo Clinic score demonstrated poor accuracy and calibration, overestimating mortality by 15% and exhibiting poor calibration, as indicated by the Hosmer-Lemeshow test (P < .01). The Mayo Clinic 30-day score showed poor discriminative ability, with a C-statistic of 0.57 (95% CI, 0.41-0.73), sensitivity of 88.9%, and specificity of 38.4%. VOCAL-Penn provided a more accurate prediction but still overestimated mortality by 3.1%, and demonstrated good calibration according to the Hosmer-Lemeshow test (P = .25) (Figure 1). The VOCAL-Penn 30-day score demonstrated moderate discriminative ability, with a C-statistic of 0.64 (95% CI, 0.47-0.80), sensitivity of 77.8%, and specificity of 51.7%. At 90 days, the Mayo Clinic score overestimated mortality by 17% with poor calibration, as indicated by the Hosmer-Lemeshow test (P = .03). The Mayo Clinic 90-day score showed moderate discriminative ability, with a C-statistic of 0.64 (95% CI, 0.52-0.76), sensitivity of 73.3%, and specificity of 54.9%. VOCAL-Penn overestimated mortality by 1.4% and showed good calibration by the Hosmer-Lemeshow test (P = .37) (Figure 2). The VOCAL Penn 90-day score demonstrated acceptable discriminative ability, with a C-statistic of 0.70 (95% CI, 0.59-0.81), sensitivity of 65.2%, and specificity of 68.1%.Figure 1Distribution and calibration plots of predicted 30-day mortality by Mayo Clinic and VOCAL-Penn scores compared to observed mortality.Figure 2Distribution and calibration plots of predicted 90-day mortality by Mayo Clinic and VOCAL-Penn scores compared to observed mortality.

Comparison with STS Mortality Scoring

Preoperative STS-predicted scores for the patients with cirrhosis were further analyzed to assess their accuracy compared to actual mortality and mortality calculated for their matched, noncirrhotic cohort. There was a weak, nonsignificant correlation between STS predicted operative mortality and observed 30-day mortality (Spearman r = 0.13; P = .25). Compared to their noncirrhotic counterparts, cirrhotic patients had significantly higher predicted risks for longer LOS (P < .01) and renal failure (P = .01). Predicted risks for 30-day mortality, morbidity and mortality, permanent stroke, prolonged ventilation, and reoperation were similar between the cirrhosis and no cirrhosis cohorts (Table E6).

In the cirrhosis cohort, predicted mortality at 30 days by the VOCAL-Penn and Mayo Clinic scores were compared to STS-predicted operative mortality, when applicable. The VOCAL-Penn cohort demonstrated consistently higher predicted mortality compared to STS scores across all procedures, with median values ranging from 3.75% for AVR + CABG to 12.05% for mitral valve replacement (MVR) + CABG. The Mayo Clinic cohort showed the highest predicted mortality risks, ranging from 9.8% for isolated MVR to 21.95% for MVR + CABG. These differences were statistically significant for isolated CABG, AVR, MVR, and AVR + CABG (all P < .001 or P = .015) (Table E7).

Survival After Cardiac Surgery in Patients With Liver Cirrhosis and Risk Factors for Long Term Mortality

Kaplan Meier analysis demonstrated an early-phase survival decline among patients with cirrhosis (HR, 2; 95% CI, 1.6-2.6; P < .01). The cirrhotic cohort had a 1-year survival was 80% and a 3-year survival, 64.5% compared the 86.5% 1-year survival and 79.5% 3-year survival in the matched cohort (Figure 3). Multivariable analysis was performed to establish predictors of long-term mortality using perioperative variables (Table E8). Predictors of lower long-term mortality included higher serum albumin and sodium levels (HR, 0.69 [95% CI, 0.49-0.96; P = .03] and 0.93 [95%, CI, 0.88-0.99; P = .02], respectively). Discussion

Mortality Prediction Models for Patients With Cirrhosis

Traditional risk calculators, such as the STS score and EuroSCORE (European System for Cardiac Operative Risk Evaluation), are used to assess the risk of operative mortality following cardiac surgery, but they do not account for liver cirrhosis as a variable.9, 10, 11 Until recently, there were no mortality risk predictors specific to cirrhotic patients undergoing cardiac surgery. In lieu of this, other medical tools, such as the CP and MELD scores, have been used outside of their scope to aid determination of surgical candidacy for this high-risk population.10,12 Data support that cardiac surgery can be performed safely in patients with CP class A cirrhosis and in selected patients with CP class B cirrhosis, and in those with a calculated score <8.2 Similarly, numerous studies have found that a higher MELD score is predictive of operative mortality; however, specific cutoff points vary across studies.10,13 The Mayo Clinic and VOCAL-Penn scores were designed as risk calculators to estimate predicted postoperative mortality following surgical procedures, including cardiac surgery.6,7 Although these calculators have been studied among the general surgery population, data are limited on the predictive value of these risk assessment tools for cardiac surgery patients.6

Our data show that both calculators overestimated mortality among patients with cirrhosis undergoing cardiac surgery; however, the VOCAL-Penn score demonstrated better calibration at both 30 days and 90 days. This might be due in part to the more comprehensive nature of the VOCAL-Penn score. Other studies have demonstrated similar results when considering all abdominal, vascular, orthopedic, and cardiac procedures, showing a consistent overestimation of the Mayo Clinic score.7 The implications of this overestimated surgical risk cannot be overstated. With limited tools available to risk-stratify cirrhotic patients before cardiac surgery, surgical candidacy should not rely solely on the current mortality prediction models, which can result in inappropriately turning down patients for life-saving procedures.

In our cohort, almost one-half of the patients underwent urgent or emergency operations, 36% of which were reoperations. Furthermore, numerous patients underwent multicomponent procedures, with a median CPB time of 112 minutes. Despite the complexity and acuity of these cases, outcomes were acceptable. Postoperative outcomes were comparable between patients with cirrhosis and their matched cohort without cirrhosis. Observed 30-day mortality was 4.1%, which was lower than predicted by both risk calculators. The American Association for Thoracic Surgery (AATS) recently developed the AATS Quality Gateway (AQG) score, a novel tool that integrates key clinical variables to more accurately predict perioperative mortality and provide center-specific risk estimates.14

Outcomes Stratified by MELD Score and CP Class

To further investigate the correlation between the MELD and CP scores and postoperative outcomes, the cohort was divided into groups based on the MELD score to <10, 10 to 15, and >15 and CP classes and then compared. Patients with CP class B or C had significantly higher rates of reoperation for bleeding and prolonged ventilation compared to CP class A patients; however, this did not result in a significant 30-day mortality difference. This could be due in part to the delayed chest closure approach that we use for patients with ongoing coagulopathy. This approach could decrease the complications associated with increased pressor requirements and right-heart compression, such as renal dysfunction or bowel malperfusion. Studies have demonstrated variable results after cardiac surgery for patients with CP B, and these patients may be considered inoperable in many centers.2,5,12 Patients with CP class C are almost always deemed inoperable, with the literature reporting unacceptably high morbidity and mortality.2,12 While coagulopathy, bleeding, and prolonged ventilation may be expected in patients with cirrhosis, our study suggests that acceptable outcomes can still be achieved for CP class B patients. Only 4 patients in this study were classified as CP class C, which serves as a testament to their limited operative candidacy. Interestingly, patients with a MELD score >15 have a significantly different postoperative course compared to those with scores <10 and 10 to 15. The literature is variable in cutoff points for increased postoperative mortality by MELD score, and it seems to be a less predictable correlative tool for estimating postoperative outcomes.13,15

Predictors of Mortality

Higher preoperative albumin and sodium levels are associated with lower long-term mortality. Albumin serves as a marker of hepatic synthetic function and overall nutritional status, and higher levels are consistently linked to improved survival in cirrhotic populations.16,17 Similarly, higher serum sodium level reflects less severe portal hypertension and a lower risk of such complications as ascites and hepatorenal syndrome, and it is an independent predictor of better outcomes.16 These findings are supported by large cohort studies and are reflected in risk models such as the Child-Pugh and MELD-Na, which use albumin and sodium to stratify surgical risk.16,17

Limitations

Although this study provides insight into the accuracy of current mortality risk prediction models for patients with cirrhosis, it has some inherent limitations. As a single-center, retrospective analysis, it is limited by its observational nature, which is subject to internal bias. Furthermore, patients who were deemed too high risk for surgical intervention and ultimately did not undergo surgery are not captured in this study. Further studies using a national database are needed to capture a wider scope of patients and surgical practices, which may be more reflective of national outcomes. Despite these limitations, we were able to analyze the accuracy of current mortality risk prediction models for patients with cirrhosis undergoing cardiac surgery and provide useful insight into the surgical evaluation of these patients.

Conclusions

Risk stratification of patients with cirrhosis undergoing cardiac surgery is difficult, as this is a heterogeneous group with numerous comorbidities and varying degrees of underlying hepatic dysfunction. Current risk prediction models, including the Mayo Clinic and VOCAL-Penn scores, overestimate mortality in this population and should not form the basis of determining surgical candidacy. Multidisciplinary discussion is vital to evaluate the whole patient and weigh the risks and benefits of each operation. Ultimately, updated mortality risk prediction tools are necessary to appropriately risk-stratify this complex patient population, perhaps also incorporating planned intervention and other comorbidities. Additionally, future investigations should include high-risk patients denied surgery, who were not included in this study, to better validate the predictive risk models.
