IBS stress reactivity phenotype is associated with blood transcriptome profiles and microstructural and functional brain changes
Authors: Jennifer S Labus, Desiree R Delgadillo, Steve Cole, Chencai Wang, Swapna Joshi, Arpana Church, Bruce Naliboff, Lin Chang, Jonathan P Jacobs, Benjamin M Ellingson, Emeran A Mayer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEvidence suggests significant interindividual differences in stress reactivity (SR), but mechanisms and therapeutic implications of these differences are poorly understood. The aim of this study was to identify the biological basis of increased SR by investigating associations between a psychometric-based phenotype with blood transcriptomics profiles of unprovoked tonic increased sympathetic nervous system (SNS) activation and neuroimaging phenotypes in irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) participants and healthy controls (HCs). A cross-sectional study design, transcriptomics profiling, multimodal neuroimaging, and psychosocial assessments were obtained in 291 IBS participants and HCs. Unsupervised clustering was applied to derive High and Low SR subgroups across all participants based on two measures of SR. General linear models tested for SR group differences in clinical and biological parameters. Exploratory analyses examined associations between SR group-specific brain alterations and gene expression. The High, compared to Low SR group showed greater cyclic AMP response element-binding protein (CREB) gene expression consistent with tonic SNS activity and proinflammatory changes in whole blood. Neuroplastic brain changes were observed in the High SR group consistent with an upregulation of ascending arousal systems, sensory processing and integration regions, and functional connectivity changes in the central autonomic network. In IBS, SR moderated sex differences in extraintestinal symptoms. The findings support a model of tonically increased, unprovoked SNS activity as a plausible risk factor for increased reactivity to psychosocial stressors and low grade immune activation in both IBS and HCs, with a greater likelihood in IBS. These findings may have important implications for personalized treatment interventions in IBS.Keywords: Stress reactivity, CREB transcription, Proinflammatory genes, Diffusion tensor imaging, Functional brain connectivity, Irritable bowel syndrome

Introduction

Irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) is a stress-sensitive disorder of brain gut interactions, currently defined by symptom criteria of chronically recurring abdominal pain associated with altered bowel habits (Mayer et al., 2023). A wealth of information has demonstrated evidence to support important roles of visceral hypersensitivity and of altered autonomic nervous system modulation of gut functions in the pathophysiology of IBS, even though the role and underlying mechanisms of altered stress reactivity (SR) has not been studied as a risk factor and disease modifier.

Chronic stress is associated with increased odds of having IBS and the persistence of symptoms (Zia et al., 2022), and similar associations have been found for other chronic illnesses. Seventy percent of patients with IBS have a history of early life and adulthood stressful life events (Park et al., 2016; Parker et al., 2019; Bradford et al., 2012; Lee et al., 2024) and reduced resilience (Park et al., 2018) (adapting positively to stress) compared to healthy controls (HCs). Furthermore, psychosocial stress is one of the major factors underlying symptom flares, and is associated with more severe symptoms and worse health-related quality of life (Böhn et al., 2013; Halpert et al., 2007; Sayuk et al., 2017). The acute stress response system is composed of the sympathetic nervous system (SNS) and the hypothalamic pituitary adrenal (HPA) axis, which are coordinated by the central autonomic network, including several brainstem nuclei. The physiological reaction to stressful life events and the subjective perception of stress shows significant inter-individual variations (Kiecolt-Glaser et al., 2020; Turner et al., 2020) which are likely to influence the heterogeneity in clinical presentation of functional and inflammatory GI disorders (Sauk et al., 2023) which may have significant implications for individualized treatment and strategies. Individual differences in SR results from both a lowered threshold for threat perception and heightened physiological and affective responses. Distinct from acute, adaptive stress responses, SR reflects tonic, trait-like sympathetic nervous system (SNS) activity, characterized by immune system gene expression, persistent emotional liability and heightened, ongoing, self-perceived stress.

Based on this model we have recently described a protocol using the combination of two well validated questionnaires (International Personality Item Pool (Goldberg et al., 2006; IPIP), Perceived Stress Scale (Cohen et al., 1983); PSS), to better quantify SR in healthy and clinical populations. In partial validation of this approach, we have shown that greater SR in patients with Ulcerative Colitis was associated with molecular markers of tonic SNS activity, increased likelihood of clinical flares, and alterations in intestinal microbiota and metabolites (Sauk et al., 2023). Given the important role of psychosocial stress in symptoms and progression of IBS, we hypothesized that SR may be a critical component of brain gut interactions at the foundation of IBS pathophysiology, and its comorbidity with other chronic pain disorders. By studying associations between a psychometric-based phenotype of increased SR with several peripheral and central biomarkers we aimed to investigate the biological mechanisms underlying increased SR, the effects of increased SR on reports of comorbidity (i.e., extra-intestinal assessments), and how SR may moderate reported sex differences in IBS.

The overall goal of this study was to identify the biological basis of tonically increased SR in a large population of IBS and HCs. We aimed to test the main hypothesis that a subgroup of individuals with IBS as well HCs, with High SR identified based on clinical characteristics, show evidence for a biological phenotype of increased transcription of SNS and inflammation-related genes, as well as functional and microstructural brain changes that may underlie development and persistence of stress related disorders. We also examine the clinical correlates of this SR phenotype within IBS and HC in order to test whether even in HCs, heightened SR may be a vulnerability factor for chronic pain.

We demonstrate that the High SR phenotype is characterized by a history of early life adversity, lower resilience, higher psychosocial comorbidity and greater intestinal and extraintestinal symptom severity, and that some of the observed brain changes are associated with SNS-related gene expression in bone marrow-derived immune cells. Furthermore, even though there are no sex-related differences in the prevalence of the High SR phenotype, SR moderates the sex differences in comorbidity in IBS.

Methods

Subject population

Male and female HCs and IBS participants were prospectively enrolled in a cross-sectional study involving observational MRI brain imaging and evaluation of IBS symptoms based on ROME III (Longstreth et al., 2006) and IV (Lacy et al., 2016) criteria from 2013 to 2019. A gastroenterology (GI) clinician obtained histories and physical examinations and confirmed the diagnosis of IBS. All procedures complied with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and were approved by the Institutional Review Board. Exclusionary criteria are described in the Supplemental Methods.

Assessment of subjective parameters

We performed extensive psychosocial and extraintestinal phenotyping across IBS and HC. IBS participants also completed IBS-specific symptom assessments. Details on these assessments are found in the Supplemental Methods.

Assessment of stress reactivity

In several studies we have validated a two-pronged approach to identification of degree of SR using self-report questionnaires that assess both the affective lability and stress perception aspects of SR. Trait emotional lability vs stability is measured by the Neuroticism scale of the well validated International Personality Item Pool- (IPIP-N) (Leger et al., 2021). The scale label ‘neuroticism’ is somewhat controversial, however, there is an extensive literature validating this trait of emotional lability as a predictor of psychological problems, heightened physiological responses to stress, increased threat perception and increased somatic symptoms (Lahey, 2009; Leger et al., 2021; Rosmalen et al., 2007). Self-perceived stress level is measured by the widely used Perceived Stress Scale which has previously been shown to reliably scale stress perception, predict vulnerability to infection, and predict medical and psychological outcomes (Cohen et al., 1983).

Psychosocial phenotyping

Current anxiety symptoms were assessed with the Hospital Anxiety and Depression scale (Zigmond and Snaith, 1983). Trait anxiety was measure used the State Trait Anxiety Inventory (Spielberger et al., 1983). GI-specific anxiety was assessed using the Visceral Sensitivity Index (Labus et al., 2004, 2007). Pain Catastrophizing was evaluated with the catastrophizing scale of the Coping Strategies Questionnaire (Swartzman et al., 1994).

Early adverse life experiences were assessed using the Adverse Childhood Events questionnaire (ACE) and the Early Traumatic Inventory–Self Report (ETI-SR). The ACE captures emotional, physical, and sexual abuse; parents who were divorced or separated, treated violently; and household member with substance abuse, with mental illness, and who was incarcerated (Felitti et al., 1998). The ETI-SR was used to assess histories of childhood traumatic and adverse life events that occurred before the age of 18 years old and covers four domains: general trauma, physical, emotional, and sexual abuse (Bremner et al., 2007). Different aspects of perceived resilience were measured using The Connor-Davidson Resilience Scale (CDRISC) and Brief Resilience Scale (BRS) which differ in complexity (Connor and Davidson, 2003; Kilpatrick et al., 2024; Smith et al., 2008). The CDRISC is focused on internal and external resources that aid in recovery from stress and has 5 domains: persistence, emotional-cognitive, adaptability, control-meaning, and meaning. The total CDRISC score ranges 0-100, with higher scores indicating higher resilience. The BRS assess the ability to “bounce back” from stress and adversity. Higher scores on the BRS reflect higher resilience in terms of bounce-back ability.

IBS symptom measures

IBS symptom severity was measured using the IBS Severity Scoring System (IBS-SSS) which assesses abdominal pain intensity and frequency, distention, dissatisfaction with bowel habits, and impact of IBS on quality of life over the past 10 days (Francis et al., 1997). Disease-specific quality of life (QOL) in the past month was evaluated using the total score from the IBS-QOL which assesses the interference of IBS symptoms on various aspects of daily activities (Patrick et al., 1998).

Extraintestinal symptom assessment

Extraintestinal symptom prevalence was assessed using several assessment tools. Somatic symptom severity was measured with the Patient Health Questionnaire without GI items (PHQ-12) (Kroenke et al., 2002). The Pennebaker Inventory of Limbic Languidness (PILL) used to assess enhanced interoception and the tendency to notice and report a broad array of physical symptoms and sensations (Pennebaker, 1982). The Complex Medical Symptom Inventory (CMSI) was used to delineate the presence of common somatic symptoms of discomfort or pain as well as sensory sensitivity to nonpainful environmental stimuli over the lifetime (CMSI_Lftm) and for 3 months out of the past year (CMSI_12m) (Williams and Schilling, 2009). Scores on the somatic awareness (CMSI_SA) and sensory sensitivity (CMSI_SS) subscales were computed (Schrepf et al., 2018).

Assessment of biological parameters

Details on Blood RNA profiling and MRI acquisition protocols, preprocessing and computational statistics can be found in Supplemental Methods.

Unsupervised learning to subtype participants by stress reactivity

As in our previous studies (Sauk et al., 2023; Jacobs et al., 2024), SR groups were defined by the PSS (perceived ongoing stress) and IPIP-N (emotional lability) using unsupervised learning across HC and IBS participants. This subtyping was performed in the two study cohorts providing data for the blood transcriptome analyses and brain imaging. Specifically, partitioning around medoids using Manhattan's distances was used to derive SR subgroups based on these two baseline measures of SR. The optimal number of clusters was selected using validity indices calculated using the R library NBclust.

RNA profile analysis

Transcript abundance values were normalized to 11 standard reference genes (Eisenberg and Levanon, 2013) and log2-transformed for linear model analyses quantifying differences in gene expression as a function of High-vs. Low-SR group while controlling for age, sex, race/ethnicity, education, income, and mRNA transcripts indicating relative prevalence of major leukocyte subsets (CD3D, CD4, CD8A, CD19, NCAM1/CD56, FCGR3A/CD16, CD14). As in previous research (Cole et al., 2020), primary mixed effect linear model analyses examined 16 pro-inflammatory gene transcripts (identified from a set of 19 pre-specified indicators used in previous research, after removal of 5 showing minimal expression variance in this sample; SD < 0.5 log2 units) with genes treated as repeated measures. In these analyses, each substantive hypothesis is tested by a single p-value assessing the relationship between a predictor (e.g., SR group) and average expression of the gene composite (e.g., 16 pre-specified inflammatory genes). Secondary analyses are described in the Supplemental Methods. For an exploratory association analysis, we also computed pro-inflammatory and anti-viral gene expression composite scores based on the 19 inflammatory genes and 32 antiviral genes involved in Type I interferon responses and antibody production as well as conserved transcriptional response to adversity (CTRA) composite (difference between the proinflammatory and antiviral scores) (Mann et al., 2020) Higher scores on the CTRA and pro-inflammatory and lower scores on the antiviral are thought be the consequence of greater SNS modulation of gene expression (Cole, 2019). To further examine potential changes in the activity of stress-responsive gene regulation pathways, we also conducted additional bioinformatic analyses of the CREB signaling pathway that mediates beta-adrenergic signaling from the SNS and the glucocorticoid receptor (GR) pathway activated by the HPA axis. These secondary analyses are described in Supplemental Methods (Secondary RNA Profiling).

DTI analyses

Using AFNI (Analysis of Functional NeuroImages) (Cox, 1996), voxel-wise differences in DTI measurements were evaluated in the brain and brainstem using a general linear model. Differences between the High and Low SR groups in the fractional Anisotropy (FA) and Median Diffusivity (MD), age, sex, BMI and diagnosis were included as covariates. FA and MD are standard measures of fiber density/connectivity for involved white matter regions, and measures of higher cellularity/cell packing for gray matter areas. For whole brain analysis, of cortical and subcortical white matter a cluster threshold was applied from permutation tests by estimating data's smoothness through command 3dFWHMx in AFNI, and then estimating the cluster extent thresholds at a level of significance, p < 0.05, through command 3dClustSim in AFNI (Labus et al., 2023). Brainstem regions of interest (ROIs) were based on the Harvard Ascending Arousal Network (AAN) Atlas: locus coeruleus (LC), dorsal (DRN) and median raphe (MRN) nuclei, parabrachial complex (PBC), ventral tegmental area (VTA), midbrain reticular formation (MRF)and the periaqueductal gray (PAG) (Edlow et al., 2012). For brain ROI interest analysis, a binary image with 1 assigned to pixels containing the region of interest and 0 assigned to regions outside prior to analysis was applied to mask out the regions of non-interest for analysis.

Resting state analysis

Using R, a priori contrast analysis was applied using the general linear model to test for differences in the resting state connectivity strength, reflecting global influence, of the ROIs in the central autonomic network (CAN) and the brainstem nuclei (See Supplemental Methods). We used p < 0.05 as a reporting threshold and also correct for the number of regions tested using a false discovery rate (FDR). Additionally, we provide a measure of the effect size difference, Cohen's d where values are interpreted as low (d = 0.20), moderate (d = 0.50), and high (d = 0.80).

Brain imaging association analyses

The link between DTI brainstem nuclei and RS ROIs showing differences between the SR groups and gene expression composite scores and the expression of the individual genes in the set were investigated using general linear models controlling for diagnosis, sex, age, BMI, race (white yes/no), and education. To control for multiple testing, FDR correction was applied for the number of regions tested and report standardized beta (Std B) values which are a measure of effect size and are interpreted as 0.10–0.29 small, 0.30–0.49 medium, and 0.50 large (Nieminen, 2022).

Analysis of psychosocial and symptom variables

Independent sample t-tests were used to characterize differences between the SR subgroups (Low-High SR) across and within diagnosis (HC, IBS) on demographic and psychosocial variables. Within IBS participants, independent t-test were also applied to determine SR differences in IBS symptoms and comorbidity/extraintestinal measures. Differences in frequencies were assessed via two-tail Fisher Exact Tests. Effect size differences were estimated using Cohen's d and 95% Confidence intervals. Moderator analyses were applied to investigate whether sex differences in the comorbidity assessments in IBS were moderated by SR using the general linear model specifying the main effects for SR group and sex along with their interactions. A significant main effect for the SR∗sex interaction term suggests moderation. To elucidate the significant moderation effects, linear contrast analyses were performed within the High and Low SR groups comparing males and females. We also use graphical methods to display the estimated marginal means of the moderated outcomes for the males and females in the High and Low SR groups.

SR subgrouping

Unsupervised learning with cluster analyses was applied to subtype HCs and IBS participants based on their response on two validated SR clinical assessments, PSS (perceived ongoing stress) and IPIP-N (emotional lability). This subtyping was performed in two study cohorts; those providing samples for blood transcriptomics analyses (N = 120, Study Cohort I) and those with brain imaging (N = 253, study Cohort II). For the 120 participants providing blood samples (Cohort I), 64 were subtyped as Low SR (45 females [55%], 38 IBS [59%]) and 56 as High SR (38 females [68%], 44 IBS [76%] (see Supplemental Fig. 1). In this cohort, there was no statistically significant difference between the proportion of males and females comprising the SR Groups (Fisher's exact test, OR = 0.89,p = 0.84). IBS participants were twice as likely to be in the High SR group compared to the HC group (OR = 2.5, p = 0.03).

Next, clustering analyses was applied to subtype the 253 HC (N = 101; 62 females [61.4%]) and IBS (N = 152; 107 females [70.4%]) participants with brain imaging data (Cohort II). Again, the algorithm identified two clusters subtyping participants into High and Low SR groups. As with the study cohort I, the derived clusters did not show distinct separation (see Fig. 2A). This was largely due to overlap between the two groups on the IPIP-N, individuals having similar levels of the trait emotional lability but reporting different levels of perceived ongoing stress (see Fig. 2B). To create a cleaner more distinct phenotypic grouping, we further refined cluster membership by removing subjects with overlapping IPIP scores, Low Stress (IPIP ≤ 20) and High Stress (IPIP>29). This final sample was comprised by 168 participants (70 HC, 98 IBS) belonging to High SR (N = 68, 49 females [72%], 55 IBS [56%], 13 HC [18.6%]) and Low SR (N = 100, 65 females [65%], 43 IBS [43%]) groups. Of these 168 participants, a total of 50 participants had both blood transcriptome data and imaging data including 34 Low SR (23 females, 17 IBS) and 16 High SR (12 females, 14 IBS). As observed previously, there were no differences between the proportion of males and females comprising the SR Groups (Fisher's exact test, OR = 1.39,p = 0.40), while IBS participants compared to HCs were more likely to be in the High SR group (OR = 5.54.p = 2.2.e−16). There were no significant differences in age or proportion of males and females comprising the IBS and HC groups. SR differences on demographic and psychosocial assessments

Differences between the High and Low SR groups across diagnosis

As shown in Table 1, the High SR group (combined IBS and HCs participants) had significantly higher state anxiety (Cohen's d = −2.31, p = 7.59e−32), trait anxiety (d = −1.63, p = 1.12 e−19), and VSI scores (d = −0.84, p = 2.98e−7). The High SR group also reported significantly greater overall ETI-SR scores (d = −0.067, p = 3.91 e−5) and also reported more early adverse life experiences as measured by the ACE total score (d = −0.61, p = 1.43e−4. The High SR showed less resilience as measured by the BRS (d = 1.54, p = 4.98e−18) and the CD-RISC subscale scores (see Table 1). No differences were observed between the proportion of men and women in the High and Low SR groups (Fisher exact test, p = 0.17).

SR differences in IBS symptoms

Examining the IBS symptom-related measures, IBS participants in the High SR group, compared to the Low SR group, showed significantly higher IBS symptom severity as assessed by increased IBS-SSS scores (Cohen's d = −0.54, p = 0.01) and reduced IBS-QOL (Cohen's d = 0.44, p = 0.04).

Influence of SR on comorbidity measures in IBS

Within the IBS Cohort, High SR compared to Low SR had significantly higher levels of comorbidity as measured by extraintestinal symptom measures including the interoception and the tendency to notice and report a broad array of physical symptoms and sensations (PILL, d = −0.90, p = 2.7E-05), somatic symptom severity (PHQ-12 score, d = −0.55, p = 7.9E-03), somatic awareness (CMSI_SA, d = −0.65, 2.3E-030), and the presence of common somatic symptoms and sensory sensitivity to nonpainful environmental stimuli over the past year (CMSI_12mo score = -0.64, p = 2.9E-03). Blood transcriptome profile differences between High and Low SR groups across diagnosis (IBS and HC)

Of the 120 HC and IBS participants providing blood samples, 7 samples were excluded as they contained insufficient RNA for assay or lacked the needed covariate data to yield the covariate-adjusted results. In the final combined sample of 113 HC and IBS individuals (59 Low SR (23 HC, 40 Female), (54 High SR (12 HC, 36 Female), bioinformatic analyses of all 567 genes showing >1.5-fold differential expression in High-vs. Low-SR blood samples (230 up-regulated; 337 down-regulated) linked High SR to increased activity of CREB family of transcription factors involved in mediating beta-adrenergic signaling from the SNS (+0.17 ± SE 0.08 log2 TFBM ratio in promoters of genes up-regulated in High-vs. Low-SR groups, p = 0.036) but no difference in the GR transcription factor involved in cortisol signaling from the HPA axis (−0.18 ± 0.25, p = 0.481). Results also linked High-SR to increased activity of the pro-inflammatory AP-1 family of transcription factors (0.27 ± 0.10, p = 0.006) but no difference in activity of the pro-inflammatory NF-κB family (−0.08 ± 0.15, p = 0.605). Among n = 46 individual with additional brain imaging data available, the High-SR group also showed significantly elevated average expression of 16 pro-inflammatory gene transcripts (e.g., IL1B, IL8/CXCL8, COX2/PTGS2, FOSL2, TNF; covariate-adjusted difference = 0.25 log2 mRNA abundance ± 0.11, t (28) = 2.31, p = 0.029).

Whole brain microstructural differences between the High and Low SR groups across diagnosis

Compared to the Low SR group, the combined High SR group (containing both IBS and HC) showed lower MD values in basal ganglia and thalamus, as well as along white matter fiber tracts connecting frontal, temporal and primary sensorimotor cortices. The internal capsule, bilateral cerebral peduncles, the left posterior corona radiata, the left posterior thalamic radiation, the left superior longitudinal fasciculus, and thalamus showed higher FA and lower MD in the High SR group (Fig. 4 and Fig. 5, Supplemental Tables 10, 11, and 12). These findings are consistent with higher fiber or cellular density, increased axon or cell size, and increased preferred directionality of the underlying neuronal structures in the High SR group.

Discussion

Two SR subgroups of individuals were identified in a large sample of IBS and HCs. Amongst IBS participants, the SR groups differed in clinical presentation including a history of early life adversity and greater psychosocial comorbidity, microstructural and functional connectivity alterations in the brain and gene expression. The main findings identified a group with higher perception and reactivity to stress (High SR as indexed by scores on IPIP and PSS questionnaires), greater gene expression for unprovoked, tonic SNS activity (as indexed by increased transcription of CREB), and proinflammatory changes in bone marrow derived monocytes. Even though the High SR phenotype was present in both IBS and HCs, it was significantly more common in IBS. In IBS participants, SR moderated the sex difference in extraintestinal symptoms. Brain imaging findings showed neuroplastic structural changes consistent with an upregulation of several brain networks involved in ascending arousal, sensory processing and emotion regulation as well as resting-state functional connectivity changes in the central autonomic network. Further, SR-associated functional alterations in the insula and the PBC were associated with a proinflammatory and CTRAs composite scores, indexing SNS modulation of inflammatory gene expression.

Phenotype of High and Low SR

The High SR phenotype in both IBS and HC was characterized by a history of early life adversity and psychosocial comorbidity, and in IBS with greater symptom severity. We have recently shown that a subgroup of patients with ulcerative colitis who show a High SR phenotype as indexed in the current study, have significantly greater frequency of clinical flares over a 3 year period (Sauk et al., 2023). When viewed together with our previous results, one may speculate that a High SR phenotype exists not only in a significant number of patients with disorders of gut brain interaction and with inflammatory bowel disease, where it correlates with disease severity and recurrence of symptoms, but is also present in a smaller number of healthy asymptomatic individuals in which this phenotype may be a risk factor for greater vulnerability to develop IBS and other stress sensitive disorders, which are more common in women. Even though not explored in this study, one may hypothesize that the High SR phenotype in individuals without IBS may be a risk factor for the development of post-infectious (PI) IBS following a bacterial or viral gastrointestinal infection. This speculation is supported by the known psychological risk factors for PI IBS which include early adversity, premorbid pain, anxiety disorders, depression, neuroticism, somatization and perceived stress (Thabane and Marshall, 2009; Sykes et al., 2003). While we did not find significant sex-related difference in the prevalence of High SR, having this phenotype moderated the frequency of extraintestinal symptoms in HCs and IBS. Also, non-IBS comorbidities were greater in female IBS patients compared to men with High SR, but not Low SR.

Genomic correlates of High SR

An association between psychological stress and expression of genes bearing response elements for transcription factors that regulate inflammatory and anti-viral processes has previously been reported (Chiang et al., 2019; Cho et al., 2019). In late adolescence, higher levels of daily interpersonal stress and shorter sleep duration were associated with upregulation of genes bearing response elements for pro-inflammatory transcription factor, nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) (Chiang et al., 2019). Furthermore, studies evaluating transcriptome profiles to predict depressive symptoms following an inflammatory challenge with low dose endotoxin suggest that increased baseline activity of transcription factors related to NF-κB and beta-adrenergic signaling (CREB) and decreased activity of GR-related transcription factors were associated with post-endotoxin depressed mood (Cho et al., 2019). Using promoter-based bioinformatics analysis, we have previously identified increased activity of CREB transcription factors (Rhee et al., 2009) in IBS patients, a population without macroscopic evidence for intestinal inflammation (Drossman et al., 2002). The expression of proinflammatory and stress-related genes in individuals without clinical evidence for gut or systemic inflammation suggests the presence of a “genomic memory” encoded by early adverse life events that increases the individual reactivity to psychosocial stress and more severe IBS symptoms.

Brain signatures correlates of High SR

The High vs Low SR group showed increased density and directionality of microstructure of brain regions involved in sensory processing and integration (basal ganglia and thalamus), and brain stem nuclei involved in ascending arousal systems (midbrain reticular formation, serotonergic dorsal raphe and the VTA.

The findings of a decreased MD and increased FA similarly describe an increase in neuronal density and/or structural connectivity into these regions. As the midbrain reticular formation plays a key role in determining which sensory signals reach conscious attention, one may speculate that our findings of higher density/connectivity in this region are a reflection of more pain or sensory information reaching conscious attention in these patients. The clinical implications of the observed microstructural alterations in the VTA are less clear, even though stress-induced increases in VTA excitability have been reported (Spyrka et al., 2020; Krishnan et al., 2008).

In addition to the microstructural signatures differentiating the two SR groups, the High SR group showed reductions in the global resting state functional connectivity of central autonomic brain regions involved in several brain networks, including the emotional arousal, salience, and default mode as well as the DRN in the brainstem. Several of these reductions, including insula subregions and parabrachial complex were significantly associated with the inflammatory and CTRA composite scores.

The microstructural and functional connectivity changes associated with High SR were found in brain regions that might underly clinical hallmarks of increased SR, e.g. changes in sensory sensitivity and autonomic nervous system responsiveness, including the serotonergic raphe nucleus and basal ganglia. This is consistent with a significant remodeling of the brain in the High SR phenotype. We have previously shown gray matter microstructural alterations in IBS affecting the basal ganglia consistent with impairment of the corticothalamic-BG-cortical loops involved in integration of sensory information and cortico thalamic modulation (Ellingson et al., 2013).

Limitations

There are several limitations to our study. The sample size was insufficient to determine differences in SR according to predominant bowel habit and other clinical parameters. However, our overall hypothesis posited that the SR phenotype cuts across HCs and subgroups of IBS. Future studies in larger cohorts will address the question of clinical subtypes. Increased tonic activity of the SNS was assessed by CREB gene transcription in peripheral blood monocytic cells, while other measures such as heart rate or blood pressure were not assessed. The reliance on CREB gene transcription as a measure of SNS tone was based on a series of previous reports validating this approach. Due to the cross-sectional nature of this study, we cannot make any definite conclusions about the causality of factors in the observed association of self-reported stress, brain changes and blood transcription profiles.

Integrative model of increased SR

When viewed together with an extensive preclinical and clinical literature (Mayer, 2000; Nyström, 2016), we propose the following model: The High SR phenotype first evolves in the developing brain as a result of interactions between early adversity and genetic vulnerabilities (Cole, 2019), resulting in neuroplastic changes in the central autonomic network and an increased tonic activity of the SNS, as well as an increased engagement of both the SNS system and the ascending arousal system to environmental stressors (Irwin and Cole, 2011). The increased SNS activity affects gene expression patterns in bone marrow peripheral blood mononuclear cells, which function like a “genomic memory” of adversity (Biltz et al., 2022). Such a genomic memory may also exist for the activation of the immune system in PI IBS suggested by the increased expression of proinflammatory cytokines (Cole, 2013). During acute stressors, even if mild, primed PBMCs can enter the systemic circulation and reach the brain (Wohleb et al., 2013), where they induce neuroplastic changes in brainstem nuclei and several brain networks involved in the sensory processing and integration regions, contributing to the visceral hypersensitivity observed in a significant subset of patients (Wohleb et al., 2013, 2014). Evidence for an association of peripheral immune cell priming has been identified in IBS patients without clinical mucosal inflammation putting them at increased risk to develop PI-IBS following severe stress or gut infections (Liebregts et al., 2007). Alternatively, the neuroplastic changes in the sensory and emotion regulation networks of the brain could result from the interactions of vulnerability genes and early life adversities even without the stress-induced inflammatory feedback from the bone marrow.

IBS is recognized as one of a set of chronic overlapping pain conditions (COPCs) that also include fibromyalgia, migraine, among others (Maixner et al., 2016). It is hypothesized that these conditions have common elements to their pathophysiology especially in regards to altered pain modulation, and affective and immune responses. It is therefore likely that many of the findings reported here may be applicable to these other conditions and warrant further study.

Theory and calculation

We demonstrate that a High SR clinical and biological phenotype in both IBS patients and HCs can be identified simply by using specific cutoffs on two well established questionnaires, the PSS and the IPIP-N. This phenotyping may have significant clinical implications. In patients presenting with new, unexplained symptoms of abdominal pain and altered bowel habits following a stressful life period or traumatic event, or following an enteric infection, clinical SR phenotyping by IPIP and PS scales may help to identify individuals at higher risk for developing IBS. In IBS, the presence of a high clinical SR phenotype may help to design personalized treatment approaches. Even though currently not recommended as an IBS therapy, those with High SR may show greater benefit from beta blockers (MacCormack et al., 2021). Beta-adrenergic blockade has been shown to blunt inflammatory and antiviral/antibody gene expression responses to acute psychosocial stress (MacCormack et al., 2021). Even though therapeutic outcomes from such treatments in unselected IBS patients have been mixed, beta blockers might be useful as adjuvant therapies in High SR patients where stress and autonomic dysfunction are prominent features contributing to symptoms. The High SR subgroup may show greater benefits from brain-targeted therapies, including gut behavior therapies, such as mindfulness-based stress reduction, gut directed cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) or hypnotherapy aimed to mitigate enhanced SR even though subgroup analyses based on SR should be performed in all treatment trials. Behavioral treatments have been shown to reliably reduce perceptions of stress in both HCs (Jafar et al., 2016; Santos-Ruiz et al., 2017) and IBS groups with IBS patients showing greater benefits such as durable decreases in GI and extraintestinal symptoms (Devenney et al., 2024) and enhanced QOL (Kinsinger, 2017). But it remains to be determined if such treatments also attenuate the increased SNS tone. Importantly, early interventions, including parental training has been shown to prevent/reduce stress-related psychopathology in at-risk children (Ajilchi and Kargar, 2013; Lam et al., 2008) and could feasibly delay or prevent neuroplastic brain changes and the development of the High SR phenotype or IBS, although further studies are needed.
