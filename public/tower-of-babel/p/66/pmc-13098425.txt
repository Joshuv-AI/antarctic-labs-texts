Shifts in Acute Kidney Injury Trends Post-Alert System Implementation: A Retrospective Observational Cohort Study
Authors: Jinyeong Yi, Jiwon Ryu, Tran Tuan Tu, Seokwoo Park, Jong Cheol Jeong, Ho Jun Chin, Keonhwa Kim, Sejoong Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Rationale & ObjectiveAcute kidney injury (AKI), a complex syndrome involving multiple organ systems, requires timely intervention to prevent adverse outcomes. Despite the promise of AKI alert systems in improving early detection and management, their long-term effectiveness remains unevaluated. This study aimed to investigate the sustained impact of an AKI alert system over a decade of implementation.Study DesignRetrospective observational cohort study.Setting & ParticipantsConducted at a Korean tertiary hospital, analyzing 13,301 adult patients with AKI, from 246,650 admissions (2013-2023).ExposureIntroduction of an automated AKI alert system (implemented June 2014).OutcomesPrimary outcomes included 14-day AKI recovery, stage progression, dialysis onset, and mortality. Process outcomes comprised early nephrology consultation, overlooked AKI cases, and discontinuation of nephrotoxic medications. Long-term outcome was assessed as ≥35% estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) decline within 3 years.Analytical ApproachOutcomes were compared across pre-alert (January 2013-May 2014), alert implementation (June 2014-December 2019), and pandemic (January 2020-December 2023) phases, using adjusted multivariable logistic analyses calculating unadjusted and covariate-adjusted odds ratios (ORs). In recent years, acute kidney injury (AKI) has been recognized as a syndrome, rather than a disease confined to a single organ.1 Although AKI can certainly occur because of kidney-specific damage caused by drugs or contrast media, it can also develop through complex interactions with various other organs. This makes AKI a significant and intricate syndrome in hospitalized patients, often serving as a marker of disease severity. The incidence rate of AKI is approximately 10%-15% among hospitalized patients, increasing to over 50% in intensive care units.2 This syndrome is linked to considerable morbidity, mortality, along with high health care costs and quality of life.

An AKI episode can affect short-term adverse outcomes such as electrolyte imbalance, acid-base disorders, and immune reaction and can also negatively affect long-term outcomes such as chronic kidney disease (CKD), kidney failure with replacement therapy (KFRT), and survival.3, 4, 5 This underscores the importance of early and prompt AKI diagnosis, followed by appropriate treatment in improving patient prognosis.6 Several approaches for early AKI detection have been documented, including serum and urine biomarkers, imaging techniques such as ultrasound, and artificial intelligence.7, 8, 9, 10, 11 However, the clinical implementation of these methods has been impeded by practical difficulties in testing and other factors. In contrast to these approaches, our team developed an electronic AKI alert system in 2014 for early detection of AKI by screening all inpatients.12 Following its development, we showed that this alert system significantly reduced the number of AKI cases left without subsequent creatinine monitoring and prompted earlier consultation with nephrologists compared with pre-implementation. Additionally, we observed a reduction in severe AKI events and improved AKI recovery rate, thus proving that an early alert system for AKI may positively influence renal prognosis of patients. Based on these initial positive findings, we aimed to evaluate the sustained effectiveness of the system in promoting timely nephrology consultation and improving renal outcomes, over a decade following its implementation.

Methods

Patient and Public Engagement Summary

Patients and the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting, or dissemination plans of this research.

Study Design and Data Collection

This retrospective cohort study, conducted at Seoul National University Bundang Hospital, a tertiary hospital in Korea, aimed to analyze the trends in AKI over a decade-long period from January 2013 to December 2023.

The primary focus was to examine the shifts in AKI patterns after introduction of an electronic alert system on June 1, 2014, and to investigate the subsequent trends over the ensuing years. The study period was divided into 2 periods: the pre-alert period (January 2013 to May 2014) and the alert operation period (June 2014 to December 2023). To account for potential external influences, such as the coronavirus disease (COVID)-19 pandemic, on AKI trends, the alert operation period was further stratified into 2 phases: the alert implementation phase (June 2014 to December 2019) and the pandemic phase (January 2020 to December 2023).

The study population comprised adult patients admitted for the first time with serum creatinine (SCr) measurements during the study period (2013-2023). AKI was defined as an increase in SCr of ≥0.3 mg/dL or an elevation ≥1.5 times the baseline value. The baseline creatinine level was the first measurement obtained within a period of 14 days before admission, through the first creatinine evaluation after hospitalization.

AKI severity was classified into 3 stages based on the Kidney Disease Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) criteria. Stage 1 was defined as an increase in SCr level of 1.5 to <2.0 times the baseline value or an absolute increase of ≥0.3 mg/dL. Stage 2 was characterized by an increase in SCr level of 2.0 to <3.0 times the baseline value. Stage 3 was defined as an increase in SCr level of ≥3.0 times the baseline value or an absolute increase to ≥4.0 mg/dL in cases in which the baseline SCr level was below 4.0 mg/dL.

Patients who died on the day AKI was detected, those with pre-existing KFRT, individuals at risk of imminent KFRT (defined as having a baseline estimated glomerular filtration rate [eGFR] <15 mL/min/1.73 m2), and those admitted under the nephrology service, who typically had advanced or dialysis-dependent kidney disease already managed by specialists in which the alert would have limited influence, were excluded, along with cases with inconsistent data records.

Data collection included a wide range of clinical and demographic information. At admission, we recorded patient age, sex, and baseline body mass index. Intensive care unit (ICU) admission and surgical intervention during hospitalization were documented. Comorbid conditions, including diabetes mellitus, hypertension, heart failure, ischemic heart disease, and cancer, were identified using International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10) codes. Baseline SCr level and eGFR were collected. eGFRs were calculated using the CKD-EPI (Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration) creatinine equation.13 The most recent hemoglobin and albumin levels measured before any AKI event were obtained, with anemia defined as hemoglobin <11 g/dL and hypoalbuminemia as albumin <3.0 g/dL. Medication history was also reviewed, documenting the use of nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system inhibitors (RAASi), and diuretics within 2 weeks before AKI occurrence. Department caring for the patient at the time of AKI occurrence was recorded.

AKI events were classified as community acquired if the first SCr measurement postadmission met AKI criteria. We tracked nephrology consultation within 7 days of AKI occurrence. Follow-up SCr concentrations and mortality were monitored for up to 3 years after AKI.

This study was conducted in compliance with ethical standards. The Institutional Review Board of Seoul National University Bundang Hospital approved the study protocol (IRB number: B-1912-580-107) and waived the requirement for informed consent because of the retrospective nature of the analysis and the use of deidentified patient data. Throughout the study, we adhered to principles of patient confidentiality and data protection in accordance with institutional and national guidelines.

Automated AKI Detection and Alert System

The hospital-wide AKI alert system was implemented through the electronic medical record system.12 When the system detected AKI based on SCr measurements during routine daily screening, it automatically generated a pop-up notification in the electronic medical record the following day. The notification included the AKI severity stage and provided physicians with the option to automatically generate a nephrology consultation request. The system aimed to facilitate early detection of AKI and prompt appropriate specialist intervention when needed.

Definition of Outcome Variables

Study outcomes were categorized into primary, process, and long-term outcomes. Primary outcomes included AKI stage progression within 14 days (defined separately as progression from stage 1 to 2/3 or from stage 1/2 to 3), AKI recovery within 14 days, dialysis onset within 14 days, and 14-day mortality. AKI recovery was defined as mean SCr level <1.2 times baseline, or, for AKI stage 1 cases meeting only the 0.3 mg/dL increase criterion, SCr <baseline plus 0.2 mg/dL. Process outcomes comprised timing of nephrology consultation (categorized as early, within 48 hours; late, 3-7 days; or none, no consultation within 7 days of AKI onset), overlooked AKI (defined as lack of creatinine evaluation within 14 days after AKI onset), and discontinuation of nephrotoxic medications (NSAIDs and RAASi) after AKI alert. Medication discontinuation was defined as prescription present on AKI onset day but discontinued the following day after alert, excluding patients discharged the next day. The long-term outcome was assessed as decline in eGFR by ≥35% within 3 years.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed to evaluate outcomes across different study phases and the impact of the AKI alert system implementation. For phase comparisons, both unadjusted and adjusted odds ratios (ORs) were calculated using logistic regression. The adjusted models included demographic factors (age categorized as <65 and ≥65 years, sex), clinical characteristics (ICU admission, caring department categorized as surgical or nonsurgical), comorbid conditions (diabetes mellitus, hypertension, heart failure, ischemic heart disease, cancer), laboratory measurements (baseline eGFR categorized as ≥90, 60-90, 30-60, and <30 mL/min/1.73 m2; anemia defined as a hemoglobin level <11 g/dL; hypoalbuminemia defined as an albumin level <3.0 g/dL), medication use (NSAIDs, RAASi, diuretics), and AKI-related factors (AKI stages 1, 2, and 3; community-acquired AKI). All covariates were prespecified based on the prior literature and clinical relevance.

To ensure the robustness of our findings, we additionally performed pairwise propensity score–matched analyses for 3 key outcomes, including 14-day recovery, overlooked AKI, and early nephrology consultation. Matching used the same covariates as the adjusted models, applying 1:1 nearest-neighbor matching with a caliper of 0.2 standard deviations of the logit of the propensity score.

For time-to-recovery analysis, propensity score weighting was applied to balance baseline covariates across study phases, using the pre-alert period as the reference group. Weighted survival curves were then generated to visualize recovery trends.14,15

To evaluate the temporal impact of the AKI alert system implementation, we conducted an interrupted time series analysis, which is particularly suited for assessing the effects of health care interventions introduced at a population level over time.16, 17, 18 We analyzed monthly rates of 3 key outcomes: 30-day recovery, overlooked AKI, and early nephrology consultation. The interrupted time series analysis allowed us to examine both immediate changes following the intervention and long-term trends, while accounting for pre-existing trends in the outcomes. Segmented linear regression analysis was performed using 3 progressive adjustment models: unadjusted (M1), adjusted for age and sex (M2), and fully adjusted for all covariates (M3). This approach enabled us to estimate both the immediate effect of the alert system implementation (level change) and its impact on the outcome trends over time (slope change). Time series diagnostics were performed using the Durbin–Watson statistic (range, 1.5-1.91), which indicated no significant autocorrelation, supporting our modeling approach without the need for autoregressive terms.

Statistical analyses were performed using both Python version 3.8.16 and R version 4.4.2. Propensity score weighting and matching were implemented using the WeightIt and MatchIt package in R.19

Changes in Key Metrics Over 10 Years Following AKI Alert System Implementation

The temporal trends in outcomes after alert system implementation are shown in Fig 2, with particularly notable improvements observed in 3 key metrics. The AKI recovery rate, one of our primary outcomes, increased substantially from 40.2% in the pre-alert period to 66.5% and 67.3% in alert implementation phase and pandemic phase, respectively (adjusted OR 2.40 [95% confidence interval {CI}, 2.16-2.66] and 2.19 [1.94-2.47], respectively). Early nephrology consultation rates increased from 6.1%-19.4% and 17.2% (adjusted OR, 3.33 [2.81-3.95] and 2.65 [2.20-3.19], respectively), whereas overlooked AKI cases decreased from 22.2% to 8.9% and 7.4% (adjusted OR, 0.49 [0.43-0.55] and 0.43 [0.36-0.51], respectively). Other notable changes in the alert implementation phase and pandemic phase included increased rates of nephrotoxic medication discontinuation after AKI alert (NSAIDs: adjusted OR 1.80 [1.42-2.27] and 2.10 [1.62-2.72], respectively; RAAS inhibitors: adjusted OR 1.26 [0.85-1.87] and 1.83 [1.21-2.75], respectively). Discussion

This decade-long analysis of our AKI alert system demonstrates its sustained effectiveness, extending well beyond the initial implementation period. Although the previous study at our institution demonstrated immediate benefits, the present findings show several important insights about the long-term impact of automated AKI detection systems in clinical practice.12

First, the improvements in key metrics were not only maintained but showed remarkable stability over the 10-year period. Most notably, the recovery rate, our primary outcome, increased substantially from 40.2% to approximately 67% and remained stable throughout the study period. This improvement, representing an adjusted OR of 2.40 (95% CI 2.16-2.66), suggests that early recognition and intervention facilitated by the alert system can effectively modify the natural course of AKI. The robustness of these findings is further supported by our propensity score-weighted analysis, which demonstrated that the improved recovery rates persist even after rigorous adjustment for temporal changes in patient characteristics. The reduction in overlooked AKI cases (from 22.2% to below 9%) and improvement in early nephrology consultation rates (from 6.1% to approximately 18%) further support the system’s effectiveness in promoting timely intervention.

However, the impact of the alert system must be interpreted within the context of evolving patient characteristics. The alert operation period showed notably different patient characteristics from those in the pre-alert period, with patients being older (median age increasing from 64-67 years), having lower baseline kidney function (median eGFR decreasing from 101.2 to 82.1 mL/min/1.73 m2), and higher proportions of surgical admissions (36.9%-56.8%). Despite these shifts toward a potentially higher-risk population, the system maintained its effectiveness, particularly in promoting AKI recovery and early intervention.

The improvement in early nephrology consultation rates (from 6.1% to approximately 18%) is especially noteworthy, because timely specialist involvement has been shown to be crucial in optimizing AKI outcomes.20,21 Our interrupted time series analysis showed notable improvements in key metrics. Most significant was the immediate increase in early consultations following system implementation (14.0 percentage points, 95% CI: 8.0%-20.0%), although the gradual decline over time warrants attention in future system refinements. The analysis of overlooked AKI rates requires careful interpretation, particularly for the pandemic period, because the statistical modeling of multiple interventions over an extended time period may affect the estimation of later period effects. Despite these methodological considerations, the overall trend suggests that the alert system contributed to improved AKI monitoring and care throughout the study period.

Although we observed these sustained improvements in recovery rates and early nephrology consultation rates, we did not observe significant reductions in AKI progression or mortality rates during the alert phases. This finding on mortality aligns with recent meta-analyses that have consistently shown limited impact of AKI alerts on mortality outcomes.22 Regarding AKI progression, our results differed from those of the meta-analysis that reported a modest protective effect, though with notable variability across the included studies.22 The lack of effect on these outcomes in our study may be explained by the evolving patient characteristics in our cohort over the decade-long period, with patients during the alert surgery phases having higher baseline risks, including older age, lower baseline kidney function, a higher proportion of ICU admissions, and an increased prevalence of cancer. The greater use of diuretics observed during the pandemic period also supports this shift toward higher-risk, more medically complex patients because it likely reflects the increased proportion of those with heart failure.

Despite the neutral effects on progression and mortality, our findings suggest that improved recovery rates may translate into better long-term outcomes. Specifically, a lower risk of significant eGFR decline (≥35%) (Table 2) was observed during the implementation phase (adjusted OR 0.86, 95% CI: 0.76-0.99) within 3-year follow-up. This suggests that early detection and intervention, leading to better recovery rates, may have lasting benefits for kidney function. However, these long-term renal benefits appeared attenuated during the pandemic, which may be explained by substantial changes in hospital admission policies and patient composition. Tertiary hospitals in Korea prioritized critically ill patients while deferring elective admissions, resulting in a cohort with higher baseline severity.23 Nevertheless, the system's resilience during the COVID-19 pandemic deserves special mention.24 Despite the unprecedented challenges in health care delivery during this period, key metrics remained stable, suggesting that the alert system provided valuable decision support even during times of increased clinical burden and modified care protocols.

Our study has several limitations that are worth considering. First, because this was a single-center study conducted at a tertiary hospital, our findings may not be generalizable to other health care settings with different patient populations or clinical practices. Second, the retrospective design limits our ability to establish direct causality between the alert system and observed outcomes. Third, although we adjusted for numerous confounders, residual confounding from unmeasured variables may persist, particularly given the significant changes in patient characteristics over time. Fourth, all covariates were prespecified based on prior literature and clinical relevance rather than selected using data-driven variable reduction methods, which may limit model parsimony. Fifth, our handling of missing data, especially for variables such as albumin and hemoglobin levels, may have introduced some bias in our analyses.

Our findings suggest several directions for system enhancement in future. Integration of machine learning algorithms may improve AKI risk prediction and progression, potentially enabling even earlier intervention.25 Although previous studies have shown ambiguous results with standardized care bundles, further research is needed to identify which specific interventions contribute most to improved outcomes, enabling development of more effective evidence-based AKI care bundles.26, 27, 28, 29 Future studies should also explore the impact of alert customization based on patient-specific risk factors and department-specific needs, including the potential benefits of real-time alerts for high-risk settings such as the ICU or emergency room to enhance the system’s adaptability and effectiveness in diverse clinical settings.

In conclusion, our decade-long experience demonstrates that an automated AKI alert system can achieve sustained improvements in key clinical outcomes, even as patient populations and health care environments evolve. These findings support the continued implementation and refinement of such systems as valuable tools in the early detection and management of AKI.
