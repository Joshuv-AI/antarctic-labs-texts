Meeting the moment: using scientific conferences and journals to promote equity and inclusion in animal science
Authors: Sarah Reed, Allison Meyer, Steve Zinn
Published: 2025

Implications

-Move Beyond Numerical Representation: Increasing diversity in membership numbers is a significant achievement that has not yet translated into meaningful inclusion for many scientific societies, especially in leadership roles and scientific publications. A shift is needed from focusing solely on numbers to fostering genuine inclusion across all organizational levels.

-Embed Diversity, Equity, and Inclusion in Research Dissemination: Diversity, equity, and inclusion principles must be incorporated into key research outputs, including publications and presentations. Integrating these values into every stage of scientific work can create a culture of inclusion that empowers future leaders and showcases the positive impact of these initiatives.

-Leverage Cultural Intelligence for Diversity, Equity, and Inclusion Advancement: Animal scientists work across diverse cultures and disciplines, offering a unique opportunity to drive diversity, equity, and inclusion initiatives. By applying the cultural intelligence inherent in our profession, we can create inclusive environments, elevating diversity, equity, and inclusion efforts in the animal sciences.

Introduction

Animal science organizations have been working to promote diversity, equity, and inclusion (DEI) for over two decades. These efforts began with a strong focus on recruiting and retaining women in the field. In the early 2000s, women made up less than 10% of professional-level animal scientists in academia, with the exception of equine science, where female involvement has a long tradition. Today, women represent more than 50% of professional animal scientists and an impressive 80% of equine scientists. Early DEI initiatives largely measured success by the increase in the number of women in the field, which, while an important achievement, has not always translated into increases in the tenure-track faculty ranks or in leadership positions. While earlier work focused on increasing the inclusion of women in the field, current and future work must include individuals from all underrepresented, underserved, and marginalized groups.

Although many animal science societies have formalized DEI efforts through committees, symposia, and inclusion standards, these initiatives often lack sufficient financial support and expertise to create truly inclusive programs that extend beyond individual organizations. Moreover, much of the assessment of success in increasing underrepresented, underserved, and marginalized groups has used the same “head count” approach that was initially used to increase the representation of women. While a well-intentioned first step, this approach has not always led to meaningful inclusion, highlighting the need to shift the focus from simple numerical representation to genuine inclusion in leadership roles, programming, scientific publications, and meetings.

While it is easy to criticize the DEI efforts of animal science organizations, there is one area in which we excel: cultural intelligence. Animal scientists work globally across diverse cultures, socioeconomic backgrounds, and scientific disciplines to achieve common scientific goals. Our profession spans a variety of institutions, from Land Grant universities and flagship R1 institutions to Hispanic-serving institutions, Historically Black Colleges and Universities (HBCUs), Tribal Colleges and Universities (TCUs), and small regional colleges. We represent the diverse nature of food production, with professionals coming from a wide range of socioeconomic backgrounds. Despite our differences, we share a common drive to feed the world in a healthy, sustainable manner. By translating the cultural intelligence inherent in our work into our scientific endeavors, we can truly advance meaningful inclusion and elevate DEI efforts within the field of animal sciences.

Empowering diverse leaders

Campaigns aimed solely at increasing membership diversity have failed to generate significant shifts in leadership dynamics, suggesting that the training provided has been either inadequate or focused on too few individuals, or that unconscious bias may result in the selection of leadership that reflects the majority group. This is evident when comparing leadership demographics today with those from 20 years ago. In 2003, less than 10% of board members of major animal science-related organizations were women (with approximately 50% of that number in equine science), whereas today, women make up about 44% of the boards. However, fewer than 10% of society fellows are women, and less than 33% of awards are given to women. The numbers for underrepresented, underserved, and marginalized animal scientists are even less; about 9% of board membership, but under 5% among fellows and award recipients. To truly transform our culture in animal science organizations, we must expand training efforts beyond a select few leaders and engage the broader membership. By doing so, we can cultivate a generation of mentors and future leaders who will drive cultural change throughout the organizations.

Publications

Scientific societies have a pivotal role in peer-reviewed publications, influencing the dissemination of research across the scientific community and to the public. Inadvertently, they also control one of the primary metrics for academic advancement. Publishing enhances visibility, reputation, and prestige, which can lead to increased opportunities for promotion, collaboration, and funding (Bedeian, 1996; De Rond and Miller, 2005; Bedeian et al., 2008). Therefore, the ability to publish is one of the most important aspects in the career of a scientist. However, women and underrepresented groups often face inequities throughout the publication process, including inadequate mentorship, limited resources, poor representation in review systems, and underrepresentation on editorial boards. Studies show that women and minorities experience longer publication delays, with African-American scholars facing the longest wait times. Papers by majority African-American or Latine teams are also cited less frequently than those authored by white researchers (Rakhra et al., 2021).

A global study of 81,000 editors across 15 science, technology, engineering, art, and math disciplines revealed that women make up fewer than 15% of editors and less than 8% of Editors-in-Chief (EiC; Liu et al., 2023). An analysis of 368 editors across 25 US and European medical/scientific journals showed that 75% of editors and EiC are white (Salazar et al., 2021). In animal science-related journals, these numbers are even less, with only 6% of EiCs and fewer than 15% of section editors being women. Underrepresented individuals are similarly scarce. These implicit biases in the peer-review process can further hinder the publication prospects, especially for women and minorities. Although reduced abstract and manuscript acceptance rates among underrepresented groups are commonly believed to be due to reduced scientific quality, when double-blind review was used in a medical journal, there was no longer a difference in rejection rates between men and women or between majority and underrepresented groups (Defilippis et al., 2025).

While increasing submissions is important, that alone is insufficient to create true inclusion in high-quality scientific publications. Continuing to provide mentorship and develop a more equitable publication system is necessary to improve success rates for these submissions. To drive systemic change, it is critical to diversify journal leadership, including EiCs and editorial boards, which directly influence the peer-review process and shape scientific discourse. While the impact of diversity in journal leadership on publishing outcomes has not been well studied, it has been consistently shown that diverse teams are more innovative with greater impact on their respective fields (Swartz et al., 2019; Yang et al., 2022). A shift in journal leadership requires not only better representation of women and underrepresented groups but also a concerted effort to address inequities in research mentorship and resources. Expanding mentorship and training for authors and reviewers will increase success rates and ensure that underrepresented scientists become better mentors, facilitating long-term impacts in publications.

Scientific meetings

Scientific meetings are essential venues for collaboration and idea exchange, but not all meetings provide inclusive and positive experiences for all individuals. Scientists from underrepresented backgrounds may feel excluded or isolated when they do not see people who share similar identities or backgrounds as presenters or in attendance. The persistent underrepresentation of women and other underrepresented groups in keynote speeches, invited talks, and leadership roles contributes to this issue. We must prioritize mentorship in scientific presentations, helping diverse scientists build the skills needed to disseminate their research and connect with a wider audience.

Moreover, many scientists encounter practical barriers to participation, such as inaccessible venues, high costs, and lack of essential services like childcare, lactation rooms, or safe bathroom spaces (Pendergrass et al., 2019). Some may even experience harassment and assault at meetings (Pendergrass et al., 2019). The structure and culture of scientific meetings often alienate individuals from underrepresented identities, hindering the exchange of diverse ideas and innovative solutions. To create more inclusive scientific meetings, we must address exclusionary practices at three key stages: (1) planning (both programmatically and logistically), (2) attendee experience during the meeting, and (3) post-meeting assessments of inclusion. Current initiatives often focus solely on increasing demographic representation, but this approach overlooks other barriers and does not take the experience of underrepresented individuals into account. Meeting organizers must look beyond their own experiences of meetings to ensure their approaches meet the needs of the few, as well as the many.

Conclusion

As animal scientists, we have both a responsibility and an opportunity to foster inclusive environments within our communities, and we must work to train our members to integrate inclusion principles throughout their scientific programs. In today’s climate, inclusion initiatives are being scaled back or removed completely in response to political pressure, despite the substantial investments that have been made. It is crucial, then, to develop strategies that showcase the impact of inclusion efforts at every stage of research and dissemination, embedding these principles into scientific work to create a culture of inclusion and empowerment for the next generation of scientific leaders. Moreover, we must consider all aspects of diversity, including all possible members that may be underrepresented or underserved, not only specific groups. This not only makes our scientific organizations stronger but also will lead to better science through a stronger community of scientists.

Author Biography

Dr. Sarah Reed is an Associate Professor in the Department of Animal Science at the University of Connecticut and the current Editor-in-Chief of Animal Frontiers. Dr. Reed earned her M.S. and Ph.D. in Animal Science and then spent 2 years as a post-doctoral research associate in the Department of Physical Therapy at the University of Florida. Her current research interests include the impacts of maternal diet during gestation on offspring growth and development, and the effects of exercise training and detraining in horses. Dr. Reed enjoys teaching, in particular introducing undergraduate students to science through class and research opportunities.

Dr. Allison Meyer is an Associate Professor in Animal Sciences at the University of Missouri. After growing up on a small cow-calf operation in central Indiana, she received degrees at Michigan State University (B.S.), the University of Missouri (M.S.), and North Dakota State University (Ph.D.). Drawing on interests from early in her life on the farm, Dr. Meyer’s research focuses on how nutrition and management of pregnant beef females affect nutrient partitioning during pregnancy and lactation, as well as subsequent effects on neonatal and preweaning calves. Dr. Meyer teaches or co-teaches courses in nutrition and beef production and greatly enjoys helping students of all levels find their love of science.

Dr. Steven Zinn is a professor of Animal Science at the University of Connecticut. He joined the faculty at the University of Connecticut in 1990 after earning his B.S. degree from Cornell University and his M.S. and Ph.D. degrees in the laboratory of H. Allen Tucker at Michigan State University. His research is focused on growth physiology and endocrinology in domestic species with an emphasis on maternal nutrition. From 2008 to 2013, Dr. Zinn was Editor-in-Chief of the Journal of Animal Science and was a founding member and the inaugural Editor-in-Chief of Animal Frontiers from 2011 to 2013. He served as President of ASAS from 2017 to 2018, was named an ASAS Fellow in 2015, and received the ASAS Morrison Award in 2021.
