A brain-state-informed framework for simultaneous extinction of fear and functional magnetic resonance imaging acquisition in rodents
Authors: Elena Andres, Felipe Aedo-Jury, Lara Hamzehpour, Dirk Cleppien, Raffael Kalisch, Albrecht Stroh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAdequately responding towards a threat is a crucial mechanism for survival. Adapting this response when a threat-associated stimulus or situation has become safe requires extinction learning and formation of an extinction memory. Functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) affords to longitudinally monitor network activity, yet, in the rodent, still suffers from significant variability of results and practical restrictions, mainly related to the different approaches of subject immobilization. Physical restraint of awake animals permits only short scanning times, while anesthesia can induce uncontrolled brain states with limited stimulus responsiveness and processing. Here, we implement a paradigm where light medetomidine sedation permits long scanning times in a stable brain state with functional characteristics comparable to the human resting state. We observe responsiveness of the brain to visual stimulation and large-scale resting-state network activity with small-world connectivity features. After visual fear conditioning outside the MRI scanner, rats exposed to the unreinforced visual conditioned stimulus in this stable persistent activity state inside the scanner (extinction) exhibit a significantly lower conditioned fear response when re-exposed to the conditioned stimulus days after scanning (test). We present a brain state-informed paradigm easily adaptable for future studies involving invasive neural manipulations to causally investigate extinction and its memory consolidation.Keywords: brain-states, fear extinction under sedation, fear conditioning, fMRI, translation

Introduction

Adequately responding towards danger is a crucial mechanism for survival and therefore evolutionary firmly established. Once learned, a cue predicting a threat will lead to a stable fear response (Pavlov 1927). However, continuing to respond with fear even when a threat-associated stimulus or situation has become safe is maladaptive and has been associated with the presence of anxiety disorders or post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) (Beck and Clark 1997; Duits et al. 2015). In a clinical setting, exposure therapy is the treatment of choice (Beck 2005), whereby a person is repeatedly confronted with the fear-inducing situation. The overall positive outcome, ie the predicted negative outcome not occurring, leads to a reduction of fear (Craske et al. 2014; Craske et al. 2018).

An established experimental approach for studying fear learning is the Pavlovian fear conditioning paradigm. Typically, as a first step, a neutral stimulus (conditioned stimulus, CS) is associated with an aversive stimulus (unconditioned stimulus, US), such as a shock, leading to the formation of a CS–US association (“fear memory”) and the expression of a conditioned fear response (CR) to the CS. During extinction, the CS is repeatedly presented without being reinforced with the US, eventually reducing the CR. Extinction is believed not to erase the original fear memory trace but to form a new CS–no US association or “extinction memory” (Delamater 1996; Rescorla 1996; Bouton 2004). Upon future CS encounters, the strength of the newly formed extinction memory relative to the original fear memory determines whether fear will return or not.

One major problem in clinical exposure therapy is the lability of the extinction memory, which can lead to the return of fear (Bouton 2004). This makes it relevant to understand the neurobiological mechanisms governing the formation and consolidation of extinction memories. In the human, functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) has proved a useful tool providing important insights into the brain regions activated and deactivated during extinction learning and extinction memory retrieval (Sehlmeyer et al. 2009; Fullana et al. 2016; Fullana et al. 2020), the neural signals that drive associative learning (Raczka et al. 2011; Thiele et al. 2021), the representational patterns underlying memory encoding and consolidation (Visser et al. 2011; Visser et al. 2013; Gerlicher et al. 2018; Hennings et al. 2022), and the brain-wide circuit and network re-organization that occurs during and after extinction (Milad et al. 2007; Haaker et al. 2013; Wen et al. 2021). These studies involved both task-based and task-free (resting-state) fMRI.

fMRI is also a highly promising method for activation and network studies in the rodent, as it affords non-invasive and repeated whole-brain imaging. A significant drawback for rodent studies, however, is the necessary subject immobilization. Studies using anesthesia for immobilization must solve the problem that fMRI measures are heavily impacted by the level of vigilance of the animal. Despite the relevance of this criterion, most fMRI studies in anesthetized animals define critical experimental conditions based on the anesthetic regimen rather than on brain activity status (Kalthoff et al. 2013; Paasonen et al. 2018). Yet, anesthetic effects on brain activity vary significantly, ie there are high inter-individual differences, and the intra-individual temporal dynamics of the anesthesia effects within an imaging session are complex and hardly predictable (Paasonen et al. 2018). This leads to significant variability also in fMRI results (Bukhari et al. 2017; Paasonen et al. 2018). Further, it is not clear to what extent anesthesia still permits the processing of external stimuli used in task-based studies or also the spontaneous neural processes targeted in resting-state studies. This question is particularly relevant in studies investigating cognitive or affective processes, such as in emotional learning and memory studies, rather than only responses to primary sensory activation.

Awake imaging might be advantageous in this regard (Chang et al. 2016; Stenroos et al. 2018; Dopfel et al. 2019; Hinz et al. 2019; Liu et al. 2020) but, here, additional physiological constraints and sources of variability emerge. Animals must be restrained to avoid movement artifacts (Chang et al. 2016; Madularu et al. 2017), but physical restraint is a source of stress that can lead to the contamination of the obtained results, particularly in experiments that investigate the brain’s stress circuitry (Febo 2011; Reed et al. 2013). To tackle these problems, researchers usually scan animals awake and restrained for a short period of time only, avoiding long-term stress. For instance, in two studies (Brydges et al. 2013; Harris et al. 2015), the blood oxygenation level-dependent (BOLD) fMRI signal was recorded in response to the CS in fear-conditioned awake rodents using three blocks of 2 min each with the stimulus. While this stimulation seemed to be sufficient to show fear-related changes in amygdala activity due to the CS, it would be favorable to implement paradigms with longer recording times permitting to capture more complex and subtle brain processes. Especially in order to study the new learning that occurs in fear extinction with repeated unreinforced CS presentation, the associated memory formation and post-learning memory consolidation, and the network re-organization accompanying these processes, longer stimulation periods as well as extended post-learning resting-state fMRI would be desirable. This can practically not be achieved in awake animals.

These considerations show that it would be of high translational relevance to find a way to investigate whole-brain neural dynamics as measured by changes in the BOLD signal over extended times in a movement-free, controlled, and responsive brain state.

In two previous studies, we determined functional brain states of sedated and anesthetized rodents, combining optic-fiber-based calcium recordings with fMRI during rest and visual stimulation (Schwalm et al. 2017; Aedo-Jury et al. 2020). We showed that fMRI-based functional connectivity measures, as also widely used in human research, can be classified based on the respective activity state of the brain, as defined by calcium recordings. Establishing a neurophysiological correlate for these fMRI measures increases their validity as brain activity markers in humans and further suggests that we can also meaningfully employ them in rodents for the purpose of determining their functional brain state. This markedly increases the possibilities of translational research.

More specifically, we observed that the lightly sedated state, maintained by the α2 adrenergic agonist medetomidine, was differentiated from the anesthetized state by the presence of large-scale neural networks reminiscent of human resting-state networks, which further showed small-world connectivity features (Aedo-Jury et al. 2020). This indicates that medetomidine sedation might be a promising method to maintain the desired movement-free and controlled, yet responsive brain state.

Indeed, assessing and confirming the stability of resting-state dynamics under light medetomidine is a necessary validation step in preclinical fMRI (Grandjean et al. 2014; Sirmpilatze et al. 2019; Pradier et al. 2021). And just very recently, the impact of brain state on awake perception has gained much needed attention (Filipchuk et al. 2022;Schwalm et al. 2022).

Resting-state fMRI patterns under light medetomidine sedation has been shown upon sensory stimulation (Sirmpilatze et al. 2019; Pradier et al. 2021). In addition, recent work has mapped differential engagement of subcortical and cortical visual pathways during light stimulation in rodents, further contextualizing visual readouts under medetomidine(-based) protocols (Gil et al. 2024).

Building on these insights, we here developed a pipeline for long task-based and resting-state measures in the context of fear extinction in sedated rats, using a visual stimulus as CS and shock as US. We asked whether, employing this pipeline, fear extinction training within the MR scanner leads to lasting reduction in CRs, which would indicate successful CS processing and extinction learning in the sedated state. We further tested if the paradigm allows us to observe robust visual activation and resting-state networks, in line with prior reports (Sirmpilatze et al. 2019; Pradier et al. 2021). Finally, we also explored brain activity changes related to the emotional qualities of the visual stimulus (that is, to the threat value it acquires during conditioning and the safety value it acquires during extinction), using standard general linear model (GLM) analysis. These exploratory analyses prepare detailed analyses of the learning signals, stimulus representation patterns, and network re-organization processes occurring in this paradigm, which we will report elsewhere.

Materials and methods

Animals

Female Lewis rats were group-housed under a 12 h light–dark cycle and provided with food and water ad libitum. Animal husbandry and experimental manipulation were carried out according to animal welfare guidelines of the Johannes Gutenberg-University Mainz and were approved by the Landesuntersuchungsamt Rheinland-Pfalz, Koblenz, Germany (23177 07/G 20-17-076). Experiments were performed when animals were approximately 10 wk old and weighed 170 to 200 g.

Design

In total, we tested 84 animals. Sixty-four rats were divided into three groups that were all fear-conditioned to a visual CS. Of these, one group received extinction training in the MRI (MRI Extinction group, MRI Ext, n = 20), one was scanned but did not receive extinction training (MRI No Extinction group, MRI No Ext, n = 20), and one received extinction training outside the MR scanner (Behavioral Extinction group, Beh Ext, n = 24). See Fig. 1A. This design allowed us to ask whether extinction was feasible under fMRI conditions, by testing whether the MRI Ext group would show lower CRs after extinction training than the MRI No Ext group, while ideally approaching the extinction performance of a group that had been extinction-trained under standard behavioral conditions (Beh Ext).

A fourth group (MRI No Shock group, MRI No Shock, n = 20) underwent identical experimental procedures as the MRI Ext group, that is, it received identical CS stimulation and MRI scanning except that it did not receive US on the fear conditioning day. See Suppl. Fig. 1A. This group served to compare CS-induced fMRI activations on the extinction day between a conditioned (MRI Ext) and a nonconditioned group (MRI No Shock), to thereby isolate activation specific to fear expression and extinction learning (that is, to the emotional meaning of the visual CS) in the MRI Ext group. This comparison allowed us to explore whether extinction in our paradigm would be accompanied by standard (GLM-based) fMRI activation, suggesting the paradigm can be used for more fine-grained analyses of neural learning and memory processes.

All three MRI groups (MRI Ext, MRI No Ext, MRI No Shock) were used for analyzing the responsiveness of the sedated brain to visual stimulation and the successful establishment of a resting-state brain state characterized by the presence of large-scale networks.

Visual stimulation

The visual stimulation in the MRI consisted of a light pulse train of 3 Hz applied for 30 s with a pulse length of 10 ms over 30 min with 30 s intertrial intervals (ITI, vis stim1). The frequency was optimized for neuronal response as shown in Schmid et al. (Schmid et al. 2016) for cross-modal experiments comprising fMRI with simultaneous calcium photometry. While the BOLD signal peaked at a stimulation frequency of 9 Hz, the number of Ca2+ responses was highest at 3 Hz and decreased at higher frequencies. Transferring these findings to stimulations in the visual cortex, we optimized the stimulation paradigm in the present experiment (CS presentation at 3 Hz) for the number of evoked Ca2+ transients and the perception of the light pulses, considering the reduced BOLD response in the visual cortex. The light pulses were generated by a LED (Fiber Photometry System, npi electronic GmbH, Tamm, Germany) and applied between the animal’s eyes by an optical fiber fixated on the head. The optical fiber had a diameter of 400 μm; the tip pointed towards the nose. The light intensity of the visual stimulation was measured to be around 10 lux (Dinh et al. 2021) and the illumination during stimulation was controlled for each measurement.

MRI experiments

MRI data acquisition was performed in a 9.4 T small animal imaging system with a 0.7 T/m gradient system (Biospec 94/20, Bruker Biospin GmbH, Ettlingen, Germany). For transmission and reception, we used a linear whole-body volume transmitter coil combined with an anatomically shaped 4-channel receive-only coil array for the rat brain. Animals were initially anesthetized by isoflurane (2% in 80% air and 20% oxygen) for scanning preparation and placement into a heated MRI cradle in the scanner. For head fixation we used a tooth bar without ear bars to ensure a natural head position with unrestricted blood and oxygen supply to the brain. Once in the scanner, a bolus of 0.04 mg/kg of medetomidine was peritoneally administered. Approximately after 5 min, the isoflurane anesthesia was switched off and animals received a 0.08 mg/kg/h medetomidine perfusion. fMRI recordings started 30 min after the cessation of isoflurane administration (Suppl. Fig. 8). The overall mean for the onsets of the first functional measurements after starting medetomidine application was (34 ± 3) min, consistent with the recommendation in the Consensus protocol published by Grandjean et al. (Grandjean et al. 2023). Body temperature, saturation of peripheral oxygen (SpO2) and breathing rate of the animal were monitored and recorded during the entire experimental procedure (Suppl. Fig. 7A–C, Suppl. Table 1) using a small animal MRI-compatible monitoring system (Model 1030, Small Animal Instruments, Inc., Stony Brook, NY, USA).

MRI sequences

All imaging sessions included T1-weighted anatomical imaging (TR =50 ms, TE = 8 ms, image size 175 × 175 × 80, FOV (26 × 34 × 28) mm3, resolution (0.15 × 0.19 × 0.35) mm3, duration 14 min, gradient echo with flip angle = 20°), and fMRI was performed using BOLD measurements with T2*-weighted images acquired with a single-shot gradient echo EPI sequence (TR = 1.5 s, TE = 14.5 ms and FA 90°, (356 × 268) μm2 spatial resolution, slice thickness 0.8 mm, 34 continuous slices, 1,200 acquisitions, duration 30 min). Finally, a high resolution in-plane T2-MSME sequence imaging the 34 EPI slices was performed (TR = 2,000 ms, TE = 12 ms, 2 averages, refocusing angle 159°, (130 × 100) μm2 spatial resolution, image size 256 × 256, slice thickness 0.8 mm, 34 continuous slices, duration 8 min 32 s).

Entry-MRI (T0)

Two weeks before the fear conditioning day (for behavioral procedures, see below), all animals of the MRI Ext, MRI No Ext and MRI No Shock groups underwent a baseline MRI starting with the acquisition of anatomical T1 images (Fig. 1A, Suppl. Fig. 2A). Visual stimulation (vis stim) was applied by an optical fiber fixated on the head placed between the eyes of the animal. After 30 min of 3 Hz blue light pulse stimulations for 30 s with 30-s ITI (vis stim1) animals underwent 30 min of resting-state measurement (rest), followed by another 30 min of visual stimulation (vis stim2).

Extinction-MRI (T2)

Three to eight days after the fear conditioning day, animals of the MRI Ext, MRI No Ext, and MRI No Shock groups underwent another MRI session. In the MRI Ext group, the session also served to conduct extinction. First, a T1 measurement and a 30-min resting-state measurement (rest1) were conducted. In the MRI Ext and MRI No Shock groups, this was followed by two times 30 min of 3 Hz blue light pulse stimulations for 30 s with a 30-s ITI (vis stim1 and 2). During this time, the animals of the MRI No Ext group paused in the scanner. Finally, all animals received a 30-min resting-state measurement (rest2; Fig. 1, Suppl. Fig. 2A).

Exit-MRI (T4)

One week after the retrieval test day, a subset of the animals of the MRI Ext, MRI No Ext, and MRI No Shock groups (n = 37; MRI Ext n = 14, MRI No Ext n = 11, MRI No Shock n = 12) underwent an MRI session starting with T1 acquisition. Then, a 30-min resting-state measurement was conducted (rest1) and followed by 30 min of 3 Hz blue light pulse stimulations for 30 s with a 30-s ITI (vis stim). Finally, animals received another 30-min resting-state measurement (rest2).

Behavioral experiments

Behavior experiments were controlled by dedicated software (Coulbourn Instruments, Holliston, MA, United States) and movement of the animals was tracked by an overhead video camera positioned above the arena.

Fear conditioning (T1)

Fear conditioning always took place outside the MR scanner in the conditioning context (context A). The MRI No Shock group was treated identically to the three other groups but received no shocks. Animals were habituated to the experimental room 1 d before fear conditioning for 1 h. Fear conditioning was conducted in a (23.5 × 23.5 × 19.5) cm3 chamber (Habitest system H, Coulbourn Instruments, Holliston, MA, United States) with Plexiglas walls and ceiling and a stainless grid floor from which an electric adverse stimulus could be elicited. The chamber was illuminated by white light (5 lux). After 2 min of habituation to the context, the first CS (3 Hz blue light pulse) was presented for 30 s. Three CSs were presented in total, with a 2-min ITI, and reinforced by a 0.5 mA electric stimulus (US) in the last 2 s. Sixty seconds after the last trial, animals were returned to their home cage.

Extinction (T2)

While the MRI groups were treated as described above (Extinction-MRI, in the MRI extinction context B), the Beh Ext group received extinction training outside the MRI scanner (in the behavioral extinction context C). Animals were placed in the same (23.5 × 23.5 × 19.5) cm3 chamber with Plexiglas walls and ceiling as used for conditioning. The floor was covered with a black plate and a round bowl filled with bedding. For additional context change relative to conditioning, an almond-flavored smell in a dish was placed below the plate. After 2.5 min of habituation to the new context, the unreinforced CS was presented 60 times for 30 s with a 30-s ITI. Sixty seconds after the last trial, animals were returned to their home cage.

Test (T3)

The retrieval test took place identically in all groups by re-confronting them with the CS. While in the MRI Ext and the Beh Ext groups this constituted a test for the retrieval of the extinction memory formed at T2 in competition with the fear memory formed at T1, in the MRI No Ext group the procedure was supposed to lead to fear memory retrieval only. In the MRI No Shock group, no specific fear or safety associations were expected to be retrieved. Animals were placed into the same setup as in behavioral extinction (T2), that is, in context C. As a consequence, the design was an ABC design for the MRI Ext group (different contexts for conditioning, extinction, and retrieval), while it was an ACC design for the Beh Ext group (extinction at T2 and retrieval at T3 in the same context). Note that extinction memory retrieval is generally more difficult in a context different from the extinction context, such as in an ABC design, due to the relative absence of retrieval signals for the extinction memory (Bouton 2002; Bouton 2004). Therefore, it was expected that there would be a stronger reduction of CRs at T3 in the Beh Ext than in the MRI Ext group. After 2.5 min of habituation to the chamber, the first unreinforced CS was presented for 2 min. All animals of the MRI groups, which later underwent a T4 scan (see Exit MRI [T4]), were additionally presented seven CSs of 2 min each, with a 2-min ITI. Sixty seconds after the last CS, animals were returned to their home cage.

fMRI analysis

Data preprocessing

fMRI data (resting and visual stimulation) processing was performed using SPM12 (Wellcome Trust Centre for Neuroimaging, London, UK, http://www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/) running on MATLAB 2017b (MathWorks®, Natick, Massachusetts, USA). Manual brain extraction of T1 and EPI images was done with ITKSnap version 3.8.0. For fMRI preprocessing, the first five volumes of each scanning run were discarded due to equilibrium effects. This was followed by realignment with second degree B-Spline interpolation and co-registration of the mean functional image to the T1-weighted anatomical image. Subsequently, the first EPI of each sequence was normalized using 12-parameter affine transformation to the in vivo rat atlas MRI template of Valdés-Hernández et al. (Valdés-Hernández et al. 2011). These normalization parameters were then applied to the functional images to minimize inter-subject anatomical differences. Lastly, normalized functional data was spatially smoothed using a 0.5 mm full-width-at-half-maximum Gaussian smoothing kernel.

GLM analysis

To analyze changes in the BOLD signal induced by visual stimulation (vis stim runs), we used SPM’s standard GLM analysis. For the purpose of establishing the responsiveness of the sedated brain to the visual stimulus, we used a regressor formed by convolving the visual stimulation time course (boxcar of 30 s every 60 s) with a cortical rat hemodynamic response function (HRF) (Lambers et al. 2020). For the purpose of establishing emotional (threat- and safety-related) stimulus processing, we chose a more exploratory approach that took into account that we had no prior experience with how the sedated rodent brain processes these acquired stimulus qualities. We therefore tested two separate models, the above boxcar model and an event-type model with an HRF-convolved delta function regressor at stimulus onset, where emotionally salient information might be preferentially processed. In all analyses, runs vis stim1 and vis stim2 were separated, to be sensitive for within-session habituation and learning effects. FAST correction for autocorrelation, removal of linear drift, and a high-pass filter (128 s) were applied in both GLMs. Motion parameters were included as regressors. The main effect of visual stimulation (on > off) was determined employing SPM’s Flexible Factorial Design for each group and visual stimulation run separately. Correction for multiple comparisons at the whole-brain level was performed using the family-wise error (FWE) method for the visual stimulation analyses only, whereas the exploratory analyses of emotional stimulus processing used an uncorrected significance threshold of P < 0.001. Minimal cluster size was kE > 50. For analyses outside SPM, the main activation cluster of vis stim1 at T0 was used for extracting average parameter estimate (beta) values for each animal and run within each group, using MarsBar (Brett et al. 2002).

Within-session extinction analyses (T2)

To quantify within-session changes during extinction, we focused on late vs. early CS responses (vis stim2 vs. vis stim1) at T2 in anatomically defined extinction-related ROIs (hippocampus [HC], infralimbic cortex [IL], amygdala, striatum; rat atlas MRI template of Valdés-Hernández et al. (Valdés-Hernández et al. 2011)) in the MRI Ext, and for comparison, the MRI No Shock group, using both the boxcar and event-related first-level models described above. At the voxel level, we ran second-level paired t-tests (vis stim1 > vis stim2 and vis stim1 < vis stim2) in SPM with each ROI as an explicit mask (small-volume inference); FWE correction was applied within the mask at the peak level (FWE).

To complement the voxel-wise analyses, ROI-level summaries were also computed outside SPM: for each animal and run (vis stim1, vis stim2), we extracted the mean beta within the anatomical extinction-related masks and formed Δ = vis stim2—vis stim1. Statistics were computed in R version 4.1.2 (2021 November 1): within-group vis stim1 vs. vis stim2 tests used paired t-tests when paired differences were normal (Shapiro test), otherwise Wilcoxon signed-rank; between-group differences in Δ (MRI Ext vs. MRI No Shock) used Wilcoxon rank-sum; brain–behavior associations in the MRI Ext group related Δ (and, for checks in IL and HC, vis stim1 and vis stim2 separately) to T3 freezing via Spearman correlations. For our a priori hypothesis that greater late-phase activity indexes better extinction (lower freezing), in HC and IL we additionally report one-tailed Spearman tests (H1: ρ < 0). Unless otherwise stated (one-tailed for directional hypotheses in IL and HC), all tests were two-tailed. All region of interest (ROI)-level tests were treated as exploratory; p-values are uncorrected.

Independent component analysis

The induction of a controlled brain state presenting functional resting-state networks was verified using the resting-state runs. For combined independent component analysis (ICA) of the motion-corrected and smoothed resting-state data of all resting-state runs of all animals, the freely available software “Group ICA of fMRI Toolbox (GIFT)”, version 4.0c (http://mialab.mrn.org/software/gift/) was used. Physiological or motion parameters were not excluded, as ICA itself is a denoising method, decomposing functional data into spatially independent, but temporally coherent brain networks (Calhoun et al. 2009). Removal of motion-induced signal variations in particular might come at the cost of destroying the autocorrelation structure of the fMRI time-series and reducing temporal degrees of freedom (Pruim et al. 2015). Mask-calculation using the first image was employed, and time-series were linearly detrended and converted to z-scores. For back-reconstruction, the “GICA”-algorithm (Calhoun et al. 2017) was used, and all results were scaled by their z-scores. We used the Infomax-algorithm with regular stability analysis without autofill. In total, the number of components was set to 20, as in previous work by us (Aedo-Jury et al. 2020) and others (Becerra et al. 2011; Kreitz et al. 2018). Our approach to differentiating signal- from noise-related components consisted in only considering components when they met the criteria: clear neuroanatomical structure, not one-sided, predominantly low frequency (at least one strong peak within 0.01 to 0.1 Hz). Visual inspection of all components led to discarding five components due to distinct single-sided activity or clusters not being clearly located inside the brain.

Graph analysis

Of the 15 identified ICA components, 11 bilateral ROIs characteristic of the default mode network (DMN) (excluding striatum and cerebellar regions) were used to extract time courses for graph analysis in each resting-state run. Note that we only included cortical DMN components. In this way, we stayed consistent with our prior work (Aedo-Jury et al. 2020), where we also only used cortical DMN regions for graph analysis for characterization of a distinctive persistent brain state. First, the partial correlation coefficients were calculated for each pair of ROIs, resulting in a 11 × 11 matrix for each animal. Values passing FDR correction on each matrix (P-value threshold < 0.05) were considered as significant connections, and positively correlated values were configured as weighted undirected networks. Finally, the two network measures of local efficiency and modularity were calculated using the Brain Connectivity Toolbox (Rubinov and Sporns 2010) in Matlab R2017a.

Behavioral analysis

Freezing behavior was scored offline using the immobility function by Noldus EthoVision XT 13 (parameters: processing and detection method gray scaling, immobility threshold below 1.5%, mobility averaging interval 10, erosion and dilation filters [erosion by 4 pixels, then dilation by 2 pixels]). In the fear conditioning (T1) experiment, the percent time spent freezing to the CSs was measured in the 28 s before the shock was delivered. Only the last of the three CSs was used in the statistical analysis, as it reflects the result of conditioning. The CS freezing response in the retrieval test (T3) consists of the first 30 s of CS presentation. Here, only the first CS was entered into statistical analysis, since unlike the MRI groups, the Beh Ext group only received one CS; further, later CS presentations are confounded by potential re-extinction. Statistical analyses were then implemented in R version 4.1.2 (2021 November 1). Bootstrapped ANOVA was conducted with within-subject factor time (T1 and T3) and between-subject factor group. Post-hoc analyses were implemented using Kruskal–Wallis rank sum test and pairwise comparisons using bootstrapped estimates. Additionally, to provide conventional frequentist p-values for T3 group differences, we conducted Dunn’s post hoc comparisons following the Kruskal–Wallis test, controlling multiplicity with Holm’s adjustment.

Data exclusion

MRI data of the following runs had to be excluded due to imaging artifacts such as inner ear cavity, ghosting (MRI Ext: n = 1 [T4 rest2], MRI No Shock: n = 4 [T0 rest1, T2 vis stim2, T2 rest1, T2 rest2]) and technical malfunction of x gradient (MRI No Ext: n = 3 [entire T4 measurement, T4 vis stim1, T4 rest2]). Behavioral data outliers were identified as exceeding 3*IQR (Tuckey 1977) and excluded (n = 2).

Results

Extinction can be achieved during fMRI under light sedation

The experimental design used to demonstrate extinction in fMRI is shown in Fig. 1A. The only difference between the MRI Ext and the MRI No Ext groups is that, at the extinction day T2 inside the scanner, the MRI Ext group was exposed to an extinction session while the MRI No Ext group was not. The major difference between the MRI Ext and the Beh Ext groups is that extinction took place inside MRI under sedation in the former (ABC design, each experimental session in a different context) and outside MRI in the absence of sedation in the latter group (ACC design with extinction at T2, and retrieval testing at T3 taking place in the same context). Conditioned freezing responses (CRs) to the last visual CS presented outside MRI during the conditioning day and the first CS presented at test are shown in Fig. 1B (for framewise freezing of exemplary animals see Suppl. Fig. 1).

Bootstrapped ANOVA after outlier removal (n = 2 at 3*IQR) with between-subject factor group (MRI Ext, MRI No Ext, Beh Ext) and within-subject factor day (T1, T3) revealed a significant group by day interaction (F(1,60) = 12.7, P < 0.001; group: F(1,60) = 3.8, P = 0.04 and day: F(1,60) = 55.6, P < 0.001, n = 62). The interaction was characterized by a significant group effect at test on day T3 (Kruskal–Wallis rank sum test, χ2 = 17.03, df = 2, P < 0.001) in the absence of a group effect during conditioning on day T1 (χ2 = 1.43, df = 2, P = 0.49). Post hoc pairwise comparisons using bootstrapped estimates at test on day T3 showed that both the MRI Ext group and the Beh Ext group exhibited significantly reduced CRs compared to the MRI No Ext group (MRI Ext vs. MRI No Ext: median diff = −12.30, 95% HPD [−20.08, −2.67]; Beh Ext vs. MRI No Ext: median diff = −36.7, 95% HPD [−24.64, −46.44]). Nevertheless, the reduction in CRs at the test was more pronounced in the Beh Ext group compared to the MRI Ext group (median diff = −24.60, 95% HPD [−8.06, −36.45]). For completeness, nonparametric post hoc tests at T3 (Dunn with Holm correction) yielded: MRI Ext vs MRI No Ext p(Holm) = 0.03; Beh Ext vs MRI No Ext p(Holm) < 0.001; Beh Ext vs MRI Ext p(Holm) = 0.03. The time interval of 3 to 8 d between conditioning and extinction (see Methods) did not influence freezing at T3 (MRI Ext: ρ = 0.38, P = 0.10; Beh Ext: ρ = −0.22, P = 0.31; pooled: ρ = 0.15, P = 0.33). This suggests that indeed extinction of fear in the rat is achievable in fMRI in a lightly sedated state.

In the MRI No Shock group, which was subjected to the identical experimental paradigm as the MRI Ext group except those animals never received a US during conditioning (Suppl. Fig. 2A), CS-induced freezing remained well below a median of 50% CS time both at conditioning and test (Suppl. Fig. 2B). This confirms that this group can be used as a nonconditioned comparison group to MRI Ext, in order to isolate fMRI activity specifically related to fear expression and extinction learning during CS presentation at T2 in the MRI Ext animals.

Controlled brain state is characterized by responsiveness to sensory stimulation

Our motivation to use light medetomidine sedation in our experimental pipeline is that this will establish a controlled brain state which should facilitate extinction because it guarantees a good trade-off between the vigilance needed for neural stimulus processing and learning (Aedo-Jury et al. 2020; Becq et al. 2020) and the requirement for immobilization. In agreement with a stimulus-responsive brain state, visual stimulation (vis stim) in the MRI groups MRI Ext, MRI No Ext, and MRI No Shock during MRI sessions T0 and T2 with the 30-s flickering blue light (which became the CS during conditioning on day T1 in MRI Ext and MRI No Ext groups; cf. Fig. 1A, Suppl. Fig. 2A) induced robust activation of the superior colliculus and the visual thalamus during all stimulation runs (for averaged visual time courses of all runs, see Suppl. Fig. 3). Only in MRI session T4, where only a subset of animals was scanned, and group sizes were smaller, supra-threshold collicular-thalamic activation could not be observed in the MRI No Ext and MRI No Shock groups. Resting-state network supports the feasibility of establishing a controlled brain state

In our design, visual stimulation is accompanied by task-free resting-state fMRI measurements (“rest” runs in Fig. 1A). Combined ICA of all resting-state runs in all animals in the MRI groups identified 15 distinct components, most prominently in cingulate and retrosplenial cortical regions that are also part of the canonical DMN (Fig. 4). This resting-state pattern is qualitatively markedly different from a pattern of undifferentiated cortex-wide activation characteristic of the deeply anesthetized slow-wave state (Schwalm et al. 2017; Aedo-Jury et al. 2020) but instead resembles a “persistent brain state” observed earlier by us under medetomidine sedation (Aedo-Jury et al. 2020).

The persistent state is characterized by a small-world network functional connectivity architecture with high modularity and local efficiency (Aedo-Jury et al. 2020). Modularity is a graph-analytic quantification of the strength of network division into modules; local efficiency represents how efficiently a network exchanges information. For each MRI day, modularity and local efficiency values (Fig. 5) were tested for effects of group and, where applicable, time (rest1 and 2 at T2 and T4). There were no group differences at T0 (Entry-MRI) and T2 (Extinction) for either metric; however, at T4 (Exit-MRI), where we scanned fewer animals, the MRI No Shock group had lower modularity values than the other groups (mixed ANOVA effects of group in modularity: T0: F(2,56) = 1.98, P = 0.15; T2: F(2,55) = 1.12, P = 0.33; T4: F(2,31) = 3.87, P = 0.03; in local efficiency: T0: F(2,56) = 2.84, P = 0.09; T2: F(2,55) = 0.34, P = 0.72; T4: F(2,31) = 1.75, P = 0.19). Exploratory analysis: Extinction learning is associated with activation of the ventral HC

Above behavioral results show that animals in the MRI Ext group were able to process the threat value of the visual CS and to learn that the CS is now safe. Our fMRI extinction paradigm, involving an extended number of CS presentations, is not primarily designed to study stimulus-driven activation or deactivation of brain regions during extinction itself. We nevertheless also explored visual stimulus-induced fMRI activity related to fear memory retrieval and/or extinction learning during CS presentation in the extinction session at T2. Specifically, we made use of the MRI No Shock group, in which the visual stimulus had not been associated with the US at the preceeding T1 session. We thus searched for greater stimulus-induced activation in the MRI Ext group than in the MRI No Shock group.

When modeling CS onsets (with an event-related regressor), we observed activation in the right ventral HC during vis stim2 (contrast MRI Ext > MRI No Shock) at a liberal threshold of P < 0.001 uncorrected (Fig. 6). When modeling the whole CS duration (30 s, with a boxcar regressor), we instead observed activation in the left ventral HC during vis stim2 (contrast MRI Ext > MRI No Shock; P < 0.001 uncorrected; see all activation peaks in Table 2).

Given the activation only appeared late during extinction, it could be interpreted as encoding the newly learned safety of the CS, an interpretation that is also in line with the documented role of the ventral HC in fear extinction (Sierra-Mercado et al. 2011; Cleren et al. 2013; Rosas-Vidal et al. 2014). At the voxel level, small-volume-corrected within-session contrasts in extinction-related ROIs did not reach significance in either the boxcar or event-related model. Exploratory selected extinction-related ROI-level summaries of within-session change (Δ = vis stim2–vis stim1) highlighted late-phase increases specific to extinction. In the event-related model, Δ was larger in MRI Ext than MRI No Shock (Fig. 7) in HC (MRI Ext mean = 0.217; MRI No Shock mean = −0.065; Wilcoxon rank-sum P = 0.044) and IL (0.150 vs −0.074; P = 0.041). Within MRI Ext, greater late-phase response tracked better extinction: IL vis stim2 as well as HC vis stim2 alone (but both not in vis stim1) showed a robust negative association with T3 freezing (IL: ρ = −0.41, one-tailed P = 0.036, HC: ρ = −0.56, one-tailed P = 0.0049). Together, these effects implicate late-phase IL–HC engagement in encoding the emerging safety of the CS during extinction. Discussion

Our results suggest that the presented extinction protocol involving fMRI during the unreinforced presentation of a visual CS as well as during stimulus-free periods in a controlled, sedated brain state allows for imaging brain activity occurring during, as well as before and after, extinction learning. The paradigm is thus suitable for the neurobiological investigation of extinction learning and extinction memory consolidation processes, using fine-grained analysis of learning signals, multivariate representational patterns, and network re-organization.

The following pieces of evidence justify this conclusion: one, fear-conditioned animals being presented with conditioned stimuli (CSs) during fMRI (MRI Ext group, day T2) show significantly reduced CRs during a retrieval test conducted after scanning (day T3) relative to fear-conditioned animals not seeing the CS during scanning (MRI No Ext group). This means that the threat value of the CSs must have been retrieved and processed during the extinction session in the sedated state and that new learning (of the safety of the CS) must have occurred. Hence, wakefulness is not required for extinction, although the relatively stronger decline of CRs in the Beh Ext group indicates that wakefulness may lead to more pronounced extinction learning and/or memory consolidation. Two, visual CS presentation in the scanner both before (T0) and after conditioning (T2) induces a robust and reproducible BOLD fMRI signal in subcortical visual areas (superior colliculus, thalamus). The robustness of visual stimulus responsiveness is such that the MRI animal groups do not differ before and after conditioning and that changes within these two sessions (T0, T2) are limited, permitting unconfounded group comparisons and analysis of within-session extinction effects. Three, the analysis of resting-state measurements in the sedated state reveals resting-state networks with small-world connectivity features (modularity, local efficiency) that parallel features of the human resting state and are clearly distinguishable from a slow-wave state as observed under deep anesthesia (Wang et al. 2010; Seitzman et al. 2019; Aedo-Jury et al. 2020). We can assume that the successful induction and maintenance of this so-called persistent state in our experimental pipeline is a facilitatory condition for the observed sensory and emotional stimulus processing and learning and memory formation. Four, the persistent state is stable and does not differ between groups across the T0 and T2 imaging sessions (but see T4, with fewer animals), and it does not differ between groups from run to run, whenever an imaging session contained several runs (T2, T4). Together with the comparable stability of visual stimulus responsiveness, this indicates that our pipeline involves the generation of a highly controlled and reproducible brain state that apparently constitutes a good experimental condition for imaging fear extinction. A particular advantage of this experimental approach is that we can not only image the neural response to a few CSs but to many repeated CS presentations and that we can also image potential nonstimulus-driven consolidation and network re-organization processes that may occur over extended time intervals after extinction learning (cf. Haaker et al. 2013; Gerlicher et al. 2018). (Results of these analyses will be presented elsewhere.) Five, exploratory analysis of differences in CS responses between MRI Ext and MRI No Shock animals yield interpretable bilateral signals in a brain area previously implicated in extinction (ventral HC). Complementing this, ROI-level summaries point to late-phase engagement of HC and IL, consistent with their joint role in encoding the emerging safety of the CS. This suggests that the character of the neural processes underlying extinction learning in our paradigm may have some similarities with awake extinction.

The following limitations of our approach need to be mentioned. Visual stimulus responsiveness and modularity at T4 were no longer equal between groups. The observed group differences may indicate that very long-term fear and extinction memory effects may be difficult to study in this paradigm. Clearly also, we cannot claim that the nature of the extinction learning and memory processes that we here observe is absolutely identical to what would occur in the awake state. So, extinction was less complete in the MRI Ext group than in awake animals, and the analysis of CS-induced regional activations and deactivations during extinction revealed moderate signals that did not extend to the entire set of brain areas typically reported in human imaging experiments (Fullana et al. 2016; Fullana et al. 2018). Moreover, the observed activation pattern in the visual cortex suggests that there might be weak visual cortex involvement that quickly habituates (within trial), yet we found consistent activation in the superior colliculus, which has been shown to be integral in detecting visual threats and orienting to visual cues (Barnes et al. 1970), indicating that visual stimuli are still sufficiently processed despite the minimal involvement of the visual cortex. This is in line with recent reports that visual cortical and subcortical pathways can be differentially recruited under light stimulation (Gil et al. 2024, 2025), showing that the superior colliculus is not a passive relay but an active hub with intrinsic dynamics, raising the possibility that subcortical dominance under sedation limits the cortical processing normally supporting extinction. Such a mechanism could contribute to the less complete extinction seen in the MRI Ext group relative to behavioral extinction. Lastly, we also have to acknowledge the potential variability in HRF across different brain regions and the limitation that the utilized HRF derived from the primary somatosensory cortex may not perfectly capture whole-brain activity in the rat brain. In a previous study (Schwalm et al. 2017), using the same imaging hardware, we also compared a canonical HRF with an HRF derived directly from rat data, finding minimal differences in activation patterns. This suggests that although regional variability may exist, the primary somatosensory HRF likely provides a reasonable approximation for whole-brain activity in the rat. Notwithstanding these limitations, the paradigm is the first to our knowledge that allows us to image, at the whole-brain level, extinction learning and memory processes longitudinally over extended time periods in a controlled experimental state in the rodent. We expect that this technological advance will be of high value for translational research in the field of conditioning. We posit that the method will also be of use in other areas of research that require controlled long-term imaging sensory and emotional processing and learning. Future studies will also be able to combine our paradigm with other manipulations, such as pharmacology, optogenetics, or pharmacogenetics that allow for investigating causal effects.
