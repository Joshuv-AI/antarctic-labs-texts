Targeting NUF2 suppresses gastric cancer progression through G2/M phase arrest and apoptosis induction
Authors: Bo Long, Huinian Zhou, Lixia Xiao, Xiangyan Jiang, Jian Li, Zhijian Ma, Na He, Wei Xin, Boya Zhang, Xiaoqin Zhu, Zeyuan Yu, Zuoyi Jiao, Yuanyuan Ji
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Gastric cancer (GC), a malignant tumor with poor prognosis, is one of the leading causes of cancer-related deaths worldwide; consequently, identifying novel therapeutic targets is crucial for its corresponding treatment. NUF2, a component of the NDC80 kinetochore complex, promotes cancer progression in multiple malignancies. Therefore, this study aimed to explore the potential of NUF2 as a therapeutic target to inhibit GC progression.Methods:Clinical samples were obtained from patients who underwent radical resection of GC at Lanzhou University Second Hospital from 2016 to 2021. Cell count assays, colony formation assays, and cell-derived xenotransplantation (CDX) models were used to determine the effects of NUF2 on GC progression. Flow cytometry was used to detect the effect of NUF2 or quercetin on cell cycle progression and apoptosis. A live-cell time-lapse imaging assay was performed to determine the effect of NUF2 on the regulation of mitotic progression. Transcriptomics was used to investigate the NUF2-associated molecular mechanisms. Virtual docking and microscale thermophoresis were used to identify NUF2 inhibitors. Finally, CDX, organoid, and patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models were used to examine the efficacy of the NUF2 inhibitor in GC.Results:NUF2 expression was significantly increased in GC and was negatively correlated with prognosis. The deletion of NUF2 suppressed GC progression both in vivo and in vitro. NUF2 significantly regulated the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathway, promoted G2/M phase transition, and inhibited apoptosis in GC cells. Additionally, quercetin was identified as a selective NUF2 inhibitor with low toxicity that significantly suppressed tumor growth in GC cells, organoids, CDX, and PDX models.Conclusions:Collectively, NUF2-mediated G2/M phase transition and apoptosis inhibition promoted GC progression; additionally, NUF2 inhibitors exhibited potent anti-GC activity. This study provides a new strategy for targeting NUF2 to suppress GC progression in clinical settings.Keywords: NUF2, Gastric cancer, Quercetin, Patient-derived xenograft, Organoid

Introduction

Gastric cancer (GC) is one of the most common gastrointestinal cancers worldwide, ranking fourth and fifth in terms of morbidity and mortality, respectively.[1] GC treatment has made great progress in recent decades[2]; however, the 5-year overall survival (OS) of patients with GC remains <30%, with the prognosis being worse in patients with unresectable and locally advanced GC.[3,4] Furthermore, there is a lack of effective GC-targeted agents. Only approximately one-fifth of patients with human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2)-positive GC can receive comprehensive treatment, such as trastuzumab.[5] Therefore, identifying novel therapeutic targets and effective GC inhibitors is of particular importance. NDC80 kinetochore complex (NUF2) localizes to the outer plate of the kinetochore, where it connects with microtubules and plays important roles in cell division, chromosome segregation, mitotic kinetochore organization, and spindle assembly checkpoints.[6,7,8] As a component of the highly conserved essential kinetochore-associated NDC80 complex, NUF2 is essential for the organization of stable microtubule-kinetochore attachments and effective kinetochore tension, which is necessary for accurate sister chromatid separation during mitosis.[9,10,11,12] Defects in chromosomal segregation during mitosis can cause genomic instability, an important characteristic of tumor progression.[13] Additionally, NUF2 depletion restricts stable kinetochore-microtubule attachment, thereby reducing kinetochore tension and resulting in mitotic Henrietta Lacks (HeLa) cell apoptosis.[14]NUF2 has been recognized as a valuable prognostic biomarker that can predict poor prognosis in various cancers.[15,16,17,18]NUF2 drives the occurrence and development of renal cancer, breast cancer, and melanoma by promoting mechanisms such as cell cycle, epigenetic activation, and stemness.[19,20,21,22] Several clinical trials have demonstrated that a NUF2 peptide vaccine combined with other peptide vaccines is well tolerated and elicits a strong T-cell response in patients with lung, advanced biliary tract, and advanced head and neck cancers, thereby improving OS.[23,24,25] However, the specific role of NUF2 in GC progression and the effects of its corresponding targeted inhibition have not been established.

This study aimed to identify NUF2 as a driver of GC progression and establish the effects of a NUF2 inhibitor in human GC organoid and patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models.

Methods

Reagents and antibodies

All antibodies used are listed as follows: NUF2 (orb41076) for immunohistochemistry (IHC) was obtained from Biorbyt (Cambridge, England), NUF2 (ab176556), and Ki67 (ab16667) were obtained from Abcam (Shanghai, China), Glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH, 10494-1-AP), B-cell lymphoma 2 (BCL-2, 1212789-1-AP), Bcl-2-associated X protein (BAX, 50599-2-Ig), Cell division cycle protein A2 (Cyclin-A2, 18202-1-AP), Cell division cycle protein B1 (Cyclin-B1, 55004-1-AP), Cyclin-Dependent Kinase 1 (CDK1, 19532-1-AP), and Cyclin-Dependent Kinase 2 (CDK2, 10122-1-AP) were obtained from Proteintech (Wuhan, China). The inhibitory quercetin (T2174, CAS 117-39-5) was obtained from TOPSCIENCE (Shanghai, China).

RNA sequences

Samples were sent to BGI Genomics Co., Ltd. (Shenzhen, China) for RNA-Seq detection and analysis. The data acquisition instrument included an ultra-performance liquid chromatograph and tandem mass spectrometer (Mgiseq2000, China). The differentially expressed genes were selected with a P-value <0.05; then, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis was conducted for differentially expressed mRNAs.

Clinical samples

In accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, all patient specimens were obtained from Lanzhou University Second Hospital with the approval of the Medical Ethics Review Board (No. 2019A-124) and written informed consent. Sixteen pairs of GC and adjacent normal tissues were used for the microarray assay; eight pairs of fresh tissues were collected within 30 min post-operation and stored in liquid nitrogen. Overall, 442 paired specimens were formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded, and fabricated into tissue microarrays (TMAs) for IHC. Additionally, 374 paired specimens with follow-up data were included in the clinicopathological and survival analyses. Gastric tissue more than 5 cm away from the tumor was defined as normal tissue. These samples were obtained from patients who underwent radical resection of GC at Lanzhou University Second Hospital from 2016 to 2021. None of these patients have received preoperative chemotherapy or immunotherapy.

IHC analysis

IHC and score evaluation criteria were based on a previous study.[26]

Quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was obtained using TRIzol reagent, and complementary DNA (cDNA) was obtained through reverse transcription using a reverse transcription kit (Takara, Japan). SYBR Green dye (Takara) was used for qRT-PCR amplification and detection (CFX96; Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). The primer sequences are as follows: NUF2, (forward [FW] 5′-TACCATTCAGCAATTTAGTTACT-3,′ reverse [RW] 5′-TAGAATATCAGCAGTCTCAA AG-3′; and β-actin, FW 5′-CTGGGACGACATGGAGAAAA-3′ and RW 5′-AAGGAAGGATGGAAGAGTGC-3′ (Takara). The ΔΔCT method was used to calculate relative mRNA expression.

Western blotting (WB)

Cells or tissues, after being ground, were collected and lysed on ice with radioimmunoprecipitation assay buffer and a protease inhibitor cocktail. Equal amounts of total protein were separated using 12% sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and transferred onto a polyvinylidene fluoride membrane (Bio-Rad). After blocking with 5% skimmed milk at room temperature for 1 h, the membrane was incubated with the primary antibody overnight at 4°C. After washing with Tris-buffered saline with Tween (TBST), the membrane was incubated with the corresponding rabbit or mouse secondary antibody (1:20,000) for 2 h at room temperature.

Cell lines and cell culture

All cells were validated by short-tandem repeat testing. HEK-293T, gastric epithelial cells (GES)-1, HGC27, AGS, and NCI-N87 cells were obtained from the Institute of Basic Medical Sciences at the Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences (Beijing, China). MKN45 and KATO-III cells were purchased from Guangzhou Saiku Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (China). Mycoplasma testing was conducted using a PCR Mycoplasma Kit G2-39 (Abcam). GES and AGS cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (Gibco Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA, USA) with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Gibco Life Technologies), MKN45, KATO-III, HGC27, and NCI-N87 cells were cultured in Roswell Park Memorial Institute 1640 medium (Gibco Life Technologies) containing 10% FBS at 37°C, and 5% CO2. Cells were passaged by trypsinization when they reached 80% confluence.

Lentiviral infection

For NUF2 knockout, we designed a NUF2 guide RNA (gRNA) (FW 5′-CACCGCTGCCTGCCTTCATGTCAGT-3′; RW 5′-AAACACTGACATGAAGGCAGGCAGc-3′) and used the Gibson system to ligate the gRNA to the Lenti-CRISPR plasmid. The constructed plasmids and packaging plasmids pSPAX2 and pMD2G were transfected with polyethyleneimine (1:1:0.5) into 293T cells to acquire the lentivirus. We calculated the multiplicity of infection of the concentrated virus, and selected the appropriate amount of virus to transfect AGS, HGC27, and NCI-N87 cell lines with high endogenous NUF2 expression; we obtained stable NUF2 knockout cell lines through puromycin screening and WB verification. For NUF2 overexpression, AGS, HGC27, and NCI-N87 were infected with lentiviral polyethylene (multiplicity of infection = 20), and screening was conducted using puromycin 72 h after infection. Lentivirus of NUF2 overexpression was purchased from Shanghai Genechem Co., Ltd. (China).

Cell viability and colony formation assay

To assess cell viability, approximately 3 × 103 cells were seeded in a 96-well plate overnight for attachment; these were then treated with different concentrations of quercetin (0 μmol/L, 0.1 μmol/L, 1 μmol/L, 10 μmol/L, 100 μmol/L, and 200 μmol/L) for 72 h. Cell viability was estimated using the CellTiter 96® AQueous One Solution Cell Proliferation Assay (MTS) Kit (Promega, USA). To assess cell viability assay using high-throughput microscopy, GC cells were established with NUF2 knockout or quercetin treatment for 72 h; cell number was counted automatically every four hours and quantified using Harmony software (version 4.9, USA). For the cell colony formation assay, 1 × 103 cells were seeded in 35-mm dishes and cultured (Wild-type [WT] vs. NUF2+/+ group: 7 days; WT vs.NUF2-KO group: 14 days). Finally, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min at room temperature; ImageJ software (version 1.54g, USA) was used for cell counting after a 24-h staining with crystal violet. For colony formation assay with quercetin treatment, after 72 h, dosed groups were treated with quercetin at 50 μmol/L in AGS, 30 μmol/L in HGC27, 70 μmol/L in N87 for 72 h, and cultured for 14 days. Colonies were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with 0.05% crystal violet.

Live-cell time-lapse imaging assay

Long-term time-lapse imaging of live cells was performed to understand the effects of NUF2 depletion and quercetin treatment on GC cells during mitosis. Approximately 1 × 103 HGC27 cells were seeded in 96-well plates overnight and were observed using a live high-throughput imaging system (Operetta CLS; PerkinElmer, USA). The cells in each well were imaged at 20× magnification every 30 min for 120 h across 18 fields of view.

Digital phase contrast (DPC) assays

Cells per well were seeded in 96-well plates and incubated overnight for cell attachment. The 96-well plates were placed in an Operetta CLSTM high-content analysis system (PerkinElmer), and the images were taken every 4 h for a total of 72 h. Harmony software (version 4.9, USA) was used to analyze the number of cells.

Flow cytometry (FCM) analysis of cell cycle and apoptosis

Cells were collected after transfection with NUF2-knockout lentivirus or quercetin treatment for 48 h. FCM process was conducted as described by Ma et al.[27] The samples were analyzed using a flow cytometer (BECKMAN CytoFLEX, USA) at a density of 1 × 104 cells. The cell cycle was analyzed using Modify LT V5.0.9 software (version 5.0.9, USA); apoptosis was analyzed using Cytoxpert 2.0 software (Beckman Coulter Inc. USA).

Tumor xenograft experiments

All animal experiments were approved by the Ethics Committee of the Second Hospital of Lanzhou University (No. D2022-312). Six- to eight-week-old female BABL/C nude mice, obtained from the Beijing Charles River Company (China), were used for the NCI-N87 cell xenograft model to assess the tumor-suppressive effect of NUF2 depletion or quercetin treatment in vivo. Approximately 4 × 106 NCI-N87 cells were suspended in 100 μL phosphate-buffered saline and 100 μL Matrigel (Corning, USA) were subcutaneously injected into mice. Tumors were imaged using an in vivo Imaging System (VIEWORK, Korea).

To evaluate the role of NUF2 in tumor growth, tumor volume (1/2 × L × W2) and body weight were measured every 2 days. After 24 days, the mice were euthanized to harvest the subcutaneous tumors. After obtaining tumor weight data, the tumor was analyzed via WB and IHC.

To evaluate the anti-cancer efficacy of quercetin in a tumor xenograft model, mice with similar tumor volume (70–100 mm3) were randomly divided into three groups (n = 8 each): vehicle, quercetin (40 mg·kg–1·day–1), and quercetin (80 mg·kg–1·day–1). Quercetin treatment was produced by dissolving 55% quercetin in 200 μL vehicle, containing 0.9% NaCl, 5% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), 10% TWEEN80, and 30% PEG300. Mice were intraperitoneally injected with the corresponding injection daily; tumor volume and body weight were measured daily. At day 18, mice were euthanized to harvest the subcutaneous tumors. After obtaining tumor weight data, the tumor was analyzed via WB and IHC.

Human GC PDX experiments

Four PDX models from four different human GCs were established and propagated, according to Vilgelm et al.[28] A PDX model with high NUF2 expression was selected for this study using pathological analysis. When the tumor volume reached 70–100 mm3, non-obese diabetic severe combined immunodeficiency gamma (NSG) mice with similar tumor volumes were randomly divided into two groups (n = 5 for each): vehicle and quercetin (80 mg·kg–1·day–1). Mice were intraperitoneally injected with 200 μL of the vehicle or quercetin daily; tumor volume and body weight were measured daily. On day 15, the PDX model mice were euthanized to harvest the subcutaneous tumors. After obtaining tumor weight data, the tumor was analyzed via WB and IHC.

Human GC organoid culturing and treatment

We established organoid cultures from GC patients, according to Seidlitz et al.[29] Quercetin treatment was conducted five days after seeding at different concentrations (0 μmol/L, 10 μmol/L, or 50 μmol/L). The concentration of quercetin was empirically based on cellular research in this experiment. Organoid diameter was measured every three days for 12 consecutive days under a 40× objective microscope. After 12 days, proliferation and viability were measured using the CellTiter 96 AQueous One Solution MTS kit (Promega, USA).

Microscale thermophoresis (MST) assay

MST assays were conducted on a Monolith NT.115 system (NanoTemper Technologies GmbH, Germany) to quantify the interaction of quercetin and NUF2. NUF2 (Proteintech, #Ag8625) was labeled with His-Tag Labeling Kit RED-tris-NTA 2nd Generation (NanoTemper Technologies, #MO-L018), followed by incubation with different concentrations of quercetin. Samples were added to monolithic capillaries (NanoTemper Technologies, #MO-K022) followed by MST analysis. The obtained values were normalized and plotted. Dissociation constants were determined using a one-point model to fit the curve.

Statistical analysis

SPSS version 26.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA) was used for statistical analyses and graphing. GraphPad Prism 8.3 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA) was used for data analysis and graph construction. Student’s t-test was conducted for the comparison of means across groups, with results expressed as mean ± standard deviation. P-values <0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

NUF2 expression increases in GC and negatively correlates with prognosis

The mRNA microarray analysis demonstrated that NUF2 expression was 5.2× higher in GC tissues than in normal tissues [Figure 1A]. Gene Expression Profiling Interactive Analysis (GEPIA) was used to investigate NUF2 mRNA expression in GC. NUF2 mRNA expression was elevated in GC tissues compared to that in para-carcinoma tissues [Figure 1B]. Next, qRT-PCR and immunoblotting assays detected NUF2 levels in eight GC tumors compared to paired adjacent normal tissue controls; NUF2 mRNA and protein levels were upregulated in tumor tissues [Figure 1C,D]. We conducted large-sample clinical validation of 442 paired cancer and normal tissue samples from GC patients using IHC; a representative image is shown in Figure 1E. IHC analysis indicated that NUF2 is highly expressed in GC tissues and is associated with poor tumor differentiation and a more advanced tumor, node, and metastasis stage [Figure 1F–H, Supplementary Table 1, http://links.lww.com/CM9/B884]. Moreover, Kaplan–Meier survival analysis showed that, compared with low NUF2 expression, patients with elevated NUF2 expression in GC tissues possessed poorer OS [Figure 1I]. Overall, this strongly suggested that NUF2 is a potential prognostic biomarker for GC and may be a promising therapeutic target for GC treatment.

NUF2 depletion suppresses GC progression by inducing G2/M cell cycle arrest and apoptosis

Initially, we selected NUF2 high-expression GC cells (AGS, HGC27, and NCI-N87) to generate NUF2-deletion or NUF2-overexpression cell lines [Supplementary Figure 1A–D, http://links.lww.com/CM9/B884]. Colony formation and DPC assays were performed to investigate the role of NUF2 in GC cell proliferation. NUF2 overexpression promoted tumor proliferation, whereas NUF2 deletion inhibited tumor proliferation in these cells [Figure 2A–D]. We performed transwell and wound scratch assays to evaluate the role of NUF2 in GC cell invasion and migration. NUF2 deficiency inhibited tumor invasion and migration in AGS and HGC27 cells [Supplementary Figure 1E–H, http://links.lww.com/CM9/B884]. To further study the role of NUF2 in GC progression in vivo, 4 × 106 WT or NUF2-knockout luciferase-expressing NCI-N87 cells were injected subcutaneously into female nude mice and changes in tumor volume were monitored. Compared to the WT group, reduced tumor volume and weight and lower luciferase signals were observed in the NUF2-knockout group [Figure 2E–H; Supplementary Figure 1I, http://links.lww.com/CM9/B884]. In addition, IHC revealed that NUF2 protein levels were lower in NUF2-knockout excised tumors than in WT tumors [Figure 2I]; additionally, NUF2 depletion resulted in reduced expression of the proliferation marker Ki67 in vivo [Figure 2J].

NUF2 is critical for the formation of stable kinetochore-microtubule attachments and kinetochore tension during mitosis.[14] To elucidate the molecular mechanisms by which NUF2 promotes GC progression, we evaluated the role of NUF2 in cell cycle regulation. FCM results demonstrated that NUF2 depletion causes G2/M phase cell cycle arrest. The percentage of cells in the G2/M phase after NUF2 depletion increased from 13.65%, 16.70%, and 17.12% to 24.22%, 30.15%, and 21.91% in AGS, HGC27, and NCI-N87 cells, respectively [Figure 3A]. Because of the important regulatory role of cyclins and cyclin-dependent kinases (CDKs) in cell cycle regulation,[30] we also investigated protein expression changes. WB results showed that NUF2 depletion decreased Cyclin-B1 and CDK1 expression, while the Cyclin-A2 and CDK2 expression remained unchanged [Figure 3B]. Since cell cycle arrest can precede cell apoptosis, we used Annexin V/7-Aminoactinomycin D (Annexin V/7AAD) labeling and FCM to determine whether NUF2 depletion could induce apoptosis, which could lead to the inhibition of GC cell proliferation and tumor growth in vitro and in vivo. Compared to the WT controls, both early- and late-stage apoptosis was increased in NUF2-knockout AGS, HGC27, and NCI-N87 cells [Figure 3C]. NUF2 depletion increased BAX protein levels and decreased BCL-2 levels in vitro [Figure 3D]. Furthermore, IHC analysis showed that Terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP Nick End Labeling (TUNEL) expression was higher in NUF2-knockout cell-derived tumors than in controls, indicating that NUF2 depletion promotes GC apoptosis in vivo [Figure 3E]. To further understand the role of NUF2 in cell cycle regulation, we performed live-cell time-lapse imaging and found that whereas mitotic progression proceeded normally in WT HGC27 cells, NUF2-knockout HGC27 cells were restricted in metaphase, eventually resulting in cell death [Figure 3F and Video 1]. These findings were further validated in AGS and NCI-N87 cells [Supplementary Figure 2A,B, http://links.lww.com/CM9/B884; Videos 2 and 3]. To explore the mechanism of NUF2 on the cell cycle and apoptosis, we conducted transcriptome analysis of NUF2-knockout and WT AGS cells; KEGG analysis showed that mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling was the most significant NUF2-upregulated pathway [Figure 3G, H]. In addition, activation of the MAPK pathway was significantly associated with the regulation of the cell cycle and apoptosis.[31] We detected the effect of NUF2 deletion on MAPK activation by immunoblotting; NUF2 deletion decreased phospho-ERK (p-ERK) and phospho-MEK (p-MEK) expression [Supplementary Figure 2C, http://links.lww.com/CM9/B884]. Our results demonstrate that NUF2 promotes GC cell proliferation in vitro and in vivo; additionally, NUF2 depletion induces G2/M phase cell cycle arrest and apoptosis, which are related to MAPK signaling.

Screening of small-molecule inhibitors targeting NUF2

Overall, NUF2 depletion was determined to inhibit GC cell proliferation and tumor growth, suggesting that NUF2 may be a potential therapeutic target. To identify small-molecule NUF2 inhibitors, we screened the Traditional Chinese Medicine database (TCMD) using virtual docking technology [Supplementary Figure 3A, http://links.lww.com/CM9/B884]. The virtual screening results, predicted by Toxicity Prediction by Komputer Assisted Technology (TOPKAT) software, showed that quercetin, which has a flavonoid structure, could act as a NUF2 small-molecule inhibitor (predicted binding affinity is –7.3 kcal) with low toxicity [Figure 4A, B]. The potential binding sites for quercetin and NUF2 were analyzed by molecular docking using AutoDock vina 1.1.2, which indicated that the quercetin hydroxyl group could form a hydrogen bond with Leu4 on NUF2; additionally, the two quercetin benzene rings could form hydrophobic interactions with Phe6, Phe130, and Ala133 in NUF2 [Figure 4C]; these interactions were determined to enable quercetin–NUF2 binding in the NUF2 protein pocket. Molecular dynamics simulations were performed using the AmberTools 20 software package; the root mean square deviation values of the protein backbone were used to analyze the dynamic stability of the models. The NUF2 protein structure and quercetin binding were stable during this simulation [Figure 4D]. To further verify the direct interaction and affinity between quercetin and NUF2, an MST assay was performed, which revealed that the binding affinity of quercetin for NUF2 was 112 nmol/L [Figure 4E]. We also conducted cytotoxicity testing in vitro, and found that quercetin exhibited low toxicity in GES-1 cells, but had a significant inhibitory effect on AGS, HGC27, and NCI-N87 cell proliferation [Figure 4F]. The half-maximal inhibitory concentrations (IC50) of quercetin in GES-1, AGS, HGC27, and NCI-N87 cells were 327.31 μmol/L, 41.61 μmol/L, 26.76 μmol/L, and 67.67 μmol/L, respectively [Figure 4G]. To determine whether there is an off-target effect, we analyzed NUF2-depleted AGS, HGC27, and NCI-N87 GC cells compared to their corresponding control cell lines. The IC50 of quercetin in control AGS, HGC27, and NCI-N87 cells was 42.82 μmol/L, 29.51 μmol/L, and 62.99 μmol/L, whereas the corresponding IC50 in NUF2 depleted AGS, HGC27, and NCI-N87 cells was 106.85 μmol/L, 86.05 μmol/L, and 410.53 μmol/L, respectively [Figure 4H]. Therefore, the tumor-killing effect of quercetin was significantly reduced in NUF2-depleted GC cells compared to that in WT GC cells. These results indicate that quercetin is a selective NUF2 inhibitor with potent anti-GC activity.

Quercetin inhibits GC progression by inducing G2/M arrest and apoptosis

Given the strong affinity of quercetin for NUF2, we investigated its ability to inhibit GC progression in vitro and in vivo. AGS, HGC27, and NCI-N87 cells were treated with quercetin at the IC50 for each cell type; DMSO was used as a control. We performed colony formation and 5-ethynyl-2′-deoxyuridine (EdU) proliferation assays, and analyzed GC cell growth using high-throughput microscopy. The EdU and DPC assays indicated that AGS cell proliferation in vitro was inhibited after 48-h treatment with 40 μmol/L, 30 μmol/L, and 80 μmol/L quercetin in AGS, HGC27, and N87 cells, respectively [Figure 5A–C]. To confirm the antitumor effect of quercetin against GC in vivo, quercetin was administered to NCI-N87 tumor-bearing mice via intraperitoneal injection once daily at low (40 mg/kg) or high (80 mg/kg) doses; tumor growth was inhibited by both doses of quercetin treatment. Compared to the control group, the fluorescence signal intensity, volume change, and tumor weight were inhibited by quercetin; however, there was no significant difference in the body weight loss of the mice in these three groups [Figure 5D–G, Supplementary Figure 3B, http://links.lww.com/CM9/B884]. IHC analysis revealed increased TUNEL expression and reduced Ki67 positivity following treatment with 40 or 80 mg/kg quercetin [Figure 5H, I]. Treatment with quercetin led to G2/M phase cell cycle arrest in AGS, HGC27, and NCI-N87 cells, with 16.70%, 17.02%, and 17.83% of the control cells, compared to 28.76%, 31.19%, and 31.10%, of the corresponding quercetin-treated cells being in G2/M phase [Figure 6A]. Cyclin-B1 and CDK1 levels significantly decreased after quercetin treatment; however, there were no significant changes in Cyclin-A2 and CDK2 levels [Figure 6B]. FCM and WB demonstrated that quercetin treatment resulted in GC cell apoptosis in vitro [Figure 6C]. Additionally, quercetin did not affect NUF2 expression but increased BAX expression and decreased BCL-2 expression. We detected the effect of quercetin on MAPK activation via immunoblotting; 40 μmol/L and 30 μmol/L quercetin in AGS and HGC27 cells, respectively, can decrease p-ERK and p-MEK expression by inhibiting ERK and MEK phosphorylation [Supplementary Figure 4A, http://links.lww.com/CM9/B884]. In addition, live-cell time-lapse imaging demonstrated that mitotic progression of HGC27, AGS, and NCI-N87 cells was restricted at metaphase when cells were treated with quercetin in vitro at their concentration of IC50. These results showed that the NUF2-targeted inhibitor quercetin inhibited GC progression by inducing G2/M arrest and apoptosis [Figure 6E, Supplementary Figure 4B,C, http://links.lww.com/CM9/B884 and Videos 4–6].

Quercetin exhibited anti-cancer activity against GC in a humanized model

Traditional cell culture methods cannot completely simulate human tumor biology.[32] Therefore, we used organoid and PDX models established from tumor tissues of patients with GC to test the efficacy of quercetin. GC organoids cultured with 0 μmol/L, 10 μmol/L, and 50 μmol/L quercetin were assessed under a microscope before quercetin treatment and three, six, and nine days after quercetin administration [Figure 7A]. MTS assays evaluated GC organoid proliferation in cultures with different quercetin concentrations. Overall, the proliferation and volume growth of GC organoids were inhibited with increasing quercetin concentrations [Figure 7B,C]. A PDX mouse model with high NUF2 expression was used to test the therapeutic efficacy of quercetin. After treatment with quercetin at 80 mg·kg–1·day–1 for 15 days, tumor weight was reduced and tumor growth was significantly inhibited compared with those in the control; no significant differences in body weight were observed between the two groups [Figure 7D–G]. IHC was used to analyze Ki67 and TUNEL expression. The Ki67 positivity rate was lower in the quercetin-treated group, demonstrating that quercetin significantly inhibited GC proliferation in vivo [Figure 7H]. Additionally, the expression level of TUNEL increased in tumors following treatment with quercetin at 80 mg·kg–1·day–1 for 15 days, demonstrating that quercetin promotes tumor apoptosis in vivo [Figure 7I]. These results suggest that quercetin could be a potential agent for GC therapy.

Discussion

In this study, we identified NUF2 as a potential therapeutic target against GC progression. Elevated NUF2 expression was determined to promote GC cell proliferation via MAPK pathway–mediated G2/M phase transition and apoptosis inhibition. The NUF2 inhibitor, quercetin, significantly diminished tumor growth in human organoid and PDX models.

NUF2 has been identified as a prognostic factor in various human cancers as it regulates tumor cell proliferation, apoptosis, and the cell cycle.[20,21,22] Specifically, NUF2 depletion results in pro-metaphase arrest and unstable microtubule-kinetochore attachment, leading to G2/M phase arrest and GC cell apoptosis.[33] Lin et al[20] determined that NUF2 is elevated in clear cell renal cell carcinoma and is associated with poor prognosis. Furthermore, NUF2 may act as an oncogene, promoting the proliferation, migration, and invasion of clear cell renal cell carcinoma by activating high mobility group AT-hook 2 (HMGA2) transcription through lysine-specific demethylase 2A (KDM2A)-mediated H3K36me2 di-methylation. In the present study, we demonstrated that NUF2 deletion significantly inhibited GC progression, suggesting that NUF2 contributes to GC malignancy and progression.

MAPKs are highly conserved proteins in mammals, which contain the protein-serine/threonine kinase family, with proteins including extracellular signal-regulated kinases (ERKs), c-Jun N-terminal kinases (JNKs), and p38 MAPKs. The MAPK pathway plays an important role in cell cycle and apoptosis regulation and is an important pathway for cancer progression.[34] Inhibiting cancer cell proliferation by attenuating the MAPK pathway results in apoptosis induction via DNA damage and G2/M phase arrest of the cell cycle.[35] Additionally, Lockhead et al[36] determined that the p38 MAPK activator, cycloheximide, can delay mitotic entry through the G2/M checkpoint. The MAPK pathway regulates apoptosis via multiple cellular mechanisms. The tumor suppressor protein p53 is a well-known transcription factor regulated by the p38 MAPK cascade during apoptosis; additionally, p53–p73 dimerization plays a critical role in the induction of apoptotic cell death.[37] Here, we found that NUF2 depletion significantly inhibited the activation of the MAPK pathway. Hence, NUF2 may facilitate the G2/M phase transition and inhibit apoptosis by activating the MAPK pathway. Nonetheless, further studies are necessary to clarify the specific NUF2-mediated mechanism of MAPK pathway regulation and to determine how this mechanism promotes GC progression.

Several promising strategies targeting NUF2 have been tested in clinical trials. The NUF2-derived 19-amino-acid peptide has been observed to inhibit lung cancer progression by inhibiting NUF2–HEC1 complex formation.[16] Furthermore, a phase I clinical trial determined that NUF2 peptide vaccination therapy extended the survival of patients with castration-resistant prostate cancer via the stimulation of NUF2-specific CD4 helper T cells and activation of cytotoxic T lymphocytes.[38,39] Treatment with several NUF2 peptide vaccines is safe and effective in patients with advanced pancreatic, head, and neck cancers.[24,25] Therefore, we believe that the targeted inhibition of NUF2 may be a potential target for GC. In this study, we identified quercetin as a specific NUF2-targeted inhibitor with potent anti-GC activity. Sundin et al[33] established a NUF2 mutant via the deletion of its entire calponin homology domain (Δ1–142 NUF2); 96% of these mutant cells failed to restore normal chromosome alignment, demonstrating that the functional region may be related to the 1–142 amino acid region of NUF2. Our virtual molecular docking analysis results revealed that the potential binding sites of quercetin to NUF2 are associated with Leu4, Phe6, Phe130, and Ala133; these amino acids are within the 1–142 amino acid region, which is consistent with prior research. Quercetin suppresses tumor growth by arresting the cell cycle and inducing apoptosis; however, its direct target remains unknown. Bioinformatics analyses indicated that there were multiple potential quercetin targets in NCI-N87 GC cells, including proto-oncogene C-Fos (FOS), aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AHR), Jun proto-oncogene (JUN), cytochrome P450 family 1 subfamily A member 1 (CYP1A1), early growth response 1 (EGR1), Fos-related antigen 1 (FOSL1), and wingless-type MMTV integration site family, member 7B (WNT7B).[40] Quercetin could bind to the purified NUF2 protein directly with a high affinity, with a Kd value of 112 nmol/L, and inhibited GC cell proliferation.

Quercetin is a flavonoid compound with a variety of biological activities, including antioxidant, antiallergic, anti-inflammatory, and antiviral properties.[41] Anti-tumor activity of quercetin has been reported in prostate, cervical, lung, colon, and GCs.[41] The mechanisms of quercetin-mediated tumor inhibition, although not completely clear, are associated with cell cycle arrest, apoptosis, and autophagy induction.[42] Additionally, the safety of quercetin has been confirmed by the US Food and Drug Administration, with a recommended daily dose of 1000 mg (most commonly 500 mg); additionally, a phase I dose escalation study demonstrated the safety of quercetin (up to 5 g orally daily).[43,44] A large Swedish population-based study determined that a high daily intake of quercetin reduced the risk of non-cardia gastric adenocarcinoma, particularly in female smokers.[45] In the present study, increasing the therapeutic dose of quercetin from 40 mg/kg to 80 mg/kg inhibited NCI-N87 cell-derived tumor growth in mouse xenografts without affecting body weight. However, the balance between efficacy and toxicity is a major challenge in drug development. Future studies need to further clarify the chemical, pharmacokinetic, and toxicological characteristics of quercetin in the context of GC treatment to optimize the therapeutic conditions of quercetin.

Quercetin has a short half-life and is rapidly cleared from the blood, with its corresponding metabolites appearing in plasma 30 min after ingestion.[46] Additionally, pharmacokinetic studies in rats found that, for both intravenous and oral administration, the first-pass effect of quercetin in the liver and gut resulted in a very low bioavailability of only 5.3%.[47] The quercetin used in our study is a natural product that has not undergone drug modification or nanotechnology embedding; this explains the reason for the high concentration of quercetin used in this study. Improving the bioavailability of quercetin through drug modifications to increase the success rate of clinical transformation will be the focus of future studies. Therefore, we believe that quercetin could be a promising personalized treatment for patients with GC with high NUF2 tumor expression.

In summary, we identified NUF2 as a driver of GC progression and provided compelling evidence for the antitumor activity of the NUF2-targeted inhibitor quercetin. Overall, this study provides a therapeutic strategy that may inhibit GC progression by targeting NUF2.
