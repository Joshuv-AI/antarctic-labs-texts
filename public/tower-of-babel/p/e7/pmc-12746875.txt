Should we go with the flow? Rethinking sperm selection through microfluidics
Authors: Aileen Portugal, Dan I Lebovic
Published: 2025

The latest sibling-oocyte study by Meseguer et al. (1) brings fresh focus to a fundamental question in assisted reproduction: can a more physiologic approach to sperm preparation translate into measurable improvements in embryo development and, ultimately, reproductive success? By comparing a microfluidic sperm-selection chip, the SwimCount Harvester (SCH), with conventional swim-up, the investigators provide convincing evidence of laboratory benefits. The next challenge, and one that remains unanswered, is determining whether these promising laboratory gains ultimately lead to better live-birth outcomes.

Currently, two main types of microfluidic devices are available, each designed to replicate the natural process by which sperm are selected in the female reproductive tract. These systems were developed to minimize the mechanical and oxidative stress associated with conventional sperm-preparation methods, such as density-gradient centrifugation and swim-up, offering a gentler and more physiologic approach. Flow-based systems (such as ZyMot) rely on laminar flow that allows motile sperm to swim across a boundary into a clean chamber, while membrane or obstacle-based systems, like the SCH studied by Meseguer et al. (1), use microchannels or filters that let morphologically normal, progressively motile sperm migrate forward while debris and immotile cells remain behind. Both share the same goal: to isolate the healthiest sperm without centrifugation.

However, does a gentler, more physiologic method of sperm selection actually improve embryo development? Meseguer et al. (1) sought to answer this question by comparing the SCH microfluidic device with the conventional swim-up method. The study’s primary outcome was the usable blastocyst rate per mature oocyte, defined as blastocysts suitable for transfer or vitrification. In their prospective, double-blind, sibling-oocyte design, each ejaculate was divided so that half of a patient’s mature oocytes were inseminated using swim-up–prepared sperm and the other half with SCH-selected sperm. This within-cycle comparison minimizes interpatient variability and provides a rigorous evaluation of competing techniques. The results were consistent: the SCH group produced more usable blastocysts, better morphology, and faster morphokinetics. As expected, sperm processed with SCH showed a statistically significantly lower deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA)-fragmentation index, 2.97% (interquartile range, 1.86–6.72) vs. 5.61% (interquartile range, 2.39–8.74); P=.001. Together, these findings strengthen the biologic plausibility that gentler handling improves sperm integrity and early embryo development.

Yet success in the laboratory raises a new question: does this difference matter clinically? In the Meseguer study (1), the reduction in sperm DNA fragmentation was statistically significant, but the clinical importance of that change remains uncertain. If the threshold of concern for DNA fragmentation is approximately 20% DNA-fragmentation index, lowering an already low value from 6% to 3% is unlikely to alter outcomes. This distinction between statistical and clinical significance may help explain why prior randomized trials in unselected populations have not demonstrated improved implantation or live-birth rates after microfluidic preparation (2, 3). The difference between low and lower fragmentation probably makes little impact on couples whose sperm DNA damage was not clinically concerning to begin with.

Intriguingly, Meseguer et al. (1) provide a glimpse of where the benefit may lie. In their subgroup analysis, the SCH advantage in usable blastocyst yield was greatest among men with baseline DFI≥20% and in cycles involving lower-quality oocytes. This observation aligns with an emerging understanding that the impact of sperm DNA damage depends on the oocyte’s ability to repair it. When the paternal genome is highly fragmented or the oocyte’s repair capacity is limited, such as in advanced maternal age, the gentler microfluidic pathway may have real therapeutic value. What remains untested is whether reducing DFI from high to normal translates into improved reproductive outcomes. The study our field still lacks is a randomized, adequately powered trial limited to high-DFI ejaculates, comparing microfluidic vs. conventional preparation and using live-birth rate per initiated cycle as the primary endpoint.

Viewed within the broader literature, the findings of Meseguer et al. (1) are consistent with prior microfluidic studies and add important confirmation. Quinn et al. (2) first reported a reduction in median DFI from 6% after density-gradient centrifugation to nearly 0% after microfluidic sorting. Later, a randomized trial by Godiwala et al. (3) showed similar improvements in sperm DNA integrity but without corresponding gains in euploidy or live-birth outcomes. The Meseguer study builds on this trajectory, confirming the laboratory benefits of microfluidics but reminding us that improved sperm DNA integrity has yet to translate into better clinical outcomes.

For men with persistently high DFI and repeated in vitro fertilization failure, the clinical alternative has often been testicular sperm extraction, which retrieves sperm with inherently low fragmentation but at considerable cost, discomfort, and potential risk. By contrast, microfluidic devices like SCH can achieve comparable DNA-integrity improvements noninvasively. As Kocur et al. (4) note, avoiding surgical retrieval while preserving the genomic advantages of ejaculated sperm represents a compelling middle ground, simpler, safer, and more accessible. Should future data confirm that this approach improves outcomes in high-DFI cases, microfluidics could become the preferred first-line intervention before surgery is ever considered.

Although live-birth data are still lacking, the laboratory findings reported by Meseguer et al. (1) already carry practical meaning for patients. Generating more transferable blastocysts from a single ovarian-stimulation cycle reduces the likelihood of repeat retrievals, reducing financial, physical, and emotional strain. For embryology laboratories, improved usable blastocyst yield enhances efficiency and cumulative success per retrieval. These intermediate gains represent meaningful benefits that can justify selective clinical adoption while the field awaits confirmatory trials.

By refining sperm preparation with a method that is physiologic, reproducible, and low impact, Meseguer et al. (1) move the field a step closer to bridging the gap between technical promise and clinical proof. Their work reinforces that progress in reproductive medicine is often incremental, beginning with improvements in what we can measure and followed by testing whether those changes affect what matters most: a healthy live birth. However, until that evidence arrives, microfluidics should be viewed as an evolution rather than a revolution in sperm selection, a technology that reminds us that sometimes progress requires us not to simply go with the flow.
