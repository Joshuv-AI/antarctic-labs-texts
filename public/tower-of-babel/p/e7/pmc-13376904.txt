An integrated nanophotonic SPRM microsystem for real-time, label-free quantification of single-cell adhesion dynamics
Authors: Songfeng Huang, Yue Jiao, Yuye Wang, Xiaoqi Dai, Junkang Deng, Jiali Tai, Xiaorui Liu, Hailong Ni, Feiran Xie, Jiajie Chen, Junle Qu, Gaixia Xu, Yonghong Shao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Quantitative characterization of dynamic cell–substrate interactions at high spatiotemporal resolution remains a key challenge for bio-integrated microsystems, particularly under rapid external perturbations. Existing approaches are limited by labeling requirements, low temporal resolution, or endpoint measurements, preventing real-time access to early cellular responses. Here, we develop an integrated nanophotonic surface plasmon resonance microscopy (SPRM) microsystem for real-time, label-free quantification of single-cell interfacial dynamics. The system establishes a multi-parameter sensing framework by jointly extracting SPR intensity, cell–substrate axial position, and adhesion area, enabling continuous and quantitative mapping of cell–interface coupling with high spatiotemporal resolution. After validating system performance under controlled perturbations, we apply the platform to monitor rapid cellular responses using photodynamic therapy (PDT) as a model system. The microsystem resolves an ultrafast weakening of cell–substrate adhesion within ~25 s of stimulation onset and enables quantitative discrimination of treatment efficacy across different conditions. Notably, these early biophysical changes precede alterations in adhesion-related biomarkers measured by conventional immunofluorescence and flow cytometry. This work establishes SPRM as an integrated sensing microsystem that converts nanophotonic signals into quantitative descriptors of cellular mechanics, providing a generalizable platform for real-time, non-invasive analysis of cellular responses. The proposed framework offers broad potential for drug screening, cell–material interaction studies, and bio-integrated sensing applications.

Introduction

Understanding and quantifying dynamic cell–substrate interactions at high spatiotemporal resolution remains a fundamental challenge for bio-integrated microsystems1–3. Interfacial parameters such as adhesion strength, axial displacement, and contact area encode critical information about cellular states and their responses to external perturbations4–9. However, existing micro- and nanoscale sensing technologies are often constrained by limited temporal resolution, dependence on fluorescent labeling10, or invasive measurement protocols11, which hinder their ability to capture rapid and transient biophysical processes in living cells under physiologically relevant conditions.

Existing standard assessment methods, such as immunofluorescence (IF) and flow cytometry (FCM), suffer from fundamental methodological flaws. They are label-dependent, requiring exogenous fluorescent probes (e.g., Annexin V) or antibodies (e.g., anti-Integrin αV)12, and rely on end-point assays13,14 that involve cell fixation, permeabilization, or digestion. These procedures irreversibly compromise cellular viability and eliminate in situ morphological and adhesion information, precluding continuous observation15. More critically, the biochemical targets detected by these methods often represent lagging events downstream in the cellular stress response cascade, long after cell fate has been determined16. As a result, the immediate cellular responses occurring within the first tens of seconds following external stimulation remain largely inaccessible.

From a microsystem perspective, achieving label-free, real-time quantification of cell–interface coupling at the single-cell level requires sensing modalities that are both highly sensitive to nanoscale interfacial changes and capable of continuous, non-invasive operation. Traditional bulk measurements of cell adhesion, such as quartz crystal microbalance (QCM)17,18 and conventional surface plasmon resonance (SPR) techniques19,20, cannot resolve microscopic details and heterogeneity information on individual cells. To probe cell adhesion processes at the single-cell level, various methods have been proposed, including atomic force microscopy (AFM)3,11,21, micro-pipette suction22, optical tweezers23, magnetic torsion cell analyzers (MTC)24,25, shear-rotating discs26, and acoustic force spectroscopy27. These methods typically involve applying external forces and therefore cannot offer non-invasive, real-time measurements of spontaneous adhesion dynamics in living cells. Consequently, there is a compelling need for developing a label-free, non-invasive, highly sensitive platform capable of in situ, real-time monitoring of cellular biophysical responses.

Label-free techniques have emerged as a pivotal paradigm in bio-imaging, enabling the observation of complex cellular dynamics without the perturbations of exogenous labels. Among these, holotomography (HT) has revolutionized 3D quantitative visualization by mapping the refractive index (RI) of subcellular organelles with diffraction-limited lateral resolution (~100 nm) and high volumetric fidelity28. However, HT’s sensitivity to the nanoscopic environment is often constrained in the axial dimension (~300 nm), particularly at the extreme cell–substrate interface. To probe these interfacial regions, interference reflection microscopy (IRM) has been widely employed for mapping membrane topography29,30. Yet, the periodic nature of IRM’s interference signal introduces inherent sensitivity dead zones and ambiguity in distinguishing the direction of axial displacement. Extending these interferometric principles, interferometric scattering microscopy (iSCAT) enables the detection of single nanoscale entities by amplifying scattering signals. However, it still faces significant challenges in suppressing non-specific background noise within heterogeneous cellular environments31,32.

Among label-free nanophotonic techniques, surface plasmon resonance microscopy (SPRM) provides a superior framework for tracking interfacial displacements33–37. The SPRM signal originates from a monotonically decaying evanescent field, whose exponential intensity–distance relationship naturally eliminates dead zones and directional ambiguity. Furthermore, the strong field localization and enhancement effects on metal films significantly improve the signal-to-noise ratio, giving SPRM an inherent sensitivity far superior to any far-field interferometric technique when detecting nanoscale events at the cell–substrate interface. As a result, SPRM38–41 and plasmon scattering microscopy (PSM)42–44 have been established as powerful label-free tools for mapping cell–substrate axial distances and analyzing adhesion strength. More broadly, recent advances in plasmonic sensing have demonstrated the potential of nanophotonic systems for transforming complex optical responses into multidimensional biochemical and biophysical information. For example, plasmonic resonance energy transfer (PRET)-based approaches have enabled real-time monitoring of intracellular electron transfer kinetics and multiple molecular fingerprints45,46. These developments highlight the broader capability of plasmonic platforms to support quantitative, information-rich sensing beyond conventional intensity-based measurements.

Despite these advances, current SPRM-based cellular studies remain largely focused on qualitative imaging or single-parameter analysis. In most cases, the measured SPR intensity or reconstructed axial position is interpreted independently, while the coupled contributions arising from axial displacement, local refractive index variation, and interfacial structural density are not systematically considered. This gap limits the ability to simultaneously resolve the synergistic changes in axial nanoscopic displacement and interfacial coupling area during rapid cellular transitions. Therefore, there is an urgent need to transform SPRM from a qualitative imaging tool into an integrated microsystem capable of providing multidimensional, quantitative descriptions of the cell–substrate interface under rapid, clinically relevant perturbations.

Here, we develop an integrated nanophotonic SPRM microsystem for real-time, label-free quantification of single-cell adhesion dynamics. By jointly extracting SPR intensity, cell–substrate axial position, and high-adhesion area, the proposed system establishes a multi-parameter signal transduction framework that converts nanophotonic responses into quantitative descriptors of interfacial coupling. Rather than functioning as a conventional imaging modality, the platform operates as an integrated sensing system enabling continuous, non-invasive mapping of dynamic cellular mechanics with high spatiotemporal resolution. Using photodynamic therapy (PDT) as a representative model of rapid cellular perturbation, we demonstrate that the system resolves ultrafast adhesion weakening events within tens of seconds and enables quantitative evaluation of treatment efficacy. This work establishes a generalizable microsystem framework for real-time mapping of cellular biophysical responses, with broad applications in drug screening, cell–material interaction studies, and bio-integrated sensing.

Results and discussions

Architecture and working principle of the integrated SPRM microsystem

We developed an integrated multi-parameter SPRM sensing system to visualize real-time adhesion changes in single cells. Its detection principle relies on precise monitoring of the plasmonic evanescent field at the gold film-cell interface, where SPR intensity exhibits exponential decay with vertical distance, enabling calculation of local cell adhesion distances (see Fig. 1a). SPRM exhibits exceptional sensitivity to local refractive index (RI) changes within hundreds of nanometers above the sensor surface (gold film). When a living cell adheres to the gold film surface, its cell body (with RI higher than the surrounding culture medium) interacts with the evanescent wave field, causing localized enhancement of the SPRM signal. Unlike traditional methods reliant on specific molecular markers, the signal provided by SPRM represents comprehensive information. This signal intensity does not originate from a single molecule but integrates multiple key parameters in real time, primarily including cell-substrate contact area, cell-substrate vertical distance, and surface matter density. Among these, surface matter density is primarily determined by dense cytoskeletal networks (such as actin) and structures like focal adhesions. Based on the detection principle of SPRM, events of weakened cell adhesion can be quantitatively captured in one or more ways: a reduction in the contact area between the cell and the substrate, an increase in the vertical distance between the cell and the substrate, and a decrease in the density of surface material (Fig. 1b). This ultimately leads to a decrease in the average RI within the SPRM attenuation field, resulting in a sharp and quantifiable decline in SPRM signal intensity at the macroscopic level. Concurrently, significant changes occur in the Z-axis position and in regions of high adhesion. This quantitative relationship makes SPRM an ideal tool for real-time, label-free tracking of dynamic changes in cell adhesion, and it can be used to assess the dynamic process of cell damage induced by PDT (Fig. 1c).

Multi-parameter quantification of cell–substrate interfacial dynamics

To demonstrate the multi-parameter quantitative sensing capabilities of the integrated nanophotonic SPRM microsystem for cell adhesion, we designed two key validation experiments using well-defined chemical and physical perturbations.

In the chemical stimulation experiment, we employed trypsin to treat adherent HeLa cells, a well-established standardized chemical method known to induce adhesion failure. As a proteolytic enzyme, trypsin acts by cleaving adhesion-related proteins (such as integrins) on the cell membrane surface, thereby disrupting the anchor points connecting the cell to its substrate. This is a drastic process that causes cells to lose adhesion, round up, and ultimately detach. In the experiment, trypsin solution was perfused into the chamber containing adherent HeLa cells. As anticipated, the reduction in cell-substrate contact area and increase in vertical distance resulted in a significant decrease in the interface refractive index. Specifically, representative cell SPR intensity images (see Fig. 2a) reveal a marked reduction in SPR intensity. Concurrently, as depicted in Fig. 2b, the Z-position of the cell-substrate interface progressively increases. Observing the real-time curves presented in Fig. 2e and f (Fig. 2d shows the ROI selection), the trend indicates rapid dissociation of adhesion points between cells and the substrate, resulting in loss of cell adhesion. The physical stimulation experiment aims to evaluate the SPRM microsystem’s capability by examining cellular responses to physical stimuli (photothermal effects). In this experiment, we employed a 532 nm laser beam focused onto the basal surface of HeLa cells for stimulation. This physical stimulus generates significant photothermal effects sufficient to disrupt cell membranes and cellular structures, particularly the cytoskeleton and focal adhesions near the basement membrane, thereby altering their interactions with the substrate. Specifically, SPR intensity images captured at six time points (0, 40, 80, 120, 160, 200 s) (Fig. 2h) reveal a gradual decrease in SPR intensity. Concurrently, as shown in Fig. 2i, the Z-position of the cell-substrate interface progressively increases. Real-time monitoring results from the SPRM microsystem (Fig. 2l, m) reveal significant changes in SPR intensity and Z-position following laser stimulation initiation (Fig. 2k shows ROI selection). Notably, the two adjacent cells exhibited distinct responses (Brightfield cell images, see Fig. S1, same below): stimulated cell #1 responded vigorously, while unstimulated cell #2 remained largely unaffected. This consistent behavior validates the SPRM microsystem’s capabilities. Furthermore, changes in adhesion status between the 532 nm laser-stimulated cell and the unstimulated cell were more intuitively presented via AQI and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${N}_{{total}}$$\end{document}Ntotal. Thus, this experiment demonstrates that SPRM can also sensitively capture adhesion failure induced by physical stimulation.

It is worth noting that we employed two distinct perturbation models to quantitatively validate the extracted parameters (SPR intensity, Z-position, and adhesion area). First, trypsin-induced cell detachment served as a chemical perturbation, during which the SPR intensity monotonically decreased while the Z-position correspondingly increased (Fig. 2e, f), consistent with the exponential decay behavior of the evanescent field. Second, upon localized photothermal stimulation using a 532 nm laser, only the Z-position of the stimulated cells showed a significant increase, whereas adjacent, unstimulated cells remained unchanged (Fig. 2l, m), demonstrating spatial specificity. These controlled perturbations confirm that the extracted parameters accurately track the expected changes in cell-substrate coupling. Furthermore, the intrinsic consistency among SPR intensity, Z-position, and adhesion area—such as the synchronous decrease in both strong adhesion area and AQI—provides cross-validation for these three physical quantities.

These benchmark experiments demonstrate that SPRM microsystem can precisely capture the dynamic changes in cell adhesion triggered by chemical and physical stimuli with a high signal-to-noise ratio and spatio-temporal resolution. This validation establishes a solid foundation for applying SPRM to dissect the early adhesion failure events that occur during PDT-induced cell damage.

Imaging characterization of reactive oxygen species and apoptosis in photodynamic therapy-treated cells

Experiments with chemical and physical stimuli were conducted to evaluate the sensitivity and robustness of the SPRM readouts, demonstrating the feasibility of using SPRM microsystems to monitor the dynamic processes of PDT. Next, we first established the biological effect timeline of our PDT model (10 µM Ce6 + 60 mW/cm² white light irradiation) using conventional fluorescence imaging methods (see Note S1), setting a baseline for subsequent cross-scale comparisons.

Using the ROS-specific probe DCFH-DA, we observed that green fluorescence began to appear within cells at the instant light was applied (t = 0 s) and continued to intensify over time, confirming the immediate generation of ROS (see Fig. 3a). Subsequently, we employed Annexin V-FITC/PI dual staining to determine the timing of apoptosis. Results showed that ~400–600 s after illumination, Annexin V-FITC-labeled green fluorescence appeared on the cell membrane—a classic marker of early apoptosis. After 1000 s, the PI dye entered the nucleus, emitting red fluorescence, indicating the onset of late apoptosis or necrosis (see Fig. 3b). As observed, SPR intensity curves across all ROIs exhibited similar decay trends. Therefore, we selected two representative ROIs from two cells for analysis. The difference between the two time points is determined by cellular heterogeneity. Subsequently, the signal continued to decay. The results from the control group validated the specificity of the PDT-induced cell adhesion signal. The stability of the Z-position and AQI in the control group demonstrated that the observed changes were caused by Ce6-mediated PDT effects, rather than photothermal effects, phototoxicity, or spontaneous cytoskeletal reorganization interfering with the main conclusions. Moreover, it should be noted that SPRM signals are influenced by both nanoscale axial displacement and local refractive index (RI) variations within the evanescent field. Biophysical processes such as cytoskeletal reorganization, alterations in membrane structure, or early apoptosis could all lead to changes in RI distribution, and SPRM captures the combined physical manifestation of these effects at the cell-substrate interface. In the early stages of PDT (<25 s), since cells have not yet undergone significant deformation, the decrease in SPRM signals primarily stems from minute axial displacements caused by the dissociation of adhesion proteins. Over time, cytoskeletal collapse (decreased density) and overall cell contraction (increased displacement) jointly contribute to the signal reduction. Consequently, the SPRM signal represents a coupled response of multiple biophysical processes, which cannot be fully decoupled within the current single-modality framework. This limitation highlights the need for future integration with multimodal imaging approaches to disentangle these contributions. Notably, the rapid response at 25 s occurs significantly earlier than the appearance of typical apoptosis markers (~400 s, Fig. 3b), indicating that changes in the SPRM signal specifically reflect the initial damage to adhesion molecules caused by ROS attack and the subsequent detachment from the interface. This physical fingerprint provides a sensitive mechanical criterion for distinguishing photodynamic effects from simple photothermal effects or spontaneous cellular activity.

Taken together, these results demonstrate that SPRM microsystem offers a multiparametric and quantitative method for characterizing adhesion states during PDT, capturing changes in SPR intensity, Z-position, AQI, and adhesion-area evolution at the single-cell level. Due to its unique advantages of real-time, high-sensitivity, and label-free monitoring, we observed that the PDT process becomes active within 25 s after light initiation. Furthermore, the multi-parameter imaging approach allows us to resolve cell-to-cell heterogeneity that is invisible to bulk biochemical assays. Therefore, the SPRM microsystem offers a powerful tool for monitoring early changes in cellular adhesion states and quantitatively evaluating PDT efficacy.

Comparison with conventional biochemical assays

To further highlight the advantages of the SPRM microsystem, we compared the performance of SPRM (real-time monitoring) with traditional methods (endpoint monitoring). Using IF and FCM, we detected changes in the integrin αV protein, which plays a central role in cell-basement membrane adhesion (see Note S2). Notably, Integrin αV binds to ligands in the extracellular matrix (such as fibronectin, laminin, and osteopontin), promoting cell-matrix adhesion. This adhesion is crucial for the positioning and stabilization of adherent cells within culture dishes. Concurrently, the SPRM microsystem primarily reflects the current cellular state by monitoring adhesion between cells and the substrate during PDT. Therefore, we selected Integrin αV as the cell adhesion molecule for experimentation and investigation.

Specifically, using FCM, the fluorescence intensity of Integrin αV was measured at different time points (see Fig. 5a). Additionally, immunofluorescence images of HeLa cell nuclei (blue), Vinculin (red), and Integrin αV (green) staining were acquired at different treatment durations (see Fig. 5c). Furthermore, statistical analysis of the fluorescence results revealed that during PDT, the fluorescence intensity of Integrin αV began to show a statistically significant decrease after 200 s of light exposure (*), with no significant difference observed between 400 s and 200 s (see Fig. 5b). Simultaneously, its expression on the cell membrane also exhibited a statistically significant decrease after 200 s of illumination (*), with no significant difference observed between 400 s and 200 s (see Fig. 5d). Conclusion

In this work, we developed an integrated nanophotonic SPRM microsystem for real-time, label-free, and in situ quantification of cell–substrate interfacial dynamics at the single-cell level. By establishing a multi-parameter signal transduction framework, the system converts plasmonic responses into quantitative descriptors of adhesion strength, axial displacement, and contact area, enabling continuous mapping of cellular mechanical states with high spatiotemporal resolution.

Using photodynamic therapy (PDT) as a representative model of rapid cellular perturbation, we demonstrated that the proposed microsystem captures early-stage adhesion weakening within ~25 s of stimulation onset, significantly preceding detectable changes in conventional biochemical markers. In contrast to traditional endpoint assays that rely on labeling and downstream molecular signals, the SPRM-based approach provides a direct, real-time readout of cellular physical integrity.

These results highlight the capability of the SPRM microsystem to transform cellular response evaluation from static, label-dependent measurements into dynamic, quantitative, and non-invasive analysis. The ability to resolve early and heterogeneous responses at the single-cell level offers important advantages for therapeutic assessment and rapid screening.

Beyond the PDT model demonstrated here, the proposed framework may be extended to the investigation of broader adhesion-related cellular responses, including cell–material interactions, mechanobiological regulation, and dynamic responses to external stimuli. Owing to its label-free and non-invasive characteristics, the platform provides a potentially generalizable approach for real-time monitoring of adherent cell systems under dynamic perturbation conditions.

Several intrinsic limitations of the current platform should also be noted. First, the evanescent plasmonic field is confined to ~200 nm above the gold surface, providing high interfacial sensitivity while limiting access to apical membrane structures and deep intracellular dynamics. Second, the current implementation is primarily applicable to adherent cells and cannot be directly extended to suspension-cell systems. In addition, the lateral spatial resolution remains anisotropic due to plasmon propagation effects on the metallic surface. Furthermore, because SPR responses originate from coupled variations in refractive index, axial displacement, and interfacial structural density, complete physical separation of all contributing variables remains challenging within a single-modality optical framework. Nevertheless, compared with conventional single-intensity SPRM analysis, the coordinated extraction of multiple interfacial observables in the present system substantially improves the interpretability of dynamic adhesion-related responses and reduces ambiguity in the analysis of rapid cellular transitions. Future work will focus on integrating the SPRM microsystem with complementary molecular and structural characterization approaches, such as fluorescence-based biochemical reporters and multimodal imaging strategies, to further elucidate the coupling between interfacial biophysical dynamics and downstream cellular signaling processes.

Material and methods

Optical setup

The optical setup is based on a Nikon inverted microscope (Ti2-E) using a 100× oil immersion objective (NA = 1.49). A semiconductor laser (MSL-III-785L-100 mW, Changchun New Industries) serves as the light source. A sCMOS camera (Gloria 4.2, Revealer) records 1024×1024 pixel images at 50 fps for chemical and physical stimulation experiments and 10 fps for PDT experiments.

In this study, a 47-nm-thick gold film was deposited onto a glass substrate via magnetron sputtering. This thickness is widely recognized as the classic optimal thickness for SPR excitation in the Kretschmann configuration as it ensures high coupling efficiency (minimum reflectance) while providing an evanescent wave penetration depth of ~100–200 nm. This penetration range is well suited for probing cell–substrate interactions, including focal adhesion complexes.

Cell culture and PDT treatment

Human cervical cancer cells (HeLa) were cultured in DMEM medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% dual antibiotics (streptomycin and kanamycin). Prior to experiments, cell suspensions were diluted ~50-fold to prevent overcrowding and seeded into the culture wells of a custom SPRM chip. Cells were cultured for 24 h to ensure complete confluence.

For photodynamic therapy (PDT) treatment, HeLa cells were cultured in medium containing Ce6 (C829662, Macklin) at concentrations of 10, 20, or 30 μM and incubated at 37 °C for 4 h. After incubation, the cells were gently washed twice with PBS to remove extracellular photosensitizer, and PBS was added to the well in preparation for observation using the SPRM microsystem. Subsequently, continuous irradiation was performed using a broadband white light source (microscope brightfield illumination system, halogen lamp) at a power density of 60 mW/cm² (measured at the sample plane) in the 405 nm wavelength band to initiate photodynamic therapy. Irradiation was maintained throughout the entire real-time SPRM monitoring process (typically lasting up to 1000 s). Cells in the control group received the same illumination but were not incubated with Ce6 to rule out non-specific interferences such as photothermal effects and phototoxicity.

Data processing

The average gray values of regions of interest (ROIs) for individual cells in SPRM image sequences were extracted using ImageJ to quantify SPRM signals. The average fluorescence intensity of images was analyzed with ImageJ software. Origin 2022 was also employed for data analysis.

Meanwhile, the spatial resolution of the SPRM system was quantitatively evaluated. The effective axial resolution (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{\boldsymbol{\sigma }}}_{{\boldsymbol{Z}}}$$\end{document}σZ) of the SPRM microsystem is determined by the system noise and the sensitivity of the SPR decay model. This nanometer-scale sensitivity enables the detection of ultra-early adhesion weakening prior to observable morphological changes.

The lateral resolution of the system is inherently anisotropic due to the surface plasmon propagation. While the resolution along the Y-axis is diffraction-limited (~300 nm), the X-axis resolution is governed by the SPR propagation length, measured to be 1.96 ± 0.24 μm using nanoparticle point spread function fitting (see Note S3). Despite this micron-scale lateral blurring, the system remains highly capable of resolving single-cell boundaries and focal adhesion regions, which typically span tens of micrometers.
