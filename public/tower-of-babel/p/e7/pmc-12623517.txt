Trends in the Management of Patients with Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus by Japanese Practitioners
Authors: Keiko Arai, Tetsuo Nishikawa, Shohei Yuasa, Koichi Hirao, Hisao Mori
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionIn recent years, diabetes management by diabetes specialists has evolved owing to various factors such as the introduction of new glucose-lowering drugs. In Japan, many patients with diabetes mellitus are managed by general practitioners (GPs), with the quality of management provided by these GPs playing a crucial role in preventing diabetes-related complications. Despite this importance, trends in diabetes management by GPs remain unclear. This study aimed to assess changes in diabetes management by GPs, comparing data from two nationwide surveys conducted in 2006 and 2018.MethodsWe compared the characteristics and pharmacotherapy of patients with type 2 diabetes (T2DM) managed by GPs and diabetes specialists in 2006 (14,312 and 1038 patients, respectively) and 2018 (6525 and 1545, respectively). Combination OAD therapy was also more prevalent in 2018 than in 2006.ConclusionThe observed trends with GPs were consistent with those observed among diabetes specialists, suggesting that many Japanese GPs are adopting current treatments strategies and may be providing appropriate diabetes management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s13300-025-01805-9.Keywords: Diabetes management, General practitioners, Nationwide survey, Oral antidiabetic drug, Type 2 diabetes mellitus

Introduction

The increasing prevalence of diabetes mellitus and its complications has become a serious global health concern. To prevent diabetes-related complications, many countries have developed standardized treatment guidelines. In Japan, a large population of patients with diabetes mellitus is managed by general practitioners (GPs), and the quality of diabetes management provided by these GPs plays a crucial role in preventing complications. Previously, we conducted nationwide surveys in 2006 and 2018 to evaluate diabetes management by Japanese practitioners, including GPs and diabetes specialists, and reported the results in a series of publications [1–6].

In recent years, diabetes management in Japan has undergone several changes. In 2012, the measurement of glycosylated hemoglobin (HbA1c) in Japan transitioned from the Japan Diabetes Society (JDS) values to the National Glycohemoglobin Standardization Program (NGSP) values to align with global standardization efforts [7]. In 2013, the JDS set a target HbA1c level below 7.0% to prevent diabetic complications [8]. In 2016, more flexible HbA1c targets were recommended for older patients to prevent hypoglycemia, depending on their cognitive and physical conditions [9]. Concurrently, pharmacological options expanded with the approval of dipeptidyl peptidase 4 (DPP4) inhibitors in 2009 and sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors in 2014 in Japan.

Data from the Japan Diabetes Management Study Group (JDDM), a study group of diabetes specialists in Japan, showed that patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) became older and more obese over time, while their HbA1c levels gradually improved [10]. Additionally, the prescribing patterns and doses of oral antidiabetic drugs (OADs) by diabetes specialists in Japan have changed [11, 12]. However, trends in diabetes management provided by GPs in Japan remain poorly documented. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate trends in diabetes management by Japanese GPs over the past decade by comparing data from two nationwide survey cohorts conducted in 2006 and 2018.

Methods

Summary of Previous Nationwide Studies in 2006 and 2018

The methodology of the 2006 and 2018 surveys has been described in detail in previous reports [1, 4]. In summary, approximately 40% of members of the Japanese Medical and Dental Practitioners for Improvement of Medical Care (JMDPIMC), which consists of practitioners throughout Japan, were randomly selected and invited to participate in this study. In both surveys, we defined diabetes specialists as members or board-certified diabetes specialists by the JDS and Japan Endocrine Society; others were classified as GPs. Patients with diabetes could choose to first visit either a GP or a specialist, and referral of patients to either GPs or specialists is at the discretion of the treating physician in the Japanese medical system. In total, 721 and 463 practitioners agreed to participate in the surveys in 2006 and 2018, respectively. Each practitioner was encouraged to register up to 30 patients in 2006 and 20 patients in 2018, consecutively. A total of 15,652 patients with either type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1DM) or T2DM were enrolled in 2006, while 8070 patients with T2DM were enrolled in 2018 [1, 4]. Data collected included the most recent HbA1c values, age, sex, height, body weight of patients, and treatment medications. These were sent by facsimile to the central analytical facility, where information was treated anonymously and subsequently analyzed [1, 4].

Ethical Considerations

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (1964) and its later amendments. The study protocol was approved by the ethics committee of the Kanagawa Association of Medical and Dental Practitioners, which includes lawyers and ethical experts (approval no. 24001; approval date January 30, 2025). The study complies with the provisions of the Declaration of Helsinki and follows the Guidelines for Epidemiological Studies of the Ministry of Health, Labor and Welfare of Japan. The requirement for informed consent was waived owing to the retrospective nature of the study and the use of de-identified data. Patients could opt out of the study, and instructions for how to do so were provided on a poster displayed in each clinic.

Participants and Data

Of the 15,652 patients in the 2006 study cohort, 15,350 patients with T2DM were included in the analysis, comprising 14,312 being managed by GPs and 1038 managed by specialists. In the 2018 cohort, 8070 patients with T2DM were included, comprising 6525 managed by GPs and 1545 managed by specialists (Table 1). Data on age, sex, HbA1c, body mass index (BMI), treatment modalities, types and number of OADs, types of sulfonylureas (SUs), and dose of SUs were compared between the two studies.

HbA1c Values for Comparison

Given that HbA1c values in the 2006 study were reported according to the JDS standard, they were converted to NGSP values for comparison using the following formula: HbA1c (NGSP) (%) = 1.019 × HbA1c (JDS) (%) + 0.3% [7].

Statistical Analysis

Differences in age, BMI, HbA1c, and dose of SUs between the two cohorts were analyzed using the Wilcoxon rank sum test based on data distribution. Pearson’s chi-square test was used to compare categorical variables, such as distributions of HbA1c, treatment modality, number and types of OADs in monotherapy, and types of SUs prescribed between the two cohorts. All statistical analyses were carried out using the statistical software package R version 4.4.0 (Qiita Inc., Nagoya, Japan), SPSS (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA), and JMP version 14.0 (SAS Institute Inc., NC, USA). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Characteristics of the Patients

In 2018, patients with T2DM managed by GPs were significantly older and had higher BMI; however, their HbA1c levels were lower than those of patients in the 2006 study (Table 1). The distribution of HbA1c levels among patients managed by GPs shifted downward in 2018, with a higher proportion achieving HbA1c < 7.0% and fewer patients exceeding 8% (Table 2). Similar trends were also observed among patients managed by diabetes specialists.

Pharmacotherapy

Combinations of more than three OADs increased in 2018, whereas both monotherapy and dual OAD therapy decreased compared to those in 2006 (Table 4). Among patients managed using monotherapy by GPs in 2006, SUs were the most commonly prescribed OADs, closely followed by α-glucosidase inhibitors (Table 5). In 2018, DPP4 inhibitors were the most commonly prescribed drugs, closely followed by biguanides (BG) (Table 5). These trends were also observed among diabetes specialists. Within the SU class, prescription of glibenclamide and gliclazide decreased, while the prescription of glimepiride increased to approximately 80% in 2018 (Table 6). Moreover, the average daily dose of glimepiride decreased from 2006 to 2018 (Table 6). Similar prescribing patterns (excluding gliclazide) were observed in patients managed by diabetes specialists. Among specialists, the prescription of gliclazide slightly increased, even though the prescribed dose decreased.

Discussion

Our findings indicated that patients with T2DM managed by GPs in 2018 were older and had higher BMI, while their HbA1c levels improved compared to those in 2006. Similar trends were observed among patients managed by diabetes specialists, as well as in reports from the JDDM, which comprises diabetes specialists [10]. The prevalence of sarcopenia and sarcopenic obesity increased with advancing age [13], resulting in increased insulin resistance. One possible reason why the HbA1c levels of patients improved despite being older and obese may be due to the increased use of BG, which has an effect in improving insulin resistance.

Our results suggest that GPs shifted the management of patients with T2DM from diet or insulin monotherapy to combination OAD therapy, with DPP4 inhibitors and BG being more frequently prescribed. Although the use of SUs decreased, with lower doses being prescribed, a similar prescribing pattern (excluding gliclazide) was observed among diabetes specialists and has also been reported by the JDDM [11, 12]. Severe hypoglycemic events are extremely low, and patients do not require dose adjustment for renal dysfunction [14]. This may explain why specialists prefer gliclazide for some patients. These trends may reflect the increased availability of new OADs and suggest that physicians now have more pharmacotherapy options than in previous years. The most recent pharmacotherapy algorithm for patients with T2DM proposed by the JDS was published in 2024 [15], recommending SGLT2 inhibitors and glucagon-like peptide 1 receptor agonist (GLP-1 RA) for patients with T2DM, obesity, chronic kidney disease, heart failure, and cardiovascular diseases [15]. However, our survey was conducted before the consensus statement by the JDS; therefore, the discussion below is based on a therapeutic landscape excluding SGLT2 inhibitors and GLP-1 RA.

The most notable change during this decade was the availability of DPP4 inhibitors in 2009 in Japan. Japanese patients with T2DM typically have reduced insulin-secreting capacity [16], and the disease often occurs at a lower BMI than that in white patients. Consequently, the glucose-lowering effect of DPP4 inhibitors was greater in Japanese patients than in white patients [16]. Additionally, the incidence of adverse effects, such as hypoglycemia and weight gain, was very low, except when combined with insulin-secreting drugs [17]. GPs often work without co-medical staff, making it challenging to provide education for patients with T2DM on self-care for diabetes management. For these reasons, DPP4 inhibitors were regarded by physicians—particularly GPs—as easy to use, and their prescription rates increased rapidly across Japan.

Another notable change was the approval of high doses of metformin (750–2250 mg per day) in Japan in 2010 [18]. Studies have shown that increased doses of metformin improve glycemic control without increasing the risk of hypoglycemia [19, 20]. Following the approval of high doses of metformin in Japan, diabetes specialists prescribed high doses of this drug more frequently [12], a trend that was later adopted by GPs.

Our results suggest that new treatment strategies for T2DM were first implemented by diabetes specialists and were subsequently adopted by GPs through clinical guidelines and specialist-led practice patterns (Table S1). Notably, the parallel trends in pharmacotherapy and glycemic outcomes observed among GPs and specialists in this study indicate that many GPs were well-informed and equipped with up-to-date knowledge comparable to that of diabetes specialists. This suggests that GPs can deliver high-quality diabetes management in real-world clinical settings in Japan. Strengthening the referral network between GPs and diabetes specialists, as well as ensuring adequate educational opportunities for GPs, may be crucial [4] for the timely adoption of new treatment strategies.

A major strength of this study is that it reflects real-world clinical practice among GPs who manage the majority of patients with T2DM in Japan. However, the study has some limitations. As noted previously [1, 4], most participating practitioners in 2006 and 2018 had an interest in diabetes management, which may have introduced bias. Additionally, data collection relied on questionnaires administered in busy clinical settings, which may have affected data accuracy, and it did not include precise information such as diabetes duration, complications, and concurrent diseases. We also could not evaluate the initial effect of specialist care in cases where the patients initially visited specialists and were later referred to GPs, as we did not distinguish these cases. The comparison was based on only two time points (2006 and 2018), which restricts the ability to assess continuous trends. Finally, it is necessary to follow up whether GPs would adapt their diabetes management to the recent pharmacotherapy algorithm released by the JDS in 2024, including SGLT2 inhibitors and GLP-1 RA.

Conclusion

Our findings suggest that many Japanese GPs have aligned their diabetes management practices with evolving guidelines and available treatment options. The similarity in prescribing trends observed among GPs and specialists indicates that GPs may possess sufficient knowledge and access to information comparable to those of specialists, enabling them to deliver appropriate and evidence-based diabetes management. Strengthening collaboration between GPs and diabetes specialists, as well as expanding access to educational resources, may further improve the quality of diabetes management across Japan.
