Bilateral lower-extremity paresis as an atypical presentation of subarachnoid hemorrhage: A case report
Authors: Saaya Maruyama, Masayuki Sato, Kuniyuki Onuma, Kazuhiro Nakamura, Noriyuki Kato, Eiichi Ishikawa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background: Subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH) is a neurological emergency typically presenting with a sudden, severe headache. Focal neurological deficits are generally unilateral and correspond to the location of the aneurysm or hematoma. Bilateral lower limb paresis as an initial manifestation is extremely rare and may delay diagnosis. This report highlights a unique case and explores potential mechanisms underlying this atypical presentation.Case Description: A 62-year-old woman presented with acute bilateral leg weakness and headache. Imaging revealed diffuse SAH from a ruptured aneurysm at the right internal carotid–posterior communicating artery junction, without mass effect . Magnetic resonance imaging revealed acute infarcts in the bilateral medial frontal lobes and the splenium of the corpus callosum, consistent with an infarction in the anterior cerebral artery territory. Angiography revealed no vasospasm, and spinal pathology was excluded. The aneurysm was successfully treated with coil embolization. The patient’s leg strength gradually improved over the 1st month.Conclusion: This case underscores the importance of including SAH in the differential diagnosis of acute paraparesis, even in the absence of classic signs. Bilateral infarcts may result from early microthrombosis or transient cerebral hypoperfusion following a sudden increase in intracranial pressure. Recognizing such mechanisms is essential for timely diagnosis and management.

INTRODUCTION

Subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH) typically presents with a sudden, severe headache.[9] While focal neurological deficits occur, they are usually unilateral and related to mass effect, ischemia, or vasospasm.[1] Bilateral motor deficits at onset are extremely rare and may lead to diagnostic confusion, often raising suspicion of spinal cord pathology.

Paraparesis as an initial manifestation of SAH is uncommon,[6] suggesting atypical mechanisms, such as early ischemia or elevated intracranial pressure (ICP) affecting the anterior cerebral artery (ACA) territories. Emerging evidence indicates that early brain injury following aneurysmal SAH may not solely depend on large-vessel vasospasm but may instead result from microthrombosis, acute intracranial hypertension, or diffuse cerebral hypoperfusion during the hyperacute phase.[3,4,10]

We report the case of a 62-year-old woman who presented with bilateral lower limb paresis at the onset of SAH caused by a rupture of a posterior communicating artery aneurysm. Imaging revealed bilateral frontal lobe and corpus callosum infarctions, with no evidence of large-vessel vasospasm. This case underscores the need to consider hyperacute ischemia and microvascular dysfunction as contributors to early neurological deterioration in SAH, even in the absence of typical imaging findings.

CASE DESCRIPTION

A 62-year-old hypertensive woman was referred to our hospital with an unusual presentation of bilateral lower-extremity paresis following SAH. She reported a sudden, severe headache at midnight, accompanied by profound bilateral lower-extremity weakness, so severe that she was unable to retrieve her mobile phone to call for help. Her symptoms persisted, and she contacted emergency services about 8 h later. Her medical history included hypertension managed with valsartan and amlodipine and a known unruptured cerebral aneurysm. She smoked ten cigarettes per day and had no family history of SAH or aneurysm.

On arrival, her blood pressure was 212/112 mmHg, and her pulse rate was 74 bpm. She appeared mildly drowsy, with a Glasgow Coma Scale score of E3V4M6, corresponding to a World Federation of Neurological Societies grade III. Neurological examination revealed profound distal-dominant motor deficits in the lower extremities: Right leg strength was graded at manual muscle test (MMT) 2, while the left leg was completely paralyzed (MMT 0). Upper limb strength and function were preserved. Laboratory tests were largely unremarkable. The prothrombin time-international normalized ratio was 0.96 (normal: 0.85–1.1), and D-dimer was mildly elevated at 1.1 μg/mL (normal: 0–1). Initial non-contrast head computed tomography (CT) confirmed the presence of diffuse SAH without hematoma or mass effect that could explain the motor deficits [Figure 1]. Brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed acute ischemic lesions in the precentral gyrus of the bilateral frontal lobes and in the splenium of the corpus callosum – regions typically involved in motor control of the lower extremities. These lesions were hyperintense on diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) and fluid-attenuated inversion recovery, with corresponding decreased apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) values [Figure 2]. Spinal MRI showed no evidence of spinal cord pathology, such as ischemia or hematoma.

Non-contrast head computed tomography revealed diffuse subarachnoid hemorrhage. No evident hematoma or mass effect was observed in the frontal lobes despite the patient’s paraparesis.

Magnetic resonance images showing acute ischemic lesions in the bilateral medial frontal lobes (arrowheads in a–c) and the corpus callosum (arrows in d–f). (a, d: diffusion-weighted imaging [DWI]; b, e: apparent diffusion coefficient [ADC]; c, f: fluid-attenuated inversion recovery [FLAIR]).

Digital subtraction angiography identified a ruptured aneurysm at the bifurcation of the right internal carotid artery (ICA) and posterior communicating artery (Pcom). Under general anesthesia, 6Fr FUBUKI Dilator kits (Asahi Intecc, Aichi, Japan) were introduced into the right ICA. Angiography revealed an irregular aneurysm measuring 5.3 × 3.6 mm, with a 1.33 mm neck and bleb at the ICA-Pcom junction. Following administration of 3,000 units of heparin, endovascular coil embolization was performed. A Target 360 XL 3.0 mm × 6 cm coil (Stryker Neurovascular, Kalamazoo, MI, USA) was deployed through a Phenom 0.017” microcatheter (Medtronic, Minneapolis, Minnesota, USA), followed by a Target 360 ultrasoft 3 mm × 6 cm coil and two nano 2 mm × 3 cm coils. The Pcom artery was preserved, and complete occlusion was achieved without perioperative complications [Figure 3].

Angiography of the ACA showed no significant vasospasm or occlusive changes up to the A2 and A3 segments. During the Matas test, the anterior communicating artery (ACom) was not visualized, and blood flow was not communicated between the areas perfused by the left and right ACAs. Venous phase imaging revealed no abnormalities, effectively ruling out venous sinus thrombosis or other structural causes. Cerebrospinal fluid obtained through spinal drain revealed an elevated white blood cell count (336/μL, 64% polymorphonuclear leukocytes) but no evidence of infection. Electroencephalography revealed no epileptiform discharges, and somatosensory-evoked potentials demonstrated a normal P37 latency. The patient’s motor deficits gradually improved over the course of a month. At discharge, MMT in the lower limbs was graded 4. After transfer to a rehabilitation facility, paralysis of the lower limbs persisted, although improvement was noted: Right lower-extremity strength recovered to MMT 5 and left to MMT 4, allowing the patient to walk with a cane.

DISCUSSION

This case illustrates a rare and atypical manifestation of SAH, in which the patient developed acute bilateral lower-extremity paresis due to infarctions in both frontal lobes and the splenium of the corpus callosum. While focal neurological deficits can accompany SAH, they are typically unilateral, resulting from mass effect, localized ischemia, or vasospasm.[1] In contrast, bilateral paraparesis at onset is extremely uncommon and often prompts evaluation for spinal cord pathology or other central causes.[6,11]

In this case, spinal MRI showed no signs of ischemia or compression, and somatosensory-evoked potentials ruled out a spinal etiology. Venous imaging and angiography revealed no abnormalities or significant vasopasm, and the absence of ACom flow further drew attention to ACA territory infarction as the likely source of symptoms. The simultaneous onset of headache and paraparesis suggests that the ischemic injury observed occurred in the hyperacute phase, likely within minutes to hours after the aneurysm rupture.

In the absence of proximal vessel occlusion or angiographic vasospasm, several mechanisms may explain the infarcts: thromboembolism from the ruptured aneurysm may have occluded distal ACA branches. Microvascular dysfunction, including peripheral vasospasm or early microthrombosis, could have led to multifocal ischemia. In this context, the concept of early brain injury after SAH, which refers to cerebral injury occurring within the first 24 h, may help explain the pathology. Early brain injury encompasses mechanisms such as hyperacute ischemia, transient global hypoperfusion, endothelial dysfunction, and microvascular thrombosis.[12,13] Experimental and clinical studies have demonstrated that SAH can cause a dramatic increase in ICP immediately after aneurysm rupture,[2] sometimes exceeding mean arterial pressure, impairing both arterial and venous flow, and contributing to diffuse ischemia.

Such early cerebral hypoperfusion leads to tissue hypoxia, disruption of the blood-brain barrier, and activation of the coagulation cascade, resulting in microthrombus formation.[16] Studies have identified microvascular thrombosis in the hyperacute phase of SAH, even in the absence of angiographic vasospasm, supporting the theory that ischemic injury may occur regardless of large vessel constriction.[7,14] From an anatomical perspective, the medial frontal lobes and corpus callosum – areas typically supplied by the ACAs – are especially vulnerable to such hemodynamic shifts.[13] These territories are located near the midline and tend to have relatively poorer collateralization than the lateral cortical regions. Furthermore, the absence of an ACom eliminated the possibility of cross-filling between the left and right ACA systems. This anatomical configuration likely increased the susceptibility of both ACA territories to simultaneous hypoperfusion. In addition, the splenium of the corpus callosum, a region particularly sensitive to hypoperfusion and vulnerable to elevated ICP, supports the possibility of diffuse early brain injury mechanisms.[15] Furthermore, DWI hyperintensity in the corpus callosum, with corresponding low ADC values, indicated cytotoxic edema, suggesting the progression of irreversible infarction.

Notably, despite the absence of visible structural abnormalities in the spinal cord, the possibility of transient spinal ischemia cannot be entirely ruled out. However, given the duration and severity of the motor deficits, as well as the clear presence of bilateral cerebral infarctions, a cortical etiology remains the most plausible explanation.

Given the bilateral nature of the lesions, other rare conditions such as reversible cerebral vasoconstriction syndrome (RCVS) and reversible splenial lesion syndrome (RESLES) type II could be considered in the differential diagnosis.[5,8] Their presentation does not fully align with the clinical course and imaging findings in this case. RCVS typically presents with recurrent thunderclap headaches and multifocal vasoconstriction of cerebral arteries, often seen in young to middle-aged women.[8] This patient’s headache was not self-limiting, and cerebral angiography did not reveal the characteristic segmental narrowing and dilatation of arteries, which is atypical for RCVS. RESLES type II is known for transient diffusion-restricted lesions involving the splenium of the corpus callosum, sometimes extending into bilateral frontal white matter.[5] It is commonly associated with systemic conditions, including infection, metabolic imbalance, and withdrawal from antiepileptic drugs. The atypical distribution, absence of clinical triggers, and lack of radiological or clinical resolution further support the diagnosis of RESLES.

Therefore, differential diagnosis should consider various conditions presenting with reversible vasoconstriction or splenial lesions. However, the observed acute and multifocal ischemic changes are atypical for these reversible entities and other common splenial lesion conditions. Rather, these findings are better explained by mechanisms associated with early brain injury following SAH, including transient global hypoperfusion, endothelial dysfunction, and distal microthromboembolism.

An additional mechanism worth considering is the mechanical distortion of the ACA caused by upward brain displacement under sudden ICP changes. Contact with the falx cerebri might have caused temporary kinking or deformation of the ACA. In this case, the absence of an ACom may have further increased the vulnerability of the ACA flow. Although this was not directly observed, such deformation could transiently impair perfusion or damage the endothelium, possibly contributing to early ischemic changes. However, significant brain displacement typically requires a mass effect, such as that caused by a hematoma or extensive edema. As no such compressive lesion was observed in this case, the likelihood of this mechanism remains low. Nonetheless, this hypothesis highlights the importance of considering transient mechanical forces in the hyperacute phase of SAH.

This case underscores the complex and multifactorial nature of early cerebral injury following SAH. The findings underscore the potential for hyperacute ischemic mechanisms, including early microthrombosis and pressure-related hypoperfusion, to cause significant neurological deficits even in the absence of classical findings such as large-vessel vasospasm or hematoma. Recognizing such atypical presentations is essential for timely diagnosis and appropriate management.

CONCLUSION

This case highlights a rare and instructive presentation of SAH, where bilateral paraparesis was the initial symptom due to bilateral ACA territory infarctions. The absence of large-vessel vasospasm or spinal pathology, combined with early ischemic changes, suggests a mechanism involving hyperacute ischemia, likely driven by microthrombosis and elevated ICP. The concept of early brain injury – including transient cerebral hypoperfusion, microvascular dysfunction, and thrombotic injury – provides a valuable framework for interpreting such atypical clinical presentations. It encourages clinicians to look beyond readily apparent or simplistic diagnostic impressions and to maintain a broad differential diagnosis. This understanding may ultimately contribute to improved clinical decision-making and patient outcomes. Timely neuroimaging and early aneurysm repair are essential for accurate diagnosis and recovery in these uncommon but critical scenarios.
