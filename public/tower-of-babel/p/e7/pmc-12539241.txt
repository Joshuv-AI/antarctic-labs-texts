Recurrent peritonitis due to Shewanella putrefaciens in a peritoneal dialysis patient
Authors: Jorge Iberico Castro, Luis Castañeda Pelaez, Jesús Rojas Jaimes, Elsa Nuñez Infanzón, Delza Tarraga Gonzales, Sergio Ronceros Medrano
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractShewanella putrefaciens is a gram-negative, nonfermenting, and motile bacillus. It can be found in freshwater, saltwater, and soil. This case report presents a 36-year-old man referred from the nephrology service with recurrent secondary peritonitis associated with peritoneal dialysis. Multidrug-resistant S. putrefaciens was isolated from peritoneal fluid. The patient showed improvement after Piperacillin/tazobactam treatment. Preventive measures against nosocomial infections should be comprehensive, including aseptic techniques, regular monitoring, appropriate antibiotic treatment, and timely replacement of devices associated with infection risk. In our case, the favorable outcome was attributed to antibiotic therapy and the replacement of dialysis devices.Keywords: Shewanella putrefaciens, nosocomial infections, antibiotic resistance

Introduction

Shewanella putrefaciens is a gram-negative, nonfermenting, motile bacillus first described by Derby and Hammer in 1931. It was initially named Achromobacter putrefaciens as a result of a study conducted on putrefied butter [1]. Over time, taxonomic research resulted in its reclassification under the genera Pseudomonas and Alteromonas; eventually, it was named Shewanella in honor of James Shewan for his contribution to the microbiology of this bacterium [2].

This microorganism is mainly found in marine environments and regions with warm climates [3]. Although S. putrefaciens rarely causes infections, cases of skin and soft tissue infections as well as bacteremia and other systemic complications have been reported [4], [5], [6]. As an opportunistic pathogen, a previous study categorized infections at the skin and soft tissue level, bacteremia, at the hepatobiliary level, and miscellaneous [7]. Moreover, despite its rare association with human infections, it has been recorded in episodes of peritonitis in patients on peritoneal dialysis, where it can cause severe symptoms [8]. Shewanella is known to infect patients with hepatobiliary disorders and those with chronic kidney disease, causing peritonitis during the hemodialysis process [9], [10].

Regarding antibiotic resistance, a previous study has reported its resistance to cephalothin, vancomycin, and ampicillin in natural environments [11].

This report mainly describes a case of recurrent peritonitis caused by S. putrefaciens in a peritoneal dialysis patient.

Case

Clinical findings

A 36-year-old man patient from the nephrology service had a history of arterial hypertension and had stage 5 chronic kidney disease. Since 2019, he has been on continuous ambulatory peritoneal dialysis. He experienced an episode of secondary peritonitis in September 2023. He represented with recurrent secondary peritonitis 5 months later in April 2024 and no organism was identified on index admission. The clinical findings at that time indicated abdominal pain that had lasted for approximately 5 h. The initial cultures were negative. He was empirically treated with a 12-day course of vancomycin and ceftazidime, which resulted in clinical improvement. However, he was readmitted later that month due to cloudy peritoneal fluid.

During physical examination, the patient was afebrile and spontaneously breathing with an inspired oxygen fraction of 21 %. Furthermore, he exhibited a regular cardiac rhythm without any murmurs. Breath sounds were normal over both lung fields. He experienced diffuse pain upon palpation, but abdominal examination did not indicate the presence of masses. No ulcers were reported around the peritoneal catheter, although there was pain.

Laboratory findings

Evaluation of the peritoneal fluid yielded the following results: on April 13, 2024, upon admission, the patient had a leukocyte count of 1152/mm³ , consisting of 95 % polymorphonuclear cells and 5 % mononuclear cells. A Gram stain revealed the presence of gram-negative bacilli at the 1 + level. On April 25, peritoneal fluid cultures tested negative for common germs.

On May 13, S. putrefaciens was isolated from the dialysis fluid. It was assumed that the bacteria were acquired from the hospital, with the case occurring in Lima, Peru. The patient reported that he had not visited risky locations, such as beaches or the ocean, where he could have acquired the bacteria. In addition, on May 14, 2024, the patient’s leukocyte count decreased to 320/mm³ , with a composition of 40 % polymorphonuclear cells and 60 % mononuclear cells. The hemogram results indicated leukocytes at 4270/mm³ , with neutrophil at 68.5 %, hemoglobin at 13.7 g/dL, platelets at 168,000/µL, and C-reactive protein at 145.69 mg/L.

On May 15, 2024, the leukocyte count increased to 440/mm³ , indicating an equal distribution of 50 % polymorphonuclear and 50 % mononuclear cells. Moreover, on May 16, the hemoglobin level increased to 14.4 g/dL, leukocytes were at 4590/mm³ , platelets rose to 185,000/µL, and C-reactive protein decreased to 81.4 mg/L. By May 20, the hemoglobin levels had increased to 16.5 g/dL, leukocytes had increased to 6670/mm³ , and C-reactive protein markedly dropped to 11.4 mg/L. Then, on May 21, 2024, the leukocyte counts significantly decreased to 10/mm³ , with 10 % polymorphonuclear cells and 90 % mononuclear cells. On May 22, the hemoglobin level reached 18 g/dL, the leukocyte count was 6490/mm³ , neutrophils accounted for 69.4 %, and platelets increased to 214,000/µL.

Finally, by May 25, the leukocytes had increased to 10,080/mm³ with 79.7 % neutrophils, hemoglobin was at 16.7 g/dL, platelets decreased to 163,000/µL, and peritoneal fluid cultures tested negative for common organisms (Table 1).

Cultures were prepared and grown on Trypticase Soy Agar, blood, chocolate, and MacConkey agar media. Gram staining indicated the presence of gram-negative bacilli (Fig. 1). After a 24-h incubation, the blood, MacConkey, and chocolate agar plates exhibited large, smooth, nonpigmented, and shiny colonies with uniform borders. These colonies tested positive for oxidase and catalase. The recommendations outlined in Bergey’s Manual [12] were followed for identification, and a sensitivity study was conducted using the VITEK®2 automated system, following the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CSLI) criteria.

The antibiogram showed sensitivity to Amikacin, Piperacillin/Tazobactam, Imipenem, and Meropenem; resistance to Ceftazidime, Aztreonam, and Ciprofloxacin; and an intermediate response to Cefepime (Table 1).

Treatment

The treatment plan included intravenous administration of Piperacillin/Tazobactam (4 g/500 mg) every 24 h for 21 days, allowing the patient to finish their treatment at the end of discharge, as well as peritoneal catheter removal and placement of a central venous catheter for dialysis. The evolution was favorable, with no recurrence of infection from the start of treatment (Piperacillin/Tazobactam 4 g/500 mg) until the 15-day follow-up, during which the patient was discharged without fever. As part of the study of the antimicrobial resistance of in-hospital agents, approval from the Institutional Ethics Committee (N°60–2023) of the Almenara Healthcare Network was obtained.

Discussion

The 36-year-old patient with hypertension undergoing peritoneal dialysis had a percutaneous Tenckhoff catheter. S. putrefaciens was isolated from dialysis fluid. It is well known that the presence of an invasive external device, such as peritoneal dialysis catheter, increases the risk of developing a nosocomial infection. Therefore, hospital systems and personnel must diligently and meticulously clean and check such devices to prevent infections [13]. It is inferred that the infection in the present case occurred during the peritoneal dialysis process the patient was undergoing. Due to bacterial isolation in the dialysis fluid, the use of the Tenckhoff catheter as a risk factor for contamination, colonization, and subsequent bacterial infection.

Although S. putrefaciens rarely causes infection, soft tissue infections, pneumonia, and abscesses in the central nervous system have been reported, which were particularly associated with the use of external drainage systems [14]. This bacterial genus thrives in diverse environments, including freshwater, saltwater, and even soil [15]. A previous study reported a case of bacteremia and meningoencephalitis in a female patient that spread through the bloodstream after sustaining an injury while swimming in saltwater [16]. Contrary to our findings, bacterial dissemination did not occur in this case. Probably due to the administration of antibiotics during treatment depending on the bacterial load as well as the patient’s immunocompetent status, as observed in the hemogram, resulting in a localized infection.

As a patient with severe chronic kidney disease, erythropoietin production was impaired, resulting in a continuous need for this component. CRP “C-reactive protein” levels ranging from 11.4 to 145.69 mg/L indicated the presence of an inflammatory process. The total leukocyte count ranged from 4270 and 10,800/mm³ , likely influenced by the patient’s antibiotic therapy and the hematological dysfunction associated with chronic nephropathy. In addition, the patient exhibited mild thrombocytopenia, with platelet counts between 185,000 and 214,000/µL. A previous study in Japan reported a case of infection and septicemia caused by S. algae in a hemodialysis patient who presented laboratory patterns similar to our case (41 and 209 mg/L; 6300 and 80,000/mm³) as well as elevated inflammatory markers, although leukocytosis and thrombocytopenia were absent [15], which aligns with certain aspects of our findings.

Infection caused by S. putrefaciens can remain localized without progressing to bacteremia, as previously reported. In such cases, biofilm formation may play a pivotal role in localized adherence [14]. It can also lead to hospital outbreaks. For example, in a study in Korea, S. algae was isolated from 25 case patients, and S. putrefaciens was isolated from 6 case patients; both species were not isolated from any single case patient. S. algae and S. putrefaciens were isolated from blood, for 9 patients (29.0 %, 8 with S. algae and 1 with S. putrefaciens); from bile, for 8 (25.8 %, 7 with S. algae and 1 with S. putrefaciens);from ascitic fluid, for 8 (25.8 %, 5 with S. algae and 3 with S. putrefaciens); from skin and soft tissue, for 5 (16.1 %, 4 with S. algae and 1 with S. putrefaciens); and from stool, for 1 (3.2 %) with S. algae, and associated with risk factors, such as sharing the same cup and having a device such as a catheter [17]. The combination of appropriate antibiotic therapy and the replacement of infection-prone devices is critical for improving the patient’s condition [14].

Conclusion

Multidrug-resistant S. putrefaciens was isolated from peritoneal fluid. The treatment plan included intravenous administration of Piperacillin/Tazobactam (4 g/500 mg) every 24 h for 21 days, allowing the patient to finish their treatment at the end of discharge, as well as peritoneal catheter removal and placement of a central venous catheter for dialysis. After treatment, the patient demonstrated favorable progression, remained afebrile, was hemodynamically stable, and was ready for discharge.
