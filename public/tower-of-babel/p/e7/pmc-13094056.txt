Extracorporeal cardiopulmonary resuscitation in a large tertiary center in the Czech Republic: three years’ experience and future directions
Authors: Filip Burša, Michal Frelich, Peter Sklienka, Marek Bebej, Juraj Rodina, Nela Walachová, Jan Varady, Jan Neiser, Radan Swaczyna, Zuzana Kučerová, Marcela Káňová, David Holeš, Michaela Lišková, Michal Burda, Martin Porzer, Jan Máca

Background

Patients undergoing conventional cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CCPR) due to out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) have a poor prognosis, with favorable long-term neurologic outcome achieved in only approximately 10% of cases [1–3]. CPR using extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO), extracorporeal cardiopulmonary resuscitation (ECPR), has been shown to improve these outcomes for selected patients with refractory cardiac arrest (CA) [4, 5], achieving hospital discharge rate of up to 30%, with good neurologic outcomes [6, 7]. ECPR is, however, not yet universally considered the standard of care, and it is available only in a limited number of highly specialized centers. This is largely caused by the challenges associated with implementing the ECPR program in larger regions due to the technological demands, need for highly experienced personnel, substantial costs, and, last but not least, because current evidence from randomized controlled trials (ARREST trial, Prague OHCA and Inception trial) shows slightly conflicting results [8–10].

The ECPR program cannot be implemented universally and the following must be considered: high population density (ensuring sufficient case volume), quality and type of roads (ensuring favorable transport speed), regional coverage by emergency service centers, and the logistics of the actual ECMO cannulation in the hospital or on scene. All this very much depends on the quality of the chain of survival, including the willingness of the bystanders to provide first aid and resuscitate if they see a collapse. Crucially, the coordination of the dispatch and emergency services personnel as well as the experience of the ECPR team must be at a very high level. The clear definition of indication and exclusion criteria is also important [11].

The primary objective of this study is to describe overall survival after ECPR. The secondary objective is to evaluate peri-ECPR parameters (e.g. time interval between pre-hospital and hospital management, initial rhythm) in relation to neurological outcomes, and to describe the long-term quality of life six months after ECPR.

Methods

This observational study was conducted in Ostrava and the Moravian-Silesian Region of the Czech Republic, comprising nearly 300,000 inhabitants in Ostrava and almost 1.2 million in the region. The ECPR program was launched at the ECMO Center of the University Hospital Ostrava (UHO) in collaboration with the Moravian-Silesian Emergency medical service (EMS) on January 1, 2022. An analysis of prospectively collected data from the registry of the Ostrava ECMO Center was conducted in accordance with the STROBE guidelines, covering the period from the launch of the OHCA ECPR program (January 1, 2022) to December 31, 2024. ICU and 6 months survival, neurologic outcomes on day 30 and quality of life at 6 months post-CA were analyzed. Long-term outcomes were evaluated using a questionnaire (see below) completed by patients who agreed to participate and signed an informed consent form.

The ECPR protocol was made available to all ground ambulance and helicopter emergency medical service (HEMS) staff and staff training was conducted (see Supplement 1). Ten minutes after the initiation of advanced CPR, typically following the third defibrillation attempt, the on-scene physician contacted the 24/7 operating ECMO center in Ostrava.

If both the attending physician and the ECMO center agreed that the particular patient could benefit from ECPR, the patient was promptly transferred to the emergency department (ED) for further evaluation under continuous mechanical chest compression (LUCAS, Lund University Cardiac Arrest System; Physio-Control Inc/Jolife AB, Lund, Sweden). Upon arrival, the resuscitation team (consisting of emergency department personnel, typically comprising three nurses and one physician) and the ECMO team (consisting of an ECMO physician, a nurse, a perfusionist, a cardiologist, or if necessary, cardiac surgeon) were already prepared with the required equipment and all available information about the CCPR was shared.

Immediate diagnostic procedures were conducted, including echocardiography focusing on the presence or absence of the return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC), of coordinated cardiac activity, and potential reversible causes of cardiac arrest, excluding cardiac tamponade. Chest ultrasound was performed to exclude fluid or pneumothorax, and the abdominal cavity was examined to exclude hemoperitoneum. Based on the consultation, the ED team and the ECMO team reached a consensus on the suitability of the indication for ECMO cannulation, which usually required approximately 3 min.

The cannulation was guided by real-time ultrasound using the Seldinger technique and the GE Vivid S70N with 3Msc probe (General Electric, 1 Neumann Way, Cincinnati, OH 45215, USA). The Cardiohelp device (Getinge AB, Cardiohelp, HLS circuit) was used for ECMO support. Subsequently, a decision was made regarding the need for coronary angiography and, if indicated, the procedure was performed with ongoing ECMO support. The patient's management was guided by the post-resuscitation care protocol, which included the administration of anticoagulation therapy with argatroban, management of sedation, antimicrobials, monitoring, anticoagulation targets, etc. (see Supplement 2 A and 2B).

Recorded data included basic demographics (age, sex, height, weight, comorbidities), times of collapse, ED admission, ECMO start, ECMO canulation success, ECMO support duration, ICU and 6-month survival, Cerebral Performance Category (CPC; values of 1–2 were considered indicative of good neurologic outcome), discharge home, and return to work. Long-term quality of life assessment was evaluated using the Short Form 36 Health Survey (SF36), questionnaire EQ-5D-5L (EuroQol 5 Dimensions 5 Levels) with Quality-Adjusted Life Year (QALY), and the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) at least 6 months after the arrest via patient interview. SF36 is a standardized questionnaire measuring the quality of life in 8 domains, each rated 0 (worst) to 100 (best), with scores < 40 indicating reduced quality of life compared to the general population. EQ-5D-5L is based on self-assessment of health on a visual analog scale (VAS) from 1–5 (1 is best). It is a descriptive system that classifies five domains: mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain and anxiety. The results can be used to calculate overall VAS score (0–100, 100 is best) and Quality-Adjusted Life Years (QALYs), an indicator that combines life expectancy and quality of life into a single value. Values above 70 indicate a maximum of slightly reduced health. The HADS is a screening questionnaire for anxiety and depression. It is based on a scale ranging from 0 to 21 and comprises seven items for anxiety (A) and seven for depression (D). Each item is rated from 0 to 3 (where 0 is best). Values greater than 11 may indicate anxiety or depression. The HADS assessment was performed after being granted the Master User License.

Statistics

The level of statistical significance was set at 0.05. Categorical variables were characterized by the absolute count and the relative frequency (in %). Fisher's exact test was employed to assess the statistical difference between groups. Numeric variables were described with medians and IQR (25–75%) and statistically tested with a non-parametric two-sample Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Resulting p-values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Holm method. All statistical analyses were performed in R version 4.1.3. R Core Team [12]. To calculate long-term outcomes, the average and median scores were determined for each domain.

Besides descriptive statistics, Physical Component Summary (PCS) and Mental Component Summary (MCS) were calculated from SF36 according to the original scoring algorithm described by Ware et al. [13]. Raw domain scores (Physical Functioning, Role Physical, Bodily Pain, General Health, Vitality, Social Functioning, Role Emotional, and Mental Health) were first standardized into z-scores using the U.S. population normative means and standard deviations. Z scores were calculated for each of the eight domains using the formula: z = (raw score − US mean)/US standard deviation. Next, conversion using factor coefficients for PSC and MCS according to Ware et al. (this gave us the Z value) [13]. Finally, T = 50 + (10 × Z) was calculated, which represents PCS and MCS.

The EQ-5D-5L digit code was converted to utility value (range from 1-full health to 0-death) according to the formula: U = 1 − (C + M + SC + UA + PD + AD), where C = constant for deviation from full health (C = 0,047); M, SC, UA, PD, AD = price penalties for individual dimensions (M – mobility, SC—Self-Care, UA—Usual Activities, PD—Pain/Discomfort, AD – Anxiety/Depression). German EQ-5D-5L value set was use as the most appropriate for the Czech population [14]. Therefore, the calculated utilities and QALYs reflect the preferences of the German general population (see Limitations). Simply, utility = 1-total sum of penalties. QALYs were subsequently calculated as the product of survival time and the corresponding utility value.

The study was registered on the public database ClinicalTrials.gov on 20th June 2025 (NCT07031466) and approved by the Ethics Committee of UHO (reference number 139/2025).

Results

Over the three years, 227 patients were considered for ECPR, and ultimately, 74 OHCA patients received ECPR, with cannulation success rate 93.2% (69/74). On total, 227 patients were consulted, 162 of whom were transported to the ED and 85 (37.4%; 85/227) cannulated after considering all available information and contraindications. Some of these patients had ROSC (n = 11) and are therefore not included in the final evaluation (see Fig. 1). Cannulation-related complications included the inability to find or cannulate the vessels (3 cases), inability to insert the guidewires (1 case), and perforation of the cannula into the free abdominal cavity (1 case). Patients who were initially indicated for transport to the ECMO center but developed ROSC during transport with persisting signs of cardiogenic shock were often connected to ECMO but were not included in this study (only those cannulated under continuous mechanical CPR were included). According to SF36 results (see Fig. 3A), the average value in bodily pain was 89.1. Similarly, high scores were reported in the emotional role and mental health domains (means of 91.8 and 82.3, respectively). Vitality showed the lowest average of 67.5. The mean global health score was 68.8, mean PCS and MCS scores were 49.2 ± 7.7 and 54.7 ± 4.9, respectively (the norm is between 40 and 60).

The EQ-5D-5L questionnaire yielded an average VAS score of 76 (range 25–100, scale 0–100, 100 is best) and median score of 90 (see Fig. 3B). The average mobility score was 1.8 (scale 1–5, 1 is best), 25% reported more serious limitations. The average self-care score was 1.3 (only one person reported a severe disability). The average score for usual activities was 1.5 (although several participants reported experiencing mild to moderate limitations). The average score for pain/discomfort was 1.6 and the average anxiety/depression score was 1.3. QALY values were calculated based on these results adjusted to a one-year period (in our surveillance, only six months were evaluated). The median QALY per year was 0.90 (ranging 0.25—1.00). The estimate of ≥ 0.58 QALY/year (indicating favorable health status) was reported by three-quarters of the respondents.

Median HADS scores were 2.5 for anxiety and 2.5 for depression (scale 0–21, 0 is best; see Fig. 3C). In all, normal range of anxiety and depression was observed in 83% of participants, while a critical level of depression was reported by 8% of patients. No patient reported a critical level of anxiety.

Discussion

In 74 patients over three years, we observed the results of our ECPR program in this study. We confirmed the effectiveness and feasibility of ECPR program in our region. Although the median collapse-to-ECMO interval (the most critical period) was 75 min, the ICU survival rate was 39.1% (27/69), with 27.5% (19/69) patients discharged directly home, with 33.3% (23/69) surviving at six months. Good neurologic outcome with full self-sufficiency was 29.0% (20/69) at 30 days (in the surviving group 74.0%; 20/27).

Our data are comparable with centers with long-standing ECPR program. Lamhaut et al. reported data from Paris, where the ECPR program began in 2011 (first period). The best survival rate of 38% was achieved in their study in the second period (after 2015, characterized by prehospital cannulation and a stricter protocol) among 27 ECPR patients. In their study, the duration of no-flow plus low-flow time (i.e., collapse-to-ECMO) was 73.4 min and ECMO implementation time was 19.3 min [15]. Bělohlávek et al. reported in their OHCA Prague study a mean collapse-to-hospital arrival time to be 49 min the in invasive strategy group (which is early intra-arrest transport and ECPR) and 60 min in the standard strategy group (which is on-site CCPR). Collapse-to-ECMO time was 61 and 62 min, respectively with ECMO implantation times of 12 and 16 min. Survival with CPC 1–2 on Day 30 was reported for 30.6% in the invasive strategy vs 18.2% in the standard strategy group (p = 0.02) [9]. Suverein et al. reported in their INCEPTION trial the arrest-to-start of cannulation time of 58± 13 min and arrest-to-ECMO interval of 74 min (IQR 63 to 87; n = 70). The median cannulation time was almost double (20 min). This could have influenced the hospital discharge rate, which was only 20% [8]. In the ARREST trial, the time from EMS call (so not exactly collapse) to ECMO was 59 min, with both survival to hospital discharge and 6-month survival rates of 43% [10]. Secondary analyses of the above-mentioned RCTs showed association of ECPR with improved neurologic outcomes [16, 17] and pooled data from ARREST and Prague OHCA studies showed better survival with favorable neurologic outcomes at 180 days in the ECPR vs control group (32.4% vs 19.7%; p = 0.015; 47.1% vs 28.3% in the shockable rhythm-only analysis; p = 0.01) [18]. ECPR is beneficial only for a narrowly defined group. Although late ROSC is possible, the selection of the most suitable candidates for ECPR after 8–24 min of CCPR (with an optimal time of 16 min) should be considered in order to avoid a long interval between collapse and ECMO and thus a worsening of the outcome [19].

The optimal system configuration should aim to start ECMO within 60 min of patient collapse which maximizes the likelihood of good outcomes [4, 7]. The pre-hospital care time, which was 55 min in our study, is likely one of the critical factors affecting the outcomes. Although we did not confirm a statistically significant association between the collapse-to-ECMO interval and good neurologic outcome, we observed a good neurologic outcome in 4/9 of patients with a collapse-to-ECMO interval under 60 min compared to 16/60 of patients with an interval above 60 min. Where logistically justified, HEMS was used for transport to shorten the pre-hospital interval in our study.

Hospital management and cannulation process should be very fast, as this can also influence the outcome. In the INCEPTION trial, cannulation was delayed compared to ARREST or OHCA Prague trials, and the neurologic outcome was poorer than in the other two studies (CPC 1–2 was only 15%) [16]. Hospital management in Ostrava seems to be highly efficient because the interval between the ambulance arrival and functional ECMO run is 19 min. This interval involves parking in front of the ED, transport of the patient to the emergency bed, information handover, echocardiography, chest and abdominal ultrasound, and ECMO cannulation and initiation itself (which usually takes approx. 10 min). We, therefore, believe that in our system, the efforts for time shortening should be rather focused on optimizing the pre-hospital processes than in-hospital management as in the latter, not much room is left for improvement.

The long collapse-to-ECMO interval, which averaged 75 min in our study, is the primary limitation to the ECPR approach. One strategy to significantly speed up the process is to implement mobile ECPR, i.e., to dispatch ECMO team to the patient on the scene. Based on the data from France, it appears that this system can reduce the collapse-to-ECMO interval by approx. 20 min, which is associated with a survival rate improvement to 38% [15].

The initial rhythm is also related to the neurological outcome. Ventricular fibrillation (VF) is considered the most favorable initial rhythm as it can be a surrogate for treatable ischemic heart disease [20]. In ECPR patients with VF, Rob et al. demonstrated an overall CPC 1–2 at discharge in more than 30% of patients, compared to 3% in patients presenting with asystole [21]. Their study also demonstrated the importance of the rhythm at the time of hospital admission. Of patients with VF both as the initial and on-admission rhythm, 43% had a CPC 1–2 at discharge, compared to 12% of asystolic patients on admission. Our findings are consistent with theirs; 30 days after arrest, 35.4% (17/48) of VF patients had a CPC 1–2. In the same study, only one out of six patients (17%) presenting with asystole followed by VF achieved CPC 1–2. In our data, only 5% (1/20) of patients with a CPC 1–2 were asystolic. Patients with PEA should be considered for ECPR, especially in cases with high suspicion of the etiology of pulmonary embolism (PE) representing a treatable pathology, unlike patients with trauma-induced bleeding, asphyxia etc. Ultrasound examination can be quite crucial in these cases, capable of confirming the PE etiology or ruling out severe bleeding. In patients with PEA, Rob et al. reported overall outcome of CPC 1–2 in 13%, increasing to 25% in the subgroup with PEA as both the initial and admission rhythm. In our dataset, favourable neurological outcomes on day 30 were reported in 33.3% (2/6) of patients with PEA due to PE.

Long-term outcomes and quality of life were assessed in our patients using standardized tests, namely SF36, EQ-5D-5L and HADS questionnaires. According to SF-36, the PCS was 49.2 ± 7.7, which is close to the population average and the MCS was 54.7 ± 4.9 even above average. Yonis et al. describe the long-term outcomes of 2,552 cardiac arrest survivors (without ECMO) in Denmark according to SF-12 [22]. The calculation of PCS and MCS scores is the same for both questionnaires (SF-36 and SF-12). Fifty-six per cent of respondents completed the survey in their study, which is similar to our recruitment rate of 52.2% (12/23). PCS was 43.3 ± 12.3 and MCS was 52.9 ± 8.3, which was slightly less than in our cohort of patients after ECPR (note that in our data, 6-month follow-up is reported, compared to the one year and more in the study by Yonis). Overall, pain was present but, in most cases, it was not severe or influencing the quality of life. Mental health and emotional state outcomes were also favorable, with low incidence of anxiety and depression. In the vitality and fatigue domains, however, poorer outcomes were observed. The average global health score was 68.8, indicating slightly negative subjective perception of health.

According to EQ-5D-5L, the self-assessment of health was in the highest quartile in our study (mean 76; median 90) and most surviving patients were completely self-sufficient, having only minor problems with mobility or usual activities. In a retrospective study by Haskins et al. on OHCA (without ECMO) with initial shockable rhythms, the highest score ≥ 80 was observed in bystander-shocked survivors compared with those shocked by first responders and paramedics [23]. The median QALY/year in our study was 0.90, which is almost normal value. The patients generally reported good results in terms of pain and mental health or anxiety. The domains of vitality (energy), subjective perception of health, and physical functioning showed the greatest need for improvement.

In a 1-year follow up, Yonis et al. reported anxiety in 73.0% and depression in 79.7% of CCPR survivors according to the HADS score [22]. Our results showed HADS anxiety scores of 83.3 and depression scores of 86.1, suggesting comparable outcomes.

Limitations

The presented study has several limitations. First, it is a monocentric study, inherently leading to a limited number of eligible patients. Outdated U.S. datasets were used to calculate the PCS and MCS, since Czech and other European datasets do not permit the direct calculation of these parameters according to the original methodology. The German EQ-5D-5L value set was used, despite certain socioeconomic differences between the Czech and German populations. The calculated utilities and QALYs therefore reflect the preferences of the German general population. In the context of the ECPR, this bias is towards underestimating an already relatively good result. In many cases (almost half), we were unable to obtain patient consent to complete the six-month quality of life questionnaire, which might have introduced selection bias and limited the generalizability of the long-term outcome analysis.

Conclusion

The results of the ECPR program for OHCA in Ostrava, launched in 2022, seem comparable with those of long-standing ECPR centers, with a survival rate of 39.1%, despite a collapse-ECMO interval of 75 min. A good neurologic outcome (CPC 1–2) was observed in 29.0% of patients at 30 days after CA. In the quality-of-life assessment six months after the arrest, survivors reported low levels of pain, anxiety, and depression. The majority of survivors were completely self-sufficient and experienced only minor problems with mobility or usual activities, with a median QALY/year of 0.90.
