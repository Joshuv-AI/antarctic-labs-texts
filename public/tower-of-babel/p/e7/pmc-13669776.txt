Reply to Singh and Srivastav’s Letter to the Editor re: Marc A. Furrer, Benjamin Lyttwin, Michael S. Pärli, et al. Incidental Prostate Cancer Is an Uncommon but Clinically Non-negligible Cause of Death in Selected Long-term Survivors of Urothelial Carcinoma: Oncological Outcomes and a Proposed Follow-up Strategy from a Tertiary Referral Center. Eur Urol Open Sci 2026;86:10–19
Authors: Marc A. Furrer, Patrick Y. Wuethrich, Niall M. Corcoran
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

We thank the authors for their detailed, clinically oriented comment on our recently published study [1], and appreciate the opportunity to clarify the points raised. The issues discussed are highly relevant, particularly regarding event attribution, clinical decision-making, and the practical implementation of surveillance strategies in this complex and heterogeneous patient population.

The discussion regarding potential misattribution of metastatic events between urothelial carcinoma and prostate cancer is conceptually important. However, this concern may be overstated in the context of our study. In our cohort, recurrence classification and cause-specific mortality were determined through comprehensive clinical, radiological, and histopathological assessment as appropriate within a structured, longitudinal follow-up framework. While overlapping metastatic patterns between these malignancies are recognized, this reflects a broader clinical challenge rather than a limitation unique to our dataset. Importantly, our data provide no evidence suggesting that such misattribution significantly influenced outcome reporting or introduced systematic bias into clinical management.

Similarly, the recommendation to anchor surveillance escalation to mandatory lesion verification strategies, such as PSMA PET/CT (Prostate-specific membrane antigen positron emission tomography/computed tomography) combined with biopsy, may not fully reflect real-world feasibility or clinical necessity. The follow-up strategy proposed in our original manuscript was intentionally pragmatic and risk-adapted, emphasizing proportionality and clinical judgment within a selective survivorship framework. While advanced imaging and tissue confirmation can be informative in selected cases, given the low absolute event rate observed and the often-indolent course of incidental prostate cancer in this setting, their routine implementation may not be justified.

The comment also suggests that the surveillance protocol lacks clearly defined actionable clinical thresholds. However, this interpretation does not fully align with the intent of our study. The proposed framework was not designed as a prescriptive treatment algorithm, but rather as a tool to identify a small subset of patients at risk for clinically meaningful progression who may benefit from further evaluation. Given the heterogeneity of patient comorbidity, functional status, competing risks, and evolving therapeutic options, decisions regarding escalation to systemic therapy or additional interventions must remain individualized and cannot be reduced to a single predefined trigger.

Concerns regarding over-investigation or premature treatment escalation based solely on biochemical recurrence are valid and were explicitly addressed in the original manuscript. We emphasized a conservative management approach, including delayed imaging guided by PSA kinetics and avoidance of intervention in the absence of clinically significant disease. In this context, the comment partially conflates the identification of recurrence with the initiation of treatment, which represent distinct steps in clinical care.

The authors appropriately highlight that prostate cancer–specific mortality is rare, which is consistent with our findings. However, the suggestion that our results may be interpreted as supporting universal surveillance does not reflect our conclusions. We consistently advocate for a selective, risk-adapted approach limited to patients with favorable urothelial cancer outcomes and higher-risk prostate cancer features.

In summary, the comment raises important considerations, but several concerns extend beyond the data or rely on unsupported assumptions, and issues were already acknowledged and incorporated into our study.
