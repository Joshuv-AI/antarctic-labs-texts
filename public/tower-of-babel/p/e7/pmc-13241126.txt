Health-related quality of life and its associated factors among adult patients with type II diabetes attending primary health in Amazônia Ocidental
Authors: Kaira Carla de Oliveira Portilho, Gina Torres Rego Monteiro, Maurício Teixeira Leite de Vasconcellos, Thatiana Lameira Maciel Amaral
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTContext:Diabetes mellitus (DM) is increasingly prevalent and associated with complications worldwide, negatively impacting the health-related quality of life (HRQoL) of affected people.Aim:To analyze the factors associated with HRQoL components in people with diabetes registered in primary healthcare.Settings and Design:A cross-sectional study was conducted among people aged 18 years or older with type-2 DM registered in Family Health Strategy units.Materials and Methods:The dependent variable of the study was HRQoL and its components, as assessed by the Medical Outcomes Study 36-item Short Form Health Survey (SF-36).Statistical Analysis Used:An ordinal hierarchical multivariate regression was used to estimate odds ratios (ORs) with a 95% confidence level.Results:Findings indicate that the physical functioning component was the most affected (median <50 points). Factors associated with overall HRQoL in people with DM included: Stress (OR: 2.22), anxiety (OR: 1.77), insomnia (OR: 1.80), and depression (OR: 2.22). The physical functioning component was also associated with female gender (OR: 1.86) and lower educational level (OR: 1.71). Other factors influenced the remaining HRQoL components, such as polypharmacy and the presence of comorbidities (respiratory and genitourinary diseases).Conclusions:These data are important for rethinking health prevention strategies in primary care, taking into account the factors that influence different HRQoL components in people with DM.

Introduction

Health conditions significantly impact the quality of life, which in turn is influenced by these conditions. This is a relevant dynamic for individuals with chronic non-communicable diseases (NCDs), such as diabetes mellitus (DM).[1] Understanding the factors that influence quality of life is essential for the effective management of NCDs. In turn, they affect treatment adherence and an individual’s ability to cope, leading to increased morbidity and mortality.[2]

DM has a complex and multifactorial etiology involving environmental, genetic, and behavioral factors that impair health-related quality of life (HRQoL), potentially causing feelings of hopelessness and distress and requiring comprehensive healthcare.[3] Globally, the total number of deaths attributed to type-2 DM increased by 43.0% from 2007 to 2017, culminating in 1.37 million deaths in the latter year.[4]

In Brazil, DM is recognized as a significant public health problem, with a self-reported prevalence of 10.2%, ranging from 5.6% in Rio Branco, Acre, to 12.1% in São Paulo.[5] The high prevalence of DM and its complications highlights the importance of directing investments toward prevention initiatives, disease control, and long-term care.[6] In primary healthcare, early diagnosis, medication management, home visits, lifestyle modification, and promotion of a healthy diet and exercise are key interventions to reduce health complications in this population.[7] Therefore, this study aimed to analyze the factors associated with HRQoL components in people with DM registered in primary healthcare in Rio Branco, Acre.

Subjects and Methods

This is a cross-sectional study conducted using data from the Chronic Diseases Study from the Quality in Health Perspective (Edoc-Quali). The research included individuals diagnosed with DM, aged 18 years or older, registered at the Family Health Strategy (FHS) units located in the urban area of Rio Branco, the capital of Acre. Exclusion criteria were individuals with impairments that hindered communication or understanding of the questions, and pregnant women.

Rio Branco has 68 FHS units distributed in two districts and located in 44 facilities, 38 of which are located in the urban area and comprise the area of interest of the study. The municipality covers an area of 8,834.942 km2, located in the Lower Acre region of the Brazilian Amazon. According to the 2010 census, the population of Rio Branco was approximately 336,038 inhabitants, with the majority living in the urban area (91.8%),[8] and a Municipal Human Development Index of 0.727 in 2010.[9]

Sampling was conducted using a two-stage cluster sampling method. In the first stage, 30 FHS units were randomly selected, and in the second stage, individuals with DM were selected within each unit. Sampling was calculated based on a prevalence of 50%, a maximum acceptable difference of 7%, a confidence level of 95%, and a design effect of 1.5, with an additional 20% added for potential losses. The study included 324 individuals with type 2 DM; however, using weighting techniques, the results were adjusted to represent the characteristics of an estimated population of 2492 individuals. For more information on sampling procedures and data collection, see Amaral et al., 2023.[10]

After randomly selecting and identifying the addresses of the participants, a community health worker delivered an invitation to the patient’s home containing all the information about the study and the date and time of the visit to the health unit. The interviews for the project followed the regulations of Resolution CNS No. 466/2012, which deals with ethics in research involving human subjects, and was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the Universidade Federal do Acre, approval opinion No. 2.753.401, with all participants signing the informed consent form. At the scheduled time, in designated rooms provided by the unit, an electronic questionnaire was applied, and physical assessments were performed, including anthropometric measurements. Vital signs were recorded, medical prescription data were written down, and biological samples (blood and urine) were collected for laboratory analysis.

The dependent variable, HRQoL, was assessed using 36 relevant items in the Medical Outcomes Study Short-Form Health Survey (SF-36). The SF-36 consists of 11 questions and 36 items and assesses eight components of quality of life divided into two groups: Physical health (physical functioning; role limitations—physical; pain; general health perception) and mental health (vitality; social functioning; role limitation—mental; mental health), as well as a question comparing current health status with that of one year ago. The components are represented by scores ranging from 0 (no perceived quality of life) to 100 (maximum perceived quality of life). This scale has been validated in the Brazilian population.[11] Scores were divided into tertiles: First tertile (lowest HRQoL), second tertile (intermediate HRQoL), and third tertile (highest HRQoL).

The independent variables assessed included sociodemographic characteristics (with categories in parentheses): Gender (male; female), age (up to 59 years old; 60 years and older), marital status (with partner; without a partner), education level (high school and above; up to elementary school; or lower educational level), and skin color (white; non-white).

The following health status and lifestyle variables were included: Body mass index (BMI), calculated as weight (kg) divided by height squared (m2), and classified as global obesity with BMI ≥30 kg/m2;[12] stress (feeling stressed was defined as “often” or “always,” whereas not feeling stressed was defined as “never,” “rarely,” or “sometimes”); smoking status (current or former smoker; never smoked); alcohol consumption (current or former drinker; never drank). According to the World Health Organization (WHO), polypharmacy is defined as the routine and concomitant use of four or more medications by a patient.[13] Therefore, polypharmacy was considered as the use of ≥4 medications.

Self-reported morbidities included: Depression, anxiety, insomnia, hypertension, dyslipidemia, respiratory diseases (defined as asthma, bronchitis, and emphysema), genitourinary diseases (including urinary tract infections, kidney disease, and chronic kidney disease), and other cardiovascular diseases (including stroke, myocardial infarction, heart failure, and other heart problems such as arrhythmia and fibrillation).

To assess the healthcare received, the following were considered: Having had an appointment in the past 3 months (yes; no) and having had a home visit in the past 2 months (yes; no). To assess DM management, the following were considered: Hospitalization due to unstable glycemia (yes; no) and adherence to therapy (using the Batalla test for diabetics). According to the Brazilian Diabetes Society, hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c%) levels close to 7% are considered.[3] Therefore, for the analysis of glycemic control in this population, diabetes control is defined as HbA1c < 7.0% and uncontrolled as HbA1c ≥ 7.0%.

A hierarchical ordinal multivariate regression was performed to control for confounding variables in the association between the listed HRQoL components in people with DM and the independent variables. The hierarchical modeling proposed in this study serves as a viable alternative for epidemiological investigations involving a large number of covariates. The hierarchy of independent variables is defined within the conceptual framework and maintained throughout the data analysis, allowing for the identification of variables most strongly associated with the outcome of interest. In this study, the model of social determinants of health, proposed by Dahlgreen and Whitehead in 1991, was used as the basis for allocating and distributing variables into blocks.[14]

The block modeling procedure was used, where HRQoL was initially adjusted for the distal variables: “gender,” “age group,” “skin color,” “marital status,” “education,” “appointment in the last 3 months,” and “home visits in the last 2 months.” In intermediate block 1, the variables “smoking,” “alcohol consumption,” “obesity,” “adherence,” “dyslipidemia,” and “duration of diagnosis” were introduced. In intermediate block 2, the variables “stress,” “anxiety,” “insomnia,” and “diabetes mellitus control” were included. Finally, in the proximal block, the variables “hospitalization for unstable glycemia,” “polypharmacy,” “depression,” “hypertension,” “other cardiovascular diseases,” “respiratory diseases,” and “genitourinary diseases” were included.

The inclusion of variables was based on the level of significance (P < 0.20) observed in the bivariate analysis and biological plausibility. Adjustment was performed within each block of the model, with significant variables (P < 0.05) from each previous block retained in the final model. All analyses took into account the effect of sampling design and calibrated weights of observations. Data analyses were performed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS), IBM Corp., Armonk, New York, USA., version 20.0, for Windows.

Results

Among the 2492 people with DM, the physical and mental components had the largest interquartile range, with the physical aspect component having a median lower than 50 points. The physical functioning and vitality components were symmetrical, with the first having a 65% interquartile range. In addition, the physical functioning, pain, general health, vitality, social functioning, role limitation—mental, and mental health components all had medians higher than 50, with the mental health component having the highest median (72.0) among the scales [Figure 1].

Significant differences in physical functioning and role limitation—physical were found with respect to gender, age group, education, stress, insomnia, other cardiovascular diseases, arterial hypertension, and polypharmacy. Only marital status, depression, and anxiety were significantly associated with the physical functioning component, whereas hospitalization for unstable glycemia was significantly associated with the role limitation—physical component. Regarding pain and general health perception, statistical significance was found for perceived stress, depression, anxiety, insomnia, other cardiovascular disease, and genitourinary disease. Gender, skin color, obesity, hypertension, hospitalization for unstable blood glucose, appointment in the past 3 months, and polypharmacy were only significantly associated with the pain component [Table 1].

According to the hierarchical adjusted analysis, general health perception, anxiety, stress, insomnia, and depression were associated with a lower gradient of HRQoL. Regarding role limitation—physical, female gender, lower educational level, and presence of stress were associated with lower odds of a higher HRQoL score, whereas age up to 59 years old was a protective factor. For the physical component, pain was associated with female gender, age up to 59 years old, lower educational level, presence of stress, depression, genitourinary diseases, and polypharmacy, whereas not having seen a doctor in the last 3 months was inversely associated with this component. In addition, regarding physical functioning, female gender, lower educational level, insomnia, and polypharmacy were associated with lower odds of scoring higher on HRQoL, whereas age up to 59 years old was a protective factor for this component [Table 3].

When analyzing the mental components of HRQoL, mental health retained statistical significance for female gender, presence of dyslipidemia, stress, anxiety, depression, and respiratory disease. Variables such as lower educational level, dyslipidemia, stress, and polypharmacy remained associated with social functioning; however, diabetes management was inversely associated with this component. Role limitation—mental was associated with non-white skin color, dyslipidemia, and stress, and diabetes management was inversely associated with this component. For the vitality component, non-white skin color, presence of dyslipidemia, stress, insomnia, depression, and polypharmacy were associated with higher odds of a lower score [Table 4].

Discussion

The present study showed that among the components assessed by the SF-36, mental health had the highest median and limitations due to physical aspects, and was the most affected scale among people with DM. These findings are consistent with a study conducted in Mexico with 392 individuals diagnosed with type 2 diabetes, using the SF-36, which demonstrated that physical factors such as BMI, waist circumference, and higher body fat percentage were negatively associated with HRQoL. Moreover, the study highlighted that physical inactivity further contributed to the deterioration of HRQoL.[15]

In a study conducted in northern India with 215 type-2 diabetics using the SF-36, social functioning was the least affected component,[16] similarly to the finding in the present study. The presence of stress was associated with most of the components analyzed, except for physical functioning. In addition, the presence of dyslipidemia showed a significant association with the mental components of HRQoL.

The results indicate that women with DM had lower HRQoL compared to men concerning the components: Role limitation—physical, pain, physical functioning, and mental health. This corroborates studies conducted in Spain, India, and Vietnam with people with DM using the SF-36 questionnaire.[161718] This finding may be related to the traditional roles played by women throughout their lives and their impact on social pressure, chronic stress, household responsibilities, and care for family members, resulting in lower treatment satisfaction, poorer adaptation to activities of daily living, and higher physical and psychological burden of disease.[19]

Age also had an association with HRQoL, with the age group up to 59 years old being associated with lower HRQoL in the components “pain” and “mental health” in the present study. However, the age group 60 years and older had lower HRQoL regarding physical limitation and physical functioning. These findings suggest that physical pain and mental health problems in adults up to 59 years of age limit their activities, whether daily activities or work tasks, which is mainly related to the high suffering brought by living with DM.[20] Moreover, in older people with DM, these two physical components were the most affected, which is a result similar to that of a cross-sectional study conducted in Spain with 60 diabetic patients, which found that HRQoL in women over 75 years old decreased significantly, especially in the physical aspects.[16] The aging process may compromise the physiological system, exacerbating the effects of chronic disease progression and contributing to the manifestation of physical limitations.[21]

In terms of role limitation—mental and vitality, lower HRQoL was associated with non-white skin color. It is worth noting that although self-reported skin color is a phenotypic characteristic, it is also a determinant of social inequalities and exposure to risk and disease.[22] This finding may be related to unfavorable socioeconomic conditions, which are reflected in the accumulation of unhealthy behaviors and lifestyles, consequently affecting HRQoL in the highlighted components.[23]

In the present study, lower educational level was also associated with lower HRQoL in people with DM, concerning physical functioning, pain, role limitations—physical, and social functioning. This finding is consistent with a cross-sectional study conducted in northern India with 215 people with DM using the SF-36 questionnaire, which found that those with higher education had higher HRQoL scores.[16] This factor may be related to the high demands of self-management of DM, considering that glycemic regulation is intrinsically linked to adherence and lifestyle changes. Thus, individuals with higher levels of education may have a better understanding of the disease and its health effects and seek early strategies for more effective DM management.[24]

Another factor associated with lower HRQoL was stress in most components, except for physical functioning. This finding is supported by a cross-sectional study conducted in Mexico with 101 people with type-2 DM, in which stress was identified as responsible for problems with glycemic control, non-adherence to therapy, and depression.[25] In addition to stress, the presence of anxiety, insomnia, and depression was associated with lower HRQoL in both the physical and mental components in this population. These data are consistent with a cross-sectional study conducted in Iran with 400 diabetic patients using the SF-36 questionnaire, which found that all scales were lower in patients with DM and depression.[26]

In this study, psychological distress was identified as the strongest predictor of lower HRQoL across several components in patients with diabetes. There is consistent evidence of a bidirectional relationship between diabetes and depression, whereby individuals with diabetes are twice as likely to develop depression compared to those without the disease. Moreover, depression increases the risk of developing diabetes and hinders daily self-management, as depressed individuals are less likely to adhere to dietary and weight loss recommendations, may be physically inactive, and thus are at greater risk of obesity. Another relevant factor is the psychological stress associated with diabetes treatment and glycemic control, which can lead to major depressive disorder due to the difficulties of maintaining acceptable daily glucose levels.[27] Supporting the findings of the present study, research conducted at the University of Szeged Clinic in Hungary, with 157 participants using the Health Psychology Research (HPR), Royal Holloway, University of London, Surrey, United Kingdom (ADDQoL-H-19) questionnaire, reported a greater tendency for individuals with diabetes to develop anxiety. Specifically, patients with better quality of life demonstrated lower levels of trait anxiety, whereas those with poorer quality of life showed a higher predisposition to anxiety.[28]

The association between DM and mental disorders such as depression may be explained by biological mechanisms in which chronic psychological stress interacts with insulin signaling dysfunction in the brain, potentially facilitating the development of depression.[29] In addition, the diagnosis of DM may trigger mourning for the loss of health, which may exacerbate depression. In this context, psychological and pharmacological treatment of this disorder can significantly improve the HRQoL of people with DM.[30]

A study conducted in Vietnam with 519 people with DM found that those with complications such as nephropathy and dyslipidemia reported lower HRQoL using the SF-36.[21] This finding is consistent with our study, as the presence of dyslipidemia was associated with all mental health components, and genitourinary and respiratory diseases were associated with mental health and pain components. It is worth noting that the presence of dyslipidemia in this population may lead to the development of kidney damage and, consequently, promote the progression of kidney disease, resulting in irreversible damage to the patient.[31]

The presence of polypharmacy was associated with lower HRQoL in the components “pain,” “physical functioning,” “social functioning,” and “vitality.” This observation may be related to the progression of DM itself and the accumulation of complications that increase the use of medications, as well as the added complexity of managing and adhering to treatment, along with the self-care required by the disease.[17]

A different finding was the higher HRQoL in social functioning and role limitation—mental associated with poor glycemic control. This finding is complex and differs from a survey conducted with 2469 Swedes with type-2 diabetes, which reported an association with lower HRQoL in those with higher levels of glycated hemoglobin. This discrepancy may be related to differences in sample characteristics and the division of glycated hemoglobin into three groups used in the comparative study.[32] In addition, a possible explanation is that social interactions may lead to increased food consumption, which may alter glycemic values. This factor requires further investigation, including the type of drug therapy affecting glycemic control, as a study in northern India found that the use of oral hypoglycemic agents improved quality of life in social functioning and mental limitations.[16]

A cross-sectional study conducted in Ethiopia with 110 people with type-2 DM reported that peripheral neuropathic pain was associated with lower HRQoL, interfering with the performance of daily activities and particularly affecting social functioning.[33] In the present study, not having visited the health unit in the last three months was associated with a higher quality of life in the pain component. This finding may be explained by the fact that diabetic patients may not feel the need to seek health services in the absence of bodily pain.

It is important to highlight the existence of significant gaps in research on HRQoL and DM that guide future investigations. There is a need to integrate clinical factors, such as glycemic control and types of medications used in treatment, with psychosocial aspects, including depression, anxiety, stress, and social determinants of health. In addition, longitudinal assessment of the progression of microvascular and macrovascular complications and their impact on HRQoL is warranted. Furthermore, it is essential to investigate how health education programs and digital interventions can sustain improvements in HRQoL among individuals living with diabetes.

The present study has limitations inherent to a cross-sectional design, and thus, the data shown cannot analyze causality between the variables studied. In addition, some comorbidities were self-reported, although self-reported chronic comorbidities may approximate information obtained through clinical examination. Finally, the exclusion of individuals with cognitive and communication impairments may have led to an underestimation of the data. However, these limitations do not invalidate the study, since it brings robustness via the representativeness of the sample of people with DM registered in the FHS and the novel character of the variables analyzed according to components for the region, although studies on the HRQoL have been growing in Brazil.

Conclusions

Our results indicate that the lower HRQoL in people with DM is strongly associated with female gender, lower educational level, dyslipidemia, stress, anxiety, insomnia and depression, polypharmacy, and the presence of comorbidities (respiratory and genitourinary diseases). These data are important for improving health intervention strategies, as well as for designing public policies that take into account local and regional conditions. It is essential to understand the most affected components, allowing the planning of actions for the promotion and prevention of complications in people with DM receiving primary healthcare.
