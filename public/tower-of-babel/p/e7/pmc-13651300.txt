Prognostic value of sarcopenia markers in geriatric patients presenting to the emergency department: a prospective study
Authors: Ahmet Burak Erdem, Veysi Siber, Serdal Ateş, Ece Zengin, Sena Yılmaz, Rasime Pelin Kavak, Emine Sarcan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEarly risk stratification in geriatric patients presenting to the emergency department remains challenging. Sarcopenia has emerged as a potential marker of reduced physiological reserve and adverse clinical outcomes. This study aimed to evaluate the prognostic value of quadricepsmuscle thickness and the paraspinal muscle index for predicting short-term mortality in geriatric emergency department patients and to examine their association with established frailty scales.MethodsIn this single-center prospective observational study, consecutive patients aged ≥ 65 years presenting to the emergency department were enrolled. Rectus femoris and vastus intermedius muscle thicknesses were measured using bedside ultrasonography. The paraspinal muscle index was calculated from thoracic computed tomography images obtained for clinical indications. Frailty was assessed using the Clinical Frailty Scale (CFS), Elderly Risk Assessment (ERA), and Identification of Seniors at Risk (ISAR). The primary outcome was 30-day mortality. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was performed to evaluate discriminatory performance.ResultsA total of 139 patients were included, of whom 28 (20.1%) died within 30 days. All sarcopenia indices were significantly lower among non-survivors (p < 0.05). In ROC analysis, the paraspinal muscle index demonstrated the highest discriminatory ability for predicting 30-day mortality (AUC 0.689, 95% CI 0.584–0.795), followed by the vastus intermedius (AUC 0.633) and rectus femoris indices (AUC 0.628). In subgroup analysis, quadriceps indices were associated with mortality only in non-inflammatory patients, whereas the paraspinal muscle index remained predictive across both groups. Among frailty tools, only CFS was significantly associated with mortality.ConclusionMuscle-based sarcopenia markers, particularly the paraspinal muscle index, are associated with short-term mortality in geriatric emergency department patients. Integration of muscle measurements with frailty assessment may enhance early risk stratification in emergency care settings.Keywords: Sarcopenia, Emergency department, Geriatric patients, Frailty, Mortality, Ultrasonography

Background

With increasing global life expectancy, the number of geriatric patients presenting to emergency departments (EDs) has risen substantially. This population is characterized by high clinical complexity, multimorbidity, and an increased vulnerability to adverse outcomes. Accordingly, early and accurate risk stratification in geriatric patients presenting to the ED is essential to optimize clinical decision-making, allocate resources efficiently, and improve prognostic assessment. However, traditional clinical evaluation tools may not adequately capture the biological heterogeneity and frailty that define this population [1–3].

Frailty assessment tools, including the Clinical Frailty Scale (CFS), Elderly Risk Assessment (ERA), and Identification of Seniors at Risk (ISAR), have been validated for predicting adverse outcomes such as hospital admission, intensive care unit admission, and short-term mortality in older adults presenting to the ED. These instruments provide a multidimensional assessment of frailty by integrating functional status, comorbidity burden, and prior healthcare utilization. However, an important limitation of frailty scales is their partially subjective nature, as their performance may be influenced by assessor experience, the patient’s acute clinical condition, and the degree of patient cooperation. Consequently, there is increasing interest in identifying more objective and readily obtainable markers that may complement frailty assessment in the emergency setting [4–6].

Sarcopenia is defined as an age-related decline in skeletal muscle mass and function and is strongly associated with frailty, functional deterioration, and mortality in geriatric patients [7]. Previous studies have shown that reduced muscle mass significantly increases the risk of all-cause mortality across various clinical settings [8, 9]. In this context, bedside ultrasonography has gained increasing attention as a rapid, noninvasive, and radiation-free method for assessing muscle mass. The rectus femoris (RF) and vastus intermedius (VM) muscles, due to their superficial anatomical location, high reproducibility, and sensitivity to both acute and chronic muscle loss, are considered ideal targets for ultrasonographic evaluation. These muscles can be assessed rapidly at the bedside without disrupting routine emergency care, making them particularly suitable for use in the ED setting. In addition, the paraspinal muscles, key determinants of postural stability and trunk muscle reserve, have emerged as valuable markers of systemic muscle depletion and overall physiological reserve. Unlike peripheral muscle measurements obtained by ultrasonography, Paraspinal muscles assessment can be performed using routinely acquired thoracic computed tomography (CT) images without requiring additional imaging or radiation exposure. These characteristics enable rapid and objective assessment of muscle reserve in the time-sensitive ED setting [10, 11].

Although substantial evidence supports the prognostic value of both sarcopenia markers and frailty scores, important knowledge gaps remain in geriatric ED populations. In particular, the prognostic significance of the rectus femoris index (RFI), vastus intermedius index (VMI), and paraspinal muscle index (PMI), which represent complementary assessments of peripheral and axial muscle mass, has been investigated in only a limited number of studies, with most data derived from hospitalized or critically ill cohorts. This limits the direct applicability of existing findings to early risk stratification in the ED.

Accordingly, there is a need to investigate rapid and practical methods for muscle assessment and their prognostic significance in geriatric patients presenting to the ED. In this context, the aim of this study was to evaluate the prognostic value of the RFI, VMI, and PMI for predicting 30-day mortality and to examine their associations with established frailty scores, including ERA, ISAR, and CFS.

Methods

Study design and setting

The study was designed as a single-centre, prospective observational cohort study and was planned and reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines [12]. It was conducted in the ED of Ankara Etlik City Hospital (Approval No: AEŞH-BADEK1-2025–383). Patients aged ≥ 65 years who presented to the ED between 9 July 2025 and 11 October 2025 were consecutively enrolled. Eligible patients were evaluated and included in the study. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their legal representatives when applicable. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Participants

Eligible patients were screened consecutively during the study period, and those meeting all inclusion criteria were enrolled.

Inclusion criteria were as follows: age ≥ 65 years, presentation to the ED, provision of written informed consent by the patient or a legal representative, absence of any physical condition precluding ultrasonographic assessment of the rectus femoris and vastus intermedius muscles, and availability of a thoracic CT scan obtained for clinical indications at the time of ED admission. Exclusion criteria included refusal to undergo muscle ultrasonography, receipt of end-of-life palliative care, and clinical conditions at presentation that precluded muscle assessment, such as shock, cardiac arrest, or endotracheal intubation. Patients with a history of lower extremity amputation, severe deformity, or major trauma were also excluded. Additional exclusion criteria comprised known or newly diagnosed neuromuscular disorders (e.g., motor neuron disease, myopathy, or muscular dystrophy), advanced chronic neurological diseases (e.g., advanced Parkinson’s disease or severe stroke with significant sequelae), major surgery within the previous three months, and prolonged immobilization. Furthermore, patients receiving long-term systemic corticosteroid therapy or other immunosuppressive treatments that could significantly affect muscle mass were excluded. Patients with active malignancy-associated cachexia, a history of unintentional weight loss exceeding 10% within the previous six months, severe spinal deformities, or conditions rendering paraspinal muscle measurements technically unreliable were also excluded (Fig. 1).

Data sources and measurements

Sociodemographic characteristics, comorbidities, serum lactate, albumin, and C-reactive protein (CRP) levels at ED presentation were recorded for all participants. Disposition from the ED [discharge, ward admission, or intensive care unit (ICU) admission] was documented. Frailty assessment was performed at the time of ED presentation using three validated instruments: the ERA, ISAR, and CFS. RF and VM muscle thickness measurements were obtained using bedside ultrasonography without delaying routine clinical evaluation or treatment. The RFI, VMI, and PMI were subsequently calculated by dividing the respective muscle measurements by height squared (m2) and were recorded for analysis. 30-day mortality status was determined using data obtained from the National Death Registry.

Frailty Assessment: Frailty assessments (CFS, ERA, and ISAR) were performed by emergency physicians who had received training in the application of these instruments. The training included both theoretical instruction and practical application using standardized clinical scenarios to ensure consistency and reliability.

The ERA index is a weighted risk scoring system based on age, total duration of hospitalization within the previous two years, marital status, and the presence of selected comorbid conditions, including congestive heart failure, myocardial infarction, coronary artery disease, diabetes mellitus, cerebrovascular disease/stroke, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, malignancy, and dementia. Each variable is assigned a predefined score, and the total score reflects the individual’s overall clinical risk profile. The ERA score ranges from −1 to 34, with higher scores associated with an increased likelihood of hospitalization and adverse clinical outcomes [13].

The ISAR is a six-item screening tool developed to identify geriatric patients at increased risk of adverse health outcomes following ED presentation. The instrument evaluates prior hospitalization, visual impairment, memory problems, dependence in activities of daily living, polypharmacy (≥3 medications), and recent deterioration in general health status. Responses to each item are summed to obtain a total score ranging from 0 to 6. Higher ISAR scores are associated with an increased risk of ED revisits, functional decline, and mortality [14].

The CFS is a clinical judgment-based frailty instrument developed to assess overall health status and functional capacity in geriatric patients. The scale grades individuals from 1 (very fit) to 9 (terminally ill) based on their level of independence in activities of daily living, mobility status, comorbidity burden, and cognitive function. Assessment is based on the patient’s baseline functional status prior to ED presentation. Higher CFS scores are associated with increased mortality, hospital admission, and adverse clinical outcomes [15].

Ultrasonographic Muscle Measurements: Muscle thickness measurements were performed in accordance with a standardized protocol to minimize measurement bias. All measurements were performed independently by two radiologists with at least three years of experience in musculoskeletal imaging. Both evaluators were blinded to clinical outcomes and mortality data. To ensure consistency, all measurements were obtained using a standardized technique, and predefined anatomical landmarks were used for muscle thickness assessment. Ultrasonographic examinations were performed using a Butterfly iQ device (Butterfly Network, Inc., Guilford, CT, USA). Measurements were obtained from the non-dominant lower extremity with patients in the supine position. A high-frequency linear transducer was used, and device settings were standardized to musculoskeletal mode with a depth range of 4–6 cm. The lower extremity examined was positioned in slight abduction with the toes pointing upward. Muscle thickness measurements were obtained at the midpoint between the anterior superior iliac spine and the superior border of the patella. The transducer was placed perpendicular to the longitudinal axis of the thigh. RF and VM muscle thicknesses were measured as the maximal distance between the inner fascial borders and recorded in millimeters (mm). All measurements were performed in accordance with current muscle ultrasonography guidelines [16]. For each muscle, three consecutive measurements were obtained, and the mean value was used for subsequent analyses (Fig. 2).

Measurement of the Paraspinal Muscle Index: PMI assessment was performed using thoracic CT scans obtained for clinical indications at the time of ED presentation. No additional imaging was performed for study purposes, and only existing clinical CT images were analyzed.

Thoracic CT scans were acquired using multi-detector CT scanner systems (GE Revolution EVO, GE Medical Systems, Milwaukee, WI, USA), and images were retrieved from the institutional Picture Archiving and Communication System. Paraspinal muscle area was measured at the level of the 12th thoracic vertebra (T12), including the bilateral erector spinae muscle groups (iliocostalis, longissimus, and spinalis) located posterior to the spinous process. A single axial CT slice at the T12 level was used for analysis. During segmentation, attenuation values between −29 and +150 Hounsfield units (HU) were considered representative of skeletal muscle tissue [17]. The cross-sectional muscle areas of the right and left paraspinal muscles were calculated using dedicated analysis software, and the mean of the bilateral measurements was used to calculate the PMI for statistical analysis (Fig. 3).

Ultrasonographic measurements were assessed by one radiologist, whereas CT-based measurements were assessed by a second radiologist. A third radiologist reviewed all measurements to ensure accuracy and consistency. All assessments were non-invasive, and no additional radiation exposure or clinical intervention beyond routine care was required for study purposes.

The RFI, VMI, and PMI were calculated by dividing the respective muscle measurements by height squared (m2), as follows:

RFI = RF muscle thickness (mm)/height (m)2

RFI = RF muscle thickness (mm)/height (m)2

VMI = VM muscle thickness (mm)/height (m)2

VMI = VM muscle thickness (mm)/height (m)2

PMI = Paraspinal muscle area (mm2)/height (m)2

PMI = Paraspinal muscle area (mm2)/height (m)2

Patient grouping

The study population consisted of geriatric patients presenting to the ED for all causes. As a result, the diagnoses assigned to patients exhibited substantial heterogeneity, which prevented a balanced distribution across diagnostic groups. This situation limited the ability to perform reliable statistical analyses based on individual disease categories. To address this limitation, disease diagnoses were classified into two main groups according to their association with inflammatory processes: inflammatory and non-inflammatory conditions. Patients with a defined focus of infection, supported by clinical, laboratory, and imaging findings, and demonstrating evidence of a systemic inflammatory response were classified as having inflammatory conditions. In contrast, patients without evidence of systemic inflammation were categorized as the non-inflammatory group.

Outcomes

The primary outcome of this study was to determine the prognostic value of the RFI, VMI, and PMI in predicting 30-day mortality among geriatric patients presenting to the ED. The secondary outcome was to evaluate the association between muscle indices and validated frailty scales. Additionally, the relationships of muscle indices and frailty scores with length of hospital stay, ICU admission, and revisit rates were examined.

Sample size

As this was a prospective observational cohort study conducted over a predefined recruitment period, no formal a priori sample size calculation was performed prior to patient enrollment. All consecutive eligible geriatric patients presenting to the ED during the study period were included. Given that the primary objective was to evaluate the discriminatory performance of muscle indices (RFI, VMI, and PMI) for predicting 30-day mortality, sample size considerations were based on the requirements of receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis rather than conventional group-comparison approaches. Following data collection, the adequacy of the sample size was assessed using established ROC-based methodologies as described by Hanley and McNeil [18]. Based on a two-sided α level of 0.05, 80% statistical power, a null hypothesis area under the curve (AUC) of 0.50, and an expected AUC of approximately 0.68 for the primary marker, alongside an observed 30-day mortality rate of 20.1%, the minimum required sample size was estimated to be approximately 114 patients, including at least 23 events. The final study cohort comprised 139 patients, with 28 deaths within 30 days, indicating that the sample size was sufficient for the primary analysis.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using Jamovi version 2.7.12. Categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages. The distribution of continuous variables was assessed using histograms and the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test, and non-normally distributed variables were reported as median and interquartile range (IQR, 25th–75th percentile).

Comparisons of categorical variables were conducted using the chi-square test. For continuous variables with normal distribution, the Student’s t-test was used, and homogeneity of variances was evaluated with Levene’s test. The Mann–Whitney U test was applied for comparisons of non-normally distributed continuous variables.

For the prediction of 30-day mortality, ROC curve analysis was performed to determine optimal cut-off values and to calculate sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, negative predictive value, and positive and negative likelihood ratios. Optimal thresholds were identified using the Youden index. A two-sided p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

During the study period, 614 patients were assessed for eligibility. Of these, 475 were excluded based on the predefined criteria, leaving 139 patients for the final analysis (Fig. 1). The study cohort comprised non-inflammatory (n = 55) and inflammatory (n = 84) patients. There were no significant differences between groups in age or sex distribution. Among laboratory parameters, a trend toward higher CRP levels was observed in the inflammatory group, approaching statistical significance (p = 0.059), whereas lactate and albumin levels were comparable. Clinical scoring systems revealed a significantly higher CFS in the inflammatory group (median 7 vs 6, p = 0.002), while ERA and ISAR scores did not differ significantly. No significant differences were observed in hospital length of stay or ED disposition. Although 24-hour and 30-day mortality rates were higher in the non-inflammatory group, these differences did not reach statistical significance (Table 1). In the inflammatory group, pneumonia (60.7%) and urinary tract infection (26.2%) were the most common diagnoses, followed by intra-abdominal infection (9.5%), cellulitis (2.4%), and central nervous system infection (1.2%). In the non-inflammatory group, the most frequent diagnoses were acute kidney injury (29.2%), acute heart failure (18.2%), acute arterial occlusion (14.5%), pulmonary embolism (12.7%), and electrolyte imbalance (9.1%). Less frequent diagnoses included gastrointestinal bleeding and acute stroke.

Discussion

In this study, both quadriceps muscle measurements and the PMI were significantly associated with short-term mortality in geriatric patients presenting to the ED. These findings suggest that sarcopenia markers may provide complementary prognostic information alongside traditional demographic and clinical parameters and may support early risk stratification in the ED setting.

Sarcopenia is increasingly recognized as a major determinant of adverse clinical outcomes in geriatric patients, including functional decline, prolonged hospitalization, and mortality. Previous studies conducted predominantly in hospitalized and critically ill populations have demonstrated a strong association between CT-based muscle measurements and both short- and long-term mortality [9, 19, 20]. However, data focusing on unselected geriatric patients presenting to the ED remain limited. By demonstrating that both peripheral (RF and VM) and axial (paraspinal) muscle measurements obtained early during ED evaluation are associated with 30-day mortality, our study adds important evidence to the existing literature in this specific clinical setting.

Ultrasonographic assessment of quadriceps muscle thickness has emerged as a practical and noninvasive marker of sarcopenia, particularly in acute care settings. Previous studies have reported that RF thickness correlates with total body muscle mass and can predict adverse clinical outcomes in hospitalized older adults and critically ill patients [10, 21, 22]. In a study of critically ill patients, Tillquist et al. reported that rectus femoris thickness was independently associated with hospital mortality, with each 1-mm increase in muscle thickness corresponding to a 12% increase in the odds of survival [23]. Similarly, Parry et al. found that reduced quadriceps muscle thickness was associated with prolonged intensive care unit stay and greater functional decline in critically ill patients [24]. Our findings are consistent with these data, demonstrating that RFI and VMI were significantly lower among non-survivors, particularly within the non-inflammatory subgroup. This observation suggests that, in the absence of systemic inflammation, muscle depletion may more accurately reflect baseline physiological reserve rather than acute catabolic processes.

The PMI demonstrated the highest discriminatory performance for predicting 30-day mortality in the overall cohort and remained significantly associated with mortality in both inflammatory and non-inflammatory subgroups. Paraspinal muscles play a critical role in posture, balance, and trunk stability, and are widely regarded as robust indicators of overall muscle reserve. Previous studies have shown that CT-based paraspinal muscle measurements are associated with mortality and frailty in trauma, oncologic, and critically ill populations [25–27]. In a retrospective study of ICU patients, Moisey et al. reported that paraspinal muscle area was independently associated with 60-day mortality and remained significant after adjustment for age and APACHE II score [28]. Similarly, Puthucheary et al. demonstrated that paraspinal muscle wasting was associated with functional decline and prolonged ICU stay in critically ill patients [29]. These findings are consistent with our results and further support the prognostic relevance of paraspinal muscle assessment in acute care settings. Our findings support the potential prognostic value of the PMI in the ED setting and suggest that it may provide more consistent prognostic information than peripheral muscle measurements, particularly in the presence of systemic inflammation. Nevertheless, the observed AUC values indicate only moderate discriminatory performance. Therefore, PMI should not be interpreted as a stand-alone prognostic tool but rather as an adjunctive marker that may complement existing clinical and frailty-based risk assessment strategies.

Among the frailty instruments evaluated, the CFS was significantly associated with short-term mortality, consistent with prior studies conducted in the ED setting [4, 5, 30]. In contrast, no significant differences were observed in ERA and ISAR scores between survivors and non-survivors. This discrepancy may be related to the partially subjective nature of frailty scales and their reliance on patient- or proxy-reported information, which may be less reliable under acute ED conditions. In this context, quantitative muscle measurements may provide complementary and more standardized prognostic information alongside traditional frailty assessments. The integration of muscle-based sarcopenia markers with established frailty scales may provide a more comprehensive approach to risk stratification in geriatric ED patients. Such a combined strategy could facilitate the early identification of high-risk individuals, support more informed decisions regarding monitoring and hospital admission, and contribute to more efficient resource allocation in the ED setting.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, its single-center design may limit the generalizability of the findings. Second, paraspinal muscle measurements were available only for patients who underwent thoracic CT for clinical indications, which may have introduced selection bias. However, only clinically acquired imaging was used, and no additional scans were performed for research purposes, thereby reflecting real-world ED practice. Third, although measurements were performed by two independent radiologists, interobserver agreement was not formally reported, which may limit the assessment of measurement reproducibility. Finally, functional outcomes and quality-of-life measures beyond mortality were not assessed.

Conclusion

In this study, quadriceps muscle measurements and the PMI were associated with short-term mortality in geriatric patients presenting to the ED. Among these, the PMI demonstrated more consistent performance across different clinical subgroups. These findings suggest that quantitative muscle measurements may provide complementary prognostic information alongside established frailty assessments. However, given the moderate predictive performance observed, these measurements should be considered supportive tools for risk stratification rather than independent predictors of clinical outcomes.
