Inpatient Utilization of Therapeutic Plasma Exchange in the United States: 2016-2021
Authors: Jianling Tao, Sijie Zheng, Lusilda Agolli, Luis Alvarez, Patrick Ahearn, David H Rehkopf, Glenn M Chertow, Neil S Kamdar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractRationale & ObjectiveTherapeutic plasma exchange (TPE) is used in a variety of autoimmune, inflammatory, hematological, and metabolic disorders. We described the contemporary scope of inpatient utilization of TPE in the United States.Study DesignA cross-sectional study.Setting & ParticipantsThe national inpatient sample dataset from year of 2016 to 2021.Exposure(s)Inpatient utilization of TPE.Outcome(s)We described demographics, associated comorbid conditions, hospital characteristics, and hospital costs by comparing patients treated with those not treated by TPE. We reported the top 25 clinical conditions treated by TPE. We described the top 10 diseases or conditions for the first discharge diagnosis as acute kidney injury (AKI) (International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision code: N17.9) treated by TPE.Analytical ApproachAll descriptive and multivariable analyses considered the complex survey design for every calendar year sampling of national inpatient sample; the results were weighted to reflect national estimates.ResultsDuring 2016–2021, a total of 106,035 hospitalizations (∼0.051% of the total hospitalizations) were treated by TPE. The 5 most common conditions treated by TPE were myasthenia gravis with (acute) exacerbation (9.53%), Guillain-Barre syndrome (6.19%), thrombotic microangiopathy (6.18%), kidney transplant rejection (5.42%), and sepsis, unspecified organism (3.62%). AKI was the 13th most common condition, accounting for 1.82% of all cases treated by TPE, while the most common underlying condition for AKI was multiple myeloma (without achieving remission). The temporal trend of indications used by TPE in the US practice from 2016 to 2021 aligns well with the updated guideline by the American Society of Apheresis. The TPE utilization was associated with higher inpatient costs after accounting for length of stay; odds ratio ranges: 3.47 (2.99-4.03)—11.07 (10.19-12.03).LimitationsLack of laboratory data.ConclusionsThe TPE continues to be used to treat a variety of serious autoimmune and inflammatory diseases, in accordance with published clinical practice guidelines.Index Words: Therapeutic plasma exchange, national inpatient sample, epidemiology, cost, United States

Therapeutic plasma exchange (TPE), also known as plasmapheresis, is the process of separating plasma from the cellular components of blood and then returning the cellular portion back to the patient. The TPE can be performed using either centrifuge technology or filtration through semipermeable membranes. The term plasmapheresis was first used by John Abel in 1914 when he reported large amounts of plasma could be collected from an animal and return the cellular portion back.1 Schwab et al2 reported treatment of Waldenstrom macroglobulinemia by plasmapheresis in 1960; since then more than 80 indications for TPE have been described.3 Most are renal, hematological, rheumatological, neurological, or transplantation-related.

The utilization of TPE is to remove plasma, which contains large molecules, autoantibodies, cytokines, albumin-bound toxins, or cholesterol, and then replace these components with albumin or fresh frozen plasma. Although TPE involves removing offending agents in the immediate term, longer-term production of that agent needs to be stopped or reduced by either immunosuppressants, intravenous immunoglobin or chemotherapy. Therefore, TPE is often used concurrently with systemic pharmacological therapies. The TPE may increase risks of infection, electrolytes disturbances, arrythmia, and bleeding.3 Performing TPE is costly. Thus, utilization of plasmapheresis has to be carried out by experienced staff, carefully weighing associated risks against potential benefits.4,5

The American Society of Apheresis has consistently updated evidence levels regarding utilization of TPE in practice; the vast majority of indications are not strongly evidence supported.6 This reflects the nature of individualized utilization of TPE in clinical practice and explains why large data-derived analysis or evidence is lacking.

Because of the requirement of training for physicians and nurses and uncommon usage, many hospitals do not have dedicated resources for TPE. Thus, many patients either need to be transferred to a larger academic center or forgo TPE. Nephrology, transfusion medicine, and hematology are disciplines that manage TPE depending on the indications. The paucity of studies that elucidate national trends in utilization of TPE and clinical presentation at the time of TPE underscore the importance of this study.

The primary aim of this study was to analyze NIS data regarding temporal trends of TPE utilization, lengths of stay and costs as utilization metrics, and patient and hospital characteristics associated with the utilization of TPE in contemporary US practice. We also described the hospitalized acute kidney injury (AKI) population who used TPE to provide an overview particularly for nephrologists.

Methods

Data Source

We used the national inpatient sample (NIS), which was developed for the Healthcare Cost and Utilization Project sponsored by the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality.7 The NIS is the largest all-payer inpatient database in the United States and approximates a 20-percent stratified sample of all discharges from US hospitals, excluding long-term acute care and rehabilitation hospitals. The NIS Core file contains commonly used data elements, including age, discharge status, and total charges. The NIS Hospital file contains information regarding hospital characteristics. Cost was defined as the actual expenses incurred in the production of hospital services, such as wages, supplies, and utilities, derived from hospital-specific cost-to-charge ratios. Outcomes, including in-hospital mortality and discharge status, can be calculated by using the included sampling weights. We selected 2016 as the first cross-section for NIS such that we could maintain consistency of the diagnosis code set based on the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD-10-CM), which was adopted since 2016. Calendar year 2021 was the latest version of the dataset available to the public at the time we conducted this analysis.

Study Population

We included all inpatient hospitalizations from 2016–2021 in NIS. Using specific ICD-10-CM procedure codes for TPE (6A550Z3: plasma pheresis single, 6A551Z3: plasma pheresis multiple), we categorized the cohort based on utilization of TPE during the hospitalization. All ICD-10-CM diagnosis and procedure codes used for this study about disease diagnoses and conditions are listed in Table S1. Ethical approval and consent were not required because this study was based on publicly available data.

Covariates

We obtained demographic variables, such as age, sex, designated race/ethnicity including White, Black, Hispanic, and others, and payer including Medicare/Medicaid, private insurance, self-pay, no charge, or others, through the NIS Core file.

From the NIS Hospital file, we obtained hospital characteristics, including US census regions of Northeast, Midwest, South, and West; teaching status as rural, urban nonteaching, and urban teaching, and bed size categorized as small, medium, and large. In NIS, designated race and ethnicity have been combined to reflect a single designated race/ethnicity combination; if a specific ethnicity was documented, then ethnicity took precedence over race.8

To account for comorbid condition burden at the time of hospitalization, we used the Elixhauser comorbidity index and its component comorbid conditions of relevance including history of arrhythmia, smoking, abnormal lipid panel, left ventricular hypertrophy, coronary artery disease, AIDS, alcohol abuse, deficiency anemias, rheumatoid arthritis/collagen vascular diseases, blood loss anemia, congestive heart failure, chronic pulmonary disease, coagulation disorders, depression, diabetes, drug abuse, hypertension, hypothyroidism, liver disease, lymphoma, fluid and electrolyte disorders, cancer, other neurological disorders, obesity, paralysis, peripheral vascular disease, psychoses, pulmonary circulation disorders, chronic peptic ulcer disease, valvular disease, and weight loss.9 Chronic kidney disease (CKD) and CKD stages were extracted directly by ICD-10 diagnosis codes. We included severity of illness and the risk for mortality with the base all patient refined diagnosis-related groups from the NIS severity file. We identified the codes for the top 25 diseases or conditions that were most frequently treated by TPE. For a subgroup of hospitalizations with the first discharge diagnosis of AKI (ICD 10 Code: N17.9) treated by TPE, we further explored the second discharge diagnosis (what will most likely be the underlying conditions requiring TPE treatment) and provided the top 10 kidney diseases or conditions in which TPE was employed.

Dependent Variables

We studied length of stay (in terms of hospital patient days), costs, discharge disposition (i.e., discharge to home, transfer to rehabilitative facility, left against medical advice), and in-hospital death among patients who received and did not receive TPE.

Statistical Analysis

All descriptive and multivariable analyses considered the complex survey design for every calendar year sampling of NIS; the results were weighted to reflect national estimates. We categorized continuous variables of age, cost, and length of stay into quartiles to enable ease of interpretation. We reported counts and proportions for categorical variables. Categorical analysis of counts and proportions among hospitalized patients who did and did not receive TPE were examined using c2 tests. We observed missing data in fewer than 5% of hospitalizations. In view of the modest degree of missing data, we performed a complete case analysis without imputation. For cost models, we examined the quantile distribution and assigned cost for a hospitalization to be within the top fourth quartile. We fitted multivariable weighted logistic regression models to determine whether there was an association between TPE utilization and costs. Length of stay was dichotomized based on the median of the distribution for multivariable analysis. Because we had assumed that hospitalizations with TPE would obligate significantly longer lengths of stay, we considered the interaction between TPE utilization and length of stay in our analysis of costs. All models were adjusted for age, sex, designated race/ethnicity, region, hospital teaching status, hospital bed size, severity of illness, and all comorbid conditions with appropriate weighting.

We used 2-tailed P-values < 0.05 to be statistically significant. We conducted the analysis using SAS Version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc) and all models were fit using PROC SURVEYLOGISTIC incorporating the sampling weights.

Sepsis with multiorgan failure and hypertriglyceridemic pancreatitis are considered category III indications for TPE, meaning the optimal role of TPE in these conditions has not been established. In ICD-10 coding, there are no specific codes for sepsis with multiorgan failure or for pancreatitis due to hypertriglyceridemia—only general codes for sepsis and pancreatitis exist. Based on clinical expertise and context, we interpret cases coded as sepsis here to represent sepsis with multiorgan failure, and cases coded as pancreatitis and treated with TPE to likely reflect pancreatitis caused by hypertriglyceridemia.

Among 12 indications with temporal changes: utilization of TPE for sepsis (sepsis with multiorgan failure) or pancreatitis (pancreatitis due to hypertriglyceridemia) increased, respectively from 0.15% to 1.64% (P < 0.001) and from 0.15% to 1.15% (P < 0.001) over the 6-year observation period; utilization of TPE for thrombotic microangiopathy or kidney transplant rejection decreased over the 6-year observation period. Utilization of TPE for Wegener’s granulomatosis with polyangiitis decreased since 2020. U071: Acute respiratory distress syndrome due to the coronavirus disease 2019 virus was first observed in 2020. There were 1,050 such cases in 2020 and 2021 treated by TPE.

Discussion

This descriptive epidemiological study reported the contemporary utilization of TPE and the temporal trend in the top 25 indications treated by TPE from 2016 to 2021 in the United States.

Delivering TPE often involves multidisciplinary management given the complexity and severity of the underlying diseases or conditions. Data from prospective randomized trials to support broad utilization of TPE are typically lacking. Published recommendations for TPE from the American Society for Apheresis were based largely on the opinions of experts from hematology, transfusion medicine, nephrology, and pathology rather than from randomized, controlled clinical trials.3

Kim et al10 described the changes about the category and grade recommendations for TPE from the fifth edition guideline made in 2010 to the ninth edition made in 2023. Persistently, most indications for TPE lie in category III, which is where the optimum role of apheresis therapy is not established/decision-making should be individualized, and Grade 2C: weak recommendation, low-quality, or very low-quality evidence.

After we explored the NIS dataset and described a cohort with antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies-associated vasculitis and active kidney involvement treated by TPE from 2016 to 2020,11 we identified a gap in understanding the contemporary practice in utilization of TPE locally and internationally. Current literature exploring NIS to study TPE usually focused on TPE-related complications or outcomes in one disease or condition. For example, research questions were focused on outcomes of TPE in severe autoimmune hemolytic anemia hospitalizations,12 or inpatient mortality, thrombotic events, major bleeding, length of stay, or costs.13 For instance, Soares Ferreira Júnior et al14 explored NIS 2016-2018 and studied bleeding complications by using propensity score analysis in a cohort comprising cases with the most common 10 causes treated by TPE. However, the authors referred an abstract in 2018 (https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/jca.21628) titled as “Nationwide practices in therapeutic plasma exchange in hospitalized patients: 2010-2014,” where they15 explored NIS 2010-2014 and listed the top 10 common conditions treated by TPE. Clearly, such information requires updating to reflect the current landscape on clinical practice incorporating TPE treatment.

There were 2 aims in this study. In addition to describing the demographics and comorbid conditions of patients treated and not treated with TPE, we focused further on examining the temporal trend of the top 25 diseases or conditions treated by TPE in the United States from 2016 to 2021. This application of real-world data elucidates the who, where, and for what in the contemporary era. Among the 12 indications with trend changes, those changes (increase or decrease) are well aligned with the trend changes in recommendations from the American Society for Apheresis10: weakened recommendations for thrombotic microangiopathy, antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies-associated vasculitis, and anti-GBM disease, and strengthened recommendations for sepsis and pancreatitis associated with hypertriglyceridemia. Notably, the down trend of utilization of TPE in granulomatosis with polyangiitis and active kidney involvement (ICD10 code: M3131) reflected evidence derived from the PEXIVAS study, which was published in 2020, and showed no significant benefit associated with the use of TPE in antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies-associated vasculitis.16 Meanwhile, in 2020 and 2021, there were cases of COVID-19 infection (ICD10 code: U071) treated by TPE.17

Korkmaz et al18 collected data from 28 apheresis units throughout Turkey, and found a total of 24,912 TPE procedures were performed for 3,203 patients from the period of 2007-2017. The majority of TPE were still used for neurological and hematological disorders, which is consistent with our study finding.

There are several indications for TPE use before and after kidney transplant, most notably treatment of antibody-mediated rejection, which is the fourth most common indication for plasma exchange in the US. Okwuonu et al19 recently published a single center retrospective analysis of a total of 131 kidney transplant recipients who received in total 860 TPE from 2017 to 2022. They found that the most common indications for TPE related to kidney transplant was antibody-mediated rejection (69%), followed by recurrent focal segmental glomerulosclerosis (15%), thrombotic microangiopathy (5%), desensitization for A, B, O blood group system incompatibility (5%) and for human leukocyte antigen incompatibility (5%), and recurrent IgA nephropathy (1%).

Another aim was to understand which diseases or conditions in the setting of AKI were treated by TPE. AKI only accounted for about 1.8% in all TPE-treated hospitalizations, and the most common condition was multiple myeloma (without achieving complete remission). Dhakal et al20 had studied NIS 1993 to 2015, and found a significant increase in the use of TPE in multiple myeloma and an almost 5-fold increase in the total hospital charges when TPE was employed.

We approached the cost analysis based on the overall utilization of TPE instead of focusing on a particular disease or condition. There was an interaction between utilization of TPE and length of hospital stay.

It is interesting to note that TPE was more commonly delivered in the South region of the United States. Geographic differences in patient populations, disease patterns, disease severity, response to treatment, and practice preferences may contribute to this variation.

The NIS is an ideal dataset for studying this question because TPE is a procedure usually occurring in the hospital, and NIS dataset captures data on 1 in 5 hospitalizations and is representative of most hospitalizations in the United States. With its robust sampling design, NIS enables generalizable point estimates of proportions, rates, and costs across payers. We included all admissions in NIS, captured all TPE, and elaborated the overview of cohort treated by TPE focusing on patient demographics, comorbid conditions, hospital-associated characteristics, and discharge level outcomes.

Although this study allows for broad estimates, there are limitations. NIS lacks laboratory data or follow-up data on longer-term mortality or re-hospitalization, which could help us to understand longer-term effects of TPE in specific disease states. We are unable to evaluate more granular aspects of the TPE procedure (e.g., type of replacement fluid frequency or complications of TPE such as hypocalcemia, hypotension, or urticaria caused by blood product infusion, or the long-term outcomes).21 This analysis is inherently a cross-sectional study, and as such, we cannot definitively determine whether coagulopathy and electrolyte disorders were true preexisting comorbid conditions. Our decision to report these as comorbid conditions aligns with common practice in studies using NIS data, as demonstrated in prior publications. Additionally, based on clinical experience, discharge diagnoses are often more reflective of preexisting conditions rather than complications that arose during hospitalization. The NIS only captures inpatient TPE data; therefore, the descriptive findings presented here do not reflect the outpatient TPE landscape.

In summary, we provided the real-world data about the utilization of TPE in the current practice in the United States. Despite incomplete understanding of its risks and benefits, TPE continues to be used to treat a variety of serious autoimmune and inflammatory diseases, increasingly in accordance with published clinical practice guidelines.
