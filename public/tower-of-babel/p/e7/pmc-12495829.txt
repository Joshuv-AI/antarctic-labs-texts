GLUT and FAPα as molecular markers for interstitial lung disease in systemic sclerosis
Authors: Bo Broens, Conny J van der Laken, Teodora Radonic, Esther J Nossent, Xue D Manz, Douwe J Mulder, Yehya Al-Adwi, C Tji Gan, Wim Timens, Alexandre E Voskuyl, Jan Willem Duitman
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe clinical management of systemic sclerosis-associated interstitial lung disease (SSc-ILD) is challenging due to its heterogeneous progression. While recent studies have shown that pulmonary uptake of 18F-FDG and 68Ga-FAPI on positron emission tomography (PET) may indicate ILD activity, the spatial distribution of their molecular targets—GLUT1, GLUT3, and FAPα—in SSc-ILD lung tissue is largely unexplored. Here, we investigate the expression patterns of these markers across varying fibrosis stages in lung tissue of SSc-ILD patients and controls to improve PET interpretation.MethodsImmunohistochemistry for GLUT1, GLUT3, and FAPα was performed on lung tissue from SSc-ILD patients (n = 9) and controls (n = 8). Fibrosis stage and cell-type-specific expression of these markers were analyzed by 2 experienced ILD pathologists, and quantitative staining was assessed using QuPath software. Marker expression was analyzed in and compared between areas with varying fibrosis stages.ResultsGLUT1 positive cells were primarily erythrocytes, GLUT3 positive cells were mainly myeloid cells and FAPα positive cells were predominantly fibroblasts, endothelial cells, epithelial cells, and macrophages. All cells positive for these markers were more present in SSc-ILD compared to controls, with GLUT1 and FAPα positive cells showing highest prevalence in areas with early signs of fibrosis.ConclusionsThe prevalence of cells expressing GLUT1 and FAPα is increased in areas with early signs of fibrosis and the expression of GLUT1, GLUT3 and FAPα is observed in different cell types suggesting that 18F-FDG and 68Ga-FAPI PET each may be useful to visualize different aspects of ILD activity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12931-025-03363-x.Keywords: Systemic sclerosis, Interstitial lung disease, Immunohistochemistry, Glucose transporters, Fibroblast activation protein alpha

Background

Systemic sclerosis (SSc) is a rare and chronic auto-immune disorder that affects the skin, musculoskeletal system and internal organs [1]. Unlike other auto-immune disorders, more than half of SSc patients die as a direct result of disease pathology, with interstitial lung disease (ILD) being the leading cause of disease specific mortality [1]. Clinically significant ILD develops in 30–40% of the patients, often requiring anti-inflammatory and/or anti-fibrotic therapy. The 10-year mortality rate for those with ILD progression is estimated at 40% [2]. The heterogeneous disease course of SSc-ILD, along with variable responses to anti-inflammatory and anti-fibrotic treatments, complicates disease management in daily clinical practice [3–5].

In clinical practice, pulmonary function tests and high-resolution computed tomography (HRCT) of the lung are used to diagnose and monitor patients with SSc-ILD [6]. On HRCT, the majority of patients with SSc-ILD exhibit a pattern of non-specific interstitial pneumonia (NSIP) [2, 6]. On tissue level, fibrotic non-specific interstitial pneumonia is more common than cellular non-specific interstitial pneumonia, in which inflammation predominates [2, 7, 8]. Less common patterns include usual interstitial pneumonia (UIP), organizing pneumonia, diffuse alveolar damage, and lymphocytic interstitial pneumonia, though combinations of these patterns may occur as well [9, 10]. However, HRCT findings and clinical characteristics alone are insufficient for personalized treatment planning, as they do not predict ILD progression or response to therapy [11, 12].

Early identification of SSc patients at risk for ILD progression is crucial. Most current treatments stabilize or slow lung function decline for SSc patients with progressive fibrotic ILD, while few patients experience significant clinical improvement [5]. Surgical lung biopsies are not routinely performed in clinical care, because it is not required for SSc-ILD diagnosis. For research purposes, surgical lung biopsies are not taken because of the risk of bleeding complications, infections and ILD exacerbation, which limits stratification or investigation at tissue level [13].

Positron emission tomography (PET) is an emerging non-invasive imaging technique currently being explored for its role in managing patients with SSc-ILD [14]. Unlike HRCT, PET imaging can visualize molecular processes in the lungs, supporting possibilities for personalized treatment and follow-up strategies [15, 16]. The most frequently used PET tracer is 18F-Fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG), which reflects glucose metabolism by detecting uptake mediated by glucose transporters (GLUT) on various cell types [17, 18]. Previous studies have demonstrated that increased metabolic activity in the lungs of patients with (SSc-)ILD was associated with pulmonary function decline [19–23]. Another promising tracer is 68Ga-FAPI, which targets fibroblast activation protein alpha (FAPα), a marker expressed on (activated) fibroblasts [24]. In a recent study, increased uptake of 68Ga-FAPI in the lungs of SSc-ILD patients was associated with ILD progression, and changes in uptake reflected response to treatment with nintedanib (i.e. decrease in tracer uptake in case of clinical response) [24].

Therefore, both 18F-FDG and 68Ga-FAPI PET-CT hold potential to improve the clinical management of patients with SSc-ILD. However, due to the limited availability of lung tissue in these patients, data about the expression of GLUT1 and 3 (main targets of 18F-FDG) and FAPα (main target of 68Ga-FAPI) in SSc-ILD lung tissue is lacking. Thus, it is unknown where and when these markers are expressed in the course of the disease. Information about the expression and localization of these markers is of interest for interpreting 18F-FDG and 68Ga-FAPI PET-CT as this may provide insight into underlying pathophysiological mechanisms of disease at patient level. As such, we aim to investigate the protein expression of GLUT1, GLUT3 and FAPα, and their association with other markers for fibrosis, by immunohistochemical stainings in lung tissue of SSc-ILD patients with variable stages of lung fibrosis and controls available after lung transplantation, autopsy or resection.

Methods

Patient selection and data collection

Lung tissue sections were selected based on availability from the “further use” biobank at the Amsterdam UMC, Amsterdam, the Netherlands and the UMC Groningen, Groningen, the Netherlands. SSc-ILD lung tissue was available post-lung transplantation (n = 6), after autopsy (n = 2) and resection because of suspected malignancy (n = 1). One or two lung tissue sections were available from each patient. Control lung tissue was available from non-affected lung of patients after resection for atypical carcinoid (n = 2), adenocarcinoma (n = 5) and sarcoma (n = 1) (Table 1). Patient data were retrospectively retrieved from electronic medical records. All patients had clinical, physiological, and radiographic findings consistent with their diagnosis. All SSc patients met the ACR-EULAR classification criteria or had a confirmed diagnosis post-autopsy [25]. This study was approved by the medical ethics committees and the scientific committees of the pathology biobank of the Amsterdam UMC (2021.0436) and UMCG Groningen (2019.00015). All patients, or their legal representatives in autopsy cases, provided both oral and written consent for the use of their data and lung tissue.

(Immuno)histochemistry

Lung sections (3–4 μm thick) were deparaffinized and rehydrated. Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked with 3% H2O2 in phosphate-buffered saline for 20 min. Antigen retrieval was performed using 10mM sodium citrate buffer (pH 6.0) or Tris-EDTA buffer (pH 9.0) for 20 min at 95 °C. Sections were then blocked with 5% normal goat serum or 2% bovine serum albumin for 30 min and incubated overnight with the following primary antibodies: anti GLUT1 (1:5000; abcam - ab15309), anti GLUT3 (1:10.000; proteintech − 20403-1-AP) anti FAPα (1:250; abcam - ab207178) and anti αSMA (1:800; santa cruz - sc-32251); in their respective blocking solutions. Blocking solutions without primary antibody served as negative controls. The following day, Brightvision (Immunologic) or Envision (DAKO) secondary antibody kits were applied for 30 min. Stainings were visualized using 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB). Slides were then counterstained with Mayer’s Hematoxylin. Sections were then dehydrated and mounted with a coverslip. Detailed information about the immunohistochemistry procedures per antibody can be found in Supplementary Data S1.

For picrosirius red (PSR) staining, deparaffinized 3–4 μm thick lung sections were placed in 0.2% PSR solution (pH 2.0) for 60 min and differentiated in 0.01N hydrochloric acid for 2 min. Sections were then dehydrated and mounted with a coverslip.

Microscopy and evaluation

Histological and immunohistochemical stainings were captured using the IntelliSite Ultra Fast Scanner (Philips) at 40x magnification. Two experienced lung pathologists (TR and WT), blinded to patient data, evaluated SSc-ILD lung tissue areas for fibrosis stage resulting in the following score: normal [0], early [1], advanced [2], and confluent [3] histological fibrotic abnormalities based on hematoxylin and eosin staining. Lung tissue was scored as normal [0] in case of normal lung parenchyma, early [1] if initial signs of non-collagenous alveolar wall thickening were observed, advanced [2] if there was a clear increase alveolar wall thickening with architectural distortion of the lung tissue and confluent [3] if there was confluent fibrosis in line with our previous study [26]. For each SSc-ILD case an area of every fibrosis score was examined if possible, but for some cases normal [0] and early [1] histological fibrotic abnormalities were difficult to find. In lung tissue sections of 9 patients with SSc-ILD, a total of 20 areas were identified and graded for fibrosis stage by an 2 expert ILD pathologists (TR and WT): 2 areas were marked as normal [= score 0], 7 areas were marked as early [score 1], 4 areas were marked as advanced [score 2] and 7 areas were marked as confluent [score 3]. Additional information about the lung tissue sections (including from which lobe the tissue was taken) and scored areas can be found in Supplementary Data S2.

Increasing fibrosis stage was confirmed by αSMA and PSR staining in the same area using adjacent sections, which are markers for myofibroblasts or smooth muscle cells and collagen, respectively. Although most of the analyzed tissue was available from patients with end-stage disease, previous research has demonstrated that different fibrosis stages can still be recognized and reflect early and late-stage disease as evident from differential gene expression patterns allowing to study fibrosis progression patterns in end-stage disease tissue [26]. Quantitative analysis of each staining was performed using QuPath software, selecting five representative regions of interest per fibrosis stage area in each tissue section. Due to image processing limitations it was not possible to analyze the entire area at once, and the mean value of these five representative regions of interest was calculated and used for further analysis. A qualitative analysis was also conducted, describing the marker expression in different cell types and its relation to fibrosis stage.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS Statistics Version 28.0 and GraphPad Prism Version 10.0. Normality was assessed with the Kolmogorov-Smirnov and the Shapiro-Wilk tests. Parametric data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, and non-parametric data were expressed as median ± interquartile range. Group comparisons were made using unpaired two-tailed t-tests, Mann-Whitney U tests, or Kruskal-Wallis tests, with appropriate corrections for multiple comparisons (Dunns test). Correlations were investigated using Pearson (for normally distributed data) or Spearman’s rank correlation (for non-normally distributed data). P-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient characteristics

In total, lung tissue sections from nine patients with SSc-ILD were included (tissue from 1 to 2 different locations in the lung were available for each patient). Most patients had diffuse cutaneous SSc (55.6%), with a mean disease duration of 4.1 years (Table 1). Two patients were former smokers, while the other seven patients had never smoked. The majority of patients were on immunosuppressive medication (66.7%; Glucocorticoids, Mycophenolate Mofetil, Azathioprine, or combinations), and none had received anti-fibrotic therapy before inclusion. A (predominant) histological NSIP pattern was observed in most patients (88.9%), with a (predominant) UIP pattern present only in one patient. Of the patients showing a predominant histological NSIP pattern, two showed a cellular NSIP pattern, five showed a mixed NSIP pattern and one showed a fibrotic NSIP pattern. Controls (n = 8), were age matched to the SSc-ILD group.

GLUT1, GLUT3 and FAPα are expressed in different cell types

In patients with SSc-ILD, expression of GLUT1 was observed in erythrocytes, but not in immune cells (Fig. 1). In contrast, GLUT3 was predominantly observed in myeloid cells, such as neutrophils, and in endothelial cells (Fig. 2). Furthermore, expression of FAPα was observed in different cell types, including fibroblasts, endothelial cells, epithelial cells and macrophages (Fig. 3). Because literature describes that FAPα expression is limited to fibroblasts in idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) [27, 28], we performed immuno-fluorescent in situ hybridization (iFISH) of FAPα mRNA to confirm specificity of the antibody. iFISH confirmed the presence of FAPα mRNA in the different cell types that are positive for FAPα in immunohistochemistry, implying specific staining to be present in more cell types than fibroblasts only (Supplementary Data S3 and Supplementary Figure S1).

Expression of GLUT1 and FAPα is altered in different stages of fibrosis

The expression of αSMA and collagen in the different fibrosis stages was quantified using immunohistochemistry or PSR staining, respectively. A gradual increase in αSMA expression was observed until fibrosis score 2 (advanced), while collagen expression was mainly more in areas with confluent fibrosis (Fig. 4A and B). In addition, GLUT1, GLUT3 and FAPα expression was quantified in the same regions. Quantitative analysis of GLUT1 showed a trend of increased expression in areas with early signs of fibrosis [score 1] as compared to areas with normal lung parenchyma. Interestingly, GLUT1 expressing cells were less prevalent in areas with advanced and confluent fibrosis [score 2 to 3] as compared to areas with early signs of fibrosis. A similar pattern of more expression in areas with signs of early fibrosis [score 1] was observed for FAPα. This pattern was less apparent for GLUT3, which showed less differences in expression level between areas with different fibrosis scores (Fig. 4B). Interestingly, expression of all markers was low in areas with confluent fibrosis. Finally, no clear differences in staining intensity between areas were observed.

Correlation of markers in SSc-ILD

Correlation plots showed a trend for positive correlation between the expression of FAPα and αSMA (R = 0.38; p = 0.10), while no correlation was found between αSMA and GLUT1 or GLUT3 (R=−0.11, p = 0.64 and R = 0.17, p = 0.49 respectively) (see Fig. 5A). As shown in Fig. 5B, FAPα correlated negatively with PSR staining (R=−0.52; p = 0.02) and GLUT1 showed a trend of negative correlation with PSR staining (R=−0.36, p = 0.11), which indicates that cells with expression of GLUT1 and FAPα were less present in areas with confluent fibrosis.

Discussion

In this study we investigated the spatial expression of GLUT1, GLUT3 and FAPα in lung tissue from SSc-ILD patients and controls in areas with different stages of fibrosis to gain insight into what processes are visualized in vivo using 18F-FDG and 68Ga-FAPI PET-CT imaging. Our key findings confirmed that cells expressing all three markers—GLUT1, GLUT3, and FAPα— showed higher prevalence in SSc-ILD compared to controls, with particularly pronounced expression of cells positive for GLUT1 and FAPα in areas with early signs of pulmonary fibrotic histological changes. Together with the expression of these markers on different cell types, these findings suggest that 18F-FDG and 68Ga-FAPI PET-CT each may reflect distinct aspects of (early) ILD activity. To our knowledge this is the first study that explores the expression of these markers in SSc-ILD lung tissue, providing insight into the clinical interpretation of 18F-FDG and 68Ga-FAPI PET-CT.

Compared to current literature, it is evident that the involvement of GLUT1 and GLUT3 in lung fibrosis has been relatively understudied, particularly in the context of SSc-ILD. A prior study in IPF has reported elevated GLUT1 expression on erythrocytes and a very limited number of immune cells in fibrotic lung tissue [18]. A relevant study using the murine pre-clinical bleomycin model – a commonly used animal model to mimic pulmonary fibrosis – supports our findings in SSc-ILD demonstrating a trend of increased GLUT1 expression in areas with early signs of fibrosis [29]. GLUT3, while well-documented to be expressed by myeloid cells [30, 31], was not studied before in fibrotic lung disease. It is also worth noting that we found a trend with less expression of GLUT1 in areas with more severe fibrosis. The fact that advanced fibrotic regions have reduced vascularization [32–34] may partly explain this effect as GLUT1 expression was mainly observed in erythrocytes and therefore potentially reflects vascularization. On the contrary, GLUT3 expression was not limited to blood vessels, but mainly observed on immune cells residing in the tissue. In line, its expression was not altered in different areas of fibrosis severity. Furthermore, for overall interpretation, it is important to note that 18F-FDG uptake is not exclusively mediated by GLUT1 and 3; other glucose transporters may also contribute to uptake. However, GLUT1 and GLUT3 have the highest expression in lung tissue of patients with different forms of ILD (see Supplementary Figure S2), making them the most likely candidates for facilitating 18F-FDG uptake in SSc-ILD [35–37] although we cannot exclude 18F-FDG uptake may also be mediated by other glucose transporters.

In contrast to GLUT1 and GLUT3, FAPα expression was found in fibroblasts, endothelial cells, epithelial cells and macrophages. A single study reported FAPα expression in the lungs of patients with IPF using immunohistochemistry. In this study expression of FAPα was reported to be specific for fibroblasts in fibroblast foci of IPF lungs, while no expression was observed in healthy controls [27], as expected because of very low presence of fibroblasts in control lung. FAPα is often described as a fibroblast-specific marker in IPF, but our study, in line with research in malignancies and inflammatory diseases, shows that FAPα expression is not limited to fibroblasts but also extends to macrophages and endothelial cells [38]. Our findings are supported by iFISH analysis (Supplementary Figure S1) and single-cell RNA sequencing data that was publicly available in the IPF cell atlas (http://www.ipfcellatlas.com). This publicly available data demonstrates that FAPα mRNA expression is not limited to fibroblasts, but is also observed in other cell types, such as endothelial cells, especially in SSc-ILD patients (Supplementary Figure S3) [39]. Overall, FAPα may play an important role in SSc-ILD pathogenesis, and further research is needed to elucidate its contribution to fibrotic disease mechanisms [38, 40–42].

18F-FDG PET-CT and 68Ga-FAPI PET-CT have not yet been compared in patients with SSc-ILD in a clinical setting. Studies in the bleomycin mouse model suggest that 18F-FDG PET-CT may capture earlier inflammatory phases of the disease, whereas 68Ga-FAPI PET-CT appears more sensitive to changes in the cellular fibrotic phase during disease progression [29, 43]. However, we found a higher prevalence of cells expressing GLUT1 and FAPα in areas with early signs of fibrosis. Since the investigated markers are expressed on different cell types involved in SSc-ILD, the connected imaging modalities may reflect distinct aspects of (early) ILD activity. While speculative, these may include processes such as inflammation and vascularization for 18F-FDG PET-CT, and fibrogenesis for 68Ga-FAPI PET-CT, given the known roles of the identified cell types expressing the targeted markers. This is supported by a clinical study in IgG4-related disease, where patients could be stratified in three subtypes: inflammatory, fibrotic and mixed disease based on 18F-FDG PET-CT and 68Ga-FAPI PET-CT [43, 44].

This study has some limitations. First, the sample size was relatively small, as lung material is not widely available due to the associated risk of taking surgical lung biopsies. Consequently, most tissue sections were obtained post-lung transplantation, primarily from patients with end-stage disease. Although we have addressed this issue by analyzing marker expression in areas with assessment of different stages of fibrosis within the excised tissues, which correlates well with the different disease stages in SSc-ILD [26], this may limit the generalizability of our results to patients with early-stage SSc-ILD. A further limitation of our statistical analysis is the use of unpaired tests for the quantitative analysis of markers in areas with different fibrosis scores, while some analyzed areas were available from the same patients and tissue sections and could be considered as paired data. However, because of the limited number of paired samples in our dataset paired analysis was not possible (see also Supplementary Data S2). Although our study provides valuable insights into marker expression of GLUT1, GLUT3 and FAPα, larger cohorts are needed to confirm these findings. Ideally, if possible, a direct comparison between 18F-FDG and 68Ga-FAPI PET-CT and marker expression at the tissue level might have confirmed target specificity. Nevertheless and importantly, earlier pre-clinical and clinical studies found proper correlations between 18F-FDG and 68Ga-FAPI uptake, and GLUT1, GLUT3 and FAPα at tissue level, which underline the relevance of studying these specific markers to enhance the clinical interpretation of these PET tracers, even without comparing them directly to clinical PET-CT scans [17, 29, 45].

Conclusions

Based on increased prevalence of cells expressing GLUT1 and FAPα in areas with early signs of fibrosis and the expression of GLUT1, GLUT3 and FAPα on different cell types, we think that 18F-FDG and 68Ga-FAPI PET-CT each may be useful to visualize different aspects of (early) ILD activity. While speculative, these may include processes such as inflammation and vascularization for 18F-FDG PET-CT, and fibrogenesis for 68Ga-FAPI PET-CT, given the known roles of the identified cell types expressing the targeted markers. Future studies should aim to evaluate both imaging modalities side to side in a clinical setting.
