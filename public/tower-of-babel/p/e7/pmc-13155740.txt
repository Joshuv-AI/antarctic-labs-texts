Accuracy of Intraocular Lens Power Calculation Formulas in Cataract Eyes after Vitrectomy with Silicone Oil Tamponade: A Network Meta-Analysis
Authors: Peng Zhao, Meng Li, Zhao-Xing Guo, Zhi-Yong Tian, Jing-Shang Zhang, Ying-Yan Mao, Zheng Xin, Zhong-Yan Li, Xiu-Hua Wan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe aim of the study was to systematically compare the accuracy of artificial intelligence and traditional intraocular lens (IOL) calculation formulas in cataract eyes after vitrectomy with silicone oil tamponade.MethodsPubMed, Cochrane, Embase, and Web of Science databases were searched to select relevant studies published up to October 29, 2025. A total of 12 formulas (SRK/T, Hoffer Q, Hoffer QST, Haigis, Barrett Universal II, Holladay I, Holladay II, RBF, LSF, EVO, Kane, and Pearl-DGS) were included for final comparison. The primary outcomes were the percentage of eyes with prediction errors (PE) within ± 0.50 D and ± 1.00 D.ResultsThis meta-analysis included 7 studies comprising 1,060 eyes. Overall, compared to traditional formulas, newer formulas demonstrated relatively higher accuracy. The results indicated that among all the formulas being compared, the optimal formula was Kane. For the percentage of eyes with PE within ± 0.50 D, Kane had the highest precision. For the IOL power percentage within ± 1.00 D, EVO performed the best, with Kane also ranking highly. Furthermore, RBF also performed very well.ConclusionKane and EVO appear to be better choices for silicone oil-filled eyes after vitrectomy.

Introduction

Vitrectomy was first developed and utilized by Machemer in 1971 as a microsurgical technique that involves entering the vitreous cavity using specialized instruments and techniques [1]. Over the past 40 years, with advancements in surgical technology and instruments, vitrectomy has become a common procedure for treating posterior segment diseases [2]. However, with the widespread use of vitrectomy, its side effects have gradually emerged, notably the accelerated development of cataracts, which may be related to various risk factors and pathophysiological mechanisms. It is estimated that up to 52% of patients will undergo cataract surgery within 1 year after vitrectomy, and 80% of patients will develop significant cataracts affecting vision within 2 years [3, 4].

A significant challenge in cataract surgery following vitrectomy is the high variability of postoperative refractive outcomes [5]. Predicting the refractive outcome of the eye after vitrectomy is difficult due to various factors. The most important factor is the absence of the posterior vitreous body, which can lead to an abnormal and fluctuating anterior chamber depth (ACD), increasing the movement of the posterior capsule and intraocular lens (IOL) after surgery [6]. In addition, patients who have undergone vitrectomy face multiple challenges, including the fragility of the zonular fibers, stability issues with the IOL, and inaccuracies in measuring axial length. These complex factors collectively increase the difficulty of predicting the effective lens position and refractive outcome for these patients [7–9]. Beyond these factors, the nature of the postoperative intraocular tamponade agent plays a significant role in refractive prediction. Among these agents, silicone oil, due to its unique physical properties – a speed of sound far lower than that of normal intraocular fluid and a refractive index higher than that of the vitreous – emerges as a key factor affecting the accuracy of refractive calculations. This material discrepancy interferes with conventional biometry: in ultrasound biometry, silicone oil leads to an overestimation of axial length, whereas in optical biometry, it results in an underestimation. This bidirectional interference complicates the calculation of IOL power considerably, significantly increasing the risk of postoperative refractive errors [10, 11]. With the advent of optical coherence tomography biometry, the precision of these measurements has significantly improved, surpassing traditional ultrasound biometry [12]. Therefore, choosing the appropriate IOL power calculation formula has become a critical factor affecting the accuracy of IOL power prediction [13].

Over the past 40 years, IOL power calculation formulas have evolved from the third generation to artificial intelligence-based formulas. Although early studies suggested that third-generation formulas like SRK/T might be more accurate in IOL power calculation, subsequent research has not confirmed this difference [14]. Fourth-generation formulas such as Haigis have improved accuracy by incorporating additional parameters like ACD, lens thickness (LT), and white-to-white distance. Fifth-generation formulas, also known as next-generation formulas, like Barrett Universal II (BUII) and Olsen, based on ray tracing methods, have shown high consistency and widespread popularity in IOL power calculation. Additionally, new formulas based on artificial intelligence, such as Kane, RBF, LSF, and Pearl-DGS, have been introduced to enhance calculation accuracy [15]. Meanwhile, an increasing number of new formulas are being proposed, such as the recently introduced LISA-PPV designed for patients after vitrectomy in a recent article [16]. Although the new generation of calculation formulas has significantly improved the prediction accuracy of refractive errors, the optimal formula for predicting refractive outcomes in silicone oil-filled eyes remains unclear. Given the unique physicochemical properties of silicone oil, this meta-analysis aimed to systematically compare the predictive accuracy of new-generation formulas versus traditional formulas in silicone oil-filled eyes after vitrectomy, and provide more clinically valuable guidance for IOL power calculation in silicone oil-filled eyes after vitrectomy.

Materials and Methods

The research process of this meta-analysis adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Given that this study falls within the category of secondary literature meta-analysis, involving neither primary data collection nor patient intervention, it did not require approval from a research ethics committee. The article structure references the PRISMA checklist, details of which can be found in the PRISMA checklist in the supplementary material (for all online suppl. material, see https://doi.org/10.1159/000551331).

Literature Search

A comprehensive literature search was conducted by two independent investigators (Peng Zhao and Meng Li) in the PubMed, Cochrane, Embase, and Web of Science, with the search deadline set to October 2025. The keywords used for the search included (Vitrectomy OR silicone oil) AND ([Cataract or Cataracts or Lens Opacities or Lens Opacity or Opacities, Lens or Opacity, Lens or Cataract] OR [Lenses, Intraocular or Intraocular Lenses or Intraocular Lens or Implantable Contact Lens or Contact Lens, Implantable or Lens, Implantable Contact] OR [phacoemulsification OR phacoemulsification]) AND (calculate OR formula). To ensure no relevant studies were missed, the author also checked the reference lists of related studies.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

The inclusion criteria for the study were (1) randomized controlled trials, cross-sectional studies, retrospective or prospective observational studies; (2) eyes that have undergone vitrectomy and retained silicone oil; (3) eyes that have received cataract surgery and implantation of an IOL; (4) comparison of at least two available IOL power calculation formulas; (5) collection of preoperative and postoperative data with an interval of no less than 1 month, providing at least one outcome variable (i.e., mean absolute error (MAE) or the percentage of predicted refractive errors within a certain range) or one that can be calculated.

The exclusion criteria for the study were (1) eyes with a history of other intraocular surgeries besides vitrectomy; (2) eyes that have undergone vitrectomy and have removed silicone oil or did not use silicone oil to tamponade; (3) eyes that did not meet the inclusion criteria due to other conditions (such as severe pterygium or significant corneal opacification); (4) preoperative ocular biometry conducted using non-IOLMaster instruments; (5) studies that did not provide target outcome data or could not be calculated.

Data Extraction and Quality Assessment

The author independently extracted the following data from each study: first author, publication year and journal, the country where the study was conducted, study period, study design, original inclusion and exclusion criteria, the total number of eyes included, patient demographics, and biometric data, type of IOL used for each patient, patients’ previous surgical history, formulas evaluated, time intervals between preoperative and postoperative biometric assessments, and outcome measurements and values.

Outcome Measurement

The percentage of eyes with prediction errors (PE) within ± 0.50 D and ± 1.00 D were used as the primary outcome indicators for this review. If no outcome values were reported, we calculated them from the raw data provided by the study. Moreover, mean error (ME) and MAE were also analyzed as secondary outcome measures.

Statistical Analysis

When comparing the percentage of eyes with PE within ± 0.50 D and ± 1.00 D, we used a random-effects model to estimate the pooled odds ratio (OR). The results were displayed using a forest plot, where lines represent the estimates and their confidence intervals from different studies, and boxes graphically illustrate the weights assigned to each study when calculating the combined estimate for a given result. Additionally, funnel plots were used to detect publication bias and small-study effects. Statistical significance was set at a p value of less than 0.05. Data analysis was performed using Stata v. 14.0 and R v. 4.4.1.

Literature Search

Figure 1 illustrates the process of literature search. A total of 758 articles were retrieved. The search results were as follows: PubMed (n = 237), Embase (n = 142), Web of Science (n = 304), and Cochrane (n = 75); among them, 245 documents were deleted due to repetition, and after removing duplicates, there were 513 articles remaining. Among the 61 articles identified, 40 were excluded due to inconsistency with our study criteria. Ten were excluded because the surgical methods did not align with the target procedure. Of the remaining 11 articles, the majority were excluded primarily for not reporting outcomes related to IOL calculation formulas [17–23].

Overall, 1,060 eyes that underwent vitrectomy with silicone oil tamponade were analyzed. In these included studies, the age of the patients was above 18. Six studies were conducted in China, and one was conducted in Germany (detailed information is shown in Table 1). The formulas included were SRK/T, Hoffer Q, Hoffer QST, Haigis, BUII, Holladay1, Holladay2, RBF, RBF, LSF, EVO, Kane, and Pearl-DGS. ME and MAE

In the comparative analysis of ME and MAE using SRK/T as the reference standard, the results are presented in online supplementary Fig. 1 (for ME) and online supplementary Figure 2 (for MAE). Hoffer QST demonstrated the best overall performance, ranking first in ME and second in MAE, reflecting high predictive accuracy with minimal systematic bias. The Kane also showed consistently robust results, placing within the top three for both metrics. While the EVO achieved the best performance in MAE, its ME was slightly less favorable. In contrast, although Pearl-DGS yielded acceptable MAE results, it exhibited a notable tendency for overestimation in ME, indicating the presence of systematic bias. Meanwhile, both the Hoffer Q and Holliday 1 performed poorly across the two metrics. In summary, based on the analysis of ME and MAE, it is recommended to prioritize the Hoffer QST, Kane, or EVO in clinical practice to achieve more accurate and reliable IOL power prediction outcomes.

Discussion

According to our literature research, there is no systematic evaluation or meta-analysis on the accuracy of IOL calculation formulas for silicone oil-filled eyes. Therefore, this meta-analysis aimed to fill this academic void by synthesizing existing research data to provide clinicians with more precise references for IOL power calculation. Our analysis indicates that newer IOL calculation formulas generally exhibit superior performance compared to traditional formulas. Specifically, the Kane and EVO stand out among the new formulas, demonstrating exceptional predictive accuracy and reliability. Furthermore, the BUII not only performs outstanding but also impresses with its stability, making it a leading formula within its category. In summary, while newly developed IOL calculation formulas offer higher accuracy in most cases, the BUII, with its outstanding performance and stability in traditional algorithms, continues to serve as an important reference standard with undeniable clinical value in specific scenarios.

In this Meta-analysis, Kane performed the best. In this analysis, Kane performed the best in the analysis of the percentage of eyes with predicted error (PE) within ± 0.50 D, achieving an SUCRA value of 90.9%, ranking first and leading by a significant margin. In the analysis of the percentage of eyes with PE within ± 1.00 D, the Kane’s SUCRA value was 82.5%, placing it second behind EVO. The results from the forest plot analysis were consistent with those of the SUCRA, indicating its outstanding performance, which may be attributed to its design. The Kane combines regression analysis and artificial intelligence technology to optimize its predictive accuracy. It is based on theoretical optics and utilizes a database of postoperative refractive outcomes for approximately 30,000 cataract patients. This formula employs efficient cloud algorithms and includes multiple variables as key parameters, such as axial length (AL), corneal curvature (K), ACD, and patient gender. Optional variables like LT and central corneal thickness (CCT) further enhance its predictive capability [15, 24]. By considering multiple ocular biometric parameters (such as AL and corneal curvature), it can more accurately predict individual refractive states. The formula emphasizes the importance of personalized treatment, adjusting the prediction model according to specific patient conditions to better meet individual needs. This personalized approach can improve treatment outcomes and patient satisfaction. The Kane is easy to understand and apply, suitable for various ophthalmic clinics and hospitals. Its calculation method is simple and intuitive, allowing doctors to quickly master and use it. Based on extensive clinical data and scientific research, it has high reliability and validity, providing doctors with a scientific tool to assess patients’ refractive states. Compared to traditional and new IOL calculation formulas, the Kane stands out particularly in reducing PE. This advantage is even more pronounced when dealing with different axial lengths. A large-scale study in 2020 involving 10,930 cases showed that the Kane achieved the highest predictive accuracy across all axial length intervals, especially for long axial lengths exceeding 26.0 millimeters, where its minimal absolute error further solidified its leading position in the field of IOL calculation [25].

In this analysis, EVO ranked second overall, just behind the Kane. In the analysis of the percentage of eyes with PE within ± 0.50 D, the SUCRA value for EVO was 70.9%, ranking third. However, in the analysis of the percentage of eyes with PE within ± 1.00 D, the SUCRA value for EVO was 82.8%, ranking first and demonstrating stability. This is related to its design principles. The EVO was developed by Tun Kuan Yeo from Singapore in 2019. The EVO is designed based on the principle of ray tracing, which calculates the IOL power by simulating the path of light propagation through the human eye. This method considers the optical characteristics of the eyeball and individual differences, thereby improving the accuracy of IOL power calculation. It uses AL, K, and ACD as predictive factors (with LT and CCT being optional). The accuracy of the EVO has been confirmed by multiple studies. For example, one study compared the accuracy of 12 different IOL power formulas, including EVO, and found that the EVO exhibited higher accuracy in both overall eyes and eyes with short axial lengths [26].

In addition to the aforementioned two formulas, RBF exhibits remarkable performance. In the analysis of the percentage of eyes with PE within ± 0.50 D, the SUCRA value for RBF was 65.6%, ranking fourth. Meanwhile, in the analysis of the percentage of eyes with PE within ± 1.00 D, the SUCRA value for RBF was 80.4%, ranking third, which was also quite prominent. The RBF, introduced in 2016 by Dr. Warren E. Hill as the first purely AI-driven method for calculating IOL power, is fundamentally based on radial basis function technology. This innovative approach relies entirely on data-driven processes, incorporating advanced pattern recognition techniques and sophisticated data interpolation algorithms developed in MATLAB. Since its launch, the RBF Formula has undergone multiple iterative upgrades, with its dataset continuously expanding. Version 2.0 incorporated data from over 12,000 eyes, while Version 3.0, officially released in September 2020, includes data from more than 30,000 eyes. In earlier versions, if there was uncertainty in the predicted refractive outcome, the RBF Formula would refrain from providing a refractive prediction and was limited to cases with plano cycloplegic refraction. By the time of RBF 3.0, the formula’s input parameters encompass key biometric data such as AL, K, ACD, LT, WTW, CCT, and patient gender, which collectively form the foundation for its precise predictions [15, 27].

This meta-analysis has several limitations. First, some of the included studies are small-sample retrospective studies, which may lead to selection bias in terms of patient characteristics, various IOL types, and variability in single-center studies. However, we believe that comparing the accuracy of IOL refractive power calculation formulas is acceptable. Second, the studies included in this analysis contain fewer formulas in the literature, which may affect the results. Third, due to the limited availability of relevant research, our study does not include newer IOL formulations such as Olsen and Panacea.

Overall, this meta-analysis compared the accuracy of 12 formulas from seven studies in calculating IOL power for silicone oil-filled eyes. The results showed that the Kane performed the best, followed by EVO, BUII, and RBF, which also demonstrated excellent performance. Furthermore, the newer generation formulas generally outperformed the traditional formulas. However, it is important to acknowledge that current clinical studies are limited. Therefore, additional follow-up studies with sufficient randomized controlled trial data are necessary to confirm the accuracy of these formulas.
