Change in Effectiveness and Safety Parameters After Initiation of SGLT2 Inhibitors for Post-Transplant Diabetes Mellitus: A Real-World Observational Study
Authors: Maria Cristina Crepaldi, Monica Vedovato, Mario Luca Morieri, Giovanni Basso, Lucia Federica Stefanelli, Marianna Alessi, Paola Fioretto, Gian Paolo Fadini
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPost-transplant diabetes mellitus (PTDM) is frequent and associated with increased cardiovascular and renal risk. Seventy patients who received treatment for ≥ 6 months were included in efficacy analyses. The primary endpoint was change in HbA1c over time. Introduction

Post-transplant diabetes mellitus (PTDM) is one of the most frequent metabolic complications following solid organ transplantation (SOT), affecting up to one-third of recipients depending on organ type and immunosuppressive regimen [1–3]. PTDM has major clinical implications, as it is independently associated with increased risk of cardiovascular events, graft dysfunction, infection-related morbidity, and reduced overall survival [3–6]. In kidney transplant recipients, PTDM significantly worsens long-term kidney function, while in liver and heart transplantation it contributes to accelerated cardiovascular disease and chronic kidney injury [4–6].

The pathophysiology of PTDM is complex and differs from that of classical type 2 diabetes (T2D). In addition to pre-existing metabolic risk factors, transplant recipients are exposed to diabetogenic immunosuppressive agents (especially calcineurin inhibitors, mTOR inhibitors, and glucocorticoids) which impair β-cell function and exacerbate insulin resistance [3, 7]. Moreover, chronic inflammation, fluctuating graft function, and the metabolic consequences of end-stage organ disease further contribute to the pathophysiology of this condition. Despite some distinctive features, current therapeutic recommendations largely extrapolate evidence from T2D guidelines, including eventual sex/gender differences, due to the scarcity of transplant-specific data [2].

In the past decade, sodium–glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors have reshaped the management of T2D. Large randomized controlled trials have consistently demonstrated not only effective glycemic control but also robust cardiovascular and renal protection, including reductions in major adverse cardiovascular events, hospitalization for heart failure, and progression of chronic kidney disease [8–10]. These pleiotropic effects make SGLT2 inhibitors particularly attractive for transplant recipients, in whom cardiovascular and renal complications represent leading causes of morbidity and mortality.

However, transplant recipients have been systematically excluded from large cardiovascular and renal outcome trials. Concerns remain regarding potential adverse effects in this population, including volume depletion, acute drops in estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) in individuals exposed to nephrotoxic immunosuppressants, and increased susceptibility to infections in an immunocompromised setting. Current evidence is limited to small observational studies predominantly in kidney transplant recipients, with short follow-up durations, typically not exceeding 24 months [11–13]. Long-term data are virtually absent, and the trajectory of renal function beyond the early hemodynamic eGFR “dip” remains insufficiently characterized in PTDM.

Given the unique metabolic, immunological, and renal vulnerability of transplant recipients, it cannot be assumed that the benefits observed in T2D directly translate to PTDM. Real-world evidence with extended follow-up is urgently needed to clarify the long-term balance between efficacy and safety in this high-risk population.

We aimed to describe the change in effectiveness parameters and to collect safety events occurring after initiation of SGLT2 inhibitors in a real-world cohort of solid organ transplant recipients with diabetes (PTDM). We specifically focused on glycemic response over time, weight trajectory, renal function evolution, and treatment tolerability.

Methods

Study Design and Setting

We conducted a single-center retrospective observational study at the Diabetes Outpatient Clinic of the University Hospital of Padua, Italy. The study included solid organ transplant (SOT) recipients treated with an SGLT2 inhibitor between January 1, 2017 and July 31, 2025.

Compliance with Ethical Standards

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the University Hospital of Padova (CET ACEV, prot 422n/AO/2) and conducted in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration of 1964, and its later amendments. All participants signed written informed consent. Patients signed written informed consent for their data to be used in the protocol referred to in the text, which covers the analysis presented in the manuscript.

Study Population

Patients were eligible if they had a history of solid organ transplantation (kidney, liver, or heart), a diagnosis of diabetes mellitus according to American Diabetes Association criteria [14], and exposure to an SGLT2 inhibitor (empagliflozin, dapagliflozin, canagliflozin, or ertugliflozin) during the study period. The current definition of PTDM includes not only patients who developed diabetes after transplantation but also patients with pre-existing diabetes who underwent solid organ transplantation [15]. Patients without a history of transplantation or without confirmed diabetes were excluded. For efficacy analyses, only individuals who had received SGLT2 inhibitor therapy for at least 6 months were included to ensure adequate treatment exposure.

Data Collection

Clinical and laboratory data were extracted from electronic medical records. The index date (baseline) was defined as the time of SGLT2 inhibitor initiation. Collected data included demographic characteristics (age and self-reported biological sex), anthropometric parameters (body weight and body mass index), cardiovascular risk factors (smoking status, hypertension, dyslipidemia), transplant-related variables (type of transplanted organ, time since transplantation, immunosuppressive regimen), and diabetes-related characteristics (duration of diabetes and concomitant glucose-lowering therapies). Laboratory parameters included HbA1c, lipid profile, urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio (uACR), and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), calculated using the CKD-EPI equation [16]. Follow-up values were collected during routine outpatient visits according to clinical practice.

Study Outcomes

The primary endpoint was the longitudinal change in HbA1c following initiation of SGLT2 inhibitor therapy. Secondary endpoints included changes in body weight, systolic blood pressure (SBP), eGFR, and uACR over time, as well as modifications in concomitant glucose-lowering therapies. Safety outcomes comprised the incidence of genitourinary infections and the rate and causes of treatment discontinuation. Genitourinary infections were categorized as urinary tract infections, including cases requiring hospitalization (e.g., urosepsis), and genital infections managed in outpatient settings.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables are reported as mean ± standard deviation, and categorical variables as number and percentage. Between-group comparisons were performed using Student’s t test or Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables, as appropriate, and chi-square test for categorical variables.

Longitudinal analyses were conducted using mixed-effects models for repeated measures, with time included as a fixed effect and baseline value of the dependent variable included as a covariate. An autoregressive first-order covariance structure was applied. A two-sided p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS ver. 23 or later.

Literature Search

A structured search was run in PubMed in November 2025 to identify clinical studies published between 2015 and 2025 evaluating the efficacy and safety of SGLT2 inhibitors in solid organ transplant recipients with diabetes. The following search string was used: (“SGLT2” OR “SGLT-2” OR “sodium-glucose cotransporter 2”) AND (“post-transplant diabetes” OR “PTDM” OR “new-onset diabetes after transplantation” OR “NODAT”) AND (“solid organ transplantation” OR “transplant” OR “kidney transplant” OR “liver transplant” OR “heart transplant”). Original clinical studies reporting metabolic, renal, or safety outcomes were considered eligible, while reviews, case reports, and studies without extractable outcome data were excluded. Titles and abstracts were screened, and full texts were assessed for inclusion. The review was descriptive and intended to provide contextual comparison with the present study.

Results

Study Population

Eighty-six solid organ transplant recipients with diabetes (time since first transplant 11.4 years) were treated with an SGLT2 inhibitor during the study period (Table 1). Of these, 70 patients received treatment for at least 6 months and were included in efficacy analyses. Sixteen patients were excluded from longitudinal evaluation because the treatment duration was < 6 months, due to recent initiation (n = 8) or early discontinuation (n = 8).

Among the 86 patients (72 men and 14 women), 52 (60.5%) developed diabetes after transplantation (PTDM), whereas 34 (39.5%) had pre-existing T2D. The majority were men (83.7%), with a baseline age of 65.9 years. Mean diabetes duration was 13.2 years, and baseline HbA1c was 7.3% (56 mmol/mol). Mean baseline eGFR was 66.8 mL/min/1.73 m2. Most patients had received a liver transplant (58.1%), followed by kidney (38.4%) and heart transplantation (3.5%). Tacrolimus-based immunosuppression was used in 73.3% of patients. At baseline, 51.2% were receiving insulin therapy, 40.1% metformin, and 30.2% GLP-1 receptor agonists. Features of the first and subsequent organ transplantations are presented in the Supplementary Material Table S1.

The mean duration of SGLT2 inhibitor exposure in the efficacy cohort was 1.6 years and only 10 participants had observation extending over 3 years.

Glycemic Outcomes

HbA1c variation over time, analyzed using the mixed model for repeated measures, was not statistically significant (p = 0.307). However, a significant reduction was observed between the second and third year of follow-up, with a mean decrease of 0.6% compared with baseline. This reduction was maintained for approximately 18 months before progressively attenuating. After year 3, HbA1c values gradually returned toward baseline and exceeded baseline levels after year 4, reaching + 0.3% at year 5 (Fig. 1a). Concomitant Glucose-Lowering Therapies

Following SGLT2 inhibitor initiation, metformin therapy remained stable in approximately 30–40% of patients. Insulin and DPP4 inhibitor use decreased by approximately 10% during follow-up, while use of GLP-1RA increased by approximately 7% (Fig. 1b).

Body Weight

Initiation of SGLT2 inhibitor therapy was followed by a significant reduction in body weight over time (p = 0.007). Weight loss was progressive during follow-up, reaching a maximum mean reduction of − 5.5 kg between years 3 and 4 (Fig. 1c). Thereafter, weight reduction stabilized at approximately − 2.8 kg beyond year 4. Patients with diabetes diagnosed after transplantation exhibited a slightly greater weight reduction compared with those with pre-existing diabetes (p = 0.012; (Table 2 and Fig. 1d).

Blood Pressure and Renal Outcomes

Systolic blood pressure (SBP) showed a trend toward reduction during follow-up but no time point reached statistical significance, and the overall longitudinal effect was not significant (Fig. 2a).

No significant changes in uACR were observed over time, irrespectively of baseline albuminuria class (Fig. 2b). uACR class remained stable in 81.4% of patients, improved in 11.4%, and worsened in 7.2%.

A progressive decline in renal function was observed during follow-up. Mean eGFR remained relatively stable during the first year but began to decrease after approximately 12 months. The longitudinal effect of time on eGFR was statistically significant (p < 0.001). After 4 ears of treatment, mean eGFR reduction exceeded 20 mL/min/1.73 m2 compared with baseline, slightly less in those starting from a baseline eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 (Fig. 2c), though this was based on only five participants with long-term observation. Only four patients (4.2%) experienced a 50% or greater loss of eGFR compared to baseline. The eGFR decline was less pronounced among kidney transplant recipients compared with recipients of other organs (p = 0.035; (Table 2 and Fig. 2d).

Safety and Treatment Discontinuation

During follow-up, 6 of 86 patients (7%) developed genitourinary infections, corresponding to an incidence rate of 5.1 cases per 100 person-years. Five cases required hospitalization for urosepsis, while one case of balanoposthitis was managed in the outpatient setting. Infection led to permanent treatment discontinuation in only four patients (4.6%).

Overall, 17 patients (19.8%) discontinued SGLT2 inhibitor therapy after a mean (SD) of 7.7 (7.5) months. Reasons for discontinuation included genitourinary infections (4.6%), worsening renal function (3.5%), drug intolerance (5.8%), inadequate glycemic control (4.6%), and comorbid conditions (1.2%).

Literature Review

We identified 22 eligible studies evaluating SGLT2 inhibitor therapy in solid organ transplant recipients with diabetes, comprising a total of 1557 patients (Table S2 in the Supplemental Material). The majority of studies included kidney transplant recipients, with follow-up durations ranging from 3 to 24 months (mean approximately 10 months). Across studies, HbA1c reductions ranged from − 0.2% to − 1.9%, and consistent weight loss was reported, with maximum reductions of approximately 5 kg. In 17 studies assessing blood pressure, systolic blood pressure decreased by up to − 7 mmHg. No long-term eGFR evaluation was available, while limited data on albuminuria suggested modest reductions. Among 884 patients in whom safety outcomes were described, genitourinary infections occurred in 11.3%, with approximately one-quarter requiring hospitalization. Treatment discontinuation due to infection was reported in 4.4% of patients, and discontinuation due to renal function decline in 2.4%.

Discussion

In this real-world cohort of solid organ transplant recipients with diabetes treated with SGLT2 inhibitors, we describe a modest and temporally delayed improvement in glycemic control, a sustained and clinically meaningful reduction in body weight, and a decline in renal function among few participants observed after the first year of therapy. Overall, we recorded a relatively low incidence of genitourinary infections. The discontinuation rate was comparable to that reported in large cardiovascular outcome trials in the T2D population. However, there were some cases of severe infection that may warrant caution with regards to the widespread implementation of therapy with SGLT2 inhibitors for PTDM.

Unlike the early HbA1c reductions consistently described in major randomized trials for T2D [9, 10, 17], the maximal HbA1c reduction in our cohort occurred between years 2 and 3. Significant changes occurred during time in the concomitant use of other diabetes drugs, including GLP-1RA, which could contribute to declining HbA1c levels. This pattern may reflect the unique metabolic environment of transplant recipients, characterized by fluctuating immunosuppressive exposure, variable insulin resistance, heterogeneous β-cell reserve, and dynamic changes in the glucose-lowering regimen. Baseline glycemic control in our population was relatively close to target, which may have limited the early effect. From a clinical standpoint, these findings suggest that in PTDM, SGLT2 inhibitors may contribute to metabolic stabilization and treatment expectations should be framed accordingly.

Weight reduction was progressive and sustained over time, reaching magnitudes comparable to those described in both transplant-specific cohorts and non-transplant T2DM populations. It should be noted that reduction in steroid dose or use, along with increase in exercise, may be factors that enable weight loss after transplantation. Given the high prevalence of overweight, metabolic syndrome, and cardiovascular risk factors in transplant recipients, weight loss may represent one of the most clinically relevant benefits of SGLT2 inhibitors in this setting. Even in the absence of dramatic glycemic changes, weight reduction may contribute to improved blood pressure control, reduced insulin requirements, and long-term cardiovascular risk mitigation.

A challenging finding concerns renal function. While SGLT2 inhibitors have robust renoprotective effects in chronic kidney disease and T2DM populations [8, 18, 19], a few patients with long-term observation in our cohort exhibited a progressive decline in eGFR beyond the first year of therapy. Importantly, the decline did not appear to reflect the early hemodynamic dip typically observed after SGLT2 initiation. In transplant recipients, chronic exposure to calcineurin inhibitors, pre-existing structural kidney damage, hypertension, and long-standing diabetes may create a renal milieu that differs substantially from that of participants in randomized trials. Reassuringly, the incidence of ≥ 50% eGFR decline was 4.2% over 1.6 years, similar to rate observed in the dapagliflozin arm of the DAPA-CKD trial (2.6%/year) and nominally lower to that observed in the placebo arm (4.8%/year) [8]. Although the signal is due to a small number of cases, it underscores the need for cautious renal monitoring and individualized risk–benefit assessment when prescribing SGLT2 inhibitors in PTDM, particularly beyond the first year of therapy. In the absence of a control group, results remain descriptive and it is not possible to determine whether the observed decline represents drug-related harm or the natural progression of chronic kidney dysfunction in this high-risk population.

When compared with the existing literature on SGLT2 inhibitors in PTDM, our findings show some consistencies and substantial divergence. In our cohort compared, the magnitude of HbA1c reduction and weight loss was broadly comparable, and infection rates were slightly lower, although severe cases (urosepsis) were proportionally more represented. The main difference lies in the longer follow-up and the observation of a significant renal decline beyond 2 years, an outcome not captured in previous studies. Therefore, our data do not contradict existing evidence but rather extend it temporally, highlighting the importance of long-term assessment in this population. However, a minority of patients were observed beyond 24 months contributing to the observed decline in eGFR, implying that more data will be needed.

This study has notable strengths. First, the observation time extended beyond the typical 6–12 months follow-up of most prior studies on SGLT2 inhibitor therapy in PTDM. Second, we included multiple organ types rather than kidney transplant recipients alone.

At the same time, important limitations must be acknowledged. The retrospective design and single-center setting limit generalizability. The sample size restricts statistical power and precludes robust subgroup analyses (such as by sex). The absence of a control group makes the study descriptive and prevents causal inference, particularly regarding renal outcomes. Furthermore, baseline renal trajectories prior to SGLT2 initiation were not available, making it impossible to determine whether the observed decline represents acceleration, stabilization, or natural progression. Also, the change in HbA1c and body weight over time should not be entirely attributed to SGLT2 inhibitors, as other therapies changed during the observation, including GLP-1RA and insulin. Therefore, the present results have to be viewed mainly as descriptive, with limited possibility of evaluating the true efficacy of SGLT2 inhibitors in this population.

Future research should prioritize prospective, multicenter studies specifically designed for transplant populations, ideally including matched control groups and predefined renal and cardiovascular composite endpoints analogous to those used in major SGLT2 trials. Long-term follow-up beyond 2 years is essential to clarify whether the renal signal we observe in this study reflects transplant-specific vulnerability or statistical fluctuation. From a clinical perspective, future work should also aim to identify subgroups of transplant recipients who derive the greatest metabolic and cardiovascular benefit with minimal renal risk, enabling more personalized therapeutic strategies.

Conclusions

In this real-world descriptive study on transplant recipients with diabetes, use of SGLT2 inhibitors was followed by sustained weight reduction and modest glycemic improvement, with an acceptable safety profile, despite some severe infections. The decline in renal function observed during the extended follow-up of a minority of participants highlights the complexity of extrapolating data from non-transplant populations and emphasizes the need for transplant-specific evidence. Until such data are available, we suggest that careful patient selection and close renal monitoring remain essential when integrating SGLT2 inhibitors into the management of PTDM.
