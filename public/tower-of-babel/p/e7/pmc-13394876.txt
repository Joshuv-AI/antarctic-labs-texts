The liver‐brain axis: A multidimensional regulatory network implicated in Alzheimer's disease pathogenesis and clinical implications
Authors: Ning Zhang, Wei Chen, Meng Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlzheimer's disease (AD) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder characterized by amyloid‐β (Aβ) deposition. The liver‐brain axis underscores the central role of the liver in modulating cognitive function through multidimensional regulatory mechanisms. As a core metabolic and detoxification organ, the liver also helps maintain cerebral homeostasis via pathways including the urea cycle, antioxidant systems, ketone body metabolism, and bile acid regulation. Dysfunction of these processes may lead to ammonia accumulation, exacerbated oxidative stress, and Aβ clearance, thereby accelerating the pathological progression of AD. Liver‐derived factors such as apolipoprotein E (APOE), C‐reactive protein (CRP), fibroblast growth factor 21 (FGF21), and insulin‐like growth factor 1 (IGF‐1) significantly increase the risk of AD through dual mechanisms‐inhibiting Aβ clearance and activating neuroinflammation, thereby directly affecting cognitive function via modulation of inflammation, metabolism, and blood–brain barrier (BBB) integrity. Neural interfaces formed by the hypothalamic–pituitary‐target gland axis and the vagus nerve enable communication from the liver to the brain, with emerging evidence also supporting a reverse influence from the brain to the liver. Emerging technologies such as molecular tracing and nanocarriers provide new tools for deciphering dynamic interactions within the liver‐brain axis. Liver‐targeted metabolic interventions show potential for reversing cognitive impairment. Unlike previous reviews that mainly focused on single pathways, this review conceptualizes the liver‐brain axis as a multidimensional regulatory network in AD. By clearly linking network nodes to potential therapeutic interventions, it provides us with a novel framework that not only describes the various mechanisms but also focuses on identifying actionable targets for disease prevention and treatment.Keywords: Alzheimer's disease, amyloid‐β, cognitive function, liver‐brain axis, neuroinflammation

INTRODUCTION

Within the intricate regulatory networks of biological systems, inter‐organ communication remains a central theme in life science research. In recent years, the convergence of neuroscience and metabolic biology has propelled the concept of the “liver‐brain axis” to the forefront of research, establishing it as an emerging avenue for investigating the pathogenesis of neurodegenerative diseases. As the largest metabolic and detoxification organ in humans, the liver not only carries out fundamental functions in substrate conversion and energy supply but also forms robust connections with the brain through refined neuro‐humoral pathways. This bidirectional regulatory relationship is indispensable for maintaining organismal homeostasis. 1 , 2

Neurodegenerative diseases, particularly Alzheimer's disease (AD), pose a significant and growing challenge to the health of the global aging population. 3 The core pathological features of AD include abnormal deposition of β‐amyloid (Aβ), hyperphosphorylation of tau protein, sustained neuroinflammation, and progressive neuronal loss. The development of these processes involves the complex interplay among multiple systems. 4 Traditional research has largely centered on intracerebral pathological changes, whereas the discovery of the liver‐brain axis offers a new perspective for understanding the complex mechanisms of AD. Growing evidence suggests that hepatic impairment can modulate the cerebral microenvironment through multiple pathways, thereby accelerating neurodegeneration; conversely, brain pathologies may induce adaptive metabolic adjustments in the liver, establishing a liver‐brain interactive network, in which liver‐derived signals influence cerebral pathology and, conversely, brain pathology may induce adaptive hepatic responses. 5

The regulatory role of the liver in neurodegenerative diseases is mediated by multiple critical pathways. At the metabolic and detoxification level, impairment of the liver's ammonia clearance capacity via the urea cycle can lead to elevated blood ammonia, which crosses the blood–brain barrier, inducing astrocyte dysfunction and neuronal toxicity. 6 Reduced synthesis of antioxidant substances such as glutathione exacerbates oxidative stress in the brain, thereby promoting the aggregation of Aβ and hyperphosphorylation of tau. 7 Ketone bodies produced by the liver serve as an alternative energy source for the brain, and their metabolic abnormalities are closely linked to impaired cerebral glucose utilization in the early stages of AD. 8 In the Aβ clearance mechanism, the liver eliminates peripheral Aβ through pathways involving the low‐density lipoprotein receptor‐related protein 1 (LRP1) and insulin‐degrading enzyme (IDE). Impairment of this function directly leads to elevated levels of Aβ in both plasma and the brain. 9 , 10 Furthermore, genetic polymorphisms in liver‐derived apolipoprotein E (APOE), particularly the APOEε4 subtype—the most significant genetic risk factor for AD—contribute to disease progression through dual mechanisms: reducing Aβ clearance efficiency and activating neuroinflammatory pathways. 11

Liver‐derived molecules and metabolites act as critical signaling mediators within the liver‐brain axis. C‐reactive protein (CRP), an acute‐phase protein synthesized by the liver, exacerbates neuroinflammation by activating the complement system, disrupting blood–brain barrier integrity, and disturbing metabolic homeostasis. 12 Fibroblast growth factor 21 (FGF21) and insulin‐like growth factor 1 (IGF‐1) play key roles in modulating AD pathology by regulating glucose and lipid metabolism, inhibiting Aβ production, and conferring neuroprotective effects. 13 , 14 Simultaneously, metabolites produced by hepatic metabolism‐such as advanced glycation end products (AGEs), branched‐chain amino acids, and bile acids‐modulate neurotransmitter balance, synaptic function, and inflammatory responses in the brain through the circulatory system, thereby serving as essential signaling molecules that facilitate liver‐brain communication. 15 , 16 , 17

The neural interface between the brain and the liver serves as the anatomical substrate for the regulation of the liver‐brain axis. Nuclei such as the arcuate nucleus (ARC) and paraventricular nucleus (PVN) of the hypothalamus modulate hepatic metabolism via sympathetic and parasympathetic pathways, while metabolic signals from the liver are transmitted back to the central nervous system through afferent fibers such as those of the vagus nerve, collectively forming a coordinated neurohumoral regulatory circuit. 18 Advanced techniques, including molecular tracing, neural pathway mapping, and transsynaptic labeling, provide critical technical support for deciphering the complex wiring of the liver‐brain axis, enabling dynamic monitoring of molecular flux and detailed mapping of neural connections. 19

Importantly, whereas existing reviews on the liver‐brain axis have largely concentrated on isolated mechanisms such as peripheral Aβ clearance or the neurotoxic effects of ammonia, the present review distinguishes itself by synthesizing three core dimensions—hepatic metabolism, liver‐derived signaling molecules, and neural interfaces—into an integrated multidimensional network model. Within this framework, we further highlight how dysfunction at different network nodes contributes to AD pathogenesis and discuss the emerging therapeutic potential of targeting these nodes. Thus, our review not only systematically consolidates current knowledge but also provides a conceptual shift from linear, single‐mechanism descriptions toward a network‐based understanding of liver‐brain communication in AD (Figure 1).

INTERPLAY BETWEEN THE LIVER AND NEURODEGENERATIVE DISORDERS

Regulatory effects of hepatic metabolism and detoxification on neurodegeneration

The liver, which is the central organ for systemic metabolism and detoxification, exerts a profound influence on the progression of neurodegenerative diseases such as AD. When impaired, hepatic function can accelerate pathological progression through several mechanisms. Growing evidence indicates that the liver regulates cerebral homeostasis via diverse pathways, including the urea cycle, antioxidant systems, ketone body metabolism, heavy metal detoxification, and bile acid signaling.

Hepatic metabolism of ammonia via the urea cycle is essential for detoxification. If this function is compromised, ammonia accumulates in the blood, crosses the blood–brain barrier (BBB), and causes astrocyte swelling and glutamatergic excitotoxicity, ultimately leading to neuronal death and synaptic dysfunction. 6 In clinical settings, elevated blood ammonia in cirrhotic patients is positively associated with increased glutamate/glutamine ratios in the brain and correlated with significant cognitive decline. 20 Animal models of chronic hyperammonemia further reveal activation of the tumor necrosis factor receptor 1 (TNFR1)‐nuclear factor kappa‐B (NF‐κB) pathway in microglia, thereby promoting tumor necrosis factor α (TNFα) release and enhanced neuroinflammation. 21

Glutathione (GSH), synthesized primarily in the liver, serves as a key antioxidant in the central nervous system. In patients with non‐alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), the capacity for hepatic GSH synthesis is compromised, leading to reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation in peripheral systems and the brain, which in turn accelerates neuronal damage. 7 Studies in AD transgenic mouse models demonstrate that liver‐specific overexpression of GSH synthase reduces both cerebral Aβ deposition and tau protein phosphorylation. 22 In addition, liver‐derived thioredoxin (TRX) has been shown to mitigate oxidative stress‐induced neuronal apoptosis by suppressing the apoptosis signal‐regulating kinase 1 (ASK1)‐JNK signaling pathway. 23

Ketone bodies (e.g., β‐hydroxybutyrate), synthesized in the liver, serve as alternative energy substrates for the brain. Since early‐stage AD is frequently accompanied by impaired cerebral glucose metabolism, enhanced hepatic ketone body production can partially compensate for this cerebral energy deficit. 8 Human observational studies have reported a positive correlation between plasma β‐hydroxybutyrate levels and preserved hippocampal volume in patients with mild cognitive impairment (MCI), suggesting a potential association but not establishing causation. 24 Preclinical evidence from animal models more directly demonstrates that ketogenic diets promote ketogenesis via activation of the hepatic PPARα signaling pathway, which in turn improves cognitive performance in AD mice. 25

The elimination of heavy metals such as copper and iron is facilitated by hepatic pathways involving metallothionein (MT) and glutathione conjugation. A downregulation in the expression of hepatic MT‐1/2 in AD can lead to copper accumulation in the brain, thereby promoting Aβ aggregation. 26 Additionally, studies indicate that in patients with iron‐overload liver diseases like hemochromatosis, increased iron deposition in the brain is observed, suggesting a potential pathway for the exacerbation of AD pathology. 27

While primary bile acids (e.g., cholic acid) produced by the liver exert anti‐inflammatory effects via farnesoid X receptor (FXR) signaling, the bile acid profile is altered in AD. In contrast, deoxycholic acid (DCA) is significantly elevated in AD mice. Strikingly, knocking out the membrane receptor takeda G protein‐coupled receptor (TGR5) reduces cerebral amyloid plaques and improves cognition, 28 implicating specific bile acid pathways in AD pathogenesis. Supporting this, clinical studies show elevated lithocholic acid in AD patients' plasma, suggesting its utility as a diagnostic biomarker. 29 These findings converge to demonstrate that hepatic metabolic functions are pivotal regulatory nodes in AD, warranting the exploration of liver‐targeted therapies.

Liver‐mediated clearance mechanisms of Aβ and their pathological implications

The liver's central role in clearing Aβ has been extensively validated. The low‐density LRP1 on hepatocytes serves as the primary receptor for peripheral Aβ clearance. Clinical correlational studies have shown that reduced hepatic LRP1 expression is associated with elevated plasma Aβ levels in patients with hepatic dysfunction. 9 In animal models, liver‐specific LRP1 knockout mice exhibit a twofold increase in cerebral Aβ deposition alongside cognitive deficits, 9 providing preclinical evidence supporting a causal role of hepatic LRP1 in peripheral Aβ clearance. Human data directly establishing causality, however, remain lacking. Furthermore, the liver secretes IDE and neprilysin (NEP), which directly degrade circulating Aβ. 10 Impairment of this clearance system is evident in NAFLD rat models, which show reduced LRP1 expression and a concomitant elevation of Aβ levels. 30 Notably, Aβ can also be excreted via hepatic transporters like ATP‐binding cassette sub‐family B member 1 (ABCB1), and diminished ABCB1 function is associated with reduced Aβ clearance. 31 , 32

Liver‐derived APOE polymorphism: Dual role in AD genetic risk and neuroinflammatory mechanisms

In AD, the predominant pathological effects of APOE4 are mediated by APOE4 produced within the brain, specifically by astrocytes and microglia, whereas peripheral APOE4 derived from the liver may play a modulatory role. The APOEε4 allele is the strongest genetic risk factor for AD, primarily by impairing Aβ clearance and exacerbating neuroinflammation. 11 Whereas the APOE3 isoform promotes peripheral Aβ clearance via binding to the LRP1 receptor, the structurally unstable APOE4 isoform has a reduced affinity for Aβ, consequently diminishing its hepatic uptake. 33 Transgenic studies demonstrate that mice with hepatocyte‐specific expression of human APOE4 exhibit significantly increased cerebral Aβ deposition and tau phosphorylation. 34 Furthermore, APOE4 activates the hepatic toll‐like receptor 4 (TLR4) signaling pathway, inducing the release of pro‐inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin 6 (IL‐6) and TNFα, which in turn amplify neuroinflammatory responses. 35 Consistent with this mechanism, clinical data show significantly higher plasma TNFα levels in APOEε4 carriers than in non‐carriers. 36 Additionally, liver‐derived APOE4 increases blood–brain barrier permeability by suppressing the platelet‐derived growth factor receptor β (PDGFRβ) signaling pathway in pericytes, thereby facilitating the entry of peripheral inflammatory mediators and Aβ into the brain. 37 These findings collectively delineate a multi‐faceted mechanism by which hepatic APOE4 drives AD pathology.

Brain‐to‐liver signaling: Adaptive hepatic responses induced by cerebral pathologies

A bidirectional interaction exists between the liver and the brain, whereby cerebral pathologies can trigger adaptive hepatic responses. For example, AD model mice show upregulation of the hepatic ketogenic enzyme mitochondrial 3‐hydroxy‐3‐methylglutaryl‐CoA synthase (HMGCS2). This suggests the liver may compensate for impaired cerebral glucose metabolism by increasing ketone body production. 38 Conversely, pathological signals from the brain can propagate to the periphery. Neurons release exosomes containing pathological proteins like phosphorylated tau (p‐Tau), which can travel via the circulation to peripheral organs, including the liver. 39

LIVER‐DERIVED MOLECULES ACTING ON THE BRAIN

C‐reactive protein, CRP

CRP, an acute‐phase protein produced by the liver, is up‐regulated by pro‐inflammatory cytokines such as IL‐6 and shows marked elevation during systemic inflammation. 40 Its primary biological role involves binding to phosphocholine on pathogens and damaged cells, thereby activating the classical complement pathway and promoting phagocytosis by macrophages, which amplifies the inflammatory response. 41 Clinical evidence indicates that elevated peripheral CRP levels are associated with an increased risk of AD and cognitive decline. Higher CRP concentrations often correlate with increased incidence of AD and more rapid progression of cognitive impairment. 42 , 43 , 44 , 45

CRP‐mediated inflammatory response and AD

The well‐established association between CRP and AD underscores the significant role of CRP‐mediated inflammatory responses in AD pathogenesis. 12 Increased CRP levels contribute to complement system activation and promote the activation of microglia and astrocytes. 46 Once activated, microglia release large quantities of pro‐inflammatory cytokines, including interleukin‐1β (IL‐1β), IL‐6, and TNFα, as well as neurotoxic mediators such as ROS and nitric oxide (NO). These factors further aggravate Aβ deposition and tau protein hyperphosphorylation, creating a self‐sustaining vicious cycle that drives disease progression. 47 Studies have shown that CRP expression is significantly elevated in the brains of AD patients compared to healthy controls and correlates positively with both Aβ plaque load and the severity of neuroinflammation. 48 Furthermore, CRP can directly interact with Aβ, promoting its aggregation and deposition, thereby amplifying its neurotoxic effects. 49

CRP‐associated metabolic dysregulation and AD

The liver, a central metabolic organ, communicates with the brain via the liver‐brain axis, facilitating bidirectional exchange of metabolites and signaling molecules. An abnormal increase in circulating CRP levels is closely linked to systemic metabolic dysregulation, which contributes critically to the pathogenesis of AD. 50 CRP can interfere with insulin signaling pathways, induce insulin resistance, and impair cerebral glucose uptake and utilization, thereby disrupting neuronal energy metabolism. 51 Concurrently, CRP influences lipid metabolism by enhancing the oxidative modification of low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL‐C), thereby increasing the risk of atherosclerosis. Cerebral vascular atherosclerosis diminishes cerebral blood flow, resulting in neuronal ischemia and hypoxia, which in turn accelerates the progression of AD pathology. 52 , 53 Moreover, CRP‐related metabolic disturbances may indirectly compromise brain function by altering hepatic metabolic and nutrient transport processes.

CRP‐mediated effects on blood–brain barrier integrity in AD

The BBB is essential for maintaining homeostasis of the cerebral microenvironment by blocking the entry of blood‐borne harmful substances while permitting the selective transport of nutrients and neuroactive molecules. 54 Elevated CRP levels have been shown to impair BBB integrity. 55 Distinct from the pericyte‐mediated effects of APOE4, CRP primarily acts by activating inflammatory signaling pathways that upregulate matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), leading to the degradation of tight junction proteins such as occludin and claudin‐5 and consequently increasing BBB permeability. 56 , 57 , 58 This disruption allows inflammatory mediators and toxic substances from the circulation to infiltrate the brain parenchyma, exacerbating neuroinflammation and neuronal injury. In addition, impaired BBB function reduces the efflux of Aβ from the brain into the blood, contributing to cerebral Aβ accumulation and thus facilitating the progression of AD. 59 , 60 , 61

Fibroblast growth factor 21 (FGF21)

FGF21 is a key metabolic regulator predominantly secreted by the liver, playing an essential role in regulating glucose and lipid metabolism and energy homeostasis. 62 , 63 , 64 In recent years, growing evidence has highlighted the link between FGF21 and neurodegenerative diseases, and its complex involvement in AD has increasingly become an important research focus.

Association between FGF21 and metabolic dysregulation in AD

FGF21, a key metabolic regulator, is strongly linked to disturbances in glucose and lipid metabolism observed in AD patients. 13 , 65 AD is frequently accompanied by abnormal glucose metabolism and insulin resistance; FGF21 has been shown to enhance insulin sensitivity, thereby improving cerebral glucose uptake and utilization. 66 , 67 , 68 In AD animal models, impaired FGF21 expression contributes to defective neuronal glucose uptake, leading to energy insufficiency and accelerated neuronal injury and loss. 69 In addition, FGF21 helps regulate lipid metabolism by lowering circulating triglycerides and cholesterol levels, which in turn alleviates inflammation and oxidative stress associated with dyslipidemia—both key contributors to AD pathogenesis. 70 , 71

Regulatory effects of FGF21 on pathological features of AD

Aβ abnormal deposition and tau protein hyperphosphorylation represent hallmark pathological features of AD. FGF21 has been shown to modulate these two core pathological processes through multiple mechanisms. 4 , 72 In terms of Aβ pathology, FGF21 not only suppresses microglial overactivation—thereby reducing the release of pro‐inflammatory cytokines such as TNFα and IL‐6 and mitigating inflammation‐driven Aβ production—but also facilitates Aβ clearance from the brain by promoting its transport across the blood–brain barrier into the peripheral circulation, which contributes to reduced cerebral Aβ deposition. Regarding tau pathology, FGF21 may inhibit hyperphosphorylation through the regulation of key kinases and phosphatases, thus helping to maintain microtubule stability and support neuronal structure and function. 73 , 74 , 75 , 76

Clinical evidence and neuroprotective mechanisms of FGF21 in AD

Clinical studies provide supporting evidence for the association between FGF21 and AD. Some reports indicate that serum FGF21 levels in AD patients correlate with disease severity, and changes in FGF21 concentration may predict disease progression in individuals with MCI as they transition to AD. 77 However, due to variables such as sample selection, detection methodologies, and individual heterogeneity, no consistent conclusion has been reached regarding how FGF21 levels change in AD patients. In terms of neuroprotective mechanisms, upon binding to its receptors, FGF21 activates downstream signaling pathways, including extracellular signal‐regulated kinase (ERK) and protein kinase B (Akt), which suppress apoptotic processes and promote neuronal survival and repair. Furthermore, FGF21 modulates the expression of brain‐derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), helping to maintain synaptic integrity and improve cognitive function through BDNF upregulation. 74 , 78 , 79 , 80

Insulin‐like growth factor 1 (IGF‐1)

IGF‐1 is a polypeptide hormone with important roles in growth, development, metabolic homeostasis, and neuroprotection. 81 , 82 , 83 , 84 , 85 In recent years, growing evidence has highlighted its relevance to AD. By signaling through the insulin‐like growth factor‐1 receptor (IGF‐1R), IGF‐1 activates downstream pathways that regulate neuronal proliferation, differentiation, survival, and synaptic plasticity, processes essential for maintaining normal brain function. 14 , 86 , 87 The involvement of IGF‐1 in AD is multifaceted, and further elucidation of its mechanisms may provide valuable insights into AD pathogenesis and reveal potential therapeutic targets.

Epidemiological association between IGF‐1 and AD

Epidemiological evidence has provided important insights into the association between IGF‐1 and AD. Cross‐sectional studies have indicated a significant relationship between lower circulating IGF‐1 levels and an increased prevalence of AD. 88 , 89 , 90 In particular, lower circulating IGF‐1 levels are generally associated with an elevated risk of developing AD. 91 Prospective cohort studies further suggest that age‐related decline in IGF‐1 coincides temporally with the increasing incidence of AD in older populations. 90 , 92 It is worth noting that the elevated serum IGF‐1 levels in AD patients reported in some clinical studies 90 do not contradict the central neuroprotective effects of IGF‐1 discussed here, as these studies also showed that cerebrospinal fluid IGF‐1 levels did not increase correspondingly, reflecting compartmental differences between peripheral and central systems. These epidemiological findings suggest that IGF‐1 may be involved in the pathogenesis and progression of AD, and that decreased IGF‐1 levels could represent a potential risk factor for the disease.

Impact on Aβ metabolism

Abnormal deposition of Aβ is a core pathological hallmark of AD, and IGF‐1 has been shown to play a key role in the regulation of Aβ metabolism. 93 , 94 IGF‐1 modulates Aβ production by influencing the activity of β‐secretase (BACE1) and γ‐secretase. 95 Specifically, IGF‐1 suppresses the expression and enzymatic function of BACE1, thereby reducing the β‐secretase‐mediated cleavage of amyloid precursor protein (APP) and subsequent Aβ generation. 96 In addition to limiting Aβ production, IGF‐1 facilitates its clearance by enhancing the phagocytic activity of microglia and activating intracellular signaling pathways that promote the recognition and degradation of Aβ deposits. 97 , 98 Supporting these mechanisms, exogenous IGF‐1 administration in AD mouse models significantly attenuates cerebral Aβ plaque load, further validating the beneficial role of IGF‐1 in Aβ metabolic homeostasis. 99

Regulation of neuroinflammation

Neuroinflammation significantly contributes to the pathological progression of AD, whereas IGF‐1 has been shown to possess considerable anti‐inflammatory properties. 86 , 100 , 101 , 102 IGF‐1 suppresses the overactivation of both microglia and astrocytes, thereby reducing the release of pro‐inflammatory cytokines such as IL‐1β and TNFα. 103 Concurrently, it upregulates the expression of anti‐inflammatory cytokines including interleukin‐10 (IL‐10), helping to restore the balance between pro‐ and anti‐inflammatory responses. 104 Moreover, IGF‐1 inhibits the activation of the NF‐κB signaling pathway, which leads to reduced transcription of inflammation‐associated genes and mitigates neuroinflammation‐related neuronal injury. 105 , 106 Under AD pathological conditions, diminished IGF‐1 levels may result in dysregulated neuroinflammation, potentially accelerating neurodegenerative processes (Figure 2).

METABOLITES FROM THE LIVER TO THE BRAIN

Energy metabolism‐associated metabolites and AD

Impaired cerebral glucose metabolism is a recognized feature of AD. The liver, which plays a central role in maintaining systemic glucose homeostasis, shows functional changes that are closely associated with AD progression. 107 , 108 , 109 Under chronic hyperglycemic conditions, advanced glycation end products (AGEs), produced with involvement of the liver, accumulate systemically. These compounds can cross the blood–brain barrier and bind to the receptor for AGEs (RAGE) in the brain, activating microglia and astrocytes and triggering neuroinflammation. 15 , 71 , 110 Moreover, activation of the AGEs‐RAGE signaling pathway promotes the production and deposition of Aβ, whose abnormal aggregation contributes significantly to neuronal injury and synaptic dysfunction in AD. 111 , 112 Insulin resistance is also frequently observed in AD patients. Diminished hepatic insulin sensitivity impairs cerebral glucose uptake and utilization, leading to an inadequate energy supply in the brain and accelerated neurodegenerative processes. 113 , 114 , 115

Altered ketone body metabolism represents a promising therapeutic target in AD. As the disease progresses, the brain's ability to utilize glucose becomes impaired, whereas ketone bodies produced by the liver can provide an alternative energy source for neuronal function. 116 Beyond serving as a metabolic substrate, β‐hydroxybutyrate modulates histone deacetylase (HDAC) activity, influencing the expression of AD‐related genes and subsequently reducing Aβ production, tau hyperphosphorylation, neuroinflammation, and oxidative stress. 117 , 118 Clinical observations support this mechanism: interventions such as the ketogenic diet, which elevates ketone body levels, have been associated with cognitive improvement in certain AD patients, underscoring the therapeutic potential of liver‐derived ketone bodies in AD pathology. 119

Amino acid metabolites and AD

Alterations in the ratio of branched‐chain amino acids (BCAAs) to aromatic amino acids (AAAs) have been observed in patients with AD. 16 , 120 , 121 Dysregulated hepatic metabolism of BCAAs and AAAs results in decreased circulating BCAA levels and elevated AAA levels, which in turn may affect neurotransmitter synthesis in the brain. 122 After crossing the blood–brain barrier, AAAs such as phenylalanine and tyrosine serve as precursors for neurotransmitters including dopamine and norepinephrine. Elevated AAA levels can disrupt neurotransmitter balance and impair normal neuronal signaling. 123 Meanwhile, BCAAs have been shown to exert neuroprotective effects; a reduction in their availability may diminish this protective capacity, thereby potentially accelerating AD progression. 124 , 125

Alterations in tryptophan metabolism are closely linked to neuroinflammation and cognitive impairment in AD. 126 , 127 , 128 Hepatic impairment can disrupt tryptophan metabolism, resulting in elevated levels of free tryptophan in circulation. After crossing the blood–brain barrier, tryptophan promotes serotonin synthesis. Altered serotonin signaling adversely affects neural plasticity and synaptic function, contributing to affective and cognitive deficits in AD patients. 129 , 130 Moreover, dysregulated activation of the kynurenine pathway of tryptophan metabolism leads to the accumulation of neurotoxic metabolites such as quinolinic acid, which induces neuronal damage and may accelerate AD pathogenesis. 131

Lipid metabolites and AD

Free fatty acids (FFAs), which are synthesized and metabolized primarily in the liver, play a significant role in the initiation and progression of AD. 132 , 133 , 134 In individuals with obesity or metabolic syndrome, dysregulated hepatic lipid metabolism leads to elevated circulating FFAs. Increased systemic FFA levels can impair blood–brain barrier integrity, promoting the brain accumulation of neurotoxic agents such as Aβ, while also activating microglia and inducing neuroinflammation. 135 , 136 , 137 , 138 , 139 Saturated fatty acids further contribute to AD pathogenesis by disrupting neuronal membrane fluidity and function, impairing synaptic plasticity, and accelerating cognitive decline. 140 , 141 In contrast, unsaturated fatty acids‐particularly ω‐3 polyunsaturated fatty acids whose metabolism involves the liver‐exert anti‐inflammatory and neuroprotective effects. These benefits are mediated through reduced Aβ deposition, suppression of neuroinflammation, and improved cognitive performance. However, impaired hepatic metabolism of protective fatty acids may diminish these beneficial effects. 14 , 142

Abnormal phospholipid metabolism contributes to the pathogenesis of AD. Phospholipids produced in the liver are essential constituents of neuronal membranes and myelin sheaths. In AD patients, dysregulated phospholipid metabolism is characterized by reduced levels of phosphatidylcholine (lecithin) and phosphatidylethanolamine (cephalin), which compromises neuronal membrane integrity and function. This disruption impairs normal neuronal signaling and synaptic plasticity. 143 , 144 , 145 Moreover, phospholipid‐derived metabolites—notably phosphatidic acid and diacylglycerol—serve as key intracellular second messengers; their dysregulation perturbs downstream signaling cascades, influences tau phosphorylation, and modulates pathways involved in Aβ production, thereby promoting AD‐related pathology. 146 , 147

Other metabolites and AD

Bile acids contribute to the pathological process of AD through the gut‐liver‐brain axis. 17 Altered levels of bile acids and their metabolites, which are synthesized primarily in the liver, have been detected in the cerebrospinal fluid of AD patients. 148 Bile acid receptors such as FXR and TGR5 are widely expressed in the brain. Activation of FXR modulates neuroinflammation by suppressing microglial activation and reducing the release of pro‐inflammatory cytokines, thereby attenuating Aβ‐induced neurotoxicity. 149 In contrast, TGR5 activation promotes the secretion of neuroprotective factors, modulates neurotransmitter metabolism, and supports cognitive function. Dysregulation of bile acid metabolism impairs these protective mechanisms, which may exacerbate AD progression. 28 Notably, the finding that TGR5 deficiency ameliorates AD pathology appears to contrast with the neuroprotective effects reported in other studies. This apparent contradiction likely reflects cell‐type‐specific functions of TGR5: while TGR5 activation in microglia or peripheral immune cells exerts anti‐inflammatory effects, TGR5 signaling in excitatory neurons may promote Aβ pathology. 28 , 149 Thus, the net effect of TGR5 modulation depends critically on the cellular context, and further studies are needed to dissect these opposing roles.

Although the pathogenesis of hepatic encephalopathy differs from that of AD, liver dysfunction resulting in elevated blood ammonia has also been implicated in AD. After crossing the blood–brain barrier, ammonia disrupts cerebral energy metabolism by depleting α‐ketoglutarate, impairing the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, and reducing adenosine triphosphate (ATP) production, a mechanism that shares similarities with the bioenergetic deficits observed in AD. 16 , 150 In addition, ammonia perturbs neurotransmitter metabolism, leading to neuronal dysfunction. Chronic hyperammonemia may thus accelerate neurodegeneration and increase the risk of AD onset 151 , 152 (Figure 3 and Table 1).

Mechanisms by which liver‐derived metabolites regulate the pathology of neurodegenerative diseases. Metabolites produced by the liver, such as AGEs, bile acids, amino acids, and FFAs, enter the brain via the blood–brain barrier (composed of transporters and endothelial cells). Thereby, they participate in pathological processes such as Aβ deposition and tau phosphorylation, ultimately influencing the onset and progression of neurodegenerative diseases.

The role of gut microbiome in liver‐brain axis

The gut microbiome serves as a critical upstream mediator of the gut‐liver‐brain axis. Although a detailed discussion of gut microbiota is beyond the primary scope of this review, it is important to acknowledge that gut bacteria play essential roles in the metabolism of both bile acids and tryptophan‐two key metabolite classes discussed above. Gut microbes regulate the conversion of primary bile acids to secondary bile acids (e.g., deoxycholic acid and lithocholic acid), which have been implicated in AD pathology. 17 , 28 Similarly, the kynurenine pathway of tryptophan metabolism is partially dependent on gut microbial enzymes, producing metabolites that influence neuroinflammation and cognitive function. 126 , 127 , 128 Thus, the gut‐liver‐brain axis represents an integrated network in which gut microbiota, hepatic metabolism, and brain function are closely interconnected.

Sex differences in the liver‐brain axis and AD

Gender is an important biological variable influencing the risk of Alzheimer's disease and liver metabolism, but research specifically addressing gender in the context of the liver‐brain axis is still relatively scarce. Epidemiological evidence consistently shows that even after adjusting for the fact that women have a longer life expectancy, women still have a higher lifetime risk of developing Alzheimer's disease than men. 153 The gender differences in liver metabolism are also well documented. Male and female individuals differ in liver lipid metabolism and inflammatory responses, a phenomenon mainly regulated by sex hormones such as estrogen and testosterone. 154 , 155 For example, existing studies confirm that estrogen can regulate liver insulin sensitivity and lipid homeostasis, which may indirectly affect brain function via the liver‐brain axis. 156 Based on these various gender‐related differences, future studies on the role of the liver‐brain axis in AD should explicitly consider gender as a biological variable.

NEURAL INTERFACE BETWEEN THE BRAIN AND LIVER

The brain and liver engage in bidirectional communication through a complex neurohumoral network, with hypothalamic nuclei and their associated neural pathways serving as the central regulatory node. These neural circuits not only help maintain hepatic metabolic homeostasis but also contribute significantly to the pathological progression of AD (Figure 4).

Schematic diagram of the core mechanisms of the neural regulatory interface in the liver‐brain axis. The hypothalamus (including ARC, PVN and VMH) serves as the central regulatory hub. It modulates liver metabolism through sympathetic nerves that release NE and parasympathetic nerves that release ACh. The liver, in turn, provides feedback to the central nervous system via metabolic signals such as FFAs. Simultaneously, CRH secreted by the hypothalamus participates in regulation through neuro‐humoral pathways, forming a bidirectional “central‐neural‐liver” regulatory network. This network maintains systemic homeostasis and influences the progression of neurodegenerative diseases.

Hypothalamic nuclei regulate hepatic metabolism

Hypothalamic nuclei and the liver

The hypothalamus serves as a neuroendocrine regulatory center that directly governs hepatic metabolism via nucleus‐specific neuronal projections. Key hypothalamic nuclei such as the arcuate nucleus (ARC), paraventricular nucleus (PVN), and ventromedial nucleus (VMH) integrate central energy‐sensing signals (e.g., leptin and insulin) with peripheral metabolic cues (e.g., glucose and free fatty acids) to enable precise regulation of hepatic glucose and lipid metabolism. 157 , 158 , 159

ARC and hepatic metabolism

The ARC of the hypothalamus acts as a central regulator of energy balance, comprising two functionally antagonistic neuronal populations. 160 , 161 (1) Agouti‐related protein (AgRP)/neuropeptide Y (NPY) neurons that stimulate feeding and reduce energy expenditure. These neurons project to the nucleus tractus solitarius (NTS) in the brainstem, activating sympathetic pathways to promote hepatic glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis. 162 (2) Proopiomelanocortin (POMC)/cocaine‐and amphetamine‐regulated transcript (CART) neurons that suppress appetite and increase energy expenditure. Acting via α‐melanocyte‐stimulating hormone (α‐MSH) signaling, they inhibit hepatic fatty acid synthase (FASN) activity and enhance fatty acid β‐oxidation. 163 A decrease in POMC neuron number within the ARC may lead to dysregulated hepatic lipid metabolism and exacerbate oxidative stress in the brain. 164

PVN and hepatic metabolism

The PVN of the hypothalamus functions as a central region for stress response and autonomic control, operating through two principal pathways. (1) Sympathetic regulation: activation of corticotropin releasing hormone (CRH) neurons in the PVN modulates blood glucose levels through the PVN‐CRH → VMH → raphe pallidus nucleus (RPa) → sympathetic nerves→liver circuitry. 18 Experimental modulation of astrocytic acetate levels within the PVN has been shown to disrupt glucose homeostasis and cognitive function in sleep‐fragmented (SF) mice. 165 (2) Parasympathetic regulation: acetylcholine (ACh) neurons originating in the PVN inhibit hepatic inflammation through projections to the dorsal motor nucleus of the vagus (DMV). Impairment of this regulatory circuit is associated with Kupffer cell overactivation and IL‐1β release, which can exacerbate neuroinflammation in AD. 166

VMH and hepatic metabolism

The VMH acts as a key integrative center for the regulation of feeding behavior and energy expenditure through the following mechanisms. (1) Adenosine 5′‐monophosphate (AMP)‐activated protein kinase (AMPK) pathway Activation: leptin receptor (LEPR)‐expressing neurons in the VMH suppress hepatic mTORC1 activity via AMPK signaling, thereby enhancing autophagy and promoting mitochondrial biogenesis. 167 (2) Fatty acid oxidation regulation: NPY in the dorsomedial hypothalamic nucleus (DMH) enhances hepatic insulin sensitivity in high‐fat diet‐fed rats by activating the hepatic phosphatidylinositol 3‐kinase (PI3K)/Akt insulin signaling pathway. 168

Neural fiber connections between the central nervous system and the liver

Afferent sensory nerves

Hepatic metabolic signals are relayed to the central nervous system through two principal afferent pathways. (1) Vagal afferent fibers: metabolic products (e.g., glucose, free fatty acids, ATP) from hepatic parenchymal cells (hepatocytes, Kupffer cells) are detected by transient receptor potential vanilloid‐1 (TRPV1)/transient receptor potential cation channel subfamily m member 5 (TRPM5) channels in the nodose ganglion (NG) of the vagus nerve. These signals are transmitted to the NTS, subsequently activating POMC neurons in the ARC. 169 , 170 (2) Sympathetic afferent fibers: during liver injury or inflammation, C‐fibers in the dorsal root ganglion (DRG) of sympathetic nerves convey damage signals via the IL‐6/signal transducer and activator of transcription (STAT3) pathway to the spinal dorsal horn. These signals further project to PVN of the hypothalamus, inducing the release of stress‐related hormones (e.g., corticosterone). 171 , 172

Efferent neural pathways

The central nervous system regulates liver function bidirectionally through the autonomic nervous system. (1) Sympathetic pathway: originating from the thoracic spinal segments (T5‐T9) IML, this pathway innervates the liver via the celiac ganglia, releasing norepinephrine (NE) to activate β2‐adrenergic receptors, promoting glycogenolysis (through phosphorylation of GSK‐3β) and lipolysis (through activation of HSL). 173 (2) Parasympathetic pathway: the preganglionic fibers of the dorsal motor nucleus of DMV release ACh through the hepatic plexus, activating M3 receptors on hepatocytes to promote glycogenesis while inhibiting the NF‐κB pathway in Kupffer cells to reduce IL‐1β release. 174

Neurohormones and the hepatic system

The hypothalamus directly regulates liver metabolism through the neuroendocrine axis. (1) CRH secreted by the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis (HPA axis): PVN stimulates the release of adrenocorticotropic‐hormone (ACTH) through the pituitary portal system, promotes the secretion of cortisol by the adrenal cortex, induces the expression of hepatic phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase (PEPCK), and glucose‐6‐phosphatase (G6PC), and enhances gluconeogenesis. 175 (2) Thyrotropin‐releasing hormone (TRH) secreted by HPT axis: PVN stimulates pituitary TSH release, promotes thyroxine (T3/T4) synthesis, and induces hepatic peroxisome proliferation (such as peroxisome proliferator‐activated receptor gamma (PPARγ)) and mitochondrial β‐oxidation. 176 , 177

ADVANCED TECHNOLOGIES FOR TRACING AND MAPPING THE LIVER‐BRAIN AXIS

In‐depth investigation of the liver‐brain axis heavily depends on advanced neural tracing and functional mapping technologies, which have provided critical tools for elucidating the complex bidirectional communication between the liver and the brain.

Molecular tracing

Molecular tracing technology plays a pivotal role in liver‐brain axis research by enabling precise tracking of the transport and transformation of specific molecules between the liver and the brain. Among various molecular tracing approaches, methods based on isotope labeling are widely employed. For instance, deuterium (2H)‐or carbon‐13 (13C)‐labeled bile acids (e.g., taurocholic acid, TCA) or ketone bodies (e.g., β‐hydroxybutyrate) can be quantitatively analyzed using liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) to assess their traversal efficiency across the blood–brain barrier following hepatic synthesis. 178 , 179 Beyond isotope labeling, fluorescently labeled biomolecules also serve important roles in molecular tracing. By conjugating fluorophores to signaling molecules such as neurotransmitters and cytokines involved in liver‐brain communication and utilizing the high‐resolution imaging capabilities of fluorescence microscopy, researchers can monitor the real‐time distribution and dynamic changes of these molecules in vivo after their release from hepatocytes or Kupffer cells. 180 For example, fluorescent dyes such as DiR have been used to track the migration of exosomes from primary tumor sites to the brain via in vivo imaging systems (IVIS). Furthermore, the combination of 18F‐fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography (18F‐FDG PET) with kinetic modeling allows quantitative assessment of the effects of liver‐derived metabolites on cerebral glucose uptake. 181

Neural tracing

Neural tracing technology serves as a fundamental approach for delineating neuronal connectivity within the liver‐brain axis. The retrograde tracing method, employing neurotropic viruses such as pseudorabies virus (PRV‐152), enables trans‐synaptic retrograde labeling of multisynaptic pathways, thereby revealing afferent projections from the liver to hypothalamic nuclei such as the ARC. This approach offers distinct advantages in identifying the origins of hepatic innervation. 182 In contrast, anterograde tracing methods are used to elucidate the efferent transmission of neural signals from the brain to the liver. For instance, injection of adeno‐associated virus serotype 1 (AAV1)‐Cre into hepatic vagal nerve terminals, combined with a Cre‐dependent tdTomato reporter expressed in the VMH, enables precise mapping of primary synaptic connections along liver‐projecting neural circuits. 183

Transmission technology

In the study of the liver‐brain axis, advanced delivery technologies provide powerful tools for simulating and monitoring the transport of substances between the liver and the brain. Among these, nanotechnology‐based delivery carriers have shown considerable potential in this field. Due to their unique size‐dependent properties and favorable physicochemical characteristics, nanoparticles can effectively deliver cargo such as drugs or tracers, thereby mimicking the trafficking of endogenous molecules along the liver‐brain axis. For instance, nanocarrier‐based delivery systems, such as liver cell membrane‐coated poly (lactic‐co‐glycolic acid) (PLGA) nanoparticles, have shown potential for targeted drug delivery across the BBB by leveraging receptor‐mediated transport mechanisms (e.g., low‐density lipoprotein receptor (LDLR) pathway), 184 offering a promising strategy for delivering therapeutic agents along the liver‐brain axis.

The limitations of advanced technology

In summary, molecular tracing, viral neural tracing, and nanocarrier technologies have greatly advanced our understanding of the liver‐brain axis by enabling dynamic tracking of molecules, mapping of neural circuits, and targeted delivery of therapeutic agents. However, each of these approaches has inherent limitations including potential artifacts, safety concerns, and substantial translation barriers that must be acknowledged when interpreting experimental findings. Currently, most of these technologies remain at the preclinical stage, and their clinical application in the context of the liver‐brain axis is still a long way off. Future research should prioritize addressing these limitations through improved tracer design, safer viral vectors, and more biocompatible nanocarriers, along with rigorous validation in large animal models and, ultimately, human studies.

SUMMARY AND PERSPECTIVES

Core conclusions

In this review, we have systematically synthesized current evidence supporting the liver‐brain axis as a multidimensional regulatory network implicated in AD pathogenesis. Unlike previous reviews that focused on single pathways, we have integrated three interconnected dimensions—hepatic metabolism, liver‐derived molecular signals, and neural interfaces—to provide a holistic framework. Key conclusions include: (1) the liver contributes to AD pathology through metabolic detoxification (urea cycle, ketogenesis), Aβ clearance (LRP1/IDE), and the production of signaling molecules (APOE, CRP, FGF21, IGF‐1); (2) these liver‐derived factors can modulate neuroinflammation, BBB integrity, and cerebral energy metabolism; and (3) the hypothalamic–pituitary‐target gland axis and vagus nerve constitute the neural substrate for liver‐brain communication.

Outstanding controversies

Despite growing interest in the liver‐brain axis, several controversies remain unresolved. First, the role of IGF‐1 in AD is complex, with some studies reporting decreased and others elevated levels, which may reflect disease stage, compartment differences, or IGF‐1 resistance. Second, the net effect of TGR5 activation on AD pathology appears contradictory—deficiency in excitatory neurons is protective, yet activation in microglia is anti‐inflammatory—highlighting cell‐type‐specific functions. Third, whether liver‐derived APOE4 contributes independently to AD risk beyond CNS‐derived APOE4 remains debated, and their relative contributions require further investigation.

Limitations and challenges

Several methodological limitations and translational barriers should be acknowledged. First, most mechanistic evidence comes from preclinical animal models (e.g., transgenic AD mice, CCl4‐induced liver injury), which may not fully recapitulate human AD pathology or the chronic, progressive nature of human liver dysfunction. Second, the compartmentalization of the liver‐brain axis—distinguishing peripheral from central events—poses significant experimental challenges. For instance, serum levels of liver‐derived factors do not necessarily reflect their CNS availability due to the BBB, and invasive sampling is required for CSF or brain tissue. Third, current tracing technologies (viral tracers, nanocarriers) carry inherent limitations, including potential artifacts, immunogenicity, and safety concerns, as discussed in Section 6. Fourth, most existing studies have not considered sex as a biological variable, despite known sex differences in both AD risk and hepatic metabolism.

Future directions

Based on the identified gaps and controversies, future research should prioritize the following: (1) well‐designed longitudinal human studies that track dynamic changes in liver function, peripheral biomarkers, and cognitive outcomes over time, with simultaneous measurement of serum and cerebrospinal fluid to dissect peripheral‐central relationships; (2) development of next‐generation viral tracers and nanocarriers with improved cell‐type specificity, reduced immunogenicity, and better biocompatibility to enable precise mapping and targeting of the liver‐brain axis; (3) incorporation of sex as a biological variable in both preclinical and clinical studies, including sex‐stratified analyses or adequate power to detect sex‐specific effects; and (4) exploration of combination therapeutic strategies that target multiple nodes of the liver‐brain network (e.g., ketogenic diet combined with FGF21 analogs or anti‐inflammatory agents) to achieve synergistic effects.

In summary, the liver‐brain axis represents a promising frontier for understanding AD pathogenesis and identifying novel therapeutic targets. Addressing the controversies, limitations, and future directions outlined above will be essential to translate mechanistic insights into clinical benefit.
