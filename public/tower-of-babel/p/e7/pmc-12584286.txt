Developing and validating an evidence-based health promotion program for reproductive donors reflecting their challenges in the process of donation decision-making: a multi-method study protocol
Authors: Elnaz Iranifard, Samira Ebrahimzadeh Zagami, Hossein Ebrahimipour, Malihe Amirian, Helen T Allan, Robab Latifnejad Roudsari
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe demand for reproductive donation has increased globally. Donors face various challenges, which make decision-making difficult for them and may impact their health and quality of life. It seems that there is no ongoing, holistic healthcare program for reproductive donors in the fertility clinics. To fill this knowledge gap, the current study explores Iranian reproductive donors’ challenges in the process of donation decision-making to incorporate the findings in developing and validating a needs-based and evidence-based health promotion program.MethodsThis multi-method study will be conducted in three stages. Stage one will employ a qualitative design based on grounded theory methodology to conduct interviews with participants, including embryo, oocyte, and sperm donors, as well as other key informants such as gynecologists, urologists, midwives, psychologists, and donors’ family members, across three cities in Iran. Using purposeful and theoretical sampling, data will be collected by face-to-face, in-depth semi-structured interviews and analyzed by the constant comparison method adopted by Corbin and Strauss. Stage two will focus on planning a health promotion program, incorporating qualitative data collected in stage one into the stages of program planning. The program planning will be based on the six steps of the Public Health Ontario model. In the third stage, the program will be validated by the nominal group technique.DiscussionThe theoretical framework generated at the first stage will demonstrate reproductive donors’ concerns regarding donation decision-making, the context in which they make their decisions, the strategies they adopt to respond to their issues, and their consequences. It will demonstrate conditions that facilitate or hinder donors’ decision-making. The findings of this stage will inform the development of a health promotion program, which will be evidence-based and tailored to the needs of reproductive donors and the sociocultural context in which they undergo the donation process. This program, subject to validation by experts, could be adopted by fertility centers to enhance reproductive donors’ health and quality of life. The findings of this study can be used as a basis for further relevant research.Keywords: Ovum donor, Semen donor, Donor conception, Grounded theory, Health promotion

Introduction

Reproductive donation, or third-party reproduction, is an assisted reproductive technique involving the use of a third party’s gamete and/or embryo for reproduction in another person or couple [1–4]. Worldwide, this practice faces numerous ethical, legal, religious, and socio-cultural challenges [5–8]. Reproductive donors may experience social stigma, ethical/religious dilemmas, psychological challenges, legal or financial disputes with recipients, and concerns regarding disclosure and its impact on their own family [6, 7, 9–16]. Oocyte donors may also experience physical side effects [10, 11]. These challenges complicate the decision to donate. Since genetic materials are shared with non-family members, donation can trigger family disputes [5]. If the recipient is a friend or relative, social pressure may influence the donor’s decision and alter interpersonal dynamics [5, 16, 17].

Due to its link with sexuality and the stigma surrounding sexual and reproductive health in some cultures, third-party reproduction has long been, and in some contexts remains, a social taboo. The decision to donate gametes can raise ethical dilemmas and create tension between the donor’s self-image and social image [5, 9], often leading them to keep the donation secret [18]. In commercial donation, donors worldwide report an instrumental view from recruitment agencies, health providers, and/or recipients, experiencing cold, distant, or inappropriate behaviours and a lack of attention to their needs or privacy [9, 15, 19, 20]. Furthermore, where donors feel attachment to donated gametes, which result in surplus embryos, factors such as the number and quality of surplus embryos and the recipients’ intention to use them in the future may affect embryo donors’ decisions [21–24].

In addition to the challenges mentioned above [5, 9, 14–16, 25], cultural, legal, and traditional contexts create specific concerns for potential donors [26, 27]. For instance, anonymous and commercial donations are banned in England [28], and in the U.S., federal regulation of gamete and embryo donation leads to national inconsistency [29]. Iran is the only Muslim-majority country in the Middle East, where reproductive donation is practiced nationwide [30–35]. Except for Israel and parts of Lebanon, third-party reproduction is either unavailable or legally banned in most countries of the region, such as Turkey [18, 35–37]. This makes Iran a destination for cross-border reproductive donation care [38].

The first live birth after oocyte donation and the first embryo donation in Iran were reported in 1994 and 1998, respectively [39, 40]. While up-to-date statistics are lacking, existing studies suggest that oocyte donation, especially commercial oocyte donation, appears to be the most prevalent approach of third-party reproduction in Iran [41–43]. It is noteworthy that, despite the prohibition of commercial embryo donation, dual gamete donation by a couple is observed in practice, likely due to the absence of specific legal regulations governing it [44]. There is no data on sperm donation. Gamete donation is permitted based on rulings by Marja al-taqlids (the highest ranking clerics or jurists within Twelver Shia in Islam), including Iran’s supreme leader, while embryo donation is regulated under the 2003 executive regulations of the embryo donation law passed by the Islamic parliament of Iran [30, 31, 33, 45].

In Iran, cultural emphasis on lineage and kinship [46], alongside legal and religious complexities, such as inheritance, parental rights, and the absence of clear, comprehensive regulation, makes reproductive donation decisions particularly challenging [33, 43, 46]. For example, many prominent Marja al-taqlids consider the gamete or embryo donor as the legal parent of the donor-conceived child (DCC), which may entail future legal and religious responsibilities for donors [31, 45, 47, 48]. Also, from a socio-cultural standpoint, reproductive donation is not well-accepted by the Iranian society [9, 49, 50], even by infertile patients [51–53]. While some may accept it as the last treatment, they often do not support the idea of friends or family members becoming donors [54].

Although reproductive donation has been practiced in Iran for decades [39, 40], there is a dearth of knowledge about reproductive donation through the lens of Iranian reproductive donors; with existing studies mostly focus on commercial egg donors [9, 41, 55].

As noted, the decision to donate is multifactorial and multidimensional [56]. Decision conflicts arise when the decision contradicts ethical, social, or personal values or when the associated costs (financial, mental, physical, and spiritual) outweigh the benefits [57–60]. This may lead to negative feelings post-decision, such as regret, self-blame, despair, and hopelessness, ultimately lowering the decision-maker’s quality of life [58, 61, 62]. Reports of decisional regrets or dissatisfaction, especially in commercial donors, are common [63–65].

Several studies emphasize improving care for reproductive donors, particularly in providing better information, psychosocial support, and counselling for both short- and long-term needs [38, 65–67]. Despite guidelines from organizations like the American Society for Reproductive Medicine, the European Society of Human Reproduction and Embryology, and the Human Fertilisation and Embryology Authority, psychosocial screening of donors remains the main goal of counselling provided to donors [68, 69]. Also, studies indicate that donors lack sufficient information [19, 21, 69–71], and that their long-term psycho-social needs, such as decision conflict and regret, and disclosure, are overlooked [65, 66, 69, 71, 72]. Nevertheless, health promotion programs have shown positive effects in improving decision-making, health, and quality of life of their intended decision makers through counselling, educational, and supportive care [73–78].

As mentioned before, reproductive donors face numerous challenges that can negatively affect their health and quality of life [5, 15, 63, 79, 80]. Iranian gamete donors also struggle with legal, cultural, and religious issues unique to their social context [9, 33, 46, 50, 81, 82]. A qualitative study by Adib Moghadam et al. (2021) found that Iranian donors perceived the decision-making process challenging, and some experienced regret afterward [83]. The authors subsequently developed a psychological intervention program using the Talbot and Verrinder Model of health promotion program planning [38], which successfully reduced depression and anxiety in egg donors, improving overall satisfaction in the subsequent clinical trials [83, 84]. Although this study provides invaluable evidence about the experiences of egg donors, it does not address the decision-making challenges faced by donors. Also, they only focused on commercial egg donors, leaving out sperm and embryo or altruistic donors. Additionally, it solely concentrates on psychological issues.

To the best of our knowledge, no studies have explored the decision-making process among Iranian reproductive donors. This study aims to understand this process in the context of a Middle Eastern, Muslim-majority country. With no existing guidelines or care programs for reproductive donors in Iran, it is crucial to develop a health promotion program tailored to their needs, considering the issues, barriers, and challenges they face. The first stage of this study will explore the Iranian reproductive donors’ decision-making process, while the second and third stages will focus on developing and validating a needs-based health promotion program, which will follow the results of the first stage of the study in combination with a review of current literature on reproductive donors’ needs and care provided to them.

Methods

Study design

This multi-method research consists of three stages; stage one, will be a qualitative study based on Corbin and Straussian Grounded theory (GT) (2015); stage two, will focus on planning a health promotion program using qualitative data from stage one adopting Public Health Ontario (PHO) Program Planning model; and stage three will address validating the developed program through collecting experts’ opinion via nominal group technique (NGT) (Table 1). Considering that preparing a well-structured research protocol is a critical component of any research study and is a demanding task that requires rigor and hard effort in all disciplines, for writing this study protocol, the 10-key-step guide to writing a protocol for a qualitative research study developed by Kaba et al. was used [85].

Stage one: qualitative study (grounded theory)

The underpinning paradigm for this qualitative research is constructivism. Constructivism ontologically emphasizes how an individual actively constructs their notions of reality through experiencing and reflecting on those experiences, resulting in the existence of multiple realities [86]. The methodology of research is based on the Corbin and Strauss (2015) approach to GT, incorporating their ontological, epistemological, and methodological assumptions. Corbin and Strauss believe that one’s responses to phenomena arise from the individual, social, psychological, political, economic, and cultural context and conditions [87]. As discussed above, potential donors may also face personal, economic, social, cultural, legal, and religious challenges in the decision-making process for reproductive donation [5–10, 22, 31, 65]. According to Strauss and Corbin, one’s emotions and feelings cannot be separated from their actions, which affect their responses to the events [87]. Numerous studies have pointed out the mutual influence of emotions and decision-making for reproductive donation, including the sense of attachment to the DCC or even one’s gamete or embryo, the moral responsibility towards the DCC, social stigma, emotional pressure from family and friends, the feeling of regret, and being caught up in a moral-social dilemma [5, 9, 65, 72, 88].

It can be concluded that decision-making is a social process that affects and is affected by a person’s self-awareness, relationships with family and friends, social interactions, and socio-cultural norms of their society [89, 90]. Reproductive donation also alters the social interactions of those involved in it, and even creates new social roles as donor, recipient, and even the DCC; at the same time, the donation process can be affected by each party’s social interactions [5, 17, 91].

Settings and participants

The setting will be in three different cities in Iran, which include one fertility clinic, one urology clinic, and one gynecology clinic, all of which offer gamete and/or embryo donation services to infertile couples. The three selected clinics were chosen based on the diversity they had in reproductive donation practices. The fertility clinic located in the northeastern city in Iran (1st site) offered both oocyte and embryo donation (surplus embryos or dual gamete donation), either altruistic or commercial, and permitted donation by married women. The urology clinic (2nd site) located in a city in the center of Iran was limited only to commercial sperm donation treatment through Intrauterine Insemination (IUI). Lastly, the gynecology clinic (3rd site) located in another city in central Iran only recruited commercial oocyte donors who were either divorced or widowed. This diversity allows us to include a broader range of reproductive donors’ experiences in the study. Participants will be Iranian reproductive donors who have donated in the past 24 months or are currently in the process of donation, and who are willing to participate in the research. In addition to the health professionals involved in reproductive donation who would be asked to participate, gynecologists, urologists, midwives, and psychologists with at least 12 months of work experience in the field of reproductive donation would be involved. Donation intermediaries and reproductive donors’ family members will also be invited to participate in the study as key informants. It is important to note that in Iran, midwives are among the main healthcare providers in the field of infertility. Also, most commercial reproductive donations happen through intermediaries. Sampling will be initiated as purposeful sampling using the strategy of maximum variation. To ensure maximum variety, participants from different socio-economic backgrounds, educational levels, marital status, number of children, number of donations, type of donation (known, unknown, commercial, altruistic), and motivation for donation will be chosen. As the study moves forward, based on the collected and analyzed data, theoretical sampling will be used to gather more precise and detailed data to fill in the gaps in the developing theoretical framework [87, 92]. In GT, the sample size is determined by theoretical saturation—that is, the adequacy of the sample to provide sufficient conceptual richness and depth to support the development of an emerging theory [87, 93, 94]. Therefore, the number of participants cannot be exactly estimated beforehand [94].

Data collection

The researcher (EI), who is a doctoral student in the field of Reproductive Health with previous experience in the field of infertility research and has been trained in qualitative methods of data collection, will recruit those who meet the inclusion criteria from the research settings. EI will provide a research information sheet to the participants and will give them sufficient time to study it, and will answer any questions that participants may have. Then, those participants who are willing to take part in the study will be given informed consent to complete and sign. Afterwards, the place and time of the interview convenient to the participants will be arranged.

Multiple sources of data can be used to conduct a GT, including but not limited to interviews, observations, documents, and videos [87]. In this study, data will be collected through interviews and observations, although the main data collection source will be semi-structured interviews with reproductive donors and other key informants. To collect data, in all three study settings, the researcher (EI), subject to receiving ethical approval, will contact the directors of the fertility clinic/office to get permission to interview at a time convenient to the participant. The interviews will take place in study settings or the place of choice of participants. All the interviews will be conducted by the researcher (EI), who is a female researcher with reasonable work experience in reproductive health. However, if due to cultural restrictions, sperm donors were uncomfortable being interviewed by a female researcher, a trained male interviewer will conduct the interviews instead. Interviewees will be aware that their voices are being recorded, they will be reminded of their right to withdraw from the study, and the confidentiality of the recorded data. The researcher will start the interview with some general open-ended questions. The flow of the interview will be determined based on the participant’s answers to those initial questions. The main interview questions will then be asked (Table 2).

To design the interview guide, we will consider key variables relevant to the context of reproductive donation in Iran. Specifically, we accounted for the type of donation (i.e., oocyte, sperm, surplus embryos, or dual gamete donation), the anonymity of the donation (known vs. unknown), and donor demographic characteristics such as marital status and infertility history. These factors will be used to tailor the interview questions accordingly to ensure contextual appropriateness and depth of data collection. Interviewees’ answers, if necessary, will be probed. Also, the researcher will use an active listening method. Interviewees’ non-verbal communication and body language will be recorded as observational notes. It is assumed that interviews will take between 30 and 120 min, and the process of data collection will last for 12 months. After each interview, data will be transcribed verbatim and analyzed, and the next interview topics will be based on the analysis of the previous interviews. Also, participants’ interactions with healthcare providers, recipients, donation intermediaries, or their family members (in cases where donation is made to a family member or when the participant is accompanied by a family member) will be closely observed in the research setting by the researcher, and field notes will be written. The method of observation will be non-participant observation [95–97], and the researcher (observer) will follow effective approaches previously used by other researchers to adhere to the ethical principles of research, including respecting participants’ boundaries and privacy, informing participants about the researcher’s presence, keeping participants anonymous, avoiding interfering with the acts, respecting participants’ autonomy, and obtaining consent [95–97].

Data analysis

Data analysis will begin after the first interview. For this reason, interviews will be transcribed verbatim, and then the interview texts will be read several times to gain a full insight into the data. Data will be analysed using the constant comparative method, which is a process developed by Glaser and Strauss and is used in grounded theory [87, 98]. In a constant comparative method, raw data will be broken into small and manageable pieces, and these pieces will be compared with one another.

To analyze data in this grounded theory study, Corbin and Strauss’s (2015) method of data analysis, including the following five steps, will be used [87].

Step one- Open Coding: Identifying Concepts: Microanalysis is a form of open coding, and most likely to be used in the early exploratory stages of analysis when an analyst is trying to get some sense of the meaning of data and to find concepts that reflect that meaning. At this step, the first data will be broken down into the meanings (Codes). Then, codes that are similar in nature (conceptually) are grouped under the same conceptual heading. Through further analysis, concepts are grouped by the researcher to form categories.

Step two- Developing Concepts in Terms of Their Properties and Dimensions: Here, each category is developed in terms of its properties and dimensions. Properties are characteristics that define concepts and differentiate one concept from another, and dimensions are variations within properties. Then, conceptual ordering, which is the organization of data into discrete categories based on their properties and dimensions, takes place. Lastly, the main issue, event, or problem area under investigation as perceived by participants will have emerged.

Step three- Analyzing Data for Context: At this step, the context in which the actions-interactions take place is identified. Context is a combination of micro and macro conditions interacting with each other. Micro conditions are those related to the individual, and macro conditions are those distinct from or outside the individual—that is, historical, social, political, etc., conditions. In order to identify context, researchers must often look at the explanations or reasons that participants give for what they say, think, feel, and do in response to the problematic situations or events they face. When analyzing data for context, researchers will ask questions and make comparisons and write memos, and create diagrams.

Step four- Bringing Process into Analysis: At this step, data analysis will be based on the identification of the actions-interactions that take place, the conditions affecting them, and the consequences of those actions/interactions. To do so, researchers can use analytic tools such as the paradigm model and the conditional/consequential matrix. The paradigm model consists of the main features of the phenomenon, conditions, actions/interactions, and consequences. At the end of this step, the process through which participants respond to the main issue using different actions/interactions, and the relationship of conditions and consequences to the process are identified.

Step five- Integrating Categories: At this step, the core category will have emerged. Core category is a concept that is sufficiently broad and abstract that summarize the main ideas expressed by the participants in a few words. The other categories are linked around the core category to form the theory. In this step, a theoretical scheme will emerge [87].

The MAXQDA software will be used to manage data. The analysis of data will be conducted by the EI under the supervision of RLR and SEZ, who are experienced researchers in qualitative research, including grounded theory methodology.

Trustworthiness

To ensure the credibility of the research, as Corbin and Strauss (2015) suggests, methodological consistency, clarity of purpose, self-awareness, writing a reflective journal, memo writing, training in how to conduct qualitative research, sensitivity to participants and data, willingness to work hard, ability to connect with the creative self, and methodological awareness will be considered [87]. Also, four general criteria of Lincoln and Guba (1985) in their approach to trustworthiness, including credibility, dependability, confirmability, and transferability, will be taken into account [99].

At the end of stage one of this study, a substantive theory about the donation decision-making of reproductive donors will be produced. Substantive theories are situation-specific, which means that data collection is confined to only one main group [87], here the reproductive donors.

Rationale for using grounded theory data in developing health promotion programs

As mentioned before, there are no current comprehensive and sustained guidelines or healthcare programs in place to provide holistic and inclusive care for all types of reproductive donors in Iran, so it is important to provide them with a health promotion program based on their needs. In the first stage of this study, the process of donation decision-making in reproductive donors, the challenges they face, their experiences, and their needs will be explored. Data from the first stage will then be used to plan a health promotion program for reproductive donors.

Qualitative research provides a suitable theoretical foundation for identifying participants’ needs and using them to design and implant programs and interventions [94]. It is notable that a deep understanding of the obstacles to achieving health, coupled with the information needed for effective planning, is an essential component of health promotion studies [100, 101]. Using the GT approach in the design of the health promotion program can also be justified by considering the concept of health and health promotion, as both health and health promotion have a social structure, and health behaviour is related to people’s social lives [102]. Health is a combination of physical, psychological, social, and cultural factors that affect the meaning and concept of health for individuals and societies [102, 103]. As mentioned earlier, GT is the study of social-psychological processes and deals with people’s social interactions [87, 104]. Several studies have pointed out the importance and role of GT in designing, modifying, or evaluating health promotion programs. Also, various health promotion programs have been designed based on the findings of GT research [74, 105–112]. In order to plan and further implement a health promotion program, sufficient data is required to understand the target population’s health-related needs, challenges, and priorities, and also gain a deep understanding of the context, its strengths and weaknesses, and the opportunities and threats it creates [113, 114]. Therefore, in line with the third objective of the study, a health promotion program will be designed based on the PHO model using the findings of the first stage (GT study) as well as an extensive literature review.

Stage two: planning a health promotion program

The comprehensive, step-by-step, and user-friendly framework of the Public Health Ontario (PHO) model offers an all-inclusive framework for designing health promotion programs that can address the unique and complex needs of reproductive donors. The PHO was created based on the Ottawa Health Promotion Charter and integrated with the theory of social determinants of health [113, 115], which are non-medical factors such as living conditions, social norms, social support, and the economic conditions of a person, which affect individuals’ health outcomes [103]. The fact that reproductive donation has little social acceptance and donors may not receive enough social support during the donation process [9, 53, 116, 117] makes the role of social determinants of health important in the design of the health promotion program for them. Insufficiency in social support, health services, and financial resources, and being under social pressures and stigma, not only can lead to disruption in decision-making [5–10, 22, 31, 65], but also, as social determinants of health, can lead to health and quality of life problems [118], which can ultimately lead to regretting the decision, self-blame and even diminished quality of life [58, 61, 62]. Health promotion programs based on individual needs, including decision-making needs, can help individuals to identify their skills and abilities and improve their self-efficacy by providing them with adequate information, social support, and optimal health services [119]. Health promotion programs are proven effective in providing appropriate approaches to help with the decision-making process [73–75].

Lastly, the research team will identify the program stakeholders based on suggestions from experts and also data from stage one (GT). The stakeholders will include reproductive donors, fertility clinics’ staff and management, recipient couples, reproductive donors’ family members, and donation intermediaries. This list may expand to include other identified stakeholders. In this step, also, a power-interest grid will be developed (Fig. 2). The power-interest grid will determine each identified stakeholder’s role in program planning based on their authority and interest in planning and implementing the reproductive donors’ health promotion program.

Power-Interest grid

Step 2: Conducting a situational assessment: In this step, reproductive donors’ current situation, their needs and expectations, factors affecting the donation process, and activities that can improve donors’ health and quality of life will be assessed. For this purpose, four sources of information will be used, including: (1) Qualitative data from stage one i.e. GT study, (2) Data gathered through interview with experts and stakeholders, (3) Data obtained from an extensive literature review of reproductive donors’ needs globally through conducting a mixed methods systematic review and (4) Data derived from reviewing guidelines, code of practices and any official document relating to providing care for reproductive donors. A SWOT (Strengths, Weaknesses, Opportunities, and Threats) analysis will be performed to determine the strengths and weaknesses of fertility centers regarding donors’ needs, and to identify the possible opportunities or threats existing regarding program planning in our fertility settings.

Step 3: Identifying goals, audiences, and objectives: In this step, the research team will identify the main goal/goals of the program, the audiences, and objectives based on the reproductive donors’ needs that were determined using data collected in step 2.

Step 4: Identifying strategies, activities, and resources: Based on the results from step two and in line with the main goals (step 3), the research team will choose strategies and activities to meet each identified need of reproductive donors. To do so, first, strategies are identified by SWOT analysis, and then the research team will use the brainstorming technique to come up with activities to achieve the strategies’ objectives. Then the resource needed for each activity will be identified. The resources will be the input of the program and include financial (e.g. budget from ministry of health, medical universities and fertility clinics or fundings from NGOs), and human resources (e.g. fertility clinic staff, students of medical sciences), the knowledge/skill to perform each activity (will be determined based on the activity), as well as facilities, equipment and materials (required for implementing activities).

Step 5: Developing indicators: At this stage, the research team will assign valid, reliable, and accessible indicators to assess expected output, short-term, mid-term, and long-term outcomes, as well as impacts of each activity.

Step 6: Reviewing the plan: At this stage, the research team will develop a logic model for the planned program, and then, in a meeting, they will review the program. Then they will try to find a solution for the program’s deficiencies and shortcomings. Finally, the program will be revised [113]. It is important to note that the program will also be reviewed by an Expert group in the third stage of the study.

Stage three: validating the health promotion program

Following program development, it will be validated by collecting experts’ opinions via the nominal group technique. This technique is a structured form of small group discussion. In the NGT, information is collected through the answers of the group members to the questions raised by the meeting supervisor, and then the group members will prioritize the ideas and recommendations. NGT seeks organizational judgmental decision-making to solve problems, provide solutions, or recommendations [120, 121]. NGT consists of five steps (Fig. 3).

Five steps of NGT

In this study, an expert panel consisting of the directors of fertility centers, gynecologists, midwives, health policy-makers, and experts in health promotion program planning will be assembled. It is important to note that the expert panel and its invitees will meet in person at study setting 1 (the primary and main setting) due to feasibility, while a separate virtual session will be held to include experts from the other two study sites located in different cities. The groups will receive the planned program before the NGT meetings. The in-person meeting will take place in the conference room of the fertility center at a convenient time for the experts. The first step of NGT is introduction/preparation, in which the program is introduced to the experts by the research team, and they will ask them for the strengths and weaknesses of the program and their ideas to improve it. Then, in the second step, the experts will generate ideas about the program in silence. After that, in the third and fourth steps, ideas will be shared and discussed by the group. Lastly, in the fifth step, the expert group will vote and rank the discussed recommendations and ideas [120, 121]. Finally, the research team will revise the program based on the top 10 ranked ideas and recommendations of the expert panel.

Discussion

This study will be conducted to explore the process of donation decision-making in Iranian reproductive donors and subsequently develop and validate a needs-based and evidence-based health promotion program. Few studies explore the decision-making process in reproductive donors, and among these, most have primarily focused on surplus embryo donors [13, 22, 23]. Takahashi et al. (2012) conducted a GT to explore the decision-making process for the fate of frozen embryos by Japanese infertile women. They reported a five-step model for decision-making as follows (1) sustaining the embryo-transfer moratorium, (2) considering the “Mottainai”-embryo or having another child, (Mottainai is a Japanese philosophy that embraces the idea of reducing waste and respecting resources and their values); (3) taking cost reasonability into account; (4) confirming partners opinion and (5) determining the effect of donation by the couple. This model is deeply rooted in Japanese culture [13]. Also, Nachtigall et al. (2005) in an ethnography reported four temporally sequential stages for disposition decision in American couples with surplus embryos, which included reassurance, avoidance, confrontation, and finally resolution [22]. Although these studies provide a great insight into the decision-making process by embryo donors, their results do not fit other societies with different values, cultures, and belief systems. Also, these studies only explored the decision-making process in infertile couples with surplus embryos, which cannot be generalized to gamete donors or commercial donors. This study will explore the decision-making process of reproductive donors in the Iranian context.

According to the findings of a cross-sectional study by Balochi et al. (2023), the constructs of the theory of planned behavior were able to predict and explain the variances of the intention to donate eggs in less than half of the participants [122]. Although the theory of planned behaviour was able to justify and/or predict the intention to donation of less than 50% of the women under the study, it is important to consider that those women were not making an active decision to donate gametes, so the result of the study cannot be generalized to egg donors. Therefore, it is necessary to investigate and explain the decision-making process in Iranian gamete and embryo donors without placing it in the framework of pre-existing theories.

The current study will contribute to the existing body of scientific knowledge about reproductive donors. The qualitative part will provide a distinctive insight into Iranian reproductive donors’ experiences and needs through their decision-making journey in the socio-cultural context of Iran. To the best of our knowledge, there is no study so far to explore the experiences of Iranian sperm or embryo donors. Also, there is limited research about Iranian oocyte donors [9, 123]. Whereas the current research will explore the experiences of sperm, oocyte, and embryo donors. This study will also contribute to our current body of knowledge about the donation decision-making process and its challenges. Donation decision-making is an initial as well as crucial step of the journey of reproductive donation for potential donors. Increased knowledge in this area will help to develop decision-aid tools, improving the decision-making process for future donors, and helping current donors to cope with short and long-term consequences of their decision in a positive manner, so that the negative effects of decision conflict or regret are minimized.

The theoretical framework that will be generated at the end of the grounded theory study will demonstrate reproductive donors’ main issue regarding donation decision-making, the context in which they face this issue, and the strategies they use to manage it and its consequences. The framework will also demonstrate conditions that influence the reproductive donors’ situation. The knowledge that will result from this study can be used in practice to improve the care and management of reproductive donors based on their needs. Also, the findings of this study can be used as a basis for further research on reproductive donors.

As aforementioned, although the challenges faced by reproductive donors can negatively affect donors’ health and quality of life, there is a limited number of studies on program planning to improve donors’ health or quality of life [38, 124, 125]. Almost all of these limited studies focus on one group of donors, and they mainly focus on just one aspect of the donors’ needs. For instance, Adib Moghaddam et al. (2020), in their study protocol of a mixed-method study, planned to develop a mental health intervention program for oocyte donors [38]. In this study, three phases were described. In the first phase of the study, the research team planned to identify the needs and strategies of the mental health promotion program for the Iranian egg donors through a content analysis. In the second phase, they decided to design the mental health intervention program based on the Talbot and Verrinder Model and validate it in a panel of experts, using the classic Delphi technique. In the third phase, they planned to implement the program as a quasi-experimental study [38]. To the best of our knowledge, their study is the only protocol from Iran that focuses on oocyte donors, and it is also the only study protocol that specifically addresses program planning for this population. Additionally, the mentioned study has some limitations that the current study wishes to overcome. It only focuses on the egg donors who were recruited from one infertility treatment center, which makes it hard to generalize the findings. Whereas in our study, both sperm and oocyte as well as embryo donors will be recruited from different fertility centers in three large cities in North East and central Iran. Also, in study planned by Adib Moghaddam et al. (2020), the focus of the planned program was only on the mental health aspect of egg donors, whereas we are going to plan a holistic health promotion program, as reproductive donors face challenges in different aspects of their lives as donors, including physical, mental, social, educational, or even ethical dimensions [5, 15, 63, 79, 80].

Strengths and limitations

By program planning in stage two of the current study, a useful health promotion program will be developed for reproductive donors, which is not only evidence-based but also based on reproductive donors’ needs and the socio-cultural context in which they go through the donation process. This program can be adopted by the fertility centers in Iran and other countries in the region and across the world with a similar sociocultural context to improve the health and quality of life of reproductive donors.

As with any research, there can be potential limitations to this study. Firstly, reproductive donation is often kept secret within the Iranian community, which may lead to difficulties in recruitment, particularly for sperm donors. To address this, we will collaborate closely with fertility centers to facilitate trust-building and participant referrals. Secondly, due to the sensitivity of the research topic, participants may feel hesitant to openly share their experiences. To mitigate this, interviews will be conducted by a trained researcher, using techniques to create a safe, confidential, and supportive environment. Also, for sperm donors who are not at ease with a female interviewer, due to cultural sensitivities, a trained male researcher will take over the interview process. Lastly, reproductive donation, especially sperm donation, is not widely accepted even among fertility treatment providers, which may pose challenges in recruiting participants and key informants. To overcome this, we will engage early with supportive fertility centers, build rapport with clinic staff, and provide clear information about the confidentiality measures and potential benefits to encourage collaboration.
