Validation of the French version of the patient-reported outcome measure in pediatric chronic kidney disease (PRO-Kid)
Authors: Karma Abukasm, Banke Oketola, Kimberley Widger, Giuseppe Pascale, Marie-Michele Gaudreault-Tremblay, Jean-Philippe Roy, Allison Dart, Mina Matsuda-Abedini
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesChildren living with chronic kidney disease (CKD) experience a high symptom burden. The PRO-Kid tool is a validated 14-item questionnaire for the assessment of symptom burden in youth aged 8 to 18 years living with CKD. The objective of this study was to evaluate the understandability of the French version of PRO-Kid.MethodsParticipants were a cohort of French-speaking Canadian children aged 8 to 18 years living with CKD stage 3 to 5, including dialysis. Participants were recruited from the Montreal Children's Hospital and the Centre hopitalier universitaire Sainte-Justine (Montreal, Canada). The English version of the PRO-Kid was translated into French and back-translated to English by an accredited translator as well as two French-speaking investigators. Participants were asked to complete the French version of the questionnaire. Cognitive interviews were conducted to evaluate the understandability of the tool and relevance of each questionnaire item, and analyzed using NVivo.ResultsTen children completed the study, with a median age of 13 years (IQR, 11.7, 15.6). All children reported that the questionnaire was easy to complete. The participants’ feedback revealed that no changes were warranted to the items, although some qualifiers were added to the item Itchiness (Démangeaisons) to improve clarity. Participants found the wording of the scale appropriate and easy to understand.ConclusionThis study supports content and face validity of the French version of PRO-Kid (8 to 18 years old). This tool can now be implemented into CKD clinics for French-speaking children. It has the potential to improve communication between children with CKD and their health care providers.

Introduction

Children living with chronic kidney disease (CKD) experience a high symptom burden (1). Symptom burden related to CKD includes a wide range of physical (e.g., nausea, fatigue and itchiness), emotional (e.g., sadness and anxiety) and cognitive (e.g., difficulty concentrating) symptoms, which evolve as the disease progresses (2–5). These symptoms often impede children's life participation and decrease health-related quality-of-life (5).

Multicentre studies have shown that children living with CKD prioritize outcomes that have a direct effect on their sense of normality and lifestyle (6,7), and despite seeking more involvement in the decision-making related to their care (6,8), they are limited by vulnerability and fear (8). Patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) have proven to be efficient tools to capture patient experience, improve communication between patients and providers, and focus clinical care on patient priorities (9). Until recently, the absence of disease-specific PROMs for children living with CKD was a major limitation to using such tools in a clinical setting. Generic PROMs (e.g., PedsQLTM (10)) or PROMs developed for the adult nephrology population (e.g., Edmonton symptom assessment system revised renal [ESAS-r:Renal] (11)) fail to capture the unique lived experience of children with CKD.

Our team therefore co-developed the patient-reported outcome measure in pediatric chronic kidney disease (PRO-Kid) questionnaire, along with children aged 8 to 18 years living with CKD and their caregivers, to address this patient-expressed need (12). This 14-item bedside questionnaire captures physical (10 items), emotional (3 items) and cognitive (1 item) symptoms endorsed by children aged 8 to 18 years living with CKD. This questionnaire was validated among English-speaking Canadian children living with CKD (13). It showed good internal consistency and strong correlation with quality-of-life metrics, as assessed with the PedsQLTM (10).

Canada being a bilingual country, the next step was to translate the validated PRO-Kid into French to increase the reach of implementation to a broader group of Canadian children living with CKD. Before implementation, this study aimed to evaluate the understandability and cultural relevance of the French version of PRO-Kid among French-speaking children living with CKD in Canada.

Methods

The study was designed around the principles of good practice established by the translation and cultural adaptation (TCA) group of the Professional Society for Health Economics and Outcomes Research Task Force (ISPOR) (14).

Translation process of PRO-Kid from English to French

The translation process was grounded in the principles of the ISPOR-TCA process and followed the steps outlined in their good practice recommendation. The approach to the PRO-Kid questionnaire translation included translation into French, reconciliation, back translation into English, and review of English back-translated versions. The initial translation into French was performed independently by an accredited translator and two French-speaking investigators (M.M.G.T. and J.P.R.). The accredited translator was from a professional translation firm and certified by the Association of Translators and Interpreters of Ontario and the Ordre des traducteurs terminologues et interprètes agréés du Québec. The back translation was done by the same team to confirm accurate translation of words and sentences, conveying the same meaning in both languages. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion. Once the research team was satisfied with the translated questionnaire, cognitive interviews were conducted with patients using the French version of the questionnaire.

Participants selection

Participants, comprising child-parent dyads, were recruited at the Montreal Children's Hospital and the Centre hopitalier universitaire Sainte-Justine, two tertiary care pediatric hospitals in Montreal, Canada, between October 2023 and March 2025. French-speaking children aged 8 to 18 years, diagnosed with stages 3 to 5 CKD (defined as estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 as determined by the bedside Schwartz equation (15)), including those on dialysis (peritoneal dialysis or hemodialysis), were eligible. Children who were unable to complete the questionnaire without assistance, including those with intellectual disability, and children living with a kidney transplant, were excluded. While patients who did not speak or understand French or English were excluded, English-speaking patients and families who identified English as their primary language were not excluded if they were bilingual and had sufficient understanding of French to be able to participate.

Consent, and assent when applicable, was obtained before enrollment. This study was approved by the Research Ethics Boards of the Montreal Children's Hospital and the Centre hospitalier universitaire Sainte-Justine.

Sampling strategy

A convenience sample was enrolled during routine clinic visits or dialysis treatment. Due to the small number of potentially eligible participants, all potential participants meeting the eligibility criteria were approached. The team aimed for at least 10 participants, but enrollment was driven by the findings of the cognitive interviews; it was deemed complete when data saturation was reached (i.e., no new information was generated by the interviews).

Cognitive interviews

Cognitive interviews were conducted to assess the content, face validity, and cultural relevance of the French PRO-Kid questionnaire. Cognitive interviews are an established approach to developing and evaluating PROMs, allowing for detailed assessment of participants’ understanding of items and specific words (16). A single trained French interviewer performed the interviews with each child-caregiver dyad. Participants were given the option of in-person or virtual interviews, using an encrypted Zoom account. Interviews were recorded, and field notes were generated by a research assistant.

At each interview, the interviewer started by explaining the objectives of the interview. The participants were then given as much time as required to complete the French PRO-Kid questionnaire. After completing the questionnaire, the participants rated their ease of completion of the questionnaire (Easy, Medium, Hard), and their understanding of the items was assessed using cognitive probing. The interview questions encompassed assessment of face and content validity, and participants were encouraged to suggest alternative wording for unclear items, if applicable. Finally, participants were asked to comment on the wording used in the scale. At the end of the interview session, participants completed the French version of the PedsQLTM questionnaire.

The interviewer rated the participants’ understanding of each item of the questionnaire based on their answers to the cognitive prompts and gave a general rating (Able to distinguish between choices; Able to understand some differences, but there is some confusion; Unable to distinguish between choices) of the participants’ understanding of the questionnaire.

Demographic and clinical data collection

Demographic information was collected via self or caregiver-report, including sex, age, ethnicity, primary diagnosis, grade level, distance from home to treatment centre, and the highest level of education of the caregivers. Clinical information was collected from the participants’ medical charts and included need for G-tube, intermittent bladder catheterizations, eGFR and CKD stage based on eGFR (15), and dialysis modality. Data were entered into a password-protected research electronic data capture (REDCap) database (17) housed at the University of Manitoba. The completed PRO-Kid and PedsQLTM questionnaires collected during the cognitive interviews were also recorded in the same REDCap database.

PRO-Kid and PedsQLTM

Details regarding the development of the PRO-Kid questionnaire have been described in previous work (12,13). In brief, the PRO-Kid captures physical (10 items), emotional (3 items) and cognitive (1 item) symptoms endorsed by children aged 8 to 18 years living with CKD, including dialysis. This questionnaire asks children to rate each symptom in the dimensions of frequency and impact on a 7-day recall period, using a 5-point Likert scale (not at all = 0, a little = 1, medium = 2, a lot = 3 and always = 4). The questionnaire responses are scored as follows: 0 = 0, 1 = 25, 2 = 50, 3 = 75 and 4 = 100. The questionnaire is scored on each dimension of frequency and impact separately, creating composite scores by adding up the scores for each question and dividing by the number of answered questions for each dimension. Higher scores indicate higher symptom burden.

The 23-item PedsQLTM 4.0 Core module includes physical functioning (eight items), emotional functioning (five items), social functioning (five items) and school functioning (five items) (10). Scores were obtained using a linear transformation of scores to a 0 to 100 scale. Higher scores indicate better health-related quality-of-life.

Data analysis

Statistical analysis

Demographic and clinical data were summarized using medians and interquartile ranges for continuous variables, and frequencies and percentages for categorical variables.

Cognitive interviews

Thematic analysis (18) was conducted based on the codes created from the field notes, using predominantly deductive coding (19). The coding framework was developed based on previous work from this group (12), which conducted cognitive interviews for a similar purpose (content assessment and face validity), along with PRO-Kid specific symptoms and structure (e.g., frequency and impact scales). The overarching concepts were guided by the TCA-ISPOR guideline to ensure a systematic assessment of all relevant aspects for the TCA of PROM tools (14). Coding and analysis were performed by a French-speaking member of the research team, who is a pediatric nephrologist (K.A.). Audio recordings of the interviews were available for consultation if needed during the analysis process, but no transcripts were produced a priori. NVivo 14 was used to analyze the data.

Items were identified as requiring rewording or simplification if at least 30% of participants found them “hard” to understand or if at least 30% had interviewer ratings of “incorrect” for their understandability. An interim analysis was performed after the first five interviews, and future reassessments were done using participants’ responses cumulatively.

To ensure credibility, peer debriefing was performed by four research team members (K.A., A.D., M.M.A. and B.O.) to discuss the data interpretation and ensure any modifications were reflective of and grounded in participants’ feedback. Consistency was ensured through thorough documentation of the analytical process using tools embedded in NVivo 14.

Construct validity

This was an exploratory analysis due to the small sample size. Convergence validity was used to assess construct validity, as done previously for the validation of the English version of PRO-Kid (13). Convergence validity was determined using Spearman correlation between the PRO-Kid scores (frequency and impact scores individually) and the PedsQLTM scores. Correlation was assessed as follows: R > 0.7 considered strong, 0.5 to 0.7 moderate, and <0.5 weak. Moderate or strong correlations were considered as indicators of an acceptable convergence validity. We expected to observe a moderate to strong inverse correlation between the PRO-Kid and PedsQLTM scores (i.e., higher symptom burden would correlate with lower health-related quality-of-life, and vice versa), similar to the data from the English questionnaire validation results (13). A P value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were conducted using R version 4.4.1.

Results

Participant characteristics

Demographic data are presented in Table 1. The recruitment process is illustrated in Figure 1. A total of 23 children were approached. Twelve children and their caregivers refused to participate: four families were not interested in this specific research, one caregiver self-reported insufficient knowledge of French, and seven families did not provide a reason. Eleven children with CKD and 13 caregivers were recruited. One child and their caregiver were excluded from the analysis because of incomplete interview data; the excluded participant lost focus about halfway through the interview. A total of 10 children and 12 caregivers were enrolled and included in the analysis. Patient participants had a median age of 13 years (IQR 11.7, 15.6) and 70% (7/10) were female. CAKUT was the most common etiology of their CKD (4/10, 40%), and the median eGFR was 29.8 mL/min/1.73 m2 (IQR 22.3, 40.4). Two children (2/10, 20%) were on dialysis, both receiving hemodialysis treatments.

Symptom identification from cognitive interviews

After completing the PRO-Kid questionnaire, participants rated the understandability of the items using cognitive probing (Table 3). Three items were identified as slightly difficult to understand by participants: Feeling left out (Se sentir seul), Pain (other than headache) (Douleur [autre que mal de tête]) and Itching (Démangeaisons).

One participant explained that they were not sure whether Feeling left out (Se sentir seul) represented something that is needed (positive) or rather, something that is worrisome (negative). They felt this should be elaborated on further, as sometimes, spending time alone is desired and not associated with something negative. The participant did not suggest any alternative wording for this item. They however had a very good understanding of the item based on the interviewer's assessment of understanding, and therefore no change to the item was deemed necessary based on their feedback.

Regarding the Pain (other than headache) (Douleur [autre que mal de tête]) item, two participants raised concerns around this item as they found it too vague, but had no problem with its understandability. The interviewers adjudicated that the participants’ understanding was good based on cognitive probing, and no alternative wording was suggested by participants. Based on previous work (12,13), this symptom was intended to be broad, aiming to capture all causes of pain other than headache, which was conceptually distinct from the item Headache during the development phase of PRO-Kid. Because of the participants’ adequate understanding of the item, and factoring in previous validation work, the team concluded that this item did not need any modification to ensure conceptual alignment with the English version of PRO-Kid.

For the item Itching (Démangeaisons), three participants did not know the definition of the French word and required help from their caregiver and the interviewer to understand its meaning. The interviewer judged the participants’ understanding to be incorrect based on cognitive probing. One child participant suggested alternative wording for the item Itching (Démangeaisons), including “piquer” and “gratter la peau.”

At the interim analysis (following the initial five interviews) and at the final analysis (after the ten interviews were completed), no changes were deemed necessary, with no more than 30% of participants reporting difficulties related to the questionnaire items. However, despite not exceeding the 30% threshold, the item Itching (Démangeaisons) was challenging for three participants. The team therefore decided to add the descriptors quelque chose qui pique la peau, envie de se gratter (which translates to something that stings/pricks your skin, feeling like scratching your skin) to the item Itching (Démangeaisons), using the participants’ suggested wording alternatives. Participants’ feedback on the PRO-Kid questionnaire

All participants (10/10, 100%) reported that the French PRO-Kid was easy to complete, and the interviewers identified the same proportion (10/10, 100%) of participants who were able to distinguish between the choices. All participants who commented (n = 9) were comfortable with the wording of the scale used in the questionnaire.

Construct validity

There was a strong inverse correlation between the PRO-Kid Frequency score and the PedsQLTM scores (r = −0.81, P = 0.005) (Figure 3), and a moderate inverse correlation between the PRO-Kid Impact score and the PedsQLTM scores (r = −0.63, P = 0.045) (Figure 4). These findings demonstrate that a higher symptom burden is associated with poorer health-related quality-of-life.

Discussion

The objective of this study was to translate the validated English PRO-Kid into French and to assess the understandability of the French PRO-Kid. The analysis of data from the children enrolled at the two Pediatric Nephrology Centres in Montreal, Quebec, revealed that the tool was easy to understand for French-speaking children aged 8 to 18 years, and the wording was relevant in the French-Canadian cultural and linguistic context. This is the first CKD-specific pediatric PROM focusing on symptom burden available in French.

Overall, the French version of the PRO-Kid tool was found to be easy to understand and complete by the participants, with excellent interviewer-adjudicated correctness in understanding of the questionnaire items. In light of participants’ feedback, no changes were made to the initial iteration of the French version of the PRO-Kid. Three participants struggled with the Itching (Démangeaisons) item. To address this, our team decided to add descriptors to the Itching (Démangeaisons) item (quelque chose qui pique la peau, envie de se gratter [something that stings/pricks your skin, feeling like scratching your skin]) to improve understandability based on participants’ feedback. No further interviews were deemed necessary, considering the core wording of the item was not changed.

The study was not powered for full evaluation of construct validity; however, the moderate to strong correlations between the PRO-Kid and PedsQLTM scores suggest good construct validity. The correlation factors are similar to the original validation results of the English version Frequency score: R −0.78 (95% CI, 20.85 to 20.70); Impact score: R −0.69 (95% CI, 20.78 to 20.56) (13). This further supports the construct validity of the PRO-Kid tool and suggests the French version conveys an equivalent meaning.

This study included 11 participants, one of whom was excluded from the analysis because of incomplete data. This participant had just turned 8 years old the week before the cognitive interview. This highlights the importance of administering age-appropriate PROMs; the PRO-Kid modified for ages 5 to 7 years, which might have been more suitable for this participant, will soon be available for clinical use.

Although some previously published linguistic validation studies for pediatric PROMs included larger cohorts of participants (Larouche et al., 30 participants (20), Nafees et al., 55 participants (21) and Jordan et al., 201 participants (22)), others use smaller participant cohorts, similar to our study, and tailor cohort size using data saturation (23,24). Data saturation appeared to have been reached in our sample, as demonstrated by the final analysis yielding results similar to the interim analysis and the absence of added information from an additional five participants. Moreover, there is a limited number of French-speaking children living with CKD in Canada. Our team approached children at the two major pediatric centres in the country, and convenience sampling allowed to reach most children in the target age group. Had more children agreed to participate, a larger sample could have been achieved, yet with limited relevance considering data saturation.

This work on a pediatric PROM focused on children who were able to complete self-reported questionnaires and thus excluded children with intellectual disabilities. This decision was based on previous work, including the validation of the English version of PRO-Kid (13). With the inclusion of all children living with CKD in mind, a modified PRO-Kid tool designed for parent-proxy completion for younger children and those with intellectual disabilities is currently being validated. This tool will also be translated into French for use with this specific patient population.

Strengths and limitations

The strength of this research includes the methodology used, which is in line with best practices in linguistic validation of PROMs established by the ISPOR-TCA group (14). The multicentre design, with the inclusion of two pediatric hospitals in the province of Quebec, also represents a strength. Our sample included a diversity of CKD stages, including two children on dialysis, although the sample included only 30% males, whereas pediatric CKD is usually more prevalent in boys (25,26). The small number of participants receiving dialysis treatments limits the possibility of subgroup analysis. The study is limited by its conduct in only one francophone country and two pediatric centres, with cultural variability impacting the results that might be obtained in other French-speaking countries.

Conclusion

The French PRO-Kid was found to have good cultural relevance and validity for use in a French-Canadian setting. The implementation of this tool in a clinical setting has the potential to enhance communication of symptoms, both in terms of frequency as well as impact, by French-speaking children with CKD with their healthcare providers. This may also facilitate the inclusion of French-speaking children in future research focused on CKD symptoms.
