Large-scale evidence for highly coupled soil multi-elements in permafrost ecosystems
Authors: Shuqi Qin, Yingjie Chen, Yuanhe Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe coupled soil element cycles are the foundation of many crucial ecosystem functions such as primary production and climate regulation. However, whether and how multiple soil elements are coupled in permafrost regions remains unclear. Here, we collected and analyzed 240 soil samples from the active layer (0–10 cm) and permafrost deposits (top 50 cm) at 24 sites along a 1000-km transect on the Tibetan Plateau. We found that a high degree of soil element coupling, defined as the mean Spearman rank correlation among 19 measured elements, was maintained in the two soil layers. Nutrient elements were more likely to be coupled than non-nutrient elements, with 19 and 11 sites revealing robust coupling of nutrient and non-nutrient elements, respectively. Furthermore, atomic properties, including atomic mass and electronegativity, affected element coupling in the active layer, but had no effect in the permafrost deposits. These findings advance our understanding of terrestrial biogeochemistry in permafrost regions and inspire further research to explore the linkage of soil element coupling with ecosystem functioning.Keywords: Coupled biogeochemical cycles, Soil multi-elements, Tibetan Plateau, Permafrost region, Atomic properties

Introduction

Biogeochemical cycles of elements are complex processes that drive the exchange of materials and energy within the ecosystems [1]. Soils, as an interface connecting the atmosphere, biosphere and lithosphere, play a crucial role in global biogeochemical cycles [2,3]. Soils contain not only nutrient elements necessary for plants and microorganisms [4], but also elements as components of minerals [5] or as pollutants [6]. These elements are generally coupled due to the basic stoichiometry of living biomass, the flow of electrons in oxidation–reduction reactions of metabolic activity, and the chelation of metal ions in organic compounds [7]. Such coupled cycles of soil elements are the foundation of multiple ecosystem functions and services such as climate regulation, pollution control and primary production [2]. However, the strength of soil element coupling could be altered by global change factors such as elevated atmospheric CO2 concentration [8] and atmospheric nitrogen (N) deposition [9], which may further affect ecosystem functioning given the positive associations between biogeochemical coupling and ecosystem functioning [10]. For example, N deposition and drought have been reported to change the coupling between soil carbon (C), N and phosphorus (P), thereby influencing soil microbial nutrient limitation, community composition and enzyme activities [11,12]. Therefore, knowledge about soil element coupling is crucial to improve our understanding of ecosystem functioning under global environmental changes.

Considering the important role of biogeochemical coupling, early studies have adopted the ratios between primary soil elements, such as C, N and P, to examine their coupling/decoupling along environmental gradients [13] or in response to global change factors [14]. Despite the insightful findings, these studies provide limited information about the overall coupling of multiple elements, as soils contain almost 90 elements naturally [15]. Recently, the pairwise associations among elements have been used to quantify the mean strength of multiple soil element coupling [10]. Based on this method, high levels of coupling among ∼20 soil elements are detected across a continental scale, with the coupling strength being affected by environmental conditions (e.g., climate, weathering, soil pH and texture) and the atomic properties of chemical elements such as atomic mass [16,17]. Composition and biodiversity of plants and soil microorganisms could also affect element decoupling/coupling due to their selective utilization of nutrients and their deposition of compounds with highly conserved stoichiometry into soil [16,17]. However, existing studies are mainly from non-permafrost regions, and thus, how multiple soil elements are coupled in permafrost areas remains unclear. Compared with non-permafrost regions, the cold climate in permafrost regions could constrain soil weathering [18], plant growth [19] and microbial activities [20], which probably leads to a different degree of soil element coupling. Particularly, the permafrost layer, the subsurface soil or rock that remains at or below 0 °C for at least 2 years [21], might exhibit distinct element coupling strength compared with the active layer (that is, the seasonally thawed surface soils) due to its long-term frozen state and weak biotic processes. Considering that climate warming-induced permafrost thaw would make the top permafrost active in biogeochemical cycles [22], evaluation of multi-element coupling in permafrost deposits and its comparison with the active layer is urgently needed to better understand biogeochemical cycles in permafrost regions under a changing climate.

The Tibetan Plateau, known as the “Roof of the World”, possesses the most extensive alpine permafrost in mid- and low-latitude regions of the world [23]. Over the recent decades, the plateau has warmed at nearly twice the global average rate [24,25], causing widespread permafrost thaw [26]. Previous studies from the Tibetan Plateau have determined the concentrations of major soil elements in the active layer [27,28], but data from permafrost deposits are still scarce, and how these elements are coupled remains unclear. To fill these data and knowledge gaps, we investigated 24 sites across the representative permafrost regions on the Tibetan Plateau [29]. We collected 0–10 cm surface soil (active layer) and the top 50 cm permafrost soil at each site with five replicates, obtaining 240 soil samples in total (Fig. 1). We determined the concentration of 19 soil elements for all the soil samples, based on which we quantified the degree of multi-element coupling for active layer and permafrost deposits separately at each site. Element coupling was defined as the average Spearman rank correlation coefficients (in absolute value) of pairwise elements, with greater average correlation representing stronger overall element coupling [16]. We also determined four types of potential predictors of element coupling including climatic variables (mean annual air temperature (MAT) and mean annual precipitation (MAP)), plant properties (Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI) and species richness), edaphic variables (soil pH and texture) and microbial properties (total phospholipid fatty acids (PLFAs) and fungal/bacterial ratio (F/B)). Overall, we aimed to investigate the strength of soil multi-element coupling in the active layer and permafrost deposits across the Tibetan Plateau and explore the drivers of the coupling. We hypothesized that the permafrost layer would show a different degree of multi-element coupling from the active layer due to the weak biological activities and distinct geochemical reactions, weathering degree and mineral content. We also hypothesized that climatic, plant and microbial properties would be the primary determinants of element coupling in the active layer, while edaphic variables would be more important in the permafrost deposits.

Materials and methods

Study area

Permafrost regions cover approximately 40% of the total area of the Tibetan Plateau, which are primarily distributed across southern Qinghai and northern Tibet [23]. The majority of the existing permafrost was formed during the Last Glaciation in the late Pleistocene when the plateau reached its present elevation [30]. The mean active layer thickness across the plateau is currently estimated to be 1.9 m [31], and is projected to increase at a rate of 2.5–17.5 cm per decade by 2100 under different scenarios [32]. To cover a wide geographic range of permafrost across the Tibetan Plateau, this study investigated 24 sites in three representative permafrost regions along a 1000 km transect (Fig. 1a). Among them, 11 sites are distributed along the Qinghai-Tibet Highway in the interior of the plateau; 10 sites are situated within the Elashankou-Qingshuihe section on the eastern part of the plateau; and the remaining 3 sites are located on the northeastern plateau near the Qilian Mountains. These sites span from 91.9°E to 99.5°E in longitude and from 32.6°N to 38.6°N in latitude. The elevation of the sites ranges from 3774 to 5141 m, while the MAT and MAP vary from −4.5 to 1.8 °C and 245 to 504 mm, respectively (Table S1). The vegetation type of the sites is alpine grasslands, including alpine steppe dominated by Stipa purpurea and Carex moorcroftii, alpine meadow dominated by Kobresia pygmaea and Kobresia humilis, and swamp meadow dominated by Kobresia tibetica. As classified by the World Reference Base for Soil Resources [33], the soil types for the sites include Cambisols, Calcisols and Cryosols.

Field sampling

We conducted field sampling at the 24 sites in 2016, obtaining a total of 240 soil samples (24 sites × 2 layers × 5 replicates) from the active layer and the permafrost deposits [29,34]. At each site, we first established a 10 m × 10 m plot, and then collected five replicated soil cores at the four corners and the center of the plot (Fig. 1b). The cores were drilled with a borehole drilling machine to a depth of 1.5 to 3.5 m dependent on the active layer thickness. We collected soil samples at two depths for each core: the top 0–10 cm soils were chosen as active layer samples because most of the biotic activities occur in this depth [16]; and the uppermost 50 cm permafrost soils were collected to represent the permafrost layer, which would thaw primarily under climate warming. For more details about field sampling, please see published studies [29,35].

Measurements of soil elements

For each of the 240 soil samples, we determined the concentrations of 19 elements, including 13 nutrient elements and six non-nutrient elements. These elements are the same as those analyzed in a global study of 16 chronosequences across six continents [16], enabling comparison between the Tibetan Plateau and other regions. The selected nutrient elements are necessary components for the structural tissues of plants, animals or microorganisms [4] and are major drivers of biological diversity, plant production and microbial activity [16]. While different species would vary in their needs for nutrients, we classified the elements according to the chronosequence study [16]: C, N, P and potassium (K) are considered as primary macronutrients; calcium (Ca), magnesium (Mg), sodium (Na) and sulfur (S) are classified as secondary macronutrients; and iron (Fe), manganese (Mn), copper (Cu), zinc (Zn) and nickel (Ni) are grouped as micronutrients. We also determined the concentration of non-nutrient macro and microelements, including silicon (Si), barium (Ba), chromium (Cr), aluminum (Al), strontium (Sr) and titanium (Ti). Prior to analyses, soils were passed through a 2-mm sieve and air-dried indoors. The visible roots were removed, and the soil samples were ground using a ball mill. Organic C concentration was determined by a dichromate oxidation technique [36]. Briefly, the soil organic matter (SOM) was oxidized by a hot mixture of K2Cr2O7 and H2SO4, followed by the measurements of excess Cr2O72- with Fe2+. The amount of reduced Cr2O72- was obtained by comparison with a blank control and used to calculate organic C content. The concentration of total N was acquired using an elemental analyser (Vario EL III, Elementar, Germany). To determine the concentration of the remaining 17 elements, we used a semi-automatic pressor (PrepP-01, Ruishenbao, China) to prepare soil samples as pressed pellets, and analyzed the samples using an X-ray fluorescence spectrometer (Axios mAX, Panalytical, Netherlands).

Determination of potential predictors

To explore the drivers of soil element coupling, we determined four types of abiotic and biotic factors, including climatic variables, plant properties, edaphic variables and microbial properties (Tables S1 and S2). For climatic variables (MAT and MAP), we first conducted Kriging interpolation to acquire their spatial distributions across the Tibetan Plateau based on the data of the meteorological stations from the China Meteorological Data Service Centre (http://data.cma.cn). The MAT and MAP for the 24 sampling sites were then extracted using the coordinate information. To account for the effects of plants on element coupling, we extracted plant species richness from a map with 1-km resolution [37]. We also obtained maximum-value composite NDVI from the Moderate Resolution Imaging Spectroradiometer (MODIS) aboard NASA’s Terra satellites (https://neo.gsfc.nasa.gov/) for every 16-day interval during sampling time (i.e., July to August in 2016). For more details about data extraction, see ref. [38]. To characterize edaphic conditions, soil pH was determined in a 1:2.5 soil-to-water mixture and soil texture was analyzed by a particle size analyzer (Malvern Masterizer 2000, Malvern, Worcestershire, UK) after removing organic matter and carbonates. Finally, to estimate the role of soil microorganisms in affecting element coupling, we conducted PLFA analysis to quantify microbial biomass and community composition. PLFA biomarkers specific to bacteria (i14:0, a15:0, i15:0, i16:0, 16:1ω7c, a17:0, cy17:0, i17:0, 18:1ω7, and cy19:0) and fungi (18:2ω6,9c) were quantified, and the microbial community composition was assessed by F/B. Data on edaphic and microbial properties were derived from ref. [29].

Statistical analyses

We conducted all numerical calculations and statistical analyses using R version 4.4.2 [39]. We used paired samples t-tests to examine the differences in element concentration and element coupling between the active layer and permafrost deposits. To quantify element coupling, Spearman rank correlations, which are robust to data lacking normality and are based on order [10], were adopted. Using Eq. 1, we also calculated the coupling value for nutrient elements (n = 13 including C, N, P, K, Ca, Mg, Na, S, Fe, Mn, Cu, Zn, and Ni) and non-nutrient elements (n = 6 including Si, Al, Ti Ba, Cr, and Sr), respectively.

To further compare the strength of the coupling against random associations among the elements, we randomly generated null models using the same dataset (that is, the data for determining the coupling value at each site) [40] and calculated element coupling for each randomization by Eq. 1. We then calculated two-tailed P values based on 999 permutations to examine whether elements were significantly coupled (i.e., P < 0.05 with the coupling value falling above 95% confidence interval of null models). The observed coupling value falling within the 95% confidence interval of null models was defined as decoupled [10].

We also calculated the coupling value for each of the 19 elements within the active layer and permafrost deposits. Firstly, the concentration of the element at each site was calculated as the average of the five replicates. Then the data of the 24 sites within each layer were used to calculate the coupling value of individual elements by averaging the Spearman rank correlation coefficients (absolute values) involving that given element and the rest of the 18 elements. Finally, we used general linear models to examine the effects of biotic and abiotic factors, as well as atomic properties of elements (including atomic mass and electronegativity) on element coupling.

Results and discussion

Differences in element concentration between active and permafrost layers

Si, Al, Fe and Ca are reported to be the most abundant elements in the continental crust after element oxygen [41]. Consistently, the four elements all showed high concentration in both the active layer and permafrost deposits across the Tibetan Plateau (> 30 g kg-1; Fig. 2), followed by elements C, K, Mg, Na, N, Ti and S (> 1 g kg-1; Fig. 2). The rest elements had relatively low concentrations of < 1 g kg-1 (Fig. 2). We then examined the differences in the measured elements between the active layer and permafrost deposits, and found that the variation was element-specific. For nutrient elements, the concentration of C, N, P, S and Mn was significantly higher in the active layer, while the concentration of K, Mg, Cu, Zn and Ni was much higher in the permafrost deposits (Fig. 2a–c). Ca, Na and Fe exhibited no difference in concentration between the two soil layers (Fig. 2a–c). Different from the nutrient elements, the non-nutrient elements were generally more significantly enriched in the permafrost deposits except for Ba and Sr (Fig. 2d).

The distribution of soil nutrient elements along the soil profile is shaped by four major processes, including biological cycling, atmospheric deposition, weathering and leaching [42]. Soil organic C in the form of SOM is primarily derived from aboveground and belowground plant detritus, and thus the plant allocation and root depth could largely affect the vertical distribution of organic C [43]. Given that approximately 90% of the root biomass is observed in the surface 30 cm soils in the Tibetan alpine grasslands (the dominant vegetation type in this permafrost region) [44], plant inputs would mainly occur in the shallow active layer and lead to higher C content in the topsoil. In contrast, permafrost deposits are generally deep (ranging between 1.5–3.5 m at the 24 sites), so that fresh C input is limited over the long term, resulting in lower C content in this layer. Soil N, like C, is contained in SOM but also originates from biological N fixation and atmospheric deposition [45]. These inputs mainly occur in surface soil and hence lead to the decline in N concentration along the soil profile. Soil P and other nutrient elements are primarily derived from weathering in most soils [42]. The higher concentration of P, S and Mn in topsoil indicated that plants may absorb these elements in subsurface soils and then recycle them to surface soils via litterfall (i.e., moving nutrients upwards) [42]. By contrast, the higher concentration of certain nutrient elements (K, Mg, Cu, Zn and Ni) and non-nutrient elements (Si, Al, Ti and Cr) in the permafrost layer was probably because these elements were rich in parent materials and were conserved in the permafrost deposits compared with topsoil. The parent materials on the Tibetan Plateau were rich in K, Mg, Si and Al, including rocks such as Ca-Mg carbonate rocks, silicate-rich and high-Al silicate castic rocks, and high-K/Na and silicate-rich aluminosilicate rocks [28]. After weathering, these elements may exhibit high concentrations but can be removed from the topsoil through processes such as leaching [28,42]. While in the permafrost layer, the frozen condition prevents the loss of these elements, resulting in higher concentrations.

Multi-element coupling across the alpine permafrost region

Our results revealed that the multiple soil elements were highly coupled across the Tibetan alpine permafrost region. Specifically, the average coupling strength among the 19 soil elements exhibited large spatial variations on the Tibetan Plateau, varying from 0.42 to 0.85 and 0.41 to 0.68 for active layer and permafrost deposits, respectively (Fig. 3a, b). For most sites, the coupling strength was significantly different from the corresponding null models (generally falling above the 95% confidence interval; Fig. 3a, b), indicating that the concentrations of these soil elements exhibited a stronger spatial correlation than what was expected by chance. Moreover, the strength of multi-element coupling was not significantly different between the active layer and the permafrost layer (Fig. 3c), which did not support our hypothesis. This meant that the more intense plant and microbial activities in the active layer did not alter the original co-occurrence of elements, which was primarily determined by the parent materials [46]. Moreover, other processes that would affect the topsoil element cycles, such as atmospheric N deposition [9] and anthropogenic element inputs into soils [47], were relatively low across the Tibetan Plateau [48,49] due to the minor human disturbance [38,50], thus leaving limited effects on topsoil element coupling.

We then separated the 19 elements into two groups to examine whether the coupling existed among both nutrient elements and non-nutrient elements. Our results revealed that for the nutrient elements, 19 of the 24 sites showed robust coupling strength (significantly higher than the null models) in the active layer (Fig. 4a) and permafrost deposits (Fig. 4b), while non-nutrient elements were highly coupled only at 11 of the 24 sites (Fig. 4c-d). Thus, nutrient elements were more likely to be spatially correlated than non-nutrient elements, which was probably because the cycles of nutrient elements are driven by biological processing with ecological stoichiometry [51,52]. Plant detritus provides a major input of SOM as well as energy and nutrients for microbial communities [51]. Soil microorganisms drive not only the decomposition of plant detritus and SOM, but also the formation of SOM by the inputs of microbial extracellular metabolites and necromass [53,54]. As plant materials and soil microbial communities show strong stoichiometric homeostasis [51], their mediation over nutrient element cycling would lead to strong coupling of these elements compared with non-nutrient elements. Similar to the overall element coupling, the coupling strength for nutrient elements and non-nutrient elements was not significantly different between the active layer and permafrost layer (Fig. 4).

To further evaluate whether the degree of element coupling in the Tibetan alpine permafrost region differed from other regions, we compared our results with data from 16 globally distributed chronosequences involving the same elements and replicates [16]. The results revealed that the element coupling strength on the Tibetan Plateau was significantly stronger than that in other terrestrial ecosystems (Fig. S1), which could be due to the differences in biological activities, soil weathering and edaphic properties among the regions. The biological activities of life in terrestrial ecosystems selectively alter the chemistry of soil, and thus could break the inherent associations of the multiple soil elements [16]. In particular, as the first-principle mechanisms driving the biogeochemical cycles, the biogeography, ecology and physiology of soil microorganisms would have profound impacts on element coupling [55]. The microbial biomass on the Tibetan Plateau is generally lower than or similar to that in other regions worldwide [56]. Meanwhile, the low temperature in permafrost regions (MAT being −4.5–1.8 °C with an average of −2.0 °C at the 24 sites in this study vs. 0.6–21.3 °C with an average of 11.4 °C in the chronosequence study; Fig. S2a) would constrain the growth and activities of soil microorganism [20], thereby limiting their re-distribution of soil elements. Similar to soil microorganisms, plant growth is also limited by the low temperatures in permafrost regions [57]. Moreover, the harsh environments across the Tibetan Plateau, such as low temperatures, low atmospheric pressure and strong UV radiation, would structure highly specialized morphological, phenological, and physiological traits of plants [58]. This means that the plants in the Tibetan alpine permafrost region might have relatively similar element demands and thus have a limited effect on multi-element decoupling. By contrast, in regions where plants exhibit species-dependent element demand according to their nutrient-acquiring strategies and biogeochemical niches [59], a range of soil elements would be selectively absorbed by plant communities, consequently leading to decoupling of soil elements [16,60]. In addition to biological activities, the cold and dry climate (MAP being 245–504 mm with an average of 371 mm at the 24 sites in this study vs. 106–2365 mm with an average of 938 mm in the chronosequence study; Fig. S2b) across the plateau would result in slower mineral weathering rate compared with the warmer and wetter environments [18]. The soils on the Tibetan Plateau also had higher pH (ranging from 6.1 to 9.5 with an average of 8.1 at the 24 sites in this study; Fig. S2c) than those in other regions (ranging from 3.6 to 9.1 with an average of 6.2 in the chronosequence study; Fig. S2c). These abiotic conditions on the plateau would favor the adsorption of cations to the negatively charged soil particles, thereby conserving elements and facilitating their coupling [16].

Drivers of soil element coupling across alpine permafrost region

We established the relationships of multi-elements coupling with potential drivers including climatic variables (MAT and MAP), plant properties (NDVI and species richness), edaphic variables (soil pH and texture) and microbial properties (total PLFAs and F/B), and found that these biotic and abiotic factors had limited effects on soil element coupling in both the active layer and permafrost deposits (Fig. S3). These results did not support our hypothesis and were in contrast with previous studies reporting that the key biotic and abiotic drivers explained 32%−36% variations of multi-elements coupling across 16 chronosequences distributed globally [16] and in 26 mountains covering five climate zones across China [17]. This divergence could be due to the different spatial scales involved in these studies. Despite the broad climate gradient across the Tibetan Plateau (Fig. S2), the other two studies spanned much wider spatial scales (that is, global and national scale) and involved more ecosystem types. These results suggest that the climatic, edaphic and biotic factors would result in different strengths of soil element coupling among ecosystems, but be less important within the same ecosystem type. Meanwhile, the results indicate the potential role of other factors such as atomic properties in driving element coupling. To test this deduction, we calculated coupling strength for individual elements and visualized the associations among the 19 elements using the undirected correlation networks for the two soil layers, respectively (Fig. 5a, b). We found that the order of elements for their coupling degree differed between the soil layers (Fig. 5c, d). The top five coupled elements were S, C, P, N and Fe in the active layer, but Al, K, Cr, Ni and Ti in the permafrost layer (Fig. 5c, d). These elements (except for Fe) also showed higher concentration in the layer where they were highly coupled compared with the other layer (Fig. 2), suggesting that the soil elements that were more enriched could also be more important in the coupling among elements. Nevertheless, when considering all 19 elements, there was no significant effect of element concentration on coupling strength in either the active layer or permafrost deposits (Fig. S4). The results also revealed that Na and Si were the least coupled elements for the active layer and permafrost deposits, respectively (Fig. 5c, d).

We then investigated the effects of atomic properties (including atomic mass and electronegativity) on element coupling. The results showed that soil element coupling was negatively associated with atomic mass (excluding Na), but positively associated with electronegativity in the active layer (Fig. 6a, b). The atomic mass of an element is considered to determine its reactivity and mobility, and the heavier soil elements being more spatially decoupled in the topsoil was possibly because many heavy elements were asymmetrically distributed in rocks with low concentrations [16]. This finding was consistent with that reported for the topsoil in 16 globally distributed chronosequences from forests, grasslands, shrublands and croplands [16], but contrasts with those detected in a N addition experiment from a semiarid shrubland [9] and in ∼10,000 observations from a range of terrestrial ecosystems including forests, grasslands, deserts, wetlands, tundra, and agricultural lands across all continents [61]. This discrepancy can be attributed to the differing focuses of the studies. Our study and the chronosequence study determined the total content of topsoil elements, which represents an integrated indicator of soil mineral composition and soil-forming processes taking place at a longer timescale. In contrast, the other two studies focused on the bioavailability of soil elements, which reflects dynamic processes occurring at shorter timescales [61]. Thus, the effects of atomic mass on soil element coupling could be time scale-dependent. Interestingly, these studies all detected that the coupling of some elements was deviated from the predicted value by atomic mass, suggesting reorganizational processes of elements in terrestrial ecosystems. For example, we found that Na was more decoupled than the prediction by its atomic mass (Fig. 6a), which on one hand may be because Na is particularly prone to leaching loss due to its single charge and relatively large hydrated radius [62]. On the other hand, large herbivores on the Tibetan Plateau, such as wild yak, Tibetan wild ass and Tibetan antelope [63], would ingest Na from soils (that is, geophagy) to satisfy their physiological Na requirements for regulating body fluid volume, osmolarity, tissue pH, etc. [64]. These processes jointly cause Na to deviate from a predictable pattern based on its atomic mass. In addition, the positive association between electronegativity and element coupling indicated that the chemical reactivity of elements, as well as the potential energy involved in the element interactions, could play an important role in affecting the spatial distribution of soil elements. Distinct from the active layer, atomic properties showed no significant relationship with element coupling in the permafrost deposits (Fig. 6c, d). Atomic properties primarily determine the potential of soil elements to react with one another and to migrate across space [9]. However, these processes could be constrained by the frozen state in permafrost deposits, thus weakening the roles of atomic properties in affecting element coupling.

Conclusion

Based on large-scale field sampling across the Tibetan alpine permafrost region, this study quantified the strength and drivers of 19 soil element coupling in the active layer and permafrost deposits. We found that soil elements across our study area maintained high levels of coupling regardless of soil layers, probably because the minor human disturbance in this area did not alter topsoil element coupling and thus conserved the original co-occurrence of multiple elements primarily determined by the parent materials. The strong coupling might then enhance ecosystem stability, given the positive relationship between coupling and ecosystem functioning [10]. Moreover, nutrient elements were more likely to be coupled compared with non-nutrient elements because nutrient cycles are driven by biological processing with ecological stoichiometry. This implies that alterations in nutrient element coupling caused by global change factors (e.g., atmospheric CO2 enrichment and N deposition) might in turn affect ecosystem functions such as plant production and microbial activities. We also found that atomic properties (i.e., atomic mass and electronegativity) were associated with element coupling strength in the active layer (excluding Na), but had no effect in the permafrost deposits. The deviation of Na from the prediction by atomic mass-coupling relationship suggests reorganizational processes (such as leaching and herbivore ingestion) as destabilizing external forces. Overall, these results advance our understanding of soil element coupling in permafrost regions, opening new questions about the drivers of coupling strength in the active layer and permafrost deposits. Future studies that link soil element coupling to ecosystem functioning and explore the impacts of global change factors on soil element coupling are encouraged to further enhance our understanding of terrestrial biogeochemical cycles in permafrost regions.

Yuanhe Yang (BRID: 03115.00.97857) is the director of Key Laboratory of Vegetation and Environmental Change, Institute of Botany, Chinese Academy of Sciences.
