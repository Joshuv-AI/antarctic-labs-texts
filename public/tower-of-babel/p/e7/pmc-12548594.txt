Reducing inappropriate transthoracic echocardiography orders in normotensive patients with acute pulmonary embolism in a community hospital: a quality improvement project
Authors: Dani Lee, Ariel Foo, Seddiq Weera, Bennett Haynen, Mohammad Refaei
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTransthoracic echocardiograms (TTEs) have limited value in guiding management of normotensive patients with acute pulmonary embolism (PE). Nevertheless, TTEs are frequently ordered inappropriately. This quality improvement project aimed to decrease inappropriate TTE orders by 30% over 6 months in patients with PE admitted to general internal medicine at a community hospital. Two interventions were implemented using successive plan-do-study-act (PDSA) cycles: educational sessions for physicians and the distribution of TTE-ordering algorithms for triaging of TTE appropriateness. Four audits on TTE orders for inpatients with PE were conducted throughout the project: a pre-intervention audit (pre-audit; March to August 2020), a post-intervention 1 audit (post-I1; August to September 2022), a post-intervention 2 audit (post-I2; December 2022 to February 2023) and a post-intervention audit (post-audit; March to August 2023). The primary outcome measure was the proportion of inappropriate to appropriate TTE orders during the pre-intervention and post-intervention periods. During the pre-audit, post-I1, post-I2 and post-audit periods, 89, 23, 20 and 158 patients, respectively, were admitted with PEs. 37, 10, 3 and 14 patients in each of these periods, respectively, received a TTE for PE-related reasons and were therefore included in the audits. 42%, 43%, 15% and 24% of patients, respectively, received a TTE for PE-related reasons. 89%, 50%, 67% and 79% of those TTEs, respectively, were inappropriate. There was a transient decrease in inappropriate to appropriate TTE orders ratio after the first intervention (p<0.005). Inappropriate investigations lead to additional healthcare costs and delays in patient care. This quality improvement project highlights an ongoing need to increase awareness surrounding TTE indications to improve appropriate utilisation. Next steps include further PDSA cycles with additional interventions to continue to try and decrease inappropriate TTE orders in the community hospital setting.

Introduction

Transthoracic echocardiograms (TTEs) are often ordered for patients with acute pulmonary embolism (PE), as over 50% of patients may go on to develop right ventricular dysfunction (RVD).1 In high-risk haemodynamically unstable patients, RVD is a predictor of mortality. In these cases, clinical findings might indicate decompensated right-sided heart failure which, along with refractory hypotension, favours systemic thrombolysis.2 For these patients with haemodynamic instability, TTEs are appropriately indicated particularly if being used to guide or re-assess changes after therapy such as thrombectomy or thrombolysis.2 3 However, there is limited evidence to support the role of TTEs in haemodynamically stable acute PEs.410 One meta-analysis concluded that while RVD may be associated with increased short-term mortality in haemodynamically stable patients, the prognostic value was too low to guide management.6 Another study found that TTE as a single test for RVD had little value in prognosticating normotensive patients with PE.11 Moreover, delaying treatment of intermediate-high risk normotensive patients with PE did not change the risk of mortality or morbidities, but reduced the risk of bleeding complications from early institution of systemic thrombolysis.12 Finally, current validated tools used to assess PE risk, including the Pulmonary Embolism Severity Index (PESI) and the Simplified Pulmonary Embolism Severity Index (sPESI), do not include TTE findings or RVD as parameters.13 14

Previous audits on TTE-ordering practices for patients with acute PEs in tertiary centres have highlighted inappropriate practices that do not adhere to the 2011 American College of Cardiology Foundation’s Appropriate Use Criteria (AUC) clinical guidelines.2 In these cases, TTEs are ordered inappropriately for haemodynamically stable patients in whom the investigation is unlikely to change prognosis or management. The rate of inappropriate utilisation of TTEs is particularly higher in community settings.2 3 15 16 Consequences of inappropriate TTEs include additional costs to the healthcare system, prolonged hospitalisations and delays of potentially critical interventions, while increasing patients’ anxiety without clear benefit or change to management.

Part of the challenge surrounding inappropriate TTE-ordering practices observed at our local community hospital is the lack of Canadian guidelines surrounding appropriate TTE indications in normotensive patients with PE. Thus, this quality improvement (QI) project used interventions that focused on educating healthcare providers and implementing supports to help assess TTE appropriateness. The project aimed to decrease the inappropriate use of TTEs in haemodynamically stable patients with acute PE admitted to general internal medicine wards by 30% over 6 months at the local community hospital.

Methods

Design

Members of the QI group for this project included physicians from the haematology and cardiology departments, medical students, a QI consultant, the charge cardiac sonographer and a research coordinator. The group met every 6–8 weeks. Stakeholders from different departments, including internal medicine, haematology and cardiology, were engaged during the project. A fishbone analysis, driver diagram, 5 why’s diagram and process map were created to identify potential areas of initiatives affecting TTE-ordering practices (online supplemental 1 and 2). The project was implemented for patients admitted under General Internal Medicine at the local community hospital, one of three acute hospitals in the local system providing care to 450 000 residents of the region. These three hospitals cover 1054 beds across multiple services, including internal medicine (and subspecialties including 36 intensive care unit (ICU) beds), Psychiatry and Surgical specialities. Over 5 months, there are approximately 190 admissions for acute PEs across the whole local hospital system and approximately 110 to the hospital at which the project was completed. The hospital has the capacity to provide thrombolysis and thrombectomy on-site.

To assess the impact of the QI interventions, the project consisted of three phases: a retrospective pre-intervention audit phase from March to August 2020 (pre-audit), which helped to define baseline gaps in utilisation and TTE-ordering practices; an intervention phase with two sequential post-intervention audits from August to September 2022 (post-I1) and December 2022 to February 2023 (post-I2), both implemented according to the plan-do-study-act (PDSA) method; and a post-intervention audit phase from March to August 2023 (post-audit) to observe changes in TTE-ordering practices once all the interventions had been completed. The post-I1, post-I2 and post-audit findings were compared with the pre-audit findings to assess whether observed changes in TTE order appropriateness could be attributable to the project interventions. The pre-audit and post-audit periods were selected over similar time periods to account for length and seasonality effects. The post-audit period helped to assess the overall sustainability of any changes in TTE-ordering practices observed during the immediate post-I1 and post-I2 audits over time.

Standards for Quality Improvement Reporting Excellence guidelines were used for this project.17

Interventions

The first intervention using the PDSA cycle was the delivery of 45 min in-person presentations by a staff haematologist on appropriate TTE indications for acute PE. These didactic sessions, which consisted of lecture-style presentations on general approach to venous thromboembolism (VTE) and evidence on use of TTEs in patients with PE, were delivered at grand round sessions to physicians and clinical associates from the internal medicine, cardiology and emergency medicine departments under the local hospital system. Four additional sessions were given over a 3-month period from May to July 2022 to an audience of approximately 30–40 physicians of the different departments.

The second intervention was the distribution of a TTE-ordering algorithm (online supplemental 3) designed based on the eStiMaTe tool in collaboration with the cardiologist lead for the echocardiography lab to facilitate and provide guidance to echocardiography lab technicians in triaging TTE orders.18 Echocardiogram technicians and cardiologists were trained on how to use the algorithm, which was emailed to them; the algorithm was then printed on paper and posted at the triage desk. Reviewing staff cardiologists were also educated on the QI project and provided support to the technicians. The algorithm was implemented from October to November 2022.

Measures

The primary outcome measure of this QI project was the proportion of inappropriate to appropriate TTE orders. The secondary outcome measures were the proportion of total number of PE-related TTEs ordered to the total number of patients admitted with acute PE, rates of therapy change based on TTE findings, rates of monitoring changes based on TTE findings, and the rates of bleeding complications, stroke, in-hospital mortality and length of stay in hospital, the ICU and the telemetry wards.

As a process measure for the first intervention, an anonymous survey (online supplemental 4) was distributed throughout July 2023 to physicians regarding their knowledge of and satisfaction with the interventions related to this project. The survey inquired about any perceived impact the project may have had on their ordering practices. The number of patients who had a troponin and BNP (B-type natriuretic peptide) ordered was tracked as a process measure for the second intervention. As a balancing measure, the number of patients who were diagnosed with acute PE, who did not receive a TTE, and for whom a TTE may have been appropriate, was identified to assess whether the interventions led to unintended comorbidities or mortality to these patients.

Data collection

Retrospective audits were completed to study the impact of the interventions. Patient charts were accessed from the hospital’s electronic health records. Charts for patients aged 18 and over who had been admitted and diagnosed with acute PE were screened. Patients were included in the audits if they were diagnosed with imaging-confirmed PE; if they received a TTE and if they were admitted as inpatients. Patients were excluded if no records of confirmatory imaging were available; if they were transferred from another hospital; if their TTE was ordered for reasons unrelated to the PE diagnosis and if the PE was chronic. Data on patients’ demographics and admission, investigations and treatments were collected retrospectively. Risk stratification was determined via the sPESI score.14 Risk of 30-day mortality and 30-day complications were calculated with the eStiMaTe tool.18 The TTE orders were adjudicated by a blinded haematologist to determine whether they were appropriate. Orders were deemed appropriate if the patient demonstrated refractory hypotension (systolic blood pressure <90 mm Hg), if they were requiring vasopressors, or if they received thrombolysis; this definition was selected based on evidence supporting the use of TTE in haemodynamically unstable patients and based on the American AUC for TTE orders in PE.2 3 All patient information that was collected was de-identified. Four total audits were performed. The pre-audit was conducted with patients from March to August 2020. The post-audit was conducted with patients from March to August 2023. Two shorter audits were performed immediately at the end of each intervention to guide planning for the next PDSA cycle. An audit after the first intervention, post-I1, was performed with patients from August to September 2022, and an audit after the second intervention, post-I2, was performed with patients from December 2022 to February 2023. To ensure reliability of the auditing process, 10 charts that were screened in the pre-audit period were selected at random to also be screened by a staff haematologist to ensure consistency in patient inclusion and exclusion. Similarly, six charts included for a full chart review from the pre-audit were selected at random to be reviewed by a haematologist to verify consistency of data collection.

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics were estimated for patient demographic, condition and frequency of TTEs. Cross tabulation was used to estimate proportions of patients across audits with inappropriate TTEs. Pearson χ2 test was used for statistical significance. Two-sided p values <0.05 were considered statistically significant. Statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS V.29.19

Review board approval

The project was exempted by the local research ethics board as it was determined to be a QI initiative (HiREB #14 244-C).

Patient and public involvement

There was no patient or public involvement in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this project.

Results

The median age ranged from 67 to 71. 83%, 90%, 100% and 86% of patients in the pre-audit, post-I1, post-I2 and post-audit groups, respectively, had a high-risk category PE according to their sPESI score. 19%, 10%, 33% and 14% of patients across the groups, respectively, had a 30-day risk of mortality greater than 11% based on their eStiMaTe score. The classifications of the PE severities are shown in online supplemental 5. Across all audit periods, all patients were started on anticoagulation (table 1 and online supplemental 6). 3%, 10%, 5% and 7% of patients in the pre-audit, post-I1, post-I2 and post-audit groups, respectively, were hypotensive and received thrombolytic therapy; none of these patients developed bleeding complications from thrombolysis. No patients underwent thrombectomy. Across all audit groups, the most common indications for TTE orders were PE alone (41%, 40%, 5% and 43%, respectively) followed by right ventricular (RV) strain and pulmonary hypertension (22%, 20%, 5% and 0%, respectively). RV strain, pulmonary hypertension and systolic dysfunction were the most common TTE findings (online supplemental 7).

Regarding unexpected problems, attendance at the educational sessions was lower than anticipated, as less than half of physicians invited from each department were present. Otherwise, there were no unintended consequences of the two interventions that were identified. There was no missing data for any of the patients included in the chart reviews.

Discussion

TTEs are often inappropriately ordered for inpatients admitted with PE. This inappropriate usage contributes to increased costs in an already strained healthcare system. The American AUC has a dedicated section addressing the appropriate use of TTE, with four PE-related recommendations, though none address the appropriateness of TTEs for assessment of RVD outside the setting of thrombolysis or thrombectomy.2 Moreover, there are currently no Canadian guidelines pertaining to appropriate TTE indications for inpatients with acute PE. Some recent reviews and guidelines, including the 2019 European Society of Cardiology (ESC) guidelines, have suggested that TTEs may be beneficial in patients with normotensive PEs to identify RVD for prognostication. However, the same ESC guidelines also indicate that RVD can be detected on CT pulmonary angiogram, which is typically already included in the initial diagnostic work-up for PE.20 21 Although there is previous literature examining TTE-ordering practices in patients with acute PE, most studies have been focused on the academic setting. As such, one of the strengths of this QI project was targeting TTE-ordering practices specifically in a community hospital setting.

In this QI project, most patients across all study periods had sPESI scores ≥1, suggesting that these patients were appropriately admitted for inpatient treatment. Notably, this remains a small proportion of all patients presenting to the local community hospital’s emergency department with VTE, as many were managed with outpatient oral anticoagulation. The majority of TTEs that were ordered across all study periods were considered inappropriate. While there was a statistically significant decrease in the ratio of inappropriate to appropriate TTEs following the first intervention, this decrease was short-lived with a slow return to a high level of inappropriate TTEs. Moreover, the originally desired goal of a 30% decrease in inappropriate TTE orders was not achieved. Evidently, however, the ratio of total number of PE-related TTEs ordered to the total number of PEs diagnosed appeared to decrease from 42% to 24%. While this finding may be of clinical significance suggesting an overall decrease in TTE orders for acute PE, it should be noted that the change was not statistically significant. Overall, despite the total decrease in TTEs ordered for patients with PE, there does not seem to be a significant increase in PE-related comorbidities or mortality.

Despite changes in inappropriate TTE-ordering practices, the profile of patients who were admitted and had acute PE was consistent across all audit periods. Most patients were high-risk, received anticoagulation, did not require a prolonged stay in hospital or ICU/telemetry and did not develop complications. This supports the notion that in normotensive patients with acute PE, TTEs have little effect on altering interventions or outcomes. Moreover, the interventions did not appear to affect these parameters significantly, likely in part due to the already short hospital stays for these patients.

On review of the fishbone diagram created for this project, interventions addressing education of ordering physicians and echocardiogram technicians were selected after discussions with stakeholders from the emergency department, internal medicine and cardiology groups. Educational sessions and distribution of resources with guidance on appropriate TTE ordering were selected based on previous literature supporting these interventions.3 Subjectively, physicians who were surveyed indicated on average a modest impact of the interventions on their TTE-ordering practices. Objectively, between the two interventions, educational sessions were the only one to lead to statistically significant changes in TTE-ordering practices in this project. Of note, this was achieved while only 55% of surveyed physicians indicated they were aware of the project. However, this effect was short-lived and reversed over time. One possible reason for this may include lack of attendance at the sessions. Other reasons include possible low retention of topics discussed during the presentations and hiring of new physicians in the time between the interventions and the survey distribution. These last two possibilities could also explain the reversal in effects of the educational sessions. A previous educational intervention by Bhatia et al sought to decrease inappropriate echocardiographs for patients admitted to the medical service. In their project, the combination of a lecture, distribution of a pocket card for appropriate TTE ordering, and a pre-intervention and post-intervention questionnaire led to a 26% reduction of TTE orders per day.3 However, a meta-analysis by Chaudhuri et al concluded that educational interventions had inconsistent effects.16 Regarding other possible interventions, Chaudhuri et al’s analysis found that physician audits and feedback reports consistently led to decreased inappropriate TTE orders.16

The TTE-ordering algorithm did not lead to any statistically significant changes in ordering practices. This may be because the algorithm only addresses inappropriate orders that do not fulfil any of the four criteria included. However, normotensive patients with other risk factors included in the algorithm would not have their TTE declined, even if inappropriate. The rationale for having some flexibility here was to account for the complex presentations patients may have. Moreover, the results show that not every patient had BNPs and troponins ordered with their work-up. This was most likely because these investigations were not indicated based on the presentation at the time, although it would preclude the algorithm’s use in these cases.

Reasons for the persistence of inappropriate TTE orders despite interventions are likely multifactorial. Physician factors are likely still the most influential. First, the low percentage of physicians who indicated they were aware of the QI project’s goals suggests that physician awareness was still inadequate. Second, during the education sessions, some physicians indicated that they would feel uncomfortable not ordering a TTE even for normotensive patients. This suggests that reluctance to change practice habits may also contribute to persistent inappropriate orders. Third, while increasing education may passively improve awareness of TTE indications, the interventions did not prompt physicians to actively re-consider TTE appropriateness in the moment that orders are being entered.

At the time this project was conducted, the local hospital system was planning a transition to an electronic medical record (EMR) system. Once the EMR is implemented, next steps for this project include a possible third intervention incorporating decision support tools in the EMR. While recent projects using decision support tools at the order entry stage to reduce inappropriate TTEs have had mixed results, similar projects in other specialities have been successful.2224 Lilly et al demonstrated a reduction in unnecessary immunological testing by embedding ordering algorithms into their test-ordering software.25 Another intervention may include a feedback system, which one analysis showed decreased inappropriate TTE orders.16 This feedback report system could also be EMR-based. If these interventions are successful, then this project could be horizontally expanded to the other hospitals under the local hospital system.

Costs of the interventions used in this QI project were minimal. The first intervention centering around physician education was provided as part of pre-established presentation series’ including grand rounds, with little cost apart from the time required to prepare the didactic sessions. Costs of the second intervention were primarily the time required to train echocardiography technicians on the algorithm. At a stakeholder meeting prior to the algorithm’s distribution, one technician identified tension with ordering physicians due to the algorithm’s effects on the way TTE orders are triaged as a potential cost. However, this concern was not raised again once the intervention was started.

One shortcoming of this project was the small sample size in the post-intervention periods, though this was largely due to this study’s QI nature. As only three individuals received a TTE in the post-intervention 2 period, data from this time may not be representative of the intervention’s true effects. Regarding patient selection, patients from the pre-audit were admitted before the COVID-19 pandemic, whereas this project’s interventions and post-audits took place after the pandemic started. As such, the possibility that patients with respiratory symptoms were managed differently or presented with different severities of illness cannot be excluded. To address this, the number of hospital visits for PE was examined before and after the pandemic. It was found that the number of emergency room visits and inpatient admissions for PE at the local hospital was similar before and after the pandemic. Moreover, the survey assessing physicians’ responses to the interventions was based on subjective opinions, rather than objective measures of physicians’ knowledge of TTE appropriateness. Surveys also have inherently low participation rates. Thus, the survey results may not be truly representative of physicians’ responses to the interventions. There is also a clear need to improve this project’s outreach efforts to include more practitioners; attempts to address this included inviting physicians from multiple specialities and providing repeated educational sessions over the intervention period. A limitation pertaining to our second intervention was the lack of data available on how many orders were declined after the algorithm was distributed. This data would be helpful in serving as additional process and balancing measures. Additionally, only one subspecialist (haematologist) was involved in adjudicating the appropriateness of TTE orders, which could theoretically increase the risk of confirmation bias. However, the adjudication protocol and findings were reviewed with a cardiologist to try and minimise this risk. Finally, the PDSA cycle, while a cornerstone of QI, has limitations. Previously documented drawbacks include its reduced effectiveness when not enough iterations are performed or when poor execution of the ‘plan’ and ‘do’ phases undermines the ‘study’ phase.26 Thus, a next step of this project may include further iterations of the PDSA cycle to optimise its utility as a QI tool.

Regarding generalisability, this QI project was conducted at a community hospital with CT, echocardiogram, ICU, telemetry, haematology and cardiology services. The hospital also has the capability to perform thrombolysis and thrombectomy. However, the findings may not be generalisable to smaller hospitals that do not have access to the same degree of imaging and specialist services.

While there is ongoing research surrounding the utility of TTEs in normotensive patients with PE, current guidelines and risk stratification tools do not support the routine use of TTEs in normotensive patients with PE. This QI project highlights an ongoing need to decrease inappropriate TTE orders in this setting. Inappropriate TTEs may result in additional costs to an already strained public healthcare system, delaying other potentially critical interventions and increasing patient anxiety. This project’s primary implication is to counter these burdens by building awareness of appropriate TTE indications for PE among healthcare providers.
