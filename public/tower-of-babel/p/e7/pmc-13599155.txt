Digital gender violence and family honor coercion: cyberbullying and digital blackmail among Palestinian women
Authors: Bilal Hamamra, Ekrema Shehab
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCyberbullying and digital blackmail (ibtizaz) have emerged as serious threats to psychological well-being, especially for women in conservative and honor-based societies. In Palestine, these digital abuses intersect with patriarchal norms, cultural stigma, and the broader context of political occupation. While global research addresses cyberbullying’s psychological impact, there remains limited empirical insight into how these phenomena manifest in occupied Palestinian territories and affect women subject to collective codes of honor, shame, and silence.AimsThis study aims to fill a critical gap in the literature by examining the intersection of digital violence with Palestinian cultural and political contexts. Specifically, we explore Palestinian women’s experiences of cyberbullying and digital blackmail, with a focus on how these forms of violence are shaped by cultural, gendered, and colonial dynamics. We seek to identify psychological consequences, survival strategies, and systemic responses or lack thereof within this sociopolitical context.MethodsAnchored in a social constructionist and feminist epistemology, the study employed qualitative thematic analysis of 305 Arabic-language posts from two Palestinian Facebook groups. Data were translated, coded inductively, and analyzed for recurring themes. Rigorous coding procedures, intercoder reliability (ƙ = 0.937), and saturation protocols ensured analytic trustworthiness. Ethical considerations included anonymization and IRB approval.ResultsFour core themes were constructed through the analysis: [1] the continuum of gendered violence from physical/verbal abuse to digital surveillance; [2] sextortion as a mechanism of control leveraging patriarchal honor and shame codes; [3] individual and collective strategies of resistance and agency; and [4] the collective demand for institutional accountability and cultural reform. Findings show that digital abuse operates as a technological extension of pre-existing social controls, leading to self-reported anxiety, social isolation, and “social death” under threats of exposure. However, women actively resist these dynamics by creating protective digital networks, reclaiming agency through defiance, and framing digital abuse as a social crime rather than a private shame.ConclusionsCyberbullying and blackmail in Palestine are social processes of control situated within patriarchal norms and the broader socio-political instability of the occupation. The study’s novelty lies in its examination of how digital violence is shaped by family honor codes while being exacerbated by the lack of sovereign legal protections. Effective intervention requires culturally sensitive legal structures, public education, and accessible psychosocial services. Framing digital abuse as a form of gendered violence and social crime is essential for meaningful reform and protection of women’s digital rights and mental health in occupied and conservative contexts.

Introduction

Definition and prevalence of cyberbullying

The rapid expansion of digital technologies has reshaped the ways individuals, particularly adolescents and children, connect and communicate. While this era of unparalleled connectivity offers many benefits, it has also created new opportunities for harmful behaviors with cyberbullying emerging as a significant concern [5–7]. Unlike traditional bullying, cyberbullying transcends physical boundaries, allowing perpetrators to target victims continuously through digital platforms, creating a situation where there is no safe haven from harassment [8, 9]. A meta-analysis research by Kowalski et al. [10] examines studies from North America, Europe, and Australia and shows that cyberbullying affects between 10 and 40% of youth globally, with prevalence rates increasing proportionally with age and digital engagement.

Psychological impact of cyberbullying

The psychological impact of cyberbullying often exceeds that of traditional bullying due to several reasons: the potential audience witnessing the humiliation is larger; harmful content can be preserved indefinitely online, allowing for repeated victimization; and the perceived anonymity of digital platforms emboldens perpetrators to engage in more extreme behaviors than they might consider in face-to-face interactions [11–13]. In a study conducted across 28 U.S. states by Hinduja and Patchin [14], it was shown that cyberbullying victims are 2.57 times more likely to attempt suicide compared to non-victims, while experiencing significantly higher rates of depression (56% versus 28%) and anxiety disorders (49% versus 22%). The social consequences are equally devastating, with a Canadian study on the social behavior of school-aged children, showing 65% of victims reporting damaged relationships, increased isolation, and diminished trust in peers [15].

Cyberbullying and digital blackmail

Cyberbullying can escalate into blackmail when perpetrators employ compromising information or content to exert control over victims through repeated threats. This transition from harassment to extortion is a dangerous evolution where the power imbalance becomes further entrenched through explicit demands for compliance under duress [16, 17]. This often happens when cyberbullying escalates from mere harassment to a conditional threat, where the perpetrator demands a specific action or compliance from the victim, promising to prevent greater harm like the release of sensitive information or additional abuse, if their demands are fulfilled; it leaves the victim feeling trapped and isolated, with potentially severe and lasting consequences for their well-being and personal security [18–20]. The psychological impact of blackmailing is immense, fostering a constant state of anxiety, shame, and powerlessness. Wolak et al. [21] conducted a study at the University of New Hampshire in the United States, which indicates that approximately 5–7% of cyberbullying cases evolve into blackmail situations, with the prevalence increasing to 12% among older adolescents and young adults. The most common form, sextortion, where perpetrators threaten to distribute intimate images unless demands are met, affects an estimated 6% of internet users aged 15–25 across six European countries, with females comprising 70% of victims according to a multi-national study by Patchin and Hinduja [14].

Cyberbullying in the Arab context

Within the Arab world, the rapid adoption of digital technologies has been accompanied by concerning rates of cyberbullying and electronic blackmail or “ibtizaz,” shaped by cultural and social factors [22, 23]. Common forms include verbal harassment and impersonation, but a pernicious manifestation is “ibtizaz,” which often involves threats to release private information or intimate images unless demands, financial or sexual in nature (sextortion), are met [24, 25]. This form of cyber-blackmail preys on cultural values related to honor (sharaf), shame, and family reputation. In societies where these constructs are paramount, especially concerning female chastity and modesty, the threat of public exposure of any information deemed compromising can have devastating consequences, not only for the individual but for their entire family, potentially leading to severe social stigma, ostracization, and honor-related violence [26–28]. Ibtizaz is a troubling trend, with a study across five Arab countries (Egypt, Saudi Arabia, Jordan, Tunisia, and Morocco) by the Arab Center for Cyberspace Research [29] finding that 27% of young adults aged 18–29 had experienced some form of ibtizaz, with females comprising 61% of victims. The cultural context influences both the nature and impact of these experiences, with honor-based social structures amplifying the consequences of exposure. This is reflected in reporting behaviors, with only 23% of victims across these five Arab countries disclosing incidents to authorities or family members, compared to global reporting rates of approximately 40%, primarily due to fear of blame and social stigmatization. In Saudi Arabia, Alqahtani et al. [30] documented cyberbullying victimization rates of 32.6% among adolescents in Riyadh and Jeddah, with 20.2% admitting to perpetration, figures substantially higher than the global average of 15%. Similarly, in a study conducted by Al-Ketbi et al. [31] in Al Ain City in the United Arab Emirates, it was found that an overall bullying victimization rate of 37% among school students, with blackmailing particularly prevalent in private schools (37%) compared to public institutions (29%). In Kuwait, Al-Ansari et al. [32] reported that 38.5% of university students in Kuwait City had experienced cyberbullying, with significant correlations to psychological distress measures. Jordan presents equally concerning statistics, showing that 41% of university students in Amman experienced cyberbullying, resulting in measurable impacts on academic achievement and mental health [24, 26, 28, 33].

Gendered impact of cyberbullying

While cyberbullying affects all demographics, women experience unique vulnerabilities due to gendered power dynamics. Research indicates that women are more likely to experience sexualized forms of cyberbullying and blackmail, with severe psychological consequences [14, 21]. In patriarchal societies, these forms of digital violence intersect with existing gender inequalities, creating compounded vulnerabilities [22, 26]. The concept of “family honor” as described by Hasan [34] and El Saadawi [35] operates as a mechanism of social control that disproportionately impacts women, making them particularly susceptible to digital forms of coercion that threaten reputation and social standing. As Shalhoub-Kevorkian and Daher-Nashif [36] argue, these dynamics are further complicated in contexts of political conflict and occupation, where women’s bodies become sites of multiple forms of control.

Palestinian context: digital violence amidst occupation

For many young Palestinians living in the West Bank and Gaza, the internet and social media are not just tools for communication but vital lifelines for education, access to information, social connection, and self-expression, especially when physical movement and opportunities are curtailed by checkpoints, restrictions, and the broader socio-economic pressures stemming from the occupation [37–39]. This reliance on digital platforms increases their exposure and vulnerability to online risks. The chronic stress, political instability, and constant sense of insecurity characterizing daily life can exacerbate the perceived psychological impact of any form of bullying, making cyber-attacks feel particularly overwhelming [40–43]. In a study conducted by Khalaf-Salah and Aloul [44] on university students in Birzeit and An-Najah universities in the West Bank, it was demonstrated that 34.7% of the students had experienced some form of electronic blackmail, with 22.3% reporting that these incidents had significant negative effects on their academic performance and psychological well-being. The study further revealed that Facebook (55.8%) and Instagram (42.3%) were the primary platforms where such victimization occurred, with verbal cyberbullying being the most prevalent form. Ishtaiwa and Abuzant’s [45] investigation of 850 Palestinian university students across Gaza and the West Bank identified a troubling pattern where 17.6% reported being pressured to provide sensitive information or perform specific actions under threat of exposure, with 8.2% specifically mentioning politically motivated coercion.

The Palestinian context is a complex intersection of cyberbullying, blackmail, and cultural values of honor and shame, further complicated by the political reality of the Israeli occupation. The cultural emphasis on personal and familial honor means that threats of exposure, particularly those involving sensitive images or information that could be perceived as shameful (especially for young women, but also for men in different ways), carry immense coercive power [37–39, 43]. Louay Zreiqat, a police officer in the West Bank, reported that the incidence of cybercrime was higher among individuals aged 18 to 25 and the Palestinian police cybercrime unit handled 502 online crimes in 2016, 2568 cases in 2018, and 1478 in 2019 [46]. He noted that some of these cases involved private pictures of naked and semi-naked women; he also reported that some of the photos are not sexual, a photo of a girl not wearing a hijab could be scandalous; a man could use this photo to pressure the girl to send more photos [46]. This observation highlights how cultural norms around modesty and honor can be weaponized in cyberbullying and blackmail scenarios. The most disturbing is the documented use of cyber blackmail by the Israeli occupation as a coercive strategy to recruit Palestinian informants or collaborators. Credible reports have detailed how Israel exploits its near-total control over the lives of Palestinians to blackmail some into collaboration [42, 47, 48]. One particularly troubling case involves a Palestinian man, who identifies himself as Zuhair Ghalith, and explains that he was blackmailed using a video showing him in a sexual relationship with another Palestinian man, who Ghalith says, was an agent of Israel, implying that he was set up. Under threat of exposure, which would have severe social consequences in a conservative society, Ghalith agreed to spy on resistance fighters for the Israeli army and was later executed by Palestinian fighters [48].

Research objectives and theoretical framework

This study addresses critical gaps in existing literature, including the limited empirical research on digital violence among Palestinian women and the insufficient understanding of how cyberbullying intersects with family honor codes under political occupation.

Our research is anchored in feminist theory and cultural studies, drawing on the work of Palestinian scholars such as Shalhoub-Kevorkian [36, 49], who documents how violence against women is shaped by intersecting systems of patriarchy and colonial occupation. We also utilize the frameworks of El Saadawi [35] and Hasan [34] to analyze how “honor” operates as a mechanism of social control in Arab societies. By applying these lenses, we aim to uncover how Palestinian women negotiate meanings around digital abuse, emphasizing voice and agency in contexts often marked by silence and stigma.

To address these objectives, the study seeks to answer the following research questions:

Methods

While thematic analysis is a well-established qualitative method, its use here enables the study to examine sensitive digital narratives in a conflict-affected and high-stigma environment where direct disclosure may expose women to social risk. By applying this framework to Palestinian women’s online accounts of cyberbullying and digital blackmail, the study documents experiences that often remain hidden because of honor-related repercussions, fear of exposure, and the physical and institutional constraints produced by occupation. This study adopts a social constructionist epistemology, which understands knowledge and meaning as produced through social interactions and language [50, 51]. This perspective is particularly suitable for examining the experiences of cyberbullying and blackmail within Palestinian society, where social norms around honour, shame, and gender intersect with digital technologies to shape the lived realities of women [43]. Anchored in feminist theory and cultural studies, our methodological approach aims to uncover how Palestinian women construct and negotiate meanings around digital abuse, emphasizing voice and agency in contexts often marked by silence and stigma [50, 51].

The analysis employs qualitative thematic analysis, a flexible and rigorous method that facilitates the identification of patterns and themes within textual data, capturing both explicit and latent meanings [50, 52]. This approach enables a detailed exploration of participants’ narratives, focusing on linguistic repetitions, culturally specific terminologies, metaphoric language, and narrative transitions to reveal underlying psychological and sociocultural dynamics [51, 52]. Such a methodology reflects our interest in foregrounding participant voices and contextualizing digital abuse within the broader Palestinian socio-political landscape. The study utilizes a codebook-driven approach to thematic analysis [53]. This methodological choice allows for the combined use of social constructionist theory and structured coding to ensure consistency across the large dataset. While reflexive thematic analysis typically avoids statistical reliability, our use of intercoder validation (κ = 0.937) serves as a measure of ‘communicative validity.’ This ensures that the researchers’ independent interpretations are verified through dialogue to remain grounded in the Palestinian context. This strategy supports rigorous data organization while preserving a feminist focus on the subjective meanings shared by the participants. This approach adheres to principles of active theme development, acknowledging that themes do not merely exist in the data to be found but are constructed by researchers through a process of interpretation [50, 53–55].

Researcher positionality

The researchers are two Palestinian professors of English from the West Bank of Palestine. Their shared cultural and linguistic background provided insight into the participants’ social realities, while their academic training ensured analytical rigor and critical reflection throughout the research process. Both researchers are fluent in Palestinian Arabic and academic English, which enabled precise translation, interpretation, and contextual understanding of the participants’ narratives. Throughout the research process, we maintained reflexive journals to document how our own experiences with digital spaces and cultural understandings of honor might influence our analysis. This reflexive practice is essential for ensuring trustworthiness in qualitative research, particularly when examining sensitive topics [50, 52].

Participants and data collection

Data were collected from two prominent Palestinian Facebook groups—Ask the Girls of Palestine and Strong Independent Women—over a period from December 2, 2024, to February 2, 2025. These groups were selected because they represent two of the largest online communities specifically for Palestinian women, with membership exceeding 50,000 each. The groups provide essential virtual forums for sharing personal accounts of cyberbullying, sextortion, and electronic blackmail [26, 43].

Participant demographic data, such as age, relationship status, and geographic location, were systematically recorded when such information was explicitly stated within the posts or visible on the users’ group profiles. To maintain data integrity, researchers only included specific demographic identifiers that were voluntarily shared by the users in their narratives or profile bios. This information was then cross-referenced with the content of the posts to ensure context-specific accuracy before the final anonymization process.

The final sample consisted of 50 unique women ranging in age from 16 to 42 years (M = 26.5, SD = 6.4). Geographic distribution included participants from Gaza (18%), Hebron (16%), Nablus (14%), Ramallah (12%), Jenin (10%), Bethlehem (10%), and various other districts (20%). Regarding relationship status, 44% were single, 32% were married, 14% were engaged, and 10% were divorced. Occupational status showed that 40% were students, 30% were employed in professional roles, and 30% were homemakers.

The analytic process followed the principle of data saturation, conceptualized here as informational redundancy within the fixed digital corpus. This state was achieved when the analysis of the final segments of the dataset yielded no new conceptual insights or modifications to the established codebook [56]. Rather than an iterative recruitment process typical of interviews, saturation was determined through a systematic review of the final 55 posts. After the core themes were constructed from the initial 250 units, the remaining data were examined to verify that they did not contradict or significantly expand the existing categories. This exhaustive review of the full dataset ensured that the constructed themes accurately represented the collective experiences recorded in the digital threads.

The analytic dataset comprised 305 distinct units of analysis, authored by these 50 unique contributors. Each unit consisted of either an original post or a narrative comment; comments were treated as independent data points only when they provided a unique personal account or significant experiential detail. To address potential bias from repeated contributions, multiple posts from the same individual were aggregated into single narrative cases, ensuring thematic representation without over-representing any single voice. Authenticity was verified by cross-referencing details within and across threads, and any duplicate or promotional content was manually identified and removed to maintain empirical integrity.

Data analysis and trustworthiness

A targeted search strategy employed culturally and contextually relevant Arabic keywords such as “التنمر الإلكتروني” (cyberbullying), “الابتزاز الإلكتروني” (electronic blackmail), “الفضيحة” (exposure), and “الشرف” (honour). These were iteratively refined based on initial reviews to ensure sociocultural appropriateness [51].

The lead researcher conducted the primary translation, while the second author performed the cross-checking and back-translation to verify linguistic consistency. Subsequently, the independent native Arabic speaker, who is a specialist in translation studies, compared the translated quotes against the original Arabic texts. This reviewer suggested minor adjustments to capture specific Palestinian colloquialisms and emotional intensity more accurately, which the authors reconciled through collaborative discussion until reaching a consensus on the final English phrasing.

Using an inductive thematic analysis, the research team conducted open and axial coding to deconstruct the translated texts into overarching themes such as psychological distress and resistance strategies [51, 57]. The analysis was supported by NVivo 12 software, which facilitated efficient management of the large dataset and enabled tracking of theme development across the corpus. To ensure reliability, multiple coders independently coded the dataset and achieved a high intercoder agreement of 97% (ƙ = 0.937, p < .001), resolving any interpretive discrepancies through consensus discussion, with final arbitration by the lead author where necessary [50].

Ethical considerations

Given the sensitive nature of cyberbullying and electronic blackmail experiences, stringent ethical protocols were implemented. All identifying information including usernames, profile pictures, and personal signatures were removed prior to analysis to protect participant anonymity and confidentiality [58]. The research team obtained formal permission from the administrators of the Facebook groups to use anonymized data for scholarly inquiry.

After receiving approval from the administrators of both networks, the researchers issued a clear statement on both social networks informing the community that a study would be conducted on publicly available posts. The statement provided information about the study’s purpose, procedures, and confidentiality measures. The study utilized a passive consent model, which is a recognized procedure in social media research when obtaining individual active consent from hundreds of contributors is not feasible [59]. Members were provided a two-week period to request the exclusion of their posts; no such requests were received. To prevent re-identification, we applied ‘light disguise’ techniques to the data. This involved modifying non-essential biographical details and slightly altering the phrasing of quotes to ensure they could not be traced back to original profiles via search engine queries, while strictly maintaining the participants’ original meaning [59] This approach aligns with ethical guidelines for using publicly available social media data in research [58].

The study adhered to ethical standards articulated by the American Psychological Association (APA, 2010) [58] and secured approval from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of An-Najah National University. Throughout the research process, particular attention was devoted to respectful and accurate representation of participants’ narratives, acknowledging the potential repercussions of exposure in honour-based social contexts and ensuring the centrality of victims’ voices in analysis. The ethical challenges of using publicly available social media data were carefully considered, including the potential for re-identification even with anonymization and the importance of contextualizing quotes to avoid misrepresentation. As recommended by Townsend and Wallace [59], we implemented additional safeguards such as slightly modifying some details in quotes that could potentially identify participants while preserving the essential meaning and emotional content.

Results

The analysis identifies four central themes: (1) The Continuum of Gendered Violence: From Embodied Control to Digital Surveillance; (2) Sextortion and the Social Weight of Honor; (3) Resistance and Agency: Breaking the Cycle of Silence; and (4) Institutional and Cultural Reform: Toward Structural Accountability. These categories demonstrate that digital abuse acts as an extension of pre-existing social controls, leading to psychological distress and social isolation. However, participants also use these digital spaces to challenge the systems that enforce their silence.

The continuum of gendered violence: from embodied control to digital surveillance

This theme conceptualizes violence as a continuous and evolving system of gendered control that extends from childhood into adulthood, shifting across physical, verbal, and digital domains. Participants’ narratives demonstrate that early experiences of familial abuse—particularly physical punishment and verbal degradation—play a formative role in shaping long-term psychological vulnerability. As one participant from Hebron (age 28, married) stated, “I prayed so many times in school that I would just die because of how hard I was hit, especially by those closest to me—my parents and siblings.” This account highlights how violence within intimate family structures becomes normalized, embedding fear and distress at an early stage.

These early experiences are not isolated but form part of a broader continuum in which verbal humiliation contributes to the internalization of inferiority and self-blame. A participant from Nablus (age 22, single) reflected this process, stating, “He used to insult me every day, call me a dog and a burden. I started to believe I deserved it.” This illustrates how repeated verbal abuse erodes self-worth and reshapes self-perception, reinforcing compliance and silence. The power of these insults lies in their linguistic function to construct the female subject as a non-subject. By using animalistic labels, the perpetrator performs a social exclusion that precedes the actual digital threat. This linguistic strategy creates a reality where the woman exists only in relation to the perpetrator’s judgment. Digital technology reconfigures this process by making the victim’s body a permanent digital record, available for constant inspection. This shift from physical control to data-based surveillance changes the nature of policing, as the perpetrator no longer needs to be physically present to exert authority over the victim’s life.

Rather than existing as separate phenomena, offline and online abuses operate as mutually reinforcing mechanisms within the same patriarchal structure. The transition to digital spaces intensifies these dynamics by introducing permanence, reach, and the threat of public exposure. A participant from Gaza (age 19, university student) described this escalation, stating, “I received a message saying, ‘We have your photos. Comply or be exposed to your whole family and village.’ I couldn’t breathe.” This account reflects how digital harassment transforms violence into a form of constant surveillance and psychological entrapment, where fear is both immediate and enduring.

Importantly, both offline and online forms of violence are sustained by honour-based social norms and collective enforcement mechanisms that discourage disclosure and reinforce silence. The threat of reputational damage extends the impact of abuse beyond the individual to the family and community, intensifying psychological pressure and limiting avenues for resistance.

Taken together, these findings underscore that gender-based violence is not episodic but cumulative and structurally embedded, generating enduring self-reported distress such as narratively constructed anxiety, internalized self-doubt, and persistent fear. The narratives of women in Gaza and the West Bank indicate that digital surveillance serves as a technological proxy for the physical movement restrictions of the occupation. When digital access is threatened, the victim loses her only path for education and social contact, making the digital threat an extension of the physical siege. The movement from embodied violence to digitally mediated surveillance reveals how evolving forms of abuse remain anchored in longstanding inequalities, calling for analytical approaches that capture both continuity and transformation across contexts.

Sextortion and the social weight of honor

This theme examines how sextortion operates at the intersection of sexual exploitation and honour-based social regulation, where perpetrators weaponize deeply embedded cultural values to enforce compliance and silence. Participants’ accounts demonstrate that sextortion is not only a form of digital abuse but a socially amplified mechanism of control, in which threats gain power through their alignment with patriarchal norms surrounding female chastity and family reputation.

A key tactic involves grooming and psychological manipulation, particularly targeting younger women’s need for validation within restrictive social environments. As one participant from Gaza (age 16, high school student) explained, “She used to send me messages telling me that I am beautiful and my body is beautiful… and this was the beginning of her work… which was giving me confidence that I am older and a beautiful girl.” This process illustrates how perpetrators strategically build trust and self-worth before shifting to coercion, exploiting emotional vulnerability as an entry point into abuse.

The coercive power of sextortion is sustained by the credible threat of honour-based consequences, including familial violence and social exclusion. A participant from Nablus (age 19, university student) articulated this fear starkly: “By God, if the pictures come out, my brothers will kill me or rename me… and no one will believe me.” Such accounts reveal how the success of blackmail relies not only on the possession of images, but on the collective enforcement of honour codes, where the perceived loss of reputation can carry life-threatening implications. In this context, control is achieved through fear that extends beyond the individual to the entire family structure.

Reputation thus functions as a powerful instrument of coercion, transforming digital content into tools of social domination. This dynamic is not limited to unmarried women but also affects those within socially sanctioned relationships. A participant from Ramallah (age 32, mother of three) described the violation of marital privacy, stating, “I received a video of me and my husband during our intimate time… filmed without our knowledge… They demanded 100,000 shekels or they would release it.” This example highlights the invasive reach of sextortion, where even legitimate intimacy becomes a site of exploitation, reinforcing the idea that women’s bodies remain subject to surveillance and control regardless of marital status.

The cultural constructs of sharaf (honour) and ’ar (shame) operate as mechanisms of social regulation, shifting the burden of the crime onto the victim and discouraging disclosure. Women’s testimonies indicate that fear of stigma, disbelief, and familial repercussions often leads to forced silence and compliance, as victims prioritize protecting their families over their own safety. The discursive power of “exposure” relies on a shared cultural vocabulary where the visual image is instantly translated into a moral failure. Technology reconfigures patriarchal control by removing the geographical boundaries of the local community; the “village” is now a global digital network. This means the social death resulting from a leak is no longer localized but becomes an absolute, borderless state. The ability to edit and redistribute images allows perpetrators to invent new “shameful” realities, showing how technology does not just replicate old habits but provides new ways to fabricate and enforce female guilt through the creation of digital evidence. This reflects a form of internalized social control, where women self-regulate their behaviour under the constant threat of exposure.

The perceived psychological impacts encompass self-reported anxiety, feelings of detachment, and social withdrawal, but these effects are inseparable from the broader cultural context in which they occur. As Shalhoub-Kevorkian [36] notes, such forms of digital violence represent contemporary extensions of patriarchal control, while Hasan [34] highlights how honour-based systems function to regulate women’s sexuality and maintain existing power hierarchies. Collectively, these accounts illuminate sextortion as a structurally embedded form of coercion sustained by the interplay between technological affordances and socio-cultural norms. Its force lies in mobilizing honour and shame as mechanisms of control, entrapping victims within cycles of fear, silence, and social isolation that extend beyond the digital encounter. The threat of blackmail is further complicated by the political context, where the fear of being shamed is often connected to the fear of being accused of political collaboration. Participants express that the lack of a sovereign legal system makes them more susceptible to abusers who use the political insecurity of the region to enforce personal compliance.

Resistance and agency: breaking the cycle of silence

This theme captures how women navigate and resist sextortion within highly restrictive socio-cultural contexts, demonstrating forms of agency that challenge both digital coercion and patriarchal expectations. While participants operate under severe constraints, their accounts reveal that resistance is not absent but instead emerges as strategic, embodied, and collective practices.

One key form of resistance is explicit refusal, which represents significant shift in narratively constructed emotional states from compliance to self-assertion. A participant from Hebron (age 23, university student) articulated this defiance, stating, “I told him, do whatever you want, but I will never send another picture. Even if I die, I will not obey you.” This refusal disrupts the coercive logic of sextortion, rejecting both the authority of the perpetrator and the broader expectation that women must sacrifice their autonomy to preserve honour.

Beyond individual defiance, participants also described collective and preventative forms of agency, particularly through digital spaces. A woman from Nablus (age 28, employed) explained, “I created new accounts and started warning other girls. We need to protect each other because no one else will.” This highlights a shift from isolation to community-based resistance, where women transform personal trauma into shared knowledge and protective networks, compensating for the absence of institutional support.

However, acts of resistance remain deeply precarious, shaped by the persistent threat of honour-based repercussions. A participant from Gaza (age 25, unmarried) reflected this tension, stating, “If the pictures come out, I will be finished. Not just socially—I mean physically. There will be no place for me.” This account underscores how agency operates within conditions of extreme risk, where resistance does not eliminate danger but instead reflects a negotiation between survival, dignity, and fear.

Importantly, breaking silence is not only an act but also a political and social challenge to dominant norms. Participants’ calls for accountability and systemic change indicate a rejection of victim-blaming narratives and a demand for structural protection. In this sense, resistance extends beyond individual coping to encompass collective re-framing of abuse as a social crime rather than a private shame.

Psychologically, these acts of defiance contribute to processes of reclaiming identity and restoring agency, even within constrained environments. This aligns with the findings of Alnabilsy, Pagorek-Eshel, and Elias [60], which emphasize how resistance and social support can enable survivors to reconstruct their sense of self.

In sum, this theme highlights that, despite operating within restrictive environments, women engage in nuanced and context-dependent forms of resistance that contest both technological abuse and the socio-cultural structures underpinning it. These practices signal not only survival but also the reassertion of agency and the gradual reconfiguration of dominant norms.

Institutional and cultural reform: toward structural accountability

This theme reflects a collective demand for systemic transformation, as participants highlight the failure of legal, institutional, and technological systems to adequately address sextortion and digital violence. Women’s narratives position these forms of abuse not as isolated incidents, but as structural problems requiring coordinated legal and cultural responses.

A central concern is the inadequacy of existing legal frameworks and their implementation. Participants emphasized that current cybercrime laws fail to account for the gendered nature of digital violence, while institutional responses often reinforce victim-blaming. A participant from Hebron (age 35, lawyer) explained, “Our laws are not prepared for this kind of violence. The police don’t know how to handle digital blackmail cases, and often they blame the victims.” This highlights a dual failure of both legislation and enforcement, leaving women without effective protection or recourse. The data suggest that this institutional failure is partly a result of the fragmented legal system in the West Bank and Gaza, where jurisdictional limits between Area A, B, and C prevent local authorities from pursuing perpetrators who cross these administrative boundaries. This legal vacuum, established by the conditions of the occupation, ensures that individual abusers can exploit the lack of state sovereignty to avoid accountability, effectively turning the political map into a tool for personal coercion.

Beyond legal gaps, participants stressed that cultural norms surrounding honour and shame continue to enable abuse by shifting responsibility onto victims. A woman from Ramallah (age 26, teacher) articulated this structural vulnerability, stating, “We need to change how we talk about honor and shame. As long as a woman’s worth is tied to her ‘purity,’ she will be vulnerable to this kind of blackmail.” This perspective underscores that legal reforms alone are insufficient without addressing the social ideologies that sustain coercion and silence.

Participants also identified technological platforms as critical sites of accountability, pointing to the limited responsiveness of social media companies in cases of digital abuse. A participant from Jenin (age 31, IT professional) noted, “Facebook and Instagram need to take more responsibility. When we report blackmail, they should respond immediately and remove the content. Instead, they tell us it doesn’t violate their community standards.” This reflects broader concerns about the misalignment between platform policies and the lived realities of gender-based digital violence, particularly in contexts where exposure carries severe social and physical consequences.

In addition to legal and technological reform, women emphasized the need for culturally sensitive support systems, including accessible mental health services and protection mechanisms that account for the intersection of digital and familial threats. These demands highlight the necessity of a holistic response framework that integrates prevention, protection, and survivor-centered care.

Overall, this theme demonstrates that addressing sextortion requires more than punitive measures; it calls for a multi-layered transformation encompassing legal reform, cultural change, platform accountability, and contextually grounded support services. Without such integrated efforts, existing inequalities will continue to shape both women’s vulnerability to abuse and their limited access to justice.

Discussion

The findings show that cyberbullying and digital blackmail against Palestinian women operate through the intersection of online harassment, family honor coercion, communal judgment, and restricted legal protection under occupation. This intersection gives digital abuse a social force that exceeds individual online harassment, because exposure can activate family discipline, communal stigma, and restricted access to institutional protection. Existing cyberbullying scholarship has largely examined online abuse through peer aggression, adolescent vulnerability, psychological distress, school climate, platform use, and digital risk behavior [10, 11, 13–15]. The present findings correspond with this literature in documenting fear, isolation, self-blame, damaged trust, and persistent distress. However, the Palestinian material shows that psychological harm is intensified when online exposure is translated into family dishonor, communal judgment, and possible social exclusion. Digital abuse therefore operates through two registers at once: the immediate threat issued by the perpetrator and the anticipated disciplinary response of kinship and community.

The findings also show how digital technologies become new venues for pre-existing patriarchal control mechanisms rather than entirely new forms of abuse. Whereas studies in Western nations, such as those by Kowalski et al. [10] and Hinduja and Patchin [14], emphasize cyberbullying as an individualistic psychological struggle, our findings highlight that Palestinian victims experience the digital realm as a collective site of communal judgment. This difference is central to the interpretation of the data. In much cyberbullying research, harm is measured through individual psychological outcomes; in the Palestinian narratives, harm is also produced through the social meaning attached to exposure. A leaked image, rumor, message, or fabricated screenshot does not remain a private injury. It can become a family event, a reputational crisis, and a mechanism through which women are disciplined by the imagined gaze of relatives, neighbors, and future marital networks. Wolak et al. [21] conceptualize sextortion through coercion, offender behavior, youth vulnerability, and the threat of distributing intimate material. The present findings support this framework while showing how sextortion acquires a distinct cultural force in the Palestinian context. Here, perpetrators do not rely only on possession of private material; they also rely on honor-based social organization, where women may be blamed for the abuse committed against them and where exposure can become a family and community crisis. Sextortion therefore operates not only as interpersonal coercion, but also as a technique of obedience, silence, and reputational containment.

The Arab Center for Cyberspace Research [29] examined electronic blackmail across Egypt, Saudi Arabia, Jordan, Tunisia, and Morocco and reported that women were disproportionately affected, with fear of stigma, blame, and social consequences reducing disclosure. The present findings resemble this regional pattern because Palestinian women also describe shame, silence, and fear of exposure as central mechanisms of coercion. The comparison with [29] therefore concerns the social logic of electronic blackmail, not quantitative prevalence. In the Palestinian case, this logic is intensified by legal fragmentation, restricted policing capacity, and the broader condition of Israeli occupation.

The analysis also shows how digital technology reconfigures patriarchal control through “platformed honor.” In this system, social media groups and algorithms act as automated judges of female conduct, remapping traditional policing onto the digital architecture. This ensures that the surveillance of women is a constant feature of the online experience. Furthermore, the language used in threats acts as a speech act that establishes the right of the abuser to dictate the social status of the victim. This finding extends Hasan’s [34] account of honor as a mechanism of patriarchal regulation by showing how honor is now mediated through screenshots, fake accounts, private messages, archived images, algorithmic visibility, and platform-based circulation. Honor is no longer enforced only through family supervision, neighborhood gossip, or direct social control. It is reproduced through the technical capacity to copy, store, edit, forward, and recirculate material beyond its original context. Digital platforms therefore alter the scale, speed, and durability of honor-based judgment.

The findings further show that the perceived psychological impact of digital violence is intensified by honor-based social structures and political instability. Previous studies link cyberbullying with anxiety, depression, psychosomatic distress, damaged relationships, reduced trust, and social withdrawal [11, 13–15]. The participants’ narratives support these associations, but they also indicate that distress is culturally and politically mediated. Fear is not limited to the perpetrator’s next message; it extends to the possible reaction of brothers, parents, husbands, neighbors, employers, and future spouses. Psychological harm is therefore inseparable from the social meanings attached to the threatened image, message, or rumor. The findings also show that women’s resistance strategies and calls for systemic change challenge the privatization of shame and shift digital blackmail into the register of public accountability. These strategies challenge victim-blaming readings of cyberbullying and blackmail [28]. Women in the corpus warn others, refuse further compliance, create protective accounts, seek advice, and reframe digital blackmail as a public crime rather than a private shame. Resistance appears here as collective knowledge-making, not only as individual coping.

While our dataset primarily documents interpersonal and community-based violence, the findings must be understood as occurring within the specific background of the Israeli occupation. The occupation serves as a structural amplifier of digital violence, not necessarily through direct state involvement in the documented cases, but by creating a legal and social setting that weakens the ability of the Palestinian state to protect its citizens. This political context turns local disputes into high-stakes crises, as women are often unable to seek formal justice due to the restricted reach of local law enforcement. This point clarifies the relationship between the empirical data and the political argument. The study does not claim that occupation forces directly produced the interpersonal cases analyzed here. It shows that occupation structures the legal and institutional environment in which women seek protection, perpetrators assess risk, and families interpret exposure. In this sense, occupation operates as a condition of vulnerability rather than as a direct actor in every case.

Interpretation of honor, shame, and platformed surveillance

Our findings support and extend feminist social constructionist theory by demonstrating how digital violence operates as both a reflection and reinforcement of existing gender power structures. This study shows that digital violence is a discursive process that actively produces honor-based power. The language used in these threats acts as a speech act that establishes the perpetrator’s right to define the victim’s social status. Furthermore, technology reconfigures patriarchal structures through “platformed honor,” where social media algorithms and groups become the new judges of female conduct. This automation of social policing suggests that patriarchy is being remapped onto the digital architecture, making the surveillance of women a constant feature of the online experience. By analyzing these discursive systems, the research moves beyond descriptive accounts to show how digital spaces actively construct new realities of gendered control. The testimonies reveal how honor, shame, and reputation function as discursive mechanisms of control that operate through both cultural and technological means. This aligns with Hasan’s [34] analysis of honor as a political tool that maintains patriarchal power, while extending it to digital contexts. The findings also support Shalhoub-Kevorkian’s [36, 49] work on how violence against Palestinian women is shaped by intersecting systems of patriarchy and colonial occupation, showing how these dynamics manifest in digital spaces. The interpretive point is that digital spaces do not simply mirror patriarchal society. The data show that digital platforms reorganize patriarchal power by making reputational judgment faster, wider, and harder to contain. This shifts honor from a local code of family regulation into a platformed mechanism of surveillance, where the possibility of circulation itself becomes a disciplinary force.

The findings also clarify how political instability shapes the psychological impact of digital violence. The chronic stress and insecurity created by the occupation appear to exacerbate the perceived psychological impact of cyberbullying, creating a cumulative sense of self-reported distress. This supports the findings of Veronese et al. [38] on how political conflict compounds the impact of violence on mental health, while extending it to digital forms of violence. Furthermore, our results indicate that sextortion acts as a tool for social regulation, an observation that expands on the work of Wolak et al. [21]. While Wolak et al. identified sextortion as a behavioral issue among youth, our data suggest that in the Palestinian context, this is a calculated attack on the victim’s social standing. The results indicate that victims are not passive recipients of this pressure; their strategies of resistance—such as creating private support networks—show a proactive attempt to negate the social power of the blackmailer. This suggests a shift from the victim-blaming paradigms observed in earlier regional studies, such as those by Al-Badayneh et al. [28], toward an active reclaiming of digital autonomy. This comparison shows that the same digital act can carry different social meanings across contexts. In less honor-saturated settings, sextortion may be framed mainly as criminal coercion, sexual exploitation, or psychological abuse. In the Palestinian narratives analyzed here, it also becomes a threat to kinship standing, marriageability, family safety, and social belonging. This contextual difference explains why women may remain silent even when they identify themselves as victims. Silence is not evidence of passivity; it is often a survival strategy within a punitive social field.

Political context, legal fragmentation, and protection gaps

The Palestinian context adds unique dimensions to digital violence that merit critical analysis. While the qualitative material focuses on interpersonal blackmail, the background of occupation provides the conditions that make these threats effective. In this setting, the threat of social exposure is more than a personal scandal; it is a potential threat to the security of the victim and her family. Even when the perpetrator is a private individual, the lack of a unified legal authority allows these crimes to go unpunished. Therefore, the occupation is a primary contextual driver that restricts the protection of women’s digital rights by maintaining a state of administrative and legal fragmentation. This analysis ensures the study recognizes the political reality without overextending the empirical data to claim direct state participation in the specific interpersonal cases reported. The fragmentation of the West Bank into administrative zones prevents local police from pursuing abusers across borders, creating a legal vacuum that individual perpetrators exploit. This legal vacuum gives digital blackmail a specific coercive advantage. Perpetrators can threaten exposure while victims remain uncertain about whether police, courts, families, or platforms will protect them. The result is a protection gap in which the victim faces immediate social consequences, while the perpetrator may remain anonymous, mobile, or beyond effective institutional reach.

While researchers like Khalaf-Salah and Aloul [44] highlighted the frequency of these attacks, they did not fully account for how the political status of the territory prevents judicial recourse. Our findings show that the inability of local authorities to operate effectively across jurisdictional boundaries (Areas A, B, and C) provides a shield for perpetrators. This distinguishes the Palestinian case from European or American contexts, where legal systems offer at least a theoretical path for resolution. Consequently, the struggle of Palestinian women to find justice is not merely a failure of local policing, but a result of the broader political restrictions that limit state capacity to enforce cybercrime legislation. The comparison with Khalaf-Salah and Aloul [44] is therefore not only empirical but interpretive. Their work establishes that electronic blackmail is present among Palestinian university students; the present study explains how gender, honor, and fragmented sovereignty shape the meaning and consequences of such abuse for women.

The occupation serves as the structural background that limits the availability of state protection and complicates the legal status of victims. Technology reconfigures patriarchal control by removing geographical boundaries; the social death resulting from a leak is no longer localized but becomes an absolute state. The discursive power of “exposure” relies on a cultural vocabulary where a visual image is translated into a moral failure. This remapping of traditional policing onto digital architecture makes the surveillance of women a constant feature of the online experience. It reconfigures digital violence by creating a context of lawlessness where women cannot easily seek justice without risking further social or political suspicion. The Israeli occupation’s control over digital infrastructure creates additional vulnerabilities, as documented in cases where occupation forces have used digital blackmail to coerce collaboration [42, 47, 48]. This political dimension of digital violence represents a form of colonial power that operates through gendered means. As Shalhoub-Kevorkian and Daher-Nashif [36] argue, femicide and other forms of violence against Palestinian women cannot be understood outside the context of colonial occupation. Our findings extend this analysis to digital violence, showing how occupation and patriarchy intersect to create unique forms of gendered coercion. The cases of politically motivated digital blackmail cited in previous literature [42, 47, 48] are analytically distinct from the interpersonal narratives in this dataset, but they reveal the wider coercive ecology in which exposure can be weaponized. Across both domains, the threatened disclosure of intimate or compromising material gains force from the same social grammar of shame, suspicion, and reputational injury.

Digital platforms, resistance, and structural response

At the same time, digital platforms offer Palestinian women spaces for connection and resistance that are otherwise constrained by physical restrictions on movement. This duality of digital spaces as both sites of violence and resistance reflects the complex nature of technology in occupied contexts. While our study focused primarily on the negative aspects of digital violence, future research should explore how Palestinian women leverage digital platforms for empowerment and community building. The data show that platforms operate as ambivalent infrastructures. They expose women to surveillance, blackmail, and reputational harm, yet they also allow women to seek advice, warn others, build solidarity, and refuse the privatization of shame. Participants’ calls for legal reform, platform accountability, public education, and psychosocial support indicate that women locate the problem beyond individual perpetrators. They define digital blackmail as a structural issue requiring legal, cultural, technological, and mental health responses. This reframing is one of the clearest forms of agency in the dataset: women transform blackmail from a private scandal into a public question of protection, accountability, and gender justice.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several limitations that warrant consideration. First, the reliance on data sourced exclusively from two Facebook groups limits the scope to women who are digitally connected and willing to share their experiences publicly or semi-publicly, potentially excluding more marginalized individuals lacking internet access or those unwilling to disclose sensitive information due to fear of stigma. Second, the use of self-reported social media posts may introduce bias, as narratives could be influenced by social desirability or performative aspects inherent in online platforms. Third, the cross-sectional nature of data collection restricts the ability to examine changes over time or causality in psychological outcomes. Finally, the study’s focus on Palestinian women’s experiences in specific regions may limit generalizability to other Arab or global contexts where cultural and political dynamics differ. An additional limitation relates to the challenge of verifying the authenticity of online posts. While we took steps to ensure the credibility of the data, such as cross-referencing similar accounts and excluding posts that appeared fabricated, the nature of social media data inherently presents challenges for verification.

Despite these limitations, the study is supported by several methodological strengths. First, the large sample size of 305 posts provides rich, diverse perspectives on digital violence. Second, the rigorous thematic analysis process, including intercoder reliability and saturation protocols, ensures the trustworthiness of the findings. Third, the study centers Palestinian women’s voices, addressing a significant gap in the literature. Fourth, the feminist social constructionist framework allows for nuanced analysis of how digital violence intersects with cultural and political structures. Finally, the study provides important insights for developing culturally appropriate interventions to address digital violence.

Recommendations for future research

Based on our findings, we recommend several directions for future research. First, longitudinal studies are needed to examine the long-term psychological impact of digital violence on Palestinian women. Second, comparative research across different Arab contexts could illuminate how cultural variations influence experiences of digital violence. Third, research on effective interventions is urgently needed, including legal reforms, educational programs, and support services. Fourth, studies should explore how digital platforms can be leveraged for resistance and community building, not just as sites of violence. Finally, research should examine how digital violence intersects with other forms of gender-based violence in Palestinian society.

Recommendations for intervention

Our findings suggest several recommendations for addressing digital violence against Palestinian women. First, legal reforms are needed to specifically criminalize digital blackmail and sextortion, with enforcement mechanisms that hold perpetrators accountable. Second, educational programs should be developed in schools and communities to raise awareness about digital violence and promote gender-equitable norms. Third, mental health services must be developed that are culturally sensitive to the unique contexts of Palestinian women. Fourth, tech companies should improve their responses to gender-based violence on their platforms, with culturally appropriate content moderation. Finally, community-based interventions should support women’s resistance strategies while addressing the cultural norms that enable digital violence.

Conclusion

This study illuminates the complex nature of cyberbullying and blackmail experienced by Palestinian women, revealing a deeply entrenched system where digital abuse intersects with patriarchal honor codes, social stigma, and political occupation. The findings demonstrate that bullying and blackmail are mechanisms of social control that inflict psychological erosion, silence victims, and reinforce gendered power imbalances. Women’s posts expose how cyber harassment transcends physical boundaries to create an omnipresent digital terror that invades mental and emotional spaces, producing self-reported anxiety, isolation, and narratively constructed emotional states. The cultural significance of honor and shame amplifies these harms, as victims face not only the threat of exposure but the potential for familial violence and social ostracization, effectively rendering them invisible or socially dead. The study also shows that honor is transformed from a local code of family regulation into a platformed mechanism of surveillance, where the possibility of circulation itself becomes a disciplinary force.

Despite these oppressive dynamics, Palestinian women exhibit remarkable resilience and agency. Their stories of resistance, survival, and proactive measures highlight the importance of empowerment and community support in mitigating harm. Yet, these efforts are precarious and underscore critical gaps in institutional responses, where inadequate legal protection and prevailing cultural norms hinder disclosure and justice. Addressing cyberbullying and electronic blackmail in this context demands holistic, culturally informed strategies that integrate legal reform, educational initiatives, and accessible psychosocial support. Recognizing these abuses as social crimes necessitates collective responsibility and systemic change to dismantle the cycles of violence rooted in patriarchal and colonial structures. These findings speak to policymakers, practitioners, and scholars concerned with digital rights, gender justice, legal protection, and survivor-centered psychosocial support in Palestine and comparable sociocultural environments.
