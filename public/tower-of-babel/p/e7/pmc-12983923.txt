Prevalence of ABO and Rh blood groups among individuals attending a tertiary care hospital in Nepal: a retrospective cross-sectional study
Authors: Arun Bahadur Chand, Bindu Sen, Jenish Khadka, Shreya Pandey, Bipin Nepal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectiveUnderstanding the regional blood group distribution is essential for safe transfusion practice and efficient blood bank inventory management, as local variations in the ABO and Rh blood group systems guide donor recruitment and minimise shortages and transfusion incompatibilities. This study aimed to determine the prevalence and distribution of ABO and Rh blood groups among individuals attending a tertiary care hospital in Lalitpur, Nepal.DesignRetrospective cross-sectional study.SettingDepartment of Transfusion Medicine and Blood Bank of KIST Medical College and Teaching Hospital.ParticipantsAll individuals attending a tertiary care hospital between 17 July 2018 and 31 July 2023 who underwent ABO and Rh blood group testing were included in this study. Individuals younger than 4 months of age and those with discrepant blood group results were excluded.Outcome measuresFrequency and distribution of ABO and Rh blood groups among different categories.MethodsA retrospective cross-sectional study was conducted among individuals attending a tertiary care hospital after obtaining ethical approval from the Institutional Review Committee (reference number: 2080/81/91) of KIST Medical College and Teaching Hospital. The blood grouping and sociodemographic data of individuals were analysed using SPSS V.17.0, and statistical significance was established at p<0.05.ResultsOut of 20 966 individuals, 12 956 (61.80% (95% CI 61.14% to 62.45%)) were female, and 8010 (38.20% (95% CI 37.55% to 38.86%)) were male. The most common blood group was A+ 6629 (31.62% (95% CI 30.99% to 32.25%)), while AB− was the least common blood group 49 (0.23% (95% CI 0.17% to 0.30%)). The RhD+ cases were 20 432 (97.45% (95% CI 97.24% to 97.67%)) and the RhD− cases were 534 (2.55% (95% CI 2.33% to 2.76%)). O+ blood (OR 1.25 (95% CI 1.18 to 1.32), p<0.001) was most common in the males, whereas A+ was common in the females (OR 1.22 (95% CI 1.15 to 1.30), p<0.001).ConclusionsOf the two major blood group systems, the ABO and RhD systems, A+ and RhD+ blood groups were the most prevalent among individuals attending a tertiary care hospital. Understanding the regional distribution of blood groups supports effective blood bank management and transfusion services, ultimately improving patient care.Keywords: Nepal, Blood bank & transfusion medicine, PUBLIC HEALTH, EPIDEMIOLOGY

Introduction

Antigens expressed on human erythrocytes identify an individual’s blood type.1 To date, 345 red cell antigens are represented by more than 40 blood group systems, of which the ABO, Rh and Kell systems are of the greatest clinical significance due to their high immunogenicity.2 Transfusion and transplantation primarily depend on the ABO and Rh blood group systems.3 Acute transfusion reactions, including allergic and haemolytic reactions and transfusion-related acute lung injury, as well as delayed transfusion reactions, such as transfusion-associated graft-versus-host disease, can result from the transfusion of incompatible blood.4

The most prevalent blood type globally is O, while blood group AB is generally the least frequent. The relative frequencies of blood groups A and B vary between populations.5 In Nepal, blood group O (35.1%) was identified as the most common, followed by A (29.7%), B (27.0%) and AB (8.2%), with 97.3% of the samples being Rh+, while 2.7% were Rh−.6 Knowledge of the frequency and distribution of ABO and Rh blood group systems is essential for the effective management of blood banks. Therefore, investigating the frequency and distribution contributes to efficient management of blood bank inventory, organ transplantation and forensic applications. It also aids in understanding genetics, geographic distribution and inheritance patterns.7 8

The distribution of ABO and RhD blood groups varies from population to population throughout the world and within countries; therefore, population-specific data remain essential. This study aimed to determine the prevalence of ABO and RhD blood groups among the individuals visiting a tertiary care hospital in Lalitpur, Nepal.

Materials and methods

Study site and participants

This is a retrospective cohort, single-centred study conducted on individuals attending KIST Medical College and Teaching Hospital, Lalitpur, Nepal, between 17 July 2018 and 31 July 2023.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

This study included all outpatients and inpatients attending the KIST Medical College and Teaching Hospital, Lalitpur, Nepal, who underwent ABO and Rh blood group testing. Individuals younger than 4 months of age and those with discrepant blood group results were excluded.

Data collection

The laboratory results for blood grouping (ABO and RhD) and participants’ demographic information (age and sex) were retrieved from an electronic database (midasDrLabs V.3.2). After obtaining complete information, it was rechecked, anonymised, verified and entered into Microsoft Excel V.2010. The data for this study were accessed on 18 June 2024.

Laboratory diagnosis

2 ml of the venous blood samples were collected aseptically into an EDTA vacutainer tube. In any case of delay, the EDTA sample was stored in the refrigerator at 2–8°C. The ABO and RhD blood groups were determined by forward grouping using a commercially available diagnostic kit, while reverse blood grouping was performed using a prepared cell suspension tube method.

Briefly, the blood grouping was performed as follows:

Forward blood grouping

A 3–5% suspension of red blood cells was prepared. One drop of anti-A and anti-B was placed in two small, properly labelled test tubes. One drop of the RBC suspension was added to each tube and mixed. The test tubes were centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 1 min. The cells were resuspended and examined macroscopically for agglutination. If agglutination was not observed, microscopic examination was performed. Results were recorded.

Reverse blood grouping

Test tubes were labelled for each RBC reagent to be tested. Two drops of the serum or plasma were added to each labelled tube. One drop of A cells and one drop of B cells were added to the appropriate tube and mixed gently. The test tubes were centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 1 min. The cells were resuspended and examined macroscopically for agglutination. If agglutination was not observed, microscopic examination was performed. Results were recorded.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was done by using SPSS V.17.0. Descriptive statistics, including frequency (n), percentage (%) and 95% CIs, were used to characterise the study variables. The χ2 test was used to determine sex-based differences in blood group distribution. ORs with 95% CIs were calculated to assess the association between sex and blood group types. P values <0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and/or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this research.

Results

Demographical distribution of participants

Between July 2018 and July 2023, a total of 20 966 individuals’ blood groups were tested for ABO and RhD type, of whom 12 956 (61.80%) were female, and 8010 (38.20%) were male individuals. The 21–30 year age group had the highest number of individuals tested for blood group 6840 (32.62%), whereas the 51–60 year age group had the least number of individuals tested for blood grouping (table 1).

Discussion

Numerous studies have observed the distribution of ABO and Rh blood groups in various geographic, ethnic and socioeconomic groupings. However, blood groups vary by geography and by ethnicity, emphasising the value of population-specific data.9 10 This study provides the geographical-specific prevalence on the distribution of ABO and Rh blood groups, including their age- and sex-wise distribution.

In our study, the overall distribution of blood groups A, B, AB and O was 6793 (32.40%), 5556 (26.50%), 1883 (8.98%) and 6734 (32.12%), respectively. Notably, blood group A+ was the most prevalent (31.62% (95% CI 30.99% to 32.25%)), followed by O+ (31.35% (95% CI 30.72% to 31.98%)), B+ (25.74% (95% CI 25.15% to 26.33%)) and AB+ (8.75% (95% CI 8.37% to 9.13%)). The least common blood type was AB− (0.23% (95% CI 0.17% to 0.30%)). Likewise, Shrestha et al at the Institute of Medicine in Maharajgunj, Kathmandu, Nepal, reported the frequencies of blood types A, B, AB and O as 4034 (29.7%), 3665 (27.0%), 1114 (8.2%) and 4755 (35.1%), respectively, among 13 568 blood samples.6 Similarly, Elsayid et al reported the distribution of ABO phenotypes, with blood group A comprising 27%, group B comprising 22%, group AB comprising 3% and group O comprising 48% of the study population.11 In contrast to the findings of this study, Talukder SI and Das RK reported that blood group O (40.6%) showed a higher prevalence, followed by blood groups A (26.6%), B (23.2%) and AB (9.6%).12 However, Singh et al reported a different ABO blood group distribution among blood donors, with the B blood group as the most prevalent (35.15%), followed by O (34.73%), A (22.09%) and AB (8.03%).13 Conversely, Ameigaal SD and Ageel AA found a different prevalence, with blood group O as the most common (43.6%), followed by A (31.7%), B (17.7%) and AB (7.0%).14 The ABO blood group distribution in this regional population differs from previous studies and may reflect migration from other regions of the country at different times or inherent population genetic variation,15 although these factors remain speculative.

In our study, there were 20 432 (97.45% (95% CI 97.24% to 97.67%)) Rh+ and 534 (2.55%) Rh− blood types, respectively. Similarly, Singh et al reported that 96.46% of individuals were Rh+ blood types, while 3.54% had Rh− blood types.13 Likewise, Raja et al found that 95.12% of the blood donor population were Rh+ for the D antigen, while 4.87% were RhD−.16 The study conducted by Chandra and Gupta found that 95.71% of the blood donor population were Rh+ blood types, while 4.87% were Rh− blood types.17 Conversely, a cross-sectional study conducted in Bani Waleed City, Libya, by Ameigaal et al found that 72.2% of individuals were Rh+, and 23.8% were Rh−.14 The high prevalence of Rh+ individuals in this cohort likely reflects the genetic characteristics of the local population, which may be important for local transfusion practices and the risk of Rh isoimmunisation.

In our study, the most common blood group was O+ (n=2537; 12.10%; OR 1.25 (95% CI 1.18 to 1.32)) among males, while A+ (n=4036; 19.25%; OR 1.22 (95% CI 1.15 to 1.30)) was the most prevalent blood group among females. In contrast to our study, Chandra and Gupta found that blood group B+ was most prevalent in both males and females, followed by groups O, A and AB.17 The observed variations may be associated with genetic diversity, migration patterns and ethnic heterogeneity. However, further molecular studies are needed to validate these observations. In our study, the prevalence of the AB− blood group was 0.11% (n=23) in men and 0.12% (n=26) in women. Consistent with previous studies, AB was the least prevalent blood group, indicating that ABO gene distribution follows a similar pattern across most ethnic groups.18 Similarly, previous studies found no significant variations in ABO blood group frequencies between sexes.19 In our study, most blood donors were aged 21–30 years (6,840; 32.62% (95% CI 31.94 to 33.30)). The findings of our investigation are consistent with global patterns. Younger persons’ increased interest in and capacity for donation may reflect greater awareness, better physical health and higher mobility, which facilitate blood donation.

This study provides valuable regional insights, but its single-centre design may not fully reflect nationwide ABO and Rh blood group prevalence and distributions, which can vary across geographical regions and topographical factors. In addition, the absence of molecular genotyping limits the detection of rare or subgroup variants.

Conclusion

The frequencies of A and O blood groups were about the same, which is the most common blood group in the study population. O+ was the most common blood group among men, whereas A+ was the most common blood group among women. Understanding the distribution and accurately identifying the blood group type enables medical professionals to take prompt preventative action and helps mitigate the serious effects of both ABO and Rh incompatibility.
