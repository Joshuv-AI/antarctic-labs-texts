D1-like dopamine receptors promote B-cell differentiation in systemic lupus erythematosus
Authors: Zhongyuan Xiang, Fengxi Wu, Zhenghao He, Fen Tan, Haoran Hu, Chun Zou, Ping Yi, Wenen liu, Ming Yang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSystemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is an autoimmune disease that currently cannot be completely cured with a great health burden. Since the production of autoantibodies plays a key role in the pathogenesis of SLE, discovering the underlying immunoregulation mechanism of B cells will be helpful for developing promising immunotherapy for SLE. In recent studies, dopamine receptors (DRDs), G protein-coupled receptors that include D1-like and D2-like subtypes, are expressed on B cells and participate in various physiological processes, involving immune responses. However, the regulatory effect of DRDs on B cells has not been determined.MethodsThis study explored the expression of DRDs on B-cell subsets from SLE patients and healthy individuals. The effects of D1-like receptor on B-cell activation and differentiation were further explored using D1-like receptor agonists or antagonists. RNA-seq and bioinformatics analyses were used to identify specific molecular mechanisms involved.ResultsThe D1-like DRDs on B cells of SLE patients were highly expressed compared with those of healthy controls (HCs). D1-like receptor agonist treatment exacerbated lupus-like symptoms in pristane-induced lupus-like mice, while D1-like receptor antagonists alleviated the lupus-like phenotypes. Inhibition of D1-like receptor signals impeded B-cell differentiation, while activation of D1-like receptor signals could promote B cell differentiation. Further RNA-seq confirmed that PTGS2, a gene related to B-cell differentiation, was up-regulated once the D1-like receptor signals were activated, while BMP6 and IL-24 were up-regulated once the D1-like receptor signals were inhibited.ConclusionD1-like receptors probably promote B-cell differentiation through the PTGS2/PRDM1 pathway.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12964-024-01885-3.

Introduction

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is a kind of multiple organ-involved autoimmune disease that occurs preferring in child-bearing age female [1]. Genetic and environmental factors contribute to the highly heterogeneous clinical symptoms of SLE. Symptoms vary greatly among different patients, even at different stages of disease, ranging from fatigue and joint pain to kidney damage and neuropathy in accordance with the severity of the disease [2, 3].

In terms of pathological mechanisms of SLE, the critical events cover the production of massive autoantibodies, the recognition of autoantigens, the formation and deposition of immune complexes, and subsequent activation of complement agents, causing inflammation and tissue damage [4]. The B cells mainly exist in germinal centers (GCs), and local T follicular cells and follicular dendritic cells promote B-cell maturation and differentiation into plasma cells (PCs) which could produce high-affinity antibodies [5]. The diversity of autoantibodies might also lead to heterogeneity among SLE patients [6, 7]. Therefore, autoreactive B cells capable of producing autoantibodies serve as a valuable therapeutic target of SLE, and exploring the molecular mechanism of B-cell activation and differentiation would be beneficial for the development of novel B-cell targeted immunotherapy.

Neuroimmune mutual regulation has become an academic consensus, and is mediated by neurotransmitters and neuropeptides [8, 9]. Dopamine is a catecholamine-type neurotransmitter and performs physiological functions by binding to dopamine receptors. Dopamine receptors (DRDs) consist of D1-like (DRD1 and DRD5) and D2-like (DRD2, DRD3 and DRD4) receptors [10, 11]. DRDs are expressed on the cell surface of various immune cells and [12, 13] participate in the development of autoimmune diseases, such as rheumatoid arthritis (RA) and experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis (EAE) [14–16]. DRD5 can promote Treg cell differentiation through tumor necrosis factor receptor-related protein, thereby alleviating the course of EAE [15]. DRD2 deficiency enhances the differentiation of CD4+ T cells into Th1/Th17 cells, thereby promoting arthritis symptoms in RA mice [16]. It has been demonstrated that DRDs play a critical role in T-cell differentiation, but the role of DRDs expressed on B cells has rarely been explored [17].

This study intended to investigate the role of DRDs in B-cell activation and differentiation, and their regulatory effects on the pathogenesis of SLE. As we observed, the mRNA expression of DRDs in B cells of SLE patients was increased, especially D1-like receptor. Then, the protein expression level of D1-like receptor in B cells of SLE patients was verified. It has been found that the D1-like receptor expression was higher in differentiated B cells than that in naïve B cells, indicating that D1-like receptors might be related to B-cell differentiation. Subsequently, in vivo and in vitro treatment with D1-like receptor agonists and antagonists to explore the effects of D1-like receptor on lupus-like phenotypes, as well as B-cell activation and differentiation, and the related RNA-seq and bioinformatics analysis revealed that D1-like receptors could promote B-cell differentiation through the PTGS2/PRDM1 pathway.

Materials and methods

Study objects

In this study, 52 patients with SLE were recruited from the Second Xiangya Hospital of Central South University. The disease activity was assessed by the SLE Disease Activity Inedx (SLEDAI) score. 44 sex- and age-matched HCs were recruited from health administration centres. All subjects were excluded if they had other autoimmune diseases, infections or inflammatory diseases. SLE patients with kidney damage were defined as those with 24-hour urine protein > 150 mg/L or with a confirmed pathological diagnosis.

This study was approved by the ethics committee of the Second Xiangya Hospital of Central South University. Ethical code 2019-30 (044).

RT-qPCR

Ficoll separation method is used to separate PBMCs from whole blood. If the experimental subject is B cells, magnetic bead separation method (positive selection) is used to separate PBMCs. RNA was extracted with TRIzol (AG, cat. 21102) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. A NanoDrop™ 2000c spectrophotometer (ND-2000, Thermo) was used to measure the RNA concentration. The reverse transcription process was performed following the manufacturer’s protocol (AG, cat. 11728) (Remove gDNA : 42 ℃/2 min→4 ℃ hold. Reverse transcription: 37 ℃/15 min→85 ℃ 5 s→4 ℃ hold). RT-qPCR was subsequently performed with SYBR Green Premix Ex Taq™ (AG, cat. 11701) using a thermocycler (Bio-Rad CFX Connect™), the amplification protocol is 95 ℃ 30 s→(95 ℃ 5 s→60 ℃ 30 s)× 45 cycles →65 ℃ 5 s→95 ℃ 5 s→End. The sequences of primers were listed in Supplementary Table S2.

Mass spectrometry

All of the serum catecholamine concentrations were determined by mass spectrometry using Waters Xevo TQ-S ultrahigh-performance liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometer at KingMed Diagnostics Co., Ltd., Changsha, Hunan, China. Firstly, 200µL serum sample was added into 200µL mixed isotope internal standard solution. After vortex mixing and centrifugation, supernatant was taken to Waters WCX 96-well plate. Secondly, after solid phase extraction, the target compound was gradient eluted into 96-well plate using Agilent Pursuit 3 PFP chromatographic column, then dried with nitrogen at room temperature, and redissolved with 200µL 0.2% formic acid aqueous solution. Finally, sample injection was performed for analysis. The specific parameters were as follows: Mobile phase A: 0.2% formic acid water; Mobile phase B: 0.2% formic acid methanol; Column temperature: 30 ℃, flow rate: 0.3 ml/min.

Cell culture and drug treatment

Separate PBMCs from whole blood and then isolate human B cells using immunomagnetic beads (positive selection) according to the manufacturer’s protocol (Miltenyi Biotec). A total of 1.5 × 106 B cells per well were seeded in a 24-well plate and stimulated with LPS (Sigma, 3 µg/ml). The D1-like receptor agonist SKF38393 (TargetMol, 5 µM) or the inhibitor SCH23390 (TargetMol, 50 µM) were added, and an equal concentration of DMSO was used as the control. The B cells were collected after 24-hour or 48-hour culture and used for further experiments.

Flow cytometry analysis

Prepare a mix solution with 10uL PBS and 0.5 uL of each marker. Counting 1 × 106 B cells, resuspend in 100uL PBS and add to mix solution. The B cells were incubated with surface-labelled antibodies on an ice surface for 45 min in the dark. The flow cytometry results were analysed with FlowJo (10.5.3). Antibodies can be found in Table S3. All cell samples were examined with BD FACSCanto™ II Flow Cytometer (USA, BD Biosciences).

RNA sequencing and bioinformatics analysis

RNA sequencing

First-strand cDNA was synthesized using reverse transcriptase and random primers, and residual RNA was synthesized using RNA. Then, DNA polymerase and dNTPs were used to synthesize the second-strand cDNA. The 3’ end of the synthesized double-stranded DNA fragment was adenosylated and subsequently connected to the splice hybridization site. Finally, PCR amplification was performed, and the product was purified with AMPure XP beads to obtain the library.

The different databases were collected according to the target data volume, and an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 was subsequently used for sequential analysis. A 150 bp paired-end reading was generated. Four fluorescently labelled dNTPs, DNA polymerase and splicing primers were added to the sequencing stream and amplified. When the measured sequence extends the complementary chain, each fluorescently labelled dNTP can release the corresponding fluorescence. The sequence information of the segment to be measured can be obtained by capturing the fluorescence information and converting the optical signal into the sequencing peak through software.

Bioinformatics analysis

To identify differentially expressed genes, the R limma and edge packages were used for sequencing the differential expression of the genes in the matrix, for which |logFC| > 0.8 and the P value < 0.05 were used. Then, enrichment analysis of the differentially expressed genes was performed according to the clusterProfiler. Finally, ggplot2 and pheatmap were used for visualization.

Animal experiments

Lupus-like mouse modeling

8-week old C57BL/6J mice were injected with 500 µL pristine intraperitoneally for lupus-like mouse modeling. The second injection of pristine was performed four weeks after the first injection. At the 20th week, mice were randomly divided into control group (n = 9), D1-like receptor antagonist-treated group (n = 10), and D1-like receptor agonist-treated group (n = 10), and subjected to equal concentrations of DMSO, D1-like receptor antagonist, and agonist by gavage, respectively. The volume of 2 ml/kg was fed at twice a week. The SKF38393 injection concentration is 10 mg/kg, SCH23390 injection concentration is 0.3 mg/kg.

The measurement of murine urine protein

Hold the end of the test strip and immerse it in urine for 1–2 s before removing it. Remove excess urine from the surface of the test strip, let it stand for 30 s, and compare it with the label to find the most similar color label. The value indicated on the color code is the test result. The baseline urine protein value was measured before the first injection of pristane, and then the urine protein was measured once a week.

Murine serum collection

The whole blood of the mice was collected through the inner canthus vein blood collection method before the first injection of pristane. Afterwards, the blood was collected every four weeks using a clean and enzyme-free 1.5mL eppendorf (EP) tube. The method of serum separation was as follows: collecting 120 µL of whole blood from mice, standing overnight at 4 ℃, and centrifuging at 3000 rpm and 4 ℃ for 15 min the next day to separate the serum.

Separation of lymphocytes from murine spleen tissue

The collected spleen was placed in the well of a six well culture plate containing 1 mL of 1640 complete culture medium to prevent spleen infarction. The spleen was grinded until there was no visible spleen tissue in the culture medium. Splenic lymph node cells were obtained after the double filtration to remove the mucosal connective tissues.

Fluorescent immunohistochemistry

Embed the kidneys and lymph nodes in paraffin and slice them. Place the prepared paraffin slices in a hybridization furnace at 70 ℃ for 1–2 h, during which preheat the turpentine and soak the baked slices in turpentine for 15–20 min. Rehydrate the slices and immerse them in 95% ethanol, 75% ethanol, 50% ethanol, and sterile water for 10 min. Subsequently, antigen repair was performed under high-pressure alkaline (EDTA, pH 9.0) conditions. The periodate is used to remove peroxidase, wash the paraffin slices with PBST, and block them with serum for incubation with primary and secondary antibodies. After incubation, the paraffin sections were stained with fluorescent dyes and observed under a fluorescence microscope. Antibody concentration used: C3 1:500 (abcam, cat.200999), IgG 1:2000 (abcam, cat.205724).

ELISA

The 1 mg/mL of Goat Anti-Mouse IgG was diluted to 4 µg/mL, each plate well was packaged with 50 µL antibody diluent for overnight at 4 ℃. After overnight, the plate was equilibrated at room temperature for 30 min, and washed three times using the newly prepared sterile PBST, each time for 5 min. 100 µL of newly prepared 5% BSA was added to each plate well using a pipette and the plate shaked at room temperature for 1 h. Then, the plate was washed using PBST three times, added the diluted sample at 100 µL/well and shaked at room temperature for 2 h.

Wash the plate three times with PBST, add 100 µL of anti-mouse IgG (H + L) diluted with 1% BSA (1:3000), and shake the plate at room temperature for 1 h. The plate was washed with PBST four times, 100 µ L/well of prepared TMB solution was added, and the plate was incubated in dark for 5–10 min. After incubation, 50 µL/well of termination solution (concentrated sulfuric acid) was added, and finally the 450 nm OD value was read within 15 min.

Statistical analysis

The data were statistically analysed by SPSS 24.0 and presented as the Mean ± SEM. A two-tailed unpaired t test or paired t test were applied for the comparison of two groups to determine the statistical significance of each index, and a two-tailed Mann‒Whitney U test was used when the data were not normally distributed. Correlations between normally distributed data were analysed using Pearson’s r test, while correlations between nonnormally distributed data were analysed using Spearman’s r test.

Results

Increased expression of D1-like receptors in B cells from SLE patients

We firstly explored the plasma concentrations of dopamine and the mRNA expression levels of DRDs in B cells from SLE patients. The plasma concentrations of dopamine and its derivatives norepinephrine and adrenalin tended to increase in SLE patients compared with those in healthy controls (HCs), but no statistical difference (Fig. S1A). Then, the SLE patients were grouped into inactive and active SLE patients according to disease activity, the plasma concentration of dopamine was significantly increased in active SLE patients than that in HC (Fig. S1B). Besides, the results of DRD mRNA expression in B cells revealed that DRD5 expression was increased, while the DRD1, DRD2, and DRD4 mRNA expression levels were not significantly changed (Fig. 1A), suggesting that the alteration of D1-like receptor expression in B cells was more obvious in SLE patients. Furthermore, the protein expression level of D1-like receptors was increased to varying degrees in all B-cell subsets of SLE patients, except for double-negative (DN) cells (Fig. 1B, C). D1-like receptor expression in B cells was also significantly elevated in active SLE patients (Fig. S2). In addition, the remarkable increase in the frequency of PCs from SLE patients was in line with the disease phenotype of autoantibody overproduction (Fig. S3).

As reported, the types of autoantibodies contribute to the heterogeneity of SLE patients [18]. The differential expression of D1-like receptors in the B cells of SLE patients with different autoantibodies was further explored. There was no significant difference in the expression level of D1-like receptors between autoantibody-positive and -negative groups (Fig. S4, S5, S6, S7, S8, S9). Moreover, correlations of D1-like receptor expression in B cells of SLE patients with other clinical index were analysed. The results showed that complement 4 (C4) consumption was positively correlated to the expression of D1-like receptors in DP and CD19+CD27+ B cells. Although the correlations of D1-like receptor expression in other B-cell subsets with C4 consumption and the Urine protein/CRE ratio were not statistically significant, the same trend was also observed (Figs. S10, S11). The above indicated the associations between the expression of D1-like receptors on B cells and increased complement consumption and kidney damage due to the deposition of a large amount of immune complexes [19].

Notably, the horizontal comparison of D1-like receptor expression in various subsets of B cells also indicated that the expression of D1-like receptors was higher in differentiated B cells (including memory B cells and PCs) than that in naïve B cells from both SLE patients and HCs. In addition, compared with that in nonactivated B cells (CD19+CD27−), D1-like receptor expression in activated B cells (CD19+CD27+) was also increased (Fig. 2A), which was more significant in active SLE patients (Fig. 2B).

D1-like receptor agonists and antagonists remarkably affected disease course in lupus-like mice

Inducible lupus-like mouse model was established through intraperitoneal injection of pristane, followed by the treatment with D1-like receptor agonists or antagonists at 16 weeks (Fig. 3A). Compared with the control group, after gavage of D1-like receptor agonist SKF38393, the urine protein level of lupus-like mice significantly increased, while after gavage of D1-like receptor antagonist SCH23390, the urine protein level of lupus-like mice decreased (Fig. 3B).

Then, kidneys and lymph nodes of lupus-like mice were collected for HE staining and fluorescence immunohistochemistry, and spleen for flow cytometry. Compared with the control group, the SKF38393 group mice suffered more severe kidney damage, manifested as the destruction of glomerular structure and an increase in the gap between the glomerulus and surrounding tissues, while the SCH23390 group mice had less kidney damage. Furthermore, the SKF38393 group harbored more severe C3 and IgG deposition, while the SCH23390 group had reduced C3 and IgG deposition than the control (Fig. 3C). Notably, compared with the control group, the formation of GCs in the lymph nodes of lupus-like mice treated with SKF38393 was promoted, while that in the SCH23390-treated mice was suppressed (Fig. S12). The ELISA results of murine serum showed that the SKF38393 group had higher level of anti-dsDNA antibody and similar anti-IgG antibody level to the other two groups (Fig. 3D, S13). In terms of splenic lymphocytes, the frequencies of splenic CD4+ naïve T and regulatory T (Treg) cells were obviously increased in SKF38393 group, while the frequencies of Th17, CD8+ Memory T, CD4+ memory T, and CD4+CD44+ T cells were decreased in SCH23390 group (Fig. S14).

D1-like receptors promoted B-cell activation and differentiation

To investigate the association of D1-like receptor expression with B-cell activation and differentiation, peripheral B cells from healthy volunteers were treated with the D1-like receptor agonist SKF38393 and the inhibitor SCH23390, with the DMSO group as the control. After 24 h of coculture, in the SKF38393 group, the frequencies of CD19+CD27+ B cells and PCs were significantly higher than those in the control group, the frequency of CD19+CD27− cells was decreased, and there was no significant difference in the frequencies of other B-cell subsets (Fig. 4A). Interestingly, the expression of the DRD1 receptor in naïve B cells and inactivated B cells (CD19+CD27−) was considerably higher in the SKF38393 group than that in the control, and there was no obvious difference in D1-like receptor expression on other B-cell subsets. However, there were no differences of the frequency of B-cell subsets and the expression of D1-like receptors between the SCH23390 group and the control group (Fig. 4A).

After 48-hour coculture, the changes in D1-like receptor expression on all B-cell subsets were similar to the results of the 24-hour coculture. Compared with those in the control group, the frequency of naïve B cells in the SKF38393 group increased, while the frequencies of other B-cell subsets (including memory B, PC, CD19+ CD27−, and CD19+ CD27+ B cells) have not been apparently altered (Fig. 4B). While in the SCH23390 group, the frequency of CD19+ CD27+ B cells among the memory B cells decreased, and the proportion of CD19+ CD27− B cells increased (Fig. 4B). Surprisingly, unlike the 24-hour coculture results, there was no significant difference in the percentage of PCs among the three groups. The above results unveiled that the D1-like receptor signalling pathway probably play a vital role in B-cell activation and differentiation.

D1-like receptors may promote B-cell differentiation through the PTGS2/PRDM1 pathway

RNA-seq and bioinformatics analysis of B cells treated with SKF38393 and SCH23390 for 24 h were conducted to investigate the specific molecular mechanisms. Compared with those in the control group, 537 up-regulated genes and 665 down-regulated genes were identified in the SKF38393 group (log FC ≥ 0.8, p < 0.05) (Fig. 5A). GO analysis indicated that the differentiatially expressed genes (DEGs) were mainly related to the activity of growth factors and immune receptors (Fig. S15). KEGG analysis revealed that the DEGs were primarily enriched in inflammatory and cytokine pathways, such as the NF-κB, TNF, and IL-17 signalling pathways (Fig. 5B). Among the DEGs, genes related to the IL-17 pathway, including CXCL1, CCL17 and PTGS2 that are widely involved in the regulation of immune function, were selected for verification. As previous studies showed, PTGS2 promoted PC differentiation by upregulating prdm1, CCL17 assisted B cells forming GCs, and CXCL1 could be used as a biomarker of SLE activity [20–22]. Our results showed that the mRNA expression levels of CXCL1 and PTGS2 were significantly increased in the SKF38393 group, as well as the mRNA expression of CCL17 and downstream molecules of PTGS2 and PRDM1 (Fig. 5C), suggesting that D1-like receptors might promote the differentiation of B cells by upregulating the expression of PTGS2 and PRDM1.

Compared with those in the control, 639 upregulated genes and 702 downregulated genes were identified in the SCH23390 group (log FC ≥ 0.8, p < 0.05) (Fig. 6A). Through GO analysis and KEGG analysis, the DEGs were mainly related to immunoglobulin production and the immune response (Fig. S16), and enriched in neural-related pathways and receptor connection-related pathways, such as the GnRH pathway and cytokine‒cytokine receptor interaction pathway (Fig. 6B). Notably, the expression of Bcl-6, which inhibits the differentiation of B cells into PCs, was also distinctly increased (Fig. 6C). Therefore, inhibiting D1-like receptors likely impeded the B-cell differentiation by upregulating BMP6 and Bcl-6.

Discussion

The incidence of SLE has been increasing over the past few decades, and it cannot be cured completely due to the unknown aetiology, thus it is necessary to explore novel target for effective treatments for SLE patients. Since high concentration of autoantibodies secreted by differentiated PCs is a key event in SLE, inhibiting B-cell differentiation is an important approach for SLE treatment. Moreover, B cells are more than a source of autoantibodies in the pathogenesis of SLE. Activated B cells also promote disease course of SLE by activating autoreactive T cells [25, 26]. To date, several studies have demonstrated that DRDs regulate the activation or immunological function of immune cells (such as Th17 and Treg cells) [15, 27], and the function and underlying mechanisms of DRDs in B cells remain explored.

In this study, we observed the mRNA expression of D1-like receptor DRD5 was significantly upregulated between SLE patients and HCs, while the mRNA expression of D2-like receptors had no noticeable changes. However, DRD3 expression in B cells has not been detected, which might result from the extremely low expression level of DRD3 in B cells. In addition, activated B cells had higher D1-like receptor expression, which was similar to previous findings that D1-like receptor signalling promoted B-cell activation [28]. Interestingly, D1-like receptor expression was also significantly increased on differentiated memory B cells and PCs in both SLE patients and normal individuals, indicative of the potential function of DRDs in B-cell differentiation.

B cells were cocultured with the D1-like agonist SKF38393 and the antagonist SCH23390 to explore the effect of DRD expression on B-cell activation and differentiation. The results showed that SCH23390 inhibited B-cell differentiation, while SKF38393 promoted the differentiation into PCs, revealing that D1-like receptor signalling promotes the differentiation of naïve B cells into PCs, which might aggravate the course of SLE. A previous study reported abnormal expression of DRDs in the T cells of SLE patients, but aberrant expression of DRDs in other immune cells has not been explored [29]. Our results demonstrated higher expression of D1-like receptors in almost all B-cell subsets of SLE patients, including naïve B, memory B, DN, DP, PC, CD19+ CD27+ and CD19+ CD27− B cells. The regulatory effects of D1-like receptors on these B-cell subsets need to be further explored.

RNA-seq and bioinformatics analysis revealed that a part of DEGs in D1-like agonist group and antagonist group were related to B cells, such as IL-1R, CD14, and S1PR3 in the SKF38393 group, as well as IL-24in the SCH23390 group [30–32]. Among the DEGs of the SKF38393 group, the expression of PTGS2 was significantly upregulated, and PTGS2 promoted the expression of prdm1, indicating that D1-like receptors might promote B cells to differentiate into PCs through the PTGS2/prdm1 pathway. Among the DEGs of the SCH23390 group, the expression of BMP6 and IL-24, which are both inhibitory molecules of B-cell differentiation, was upregulated. Therefore, the specific pathway by which D1-like receptor inhibition interferes with PC differentiation remains to be further verified.

Pristane-induced lupus-like mouse model serves as one of the commonly used animal models for SLE, and urinary protein, serum autoantibody concentration, and renal deposition of immune complexes are used to reflect the severity of the disease [33–35]. After gavage of D1-like receptor agonist SKF38393 to lupus-like mice, the levels of urine protein and serum autoantibodies in the mice increased, and renal deposition of immune complexes also increased. Meantime, the phenomenon was completely opposite in D1-like receptor antagonist-treated lupus-like mice. These results indicated that D1-like receptor pathway could affect the disease course of SLE in inducible lupus-like mice. In addition, the activation of D1-like receptor pathway also affected the frequency of lymphocyte subsets and promoted the formation of GCs in lupus-like mice. In the SKF38393-treated group, increased frequency of splenic Treg cells suggested a potential functional recovery of adaptive immune system [36, 37]. Meanwhile, the reduced percentage of pro-inflammatory Th17 cells in the SCH23390 group indicated a reduced inflammatory response in D1-like receptor antagonist-treated lupus-like mice [38]. Previous studies have shown that antagonizing D1-like receptors can inhibit Th17 cell differentiation and alleviate the course of various diseases such as experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis and nephritis [39, 40]. Our research results are similar, which seems to be one of the evidences that D1-like receptors affect B cell differentiation, as B cells can promote Th17 cell differentiation [41, 42].

DRDs are members of the G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs) family, which is the generic term for a class of membrane protein receptors that widely exist in various cells of the body, and GPCR often serves as a drug target [43, 44]. Given the findings of this work, the blockage of D1-like receptors in B cells of SLE patients might be a promising therapeutic strategy through preventing the differentiation of PCs.

Conclusion

In summary, D1-like receptors may promote the activation of B cells and the differentiation of PCs through the PTGS2/PRDM1 signalling pathway. Meanwhile, D1-like receptors significantly exacerbate lupus-like symptoms in SLE mice.
