Sandwich immunoassay for evaluation of serum Kita-Kyushu Lung Cancer Antigen-1 as a potential diagnostic biomarker for gastric cancer
Authors: Tianran Chen, Lin Li, Xin Cao, Yumeng Wang, Qin Liu, Baorui Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Identification of DARPins that bind specifically to KK-LC-1.•Development of a robust method for quantifying KK-LC-1 in serum.•Evidence supporting KK-LC-1′s potential utility in gastric cancer diagnosis.Keywords: KK-LC-1 biomarker gastric cancer

Introduction

Gastric cancer (GC), one of the most prevalent cancers and the fifth leading cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide, accounts for nearly 960,000 cases and over 660,000 deaths annually[1]. Due to its nonspecific symptoms, the diagnosis of gastric cancer is frequently delayed. These factors often lead to patients missing the optimal treatment window and experiencing poor outcomes. Currently, upper gastrointestinal endoscopy and pathological examination are considered the gold standard for diagnosing gastric cancer (GC)[2]. However, this technique is semi-invasive, costly, and heavily reliant on the skill of the endoscopist, which limits its clinical application. Recent advancements in serum biomarker technology have rendered the diagnosis and monitoring of GC non-invasive and convenient. Clinical markers, such as CEA and CA19–9, exhibit low positive rates in GC diagnosis[3]. Consequently, identifying high-sensitivity and specificity serum biomarkers for GC holds significant clinical importance.

Cancer testis antigens(CTA) are a group of immunogenic proteins that are expressed in the testis and tumor tissues, but not in normal tissues. Considering CTAs' expression patterns, they are potential biomarkers and therapeutic targets[4]. Kita-Kyushu Lung Cancer Antigen-1 (KK-LC-1), also known as CT83, is a member of the cancer testis antigen (CTA) protein family that has been shown to be frequently expressed in gastric cancer[5]. A previous study found that the expression level of KK-LC-1 in lung adenocarcinoma serum samples was higher than in healthy control[6]. However, no studies have evaluated the relationship between the expression level of KK-LC-1 in serum samples and gastric cancer.

Sandwich immunoassay utilizing fluorescent magnetic beads is a technique that employs highly specific binding between antibodies and antigens, with magnetic microbeads serving as the solid-phase carrier to quantitatively assess the concentration of chemical substances[7]. This method offers several advantages, including high sensitivity, high throughput, and user-friendly operation[8]. Currently, this technology has been extensively applied for the detection of cytokines[9], bacteria[10], biomarkers[11], and other biological molecules.

Designed Ankyrin Repeat Proteins (DARPins) are artificial proteins composed of repeating modules derived from natural ankyrin proteins, including an N-terminal capping repeat, three internal repeats, and a C-terminal capping repeat[12]. Compared to antibodies, DARPins have several advantages, including low molecular weight, exceptional stability, reduced immunogenicity, and the ability to be expressed in E. coli [13]. These characteristics contribute to their widespread application in areas such as radianuclide imaging[14], fusion proteins[15], and drug conjugates[16].

In this study, we assessed the expression of KK-LC-1 in gastric cancer by utilizing public databases, such as TIMER and TCGA, followed by validation through immunohistochemistry. Subsequently, we screened specific DARPins targeting KK-LC-1 using phage display technology and established a rapid sandwich immunoassay based on fluorescent magnetic beads that enabled sensitive and reliable measurements of KK-LC-1 concentration. Furthermore, this method was employed to evaluate the expression levels of KK-LC-1 in serum samples from 168 healthy individuals and 104 gastric cancer patients, revealing for the first time that serum levels of KK-LC-1 were significantly elevated in gastric cancer patients compared to healthy controls. Finally, we investigated the correlation between KK-LC-1 expression levels and immune cell infiltration in gastric cancer patients using available databases. Our findings suggest that KK-LC-1 may serve as a novel diagnostic marker for gastric cancer.

Materials and methods

Clinical samples

All samples utilized in this study were collected between May 2023 and December 2023 from Nanjing Drum Tower Hospital. To investigate the diagnostic value of serum KK-LC-1 expression in gastric cancer, serum samples were categorized into two groups: 168 healthy donors and 104 gastric cancer patients. In addition, 40 gastric cancer tissue specimens were obtained from the Department of Pathology at our hospital. All gastric cancer patients were diagnosed through histopathological examination. Clinicopathological information for all gastric cancer patients was meticulously documented, including factors such as age, sex, tumor dimensions, and TNM classification among other details. This study received approval from the Ethics Committee of Nanjing Drum Tower Hospital (2024–317–01).

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Paraffin-embedded gastric tissue was sectioned, dewaxed, hydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval in a microwave oven before blocking. Primary antibodies (Thermo Fisher, MA5–24,711, USA) were used overnight, followed by secondary antibodies (Abcam, ab6789, UK) for 2 h. Subsequently, the sections were stained with diaminobenzidine (DAB) and counterstained with hematoxylin. Negative controls were established using phosphate-buffered saline instead of the primary antibody. Based on the percentage of positive cells and the staining intensity of positive cells, KK-LC-1 immunohistochemical staining scores were calculated as follows: (1) staining intensity: 0 points for no positive staining, 1 point for light yellow, 2 points for moderate brown, and 3 points for dark brown; (2) positive cell percentage: 0 points for <5 %, 1 point for 6 %–25 %, 2 points for 26 %–50 %, 3 points for 51 %–75 %, and 4 points for >75 %. The immunohistochemical evaluation score was calculated as follows: point = staining intensity × percentage of positive cells. An immunohistochemical score of 0 indicates negative (-), scores from 1 to 4 indicate weakly positive (+), scores from 5 to 8 indicate intermediate positivity (++), while scores from9 to12 indicate strong positivity (+++). Samples with immunohistochemical scores ≥1 (≥+) were classified as positive for subsequent analyses. The scoring results were independently assessed by two experienced pathologists.

Phage panning, DARPin expression and purification

Our team acquired a DARPin phage library from Abiocenter and conducted the screening procedure as previously described[17]. After five rounds of panning, two enriched sequences were obtained, designated as K1 and K2. The phagemid DNA was sequenced by Genewiz, and the DNA sequences of the positive phage clones were analyzed using SnapGene software. The amino acid sequences of the DARPin domains for K1 and K2 are detailed in patent application 202,311,760,338.2. Expression plasmids containing N-terminal His6 tags for K1 and K2 were constructed and transformed into E. coli BL21 (DE3) by GenScript. The genetically modified E. coli were cultured for 4 h at 37 °C in LB-kanamycin medium (10 g/L tryptone, 5 g/L yeast extract, 10 g/L NaCl, and 50 mg/L kanamycin) and induced overnight with 50 mM IPTG at 16 °C. E. coli cells were lysed by sonication, after which the lysate was clarified by centrifugation at 13,000 g for 30 min; subsequently, the soluble lysate was filtered through a 0.22 μm PES membrane. Proteins were purified using a nickel column (Cytiva catalog number: 17,371,206, USA) and their concentrations were determined using a BCA Protein Quantification Kit (Vazyme E112–01, China).

ELISA

KK-LC-1 protein solution (10 μg/ml) was incubated overnight in high-adsorption 96-well plates and subsequently blocked with 1 % BSA PBST. The purified K1, K2, and negative control proteins were serially diluted four-fold and added to the corresponding wells for incubation at room temperature for 2 h. Subsequently, they were co-incubated with an anti-His antibody for 1 hour. Finally, TMB chromogenic solution was added for 2 min, after which a stop solution (1 M HCl) was introduced. The absorbance at OD450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (Thermo Fisher), and the resulting data were plotted using GraphPad Prism version 9.3.0.

Establishment and optimization of a sandwich immunoassay

A sandwich immunoassay method for sensitive detection of KK-LC-1 was established. A reaction mixture containing 300 µg EDC (10 mg/ml), 300 µg NHS (10 mg/ml), and 3 × 10^7 fluorescent magnetic beads (WEDO, MFB100401A, China) was prepared and incubated at 37 °C in the dark for 30 min. Subsequently, a magnetic rack was employed to perform magnetic separation and remove excess EDC and NHS. The modified magnetic beads were then incubated with 160 µg of K1 (0.4 mg/ml) at 37 °C in the dark for 2 h, followed by using a magnetic rack to eliminate unbound K1, resulting in K1 conjugated with fluorescent magnetic beads. Additionally, 0.2 mg K2 (1 mg/ml) and 0.28 mg of biotin (5 mg/ml) were incubated at room temperature for 3 h before purification using a PD-10 column (Cytiva, catalog number:17,085,101, USA) to obtain biotin-modified K2. Finally, a combination consisting of 50 µl each of the magnetic bead-conjugated K1, biotin-conjugated K2, and analyte was prepared at room temperature for an incubation period of 30 min.

Subsequently, a magnetic rack was used to remove any excess unbound protein, after which PE Streptavidin antibody (BioLegend, catalog number: 405,203, USA) was added. The marker signal was detected using Beckman flow cytometry, and the concentration of KK-LC-1 was quantified based on the signal intensity (Fig. 4A). To establish the optimal assay conditions, ideal dilutions for K1 (1:100, 1:500, 1:1000, and 1:2000) and appropriate dilutions for K2 (1:100, 1:500, 1:1000, and 1:2000) were determined utilizing orthogonal methodologies. The optimal conditions were identified when the maximum ratio of median fluorescence intensity (MFI) between positive samples and negative control samples (P/N) was achieved

Bioinformatics data

The integrated TCGA pan-cancer and GTEx datasets, obtained from the UCSC XENA website (https://tcga-data.nci.nih.gov/tcga/), were utilized to investigate the differential expression of CT83 between cancerous tissues and adjacent non-cancerous tissues across various tumor types. Additionally, we downloaded the TCGA-STAD cohort data from the GDC Data Portal (portal.gdc.cancer.gov/projects/TCGA-STAD; accessed on 10 October 2024) to further explore the association of CT83 expression in gastric cancer with its matched paracancerous tissues. STAR-counts data for gastric cancer were also retrieved from TCGA database (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov). The immunedeconv package (version 2.0.3) was employed to estimate the immune infiltration levels between groups using both the xCell and TIMER algorithms.

Statistical analysis

All bioinformatics analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.3.3). Additional data were statistically analyzed with SPSS version 26.0 and GraphPad Prism version 9.3.0 statistical software. Following the Normality and Lognormality Tests, we found that none of our data adhered to a normal distribution. Consequently, the Mann–Whitney U test was employed for comparisons between the two groups. The Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve and Area Under the Curve (AUC) were generated and calculated to assess the diagnostic performance of serum KK-LC-1 in gastric cancer, with the cut-off value determined using the Youden index. Results were deemed statistically significant when the p-value was <0.05. Asterisks in the upper left corner denote significance levels: * indicates p < 0.05, ** indicates p < 0.01, and *** indicates p < 0.001.

Results

Overexpression of KK-LC-1 in gastric cancer

We examined the mRNA expression of KK-LC-1 across various carcinomas and normal tissues using data from TCGA and GTEx databases. Our analysis revealed that KK-LC-1 was expressed in multiple tumor types, including gastric cancer (GC), at levels significantly higher than those observed in normal tissues (Fig. 1A). The mRNA expression levels of KK-LC-1 in GC samples (n = 414) were markedly elevated compared with those in normal tissues (n = 210) (Fig. 1B). This finding was corroborated by an analysis of 27 matched pairs of GC samples and healthy gastric tissues, which yielded consistent results (Fig. 1C). Simultaneously, our analysis of the TCGA-STAD cohort (241 males, 134 females) demonstrated no statistically significant sex-based differences in KK-LC-1 expression patterns (p > 0.05) (Figure S1A). Furthermore, extended evaluation of matched tumor-adjacent tissue specimens from 19 male and 8 female patients revealed consistently elevated KK-LC-1 expression levels in tumor tissues compared to adjacent normal tissues across both genders (Figure S1B). These findings collectively indicate minimal sexual dimorphism in the tissue-specific expression patterns of KK-LC-1. Based on these studies, KK-LC-1 appears to be a highly specific biomarker for GC, as it is exclusively found in GC tissues and not present in normal tissue samples. We measured KK-LC-1 expression levels in 41 cases of human gastric cancer via immunohistochenmistry staining (Fig. 1D). KK-LC-1 was positive in 75 % of the patients, among which 14 % were weakly positive, 46 % were intermediate, and rest 14 % were strongly positive (Table 1), which further confirmed that KK-LC-1was highly expressed in GC tissues.

Screening phage library for specific KK-LC-1-binding DARPins

To obtain specific DARPin binding to KK-LC-1, phage library screening against KK-LC-1 were performed (Fig. 2A). The enrichment is gradually increasing in each round, which indicated that the KK-LC-1-binding phages were effectively enriched (Fig. 2B). After five rounds of screening, 30 randomly chosen phage clones were amplified and DNA sequences were determined. Two sequences, named K1 and K2, with frequencies of 30 % and 45 % were chosen for subsequent applications and validation. The binding affinity of K1 and K2 for KK-LC-1 (EC50, nM) was evaluated by ELISA, with 8.727 nM for K1 and 32.3 nM for K2 (Fig. 2C).

Clinical value and diagnostic role of KK-LC-1 in GC serum

Next, we collected serum samples from 104 GC patients, and 168 healthy donors to investigate the diagnostic significance of KK-LC-1 in the serum of GC patients. The expression level of KK-LC-1 in GC patients was substantially higher than that of healthy donors (P < 0.0001) (Fig. 4A). For investigating the diagnostic value of KK-LC-1, ROC curves were performed and areas under the curve (AUC) were calculated. KK-LC-1 had an AUC of 0.756 (95 % confidence interval (CI) 0.695–0.817). Meanwhile KK-LC-1 had 74 % sensitivity and 70 % specificity in differentiating GC patients from healthy donors when the cut-off point was 13.3 nM and the Youden index was 0.43, demonstrating a good diagnostic value (Fig. 4B). Mann Whitney test was used to evaluate the relationship between KK-LC-1 expression and clinicopathological parameters in 104 GC patients. These results showed that the expression level of KK-LC-1 was associated with metastasis (P = 0.001), TNM stage (P =0.0482), CEA (P = 0.01), CA242 (P = 0.009), CA199 (P = 0.0426), but showed no significant differences with other parameters (sex, age, Lauren classification, Her2) (Fig. 4C-4G) (Table 3). Serum KK-LC-1 levels in healthy donors also showed no sex-based differences (P = 0.137) (Figure S2). In additional, we investigated whether the expression level of KK-LC-1 in gastric cancer tissues was correlated with that in serum, and the results indicated that patients with positive KK-LC-1 expression in GC tissues also had higher KK-LC-1 levels in the paired serum (Fig. 4H). We rigorously explored combinatorial biomarker strategies using available healthy control data (n = 116/168 for CEA; n = 65/168 for CA199). Our analyses revealed meaningful diagnostic improvements: combining KK-LC-1 with CEA increased the AUC to 0.778 (95 % CI: 0.716–0.840), outperforming both individual CEA (0.672; 95 % CI: 0.601–0.744) and KK-LC-1 (0.757; 95 % CI: 0.692–0.822) (Figure S3A). Similarly, pairing KK-LC-1 with CA199 achieved an AUC of 0.742 (95 % CI: 0.666–0.817), significantly surpassing CA199 alone (0.546; 95 % CI: 0.459–0.632) while remaining comparable to that of KK-LC-1 alone (0.746; 95 % CI: 0.653–0.842) (Figure S3B). These findings suggest that KK-LC-1 has potential as a serum biomarker for the diagnosis of GC and its diagnostic accuracy could potentially be enhanced through combination with other biomarkers.

Association between KK-LC-1 expression and immunity in GC

Immunity plays an important role in tumor progression, affecting the treatment and prognosis of patients with GC. Therefore, we explored the association between KK-LC-1 expression and immune cell infiltration by TIMER. Our results showed that the expression of KK-LC-1 was negatively correlated with B cells (r = −0.108, p = 3.67e–02), CD4+ T cells (r = − 0.113, p = 2.9e–02), CD8+ T cells (r = − 0.155, p = 2.63e–03),macrophages (r = − 0.188,p = 2.62e–04), centriocytes(r = − 0.195, p = 1.48e–04) and DC cells(r = − 0.212, p = 3.57e–05) (Fig. 5A). Then,we compared TME infiltrating immune cells between the groups with high and low KK-LC-1 expression in GC by XCELL. These results suggested that the group with higher KK-LC-1 expression had lower immune cells infiltration in Macrophage M2, Myeloid dendritic cell, CD8+ T cell, CD4+ memory T cell, CD4+ naive T cell,B cell,Monocyte (Fig. 5B-C).

Discussion

GC is associated with a high incidence and mortality rate, with diagnosis often relying on gastroscopy. However, since gastroscopy is an invasive procedure, patient compliance tends to be suboptimal. In clinical practice, there are limited diagnostic serum markers for gastric cancer; therefore, it is essential to explore novel serum diagnostic markers for this disease[18]. KK-LC-1, as a member of CTA family, has been shown in various studies to have high expression rates in gastric cancer, and may serve as a promising serum diagnostic biomarker.

In this research, we employed public databases and immunohistochemistry to validate the high expression of KK-LC-1 in gastric cancer tissues (75 %), which is in line with previous studies[19,20] and indicates that it might serve as a potential target for the diagnosis of gastric cancer. We employed phage display technology to identify DARPin K1 and K2, which specifically bind to KK-LC-1. Building upon this foundation, we developed a new method for quantifying the concentration of KK-LC-1 protein utilizing sandwich immunoassay. We optimized the detection conditions by the orthogonal method and obtained a linear standard curve within the effective range of 0 - 240 nM, demonstrating that this method can effectively quantify the protein concentration of KK-LC-1.

Subsequently, we collected serum samples from 168 healthy individuals and 104 patients diagnosed with gastric cancer, utilizing sandwich immunoassay to quantify the levels of KK-LC-1 expression in serum. This study represents the first analysis of KK-LC-1 expression in the serum of gastric cancer patients. Compared to healthy donors, there was a significant increase in KK-LC-1 expression in the serum of GC patients. Upon conducting ROC curve analysis, KK-LC-1 expression demonstrated a sensitivity of 74 % and a specificity of 70 %, thereby affirming its potential as a serum diagnostic marker for this malignancy. Next, we collected clinical data from the patients and examined its correlation with serum KK-LC-1 expression. Our findings revealed that KK-LC-1 expression was positively correlated with TNM staging, distant metastasis, and other biomarkers CEA, CA125, CA199; these results suggest that it may serve as a promising therapeutic target for gastric cancer. Then, we observed a positive correlation between serum KK-LC-1 expression and tissue KK-LC-1 expression, suggesting that this biomarker may serve as a companion diagnostic tool to guide the selection of patients for subsequent therapies targeting the KK-LC-1. Furthermore, we compared the diagnostic performance of the GC biomarkers CEA and CA199 with KK-LC-1 using ROC analysis, we found that KK-LC-1 demonstrated superior diagnostic efficiency. Moreover, the combination of KK-LC-1 with CEA further enhanced the diagnostic accuracy.

However, due to some limitations in our study design, we were unable to monitor changes in KK-LC-1 levels over time within the same group; therefore, this limited our ability to explore its role in tracking disease progression. We will persist in the collection of samples and undertake additional data analyses to access the diagnostic and prognostic significance of KK-LC-1 in gastric cancer.

We investigated the correlation between KK-LC-1 and immune cell infiltration in gastric cancer, revealing a negative association with B cells, CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, macrophages, neutrophils, and dendritic cells (DCs). Building on this finding, we further examined the relationship between the expression levels of KK-LC-1 and immune cell infiltration within the gastric cancer tumor microenvironment. Our results indicated that patients with high KK-LC-1 expression had significantly reduced numbers of macrophages, T cells, and DCs compared to those with low expression levels. Therefore, we propose that patients with high levels of KK-LC-1 expression might experience less favorable responses to immunotherapy. Moreover, we suggest that subsequent therapeutic strategies designed to enhance local immune cell infiltration—such as CAR-T therapy and cell engager therapy—may confer substantial benefits.

KK-LC-1 is also highly expressed in various cancer types other than gastric cancer, including lung cancer[21], liver cancer[22] and triple-negative breast cancer[23]. Furthermore, existing studies have proved that KK-LC-1 can serve as a diagnostic and prognostic serum biomarker for lung adenocarcinoma[6]. Our research also proved its value as an effective serum biomarker for the diagnosis of gastric cancer. Building on these findings, we intend to investigate in future research whether KK-LC-1 retains diagnostic potential in other cancer types characterized by high expression levels, and to assess whether its serum expression varies across different tumor types.

In conclusion, we have identified for the first time that KK-LC-1 is significantly elevated in the serum of gastric cancer patients, thereby establishing it as a reliable serum biomarker for gastric cancer diagnosis. Additionally, we have developed a method to quantify the concentration of KK-LC-1 protein. In the future, we will further explore the diagnostic and prognostic significance of KK-LC-1 in gastric cancer and various solid tumors.
