ZXDB Drives Macrophage Inflammatory Programming in Sepsis‐Induced Acute Kidney Injury by Recruiting EIF4A3 to Enhance ACACA Translation
Authors: Haiyang Wu, Wei Gang, Li Wang, Jing Sun, Shuangxi Li, Zhiyong Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMacrophage‐driven inflammation is central to the pathogenesis of sepsis‐induced acute kidney injury (SI‐AKI), yet the role of RNA‐binding proteins (RBPs) in post‐transcriptionally regulating this process remains elusive. Here, we identify the RBP zinc finger X‐linked duplicated B (ZXDB) as an upstream contributor to SI‐AKI by promoting a pathogenic, pro‐inflammatory macrophage activation state. We found that ZXDB expression is consistently elevated in M1‐like macrophages within the kidney during SI‐AKI, where it stimulates pro‐inflammatory cytokine secretion and glycolytic reprogramming. Mechanistically, we discovered that ZXDB directly interacts with EIF4A3, a core exon junction complex (EJC) DEAD‐box RNA helicase, via its aa151–300 region, thereby enhancing ACACA 5′UTR‐dependent translation of ACACA, a metabolic enzyme that mediates downstream pathogenic effects associated with ZXDB activation. Critically, macrophage‐specific deletion of Zxdb attenuated disease severity in a mouse model of SI‐AKI, preserving renal function and attenuating inflammation. Taken together, our study uncovers a novel ZXDB‐EIF4A3‐ACACA axis that orchestrates macrophage‐mediated kidney injury through translational control of metabolism, thereby suggesting ZXDB as a potential therapeutic candidate for SI‐AKI.

Introduction

Sepsis, a life‐threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection, remains a leading cause of mortality and critical illness worldwide, imposing a staggering burden on healthcare systems [1]. Among its most frequent and devastating complications is sepsis‐induced acute kidney injury (SI‐AKI), which affects up to 50% of septic patients and is independently associated with increased risk of chronic kidney disease, long‐term disability, and death [2]. The pathogenesis of SI‐AKI is complex, but accumulating evidence points to an important role of a dysregulated inflammatory microenvironment within the kidney, where infiltrating immune cells orchestrate a cascade of tissue damage [3]. However, the precise upstream molecular regulators that initiate and sustain this pathogenic immune response within the kidney remain poorly understood, representing a major barrier to the development of effective therapies [4, 5].

Macrophage activation is highly plastic and exists along a spectrum of stimulus‐dependent functional states. While the classical M1/M2 framework remains a useful operational shorthand in many experimental settings, it does not fully capture the diversity of inflammatory macrophage programs observed in vivo, including distinct regulatory/inflammatory subsets (e.g., M2b‐like states). Accordingly, throughout this study, we use ‘M1‐like’ to describe a pro‐inflammatory activation state defined by multiparametric marker combinations and inflammatory output, rather than a consistent polarization category. Understanding this intricate macrophage plasticity and the specific signals driving different polarization states is vital for developing targeted immunotherapies for various diseases [6].

Central to this renal inflammatory milieu are macrophages, which function as a double‐edged sword in the progression of AKI [7, 8]. In the early phase, pro‐inflammatory (M1‐like) macrophages drive tissue injury by releasing a broad array of cytotoxic mediators. In contrast, during the resolution phase, anti‐inflammatory (M2‐like) macrophages are essential for tissue repair and regeneration [9]. A persistent imbalance, skewed toward pathogenic M1‐like macrophages, is now recognized as a key determinant of disease severity and poor prognosis in SI‐AKI [10]. Despite this understanding, therapeutic strategies aimed at modulating macrophage function, such as inhibitors of canonical pathways like Toll‐like receptor (TLR) or NF‐κB signaling, have shown limited efficacy in clinical trials, often due to a lack of specificity and systemic side effects [11]. This underscores an urgent need to identify more precise, upstream regulators of pathogenic macrophage activation. Therefore, deciphering the intrinsic molecular programs that dictate macrophage polarization, particularly those operating beyond canonical signaling pathways, is paramount for developing next‐generation therapeutics.

A burgeoning field of research, termed “immunometabolism,” has revealed that macrophage function is inextricably linked to its metabolic state. The acquisition of a pro‐inflammatory M1‐like phenotype is causally dependent on substantial metabolic reprogramming aerobic glycolysis, a process that fuels the production of inflammatory mediators [12, 13]. While this discovery has opened new therapeutic avenues, direct interventions targeting metabolic pathways often lack cellular specificity and can disrupt essential homeostatic functions, highlighting the need for strategies that target the upstream drivers of this metabolic‐inflammatory coupling [14].

Zinc finger X‐linked duplicated B (ZXDB) is a zinc finger protein encoded by a gene located on the X chromosome (Xp11.21), belonging to the ZXDA/ZXDB duplicated gene family [15]. ZXDB functions primarily as a transcriptional coregulator, capable of interacting with the class II transactivator (CIITA) to modulate MHC class I/II gene expression, thereby influencing immune and inflammatory signaling [16]. Although direct evidence connecting ZXDB to SI‐AKI is currently lacking, its molecular features imply potential relevance.

Beyond the well‐trodden paths of transcriptional and epigenetic control, post‐transcriptional regulation orchestrated by RNA‐binding proteins (RBPs) is emerging as a critical, yet underexplored, layer of immune governance [17, 18]. RBPs can rapidly and selectively modulate protein expression by controlling mRNA stability, localization, and, most pertinently, translation. DEAD‐box RNA helicases, including EIF4A family members and the EJC component EIF4A3, are increasingly recognized as key regulators of translational control, yet their role in regulating immunometabolism during acute organ injury remains largely uncharted territory [19, 20]. Within the landscape of immunometabolism, acetyl‐CoA carboxylase alpha (ACACA), the rate‐limiting enzyme for de novo fatty acid synthesis, has emerged as a critical regulatory node. Beyond its canonical metabolic role, recent studies have implicated ACACA in controlling inflammatory responses, positioning it as a key potential link between metabolic state and immune function [21, 22]. However, the upstream post‐transcriptional mechanisms that govern ACACA expression during acute inflammation are completely unknown.

In this study, we define the role of the ZXDB‐EIF4A3‐ACACA axis in SI‐AKI. We demonstrate that this RBP‐driven pathway couples fatty acid synthesis to glycolysis in macrophages and show that its targeting represents a promising therapeutic strategy with strong in vivo validation.

Materials and Methods

Animals

Wild‐type (WT) male C57BL/6J mice, aged 4 weeks (adapt to the environment until the experiment starts at 8 weeks of age in mice), were procured from Shanghai Yaokang Biological Co, Ltd. Myeloid‐specific Zxdb knockout mice (Mac‐Zxdb‐KO) were created by crossing Zxdbfl/fl mice (obtained by CRISPR/Cas9) with Lyz2‐iCre transgenic mice on a C57BL/6J background. Genotyping was performed by PCR. Primers for the Zxdb 5′ arm were AAACACCCAATGGGGCTGACA and GTGGACATATGGCTTCTACGCTCTC; for the Zxdb 3′ arm, GCTCCTAATCGCTTAACCATCTCTC and CTGATAAACACCAGACCCACAAAAG; for the Lyz2‐iCre 5′ arm, GAACACACCTGGAAGATGCTCC and CATCCTTGGCACCATAGATCAGG; and for the wild‐type Zxdb allele, GCTAAAGGCAGAAGGGAGAGACTC and ACTGATGCTTTCTGTCAAATGACCTC. Littermate Zxdbfl/fl mice that did not express Cre were used as control (WT). The mice were maintained in a specific pathogen‐free (SPF) environment under 12‐h day/night cycle and provided ad libitum access to standard chow and water. Successful myeloid‐specific deletion of Zxdb in Mac‐Zxdb‐KO mice was confirmed by qRT‐PCR and Western Blotting of bone marrow‐derived macrophages (BMDMs), which showed > 80% reduction of Zxdb expression compared with littermate Zxdb^fl/fl controls.

Cell Culture and Treatments

Murine macrophage cell line RAW264.7 and human monocytic cell line THP‐1 were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA). RAW264.7 cells were cultured in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, 11965092; Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, 10099141; Gibco) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (15140122; Gibco). For RNA/Western blot experiments, RAW264.7 cells were seeded at 5.0 × 105 cells per well in 6‐well plates (2 mL/well). For ELISA/conditioned media, 1.0 × 105 cells per well in 24‐well plates (0.5 mL/well). For CCK‐8 assays, 2.0 × 104 cells per well in 96‐well plates (100 μL/well). Cells were treated at 70%–80% confluence. Unless otherwise stated, all in vitro experiments were performed with at least three independent biological replicates. For each condition, technical duplicates (ELISA) or triplicates (CCK‐8, qPCR) were included.

THP‐1 monocytes were cultured in RPMI‐1640 medium (11875093; Gibco) with 10% FBS, 1% penicillin–streptomycin, and 0.05 mM 2‐mercaptoethanol (M3148; Sigma‐Aldrich). THP‐1 monocytes were differentiated into macrophages by treatment with 100 ng/mL phorbol 12‐myristate 13‐acetate (PMA, Sigma‐Aldrich, P8139) for 48 h before experiments. For differentiation, 1.0 × 106 cells per well (6‐well) were seeded and treated with PMA (100 ng/mL, 48 h), followed by a 24 h rest in fresh complete medium before experiments.

Bone marrow‐derived macrophages (BMDMs) were isolated from the femurs and tibias of WT C57BL/6J mice. Bone marrow cells were flushed and cultured in DMEM containing 10% FBS, 1% penicillin–streptomycin, and 20 ng/mL macrophage colony‐stimulating factor (M‐CSF, PeproTech, 315‐02) for 7 days to differentiate into M0 macrophages. For pro‐inflammatory activation, M0 macrophages were stimulated with 100 ng/mL Lipopolysaccharide (LPS, from E. coli O111:B4, L4391; Sigma‐Aldrich) and 20 ng/mL Interferon‐gamma (IFN‐γ, 315‐05; PeproTech) for 24 h. For M2‐like polarization, cells were treated with 20 ng/mL Interleukin‐4 (IL‐4, 214‐14; PeproTech) and 20 ng/mL Interleukin‐13 (IL‐13, 210‐13; PeproTech) for 24 h. For in vitro inflammatory stimulation experiments, RAW264.7 or PMA‐differentiated THP‐1 macrophages were treated with 100 ng/mL LPS for 6 h.

SI‐AKI Model

SI‐AKI was generated in mice by employing the widely used polymicrobial sepsis model of Cecal Ligation and Puncture (CLP) [23]. In brief, mice were anesthetized with isoflurane. The midline laparotomy was carried out, and the cecum was exteriorized, ligated distally to the ileocecal valve (50% ligation), and punctured two times with a 21‐gauge needle. A small portion of fecal content was released. The cecum was retracted into the peritoneal cavity, and closure of the incision in layers was done. Sham‐operated mice were treated similarly, devoid of CLP. Subcutaneously, 1 mL pre‐warmed sterile saline was injected into mice as a measure of resuscitation following surgery. Animals were closely observed, and samples of kidneys and blood were taken at 24 h post‐surgery.

Plasmids, shRNA Knockdown, and Overexpression

Short hairpin RNA (shRNA) against mouse Zxdb, human ZXDB, human EIF4A3, or non‐targeting control shRNA was inserted into pLKO.1 lentiviral vector (8453; Addgene plasmid). Lentiviral particles were generated in HEK293T cells by using psPAX2 (12260; Addgene plasmid) and pMD2.G (12259; Addgene plasmid) as packaging plasmids. Macrophage cell lines were transduced using lentivirus in the presence of polybrene (H9268; Sigma‐Aldrich) and selected by puromycin (P8833; Sigma‐Aldrich) at 2 μg/mL for 3 days. Knockdown efficiency for each shRNA was determined 72 h post‐transduction by qRT‐PCR and Western Blotting, and only shRNAs achieving > 70% reduction at both mRNA and protein levels were used in subsequent experiments. For overexpression constructs, successful expression was verified 48 h post‐transfection by qRT‐PCR and Western Blotting, showing at least a 3‐ to 5‐fold increase relative to empty vector controls. For rescue experiments, shRNA‐resistant constructs were validated to restore target protein expression to ≥ endogenous levels.

The full‐length mouse Zxdb, human ZXDB, human ACACA, and interaction‐deficient ZXDB‐MUT constructs were cloned into the pcDNA3.1(+) vector (Invitrogen). The ZXDB RRM domain deletion mutant (ZXDB‐MUT, deleted aa 151–300) was made using the site‐directed mutagenesis kit (Q5 Site‐Directed Mutagenesis Kit, E0554S; NEB). Plasmids were transferred into cells using Lipofectamine 3000 (L3000015; Invitrogen) following the supplier's protocols. Successful overexpression was validated 48 h post‐transfection by qRT‐PCR and Western Blotting, which demonstrated an increase in the expression of the respective constructs compared to cells transfected with an empty pcDNA3.1 (+) vector. For rescue experiments, cells were co‐transduced/transfected with the relevant shRNA and overexpression vectors, and the expression of the shRNA‐resistant constructs was confirmed by Western Blot.

RNA Extraction and Quantitative Real‐Time PCR (qPCR)

RAW264.7 or THP‐1 macrophages were seeded at 5.0 × 105 cells per well in 6‐well plates and harvested at indicated time points for RNA or protein extraction. Total RNA was prepared from cultured cells or kidney tissue using TRIzol reagent (15596026; Invitrogen). A reverse transcription kit, iScript cDNA Synthesis Kit (1708891; Bio‐Rad), was used to synthesize cDNA. qPCR was conducted on the CFX96 Real‐Time PCR Detection System (Bio‐Rad) with PowerUp SYBR Green Master Mix (A25742; Biosystems). The 2−ΔΔCt method was used to calculate relative gene expression, which was normalized to Gapdh (mouse) or GAPDH (human) expression. Primers used for qPCR were as follows: for mouse Zxdb, forward CCTACCAGTGCGCGTTTTC and reverse GCCTTGT CGTACTGCTTGC; for human ZXDB, forward TTACCAGCCAGAGACAATGA and reverse AGATTCCAGAGTTCACCAATGC; for human EIF4A3, forward AAGGGAGAGATGTCATCGCAC and reverse GCTTGAGTTTCACGAACCTGA; for mouse Eif4a3, forward AGGAGGACATGACCAAAGTGG and reverse TGCTGAATCGCTGAAGGTTTT; for human ACACA, forward ACATCCCGTACCTTCTTCTA and reverse TAAGCCTTCACTGTTCCTTC; for mouse Acaca, forward AATGAACGTGCAATCCGATTTG and reverse ACTCCACATTTGCGTAATTGTTG.

Western Blotting

1.5–3 × 106 RAW264.7 or THP‐1 macrophage cells (or equivalent protein amount from tissue) were used per sample to yield 20–40 μg protein per lane. Cells or kidney tissues were lysed in RIPA buffer (89900; Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktails (PPC1010; Sigma). Protein concentrations were determined using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (23225; Thermo Fisher Scientific). Equal amounts of protein (20–40 μg) were separated by SDS‐PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes (IPVH00010; Millipore). Membranes were blocked with 5% non‐fat milk or BSA in Tris‐buffered saline with 0.1% Tween‐20 (TBST) for 1 h at room temperature. Membranes were then incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against: ZXDB (A303‐656A; Invitrogen, 1:1000), ACACA (21923‐1‐AP; Proteintech, RRID:AB_11042445, 1:1000), EIF4A3 (17 504‐1‐AP; Proteintech, RRID:AB_2097393, 1:1000), iNOS (22226‐1‐AP; Proteintech, RRID:AB_2879038, 1:1000), CD86 (13395‐1‐AP; Proteintech–, RRID:AB_2074882, 1:1000), ARG1 (16001‐1‐AP; Proteintech–, RRID:AB_2289842, 1:1000), CD206 (18704‐1‐AP; Proteintech, RRID:AB_10597232, 1:1000), GAPDH (60004‐1‐Ig; Proteintech, RRID:AB_2107436, 1:10000). After washing with TBST, membranes were incubated with the appropriate HRP‐conjugated secondary antibodies CST for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using SuperSignal West Pico PLUS (34580; Thermo Fisher Scientific) and imaged using the ChemiDoc MP System (Bio‐Rad). Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA). Densitometric quantification was performed using ImageJ, and band intensities were normalized to GAPDH for cell lysates or to total protein loading (Ponceau S staining) for tissue lysates.

Flow Cytometry

To phenotype macrophage activation states, cells were collected and stained using antibodies against surface markers: CD86 (105011; BioLegend, (PE), 1:100) and CD206 (141705; BioLegend, (APC), 1:200). Following surface staining, cells were fixed, permeabilized, and subjected to intracellular staining for iNOS (696807; BioLegend, (APC), 1:100), Arg1 (369703; BioLegend, (PE), 1:100), and IL‐10 (506806; BioLegend, (APC), 1:100) to enable multiparametric phenotypic classification. Macrophage subsets were operationally defined as CD86+iNOS+ (M1‐like), CD206+Arg1+ (M2a‐like), and CD86+IL‐10+ (M2b‐like) populations. Cells were run on BD FACSCanto II flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA). FlowJo software (BD Biosciences) was used to analyze data.

Enzyme‐Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA)

Concentrations of cytokines (IL‐1β, IL‐4, IL‐13, TNF‐α, IL‐10, IFN‐γ) in cell culture medium or serum of mice were determined using commercial ELISA kits following the manufacturers' protocols (Invitrogen). For conditioned medium, cells were cultured at 1.0 × 105 cells/well in 24‐well plates for 24 h, and supernatants were collected after centrifugation at 300× g for 5 min. Cytokine concentrations were determined from standard curves and subsequently normalized to the total protein content of the corresponding well measured by BCA, and reported as pg. cytokine per μg cellular protein, thereby controlling for differences in cell number or viability. For serum samples, cytokines were measured directly from undiluted or appropriately diluted serum and reported as pg/mL, with all samples processed using identical blood collection and centrifugation conditions (2000× g, 10 min). All ELISAs were performed in technical duplicates and repeated in at least three independent experiments.

Cell Viability Assay

Cell viability was determined using CCK‐8 (CK04; Dojindo Molecular Technologies). Briefly, 2.0 × 104 cells were plated in 96‐well plates (100 μL/well), treated as mentioned, and incubated in CCK‐8 reagent for 2 h. Absorbance at 450 nm was determined using a microplate reader (SpectraMax M5, Molecular Devices). Technical triplicates per condition, biological replicates n ≥ 3. The proliferation fold change was calculated as: Proliferation fold = OD₄₅₀(experimental)/OD₄₅₀(control).

Apoptosis Assay

Apoptosis was measured using the Annexin V‐FITC/Propidium Iodide (PI) Apoptosis Detection Kit (556547; BD Biosciences) and flow cytometry analysis as mentioned above. Briefly, cells were harvested, washed twice with cold PBS, and resuspended in 1 × binding buffer. Annexin V‐FITC and PI were added and incubated for 15 min at room temperature in the dark. Samples were analyzed using a BD FACSCanto II flow cytometer, and data were processed using FlowJo software. Cells positive for Annexin V‐FITC but negative for PI were defined as early apoptotic cells, and cells positive for both Annexin V‐FITC and PI were defined as late apoptotic cells. The apoptotic rate (%) was calculated as: [(Annexin V+/PI− cells + Annexin V+/PI+ cells)/Total gated cells] × 100%.

Metabolic Assays

Measurement of lactate in cell culture medium was done using Lactate Assay Kit (MAK064; Sigma‐Aldrich). Cellular ATP content was measured using an ATP Assay Kit (CellTiter‐Glo Luminescent Cell Viability Assay, G7570; Promega). Assays were carried out based on manufacturers' instructions.

Histology and Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Kidneys were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) overnight, processed, and paraffin‐embedded. Sections (4–5 μm) were cut and H&E‐stained for morphological analysis. Injury to renal tubules was blindly scored for dilation of tubules, cast formation, and necrosis of cells in 10 non‐overlapping High‐Power Field (HPF) of one kidney section. For immunohistochemistry, paraffin sections were dewaxed, rehydrated, and antigen retrieval using citrate buffer pH 6.0 was done. Sections were blocked and incubated overnight at 4°C using primary antibody against KIM‐1 (AF1817; R&D Systems, 1:200). An HRP‐conjugated secondary antibody and DAB substrate kit (SK‐4100; Vector Laboratories, 1:500) were used for detection. Sections were stained using hematoxylin and visualized using an Olympus BX53 microscope.

TUNEL Assay

Apoptosis in sections of kidney was revealed using the In Situ Cell Death Detection Kit, TMR red (12156792910; Roche) following the recommended protocol of the vendor. Nuclei were stained using DAPI (D9542; Sigma‐Aldrich). The number of TUNEL‐positive cells under a fluorescence microscope in 10 random fields of each section was counted.

Immunofluorescence (IF)

For cellular immunofluorescence, BMDM and THP‐1 cells grown on sterile coverslips were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 15 min, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X‐100 (T8787; Sigma‐Aldrich) for 10 min, and blocked with 5% BSA for 1 h at room temperature. Cells (approximately 1–2 × 106 per coverslip) were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against ZXDB (A303‐656A; Invitrogen, 1:200) or EIF4A3 (17 504‐1‐AP; Proteintech, 1:200). After washing, cells were incubated with Alexa Fluor–conjugated secondary antibodies (A‐11001; Invitrogen, Alexa Fluor 488, 1:500) for 1 h at room temperature. Coverslips were mounted using ProLong Gold Antifade Mountant with DAPI (P36931; Invitrogen) and imaged using a Zeiss LSM 880 confocal microscope.

For tissue immunofluorescence, mouse kidneys were harvested, fixed in 4% PFA, and embedded in OCT compound for cryosectioning. Cryosections (5–7 μm) were mounted on glass slides, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X‐100 for 10 min, and blocked with 5% BSA for 1 h at room temperature. Tissue sections were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies specific for macrophage and epithelial markers, including F4/80 (11‐4801‐82; Thermo Fisher, 1:200), E‐cadherin (53‐3249‐82; Thermo Fisher, 1:100), CD86 (105006; BioLegend, 1:200), and CD206 (141708; BioLegend, 1:200). After washing, sections were incubated with Alexa Fluor–conjugated secondary antibodies (Invitrogen, Alexa Fluor 488, 1:500) for 1 h at room temperature. Slides were mounted with ProLong Gold Antifade Mountant with DAPI and imaged using a Zeiss LSM 880 confocal microscope.

Immunoprecipitation (IP) and Co‐Immunoprecipitation (Co‐IP)

Cells were disrupted using IP lysis buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 1% NP‐40, phosphatase inhibitors). Lysates of cells (1–2 mg protein) were incubated overnight at 4°C in the presence of an antibody to ZXDB (2 μg per IP), EIF4A3 (2 μg per IP), or GAPDH (60004‐1‐Ig; Proteintech, 2 μg per IP) or, for exogenous co‐IP, anti‐tag antibodies (anti‐FLAG M2 affinity gel, A2220; Sigma‐Aldrich). They were then incubated in the presence of Protein A/G magnetic beads (88802; Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 2 h. Beads were washed thoroughly using lysis buffer, and immunoprecipitated proteins were retrieved and detected by Western blotting.

IP‐Mass Spectrometry (IP‐MS)

The cells of THP‐1 were lysed, and endogenous ZXDB was immunoprecipitated as mentioned above by means of anti‐ZXDB antibody or control IgG. Immunoprecipitated proteins were resolved briefly by SDS‐PAGE, cut, and treated to in‐gel digestion using trypsin. The peptides were scrutinized by liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) on a mass spectrometer Q Exactive HF (Thermo Fisher Scientific) interfacing an Easy‐nLC 1200 system. Protein identification was done using Proteome Discoverer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) searching against the UniProt human database. The potential interactors were filtered according to peptide number, score, and enrichment against the IgG control.

GST Pull‐Down Assay

The GST‐tagged ZXDB RRM domain (aa 151–300) and GST control protein were expressed in E. coli BL21(DE3) cells and were subsequently purified on Glutathione Sepharose 4B beads (17075601; GE Healthcare). His‐tagged EIF4A3 was expressed and purified. GST or GST‐RRM domain fusion proteins, which were purified and bound to beads, were left to incubate with purified EIF4A3 at 4°C overnight. Washed bound proteins were subsequently released and detected by Western blot analysis using anti‐EIF4A3 antibody.

RNA Immunoprecipitation (RIP) Assay

Magna RIP RNA‐Binding Protein Immunoprecipitation Kit (17‐700; Millipore) was used to perform RIP assays, following the manufacturer's protocol. In brief, cell lysates were incubated with antibodies to ZXDB, EIF4A3, or control IgG that were pre‐complexed with magnetic beads. Following washing, co‐precipitated RNA was recovered, and qPCR was used to quantify levels of ACACA mRNA, which were normalized to input RNA.

RNA Pull‐Down Assay

Biotinylated RNA probes corresponding to the human ACACA 5′UTR or a control sequence were synthesized in vitro using Biotin RNA Labeling Mix (11685597910; Roche) and T7 RNA polymerase. Biotinylated probes were incubated with macrophage cell lysates. RNA‐protein complexes were captured using streptavidin magnetic beads (Dynabeads M‐280 Streptavidin, 11205D; Invitrogen). After washing, bound proteins were eluted and analyzed by Western blotting for EIF4A3.

Polysome Profiling

The cells were pre‐treated with cycloheximide (C7698; Sigma‐Aldrich, 100 μg/mL) for 10 min and subsequently lysed in polysome lysis buffer. Cleared lysates were overlaid onto 10%–50% sucrose gradient that was made in gradient buffer. Gradients were centrifuged at 39000 rpm for 2 h using an SW41 Ti rotor (Beckman Coulter). Gradients were fractionated using a gradient fractionator (Brandel or BioComp) that came with an accompanying UV monitor (254 nm). RNA was recovered from fractionated samples and assessed using qPCR for ACACA mRNA distribution in monosome and polysome fractions.

Luciferase Reporter Assay

The human ACACA 5′UTR was cloned into the Firefly luciferase coding region of the pGL3‐Basic vector (E1751; Promega). Cells were co‐transfected with the ACACA 5′UTR reporter construct, a control Renilla luciferase plasmid (pRL‐TK, E2241; Promega), and corresponding expression plasmids (ZXDB OE, EIF4A3 KD). Firefly luciferase activity was detected 48 h following transfection using the Dual‐Luciferase Reporter Assay System (E1910; Promega) following the product's instructions.

Bioinformatic Analysis

The initial identification of ZXDB centered on differential analysis of sepsis‐related publicly accessible GEO datasets. Specifically, two gene expression profiles from mouse kidney tissues in sepsis models were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (NCBI): GSE211210 and GSE185263. For each dataset, raw data were normalized, and differential gene expression analysis was performed using the GEO2R online tool (based on the ‘limma’ package in R) to compare gene expression between septic and sham/control groups. Genes with an adjusted p‐value < 0.05 and an absolute log2 fold change (log2FC) > 1 were considered differentially expressed genes (DEGs) (Table S1). The common DEGs between GSE185263 and GSE211210 were identified using an online Venn diagram tool (VennPainter) to reveal the intersection of consistently altered genes in both datasets (Figure S1A).

To further prioritize key functional effectors downstream of the initial screening and to identify central regulators within the dysregulated gene network, protein–protein interaction (PPI) network analysis was performed based on the common differentially expressed genes identified from GSE185263 and GSE134347, with a particular focus on metabolic hub genes such as ACACA. Quantitative topological analysis of the PPI network, including node degree centrality, was conducted to rank highly connected hub genes, and the results of this analysis are summarized in Table S2. PPI network analysis and visualization were conducted using the STRING database (string‐db.org) and Cytoscape software (cytoscape.org). The 136 commonly differentially expressed genes identified from the Venn diagram analysis were uploaded to STRING to construct the PPI network. Only interactions with a minimum required interaction score (confidence score) > 0.4 were included. The generated PPI network was then imported into Cytoscape for further visualization and topological analysis.

Statistical Analysis

Data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analysis was carried out using GraphPad Prism (version 8.3.0). A two‐tailed unpaired Student's t‐test was used for comparisons of two groups, and one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for multiple group comparisons using Tukey's or Bonferroni's post‐test. P‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Result

ZXDB is a Driver of Pathogenic Macrophage Activation During SI‐AKI

To investigate the molecular drivers of macrophage dysfunction in sepsis‐induced acute kidney injury (SI‐AKI), we first performed a bioinformatic screen that identified the RNA‐binding protein ZXDB as a top candidate potentially involved in the pathogenesis (Figure S1A). To validate this in silico finding in a clinically relevant setting, we subjected mice to a cecal ligation and puncture (CLP) model of SI‐AKI. Histologically, SI‐AKI kidneys exhibited severe tubular injury and interstitial inflammatory cell infiltration (Figure 1A). ZXDB mRNA levels were increased by ~119% compared with sham controls, accompanied by a consistent elevation of ZXDB protein expression (Figure 1B,C). Consistent with our prediction, we observed a consistent upregulation of both ZXDB protein and mRNA levels in the kidneys of SI‐AKI mice. Given the important role of macrophages in driving renal inflammation, we sought to determine the primary cellular source of ZXDB. To investigate the cellular source of ZXDB, we examined its expression in the SI‐AKI model. In stark contrast, its expression was clearly upregulated specifically in macrophages (Figure 1D). This finding was further corroborated in vitro, where ZXDB expression was consistently induced in bone marrow‐derived macrophages (BMDMs) polarized toward an M1‐like, but not M0‐like or M2‐like, phenotype. ZXDB expression was highest in M1‐like inflammatory macrophages, showing a ~262% increase, whereas M2‐like cells exhibited a ~30% decrease relative to M0‐like controls (Figure 1E). Furthermore, stimulating these macrophages with lipopolysaccharide (LPS) to mimic an inflammatory challenge consistently increased ZXDB expression (Figure 1F–H). In RAW264.7 macrophages, LPS stimulation increased ACACA protein abundance by ~42%, whereas EIF4A3 depletion clearly attenuated this response, reducing ACACA levels by ~47% relative to LPS alone (Figure 1F). LPS also elevated Zxdb mRNA expression by ~166% (Figure 1G) and consistently increased ZXDB protein levels by ~635% in THP‐1 cells (Figure 1H). To probe the functional consequence of this upregulation, we knocked down Zxdb in macrophages using shRNA (Figure S1B,C). They were then challenged with LPS.

Notably, Zxdb knockdown conferred consistent protection against LPS‐induced stress, as evidenced by a substantial increase in cell viability and a consistent reduction in apoptosis (Figure 1I–L). It substantially restored macrophage viability at both 24 h (a ~46% increase) and 72 h (a ~32% increase) following the LPS challenge (Figure 1I,J). Mechanistically, this protection was driven by a consistent reduction in apoptosis, as Zxdb knockdown decreased the population of apoptotic cells by ~58%–61% (Figure 1K,L). Taken together, these results establish that ZXDB is selectively upregulated in pro‐inflammatory macrophages during SI‐AKI, where it drives a pro‐apoptotic and injurious phenotype.

ZXDB Orchestrates Pro‐Inflammatory Macrophage Activation by Reprogramming Glycolytic Metabolism

Having established that ZXDB mediates macrophage injury, we next sought to determine its role in governing macrophage activation states during an inflammatory challenge. To this end, we assessed the impact of Zxdb knockdown on LPS‐stimulated macrophages. ZXDB acts as an important modulator regulating LPS‐driven macrophage polarization. LPS consistently expanded M1‐like (CD86+iNOS+) macrophages (~428% in RAW264.7; ~434% in THP‐1), an effect consistently abrogated by ZXDB knockdown (~64% and ~63% reduction, respectively). In contrast, ZXDB depletion selectively promoted M2a‐like (CD206+Arg1+) polarization, increasing this subset by ~564% in RAW264.7 and ~1128% in THP‐1 cells, while M2b‐like (CD86+IL‐10+) macrophages were only moderately induced by LPS (~158% in RAW264.7; ~205% in THP‐1) and not further altered upon ZXDB knockdown (Figure 2A,B). This coordinated phenotypic shift was confirmed at the molecular level, where Zxdb knockdown simultaneously suppressed key inflammatory‐associated markers (e.g., reducing iNOS protein by ~57%) and consistently restored M2‐like markers (e.g., increasing CD206 protein by ~573%) across multiple datasets (Figure 2C,D and Figure S2A–D). Consistent with this substantial phenotypic switch, ZXDB knockdown notably reshaped the cytokine secretion profile of activated macrophages. The LPS‐induced surge in pro‐inflammatory cytokines (TNF‐α, IL‐1β, IFN‐γ) was consistently attenuated by ~40%–49% upon Zxdb knockdown. Conversely, the secretion of anti‐inflammatory cytokines (IL‐10, IL‐4, IL‐13), which was suppressed by LPS, was consistently restored by ~55%–78%. This pattern was observed across multiple cell models (Figure 2E,F). These data indicate that ZXDB is an important regulator of the inflammatory output of macrophages. Furthermore, given the intimate link between M1‐like macrophage activation and a glycolytic metabolic state [24], we investigated the metabolic consequences of ZXDB depletion. Consistent with its role in polarization, ZXDB also governs metabolic reprogramming. Its depletion counteracted the LPS‐induced glycolytic shift, reversing changes in key metabolic markers by approximately ~22%–60% (Figure 2G–J). This metabolic antagonism solidifies ZXDB's role as an orchestrator of a comprehensive pro‐inflammatory program.

ZXDB Promotes Macrophage Activation by Regulating the Expression of the Metabolic Enzyme ACACA

To identify downstream effectors of the ZXDB‐driven pro‐inflammatory program, we integrated cross‐dataset transcriptomic screening with PPI network analysis. Common DEGs identified from the initial screening were subjected to STRING‐based network modeling, which highlighted fatty acid biosynthesis as a core process and prioritized ACACA as a highly connected hub gene (Figure 3A,B and Table S2). The full list of these common DEGs is provided in Table S1. Consistent with a role in inflammation [25], we found that ACACA expression was induced during pro‐inflammatory (M1‐like) macrophage activation, with Acaca mRNA increasing by ~125% and its protein by ~136%. Conversely, both were reduced during M2‐like polarization (~29% and ~56%, respectively) relative to M0 macrophages (Figure S3A,B). Critically, Zxdb knockdown demonstrated that ZXDB is essential for ACACA induction, attenuating the LPS‐induced increase in ACACA protein (~92% reduction) while consistently attenuating its mRNA response by ~46% (Figure 3C,D). To consistently establish ACACA as the critical downstream mediator of ZXDB's function, we performed a series of rescue experiments. Restoration of ACACA consistently counteracted the survival advantage conferred by ZXDB depletion. In both RAW264.7 and THP‐1 macrophages, ACACA re‐expression consistently increased apoptotic cell fractions compared with ZXDB knockdown alone, resulting in an approximate ~97% increase in apoptosis. These data indicate that ACACA is required for maintaining the apoptosis‐resistant phenotype observed in ZXDB‐deficient macrophages (Figure 3E–H). Flow cytometric analyses demonstrated that restoration of ACACA partially reinstated the M1‐like phenotype (CD86+iNOS+) suppressed by ZXDB knockdown, while consistently constraining the expansion of the M2a‐like subset (CD206+Arg1+). In contrast, the M2b‐like population (CD86+IL‐10+) exhibited only modest alterations upon ACACA re‐expression (Figure 3I,J). Consistently, transcriptional analyses revealed that ACACA restoration partially rescued the expression of M1‐like associated genes (CD80, CD86, CD40, iNOS) while consistently suppressing M2a‐associated markers (CD206, CD163, ARG1) induced by ZXDB depletion (Figure S3C–G). Consistent with its effects on macrophage polarization, ACACA re‐expression reinstated inflammatory cytokine secretion suppressed by ZXDB depletion. Specifically, levels of pro‐inflammatory cytokines such as IL‐1β were increased by ~113%, accompanied by comparable restoration of other inflammatory mediators. These results indicate that ACACA is essential for sustaining the inflammatory output of macrophages downstream of ZXDB signaling (Figure 3K,L). At the metabolic level, ACACA re‐expression abolished the protective metabolic phenotype induced by ZXDB depletion. Restoration of ACACA led to a ~31% reduction in intracellular ATP levels and a ~29% increase in lactate production, indicating a shift toward a metabolically stressed, pro‐inflammatory state (Figure 3M–P).

ZXDB Recruits the Translation Factor EIF4A3 via Its RRM Domain to Enhance ACACA mRNA Translation

Given that ZXDB is an RNA‐binding protein, we hypothesized that it regulates ACACA post‐transcriptionally by recruiting specific cofactors [26]. Our search for ZXDB‐interacting proteins via proteomic analysis identified the eukaryotic translation initiation factor 4A3 (EIF4A3) as a high‐confidence binding partner (Figure 4A,B), while noting that EIF4A3 expression itself was stable across macrophage polarization states (Figure S4A,B). We confirmed a direct physical interaction between endogenous ZXDB and EIF4A3 using co‐immunoprecipitation and further observed their co‐localization within the cytoplasm of macrophages (Figure 4C–E). Both shRNAs efficiently depleted EIF4A3, reducing its mRNA to ~74% (shEIF4A3–1) and ~85% (shEIF4A3–2) of control levels (Figure S4C). To establish the functional significance of this interaction, we found that EIF4A3 is required for the inflammatory induction of ACACA. Knockdown of EIF4A3 was sufficient to blunt the LPS‐induced upregulation of ACACA, reducing its mRNA levels by ~48% and its protein abundance by ~47% (Figure S4D,E). Critically, we found that EIF4A3 was required for ZXDB's regulatory function. To confirm the functional hierarchy, we showed that ZXDB's ability to upregulate ACACA is largely dependent on EIF4A3. While ZXDB overexpression drove a ~242% surge in Acaca mRNA, co‐silencing EIF4A3 cut this induction by approximately half (~52% reduction) (Figure 4F,G). Although ACACA expression was not completely abolished upon EIF4A3 depletion, the attenuated effect of ZXDB in the absence of EIF4A3 indicates that EIF4A3 contributes functionally to ZXDB‐mediated enhancement of ACACA expression. These findings suggest that EIF4A3 is an important component of this regulatory process, while additional EIF4A3‐independent mechanisms may also be involved [27]. Accordingly, we hypothesized that the regulation occurs at the translational level. Indeed, polysome profiling revealed that ZXDB overexpression consistently increased the association of ACACA mRNA with actively translating polysomes (Figure 4H). Our data indicate that ZXDB and EIF4A3 co‐regulate ACACA at the translational level. ZXDB's ability to enhance ACACA 5′UTR‐driven luciferase activity (~142% increase) was dependent on EIF4A3 (Figure 4I). Supporting this, ChIP‐qPCR revealed enrichment of EIF4A3 at the ACACA promoter region, likely reflecting indirect or complex‐mediated association, showing enrichments of ~285% and ~233%, respectively (Figure 4J,K). And a direct binding assay confirmed that EIF4A3 can interact with the ACACA 5′ UTR (Figure 4L). Thus, ZXDB functions as a specific translational enhancer for ACACA mRNA by actively recruiting the EIF4A3‐containing translation machinery to its 5′ UTR. To pinpoint the structural basis for this interaction, we performed structure–function analyses using a series of ZXDB deletion mutants. This approach identified the RRM (RNA Recognition Motif) domain (amino acids 151–300) as the specific region required for its interaction with EIF4A3 (Figure 4M–O). Taken together, these data delineate that ZXDB utilizes its RRM domain to recruit the translation factor EIF4A3 to the 5′ UTR of ACACA mRNA, thereby enhancing its translation and driving the downstream pro‐inflammatory program.

The ZXDB‐EIF4A3 Interaction Is Required for the Pro‐Inflammatory Function of ZXDB

To consistently test whether the physical interaction between ZXDB and EIF4A3 is the critical event driving ZXDB's pro‐inflammatory function, we engineered a mutant ZXDB (ZXDB‐MUT) with alterations in its RRM domain that selectively abrogate EIF4A3 binding. As expected, co‐immunoprecipitation confirmed that ZXDB‐MUT failed to interact with EIF4A3 (Figure 5A). We next assessed the functional consequence of this single molecular disruption. In stark contrast to its wild‐type (WT) counterpart, ZXDB‐MUT was completely incapable of increasing ACACA protein levels or enhancing ACACA mRNA translation upon inflammatory stimulation (Figure 5B–D). Remarkably, disrupting the ZXDB‐EIF4A3 interaction via a targeted mutation (ZXDB‐MUT) completely abolished the pathogenic functions of ZXDB. When overexpressed in LPS‐stimulated macrophages, ZXDB‐MUT was functionally inert, failing to exacerbate cell death, promote M1‐like macrophage activation, trigger a cytokine storm, or induce a glycolytic shift. Specifically, while wild‐type ZXDB clearly increased apoptosis by ~285%, the ZXDB‐MUT construct reversed this effect, reducing apoptosis by ~56%. Similarly, the mutant lost its ability to drive M1‐like macrophage activation; it failed to induce the CD86+ inflammatory population and nearly eliminated the pro‐inflammatory transcriptional program, as evidenced by a ~79%–82% reduction in key M1‐like genes like iNOS and CD86. This loss of function was also reflected in the cytokine profile, where the mutant failed to amplify pro‐inflammatory cytokines like TNF‐α and IL‐1β. Finally, the metabolic effects were also reversed, with the mutant unable to drive the ~141% increase in lactate production seen with wild‐type ZXDB. Together, these data demonstrate that the pro‐inflammatory activity of ZXDB is strictly dependent on its ability to interact with EIF4A3 (Figure 5E–I, S5A–D). Thus, the ZXDB‐EIF4A3 interaction is essential for the translational upregulation of ACACA and the subsequent pro‐inflammatory macrophage response.

Macrophage‐Specific Deletion of Zxdb Protects Against Sepsis‐Induced Acute Kidney Injury In Vivo

To ascertain the physiological and pathological relevance of our findings, we investigated whether targeting ZXDB specifically in macrophages could mitigate SI‐AKI in vivo. To this end, we generated myeloid‐specific Zxdb knockout mice (Lyz2‐Cre; Zxdbfl/fl, hereafter Mac‐Zxdb‐KO) and subjected them and their wild‐type (WT) littermates to the CLP model of SI‐AKI (Figure 6A). Macrophage‐specific deletion of Zxdb provided protection against SI‐AKI. Compared with WT mice, Mac‐Zxdb‐KO mice displayed an attenuation of renal pathology, including reduced tubular injury and inflammation, together with partial preservation of tissue architecture (Figure 6B). This histological protection corresponded with improved renal function, as Mac‐Zxdb‐KO mice showed consistent reductions in serum creatinine (~24%) and blood urea nitrogen (BUN) (~32%) compared to WT SI‐AKI controls (Figure 6C,D). This protection was confirmed at the tissue level, where Mac‐Zxdb‐KO mice showed consistent reductions in both the injury marker KIM‐1 (~65% decrease) and in parenchymal apoptosis (~81% decrease) compared to WT controls (Figure 6E,F). At the cellular level, immunofluorescence analysis revealed a consistent shift in the renal macrophage landscape. While the kidneys of WT mice were heavily infiltrated with pro‐inflammatory M1‐like (F4/80+CD86+) macrophages, the kidneys of Mac‐Zxdb‐KO mice displayed a consistent skew toward a reparative M2‐like (F4/80+CD206+) phenotype (Figure 6G). Macrophage‐specific Zxdb deletion rebalanced the systemic cytokine response to SI‐AKI. It consistently attenuated the pro‐inflammatory surge, reducing both IL‐1β and TNF‐α by ~25%, while concurrently restoring the anti‐inflammatory cytokine IL‐10 by ~62% compared to WT controls (Figure 6H). These data demonstrate that Zxdb amplifies the pro‐inflammatory cytokine milieu during SI‐AKI. Collectively, these in vivo data provide convincing evidence that macrophage‐intrinsic ZXDB is a critical driver of SI‐AKI pathogenesis. Notably, macrophage‐specific Zxdb deletion improved multiple injury parameters, with some readouts approaching sham levels, while others showed partial but consistent improvement.

Our final model illustrates a pathogenic cascade wherein, under inflammatory conditions, the RBP ZXDB recruits EIF4A3 to enhance ACACA mRNA translation, and the resulting surge in ACACA protein orchestrates a dual program of glycolytic reprogramming and M1‐like macrophage activation, culminating in a cytokine storm that drives acute kidney injury (SI‐AKI) (Figure 7).

Schematic diagram of the proposed mechanism by which ZXDB promotes M1‐like macrophage polarization and exacerbates SI‐AKI. In macrophage, ZXDB interacts with EIF4A3, promoting the translation of the ACACA gene. The resulting increase in ACACA protein expression enhances glycolysis and lactate production. This metabolic reprogramming shifts macrophage polarization toward M1‐like macrophage activation (characterized by increased Cd86, Cd80, Cd40, and iNOS) and away from an anti‐inflammatory M2‐like phenotype (characterized by Cd206, Cd163, and Arg1). The dominance of M1‐like macrophages leads to an elevated secretion of pro‐inflammatory cytokines (TNF‐α, IFN‐γ, IL‐1β) and reduced anti‐inflammatory cytokines (IL‐10, IL‐4, IL‐13), which collectively drive the pathogenesis of acute kidney injury.

Discussion

Sepsis‐induced acute kidney injury (SI‐AKI) remains a formidable clinical challenge, largely driven by dysregulated macrophage‐mediated inflammation [28, 29]. In this study, we unveil a novel post‐transcriptional regulatory axis, ZXDB‐EIF4A3‐ACACA, that acts as a previously uncharacterized regulator of pathogenic macrophage activation in SI‐AKI. Our findings demonstrate that ZXDB drives a pro‐inflammatory metabolic program by recruiting the translation machinery to ACACA mRNA, thereby promoting M1‐like macrophage activation and exacerbating renal injury. This work bridges a critical gap in our understanding, delineating a direct mechanistic link between an RNA‐binding protein, translational control, metabolic reprogramming, and organ‐specific inflammation, thus offering new therapeutic avenues for this devastating condition.

We acknowledge that CD86 is not an exclusive marker of classical M1‐like inflammatory macrophages and can also be expressed by other inflammatory subsets, including M2b‐like macrophages. Therefore, in this study CD86 was interpreted as an activation‐associated costimulatory marker rather than a consistent indicator of pro‐inflammatory (M1‐like) macrophage activation. To strengthen phenotypic inference, macrophage activation states were operationally defined using multiparametric marker combinations (e.g., CD86 with iNOS or IL‐10, and CD206 with Arg1) together with functional readouts (cytokine secretion and metabolic changes). Accordingly, we use the term ‘M1‐like’ as an operational descriptor of a pro‐inflammatory activation program rather than a rigid polarization category.

Our identification of ZXDB positions it as a unique and potentially superior therapeutic target in the context of SI‐AKI. While previous research has extensively characterized canonical inflammatory pathways such as TLR/NF‐κB signaling and NLRP3 inflammasome activation [30, 31], these pathways are often broadly expressed and function as downstream effectors, making their therapeutic inhibition prone to systemic immunosuppression [32, 33]. In contrast, ZXDB appears to function as an upstream hub, with its expression selectively and rapidly induced in pathogenic macrophages during the early onset of sepsis [34, 35]. This offers a potentially wider and earlier therapeutic window. The specific upregulation of ZXDB in macrophages suggests that targeting ZXDB could achieve a high degree of cellular specificity, minimizing off‐target effects that plague broader anti‐inflammatory strategies.

A central tenet of our findings is the tight coupling of inflammation and metabolism, a concept of growing importance in immunology known as “immunometabolism” [36, 37]. The pro‐inflammatory M1‐like macrophage phenotype is inextricably linked to a metabolic shift toward aerobic glycolysis, often termed the “Warburg effect,” which provides rapid ATP and biosynthetic precursors for inflammatory functions [38]. Our study mechanistically anchors ZXDB to this process via ACACA. By enhancing the translation of ACACA, the rate‐limiting enzyme for de novo fatty acid synthesis, ZXDB likely rewires cellular metabolism to not only support membrane biogenesis for phagocytosis but also to influence acetyl‐CoA flux, which could indirectly amplify glycolytic pathways [39]. This highlights a crucial insight: pathogenic inflammation is not merely a signaling event but a metabolically active state. Intervening at the metabolic level, as our data suggest, may offer a more consistent and durable therapeutic effect than simply blocking individual downstream cytokines, which often exist in a redundant and compensatory network [40, 41].

Mechanistically, our study breaks new ground by highlighting the primacy of translational control in governing the immune response, a domain that has been historically overshadowed by studies on transcriptional and epigenetic regulation [28, 42]. We demonstrate that ZXDB does not act alone but functions by recruiting the translation initiation factor EIF4A3. The RRM domain‐mediated interaction represents a sophisticated mechanism for selective translational enhancement, ensuring that specific transcripts like ACACA are prioritized for translation amidst a global inflammatory challenge. This finding adds to the emerging paradigm that RBPs act as dynamic “rheostats” of protein synthesis, fine‐tuning the cellular proteome with a speed and specificity that transcription alone cannot achieve [43]. Furthermore, EIF4A3 itself is a fascinating factor, implicated in both normal cellular processes and pathologies like cancer [44, 45], suggesting that the ZXDB‐EIF4A3 partnership could be a context‐dependent regulatory module with broader implications beyond SI‐AKI.

The identification of ACACA as the lynchpin effector of the ZXDB pathway underscores its central and druggable role. As the key enzyme for fatty acid synthesis, ACACA stands at the crossroads of metabolism and inflammation, making it a “two birds, one stone” therapeutic target [25]. Its inhibition not only disrupts the metabolic fuel supply for M1‐like inflammatory macrophages but may also directly modulate inflammatory signaling pathways that rely on lipid mediators [46]. Crucially, ACACA is already a validated drug target in other fields. Pharmacological inhibitors such as Soraphen A, TOFA, and the clinical candidate CP‐640186 have shown efficacy in models of metabolic disease and cancer [47, 48]. Our findings provide a strong rationale for repurposing these existing agents or developing novel ACACA inhibitors specifically for the treatment of inflammatory conditions like SI‐AKI, a strategy with a potentially accelerated path to clinical translation.

The therapeutic promise of this axis was ultimately validated by our in vivo experiments. The measurable protection against SI‐AKI observed in macrophage‐specific Zxdb knockout mice provides strong genetic evidence for the pathogenic role of macrophage‐intrinsic ZXDB. This cell‐type‐specific approach is critical, as it proves that the observed phenotype is not due to systemic or off‐target effects, but is directly attributable to the function of ZXDB within the key pathogenic cell type. This targeted genetic validation paves the way for translational strategies aimed at selectively modulating macrophage function. The development of macrophage‐targeting nanomedicines, for instance, which can deliver siRNAs against ZXDB or small‐molecule inhibitors of ACACA, represents a feasible and highly attractive clinical approach to replicate the therapeutic efficacy seen in our model [49].

Despite the comprehensive nature of our study, we acknowledge several limitations that open avenues for future investigation. First, our work primarily focuses on the acute, injurious phase of SI‐AKI; the role of the ZXDB‐ACACA axis during the subsequent resolution and repair phases remains unknown. Second, while we identified ACACA as a critical target, it is plausible that the ZXDB‐EIF4A3 complex co‐regulates a broader suite of mRNAs that collectively contribute to the inflammatory phenotype. Third, our findings, while consistent in murine models, require further validation in human tissues and larger patient cohorts. Future work should therefore employ single‐cell translational profiling (e.g., scRibo‐seq) to map the complete regulon of ZXDB in different macrophage subsets. Preclinical studies using pharmacological ACACA inhibitors are warranted to confirm the therapeutic potential. Finally, validating the expression and prognostic significance of ZXDB in a well‐defined clinical SI‐AKI cohort will be essential for its development as a biomarker and therapeutic target.

In line with the current understanding in the field, we concede that reducing macrophage polarization to a simple M1‐like vs. M2‐like dichotomy is an oversimplification. Macrophage activation is a dynamic and continuous spectrum, and these cells demonstrate remarkable plasticity in response to their microenvironment [50]. The M1‐like/M2‐like model, while conceptually useful, particularly in in vitro settings, fails to account for the existence of numerous intermediate and mixed activation states observed in vivo [51]. In this study, macrophage activation states were assessed using an operational framework (including M1‐like, M2a‐like, and M2b‐like phenotypes) to describe the dominant functional skewing under our experimental conditions. We propose that future investigations should move beyond this binary framework, perhaps by adopting a stimulus‐based nomenclature (e.g., M(LPS), M(IL‐4)) or by utilizing multi‐parameter approaches to better define macrophage heterogeneity [52, 53]. Such nuanced approaches will be vital for a more precise understanding of the multifaceted role of macrophages in SI‐AKI.

In conclusion, our study delineates a novel, hierarchical ZXDB‐EIF4A3‐ACACA axis that governs pathogenic macrophage activation and drives the progression of SI‐AKI. This work not only uncovers a fundamental mechanism linking translational control to immunometabolism but also identifies a “translation‐metabolism” continuum as a potent, dual‐vulnerability therapeutic target. We envision that developing precision therapies, such as small‐molecule inhibitors or RBP‐targeting RNA therapeutics, perhaps in combination with metabolic modulators, will pave the way for a new paradigm in treating sepsis‐induced organ injury and other macrophage‐driven inflammatory diseases.
