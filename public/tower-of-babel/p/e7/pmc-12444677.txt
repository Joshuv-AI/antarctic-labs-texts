High Quantum Yield Nitrogen and Boron Codoped Orange Fluorescent Carbon Quantum Dots for Antibacterial and Bacterial Imaging
Authors: Taofeng Zhang, Maolong Chen, Hongli Wang, Yu Wang, Zexuan Liu, Tan Chen, Fang Wang, Dan Xu, Ai-Mei Yang, Hui Wang, Zhencun Cui
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Long-wavelength fluorescent carbon quantum dots, as an emerging nanomaterial, could effectively overcome the problems of low detection efficiency, strong antibacterial resistance, and severe imaging interference of traditional methods in the diagnosis and therapy of bacterial infections. Herein, an innovative design of nitrogen and boron codoped orange fluorescent carbon quantum dots (NB-CDs) with both bacterial recognition and binding, long-wavelength fluorescence fast imaging, and broad-spectrum antimicrobial function was developed. The diagnostic and therapeutic integrated nanoplatform was successfully constructed by the solvothermal method using citric acid (CA) as the carbon source, safranine T (ST) as the nitrogen dopant, and 3-aminobenzeneboronic acid (M-APBA) as the boron source. The carbon quantum dots possessed orange fluorescence emission (λEm = 581 nm) and high quantum yield (QY) (41.50%), which could effectively avoid the interference of tissue spontaneous short-wavelength fluorescence. The boronic acid group on the surface of the carbon quantum dots is beneficial for covalent binding to bacteria, achieving a synergistic function of antimicrobial and imaging. Meanwhile, the carbon quantum dots exhibited significant antibacterial activity against both Escherichia coli (E. coli) and Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus) (bactericidal rate > 99%), with the minimum bactericidal concentrations (MBC) being 6.90 and 6.13 mg/mL, respectively, and enabled rapid fluorescence labeling imaging of bacteria within 1 h. In this study, an integrated diagnostic and therapeutic system was constructed by combining the binding ability of boric acid groups to bacteria with the optical properties of long-wavelength orange fluorescent carbon quantum dots, providing a new avenue for the development of smart antibacterial materials.

Introduction

Bacterial infections represent a global threat to human health, and global public health systems are facing serious challenges from infectious diseases caused by multidrug-resistant bacteria (MDR). According to the World Health Organization (WHO), the number of deaths caused by the problem of antibiotic resistance is increasing every year, with millions of cases reported each year and the number expected to rise to 10 million by 2050. The overuse of antibiotics is a major cause of the rapid development of drug resistance, which is causing many traditional antimicrobial drugs to gradually lose their efficacy. Traditional diagnosis of bacterial infections relies on microbiological culture and drug sensitivity tests, which are time-consuming and have low sensitivity. Currently used fluorescent imaging probes, such as green fluorescent protein and fluorescein, have obvious limitations, such as an inability to be exogenously labeled, susceptibility to photobleaching, limited emission wavelengths, and biological toxicity. , Moreover, traditional antimicrobial drugs such as antibiotics cannot solve the problem of bacterial resistance during treatment. Therefore, the research of new antibacterial nanomaterials and technology systems for diagnosis and treatment integration with both fast imaging and efficient antibacterial function has become an urgent need.

Carbon quantum dots (CQDs), as an emerging nanomaterial, have received much attention in the biomedical field in recent years due to their excellent optical properties, low toxicity, and good biocompatibility, and have been used in biochemical detection, optical devices, bioimaging, drug delivery, and disease treatment. The luminescence mechanism of carbon quantum dots mainly originates from their surface states and quantum-limited domain effects, and multicolour luminescence from the ultraviolet to the near-infrared can be achieved by modulating their surface chemical structures and sizes. Studies have shown that short-wavelength fluorescence (e.g., blue, 450–500 nm) is susceptible to tissue autofluorescence (e.g., emission of NADH, elastin at ∼480 nm) and Rayleigh scattering interference in bioimaging, resulting in a reduced signal-to-noise ratio, long-wave penetration depth due to deeper tissue penetration, and lower autofluorescence background. , In addition, orange fluorescent dots avoid channel interference with common biomarkers (e.g., DAPI, Hoechst), making it suitable for multicolour colocalization analysis. Therefore, carbon quantum dots with longer wavelengths are more conducive to biological detection and imaging. The introduction of heteroatoms can significantly regulate the electronic structure, surface states, and band gap of carbon quantum dots, thereby optimizing their fluorescence properties. For example, Deng et al. reported that S, N-doped carbon dots exhibit a high fluorescence QY, excellent photostability, and outstanding biocompatibility, making them suitable for the detection of 6-thioguanine and its bioimaging. The electronegativity of N is close to that of C. However, N atoms provide lone-pair electrons, which can introduce electron-rich surface states, narrow the energy gap of carbon quantum dots, and promote the long-wavelength emission (redshift). N doping passivates the surface nonradiative recombination sites and reduces the energy loss. Also, the empty orbitals of B atoms can act as electron traps and change the charge distribution, thus enhancing the QY of carbon quantum dots. For example, Deng et al. reported a B,N-codoped carbon dot with an absolute fluorescence QY of up to 86.58% for sensing and imaging. Qiu et al. reported a B,S,N-doped carbon dot with red fluorescence and high QY for bioimaging.

In addition to excellent optical properties, carbon quantum dots exhibit significant antibacterial activity. Carbon quantum dots with a size of less than 10 nm have excellent membrane permeability and can inhibit the bacterial efflux pump mechanism. Their antimicrobial mechanisms mainly include cell membrane disruption, reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation, and interference with bacterial metabolism. For example, Gedanken et al. reported that nitrogen-doped multifunctional carbon dots can be used to eliminate Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacteria by generating ROS. Pandiyan et al. reported that a carbon quantum dot derived from sugar cane was used for antibacterial purposes. The antibacterial mechanism was that the functional groups of the carbon quantum dots might interfere with the functions of cellular enzymes and inhibit cell proliferation. The bactericidal effect of nitrogen–boron doped carbon dots reported by Gedanken et al. is attributed to the positive surface charges of the doped carbon dots and the generation of ROS, which cause damage to the bacterial membrane and lead to cell death. However, traditional antibacterial agents usually have only a single antibacterial mechanism in their applications, which leads to the development of bacterial resistance. Therefore, the development of new antibacterial drugs has become a current research hotspot.

In the preparation of carbon quantum dots, citric acid (CA) serves as the core of the carbon skeleton, and the sp2 carbon domains formed by its dehydration condensation can provide the substrate for fluorescence emission, while the carboxyl and hydroxyl groups located on the surface endow the carbon quantum dots with excellent water dispersibility and pH stability. , Safranine T (ST) is a common biological dye with good photostability and biocompatibility, and as a nitrogen-doping source, its phenazine structure introduces an intramolecular charge transfer (ICT) effect and extends the π-conjugated system to shift the fluorescent red. The boronic acid moiety of 3-aminobenzeneboronic acid (M-APBA) can form a reversible covalent bond with the cis-diol structure of peptidoglycan and lipopolysaccharides on the surface of the bacteria, which enables carbon quantum dots to recognize and bind to bacteria, causing the bacterial cell membrane to rupture and killing the bacteria. Through the synergistic effect between precursor substances, antibacterial and rapid bacterial labeling imaging can be achieved.

Currently, a variety of carbon quantum dots with bacterial diagnostic function have been investigated and prepared, but they generally face the problems of short fluorescence emission wavelength, low QY, and low bacterial binding ability. In this study, the orange fluorescent carbon quantum dots with antibacterial activity were designed via a heteroatom codoping strategy (Scheme a), which could exert antimicrobial activity while imaging. Moreover, the carbon quantum dots had a high fluorescence QY and long emission wavelength (λEm = 581 nm), with excellent photostability and water solubility, and exhibited significant antimicrobial activity against both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria. Meanwhile, the carbon quantum dots had rapid bacterial labeling and imaging capabilities (Scheme b). This study innovatively combined a boronic acid group bacterial recognition and binding strategy with the high QY orange fluorescent carbon quantum dot design to achieve an integrated diagnosis and treatment of rapid binding imaging and efficient antimicrobials.

Results and Discussion

Synthesis and Characterization of NB-CDs

To achieve bacterial-specific recognition and efficient antibacterial-imaging synergy, Citric acid, Safranine T, and 3-aminobenzeneboronic acid were used as precursors, and solvent-heat processing was carried out at 180 °C to obtain pink solid NB-CDs. The morphology, distribution, and size characteristics of NB-CD were characterized by transmission electron microscope (TEM). As shown in Figure a, the NB-CDs were uniformly distributed spherical shapes with particle sizes ranging from 1.39 to 5.07 nm, with an average size of 2.75 nm. The lattice fringes of the NB-CDs could be clearly observed in the high-resolution TEM images, indicating that the NB-CDs had a crystal structure, and the lattice spacing of the individual NB-CDs was 0.21 nm. The crystal structure of NB-CDs can be characterized by X-ray diffraction (XRD). The lattice spacing of graphene (002) corresponded to the diffraction plane of graphitic carbon (101), suggesting that the carbon cores of the NB-CDs were crystalline grains with good crystallinity. The narrow and sharp peaks in the XRD pattern of NB-CDs further verified the crystallinity of NB-CDs, indicating that the NB-CDs were polycrystalline and similar to the XRD pattern of polymer carbon quantum dots, with the main peaks at 18.02, 25.92, 35.94, and 43.16°(Figure b). , The particle surface charged state of NB-CDs was characterized by zeta potential testing. As shown in Figure c, zeta potential measurements showed that the potentials of citric acid, Safranine T, and 3-aminophenylboronic acid were +10.46, −0.56, and −27.60 mV, respectively, and the potential of the NB-CDs was +12.12 mV (Figure c). Since the cell walls of Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria contain phosphate groups (−PO4 2–), which are dissociated at physiological pH, and the surface is negatively charged, positively charged NB-CDs are more likely to be tightly bound to bacteria through electrostatic attraction.

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR) was used to analyze the chemical bonds and surface functional groups of NB-CDs by detecting the absorption peaks of the molecules on infrared light. As shown in Figure d, the absorption peaks in the FT-IR spectrum in the range of 600–940 cm–1 were attributed to the bending vibrations of O–H and N–H (δO–H/δN–H). This vibrational frequency is relatively low and is mainly influenced by the strength of the hydrogen bonds. The characteristic peaks at 1746 and 1709 cm–1 corresponded to the asymmetric stretching vibrations of CO (νCO). The peak near 1746 cm–1 is usually attributed to the CO stretching vibration in free carboxylic acids or esters, whereas the lower frequency at 1709 cm–1 strongly suggests the presence of a hydrogen-bonded carboxyl group (−COOH). While the peaks at 3496 and 3294 cm–1 were attributed to the O–H and N–H stretching vibrations, the broader peak at 3294 cm–1 points more typically to the N–H stretching vibrations (especially the primary amine, NH2). Also, these characteristic peaks indicated that the surface of NB-CDs is rich in hydrophilic functional groups such as hydroxyl, carboxyl, and amino. , The presence of these functional groups endowed NB-CDs with hydrophilicity and stability in aqueous solutions. In addition, the absorption peaks in the range of 1100–1200 cm–1 could be attributed to the stretching vibration of C–O and C–C. The characteristic peaks at 1390 and 1426 cm–1 corresponded to the stretching vibrations of O–B and C–N, respectively. The results showed that boron (B) and nitrogen (N) elements were successfully doped into the carbon skeleton of the NB-CDs.

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) can be used to characterize the elemental composition, chemical bonding states, and electronic structure of material surfaces. In order to investigate the chemical structure of the NB-CDs, the XPS spectrum was analyzed. As shown in Figure e, the NB-CDs were mainly composed of carbon (C 1s, 53.56%), boron (B 1s, 1.2%), nitrogen (N 1s, 0.91%), and oxygen (O 1s, 44.33%) elements, which further demonstrated that nitrogen and boron were successfully doped into the NB-CDs. The high-resolution C 1s spectrum in Figure f showed three peaks at 284.8, 286.7, and 288.9 eV, corresponding to CC/C–C, C–N/C–O, and CO, respectively. The high-resolution N 1s spectrum displayed three peaks at 399.3, 400.0, and 401.5 eV, indicating that the surface nitrogen of the NB-CDs mainly existed in the form of pyridine N, −NH2, and pyrrole N (Figure g). , The binding energies at 532.1, 533.0, and 533.9 eV were attributed to CO, C–OH, and B–O, respectively (Figure h). For the spectrum of B 1s, the peaks located at 192.1 and 192.7 eV belonged to B–C and B–O (Figure i). The above results showed that the surface of the NB-CDs contains hydrophilic functional groups such as hydroxyl, carboxyl, and amino groups, which makes NB-CDs have good water solubility and are helpful for antibacterial and imaging applications.

Fluorescence Properties and Stability of NB-CDs

Analysis of the fluorescence emission centers of NB-CDs by 3D scanning with a fluorescence spectrophotometer. The optimal excitation wavelength of NB-CDs was determined to be 553 nm, and the maximum emission wavelength (λEm) was observed at 581 nm (Figure a). At an excitation wavelength (λEx) of 553 nm, the NB-CDs exhibited significant orange fluorescence emission characteristics, and the maximum emission wavelength was located at 581 nm. In addition, the electronic transition properties of NB-CDs were determined by UV–vis absorption spectroscopy. As shown in Figure b, the UV absorption spectrum showed two characteristic absorption peaks in the range 200–700 nm. The strong absorption peak at 230 nm was attributed to the π–π* electronic transition of the CC bond, while the absorption peak at 350 nm corresponded to the n−π* transition of the CO and CN bonds. Furthermore, a wide absorption band appeared in the lower energy range of 500–600 nm (λAbs = 552 nm), which may be due to the absorption of the aromatic domain in the carbon core, which is consistent with the reported characteristic absorption behavior of carbon quantum dots.

Notably, the maximum emission wavelength of the NB-CDs aqueous solution remained unchanged in the excitation wavelength range 503–573 nm, which exhibited excitation wavelength independence (Figure c). That is to say, when NB-CDs are excited by light of different wavelengths, their emission wavelengths remain unchanged, while only the fluorescence intensity changes. In addition, when the excitation wavelength was increased from 503 to 553 nm, the fluorescence emission intensity was gradually enhanced. However, when the excitation wavelength exceeded 553 nm, the fluorescence emission intensity showed a downward trend. This excitation wavelength-dependent fluorescence emission intensity change may be related to the surface state and energy level structure of the NB-CDs. Quantum yield (QY) is a quantification of fluorescence efficiency. The higher the QY, the higher the efficiency of the carbon dots in converting the absorbed light energy into fluorescence, and the stronger the photoluminescence performance of the material. The QY of NB-CDs was calculated to be 41.50%, which indicates that the NB-CDs have excellent optical properties, and their fluorescence QY is significantly higher than that of most reported carbon quantum dots (Table ). Moreover, the NB-CDs achieved long-wavelength fluorescence emission while maintaining high QY, which makes them have important application potential in the field of biological imaging.

In addition, the stability of NB-CDs was systematically evaluated by photobleaching resistance, pH-dependent, and ion concentration resistance experiments. As shown in Figure d, the NB-CDs exhibited excellent fluorescence stability during the observation period of up to 1 week after exposure to light, and their fluorescence intensity was consistently maintained at more than 80% of the initial value, indicating that they had good antiphotobleaching. Furthermore, the pH stability results of the NB-CDs showed that they could maintain stable fluorescence emission wavelength and intensity in the range pH 4–9 (Figure e). Fluorescence stability across a wide pH range is critical for bioimaging because the pH of biological systems (e.g., cytoplasm, organelles, body fluids) typically varies within this range. The results of ion concentration resistance experiments showed that the fluorescence intensity of NB-CDs remained stable when dispersed in different metal ions and different salt-negative ions (Figure g,h). The above results indicated that the NB-CDs had good environmental adaptability and stability, which laid the foundation for application in complex biological systems. In addition, the fluorescence properties of NB-CDs dispersed in different solvents revealed that NB-CDs exhibited the strongest fluorescence intensity and long-wavelength emission characteristics in water and PBS buffer (Figure f), which provided an advantage for their application in bioimaging.

In Vitro Antibacterial Activity of NB-CDs

The antibacterial properties of the NB-CDs were evaluated by minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) and minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC) using Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus) and Escherichia coli (E. coli) as model strains. Different concentrations of NB-CD solutions were mixed and incubated with the two bacteria separately to assess the bacterial survival, and the results demonstrated that NB-CDs exhibited significant antimicrobial activity against both bacteria. The MIC of NB-CDs was determined by observing the growth of the bacteria. As shown in Table , when the concentration of NB-CDs was 1.25 mg/mL, the culture medium was clear and transparent with no colonies. When the concentration of NB-CDs was diluted to 0.625 mg/mL, colonies appeared in the culture medium. Therefore, the MIC of NB-CDs against E. coli and S. aureus was 1.25 mg/mL.

The MBC of NB-CDs was determined by using the plate counting method. With increasing NB-CDs concentration, the number of colonies of E. coli and S. aureus was significantly reduced. When the concentration of NB-CDs exceeded 10 mg/mL, almost no colonies were observed (Figure a). As shown in Figure b, when the concentration of NB-CDs was 10 mg/mL, the antibacterial rate exceeded 99%. According to the fitting curve analysis of the dose–effect relationship between the concentration of NB-CDs and the antibacterial rate, the MBC value of NB-CDs for E. coli was 6.90 mg/mL, and the MBC concentration for S. aureus was 6.13 mg/mL (Figure c, d). In addition, bacterial Live/Dead staining showed that when the concentration of NB-CDs reached 6.90 mg/mL, all E. coli were almost killed. Similarly, when the concentration of NB-CDs reached 6.13 mg/mL, they exhibited a complete killing effect on S. aureus (Figure ). Overall, NB-CDs showed significant antimicrobial effects against both E. coli and S. aureus, and the MIC was 1.25 mg/mL, with an MBC of 6.90 mg/mL against E. coli and 6.13 mg/mL against S. aureus. The antimicrobial activity of carbon quantum dots is mediated by a variety of mechanisms. The antimicrobial mechanism of NB-CDs may be attributed to the positively charged functional groups (e.g., −NH2, −OH, and quaternary ammonium salts) enriched on the surface, penetrating the negatively charged bacterial membranes by electrostatic adsorption, causing damage to the bacterial cell membranes. Alternatively, possibly under light conditions, carbon dots can generate ROS (e.g., −OH, 1O2, H2O2) through photocatalysis, oxidatively damaging the bacterial cell membrane, proteins, DNA, and other key components. In addition, small-sized carbon dots (usually <10 nm) can penetrate into the bacterium and interfere with metabolic activities.

Bacterial Imaging Properties of NB-CDs

The NB-CDs possess high fluorescence quantum yield (41.50%), excellent photostability, and good biocompatibility. Based on these advantages, the application of NB-CDs in bacterial fluorescence imaging was evaluated. NB-CDs were coincubated with E. coli and S. aureus for 30 min and 1 h, respectively, and the imaging effects were observed. As shown in Figure , when NB-CDs were coincubated with bacteria for 30 min, the NB-CDs fluorescence signal had good spatial overlap with the bacteria, but there was a small amount of unlabeled bacteria. When the coincubation time was extended to 1 h, all the observed bacteria showed complete fluorescence labeling, and the bacteria were completely fused with the fluorescence signal. The results demonstrated that NB-CDs could be used as an efficient fluorescent marker for bacterial imaging. Notably, the short-term rapid labeling properties of NB-CDs may be derived from the boric acid groups in their structure, which can be specifically covalently bound with the cis-diol structure in the peptidoglycan layer of Gram-positive bacteria and the lipopolysaccharide layer of Gram-negative bacteria via borate ester bonds, thereby realizing the rapid recognition of carbon quantum dots to bacteria.

Conclusions

In this study, nitrogen and boron codoped carbon quantum dots (NB-CDs) with antimicrobial activity and fast bacterial imaging were successfully prepared for the first time by a one-pot solvothermal method using citric acid, Safranine T, and 3-aminobenzeneboronic acid as precursors. The maximum emission wavelength of the NB-CDs was 581 nm, the fluorescence quantum yield was high (41.50%), and the NB-CDs had the characteristics of nanosize and polycrystalline structure. In addition, the boric acid, carboxyl, hydroxyl, and amino groups on the surface of NB-CDs endowed them with good biocompatibility and the ability to bind to bacteria. The results of the in vitro antibacterial test confirmed that NB-CDs had highly effective antibacterial activity. Their MIC values against E. coli and S. aureus were both 1.25 mg/mL. The MBC value against E. coli was 6.90 mg/mL, and the MBC value against S. aureus was 6.13 mg/mL. The results of the bacterial imaging experiment indicate that NB-CDs have the ability to achieve rapid imaging within 1 h. Overall, this study innovatively combines the boric acid binding strategy with the design of long-wavelength fluorescent carbon quantum dots to construct an integrated theranostic platform, which not only offers a novel solution for bacterial tracer imaging and antibacterial synergy but also provides a new method for developing intelligent antibacterial systems with real-time feedback functions.

Materials and Methods

Materials

Citric acid, Safranine T, and 3-aminobenzeneboronic acid were purchased from Anhui Zaisheng Technology Co. LB medium, MH medium, Agar, PBS buffer, sodium chloride, sodium hydroxide, glacial acetic acid, anhydrous ethanol, and methanol were purchased from Gansu Huapu Biotechnology Co. E. coli and S. aureus were provided by the Lanzhou University of Technology. All the reagents were analytical grade and had not undergone further purification. Ultrapure water was used throughout the experiment process.

Synthesis of NB-CDs

To prepare NB-CDs, 1.0015 g of citric acid, 0.0204 g of Safranine T, and 0.0302 g of 3-aminobenzeneboronic acid were dissolved in 20 mL of anhydrous ethanol and sonicated for 10 min to dissolve them completely. The solution was transferred to a polytetrafluoroethylene-lined reactor and heated to 180 °C in a metal sand bath for 8 h of reaction. At the end of the reaction, the solution was cooled to room temperature. After centrifugation at 10,000 rpm for 10 min, the supernatant was taken and filtered through a 0.22 μm filter membrane, followed by dialysis using a dialysis bag with a molecular weight cutoff of 1000 Da for 24 h. Finally, the sample was dried to obtain the solid product.

Characterization of NB-CDs

Fluorescence spectra were measured by using a F4500 fluorescence spectrophotometer (Hitachi Ltd., Japan). Ultraviolet–visible (UV–vis) absorption spectra were recorded by using a UV-5200PC UV–vis spectrophotometer (Shanghai Yuan Analytical Instrument Co., Ltd., China). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and high-resolution TEM (HRTEM) images were taken by using a JEM-2100F transmission electron microscope (Nihon Electronics JEOL Co., Inc.). The ζ-potential of CDs was evaluated by a Zetasizer Nano ZSE (Malvern Panalytical, UK). X-ray diffraction spectra (XRD) were recorded by using a Smartlab X-ray diffractometer (Rigaku, Japan). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was recorded and analyzed using an ESCALAB 250xi X-ray photoelectron spectrometer (Thermo Fisher, USA). Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR) was recorded and analyzed using a Nicolet 380 FT-IR spectrometer (Thermo Fisher, USA). Fluorescence imaging was performed using an LSM700 laser confocal microscope (ZEISS, Germany).

Quantum Yield (QY) Measurements of NB-CDs

In this experiment, the relative fluorescence quantum yield (QY) of NB-CDs was determined by the reference method. Using 0.01 mol/mL rhodamine B (quantum yield at 540 nm: 0.89) dissolved in anhydrous ethanol as a reference, the absorbance at 540 nm and the emission spectra under 540 nm excitation of the carbon-dotted ethanol solution and the ethanol solution of rhodamine B were measured separately, and the quantum yields of the NB-CDs were calculated by the following eq : QCD=QR×ICDIR×ARACD×η2CDη2R 1 where Q CD denotes the quantum yield of the carbon spot; I denotes the integrated area of the fluorescence emission peak; A denotes the absorbance at the optimal excitation wavelength (not exceeding 0.05); η denotes the refractive index of the solvent used (the refractive index of ethanol is 1.36); and the subscript R denotes the reference standard.

Fluorescence Stability Test of NB-CDs

Photobleaching Resistance Experiments

Five mL of NB-CDs (10 μg/mL) was prepared in PBS buffer (20 mM, pH 7.4), and the fluorescence intensities of these solutions were measured and recorded every 24 h. Six parallel experimental and control groups were set up, and the fluorescence intensities were monitored for 1 week. The fluorescence intensity was monitored over time.

pH-Dependent Experiments

Buffer solutions of different pH (4–9) were prepared with acetic acid-sodium acetate. 100 μg/mL NB-CDs solid was dispersed in the above solutions of different pH, shaken vigorously, and left at room temperature for 10 min. Fluorescence emission spectra were then collected under 553 nm of excitation.

Ion Concentration Resistance Experiments

Five mL of various metal ion solutions and negative ion solutions were prepared with PBS buffer (20 mM, pH 7.4). Then, the same concentration of NB-CDs solution was added to these solutions, shaken vigorously, and left at room temperature for 10 min. Six parallel experimental groups and control groups were set up. Finally, the fluorescence spectra of these solutions were measured, recorded, and analyzed.

In Vitro Evaluation of Antibacterial Activity

Bacterial culture

S. aureus and E. coli strains were kept at −20 °C. Bacteria were cultured in LB liquid medium at 37 ± 1 °C with oscillation at 180 rpm for bacterial enrichment. The concentration of bacteria was determined by measuring the optical density at 670 nm (OD670) via UV–vis spectroscopy. One mL of the bacterial suspension was centrifuged (6000 rpm) for 10 min, and the precipitate was washed three times with PBS buffer (20 mM, pH 7.4) and redissolved in 1 mL of PBS buffer. After mixing and a 106-fold dilution, 100 μL was taken to coat the plate, and the agar plate was inverted and placed in a constant temperature incubator at 37 ± 1 °C for 16–18 h. The bacterial viability of the agar plate was observed after the incubation was completed, and if the bacterial viability was good, then the agar plate was sealed and placed in a refrigerator at 4 °C for storage.

Determination of Minimum Inhibitory Concentration (MIC)

Concentration gradient dilution of NB-CDs was carried out in 96-well plates and a blank control group was set up, and a bacterial solution with a concentration of 1.0 × 108 CFU/mL in a volume of 100 μL was added to each well; then, it was observed by agar plate culture after incubation for 18 h at 37 ± 1 °C, and the lowest concentration of the drug that completely inhibited the growth of the bacteria was taken as the MIC.

Determination of Minimum Bactericidal Concentration (MBC)

Using the plate counting method, NB-CDs were dissolved in 20 mM PBS buffer (pH 7.4) to prepare a solution with a concentration of 20 mg/mL and diluted in a concentration gradient. 100 μL of each concentration of NB-CD solution (experimental group) and an equal amount of PBS buffer (blank control group) were mixed with 100 μL of bacterial suspension at a concentration of 1.0 × 108 CFU/mL and incubated with shaking at 37 ± 1 °C for 12 h. Then, 100 μL of the mixture was uniformly coated on an LB solid agar plate and placed in the incubator at 37 ± 1 °C for 18 h. The growth of colonies was observed by counting the colonies and observing the growth condition; the bactericidal rate was calculated according to the following eq : Sterilizationrate%=(N0−Ni)N0×100% 2 where N 0 is the number of colonies on the blank control agar plate and Ni is the number of colonies on the agar plate of the experimental group treated with NB-CDs. The higher the bactericidal rate calculated by the formula, the better the antimicrobial activity of the NB-CDs at that concentration.

Staining of Live/Dead Bacterial Cells

Live/Dead bacterial staining was performed by using DMAO (green)/PI (red). First, E. coli and S. aureus were cultured in LB liquid medium to the logarithmic growth stage. Different concentrations of carbon spots were used to act on the bacteria for 12 h. An appropriate amount of the bacterial solution was taken and centrifuged at 8000 rpm for 5 min. The supernatant was discarded and washed once with PBS buffer, and then the concentration of the bacterial solution was adjusted with PBS buffer to about 1.0 × 108 CFU/mL. The solution was mixed according to the ratio of 1 μL of DMAO/PI for every 100 μL of the bacterial solution and incubated for 30 min at 37 ± 1 °C away from the light. At the end of incubation, it was washed with PBS 3 times. 10 μL of the bacterial solution was taken and added to the slide. The coverslip was then covered, and the staining effect was observed under a laser confocal microscope. DMAO is green fluorescence (Ex/Em = 503/530 nm); PI is red fluorescence (Ex/Em = 535/617 nm).

Bacterial Imaging Experiments of NB-CDs

E. coli and S. aureus were cultured in LB liquid medium at 37 ± 1 °C until logarithmic growth. One mL of the bacterial suspension was taken and centrifuged at 8000 rpm for 5 min, then washed three times with PBS buffer (pH 7.4) to remove the medium, and the precipitate was suspended in 900 μL of PBS buffer. Then, 100 μL of NB-CDs solution (final concentration of 1.25 mg/mL) was added to the bacterial solution. After incubation for 30 min and 1 h, the stained bacteria were washed twice. Finally, the bacteria were observed by imaging them under a laser confocal microscope.

Statistical Analysis

All values were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were performed using one-way analysis of variance (one-way ANOVA) to compare the groups. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.
