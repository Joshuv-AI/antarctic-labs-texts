The effectiveness of radial extracorporeal shock wave therapy (rESWT) in plantar fasciitis: a 12 months randomised controlled trial in a Tunisian rehabilitation department
Authors: Loubiri Ines, Moncer Rihab, Bhiri Sana, Layouni Saoussen, Gaddour Mariem, Frigui Sinene, Jemni Sonia
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCurrent management strategies for plantar fasciitis primarily involve conservative treatments. However, the overall effectiveness of extracorporeal shock wave therapy (ESWT) as a specific intervention for this condition has been a subject of ongoing debate in the medical community and its long term efficacy remains controversial. This study’s objective is to assess the effectiveness of extracorporeal radial shock wave therapy (rESWT) for treating plantar fasciitis treatment compared to physiotherapy combined with ultrasound sessions and to screen for foot disorders implementing baropodometry, in a Tunisian tertiary care rehabilitation department.MethodsPatients who presented to the rehabilitation department between November 2022 and November 2023 with Plantar Fasciitis were randomly assigned to the rESWT and the Physiotherapy combined to ultrasound groups. The diagnosis of PF was confirmed by physical examination. X-ray radiography was used to exclude a fracture of the calcaneus.ResultsOur study included 129 patients diagnosed with plantar fasciitis (PF), who were randomly allocated into two groups: 66 participants received radial extracorporeal shockwave therapy (rESWT), and 63 received physiotherapy combined with ultrasound therapy. The average age of these patients was 50.33 years (SD 10). The majority of participants were female, accounting for 101 individuals (78%). The average Body Mass Index (BMI) across the study group was 29.2 kg/m2. Regarding lower limb conditions, pes cavus (high arches) was the most common, observed in 58 participants (45%), followed by pes planus (low arches) in 47 participants (36%).Both treatment groups showed a significant decrease in Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) scores across all follow-up periods (p<0.001). However, no significant difference was found in the mean VAS scores between the two groups at any point after treatment.ConclusionBoth protocols (rESWT and physiotherapy combined to ultrasound) were effective for improving morning pain at short term and midterm follow-up periods. Foot disorders should be initially evaluated and considered while treating.Trial registrationThis trial is officially registered with the Pan African Clinical Trial Registry under the registration number PACTR202308802969500 first registered on (24/08/2023).Keywords: Fasciitis, plantar; Extracorporeal shockwave therapy; Physiotherapy; Ultrasound, baropodometric parameters

Background

Plantar fasciitis (PF) is a prevalent foot condition and stands as the most frequent cause of heel pain, reportedly responsible for approximately 11–15% of clinical presentations that necessitate treatment [1, 2]. According to recent data, it affects about 10% of the population [3]. It is present in both sedentary and athletes [4] with highest incidence between 45and 64 years old and it is more common among women [5] and up to one-third of patients may present with bilateral PF [6–9].

Initially categorized as an inflammatory condition, more recent investigations suggest that plantar fasciitis (PF) is predominantly a degenerative process, linked to a variety of contributing factors [10, 11]. Those factors includes high body mass index (BMI), sedentary, excessive running, military personnel, persons with occupations requiring prolonged standing/walking occupations, work-related weight bearing activities, limited ankle dorsiflexion, and foot biomechanical disturbance [2, 8, 12, 13].

The most frequent complaint of affected patients is pain, particularly during the first few steps in the morning and may be continuous with the alteration of daily activities [12].

The condition is primarily diagnosed based on symptoms, such as heel pain experienced during walking, and clinical findings, specifically pressure pain over the anteromedial aspect of the inferior heel [14].

Despite its simple diagnosis, management of PF is not well documented. In 80% of cases treatment is conservative. It includes several approaches typically involving rest, modifying footwear, orthotics, pharmacological agents and physical therapy, incorporating cryotherapy, stretching and strengthening exercises. Further options include ESWT, localized corticosteroid injections, biological therapies such as platelet-rich plasma(PRP) and prolotherapy [15].

Radial Extracorporeal Shockwave Therapy (rESWT) operates by delivering acoustic waves that elicit specific cellular and tissue responses. Its therapeutic effects are attributed to mechanisms such as stimulating new blood vessel formation.

From a clinical perspective, rESWT is a recognized treatment for chronic musculoskeletal ailments refractory to conventional therapies, such as epicondylitis, and calcific shoulder issues. For decades, it has been a widely adopted alternative treatment for plantar fasciitis (PF). This is largely due to its non-invasive nature, the quick recovery time it offers, and the overall convenience it provides for patients’ daily lives [14, 16–19].

A recent systematic review has endorsed stretching, taping, and patient education as key components of first-line management for plantar fasciitis (PF). However, a meta-analysis from 2021 highlighted an ongoing lack of consensus on the most effective treatment for PF, noting inconsistent outcomes across various interventions [20]. Indeed, only a single trial comparing radial Extracorporeal Shock Wave Therapy (ESWT) with plantar fascia stretching was identified and considered for secondary proof of efficacy [20].

To the best of our knowledge, no prior study has directly compared the long term efficacy of radial ESWT to physiotherapy combined to ultrasound and assessed the baropodometric profile in their statistical analysis. In Tunisia, no studies in our country have evaluated the efficacy of this treatment in PF neither the baropodometric evaluation of patient with PF.

Objective

The objective is to assess the effectiveness of radial shock wave therapy in plantar fasciitis treatment considering pain and satisfaction compared to physiotherapy combined to ultrasound sessions.

The secondary objective is to screen for foot disorders implementing baropodometry.

Methods

Study design

This investigation is structured as a randomized controlled trial featuring two parallel groups. All participants for this study will be enrolled exclusively from the Department of Physical Medicine and Rehabilitation at Sahloul University Hospital, located in Sousse, Tunisia.

Participants

Individuals presenting with heel pain to the rehabilitation department between November 2022 and November 2023 were considered for eligibility, provided they were between the ages of 18 and 70 years. A physiatrist performed a comprehensive physical examination to confirm the diagnosis of plantar fasciitis (PF). Furthermore, X-ray imaging was utilized to effectively rule out any calcaneal fractures.

The inclusion criteria are as follows

Participants in this study will present with plantar fascia pain localized to the proximal insertion of the plantar fascia on the medial calcaneal tuberosity. This pain must have persisted for longer than three months and be accompanied by tenderness upon palpation of the affected area. To ensure effective communication and informed consent, all participants must demonstrate comprehension of either spoken and written Arabic or French. Furthermore, only individuals reporting a Visual Analog Scale (VAS) pain intensity of 5 or greater (on a scale of 0–10) during activity at baseline will be eligible for inclusion.

The exclusion criteria are as follows

Individuals will be excluded if they have not received any prior treatment for the condition, present with associated tendinopathy, or have undergone radial shock wave therapy within the last three months. Other exclusion criteria include a rheumatic etiology for the pain, previous foot or ankle surgery with remaining osteosynthesis material, the presence of a tumor or active infection in the treatment area, calcaneal fracture or any contraindications for radial shock wave therapy (such as anticoagulant use, bleeding disorders, a pacemaker or epilepsy,). Participants who do not meet these inclusion criteria or choose not to participate will receive standard care. Please note, relevant data may be shared with authorized university personnel or regulatory authorities, with participant permission, but this trial does not involve the collection or storage of biological specimens.

Randomisation and blinding/study protocol

Eligible patients will receive comprehensive details about the trial, both verbally and in writing, from a physician. Following this, individuals who provide their informed consent will be randomly assigned to one of the two study groups using straightforward methods like a coin toss or dice roll. To ensure allocation concealment, sealed opaque envelopes were employed, although the study itself was open-label due to the nature of the interventions, meaning participants were aware of their specific treatment. While complete blinding wasn’t practical given participant knowledge of their assigned therapy, information bias was minimized by having outcome measures assessed by an independent individual who was unaware of the treatment each patient received.

The patients in the first group were given six sessions of rESWT treatment (once a week). The patients in the second group were given twelve sessions of physiotherapy treatment (plantar stretching, deep transverse massage) combined to ultrasound therapy (US) three times a week for four weeks.

All the patients in ESWT group received the same dose per protocol (10 Hz, 2000 pulse, 2.5 bar energy density) using BTL-6000 ® Radial Shockwave Therapy device.

The study adhered to the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) guidelines throughout its design, conduct, and reporting (Fig. 1).

Outcome measures and assessment

Pain relief was designated as the primary outcome measure, given its centrality as the predominant symptom experienced by individuals with plantar fasciitis. Changes in pain intensity were quantified using the Visual Analogue Scale (VAS), a widely recognized and validated instrument. For the purpose of analysis, follow-up periods were categorized as follows: short-term evaluation encompassed time points from one week up to three months; medium-term assessment extended beyond three months and concluded at six months; and long-term outcomes were considered for any duration exceeding six months [20].

VAS assessments were performed at baseline, immediately after the treatment, and one month after, three months, six months and 12 months by a physician who was blind to the treatment allocation. Roles and Maudsley score (RMS) was assessed at base line, three and twelve months after treatment.

Pain intensity was primarily evaluated using the Visual Analogue Scale (VAS), a widely adopted tool for self-reporting pain. This scale presents as a 10-centimeter horizontal line. One end is marked “no pain,” while the opposite end signifies “the most severe/unbearable pain you ever felt in your lifetime.” Patients simply mark a point on this line that best reflects their current pain level, which is then measured with a ruler to yield a score between 0 and 10.

Pain intensity, measured using the Visual Analogue Scale (VAS), was evaluated at multiple specific intervals. These assessments took place at the initial baseline, immediately after treatment administration, and subsequently at one, three, six, and twelve months post-treatment. To mitigate potential bias, a physician who was blinded to the treatment allocation conducted all VAS evaluations. In addition to pain assessment, the Roles and Maudsley score (RMS), a metric for functional outcomes, was recorded at baseline, as well as at the three- and twelve-month follow-up points after treatment.

We calculated the overall treatment success rate, along with the success rate for each individual study group, at both short-term (3-month) and long-term (12-month) follow-up points. We defined treatment success, classifying patients as “good responders,” if they achieved a Roles and Maudsley Score (RMS) grade of good or excellent.

The modified Roles and Maudsley score is a four-point scale designed to assess a patient’s pain in the context of their daily activities. Here’s a breakdown:

RMS 1 (Excellent): Indicates an excellent quality of life, characterized by no symptoms, unlimited pain-free walking ability, and complete patient satisfaction with the treatment outcome (when evaluated post-intervention).

RMS 2 (Good): Reflects a good quality of life, where the patient can walk for more than one hour without pain, symptoms are significantly reduced after treatment, and the patient expresses satisfaction with the outcome.

RMS 3 (Acceptable): Suggests an acceptable quality of life, marked by an inability to walk for more than one hour without pain. Symptoms are somewhat improved, pain is more tolerable than before treatment, and the patient is mildly satisfied with the outcome.

RMS 4 (Poor): Denotes a poor quality of life, where the patient cannot walk without severe pain, symptoms have not improved or have worsened after treatment, and the patient is not satisfied with the outcome [11].

We defined treatment success based on two primary outcome measures: modifications in pain scores (using the VAS) and changes in the Roles and Maudsley Score (RMS). Specifically, a positive response meant either the modified RMS reached 1 or 2 in follow-up studies, or the VAS score was ≤ 4 on the 11-point scale, or there was an improvement of over 50% from baseline VAS in subsequent assessments [11].

Recurrence of pain was defined as changes in VAS > 5 at 6 months after a successful result at 3 months.

As secondary outcomes were measured baropodometric parameters using a digital platform (Medicapteurs, WIN TRACK Software 9.4). The static baropodometric variables considered in this study were: foot load distribution in forefoot and rearfoot (%), mean plantar pressure (gr/cm2), peak plantar pressure (gr/cm2), total contact area and heel contact area (cm²) in the left and the right side (Fig. 2).

Plantar pressure, load distribution and contact area in static analysis

We assessed participants’ static posture individually, capturing data at a single moment while their eyes remained open. Each patient was instructed to stand barefoot and motionless on a specialized platform. Their feet were positioned parallel, and they maintained a relaxed posture with their arms resting alongside their body. Throughout the evaluation, participants were asked to remain silent and fix their gaze on a designated target marked on the wall, located two meters from the platform at eye level.

Study population

Sample size calculation

The required sample size for this study was determined based on a significance level (alpha) of 0.05 and a statistical power of 80%. Our objective was to detect a clinically meaningful reduction in Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) scores of 4 points (e.g., from 9 to 5). Based on these parameters, the minimum calculated sample size was 35 participants per group. Accounting for an anticipated dropout rate of 20%, the final target sample size was adjusted to 84 participants, with 42 allocated to each intervention group.

Statistical analysis

All data collected were analyzed using SPSS version 20.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Before the study began, a sample size calculation was carried out to determine the necessary number of participants.For presenting the data, qualitative variables are shown using absolute and relative frequencies. Quantitative variables are presented as their means along with their standard deviations. To compare frequencies, the Chi-squared (χ 2) test was employed, while the Student’s t-test was used for comparing means. A significance level for all tests was set at a P-value of 0.05.

Results

Baseline characteristics of patients

Overall 129 patients participated to the trial. They were randomly assigned to the ESWT (n = 66) and Physiotherapy combined to ultrasound (n = 63) groups (Fig. 1). Four patients had missing baropodometric data.

The participants in the study had an average age of 50.33 years, with a standard deviation of 10 years. Their mean body height was 164.8 cm (SD 7.4 cm), and the average body weight recorded was 80 kg (SD 13.6 kg).

One hundred and one (78%) of the participants were females, while 28 (22%) were males. The baseline demographic data of the two groups were similar to each other, including age, weight, height, body mass index, affected foot, and symptom duration with no significant difference (Table 1). Most of patients in ESWT group were females 71% vs. 86% females in physiotherapy group. This difference was statistically significant (p = 0.046).

Discussion

The primary objective of the study was to evaluate the effectiveness of rESWT in plantar fasciitis treatment assessing pain and satisfaction compared to physiotherapy combined with ultrasound sessions.

In this study, patients diagnosed with plantar fasciitis (PF) were randomly assigned to one of two distinct treatment groups: one group received twelve sessions of physical therapy combined with ultrasound (US) treatment, while the other received six sessions of radial extracorporeal shockwave therapy (rESWT).

Regarding the socio-demographic characteristics of the participants the mean age was 50.33 years. A systematic review indicates that chronic plantar heel pain is most commonly observed between 40 and 59 years of age [13].

Concerning gender distribution, 101 (78%) participants were female, and 28 (22%) were male. This female predominance aligns with findings in previous scientific literature [21, 22]. In terms of occupation, 47 (36%) were healthcare professionals, 36 (28%) were housewives, and 18 (14%) were office workers. The number of hours spent standing was not assessed in our study.

Occupational activities have been associated to plantar fasciitis in literature. Individuals with increased occupational standing time on hard surfaces and those who spent the majority of their workday on their feet were more likely to have PF. Physical activity performed three times a week for more than 20 min was associated with a decreased prevalence of PF.

Existing studies frequently highlights an association between certain occupational demands and plantar fasciitis (PF). Specifically, individuals whose professions involve extended periods of standing on unforgiving surfaces, or those who spend the majority of their work shift upright, demonstrate a greater propensity for developing PF. Conversely, findings suggest that engaging in physical activity three times per week for durations exceeding 20 min is correlated with a reduced prevalence of plantar fasciitis [23].

Regarding the participants’ BMI, the mean value was 29.2 kg/m2.

Obesity (BMI > 30 kg/m2) was observed in 71 patients (55%). Furthermore, a high BMI was significantly associated with baseline Visual Analog Scale (VAS) scores (p < 0.001).

Hamstra-Wright et al., concluded that in a physically active population, the primary risk factors for PF are increased BMI and excessive plantar flexion. They explained that a greater degree of plantar flexion range of motion (ROM), and increased BMI contribute to an increased tensile load on the force-absorbing structures (10). The degree of plantar flexion was not assessed in our study.

Individuals with a BMI > 30 kg/m2 had a higher risk of plantar fasciitis (odds ratio: 5.6) compared with those with a BMI ≤ 25 kg/m2 [24].

Foot examinations revealed anatomical disorders in both groups. Overall pes planus (flatfoot) was identified in 47 (36%) participants with a statistically significantly higher prevalence in the ESWT group (p = 0.017).

Pes cavus was observed in 58(45%) of cases with no statistically significant difference between the two groups (p = 0.11). The most commonly reported lower limb conditions in previous studies include pes cavus (high-arched foot), and pes planus (excessive foot pronation) [8]. In contrast, Zenat Khired et al.‘s study (2022) reported pes planus (low-arched) in only 3.7% of their cohort [25].Therefore, each biomechanical foot disorder may disturb plantar pressure and contribute to PF, highlighting the importance of considering these factors in PF management.

The secondary objective of the present study was to assess static baropodometric parameters at baseline. To the best of our knowledge, this study appears to be the first to assess baropodometric profile in plantar fasciitis while comparing two treatment modalities (ESWT and physiotherapy combined with ultrasound sessions) particularly in Tunisia and Africa. Previous studies have assessed foot posture, gait analysis and dynamic patterns in specific populations with PF, such as women, nurses, and runners [10, 25–28].

Among the static parameters, there were no significant differences in peak plantar pressure, mean plantar pressure, load distribution, or total contact area between the two groups, with the exception of the left heel contact area in the ESWT group (p < 0.005).

A significant association was found between baropodometric variables and baseline heel pain, as measured by the Visual Analog Scale (VAS) (p < 0.001). These baropodometric parameters included peak plantar pressure, mean plantar pressure, total contact area, and heel contact area. Notably, a significant positive correlation was observed between the intensity of pain and the overall contact area of the plantar region with the ground. This positive and significant relationship was particularly evident on the left side—identified as the most affected limb—where pain intensity correlated directly with contact area (p < 0.001).

Further analysis of load distribution via baropodometry indicated that the rearfoot region exhibited the highest concentration of load, accounting for 57% on the right side and 56% on the left side. These measured load values are notably higher than the 50% load typically considered a normal standard.

A vast number of investigations have explored the effectiveness of various therapeutic approaches for plantar fasciitis (PF). Many of these studies consistently show that Extracorporeal Shock Wave Therapy (ESWT) is more effective than a placebo. However, there are few studies that have directly compared ESWT’s effectiveness with other active treatments for PF [29].

Shockwaves are defined as acoustic waves characterized by a rapid and abrupt increase in pressure. A key characteristic is their ability to concentrate energy flux within a very small, targeted area. The proposed mechanisms for the effect of ESWT include hyperstimulation analgesia and stimulation of neovascularization and collagen synthesis in degenerative tissues [14].

Radial ESWT utilizes pneumatic waves generated by an air compressor through a tube at the end of an applicator. A projectile within the applicator is accelerated by compressed air, striking the applicator head and generating a pressure wave that is transmitted into the body [24].

Previous studies assessed the efficacy of either focused or radial ESWT for treating PF. They found that ESWT was overall effective in reducing VAS scores and improving Roles and Maudsley Scores at 12 weeks. They reported good results after the application of ESWT in patients with plantar fasciitis, with success rates ranging from 34 to 88%, which is consistent with our findings [14, 20].

In our study, the overall success rate was 73%. The success rate for the ESWT group was 54% versus 46% for the physiotherapy group at the short-term (3 months) follow-up, with no statistically significant difference (p = 0.32). Several studies have reported satisfactory short-term pain relief and functional outcomes, including decreased morning and activity pain, and pain associated with walking, but long-term efficacy remains unknown due to a lack of long-term data [29–31].

Our study’s findings indicate that both treatment groups experienced comparable improvements in pain reduction, specifically concerning Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) pain scores. We observed a notable decrease in first-morning pain for participants in both groups. This outcome aligns consistently with results reported in prior research [31].

Even though our study didn’t find a statistically significant overall difference between the groups, the Extracorporeal Shock Wave Therapy (ESWT) group did exhibit a slightly greater reduction in morning pain, particularly at the three-month assessment, compared to the physiotherapy group.

A previous study indicated that individuals seeking to avoid invasive treatments could rely on physiotherapy for symptom resolution [32].

In contrast to our observations, Jan D. Rompe et al. (2010) reported findings suggesting that a structured program of manual stretching exercises, specifically targeting the plantar fascia, demonstrated superiority over repetitive low-energy radial shockwave therapy for managing acute symptoms of proximal plantar fasciitis [30].

The body of evidence regarding ESWT has certain limitations that have impacted the synthesis of related trials. Continuous outcomes, such as overall pain, first-step pain, or function, were often reported at baseline but subsequently presented as an overall success rate for heel pain at follow-up.

Given that both treatments demonstrate effectiveness, integrating both modalities could offer a highly beneficial protocol for plantar fasciitis (PF). Reviews of studies involving PF patients have often indicated that a single therapeutic approach is generally insufficient, thereby suggesting that a combined strategy employing multiple modalities might be more effective [33].

Indeed, research by Akınoğlu B et al. has shown that combining ultrasound (US) therapy with exercises, as well as integrating radial extracorporeal shockwave therapy (r-ESWT) with exercises, led to greater improvements in parameters like walking speed when compared to exercise therapy alone [34].

In our study, there was no statistically significant difference between the two treatment modalities at one, three, and six months.

Cost-effectiveness of both ESWT and physiotherapy sessions has not been evaluated in the literature [20].

ESWT devices are not always available in our tertiary care unit. Physiotherapy combined with ultrasound therapy can be helpful for treating PF resistant to oral and local treatment. However, it appears that ESWT sessions are less cost-effective than physiotherapy sessions, although they are more appreciated by patients.

Considering the strengths of our study, to the best of our knowledge, our study is the first randomized controlled study in developing countries to compare two treatment modalities and screen for foot disorders using baropodometry in plantar fasciitis.

The population size included in this study is larger compared to the sample size of the previous studies.

However, the findings of this study should be interpreted with caution, given certain limitations. Firstly, participant blinding was not performed, which may have introduced patient-related bias. Nonetheless, patients were not given a choice in treatment group selection. Secondly, foot posture and dynamic analysis of the podiatric profile were not assessed. Further study is needed to assess the long-term effects of load distribution by comparing baropodometric results from before and after treatment.

Conclusions

Our randomized controlled study concluded that both the radial extracorporeal shockwave therapy (rESWT) protocol and the physiotherapy combined with ultrasound protocol yielded similar improvements in pain reduction for plantar fasciitis. We found no statistically significant differences between the two groups. Importantly, both treatment approaches proved effective in alleviating pain during both the short-term and mid-term follow-up periods. In addition to foot-specific management, considering foot disorders in evaluation and their correction may improve results, a factor that should be further evaluated across all follow-up periods.
