Accuracy of machine learning-assisted prediction of the future need for orthognathic surgery in patients with cleft lip and palate.

- Korean J Orthod 2025;55:365-379

I read with great interest the article and commend the authors for applying machine learning methods to address an important research question.1 However, I would like to raise the following methodological concerns for clarification and further discussion.

Q1. The manuscript reports the use of principal component analysis (PCA) for dimensionality reduction prior to model development. However, the procedures for data preprocessing before PCA are not clearly described. Specifically, how were outliers identified and handled before PCA application? In addition, PCA assumes linear relationships among variables, yet the independence or correlation structure among features was not discussed. Could the authors clarify whether feature interdependence was evaluated and how this may have influenced the PCA results?

Q2. The issue of class or sample imbalance does not appear to be addressed in the study. Given that imbalanced datasets can substantially affect model training, performance metrics, and generalizability, could the authors clarify whether sample imbalance was present? If so, were any strategies—such as resampling, class weighting, or alternative evaluation metrics—considered to mitigate potential bias?

Q3. While machine learning techniques were employed, the manuscript does not clearly specify the sample size used for model training and validation. Considering that sample size is a critical factor influencing model stability and the risk of overfitting, could the authors provide more detailed information regarding the number of samples used at each stage of model development and justify the adequacy of the sample size for the chosen algorithms?

A1. We appreciate the reader’s interest in the methodological details of our study and the opportunity to provide further clarification.

Regarding data preprocessing, all cephalometric variables were derived from a clinical dataset. As described in the Materials and Methods section,1 prior to machine learning analysis, the raw measurements had already undergone expert-based screening by experienced orthodontists as part of routine clinical quality control. Therefore, no additional algorithmic outlier detection or exclusion procedures were applied at the machine learning stage. Continuous variables were subsequently standardized using z-score normalization to ensure comparability across features.

PCA was used solely as an exploratory tool to examine the overall data structure and class separability between the surgery and non-surgery groups at the pre-adolescent stage (T0). This usage is illustrated in the workflow presented in Figure 1 and further described in the PCA subsection of the Methods section.1 Importantly, PCA was not employed for dimensionality reduction in model training. The support vector machine (SVM) model was constructed using the full set of original cephalometric variables after z-score normalization, rather than PCA-transformed components.

Although PCA is based on linear combinations of correlated variables, in this study it was applied only to visualize variance patterns and group overlap, as shown in Figure 2,1 rather than to enforce feature independence or transform inputs for prediction. Consequently, the assumptions associated with PCA did not influence the training or performance of the predictive model.

We hope this explanation, together with the referenced sections and figures in the original manuscript, helps readers better understand the role of PCA and the preprocessing strategy adopted in our study.

A2. We appreciate the reader’s comments regarding potential class imbalance and its implications for model development, evaluation, and decision-making.

In this study, the distribution between the surgery and non-surgery groups was 25.3% (n = 62) and 74.7% (n = 183), respectively, as summarized in Table 2.1 While some degree of class imbalance was present, this distribution reflects the real-world clinical prevalence of orthognathic surgery among patients with cleft lip and palate rather than an extreme imbalance scenario.

To account for this distribution during model development, stratified sampling was consistently applied when splitting the dataset into training/validation (80%) and independent test (20%) sets, ensuring proportional representation of both classes at each stage of analysis. This procedure is illustrated in the machine learning workflow shown in Figure 1 and described in the Methods section.1

Model performance was evaluated using multiple complementary metrics, including area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC), sensitivity, specificity, precision, and F1 score, as reported in the Results section. AUROC was selected as the primary evaluation criterion because of its relative robustness to class imbalance. Sensitivity and specificity were also explicitly reported to assess balanced classification performance.

In addition, the decision threshold for class assignment was determined based on the receiver operating characteristic curve, with the aim of minimizing the difference between sensitivity and specificity. As shown in Figure 3,1 the selected threshold resulted in closely matched sensitivity (83.3%) and specificity (83.8%), indicating that the classifier was not biased toward either class despite the underlying imbalance.

We did not apply additional resampling techniques, such as oversampling, undersampling, or class-weighted loss functions, as our objective was to preserve the original data distribution and maintain clinical interpretability. Furthermore, the SVM classifier was chosen for its margin-based optimization, which is known to provide stable generalization performance in moderately imbalanced, high-dimensional datasets, as described in the Methods section.

We hope this explanation clarifies how class imbalance was explicitly considered not only during data splitting and performance evaluation, but also in the final decision-making process of the predictive model.

A3. We appreciate the reader’s interest in the sample size, data usage, and model stability of our study and welcome the opportunity to provide additional context.

The final dataset consisted of 245 patients with cleft lip and palate, including 62 patients in the surgery group (25.3%) and 183 patients in the non-surgery group (74.7%), as summarized in Table 2.1 It is important to note that each data instance in this study required long-term longitudinal follow-up from T0 to young adulthood (T1), with an average longitudinal follow-up duration of approximately 10 years. As such, the sample size reflects the realistic constraints inherent to longitudinal clinical studies that aim to capture definitive surgical outcomes.

For model development, the dataset was divided into training/validation (80%) and independent test (20%) sets using stratified sampling to preserve class proportions. This data partitioning strategy, along with the corresponding sample sizes and label distributions for each subset, is explicitly illustrated in Figure 2 and described in the Methods section.1 The independent test set was reserved exclusively for final performance evaluation and was not involved in any stage of model training or hyperparameter selection.

Within the training/validation set, hyperparameter tuning was performed using stratified 10-fold cross-validation to optimize the kernel function and regularization parameter of the SVM model. This approach enabled efficient use of the available data while mitigating the risk of overfitting, particularly in the context of limited sample size.

The SVM classifier was selected because of its margin-based optimization, which is well suited for high-dimensional feature spaces with relatively small sample sizes. In addition, the use of a linear kernel reduced model complexity and facilitated interpretability.

Finally, model performance was evaluated on the independent test set using multiple complementary metrics, as presented in the Results section and Figure 3,1 providing an unbiased assessment of generalization performance.

We hope this explanation clarifies how sample size limitations, data availability, and overfitting risk were explicitly considered and addressed through the study design, validation strategy, and transparent reporting of data usage.
