Time to treatment failure and its predictors among children attending antiretroviral therapy in Jimma, southwest Ethiopia: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Tafesse Gizaw, Abeje Kebede, Haile Fikadu Hunduma, Henok Ashenafi Serba, Lata Fekadu, Fira Abamecha, Yenealem Gezahegn
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTreatment failure of antiretroviral therapy (ART) has become an increasingly global health issue. In Ethiopia, prior studies on ART failure rates have focused primarily on adults in hospital settings with varying definitions of treatment failure, leaving pediatric data scarce. Thus, this study aimed to determine the incidence rate of treatment failure and its predictors among children enrolled in first-line ART in Jimma, Ethiopia.MethodsA retrospective cohort study was conducted at health facilities in Jimma, Ethiopia. A total of 312 children who were enrolled in first-line ART from January 2015 to December 2024 were selected via systematic random sampling. The data were retrieved by reviewing the child’s medical records and analyzed using Stata software. We utilized a multivariable Cox regression model to identify predictors of treatment failure and adjusted hazards ratio (AHR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) to measure the associations. A p-value < 0.05 was used to declare statistically significant associations.ResultsThe proportion of pediatric first-line ART failure in this study was 15.71% (95% CI: 11.57, 19.85), with an overall incidence density of 2.07 per 1,000 person-months (95% CI: 1.56, 2.74). The predictors significantly associated with ART failure were underweight (AHR = 3.95, 95% CI: 1.63, 9.57), anemia (AHR = 3.01, 95% CI: 1.51, 6.01), delayed ART initiation (AHR = 3.46, 95% CI: 1.32, 9.06), fair or poor adherence (AHR = 11.36, 95% CI: 5.22, 24.73), and advanced HIV disease (AHR = 2.49, 95% CI: 1.34, 4.66). Conversely, caregivers with positive serostatus (AHR = 0.191, 95% CI: 0.078, 0.46) and children aged 10–14 years (AHR = 0.064, 95% CI: 0.023, 0.17) had a significantly reduced risk of failure compared with their counterparts.ConclusionFirst-line ART failure among children remains high, rendering achieving the nation’s HIV-free generation target difficult. To improve pediatric HIV outcomes in Ethiopia, future interventions should focus on rapid ART initiation, adherence counseling, nutritional support, and early anemia screening and management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12887-026-06674-0.Keywords: Treatment failure, First-line, Antiretroviral therapy, Children, Predictors, HIV

Introduction

The scaling up of lifesaving antiretroviral therapy (ART) has been a breakthrough in improving the survival rates of children living with HIV (CLHIV). Nevertheless, treatment failure has become a threat to its effectiveness, potentially rendering a country’s HIV response at risk of stalling [1, 2]. According to the WHO, treatment failure (TF) refers to the progression of disease and replication of HIV despite therapy [3, 4]. In the context of children receiving ART, TF refers to a suboptimal response or a lack of sustained response to prescribed therapy [5]. ART treatment failure is categorized on the basis of three criteria: clinical failure (based on clinical criteria), immunologic failure (CD4 criteria), or virologic failure (viral load criteria) [4, 6]. Since 2016, the WHO and national guidelines recognize the viral load criteria as a preferred approach for monitoring the treatment response and confirming its failure, with the recommendation of routine viral load testing at 6 months, 12 months, and then annually after initiating ART [4, 6].

Treatment failure is becoming an increasingly global health issue, with up to 25.86% of children on ART experiencing failure in sub-Saharan Africa [7]. In Ethiopia, the overall incidence of ART treatment failures among adults varies between 1.75 and 2.08 per 1,000 person-months of observation [8, 9]. Typically, children experience higher rates of ART failure than other age groups [4], owing to immature immune systems, reliance on caregivers, and limited regimens options [10]. The failure rates vary considerably across countries, with studies in Mozambique and Uganda showing a 29% prevalence [11]. In Ethiopia, this situation is alarming, with a high burden of pediatric HIV cases [12], and studies conducted in different regions have revealed varying treatment failure rates: 12.19% in the Amhara region [13], 14% in the hospital of Gondar [14], 17.2% in Addis Ababa [15], and 23.8% in the Tigray region [16].

Children experiencing failure in ART are strongly associated with elevated viral loads, resulting in disease progression and a weakened immune system [4, 17]. This, in turn, increases susceptibility to opportunistic infections, leading to serious health complications or even death. Moreover, retaining patients on a failing treatment exposes them to the risk of disease relapse and onward transmission [18, 19]. Notably, TF not only puts the health of CLHIV at risk but also public health through the dissemination of drug-resistant HIV variants [20], thereby complicating future treatment endeavors.

In Ethiopia, studies have shown that several factors can play a role in the occurrence of treatment failure, including individual-level factors (e.g., adherence challenges) [21, 22], family-level factors (e.g., lack of parental support, residing in rural areas) [23, 24], disease-related factors (e.g., WHO clinical stage III or IV, baseline CD4 count, OIs) [16, 18, 25], and health system-level factors (e.g., inadequate access to care, stock-outs of medication, or drug substitutions) [21, 22, 26].

Ethiopia is committed to creating an HIV-free generation and ending AIDS as a public health threat by 2030 [6]. Moreover, the country has adopted WHO-recommended tests for HIV screening and monitoring treatment response, recognizing viral load (VL) testing as an earlier and more sensitive method for diagnosing and confirming TF [4, 6]. However, access to VL testing is still a notable obstacle due to infrastructural, logistical, and patient-related challenges, hindering effective monitoring in children [27]. As a result, TF remains a significant public health concern in Ethiopia.

In Ethiopia, existing studies on pediatric ART outcomes are limited by the use of single-facility data [14, 28], hospital-based sampling excluding health center contexts [29–31], cross-sectional study designs [23, 28], and varying TF definitions and criteria [15, 16, 28, 32], as well as limited data on long-term outcomes (10 years and beyond) for children who remain on first-line ART [15, 24, 32]. These approaches may lead to a misestimated incidence and suboptimal care for CLHIV. While some classify children as those ≤ 18 years old, others include all patients on ART [24, 33]. Despite the study population being children, the roles of several crucial predictors, including the timing of ART initiation, the caregiver’s serostatus, and nutritional status, in influencing treatment outcomes remain understudied in pediatric cohorts [13, 34].

To achieve the global goal of ending AIDS as a public health threat, understanding the factors contributing to treatment failure among children (aged under 15 years) who are receiving first-line ART is essential. To this end, this study aimed to determine the incidence of treatment failure and identify its predictors among this vulnerable population. The findings of this study could inform policymakers, stakeholders, and HIV programme managers in developing, implementing, and assessing targeted interventions to improve practices and policies in pediatric HIV care and treatment monitoring.

Methods and materials

Study setting and period

The study was conducted in Jimma, Oromia, Ethiopia. Jimma is approximately 355 km from Addis Ababa, the capital of Ethiopia. Administratively, it encompasses 20 woredas and three towns—Jimma town, Agaro town, and Limmu Genet town [35, 36]. In terms of health facilities, Jimma had nine government hospitals, 126 health centers, over 534 health posts, four private hospitals, and various private clinics, offering essential health services to the community. Among these facilities, only five government hospitals and 14 health centers had pediatric patients on first-line ART from January 1, 2015, to December 30, 2024. A total of 609 pediatric patients were enrolled in first-line ART follow-up during the study period (reports from Jimma Zone, Jimma Town, and Agaro Town health offices). The high burden of pediatrics HIV clients and diverse range of health facilities, including those specialized in pediatric HIV care and treatment, underscores the importance of Jimma as a preferred study area. This study was conducted from April 21 to June 20, 2025.

Study design

A multicenter setting, a retrospective cohort study was employed. This design enables follow-up of participants from ART initiation until the end of the observation period, facilitating direct assessment of time-to-event data. Predictors of treatment failure were measured at baseline and during follow-up. The end of the follow-up date for each participant was defined as either the date of confirmed treatment failure (for event observations) or the last recorded viral load test date before the study’s end (for censored observations). The date of treatment failure was established as the first recorded date when a child had two consecutive viral load measurements exceeding 1000 copies/mL since initiating ART.

Population and eligibility criteria

The study population consisted of all eligible children aged under 15 years who were enrolled in first-line ART from January 1, 2015, to December 31, 2024, at randomly selected ART follow-up health facilities in Jimma, Ethiopia. This study included children who were enrolled in first-line ART from January 2015 to December 2024, received first-line ART for a minimum of six months, and had at least two follow-up visits since initiating ART. We excluded children with incomplete medical records, those with more than 5% missing data across the main independent variables required for analysis, and those whose information regarding the date of the event or VL test results (the primary outcome variable) was missing.

Sample size and sampling procedures

The sample size for the study was determined using power analysis for Cox proportional hazards regression with Stata software. This calculation was based on the following assumptions: a significance level of 5% (for a two-sided test), a power of 80%, an adjusted hazards ratio (AHR) of 2.29 for fair adherence status derived from a previous study [37], a calculated event occurrence probability of 14.3% among children with fair adherence [37], and the assumption that no participants would withdraw from the study. On the basis of these assumptions and inputting the data into Stata software, the software estimated 46 events and a sample size of 320 (see Additional file 1: Predictor variables with parameters used for sample size determination in Jimma, Ethiopia). With the addition of 10% to account for incomplete records/missed information, the total sample size required for the study was estimated as 352 [37].

Nine health facilities, comprising four hospitals and five health centers, were randomly selected from a total of 19 facilities that had pediatric ART cases during the data extraction period. The health facilities included in this study had a total of 528 cases across the selected health facilities (see Additional file 2: Schematic representation of sampling procedure employed to select study participants).

To ensure the representativeness of the sample size across the health facilities, the final sample size was proportionally apportioned to all the study sites on the basis of the number of pediatric ART cases. The patient’s medical record number was utilized to prepare a yearly time-ordered sampling frame. Using a systematic random sampling method, eligible study participants were recruited from HIV care/ART follow-up records. Systematic sampling was chosen, as this method ensures even coverage across the entire data extraction period (2015–2024), thus avoiding overrepresentation of specific time periods [38].

Study variables

The dependent variable for this study was the time to treatment failure among children enrolled in the first-line ART regimen. In this study, the time to treatment failure denotes the duration in months from the initiation of first-line ART to the development of an event (TF). The independent variables included sociodemographic characteristics; ART treatment-related factors; clinical and immunological characteristics; nutrition-related factors; and behavior-related factors.

Data collection procedures (instrument, personnel, and technique)

The required data were retrieved using a data abstraction tool developed after a review of the relevant literature and consultation with national HIV prevention, care, and treatment guidelines [4, 6]. The tool was designed in English, as the study uses secondary data. It encompasses sociodemographic characteristics, clinical and immunological characteristics, ART treatment-related factors, nutrition-related factors, and follow-up events (see Additional file 3: Data abstraction tool). Two clinical nurses (Bachelor’s degree) who have hands-on experience in HIV care/ART follow-up registers were recruited to assist in collecting the intended data under the close supervision of supervisors.

Data collection technique and its source

The ART medical registers and follow-up cards contain comprehensive information for each child, including their sociodemographic characteristics such as age, sex, residence, and caregiver details. The records also contain important information, such as the WHO clinical stage, admission anthropometry, admission history, physical examination findings, medical complications, and clinical features. Additionally, the follow-up information included data on weight and treatment outcomes. In this study, relevant information from these records was retrieved using document reviews.

Data quality assurance

We pretested the data abstraction tool for the presence of variables in the patient registration records through a preliminary chart review of 5% (16) of the sample. Before the start of data collection, training was provided for data collectors on the contents of the data extraction tool and the procedures of extracting data from patient medical records. Moreover, we performed technical data quality checks to ensure consistency and completeness before entering the data.

Data processing and analysis

The data collection process was facilitated by deploying the Kobo Toolbox software. At the end of the data collection, we exported the data to Microsoft Excel 365 for cleaning and coding purposes and then analysed them using Stata software version 17. The SmartCare-ART database was used only to retrieve missing of laboratory data (viral load results and CD4 cell counts). Variables related to sociodemographic characteristics, caregiver factors, clinical staging, adherence, nutritional status, and ART initiation timing were not supplemented and were obtained exclusively from routine ART follow-up records.

Summary statistics, including frequencies with percentages for categorical variables and means with standard deviations or medians with interquartile ranges for continuous variables, were computed. Anthropometric measurements, including weight-for-age z-scores (WAZ) and height-for-age z-scores (HAZ), were calculated via WHO anthro (for children aged < 5 years) and anthroplus (for children aged ≥ 5 years) software on the basis of the WHO nutritional assessment guidelines.

We used the Kaplan‒Meier method, along with the log-rank test, to compare failure experiences between the categorical variables and to assess whether the differences in failure rates were statistically significant. The cumulative magnitude of failure was estimated from the proportion of children with viral loads above 1000 copies/ml at baseline and during the follow-up periods. The incidence of treatment failure was calculated per 1000 person‒months of follow‒up.

A test of time-varying covariates against time (log(time)) shows that none of the time-varying covariates were significant, collectively or individually, implying that the model did not violate the proportional hazards (PH) assumption. The Schoenfeld global test also confirms that the overall model satisfies the PH assumption, with a chi-square value of 6.63 and a p-value of 0.95. Moreover, the absence of multicollinearity was confirmed, as the variance inflation factor values for all predictor variables were less than 2, with a mean value of 1.17 (see Additional file 4: Testing Proportional hazards assumption and Collinearity Diagnostics).

We employed a multivariable Cox regression model to identify predictors of treatment failure and adjusted hazards ratio (AHR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) to measure the associations. A statistically significant association was declared at a p-value less than 0.05.

Operational definitions

Jimma: encompasses health facilities (health centers and hospitals) in the Jimma zone, Jimma town, and Agaro town.

Treatment failure (event of the study): Viral load above 1000 copies/ml based on two consecutive VL tests, with adherence support following the first elevated VL test after 6 months of initiating ART during the follow-up time of the study [6].

Censored: A child who died before experiencing TF, lost to follow-up, transferred out, regimen shifted to an adult dose, or was free from the event during the follow-up time of the study.

Lost to follow-up: if no follow-up visit for ≥ 3 months [6].

Regimen switch: changing from the first-line to second-line regimen.

Regimen substitution: replacing the preferred first-line regimen with an alternative first-line ART regimen.

Timing of ART initiation: classified as 'Rapid' if ART is initiated on the same day or within seven days of a confirmed HIV-positive diagnosis (if ready), or 'Delayed' if it is initiated after seven days from the date of confirmed HIV-positive diagnosis, unless indicated to delay treatment [4, 6, 39].

Anemia: Hgb concentration of less than 11 g/dl for 6–59 months of age, less than 11.5 g/dl for ages 5–11 years, and less than 12 g/dl for ages 12–14 years old [6].

Adherence status: recorded as 'good' (≥ 95% or ≤ 2 doses missed per month), ‘fair’ (85%—94% or 3–8 doses missed per month), or ‘poor’ (< 85% or ≥ 8 doses missed per month) [6].

Underweight: weight-for-age z-scores less than −2 [6].

Stunted: height-for-age z-scores less than −2 [6].

Results

Description of study participants

This study reviewed a total of 335 medical records of children on first-line antiretroviral therapy (ART) from four hospitals and five health centers. Of these, 13 incomplete records and 10 records with missing values were excluded from the final analysis, making the study's total sample size 312 children living with HIV (CLHIV).

Sociodemographic characteristics of the study participants

More than half of the study participants were female (163, 52.2%), and 171 (54.8%) resided in urban areas. Three-fourths (234, 75%) of the study participants belonged to the 10–14 years age group. Regarding the caregivers of the children, most were parents (254, 81.4%), and 261(83.7%) were HIV-positive (Table 1).

Clinical, and immunological characteristics of study participants

Thirty-two participants (10.3%) were in advanced HIV disease (WHO stages III or IV) at baseline. Thirty-six participants (13.1%) had a baseline CD4 cell count of less than 200 cells/mm3, with an overall median of 434 cells/mm3 (IQR of 281–618). Fifty-seven (18.3%) had high-level viraemia (viral load > 1000 copies/mL) at the end of the study’s follow-up period, and 40 (14.7%) were anemic at baseline. In addition, 94 (30.1%) of the study participants had opportunistic infection at initiation of ART (Table 2).

Antiretroviral therapy treatment-related factors

The median duration of the study participants on the first-line ART was 78.5 months, with an interquartile range of 44 to 111 months. Among 312 study participants, 89 (28.5%) had not completed cotrimoxazole prophylaxis, 139 (44.6%) had also not completed tuberculosis prevention treatment, and 71 (22.8%) had either fair or poor adherence to their regimens. Moreover, the HIV status was not disclosed to 154 (49.4%) participants. Two hundred fifty-five (81.4%) had a history of regimen substitutions, and 144 (46.1%) initiated ART after seven days of confirmed HIV-positive status (Table 3).

Nutrition-related factors

According to the WHO nutritional assessment guidelines, 64 (20.5%) and 16 (5.1%) of children living with HIV were classified as underweight and stunted, respectively.

Time to treatment failure, and its incidence

As shown in Fig. 1, the median time to treatment failure on first-line ART was not reached, with a total follow-up of 23,614 person-months of observations. Among the children who had enrolled in first-line ART, 49 (15.71%) experienced treatment failure (95% CI: 11.57, 19.85). The overall incidence density of treatment failure was 2.07 children per 1,000 person-months of observations (95% CI: 1.56, 2.74). The cumulative probability of treatment failure on first-line ART during the 60th and 96th months was about 10% and 15% respectively.

Comparison of failure functions

The Kaplan–Meier failure function, along with the log-rank test, was used to compare failure experiences between various categorical variables. Without adjusting for other covariates, there was a significant difference in the median failure times by WHO clinical stages (log-rank test, χ2 = 113.24, p < 0.0001), baseline anemia status (χ2 = 14.90, p < 0.0001), caregiver's serostatus (χ2 = 19.09, p < 0.0001), duration on ART (χ2 = 78.01, p < 0.0001), disclosure of status to children (χ2 = 19.17, p < 0.001), history of regimen substitutions made during first-line ART (χ2 = 9.69, p < 0.01), records of missed clinic appointments (χ2 = 6.11, p < 0.05), and recent adherence status (χ2 = 117.17, p < 0.0001), as displayed in Fig. 2. However, a significant failure rate was not observed for gender, residence, and facility type.

Predictors of time to treatment failure among children on first-line ART

In the multivariable Cox regression analysis, seven variables—weight for age z-scores, timing of ART initiation, child’s age category, baseline WHO clinical stages, recent adherence status, being anemic, and caregiver’s serostatus—were identified as statistically significant predictors of time to treatment failure at a 95% confidence interval (95% CI).

During the first-line ART follow-up period, children who were classified as underweight based on their weight-for-age z-scores (WAZ) at baseline had a significantly greater risk of treatment failure when compared to those with normal WAZ scores (AHR = 3.95, 95% CI: 1.63, 9.57). Likewise, several predictor variables were significantly associated with an increased risk of treatment failure: being anemic at baseline (AHR = 3.01, 95% CI: 1.51, 6.01), delayed ART initiation (AHR = 3.46, 95%CI: 1.32, 9.06), recent fair/poor adherence (AHR = 11.36, 95% CI: 5.22, 24.73), and baseline WHO clinical stage III/IV (AHR = 2.49, 95% CI: 1.34, 4.66). Conversely, the caregiver's serostatus being positive (AHR = 0.191, 95% CI: 0.078, 0.46) and the age group of 10–14 years (AHR = 0.064, 95% CI: 0.023, 0.17) were identified as protective predictors against the risk of treatment failure (Table 4).

Discussion

This study aimed at determining the incidence rate of treatment failure and its predictors among a cohort of 312 children on first-line ART in Jimma, Ethiopia. The overall incidence density of treatment failure in this cohort was 2.07 children per 1,000 person-months of observations, which is comparable to the studies of the Amhara region (2.21 per 1000 person-months) [15] and Shashemene town (26 per 1000 person-years) [29]. However, it is lower than that reported in studies conducted in other parts of Ethiopia [15, 25, 33]. The lower incidence observed in the study could be partly related to differences in study settings, the operational definitions of treatment failure and children, and the inclusion of health centers that have limited access to viral load monitoring [27].

This study revealed a 15.71% prevalence of ART failure among children enrolled in first-line ART. This finding highlights crucial implications for public health and policy, emphasizing the need for strengthening targeted interventions for pediatric HIV. Such interventions are vital for reducing the risks associated with treatment failure, such as drug resistance, extra healthcare expenses, disease transmission and progression, increased morbidity and mortality, and the long-term health and well-being of affected children. This finding is comparable to studies conducted at the University of Gondar (14%), Southern Ethiopia (15%), and a systematic review (14.98%) [14, 40, 41]. However, it is lower than the results reported in Cameroon (25%), Uganda (29%), and Kenya (37%) [11, 30, 42]. The discrepancies in failure rates could be attributed to variations in follow-up durations, sample sizes, and national ART initiatives [43].

In essence, malnutrition and HIV infection have synergistic effects, forming a vicious cycle. The results of the study indicated that children who were underweight at baseline had an approximately fourfold greater risk of treatment failure than their counterparts (AHR = 3.95). This result aligns with studies conducted at Jimma University [28] and the University of Gondar [23]. Increased energy requirements in children living with HIV, complicated by malnutrition, can weaken the immune system, potentially increasing the risk of treatment failure [44]. Thus, meeting these children's nutritional needs is vital for favorable treatment outcomes.

Anemia, as the most common hematologic abnormality in children with HIV, is also a marker of HIV disease progression [45]. In this study, children who initiated ART with anemic status were three times more likely to experience early treatment failure than non-anemic children (AHR = 3.01). This finding coincides with the research conducted at the University of Gondar [23]. Another study also revealed that children who were not anemic at the start of ART had significantly better virological responses to therapy compared to anemic children [46]. Therefore, policies, strategies, and programs need to emphasize screening for anemia status during regular clinic visits and treating it early to improve treatment outcomes.

Current guidelines recommend rapid ART initiation (the same day or within one week if ready) for all people diagnosed with HIV unless indicated to delay treatment. In our study, children who initiated ART after seven days of confirmed HIV-positive status had an approximately threefold greater risk of treatment failure than those who initiated ART within a week (AHR = 3.46). While studies have revealed considerable proportions of children initiated ART late and identified associated factors [47], none have examined the effect of the timing of ART initiation on treatment failure.

Treatment adherence among children is a significant issue that requires attention. In this study, children with a record of fair or poor adherence (AHR = 11.36) faced a significantly higher risk of failure than those with good adherence. Prior studies, conducted at federal hospitals [19], referral hospitals in the Amhara [13], Kolfe Keranyo subcity [15], and the University of Gondar Hospital [14], support this finding. Thus, strengthening adherence counseling at every follow-up visit, as well as implementing community-based adherence programs (e.g., peer support, mobile reminders) targeting caregivers, is imperative.

The study findings showed that advanced disease stages at baseline, specifically WHO clinical stages III and IV, posed a greater risk of treatment failure compared to their counterparts (AHR = 2.49). This aligns with global findings and underscores a critical issue: the heightened risk of disease progression in these patients can significantly undermine the beneficial effects of ART on immune function [25].

The hazard of treatment failure in the current study decreased by 80.9% for children whose caregiver's serostatus was positive (AHR = 0.191), aligning with a 2019 study that identified caregivers' negative serostatus as a predictor of treatment failure [48]. This can be attributed to the fact that HIV-positive individuals are better informed about nutrition and medication adherence and may provide a more socially enabling environment [49], potentially leading to better treatment outcomes. Further research is needed to explore the sociodemographic characteristics and awareness of caretakers, as these are critical predictors of treatment failure.

This study revealed that children aged 10–14 years had a lower risk of treatment failure than did those aged 0–9 years (AHR = 0.064). This aligns with a prior study [50] and may be due to factors such as immune system development, pediatric-friendly formulations, adherence issues, and increased awareness of serostatus in adolescents [10], underscoring the need for age-tailored counseling of children by caregivers on consistent medication administration and emphasizing early diagnosis and initiation in young children.

Strengths, limitations, and further study

This study included data from multicenter settings with a ten-year follow-up, thus providing a broader perspective on pediatric ART outcomes. It also evaluated understudied predictors, including age, ART initiation timing, caregiver serostatus, and nutritional status. Owing to the nature of the retrospective design, some variables (e.g., caregiver socioeconomic status, parent survival, education, and occupation) had significant missing data and were excluded; potentially, they could have an effect on treatment failure in children. The sampling approach used to distribute the study facilities by level (hospital or health center) based on patient volume and geographic distribution may affect the generalizability of the results. The study findings suggest the need for longitudinal research to determine the causal factors, including drug resistance dynamics associated with first-line ART failure among children, and a qualitative study exploring practices regarding the management of ART failure.

Conclusions and recommendations

The treatment failure rate of first-line ART among children is still high, in terms of reaching the nation’s HIV-free generation target of less than 10%. Moreover, being underweight, delayed initiation, anemia, a history of poor and fair adherence, and advanced disease stages at baseline were significantly associated with antiretroviral treatment failure. Conversely, children whose caregivers had positive serostatus and those aged 10–14 years had a significantly lower risk of failure than their counterparts. Therefore, future interventions should focus on rapid ART initiation, adherence counseling, nutritional support, and early anemia management to improve pediatric HIV outcomes in Ethiopia. Furthermore, health professionals working at health facilities of ART clinics should strengthen adherence counseling at each follow-up visit (e.g., pill reminders, caregiver education), including home-based visits for fair and poor adherent children, conduct screening for anemia status at ART initiation and in regular clinic visits, and strengthen same-day ART initiation in children newly diagnosed with HIV.
